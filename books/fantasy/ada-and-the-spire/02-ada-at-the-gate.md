## 2. Ada at the Gate

The courier died at dawn, and with him died the last of Wendell's illusions.

He buried the man beneath the rowan tree at the valley's mouth, where the soil was softest and the roots would hold him like cupped hands. The ravens had kept watch through the night, and when Wendell finished tamping the earth, the eldest of them — a ragged, one-eyed thing named Sorrel in his mind — hopped down from her branch and spoke in the voice of dry leaves.

*Cold now. Quiet. The red has stopped.*

"Yes," Wendell said aloud, because there was no one left to hear him speak plainly. "It's stopped."

*You should not have pulled the arrow out,* Sorrel said, not unkindly. Ravens had no gift for comfort. *But you tried. That is a thing.*

Wendell wiped his hands on his trousers and looked down the length of the valley — the terraced barley going gold, the goats on the high shoulder of the fell, the beehives humming their low gold note behind the cottage. Twenty-two years he had lived here, and in all that time the valley had held him the way a closed fist holds water: completely, secretly, without ever letting magic find him. Here his gift was only a quietness between himself and the beasts, a listening that no hunter's charm could catch, because here there was no magic to catch. The valley forgot him. He had believed it always would.

*Someone comes,* said Sorrel, suddenly, her head snapping toward the pass. *Two legs. One. Walking wrong — favoring the left. Smelling of ink and far cities and fear.*

Wendell went still. "How far?"

*Half a morning. Maybe less. She stops often. She looks behind her more than ahead.*

*A she,* Wendell noted, and felt something loosen slightly in his chest — though he knew better than to trust relief. *She.* A lone woman did not usually come hunting gifted men into valleys that forgot. But lone women could be bait, or scouts, or worse: the patient kind who worked alone and reported to no one.

He spent the morning doing what he always did when the world threatened to notice him: he made himself small. He sent word through the bees, whose gossip traveled faster than any rider — *stranger coming, stay away from the road, take the children to the high pasture* — and by midday the valley wore its emptiest face. Smokeless chimneys. Shut doors. Only Wendell stood visible at the gate of his own croft, leaning on the fence post as if he had nothing in the world to do but watch the weather.

The stranger came down the pass an hour past noon.

She was younger than he expected — twenty-five, perhaps thirty, with the kind of sharp, tired beauty that comes from too many nights sleeping badly in strange beds. Her riding coat was good quality but travel-worn, its hem dark with mud, and she carried no weapon he could see except a long knife at her hip and a leather satchel slung across her body, which she held the way a mother holds a child. Her hair was dark brown shot through with premature grey at the temples, as if something had reached into her head and dragged its fingers through.

She stopped ten paces from the gate and looked at him for a long moment.

"You're the beast-speaker," she said.

"I'm a goatherd," Wendell said.

"You're a goatherd," she agreed, "who is currently being told by every bird within a mile that I'm coming, what I'm carrying, and whether my knife is sharp." She glanced up at a kestrel hanging motionless on the wind above the fell. "That one's been following me since the ridge. It thinks I smell interesting."

Wendell said nothing. The kestrel, whose name in his mind was a swift bright shape like a falling stone, said: *She is not afraid of me. Most two-legs are afraid of me. She watches me back.*

"Who are you?" he asked.

"Ada." She said it simply, without title or flourish. "I'm an archivist. I serve the crown."

Something cold slid down Wendell's spine. "Then you've come about the proclamation."

"I've come about the courier." Her eyes flicked to the fresh-turned earth beneath the rowan. "Merrick Aldous. Second courier of the western route. He carried a proclamation into this valley eleven days ago and was supposed to carry the valley's answer back. Instead he took an arrow from a hunter's patrol on the pass and crawled the last three miles to die in your barn." She said it flatly, factually, but Wendell saw her jaw tighten. "I'd like to know what he told you before he died."

"He told me the crown wants us culled," Wendell said. "He told me with his last breath. Is that the official version too?"

For a moment something moved behind her eyes — something quick and bitter and very old for her young face. Then it was gone, smoothed over like a hand passing over a bedsheet.

"The crown's proclamations are not mine to interpret," she said. "I'm here for the records. Merrick carried dispatches besides the proclamation. Sealed ones. If he gave them to you—"

"He didn't."

"Then I'll need to search your croft."

"No," Wendell said, "you won't."

They regarded each other across the gate, and Wendell became aware that the whole valley was watching with him. The bees had gone silent in their hives, a held breath of gold. The goats on the fell had stopped grazing, heads raised. Even the worms in the newly turned grave seemed to pause. And above it all the kestrel wheeled, and wheeled, and sang its falling-stone song directly into Ada's upturned face.

And Ada — crown archivist, servant of the king — flinched.

Not from the bird. From the words.

Wendell felt it happen the way one feels a floorboard shift underfoot. The kestrel's mind touched hers the way it touched all minds, lightly, reading surface things — and what it read came pouring back to Wendell in a wash of images not his own: *a door of black iron, a room of paper, hands slick with sweat, running, running, a satchel pressed to her chest, a name spoken in a dead woman's voice —*

*She lies,* said the kestrel, puzzled. *Her mouth says crown. Her inside says run.*

Wendell let out a breath he hadn't known he was holding. "You're not an archivist of the crown," he said quietly. "Or if you are, you've stopped being one recently. Very recently. There's a door — iron, black — you went through it running. You stole something. You've been afraid since before you reached the pass, and you're more afraid now, standing here, than you were on the ridge with the hunters somewhere behind you."

Ada had gone very pale. Her hand had drifted, quite unconsciously, to the satchel.

"What are you?" she whispered.

"A goatherd," Wendell said. "I told you."

* * *

They sat inside the croft with the door shut and the shutters drawn, because Ada insisted, jumping at every shadow the way a rabbit jumps at its own reflection. She sat across the table from him with the satchel in her lap and both hands on it, and for a long while she said nothing at all. Outside, the bees resumed their hum, cautiously, like an orchestra returning after a false start.

"Merrick," she said finally. "Did he suffer?"

"Hours. The arrow was deep. I eased what I could." Wendell turned his cup slowly on the table. "He asked me to tell someone in the capital that the culling lists are wrong. That they don't match the census. That names are being added that shouldn't be there — and some struck off."

Ada's breath caught audibly.

"He said—" Wendell frowned, reaching for the memory of those last rattling whispers. "He said the lists come from the Spire itself. Not from any ministry. And that no minister he'd ever served dared question them."

When he looked up, Ada had closed her eyes. When she opened them again, the mask was gone — not replaced by tears, but by something harder and more exhausted: the face of a person who has been carrying a weight alone for so long that setting it down, even partially, hurts more than holding it.

"My name is Ada Vey," she said. "I was an archivist of the crown. Third rank, restricted collections, Spire access." She patted the satchel once, lightly, like touching a wound. "Three weeks ago I walked out of the black iron door with this under my coat, and I have been running since. There are hunters on the roads with my face memorized. There is a price on me larger than the price on any gifted runaway in the kingdom." She laughed, short and joyless. "Do you know why? Because the gifted can hide. What I stole cannot."

"And what did you steal?"

"Paper," she said. "Forty years of paper. Census rolls against culling orders. Treasury disbursements against execution records. Letters in the king's own hand — dated, signed — that contradict other letters in the king's own hand written on the same day." Her voice dropped. "Ledgers of payment to men who do not officially exist. And a genealogy, Master Voss. A royal genealogy with pages cut out and sewn back in with different thread."

Wendell's mouth had gone dry. "What are you saying?"

"I'm saying I spent nine years in that archive, and I am very good at reading what isn't written down." She leaned forward. "I'm saying the king is not the king. Whatever sits on that throne and signs those orders — it hasn't been the man for a generation. The man is still alive. I found provisioning records for a private household within the Spire grounds, maintained continuously for thirty-one years, staffed by servants whose contracts list no name. Something wears him, Voss. Something uses his hand to sign culling orders, and feeds, and grows, and hunts people like you across three kingdoms — and I am the only person alive who has proof, and no army, and nowhere left to run except into a valley that supposedly forgets."

The fire ticked in the hearth. Somewhere outside, a goat complained to another goat about the quality of the grass, and Wendell translated it absently, automatically, the way a musician hears a note.

"Why come to me?" he asked. "If you're running, why stop? Why risk the valley?"

"Because the animals talk to you." She said it without embarrassment. "Merrick told me, dying, that the beast-speaker of this valley would either turn me over or help me, and that I'd know which within a minute of meeting him, because the birds would tell me." A ghost of a smile. "The kestrel told me. In its way."

"It told me about you, too."

"I know. I watched your face change." She straightened, and now something new entered her voice — the careful tone of a merchant opening negotiations she has already lost twice before. "So here is my offer, and I'll make it only once. I have a route south. Old smuggler ways, safe houses, people who owe me. I can get you out of this valley before the next patrol comes — and a patrol will come, Voss, because Merrick never sent his answer back, and missing couriers bring questions, and questions bring riders. In exchange, you give me your gift."

Wendell went very still. "My gift isn't a purse to be handed over."

"I know how gifts work better than you think — I've read the old records, the pre-crown records, the ones they burned everywhere else." Her eyes were steady and unflinching. "I'm not asking you to surrender it to the crown. I'm asking you to use it. For me. Come with me to the capital, speak with the beasts of the city — the rats in the Spire walls, the dogs in the yards, the pigeons on the roofs. They see everything. They remember everything. No archive holds half of what the animals of that city know." She tapped the satchel. "My paper tells us the king is a lie. Their eyes will tell us what the lie is. And then—" she exhaled, "—then we decide what two people with no army can possibly do about it."

"That's not an offer of escape," Wendell said slowly. "That's an offer of suicide."

"Probably." She didn't flinch. "But the alternative is waiting here until the riders come, and I've read the culling lists, Voss. I know what happens in the villages after. They don't just take the gifted. They take anyone who sheltered them. Anyone who saw. Anyone who buried a courier under a rowan tree and lied to an archivist about it." She rose, shouldering the satchel. "You're already in it. You've been in it since you pulled that arrow out. The only choice left is whether you run toward the fire or sit here until it reaches you."

Outside, the light was failing. On the fence post, Sorrel the raven had landed without sound, and she looked at Wendell with her one bright eye, and she said the thing that decided him — not because it was wise, but because it was true:

*The valley forgets,* she said. *But forgetting does not last forever. Nothing lasts forever. Better to walk out while your legs are yours.*

Wendell looked at Ada Vey — thief, liar, fugitive, the most dangerous kind of stranger: an honest one — and he thought of the courier's blood on his barn floor, and the cut threads in a royal genealogy, and a hollow king signing death warrants with a hand that was not his own.

"One condition," he said.

Ada paused at the door. "Name it."

"When we reach the capital, we find out what happened to Merrick's family. First thing. Before anything else."

Something flickered across her face — surprise, and beneath it, faintly, respect. "First thing," she agreed.

She put out her hand. Wendell took it. Her grip was cold and strong, the grip of someone who had learned that warmth was a luxury paid for in blood.

"We leave before dawn," she said. "South, through the smugglers' pass. And Voss—" she glanced back at the rowan tree, the fresh grave, the valley going blue with evening, "—whatever you've heard about the Spire, whatever the wolves say, whatever the rats whisper — believe it. All of it. The truth of that place is worse than anything the animals could invent."

Above the fells, the first stars were coming out, and the kestrel began its slow descent toward the croft, singing its falling-stone song into the gathering dark — and in its song, threaded thin as wire, Wendell heard something new. Not warning. Not fear.

An invitation.

Far to the south, something in a tower of black glass had begun, very softly, to sing back.
