## 3. What the Wolves Remember

The pass above the valley was called the Shepherd's Throat, and it earned the name honestly. The trail narrowed to a shelf of stone no wider than a cart, with a drop on the left hand that went down so far the river at its bottom was only a sound, a silver whisper threading through the dark. Wind came up that throat like breath drawn through teeth.

Wendell climbed it with his coat collar up and Ada's silhouette ahead of him, black against a sky still bruised with the last of the night. Behind them, three days' hard walking down, the valley slept on under its strange, giftless quiet — and somewhere beyond it, he knew, riders were already moving. The courier's proclamation had said as much: *all gifted persons to present themselves for culling*. Culling. As if they were deer with too many antlers. As if they were sickness in a herd.

He had not wanted to leave. That was the truth of it, the small shameful truth he carried up the mountain like a stone in his boot. Twenty-nine years he had lived in that valley, and not one of them had been wasted on ambition. He had mended hawks' wings and sat up all night with foaling mares and argued politics with a raven named Sorrel who thought herself wiser than any bishop. Magic could not follow him there — whatever old bargain had been struck between the valley and the world had seen to that — and so his gift lay in him like a banked fire, warm but harmless, and he had told himself for twenty-nine years that this was enough.

The animals had known better. On the morning he left, the whole valley had come to see him off: deer standing in the mist at the field's edge, foxes sitting in a row along the fence like a jury, old Bramble the ox lowing so mournfully that Wendell had to press his forehead against her wet nose just to keep from turning back.

*Go*, she had said, in the wordless way of beasts. *Go, before the two-legs with iron come. We cannot hide you. We were never able to hide you. You only believed we could.*

Now the wind shoved at his back like a hand between the shoulder blades, hurrying him toward something worse, and he let it.

* * *

They made camp at midday in the lee of a fallen pine, where the trail bent away from the drop and the rock wall offered a little shelter from the wind. Ada built no fire. She ate hard cheese and dried plums from her pack with the methodical efficiency of a woman feeding a machine, and watched the trail behind them the way other people watch a door.

Wendell had been listening since dawn. Not with his ears — with the older sense, the one that needed no magic here because it wasn't magic at all, or so he'd always told himself. It was simply how he heard. And what he heard, threaded thin through the wind and the stone, was a conversation happening above them, high on the ridgeline, in voices like torn cloth.

*They are coming up the Throat,* the voices said. *The soft ones. The one who smells of hearth-smoke and the one who smells of ink and fear.*

He set down his food. "There are wolves," he said quietly. "Four of them. On the ridge above us."

Ada's hand went to the knife at her belt before the meaning of his words caught up with her face. "Wolves don't talk."

"No," Wendell agreed. "But they listen. And I've been talking to animals since I was six years old, which is why we're running, in case you've forgotten." He tilted his head, following the thread of their speech. "They're not hunting us. They're curious. They say—" he paused, translating the scent-and-image language into words, "—they say we smell like the city. Like bells and ash. They knew this place once, before men cleared it. They're old."

Ada studied him for a long moment. In the flat grey light of the pass her face looked carved rather than born, all planes and shadow, except for her eyes, which were restless and quick and much too young for the rest of her. "How old?"

"Older than me." He rose, brushing pine needles from his knees. "Older than the valley, maybe. If you want to know what the capital is really like, ask something that walked its streets before the culling began. Ask something that remembers when the Spire was young." He glanced back at her. "You stole records out of the royal archive. Records can lie. Wolves can't."

"They can be wrong," Ada said.

"So can archivists. Come and meet them anyway."

* * *

The wolves came down to them at dusk, when the sky over the eastern peaks had gone the color of an old bruise and the first stars stood in it like nails. Four of them, grey as the scree, moving with the unhurried confidence of creatures who have never once in their lives been prey. The leader was a female with a grizzled muzzle and one ear cropped short by some ancient fight; she stopped ten paces off and regarded Wendell with amber eyes that held no warmth and no malice either, only assessment, the cold arithmetic of a thing deciding what you are.

"You speak," she said. Her voice in his mind was dry wind over stone. "The soft ones used to speak, in the old days. Then they stopped. Then they started again, badly, wearing crowns."

"I'm Wendell." He kept his hands loose at his sides, his weight easy. Beside him Ada had gone very still, the stillness of a woman doing arithmetic about distances and knives. "We're traveling to the capital. To the Spire."

The wolf's lips lifted from her teeth — not quite a snarl, not quite anything else. "Why would you go there? Nothing goes there. Things only go *in*."

"That's rather what we've heard," Ada said, before she could stop herself, and then pressed her lips together, annoyed at having spoken to a wolf.

"She talks," the wolf observed. "Does she know what she carries? She smells of paper and stolen things and bloodline. Old bloodline. Spire-bloodline."

Wendell felt Ada go rigid beside him. He did not look at her. "Tell me about the Spire," he said instead. "You roamed the capital once. Before."

"We roamed everything once." The wolf sat, folding her haunches beneath her with terrible dignity, and her three companions settled around her like stones rolling into place. "Before the walls, before the bells. There was a hill, and on the hill a spring, and the spring sang. All the water in that country came from it, and all the water remembered the song. Fish sang it. Reeds sang it. When our grandmothers' grandmothers drank, they sang it in their sleep." Her ears flattened. "Then men built a tower on the hill and called it holy, and bound something into the foundation stone, and the singing changed."

"Changed how?" Wendell asked, though some cold part of him already knew.

"It sings wrong now." The wolf's voice dropped, and the other wolves lowered their heads, and even the wind seemed to lean in to listen. "It is still a song. That is the horror of it. It is beautiful, the way a snare is beautiful, the way ice over deep water is beautiful. It goes down and down and never comes back up. Our kind will not drink from the rivers below the city anymore. The water carries the song. The birds that nest on the Spire forget their own names by midsummer. Dogs that live in its shadow dream nothing but the song, every night, until they die of tiredness." She fixed her amber eyes on Wendell. "And the man in the tower — the crowned one — he sings it loudest of all. From inside. Where the song keeps him."

"The king," Wendell said slowly. "You're saying the king is—"

"Kept," said the wolf. "Like a bird in a cage that thinks the cage is the sky. We have watched four of them now. Each one walks into the tower young and comes out — less. Each one speaks with a voice that isn't quite his own. Each one dies smiling, and the smile is not his either, and then the crown goes onto the next head, and the next head begins to sing." She bared her teeth fully now, and it was not aggression; it was grief, worn as armor. "Your kind calls it inheritance. An unbroken line, blessed and sacred. We call it a leash, handed down. We have watched leashes a long time, soft-speaker. We know one when we see it."

The wind moved through the pass. Somewhere far below, the river whispered on, indifferent.

"And you," the wolf said, and her nose pointed at him, precise as a compass needle. "You smell of the same root. Older than the crown. The song and your gift grew from one soil, soft-speaker. Can't you taste it? When you speak to beasts — where do you think the speaking goes? What do you think carries it?"

Wendell opened his mouth and found nothing in it.

Because he *could* taste it, suddenly, the way you taste a note you've hummed all your life and finally hear played on another instrument. His gift had never felt like magic — that was why the valley's silence had never silenced it. It was older than magic, quieter, a current underneath the current, and now, hearing the wolf describe the Spire's song, he recognized the shape of it the way you recognize your own handwriting on a stranger's letter. The same grammar. The same deep, patient, binding voice.

He was kin to it. Whatever wore the king like a coat, whatever sang from the foundations of the tower — it spoke the language he had loved all his life. It was the reason he could speak at all.

"Wendell." Ada's hand closed on his arm, and her voice was careful, the voice of someone approaching a wounded animal. "What is she saying?"

He couldn't answer. The wolf was watching him with something that in a human face he would have called pity.

"One more question," he managed. His own voice sounded far away. "The thing in the Spire. The thing that sings. Do you know what it was? Before men bound it?"

The wolves looked at one another. The old female's ear twitched.

"Our oldest grandmother sang of it," she said at last. "She said it was not a demon, whatever the priests claim. She said it was one of *us*. A guardian-beast, old as the spring itself, who let men bind it to protect them — and stayed bound so long it forgot it had ever been free. It does not devour kings out of malice, soft-speaker. It devours them because it is starving, and has been starving for nine hundred years, and no one has ever once asked it what it wants." She rose, shaking the dust from her grey coat. "You are going to the tower. You carry its own voice in your mouth. Go and ask it."

They melted back into the dark of the ridge, four shadows becoming none, and Wendell stood in the failing light with Ada's grip tightening on his arm and the whole architecture of his life quietly rearranging itself around a single unbearable thought:

To free the king, he would have to speak to the monster. And the monster spoke *his* language. And some buried, treacherous part of him — the part that had spent twenty-nine years longing for someone, *anything*, to talk to —

already missed it.

"What did she tell you?" Ada demanded. "Wendell. *What did she say?*"

He turned to her, and the words came out of him like something being born backwards, painful and inevitable.

"Ada. What exactly did your stolen records say about where the royal gift comes from?"
