## 5. The City of Bells

The capital announced itself long before we saw it — first as a smudge of woodsmoke on the horizon, then as a sound. Bells. Dozens of them, layered over one another, marking hours and half-hours and saints' days I no longer believed in, so that the city never fell silent, never slept, never let a single thought finish itself.

I had forgotten how much I hated that sound.

"Stop fidgeting," I said to Wendell, for the fourth time since dawn. He sat in the cart among the two mules and the shaggy mountain pony we'd bought at the last market town, wearing a leather apron that made him look like exactly what he was pretending to be: a provincial beast-trainer come to sell his stock to the royal menagerie. His hands, those hands that could quiet a wolf with a whisper, were knotted around the reins like a man holding onto a cliff edge.

"I can hear them," he said quietly. "The animals in the city. There are so many, Ada. Dogs, rats, horses, birds in cages—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening. "They're all talking at once."

"Then don't listen."

"You say that like it's a door I can close." He gave me a look that was not quite accusation and not quite plea. "You taught me shields for my mind. Who teaches me shields for this?"

I had no answer, so I gave him none, and the bells swallowed the silence between us whole.

At the Southgate, the queue of carts stretched half a mile, and we crawled forward through a gauntlet of gray-cloaked inspectors bearing the Spire's mark: a tower within a circle, stamped in black wax on every document they touched. I had walked through this gate a hundred times as an archivist's apprentice, head down, ink-stained, invisible. Invisibility had been my art long before I met Wendell Voss. I wore a courier's livery now, faded and unremarkable, with forged papers naming me Handler Maren Cotter, licensed under a beast-trainer named Oskar Hale.

Oskar Hale. Wendell had chosen the name himself, and I still didn't know whether it was a joke or a grief. I hadn't asked.

"Hale," the inspector said, consulting his ledger without looking up. "Purpose of entry?"

"Selling stock to the menagerie," I said, bored, tired, exactly as boring and tired as any handler who'd spent nine days on the road. "Royal warrant's in the satchel."

He waved us toward the secondary inspection, where a second inspector walked the length of the cart, prodding the mules, checking hooves. Wendell sat very still. I watched the inspector's hand hover near the pony's flank and saw something pass between the animal and the man beside me — something I could not see but could feel, like a change in air pressure. The pony did not startle. The inspector moved on.

"Gifted persons are required to declare themselves," the first inspector recited, in the flat tone of a man saying words he said forty times a day. "Culling is mandatory by decree of the Crown. Failure to declare is treason."

"Of course," I said. "We're just beast-folk. No gifts here. Only patience."

He almost smiled. Almost. He stamped our papers and waved us through, and the gate's shadow slid over us like water closing over a stone, and then we were inside.

Inside. The city hit Wendell like a wave hits a swimmer who has misjudged the tide. I felt it more than saw it — the way he went rigid beside me, the way his breathing shortened, the way his eyes darted to every dog trotting past, every pigeon bursting upward, every rat flowing along the gutter edges like dark water. The animals of Corvane were speaking to him, thousands upon thousands of them, and most of what they had to say, I would learn soon enough, was terrible.

* * *

The archive district smelled the way it always had: dust and tallow and old vellum, the ghost-scent of ten thousand extinguished candles. I had spent six years of my life in these streets and had told myself I'd feel nothing coming back. I was wrong. My body remembered everything — the shortcut behind the bindery, the tavern where the copyists drank, the exact number of steps up to the reading room where I had first found the sealed folio that ruined my life and started this journey.

I left Wendell at the Gilded Bridle inn with strict instructions: stay with the animals, speak to no one, and above all do nothing that might draw attention. Then I went to find Sefa.

Sefa Quill ran a bindery off Inkwell Lane, a legitimate business, mostly. She also kept the only unofficial index of the crown archives that existed anywhere in Corvane — a card catalog of secrets, cross-referenced in her own cipher, built over twenty years of copying documents she was never supposed to read twice. She had been my teacher before she was my accomplice, and when I fled the capital eleven months ago with stolen folios under my coat, she was the one who held the gate open.

Her shop bell rang wrong when I entered. That was the first sign. The second was that Sefa looked up from her workbench and went white.

"Maren," she breathed. "Saints' teeth, girl. You're supposed to be dead."

"Rumors of my death were greatly exaggerated by me."

"Don't joke." She came around the bench, took my face in her ink-blackened fingers, and studied me like a damaged manuscript. "There's a bounty, Ada. Not the public one — the quiet kind. The Spire's hunters carry your description. They know you're an archivist. They know what you took." Her eyes flicked to the shuttered window. "Why in all the hells did you come back?"

"Because the answer is here. The inheritance — the succession records. I got fragments out, but the full chain is in the restricted vaults, and I need someone who knows the rotation of the seals."

"And you thought you'd stroll in and ask." Sefa laughed, but there was no humor in it; it came out cracked. She poured two cups of plum brandy from the bottle she kept hidden in the false spine of a hymnal, and pushed one into my hand. "Drink. Then listen, because what I know will curdle that brandy in your gut."

I drank. It burned. I listened.

"The disappearances started eight months ago," she said. "Gifted folk who declared themselves for culling — the official story is they're relocated to work camps in the east. But the work camps don't exist, Ada. I've checked manifests, grain shipments, road tolls. Nothing goes east. What does happen is this: every tenth day, a column of wagons leaves the Culling House after dark and goes to the Spire. And every tenth day, the Spire's bells ring at midnight, though no ringer is ever seen."

My mouth had gone dry despite the brandy. "You think the culled are taken into the Spire."

"I think more than taken." She lowered her voice until it was barely a thread. "My cousin's boy worked the Spire kitchens until last spring. He said the servants were forbidden from the lower levels, but the ones who disobeyed — the ones who talked — spoke of a room at the root of the tower. A room that hums. And they said the old king's confessor went down there once a month with a ledger, and came back up thinner each time. Like something was being written *out* of him." She gripped my wrist. "Ada. Whatever spirit you told me about, whatever wears the king — I think it isn't fed by ceremony anymore. I think it's fed by *people*. By their gifts. The culling isn't purification. It's harvest."

Outside, the bells began to ring the hour, and I understood for the first time why there were so many of them. Not to mark time. To drown something out. To keep the whole city from hearing whatever sang beneath them.

* * *

Wendell was waiting in the inn's stable yard when I returned, sitting on an overturned feed trough in the gathering dusk, and I knew before I reached him that something was wrong. He had the stillness of a man listening to something enormous.

"They're gone," he said, without preamble. "The gifted ones who surrendered."

"How do you—"

"The dogs talk about it. The rats too, and the rats see everything — they live in the walls of the Culling House." His voice was low and steady, which somehow made it worse. "Wagons leave at night. The animals near the Spire won't go near it. Cats, pigeons, rats — they've all abandoned the blocks around the tower. Ada, the *pigeons* left. Pigeons don't leave a city. There's something at the base of that Spire that frightens them out of their roosts, out of their natures." He looked up at me, and in the lamplight his eyes were hollow. "And the horses that pull the wagons back? They come back empty, but they come back *lighter*. Like they've carried away less than they brought. The grooms say the wagons weigh nothing on the return trip."

"Because there's nothing left in them to weigh," I said. Sefa's words and Wendell's had met somewhere in the middle of the yard and become one truth, and the truth was monstrous. "The culling feeds it. Every gifted person who surrenders is carried to the Spire and poured into that thing like wine into a cup."

Wendell stood. He paced once, twice, and the mules in their stalls turned their heads to watch him, and I realized they were watching him the way people watch a fire — drawn and afraid at once.

"Do you know what the worst part is?" he said. "It's not the horror of it. It's that I understand it. When I heal an animal, I take its pain into myself and let it go. That's the gift. That's the *root* of it — the same root as the thing in the king. It takes. It holds. It pours itself into another vessel." He stopped pacing. "Every day I'm in this city, I can feel it pulling at me. Like recognizes like, Ada. The Spire knows I'm here. I felt it today when we passed within a mile of it — a hum, right at the edge of hearing, like it was tasting the air."

"That's why we keep you away from it," I said. "That's the plan. You stay with the animals, I get the succession records, and we learn how to break the line before that thing ever learns your name."

"It already knows my name." He said it simply, terribly, the way a man reports weather. "The dogs told me. There are hunters asking at every stable and kennel in the city for a valley man with a beast-gift. They're calling you his handler. They have your description, Ada — your real one. Someone talked."

*Sefa*, I thought, and then dismissed it — no. Sefa had gone white with fear for me, not fear of me. Someone else. Someone in the network. Someone whose cards were now marked.

Before I could speak, the stable door creaked, and one of the inn's cats flowed out of the darkness toward Wendell — a scarred orange tom with a torn ear, moving with the strange deliberation of an animal carrying a message. It sat down in front of Wendell and stared at him, and Wendell went very pale.

"What is it?" I whispered.

"It says a man paid the innkeeper's boy a copper to watch us." Wendell's voice had dropped to almost nothing. "A gray-cloaked man with the Spire's mark. And it says—" He swallowed. "It says the man is walking up the lane right now."

From beyond the inn wall, faint and patient, the bells of Corvane began to ring midnight — all of them at once, though no hour had struck, though no ringer rang. And beneath their brazen roar, so deep I felt it in my teeth rather than heard it, the Spire began to sing.
