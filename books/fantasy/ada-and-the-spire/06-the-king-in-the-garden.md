## 6. The King in the Garden

The summons came on vellum the color of old teeth, sealed with a stag's head pressed in black wax.

*Wendell Voss, beast-trainer of the eastern road, is commanded to attend His Majesty's menagerie at his earliest convenience. The royal menagerie-master has died. The beasts grow restless. It is said you have a way with them.*

Ada read it twice by candlelight, her face going through a series of small, precise movements — like a lock testing keys. "It is said," she repeated. "Someone talked."

"The mule," Wendell said. He sat with his back against the wall of their rented room, knees drawn up, listening to the city breathe below them. Even here, four floors above the tannery row, he could feel it: the low murmur of ten thousand animals packed into courtyards and stables and cages, all of them talking, all of them afraid. "The woman who owned him. She watched me too long. I saw her counting my hands."

"Counting your hands?"

"Seeing if they were clean." He flexed his fingers. In the valley, his hands had been farmer's hands, honest and unremarkable. Here they had begun to look like what they were: instruments. "Or she told someone, and someone told someone, and now the crown wants its wandering beast-speaker where it can see him."

"Or it's exactly what it looks like," Ada said. "A job. A dead menagerie-master, animals going mad in their cages, and a stranger with a reputation. Kings do keep animals, Wendell. It's one of the few human things about them."

"And if it isn't? If it's a snare?"

She was quiet for a moment. Candlelight moved in her eyes, and for once she did not look like an archivist or a liar or a fugitive. She looked tired, and young, and afraid in the specific way of people who have already lost everything once and know the shape of losing. "Then we're caught either way," she said finally. "They know your name. They knew it before the summons came — that's why the summons came. Refusing won't make you invisible. Nothing makes you invisible anymore." She set the letter down. "I'll be close. The garden wall has a gate the groundkeepers use. If you can get to it—"

"If I'm caught at it, we're both caught."

"Yes," Ada said simply. "That's what family means. I looked it up once, in a book of heraldry, of all things. It means the traps are shared."

He almost smiled. Almost. Outside, the bells of the morning watch began, and beneath them, faint as a pulse under skin, he heard the Spire singing its wrong note.

* * *

The royal gardens lay behind the palace like a held breath.

Wendell had expected marble and order, hedges cut into obedience. Instead the gates opened onto something older and wilder — an orchard gone half to ruin, gravel paths swallowed by moss, roses climbing unchecked over a trellis whose lattice had rotted away so that the flowers seemed to float unsupported in the grey air. Autumn had come early to the capital, or perhaps the capital simply wore autumn the way other places wore seasons, and the leaves came down around him in slow spirals, red and gold and a brown so deep it was nearly black.

And everywhere, the animals.

He heard them before he saw them. The menagerie occupied the garden's far quarter, and its voices reached him across the orchard like a tide: the coughing bark of a fox-deer, the muttering of ravens, the endless low complaint of something large and cat-shaped pacing behind bars. But underneath the ordinary noise ran another sound, one that made the hair rise along his arms. Fear. Not the sharp fear of a startled creature but the long, worn fear of animals that had been afraid for years, until fear had become the shape of their thoughts.

A gardener met him at the orchard's edge — a stooped man with soil in the creases of his knuckles and eyes that did not meet Wendell's. "Menagerie's through there," he said, jerking his chin. "Master Corvin died Tuesday. Fell into the bear pit." He paused. "Bears hadn't been fed since Sunday."

"They pushed him?" Wendell asked quietly.

The gardener's gaze flicked up, then away. "No one pushed Master Corvin anywhere," he said, in the tone of a man reciting words handed to him from above. "The king will receive you after you've seen to the animals. Alone." Another pause, longer. "He likes the garden. He walks it. You may see him. Don't run. Running upsets him."

*Upsets him.* Wendell turned the phrase over as the gardener shuffled away. Upset the king. As though the king were one more animal in the collection, and the whole garden his cage.

The menagerie itself was worse than he had imagined. Cages of iron and gilded bronze stood in long ranks, each labeled with a painted plaque bearing the beast's name and country of origin, as though origin could substitute for sky. A snow lynx from the northern ranges lay flat on its belly, ears pinned, staring at nothing. A crane stood on one leg in a pen barely larger than itself, its remaining wing feathers clipped to stubs. Two wolfhounds — wolves, truly, though the plaques called them hounds — pressed themselves against the back wall of their enclosure when Wendell approached, hackles lifted, and then, slowly, unbelievingly, lowered them again.

*You hear us,* the elder wolf said. Her voice was rust and river-stones. *After all this time. After the quiet ones stopped coming.*

*I hear you,* Wendell said. He crouched by the bars, not touching, just present, the way he'd learned to sit with frightened creatures in the valley. *I'm sorry. I'm sorry it took so long.*

*The quiet ones used to come,* the wolf said. *Before the Spire sang wrong. Before the tall sickness. They would walk these paths and speak to us, and the king—" She stopped. Her sister whined, a thin thread of sound.

*What about the king?*

The elder wolf looked at him with yellow eyes, and what he saw in them was not hatred. It was pity. *Go and see,* she said. *But go gently, speaker. What wears him is old, and old things are most dangerous when they are frightened. And it is very frightened now. It has been frightened since the girl.*

*Since the—*

*Go and see,* she said again, and turned away, and would say no more.

* * *

He worked through the afternoon because work was the only prayer he knew. He fed the lynx and spoke to it until its ears came forward. He waded into the crane's pen and stood motionless until the bird's terror broke against his stillness like water against a stone, and then it stepped close, and tucked its ruined wing, and leaned its long neck against his shoulder with a sigh that went through him like a blade. He cleaned the bear pit himself, alone, unarmed, while the great brown shape watched him from its den with eyes like wet amber, and when he finished he stood at the pit's edge and said, aloud, in the tongue that needed no air: *I know why you did it. I don't forgive it yet. But I know.*

The bear made a sound like a landslide far away, and put its head down, and slept.

The light was failing when the king came.

Wendell heard him first — not footsteps, for the man walked softly, but the absence of birdsong moving ahead of him through the orchard like a bow-wave. Then the shapes of the trees resolved, and there was a figure among them, robed in grey, silver-haired, walking without purpose down a path that went nowhere.

Wendell had seen portraits of King Aldous in the archive windows and tavern broadsheets: a broad-chested man with a strong jaw and a conqueror's smile. The thing that walked the garden wore that face the way a coat hangs on a hook. The flesh had fallen in. The smile-lines remained, carved deep, but nothing lived behind them; they were geological features, remnants of some earlier age. His eyes were the worst. They were pale, almost colorless, and they moved constantly, scanning the borders of the garden, the trellises, the darkening windows of the palace — always checking, always watching for something that might come over the wall.

*It looks for her,* whispered the ravens from the bare branches overhead. *Every day it looks for her.*

The king stopped three paces away. For a long moment neither of them spoke. The wind moved the dead leaves between them.

"You are the beast-speaker," the king said. His voice was dry paper. "Corvin's letters said you calmed the lynx in an hour. Corvin could not calm it in nine years." The colorless eyes fixed on Wendell, and in them, for an instant, Wendell felt something vast turn over and look at him — something coiled deep beneath the ruined face, ancient and cold and curious, tasting the air of his mind the way a snake tastes with its tongue.

Then it withdrew. And the eyes changed.

It happened like a drowning man surfacing. The pale gaze filled suddenly with color — brown, warm, human, horrified — and the mouth twisted, and the king's hand shot out and gripped Wendell's wrist with a strength that had no business living in that body.

"It sleeps at dusk," the king breathed. "Only at dusk. Listen to me. Listen. There is so little time." His breath was sour and shaking. "It took everything. My mother. My sons. It wears me to the councils and smiles with my mouth and signs with my hand and I am in a room inside myself, a small room, and the walls are made of its voice. Twenty-two years. Twenty-two years in the small room." Tears cut channels down the hollow cheeks. "But there was one it could not finish. One it reached for and could not hold. Do you understand? It fears her. It has burned half the archives and emptied whole villages looking, and it grows more afraid every year, and its fear is the only weapon I have left."

"Your daughter," Wendell said. The words came out of him without permission.

The king's grip tightened until the bones ached. His eyes — those brief, borrowed, human eyes — searched Wendell's face with desperate intensity, and Wendell understood suddenly that the man was reading him, hunting for something, and finding it: the valley in his accent, the road-dust on his boots, the company he kept.

"Find my daughter," the king whispered. "Find her first. Whatever you are, whatever you came here for — find her before it does. Tell her—" His voice cracked like ice on a pond. "Tell her the lullaby was real. Tell her I remember all of it. Every verse."

Then the light went out of his face. It was not gradual. It was a lamp shuttered, a door slammed, and the pale eyes returned, flat and cold and patient, and the hand released Wendell's wrist finger by finger, deliberately, the way a creditor counts coins.

"How interesting," the king said, in a voice like a well at midnight. "It seems my father's gift runs further than we thought. Further than the blood." The colorless gaze held Wendell's, and behind it something smiled without using the mouth. "You will attend the menagerie daily, beast-speaker. We have so much to learn from one another."

The king turned and walked back into the orchard, and the birdsong resumed behind him like water closing over a stone.

Wendell stood alone in the falling dark, his wrist throbbing, the king's tears still wet on his own sleeve where the man had gripped him. Overhead, the ravens stirred and muttered, and beyond the garden wall, faint but unmistakable, he heard quick footsteps — Ada, coming to the groundkeepers' gate, coming for him, alive and breathing and hunted.

*It fears her,* the old wolf had said. *It has been frightened since the girl.*

And Wendell, standing in the ruins of the king's garden, understood at last what he had been sent to find — and that he had known her name all along.
