## 7. Ada's Inheritance

The safehouse smelled of tallow and wet stone, and I could not stop looking at my hands.

They were my mother's hands. I had known that since I was nine years old, when a laundress in the village where we hid had grabbed my wrist to pull me from a stream and gone pale at the sight of them — the long fingers, the crescent scar of a burn across the left palm that Mother had earned pulling bread from an oven the winter I was born. *You have her hands,* the woman had whispered, and crossed herself, and never looked at me again.

Now Wendell sat across the guttering candle from me, feeding strips of dried fish to the one-eyed magpie that had adopted him somewhere outside the city walls, and I understood that the time for hiding my hands was over.

"We should talk about the garden," he said quietly.

The magpie cocked its head at me. Even it was waiting.

* * *

I had rehearsed this confession in a hundred shapes on the road — as a weapon, as a plea, as a thing I would never say at all. None of the rehearsals survived the moment. What came out instead was small and flat, like a coin dropped on stone.

"My name is not Ada Vess. That was my mother's surname, and I kept it because she died for it." I turned my palms upward so the candlelight found the crescent scar. "My father is Aldric Renner. The king."

Wendell did not move. The magpie did not move. Somewhere below us, in the flooded cellar of the old dye-house, water dripped with the patience of a thing that had all the time in the world.

"The records I stole," I went on, because if I stopped now I never would begin again, "were not stolen to sell. They were stolen because they are mine. The inheritance ledger. Eleven generations of names, each one a king, each one a vessel. And beside every name, in the archivist's hand, the date the spirit entered them — and the date their minds went dark." I swallowed. "There is one name missing from the ledger. A twelfth line, struck through before the ink dried. *A daughter, born out of wedlock, hidden.* Me."

"You're the heir." His voice was very even. Too even. I had heard him speak to a dying wolf in that same voice, gentle and distant, holding grief at arm's length like a hot pan.

"I'm the heir. The only blood the spirit couldn't finish taking. My mother carried me out of the Spire the night I was born, and the spirit has been hunting us ever since. It found her when I was six." The candle flame bent, though there was no draft. "It wore my father's body into our hiding place. He held her while it watched through his eyes. Then it burned the house, and I ran, and I have been running and stealing and lying for nineteen years, Wendell, and I am so tired."

The magpie made a soft sound, almost human, almost pitying.

"So the archive work," Wendell said. "The contacts. The way you knew which vaults to crack. You weren't a fugitive who happened to be an archivist."

"I was an archivist. That part was true. I served eight years under a false name before I stole the ledger." I laughed, and it came out cracked. "You want to know the worst of it? Everything else I told you was true. The culling feeds the Spire. The spirit devours its wearers. Your gift shares its root — I didn't know that until the wolves told us, but I'd have led you there anyway, because you were always the only one who could end it. I just... neglected to mention that ending it required walking the heir onto the throne dais like bait."

There it was. The word hung between us: *bait.*

Wendell set down the fish. The magpie snatched it and fluttered up to the beam, and then it was just the two of us and the drip of water and twenty-two days of road collapsing between us like a bridge burning behind me.

"You used me."

"Yes."

"You let me walk into the menagerie. You let the king's own summons find me. You knew what waited in that garden and you stood in the carriage and wished me luck."

"I needed you to see him." My voice broke and I hated it. "I needed someone to see him and come back and tell me if there was anything left of my father inside that thing. Because if there wasn't — if the spirit had eaten all of him — then the plan was simple and terrible and I could do it alone. But there *was* something left, Wendell. He said *find my daughter.* He's been saying it for nineteen years through whatever cracks it leaves him, and no one has ever listened, because no one can hear what a hollowed man is really asking." I pressed my mother's hands against my mouth. "You heard him. You're the only person alive who could."

"And if I hadn't? If I'd come back and said the man was empty?" He leaned forward, and the candlelight carved his face into planes and shadow. "What was the plan then, Ada? Or Ada-Vess, or whatever your name is. Was I still useful?"

"I don't know." It was the truth and it was unforgivable. "I think I'd have lied again. I think I'd have told myself the lie was mercy."

He stood. For one wild moment I thought he would strike me, or leave, or both, and I found I deserved either. Instead he walked to the narrow window, where the bells of the capital were ringing the third hour past midnight, and put his hand flat against the cold glass.

"Do you know what the valley animals say about liars?" he asked the window. "Nothing. They don't have a word for it. A creature that deceives is simply broken, or hungry, or afraid. There's no moral category. Just cause." He turned. "So tell me the cause. All of it. Not the version you've been rationing out since the pass. All of it, and then I'll decide whether I'm walking beside you tomorrow or burying you in that cellar."

* * *

So I gave him all of it.

I told him about my mother teaching me cipher by firelight, turning state secrets into children's games. About the night of the fire, and the smell of cedar smoke that still woke me some nights with my heart hammering. About the archive, and the old blind keeper who had suspected what I was and taught me anyway, because he had buried four kings and wanted the fifth line ended. About the ledger's last page, where the founding record described the spirit's origin in a hand so ancient it was nearly picture: *a guardian beast, bound in service, fed on the strength of kings until it forgot how to be anything but hunger.*

And I told him the part I had never said aloud, not even to myself in the dark.

"I don't want the crown, Wendell. I want it *unmade.* Not claimed — unmade. The ledger says the inheritance passes at the moment of coronation, blood to blood, willingly received or not. As long as I live, the spirit has a door. Every year it grows more desperate; every year the culling widens, because it's trying to build itself a body strong enough to survive without the royal line. It needs me the way a drowning man needs air. Which means—" I met his eyes. "Which means the safest thing in the world, for everyone I might ever love, would be for me to die before I reach that dais. And I have not been able to make myself do it. Nineteen years, and I haven't been able to make myself do it. That's the truth underneath everything. I'm a coward wearing my mother's hands."

The silence after that was long. The candle burned down another finger's width. Above us the magpie slept, head under wing, trusting us in the way animals trust — completely, and without any evidence we deserved it.

When Wendell finally spoke, his voice had lost the frost.

"You're not a coward. You're a woman who chose to stay alive long enough to fix what killed her mother. Those aren't the same thing." He came back to the table but did not sit. "I'm furious with you. Do you understand that? I hid in that valley for eleven years believing invisibility was safety, and you were the first person who made me believe in something bigger than hiding, and half of it was built on a lie. That doesn't heal in one night. Maybe it doesn't heal at all."

"But?"

"But the king begged you be found. Through everything that thing has done to him, he's been reaching for you for nineteen years." Something moved in his face — that terrible gentleness he carried for broken creatures, the one that had made me trust him before I trusted anyone. "I can't hate the daughter of a man who asked for her with his last whole thought. And I can't abandon the one person whose death would hand that spirit exactly what it wants."

"Then we're still—"

"We're still going." He sat down heavily, and pushed the last strip of fish toward me like an offering, like a treaty. "But we do it my way now. No more rationed truths. You tell me everything before I need it, not after. Agreed?"

"Agreed." My eyes were burning. I let them. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." He glanced toward the window, and I saw his shoulders change — saw the beast-speaker in him go still and listening, the way he did when the world's quieter voices started talking. "Ada. The birds are wrong tonight."

"What do you mean, wrong?"

"I mean the starlings over the palace quarter aren't sleeping. Ten thousand of them, wheeling in the dark, and they're all saying the same thing." He looked at me, and I felt the floor tilt beneath nineteen years of careful plans. "They're saying the gates of the Spire have opened. And they're saying the king is calling for his daughter — loudly enough that the whole city hears it."

* * *

We were at the window when the bells began.

Not the midnight bells, not the watch bells — a peal I had never heard, deep and arrhythmic, rising from the Spire's crown like a voice clearing its throat after a century of silence. Below us, lights kindled in the streets. Doors opened. And threading through it all, thin and cold, came a sound I recognized with my whole body, the way you recognize your own name spoken by a stranger:

My father's voice, magnified by stone and spirit, rolling across the rooftops of the city he no longer ruled.

"*Bring me my daughter,*" it said, "*and be rewarded beyond the reach of crowns.*"

Beside me, Wendell went rigid. I saw his pupils widen, saw his lips part around words that weren't his — and when he answered, his voice came out doubled, layered, as though something vast were speaking through the shape of him.

"It's offering," he whispered, in a voice that was only half his own. "Ada — it's offering me *everything.* All I have to do is deliver you."
