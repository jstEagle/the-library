## 8. The Hunt Closes

The archive's depths had no windows, and so no way to know whether it was night or morning above. Wendell measured time instead by the rats.

They came to him in the dark between the shelves, whiskers trembling, and he listened to their small reports the way other men read dispatches. *Soldiers at the third gate. They have dogs now — big ones, mastiffs with cropped ears, and the dogs are angry because they are hungry and afraid.* He passed it on to Ada without looking up from the ledger she'd spread across a reading table, its vellum soft as old skin.

"Third gate," he said. "Mastiffs."

Ada's pen did not stop moving. "Then we have until the mastiffs get bored, or until someone remembers the archive has a second entrance, whichever comes first." She wrote quickly, her hand steady, though he had learned to read the tension in her shoulders the way he read animals: not fear exactly, but the coiled readiness of a creature that has decided where it will run before it needs to. "Copy this. The inheritance rite. If we're separated, you need to know it even if I don't survive to teach you."

"Don't say that."

"I'm being practical." She pushed the ledger toward him. "Practicality is how I've stayed alive for six years. Sentiment is what killed my mother."

He copied. His hand was not as steady as hers. Around them the archive breathed — dust and lamp oil and the sweet rot of books no one had opened in a century — and somewhere far above, muffled by ten floors of stone, the bells rang the hour. The city's animals had told him what the bells meant now. Not time. *Counting,* they said. *The Spire counts something. It has been counting since the king stopped coming to the garden.*

* * *

They heard the hunters before the rats could warn them, which was how Wendell knew it was bad. The rats always warned him first. But tonight the warning came from above — boots, many boots, and the particular silence of dogs being held on short leads, straining but forbidden to speak.

Ada snuffed the lamp with two fingers. In the sudden dark she found his wrist and squeezed once. *Run,* the squeeze said. *Split up.* He squeezed back. *No.*

"They'll take one of us either way," she whispered. "Better if it's me. I'm what they want."

"You're what they want," he agreed, "which is why I'm not leaving you alone in the dark with them."

Her breath caught — small, human, unarchival — and then the lantern-light bloomed at the end of the aisle, and there was no more time for whispering.

They came down the shelf-corridor like water finding a crack: six hunters in grey, faces masked, and behind them the mastiffs, huge and miserable, drooling fear onto the stone. Wendell felt their minds like wet wool against his own. He did the only thing he could think of. He spoke to them.

*Not these two,* he said, pouring calm into the thought the way he'd pour water for a thirsty mule. *Not these. Smell the air. Smell the oil. There are others here — can't you smell them? Others who were here an hour ago, whose trail goes out through the west stacks—*

It was a lie, and the dogs knew it was a lie, and for one terrible heartbeat they looked at him with eyes that understood everything — that he was gifted, that he was lying, that men in grey held their leads — and forgave him anyway. One of them whined. Then the handlers dragged them forward, and the moment broke.

"Archivist Voss." The lead hunter's voice was flat as slate. "And the thief Ada. Her Majesty's Spire thanks you for sparing us a search."

"We weren't aware we'd been invited," Ada said, and Wendell heard it — the blade under the courtesy, the woman who had talked her way past a hundred doors. "Though I should tell you the inheritance records are forgeries. All of them. The whole succession is built on—"

The hunter struck her across the mouth with a gauntleted hand. Wendell lunged; two hunters took his arms; a third put a knife against his throat, casual as trimming a wick.

"The Spire doesn't hear lies," the lead hunter said. "Bind them both. The beast-speaker comes too. His Holiness's spirit finds him... interesting."

*Interesting.* The word followed Wendell down into himself like a stone dropped into a well. He was still turning it over when the sound began.

It came up through the floor first — a low hum in the stones, felt in the teeth before the ears. Then the lamps along the walls flickered blue, all together, and the mastiffs began to scream. Not bark. *Scream*, pressing themselves flat against their handlers' legs, tails clamped, making sounds Wendell had never heard a dog make and hoped never to hear again.

"What—" one of the hunters started.

The wall came down.

Not all of it. One section of the ancient shelving burst inward in a storm of splinters and three hundred years of dust, and through the gap poured lamplight and torchlight and the smell of woodsmoke and horses and — Wendell's heart slammed against his ribs — *people*. A dozen of them. Two dozen. Faces he knew from the road, from the burned settlement, from the hidden valley camps: Marta with her burn-scarred hands raised and fire crawling along her knuckles like tame bees; old Sef, the mute tinker, whose gift lived in his fingers and made any lock weep open; the twins Pell and Pem, who moved like wind given bodies; the mule — *his* mule, the one he'd healed on the road, stubborn and gray and utterly unafraid — standing in the breach with its ears forward.

"You didn't think," Marta called, firelight gilding her grin, "we'd let you get culled without us?"

For one shining moment, the archive was war and wonder at once. Sef's fingers danced and the hunters' manacles sprang open like startled birds. The twins became two blurs, and three hunters went down tangled in their own crossbow strings. Marta threw fire, careful fire, fire that burned ropes and weapons and never flesh, because that was Marta's law and she'd taught it to every flame she'd ever lit. And the mastiffs — the mastiffs turned. Wendell would remember that all his life. They turned as one, shook off their leads, and stood beside the outcasts, growling at the men who had starved and beaten them, and Wendell thought: *this is what we are building. Even the dogs know.*

"Go!" Ada shouted, shoving the ledger into his chest. "West stacks, the river door — Sef knows it! Go, all of you, I'll hold the aisle!"

"You will *not*—"

But she was already moving, already talking, her voice rising into the register he'd heard her use on gate-guards and toll-keepers, weaving words like netting — and the lead hunter laughed. Laughed, and reached beneath his cloak, and drew out a rod of black iron banded in white gold, and the Spire's mark burned cold on its end.

Wendell knew that rod. Every animal in the city had described it: the thing the hunters carried into the tenements, the thing after which children were simply gone.

"No!" He tore free of the twins' grip. Too late. The rod touched Ada's shoulder and light like frozen lightning ran through her, and her voice stopped mid-word, and she folded to the stone like a coat slipping off a hook.

The archive went very quiet. Even the fighting paused — outcasts and hunters alike staring at the stillness of her, at the thin thread of blood at the corner of her mouth, at the way the blue lamplight bent toward her body as if drinking.

"She's claimed," the lead hunter said, almost gently. "The Spire takes what it takes. Kill the rest if they resist. Leave the beast-speaker. His Holiness wants him breathing."

What happened next, Wendell did mostly on instinct. Marta's fire roared a corridor wide enough to run through; Sef screamed silently and a supporting arch cracked and dropped half a ton of shelving across the aisle, cutting the hunters off; the twins hauled him bodily toward the river door while he kicked and cursed and called Ada's name into the dust-choked dark. The last thing he saw of her was her hand — reaching, fingers spread, toward him. Not pleading. *Pointing.* Pointing west, toward the door, toward the Spire's opposite tower, toward the throne room at its crown.

*Go there,* the gesture said. *Finish it. Don't waste me.*

* * *

The river door opened onto black water and colder air, and the outcasts piled out onto the mud-slick steps gasping, singed, alive. Behind them the archive burned — Marta's fire had gotten loose in the old paper, and none of them could bring themselves to mourn it much. Above the city, the Spire stood against the stars like a needle through the world's fabric, and its blue light pulsed now, slow and deep, like a heartbeat that had noticed something.

Wendell stood at the water's edge with the ledger clutched to his chest and the mule's warm nose pressed against his neck, and he made himself breathe. In. Out. The way he told frightened animals to breathe.

"They'll take her up the Spire tonight," he said. "The rites need darkness. The rats told me — the inheritance happens at the dark of the moon, and the moon dies tomorrow."

"Then we have a day," Marta said.

"A day to assault the most fortified tower in the world with two dozen outcasts and a mule." Sef couldn't laugh, but his hands laughed for him, quick bitter shapes in the torchlight.

"No." Wendell turned. The river moved behind him, black and patient, and the Spire's pulse reflected in it, blue on black. "A day is what they think we need. They're watching the gates, the roads, the river. They expect grief. Grief makes people stupid — grief makes people *siege*." He touched the ledger. "She pointed. Did anyone else see? She pointed at the Spire. She wasn't saying goodbye. She was telling me where to go."

Pell frowned. "You can't mean to walk in."

"The hunters were ordered to leave me breathing. His Holiness wants me." He said the word *wants* carefully, tasting its shape. "That's not just a threat. That's a door. A door they'll leave open, because they believe I love her too much to be useful anywhere but in chains." He looked around at them — the fire-tender and the tinker, the wind-twins, the gray mule, the dogs that had chosen new masters in a single burning night. "So I won't come in chains. And I won't come alone. But I *will* come through the front gate, tomorrow, when the moon dies — and while every eye in that tower is on the rite, on her, on me — you'll be doing whatever Sef and the twins do best."

Sef's hands went still. Then, slowly, they began to move again, and what they said made the twins grin like wolves.

"And if it's a trap?" Marta asked quietly. "If they take you, and her, and it's done?"

Wendell looked up at the Spire, at the wrongness singing in it that the wolves had named long ago, at the parasite wearing a king like a crown. Somewhere inside it, Ada was waking in the dark, alone, counting hours. Somewhere inside it, a hollowed man might still be reachable, might still remember a daughter, might still be begging strangers in gardens to find her.

"Then it's a trap I walk into with my eyes open," he said. "I spent my whole life hiding in a valley that forgets, believing invisible meant safe. I'm done believing that." He swung the ledger strap across his chest and put his hand on the mule's rough neck. "Tomorrow, at moonset, I give myself to the Spire. And we'll see, come the dark of the moon, whose trap it turns out to be."

Above them, the Spire pulsed. Once. Twice. Like something licking its lips.

Like something that had already begun to count.
