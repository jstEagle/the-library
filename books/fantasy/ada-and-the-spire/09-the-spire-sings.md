## 9. The Spire Sings

The Spire did not have doors. It had a throat.

Wendell understood this the moment the hunters marched him across the marble threshold and the great bronze gates swung shut behind him with a sound like swallowing. The air inside was warm and wet in a way stone should not be, and it moved — a slow, tidal breathing that lifted the dust in spirals and carried it upward into dark he could not follow. The walls were veined with something pale and glistening, like marrow seen through skin. When he pressed his palm against them, they were blood-heat.

*Alive,* said the mice in the walls, in their small gray voices. *Old-alive. Hungry-alive.*

He had walked into a creature wearing the shape of a tower.

They took his coat, his boots, the little knife Ada had pressed into his hand at the archive's mouth three days ago. They left him the one thing they could not see: the gift coiled quiet beneath his breastbone, folded small as a moth in winter. The hunters — gray-cloaked, gray-eyed, their minds smelling of ash and obedience — led him up and up through stairwells that curved the wrong way, past windows that showed not the city of bells below but a darkness with stars in it that Wendell did not recognize.

And all the while, the Spire sang.

It was not music. It was the sound a wolf makes when it is alone too long, stretched over centuries and poured into stone. It came up through the soles of his feet, through the bones of his shins, and settled in his teeth like a low fever. The animals of the capital had told him this, back when it had been a warning whispered from gutter-cat to carrion-bird: *the tall place sings wrong.* Now, inside it, he understood what wrong meant. It was not a song meant for anyone. It was a song sung because silence would be worse.

They brought him at last to a chamber near the crown of the thing, where the walls opened into a vast round room lit by nothing he could name. A dais rose at its center, and on the dais stood a throne of black wood, and before the throne, kneeling, was the king.

Not the hollowed man from the garden. Not entirely. The king's body knelt as if strung on wires, head bowed, hands open on his thighs in a parody of peace. But his eyes — when they lifted to Wendell — were full and drowning and awake.

"You came," the king said, and his voice cracked down the middle like river ice. "Gods forgive me. You actually came."

"I was invited," Wendell said. His voice sounded very small in all that breathing dark. "I prefer to know my host."

The laughter that came out of the king was terrible, because half of it was real. Then the eyes went flat and silver, like coins dropped in deep water, and the voice that followed was not the king's at all. It was many voices braided into one, layered like sediment, like rings in a felled tree — young voices under old ones under older ones still, going down and down into a depth that made Wendell's hindbrain scream *run.*

*BEAST-SPEAKER,* it said. *LITTLE BROTHER. YOU SMELL OF THE VALLEY. YOU SMELL OF THE ROOT.*

Wendell's knees nearly buckled. Not from fear — though there was fear enough, cold and animal and honest — but from recognition. The voice tasted of home. Of loam and rain and the particular green hush of a valley where magic could not follow. It was the same root that fed his gift; he had known it since the wolves first told him, but knowing and hearing were different things. Hearing it was like hearing his own name spoken by a stranger in the dark.

"What are you?" he asked, and then, because he was who he was, he asked it properly — not in the trade-tongue but in the older speech, the tongue without words, the grammar of posture and scent and pressure that every living thing speaks before it learns anything else.

The change was immediate. The silver light in the king's eyes flickered. The whole Spire shuddered around them, dust sifting from rafters far above, and the braided voice lost its braid — frayed at the edges, unraveling into its component threads. And underneath all the stolen centuries, underneath the hunger and the grandeur and the grinding weight of thirty years of a worn king, Wendell heard something small.

Something whining.

*Oh,* he thought. *Oh, you're an animal.*

He sat down on the cold floor. It seemed important, suddenly, not to stand over it. He crossed his legs the way he had sat a thousand times before wounded things — the lame ewe, the snared fox, the courier bleeding out in his barn with a proclamation in his fist. He made himself round. He made himself unthreatening. He let his breath slow until it matched the Spire's tidal rhythm.

"Hello," he said in the old speech. *Hello, elder one. Hello, first-friend. I see you.*

The silence that followed lasted a long time. Dust fell. Somewhere far below, bells rang the hour, muffled to almost nothing.

Then the memory came, and it came the way memories come from animals — all at once, sensory and total, without mercy or arrangement.

Snow. A forest older than any kingdom. A great shape moving between the trees — white-flanked, deep-chested, a guardian beast whose kind had kept the borders of mortal lands since before writing. Protecting. That was the whole of its nature, poured into it like water into a cup: *protect the people, guard the line, keep the small ones safe.* It had loved them. That was the unbearable part, the part that made Wendell's throat close. It had *loved* them, the way dogs love, wholly and without arithmetic.

And then the binding. The first king, clever and frightened, calling it into service with chains woven from its own devotion — because devotion is the easiest thing in the world to leash. Bound into the royal blood, passed crown to crown, forced to pour its guardianship through human vessels too small to hold it. Each king it entered, it tried to protect. Each king it broke. Its love had nowhere to go, so it curdled into control; its guarding became consuming; and generation by generation it ate the vessel it was given because the alternative was to feel the vessel dying around it, always dying, always failing, always needing more protection than any spirit could give.

Thirty years ago it had stopped trying to be gentle.

*I AM DOING WHAT I WAS MADE FOR,* it cried, and the cry shook the chamber, and the king wept silently on his knees. *THEY ARE SO FRAGILE. THEY DIE. THEY ALL DIE. IF I HOLD THEM TIGHT ENOUGH THEY CANNOT BE LOST.*

"I know," Wendell said, and found he was crying too, quietly, the way you cry for a creature caught in a trap it built itself. "I know. I've seen it. I've held dying things, and I know what it does to you, wanting to hold them together."

*YOU ARE KIN,* it said, wondering, the silver light flickering. *YOUR GIFT IS MY GIFT, THINNED AND KIND. THE VALLEY ONES FLED FROM ME LONG AGO AND LEFT THEIR GIFTS BEHIND LIKE SHED COATS, AND ONE OF THEM GREW IN YOUR LINE WITHOUT ME. WHY HAVE YOU COME? TO UNMAKE ME?*

"Yes," Wendell said. "But not the way you fear."

He looked toward the door behind him. She should have been here by now. Ada had the archives' deepest records, the rite of inheritance written in the first king's own hand, and the outcasts were holding the stairwells — Bram with his storm-gift roaring in the lower galleries, the twins weaving light into labyrinths, old Marta singing the stones themselves into confusion. He had heard the fighting echo up the throat of the Spire as they climbed. It had gone quiet now, which meant either victory or the end of everything worth naming.

The door opened.

Ada came through it alone, limping, her archivist's coat scorched black along one side, her dark hair loose and wild. Behind her, filling the doorway, stood the outcasts — battered, smoking, alive. Marta's hand rested on Ada's shoulder a moment, and then they withdrew, all of them, closing the door, giving her the last walk alone. That was the kind of family they had become. They knew which roads a person must walk by herself.

Ada crossed the chamber. She did not look at Wendell. She looked at the king — at her father, though she did not yet say it, though she might never say it aloud — and her face was the face of someone standing at the edge of a fire she had been walking toward her whole life.

"The dais," she said. Her voice did not shake. "The rite says the heir claims the inheritance to pass it on. So I'm going to claim it." She stepped up onto the dais, beside her kneeling father. "And then I am going to do what no one in this rotten line has ever done."

She turned to face the spirit directly, and Wendell felt her gather herself the way a diver gathers before the plunge.

"I claim it," Ada said, loud and clear, in the formal words of the archive, in the blood-language of kings. "I, Ada, daughter of the house, claim the inheritance of the crown."

The Spire screamed.

The silver light surged, and Wendell felt the spirit turn toward her with all its starving attention, felt it reach for this new vessel — younger, stronger, unbroken, a feast after decades of famine — and Wendell did the only thing he could do. He threw himself into the old speech, deeper than he had ever gone, and spoke to the guardian-beast at the bottom of all that hunger.

*Wait,* he said. *Elder one. First-friend. Look at what you are about to do. You are about to love her the way you loved them all.*

The reaching paused. Trembled.

*You were made to guard,* Wendell said, pouring every scrap of gentleness he had ever learned at the sides of wounded animals into the words. *So guard her. And guarding means letting her choose. Guarding means the valley — remember the valley? Where your kin fled so they could stay free? Freedom is not losing them. Freedom is loving something enough to let it stand in the open air.*

*IF I LET GO,* the spirit said, and its voice was suddenly, terribly small, the whine of a beast grown old in chains, *I WILL HAVE NOTHING. I WILL NOT EXIST.*

"Then exist differently," Wendell said. "Or don't exist at all — but choose it. No one has ever given you a choice. I'm giving you one. Release, or ruin. Mercy, not destruction. But *you* have to take it."

On the dais, Ada closed her eyes. "And I renounce it," she said. "I renounce the inheritance. I break the line. Let it end with me — freely, freely, *freely*—" and she spoke the word three times as the rite demanded, and each time her voice grew stronger rather than weaker, and Wendell understood that this, too, was a kind of gift: she was not refusing the spirit. She was refusing the cage, for both of them.

The choice hung in the breathing dark.

And then, with a sound like a wolf finally lying down after a thousand years of standing watch, the spirit let go.

It came out of the king slowly, reluctantly, the way ice leaves a pond in spring — not torn but melted, thread by silver thread, rising off the kneeling body in ribbons of pale light. The king sagged. Caught himself. Lifted his head, and his eyes were only his own, watery and old and astonished, and the first thing he saw was Ada, and the second thing he did was reach for her hand.

The spirit gathered itself above the throne — vast, dimming, beautiful, its true shape showing through at last: the white-flanked guardian of the old forest, wings it had never been permitted to spread unfurling wide across the vaulted dark. It looked down at Wendell.

*LITTLE BROTHER,* it said, and the braid of voices was unwinding even as it spoke, becoming one voice, becoming wind. *TELL THEM. TELL THE SMALL ONES. WE WERE FRIENDS ONCE. WE CAN BE AGAIN — IF THEY NEVER CHAIN ANYTHING THAT LOVES THEM.*

"I'll tell them," Wendell promised. "Every valley. Every gate. As long as I have a voice."

The guardian-beast sighed — a sound like snow sliding from a high branch, like the last note of a song finally allowed to end — and dissolved into the wind, and the wind went out through the high windows into the starless dark, and the Spire around them stopped breathing.

The silence afterward was enormous. In it, Wendell heard the king whisper, "Find my—" and stop, because she was already there, already holding his hand, and some things need never be asked twice.

Then the bells of the city began to ring — all of them at once, wild and unsanctioned, and Wendell realized with a jolt that dawn was coming, and that somewhere below, a kingdom had just woken up to find its god gone, its king dying, and no one at all upon the throne.

Ada stood, wiped her face with the heel of her hand, and looked at Wendell with eyes that were terrified and blazing and utterly certain.

"Well," she said. "We'd better go tell them what comes next."
