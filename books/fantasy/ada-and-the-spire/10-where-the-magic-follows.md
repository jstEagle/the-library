## 10. Where the Magic Follows

The king died at dawn, in the garden, with dirt under his fingernails.

They had carried him down from the Spire on a litter of woven willow, because he had asked — *begged*, in the thin voice of a man newly returned to his own body — to feel the ground one more time before it took him back. The physicians wanted marble and incense and the ceremony of a dying crown. Wendell had knelt beside him instead and asked what he wanted, truly wanted, and the king had looked at his own hands as though they belonged to a stranger, and said: *soil. Something that grows.*

So they laid him beneath the plum trees in the royal garden, where the frost had not yet surrendered, and Wendell spoke quietly to the worms and the beetles and the half-asleep roots until they understood that this man was not to be feared, only held.

He lasted three hours into the morning light. Long enough to see Ada kneel beside him. Long enough to press her hand against his cheek with fingers that shook like winter branches, and say, "You have her eyes. Your mother's. I looked for them every day and told myself I was looking for a ghost." Long enough to whisper, "Free," once, softly, like a word learned in another language. And then he was gone — not devoured, not hollowed, not worn away by the thing that had ridden him for thirty years, but simply finished, the way a song is finished when the last note has been given its full length.

Wendell felt it go. He felt everything go.

The spirit did not die; nothing so old could simply die. But when Ada stood on the throne dais and claimed her inheritance with her whole voice and then renounced it — *I refuse you, I unmake you, the line ends with me and ends gladly* — the great coiled weight of it had loosened, thread by thread, like a knot untied by patient fingers. It had risen through the Spire's broken heart, and Wendell had spoken to it in the tongue of beasts, the tongue it had spoken before men bound it into service and called its guardianship a crown. *You were a shepherd once,* he had said. *Go be wind again.* And it had gone — out through the shattered windows, up past the bells, dissolving into the cold bright air over the city like breath on glass, and for one impossible moment every animal in the capital had lifted its head and made a sound that was almost gratitude.

Now the garden was quiet. The plum trees dripped. Somewhere beyond the wall, the city's bells rang without anyone ordering them to — people ringing them themselves, wildly, badly, out of joy or grief or both, and no one stopping them.

Ada stood over the fresh-turned earth with her hood down, letting the city see the face of the woman who would not be their queen.

"They'll ask again," Wendell said. "The council lords. The crowds. They'll ask until someone says yes."

"Then they can keep asking." She crouched and pressed her palm flat against the soil, the way she pressed her palm against old documents, reading what was written there. "My mother died so I wouldn't inherit a cage. My father died so I wouldn't inherit a monster. I'm not going to honor either of them by building myself a nicer one." She looked up at him, and her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "A council. Gifted and ungifted both. Beastspeakers and archivists and fire-hands and plain farmers, all of them with the same vote. That's the kingdom I'll give them, if they want it. If they don't—" she shrugged, and something young and tired moved behind her face, "—then let them find a better idea. I've been an heir my whole life without knowing it. I'd like to try being a citizen."

The mule — Bramble, who had died on a roadside in chapter and chapter ago and now breathed and stamped and complained about oats with the vigor of the genuinely resurrected — nudged Ada's shoulder with a nose like warm velvet. She laughed, wet and startled, and buried her face in his neck.

Wendell turned toward the wall, where the others waited. Marta of the burned settlement, whose gift was finding lost things, who had found them in the archive dark and led the outcasts up through the Spire's throat. Old Sef with his weather-hands, still gray from the fighting. The twins who could walk through walls and had walked through several that day, laughing the whole way, though one of them hadn't stopped shaking since. His found family, gathered in a royal garden like sparrows in a palace, waiting to see what he would do.

He knew what he would do. He had known since the spirit dissolved, since the moment the last thread of the inheritance snapped somewhere deep in his blood and he felt, for the first time in his life, entirely alone inside his own skin — and discovered that alone was not the same as empty.

"I'm going home," he said.

* * *

The valley had changed. Or rather, the valley had stopped pretending.

The protection that had wrapped it for generations — the old magic, the same root as the spirit's, the same root as his, woven into the hills by some ancestor's bargain — was gone. Wendell felt its absence the way a man feels a healed bone on a cold morning: not pain, exactly, but memory. The mist that had always curled at the passes, turning travelers gently around, was simply mist now. The road in was a road in. Anyone could come.

And anyone had. When they crested the last ridge in the grey light of late afternoon, Wendell saw smoke rising from the valley floor — too many fires, wrong fires — and his heart dropped like a stone into a well.

But the animals met him before fear could finish its work. A fox first, streaking upslope with her tail low, then a scatter of crows, then old Hesta the ewe, who pushed her blunt head against his knee and flooded him with images: strangers, yes, many strangers, but no fire set to homes, no beasts slaughtered, no chains. Strangers carrying timber. Strangers digging post holes along the riverbank. Strangers being fed soup by Wendell's terrified, astonished neighbors.

"They heard," Ada said quietly beside him. "Word travels fast when a kingdom changes hands. People are coming, Wendell. The hunted ones. They heard there's a valley where a beast-speaker lives, and they're coming the way pilgrims come."

"They're coming because the mist is gone," Wendell said. "Because hiding doesn't work anymore."

"Yes," Ada said. "That too. Both things can be true."

He walked down into the valley with his family at his back and his gift open wide for the first time in his life — no shielding, no shrinking, no careful silence. Every creature within a mile turned toward him. Dogs howled welcome from doorways. Hawks circled twice overhead and screamed what might have been his name. And the people — his neighbors, the miller and the weaver and old Tomas who had sold him lamp oil for ten years without ever quite meeting his eyes — came out of their houses and stood in the lane and watched him pass, and one by one, hesitantly, they began to clap.

It was the clapping that undid him. Not the Spire, not the spirit, not the terrible bright mercy of the throne room. Ten years of invisibility, and it ended with flour-dusted hands applauding in a muddy lane.

Marta put her hand between his shoulders. "Breathe," she said, in the tone of someone who had also once been seen for the first time.

* * *

They built through the autumn.

Not a fortress — Wendell refused that, over Ada's professional objections, over Sef's grumbling, over the twins' disappointment. A school, or the beginning of one. A long hall of river stone and timber where gifted children could learn what their gifts were without learning to be afraid of them. A pasture where Bramble taught frightened horses that a human's touch could mean healing. A library, because Ada insisted, and because Ada's insistence had toppled a dynasty, so no one argued. Shelves of stolen records returned, catalogued, opened to any reader. The truth of the Spire, written plainly, so that no one could ever again wear a lie like a crown.

The valley filled. Families arrived with children who lit candles with a glance or spoke to crows or dreamed true dreams, and the valley's original families stayed — not all of them, not all gladly, but enough, and the ones who left left with fair prices and open letters, which was more than kingdoms usually managed.

On the first hard frost of winter, Wendell climbed the ridge above the hall alone, as he had climbed it alone a hundred times in his years of hiding. Below him, lamplight bloomed in the windows. Smoke rose straight and unhurried from the chimneys. Somewhere in the hall, Ada was arguing with Sef about shelving, and the twins were teaching Marta's daughter to skip stones through a wall, and Bramble was stealing bread again, and every one of those small sounds reached him clearly, because the foxes and the owls carried gossip the way rivers carry water.

He sat down in the frost-bright grass and listened.

The valley's magic was gone. He checked, the way he always checked, reaching out along the old channels of his blood — and found nothing there but himself. No inherited weight. No borrowed power. Only his own small gift, his own chosen life, the inheritance remade as a thing he carried rather than a thing that carried him. The wind came down off the mountain and moved through the bare trees, and it sounded, just barely, just for a moment, like something vast and old breathing easily at last.

"You were a shepherd once," he said to it. "Go well."

The wind moved on. Down in the valley, a child laughed, and a dog answered, and the lights burned on.

Wendell Voss stayed on the ridge until the stars came out, keeping watch over nothing, guarding no one, needing to hide from no one — a free man in a valley that forgot how to forget, listening to the world begin.
