## 1. The Ledger of Tonight

The counting-house of the Guild of Impossible Things smelled of tallow and old rain — the old rain being literal, for a jar of it had been traded in 1841 and had never quite stopped weeping through its wax seal. Marguerite Amberdale had long since stopped minding. He had stopped minding most things, which was, he suspected, the entire point of exile.

He sat at the third desk from the window, where the candlelight came slantwise through panes warped by a century of weather that had happened indoors. Around him rose the shelves: ledger upon ledger upon ledger, their spines lettered in the guild's careful hand. *Thunder, Bottled — Inbound.* *Years, Borrowed — Outstanding.* *Names, Sold Back to Their Owners (Partial).* The Guild of Impossible Things did not deal in impossibility, whatever the brass plate on the door claimed. It dealt in the accounting of it. Someone had to keep track of what could not otherwise be counted, and that someone, tonight as every night for six years, was Marguerite.

He dipped his pen. The nib caught the light like a small black beak.

"Bottled thunder," he murmured, reading the entry he'd been avoiding since supper. "Crate of nine, consigned by the Weathermongers' Compact of Ilbreth. One jar arrived empty."

That was the trouble with thunder. It did not like captivity. A jar would sit obediently on a shelf for decades, growling faintly when the moon was wrong, and then one evening you would come down with your inventory book and find nothing inside but a smell like the moment before lightning. The guild's solution was elegant and slightly shameful: they wrote the missing thunder down anyway, and charged the Compact for storage of an absence.

Marguerite entered it. *Nine received. Eight present. One escaped into atmosphere, whereabouts unknown, value unchanged per Article Twelve.* His handwriting was small and exact, each numeral a little fortress. Six years of ledgers had taught him that precision was a kind of hiding place. If your letters were perfect, no one looked behind them.

The candle guttered. Somewhere above him, in the guildmaster's tower, a floorboard creaked the way it always creaked at half past eleven, when old Mistress Vael made her nightly circuit and pretended not to check on her ledger-keeper. He kept his head bowed until the creaking passed. There were perhaps forty people in the world who knew Marguerite Amberdale had ever existed, and he preferred to keep the number exactly there.

* * *

The work went on. Borrowed years next — a widow in the Low Quarter had sold back eleven of hers to pay a debt, and the years had to be logged individually, because years were contrary stock. They spoiled if lumped together. Marguerite gave each its own line: *one year of learning to whistle; one year of a first snow seen through a sickroom window; one year of marriage, mostly good.* The guild required descriptions so the years could be resold honestly. He had written thousands of these. He sometimes thought he had written more of other people's lives than he had lived of his own.

His own life fit comfortably in a single column. Exile, dated. Name surrendered, dated. Storm-gift sealed under oath, dated. Three lines, all in red ink, all final. He did not look at them often. Tonight he did not look at them at all, because looking at them made the scar along his left forearm ache — the scar shaped, if he was honest with himself, like a fork of lightning, though he told everyone who noticed it (no one) that it was a kitchen burn.

It was nearly midnight when he finished the thunder and the years and turned, out of habit rather than duty, to the shelf at the back of the room. The habit was called *closing the circle*, and it belonged to the desk itself, not to any rulebook. Every ledger-keeper before him had done it, he'd been told. At the end of the night you consulted the Almanac of Dates, confirmed that tomorrow was real, and initialed the confirmation. Superstition dressed as procedure. Marguerite performed it the way he performed all his rituals: thoroughly, and without belief.

The Almanac of Dates was the oldest book in the counting-house, older than the guild's charter, older than the building. It was bound in grey leather gone soft as moth-wing, and its pages were not paper but something thinner and colder, like skin that had been very patient for a very long time. No one knew who had written it. The entries began a hundred years ago and continued forward — that was the impossible part, the thing the guild had built its whole strange house around — continuing forward year by year, date by date, in a hand that never varied, describing what each day would *be*.

Not prophecy, precisely. Prophecy shouted. The Almanac only noted, in the tone of a clerk recording rainfall. *14th of the Reed Moon: fog on the river; a debt repaid in the wrong coin.* *15th: two brothers quarrel over a chair.* Small things. The guild had learned to trust the small things absolutely, and to ignore the large ones, because the large ones were embarrassing.

Marguerite turned to tomorrow's page.

Then stopped.

Tonight's date was already written. That was correct — that was how the book worked; the entry appeared at dawn of the day it described, or so the theory ran, though no one had ever actually watched it happen. But the entry for *tonight*, the twenty-ninth of the Reed Moon, was longer than usual. Longer than any entry he had read in six years of nightly consultations. And it was not about fog, or debts, or quarreling brothers.

*29th of the Reed Moon,* the book said, in that patient, clerkish hand. *A ledger-keeper finds this page.*

Marguerite set down his pen very carefully, as though it might go off.

He read it again. *A ledger-keeper finds this page.* Nothing else. No fog, no river, no small trustworthy thing. Just that, and beneath it — he leaned close enough that his breath dimmed the cold page — a second line, in smaller script, crowded against the bottom margin as if it had been waiting years for room:

*He will wonder about the handwriting.*

He wondered about the handwriting.

Because here was the thing that lifted the hair on his arms and set the jar of old rain weeping faster on its shelf: the hand was not the Almanac's hand. The Almanac's hand was upright, unhurried, anonymous as water. This hand — both lines, the finding and the wondering — was *his*. Not similar. His. The little fortress numerals. The way his lowercase *e* leaned eastward. The tiny hesitation-stroke he made before every capital letter, a flaw he had never trained out of himself because no one had ever cared enough to correct it, because no one read his writing except ledgers and dead men.

Marguerite stood up. His chair scraped, and the sound seemed enormous in the candlelit hush, and somewhere in the dark stacks a bottle of something answered with a low, uneasy chime.

"This is a joke," he said aloud, to the shelves. The shelves, being guild property, declined to confirm or deny.

There were explanations. Of course there were. He was a ledger-keeper; explanation was his trade. Someone had copied his hand — apprentices did such things, though the guild had had no apprentice in nine years. Someone had forged the page — but the page was old, the cold skin-pulp of it yellowed at the edges exactly like its neighbors, and ink did not yellow backward. Or the simplest explanation, the one that sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone: the book was a hundred years old, and a hundred years ago someone had known that on the twenty-ninth of the Reed Moon, a ledger-keeper would stand at this desk at this hour and find this page, and that ledger-keeper wrote the note himself, in advance, in the only hand he trusted.

His own.

"Tomorrow," Marguerite said, and his voice came out steadier than he felt, which was another kind of hiding place. "What does it say about tomorrow?"

He turned the page.

The entry for the thirtieth of the Reed Moon was short. Shorter than fog-and-debts short. It said: *She arrives.*

And the page after that — the thirty-first — was blank. Not unwritten-blank. *Erased*-blank, scraped smooth, the way the guild scraped pages when an account was closed and its contents forbidden. He had seen that particular smoothness ten thousand times. He knew it the way a jailer knows the sound of a lock.

His heart was doing something unprofessional. He pressed his palm flat against the desk until it remembered its manners.

Beyond the counting-house windows, Vhalen lay quiet under a sky the color of unpolished pewter. Lamplight in the Low Quarter. Smoke rising straight up from a thousand chimneys, because there was no wind, because there was never any wind in Vhalen anymore, not since — 

Not since. He reached for the end of the thought and found the familiar smoothness there too, the scraped-blank feeling he had learned, over six years, not to press on. Some accounts were closed. Some contents were forbidden. He was very good at not pressing.

But the Almanac had pressed. The Almanac, a century old, patient as skin, had waited all this time to say *a ledger-keeper finds this page* — and then, on the very next day, *she arrives*, and then nothing at all. Books of dates did not simply stop. Even the guild's charter acknowledged that: the Almanac ran forward without end, that was its nature, that was why the guild kept it locked in a counting-house instead of selling it, because a book that knew every tomorrow was worth more than every vault in Vhalen combined.

Unless the tomorrows had run out.

Marguerite flipped forward. The thirty-first: scraped blank. The first of the Salt Moon: blank. The second: blank. Page after page after page, all the way to the back cover, all the way to the grey leather soft as moth-wing — dozens of days, months, a whole unwritten year, erased in that same smooth, deliberate, forbidden way. He turned them faster. The pages whispered against his fingers like sleet. Blank, blank, blank — and then, at the very end, wedged against the inside cover as if it had slipped there and been forgotten, a single loose leaf in a different hand entirely. Older. Angrier. The letters pressed hard enough to tear.

*All entries end in the country that is not there.*

Marguerite read it four times. The candle burned lower. On its shelf, the jar of old rain had gone perfectly still, as if it too were listening.

"The country that isn't," he said slowly. "There's no such—"

And there the sentence died, snagged on that same smoothness, that same scraped-blank place in him where a thought should have been and wasn't. He stood in the candlelight with his mouth open around the shape of a word he could not finish, and understood, with the particular horror of a man who keeps accounts for a living, that something had been subtracted from him. Neatly. Without a remainder. By professionals.

Mistress Vael's floorboard creaked overhead — earlier than usual, or later, he couldn't have said; time had gone strange and syrupy. Marguerite closed the Almanac. His hands were steady. He noted that they were steady, and entered the observation nowhere, because some things must never be written down.

He should report it. That was procedure. An anomaly in the Almanac outranked bottled thunder, outranked borrowed years, outranked everything; the guildmaster would want to know within the hour. He got as far as the foot of the stairs before he stopped, one hand on the banister, listening to the silence above.

*A ledger-keeper finds this page. He will wonder about the handwriting.*

If he reported it, the book would go up to the tower, and he would never see it again, and whatever the next page said — *she arrives* — would arrive to a guild that already knew everything and told him nothing, the way it had for six years. Whereas if he—

If he simply forgot to mention it tonight. If he balanced the circle himself. If a ledger-keeper, whose entire art was making absences look intentional, chose to keep one small absence of his own—

The floorboard creaked again, closer now, directly above the stairwell. Marguerite took his hand off the banister, walked back to his desk with the unhurried tread of a man with nothing in his pockets but routine, and snuffed his candle.

In the dark, the Almanac of Dates sat shut on its shelf, holding its one warm secret against its cold grey cover. And outside, far off across the rooftops of Vhalen, past the river, past the walls, in the direction Marguerite could never look without that scraped-blank ache behind his eyes — thunder rolled. Faint. Distant. Wrong.

There was no storm forecast anywhere in the sky.

But there was, he now realized, an empty jar on the inbound shelf, and Article Twelve said its value was unchanged, and somewhere out in the dark a piece of escaped weather was coming home — or something wearing thunder's voice was coming to collect a debt the guild had written down and hoped everyone would forget.

Marguerite stood in the dark of the counting-house and listened to it come, and did not move, and did not sleep, and wondered — helplessly, professionally, in his own unmistakable hand — about tomorrow.
