## 2. A Girl with Thorns in Her Hair

The counting-house smelled of tallow and old rain, which was itself an impossibility, since no rain had fallen in Vhalen's quarter of the city for eleven days. Marguerite had learned not to trust his nose. It remembered things the rest of him had been made to forget — petrichor on hot stone, the green breath of a storm still leagues out at sea, the copper tang that came before lightning split a mast. His nose remembered a country he could not name.

He set the thought down the way he set down all such thoughts: carefully, in its proper column, and moved on.

The ledger before him was tonight's work. *One bottle of thunder, small, uncorked by accident in the cellar of the House of Nine Sighs; contents dispersed among forty sleepers, who will wake tomorrow believing they have dreamed of their fathers.* He entered it in a fair, narrow hand. Beside it: *Three years borrowed from a miller of Lowmarsh, repayment deferred to his grandson, interest payable in first memories.* The guild did not deal in gold. Gold was honest. Gold merely ruined you. The Guild of Impossible Things dealt in everything else.

His quill scratched. The candles leaned in the draft from the high windows, and their shadows swung across shelves of ledgers rising into the dark like the ribs of some drowned cathedral. Somewhere above, the guildmaster's bell chimed the ninth hour, though Marguerite had privately concluded long ago that the bell chimed whatever hour it pleased and let the clocks catch up afterward.

It was while blotting the entry about the thunder that he saw the book.

It sat where no book should have been — third shelf, between the Registry of Borrowed Names and a slim volume bound in fish-skin that catalogued doors (the guild owned four; only two admitted to it). He knew every book on that shelf. He had dusted that shelf for six years. The book had never been there.

It was old. That was the first thing. Old the way stone is old, old the way grief is old — not worn but *settled*, as if it had been in place so long that the shelf had grown around it. Its cover was grey leather gone soft as moth-wing, unmarked by title or sigil. When he lifted it, it weighed what a sleeping bird weighs, which is to say almost nothing and far too much.

*Prophecies,* said the spine, in letters that were less written than weathered.

Marguerite told himself he would only look. Curiosity, in a ledger-keeper, was a kind of theft, and theft in the guild was punished by becoming inventory — he had seen a man once who had pocketed a bottled year and now lived in a jar on the second floor, dreaming in brine. But his hands opened the book anyway, the way hands open when they remember something the mind has surrendered.

The pages were dense with entries. Dates. Each date followed by a line or two of prophecy in a hand that started formal and grew, over decades, looser, faster, more certain of itself. He read at random:

*The 14th of Emberfall: a ferryman will refuse payment from a woman with two shadows. She will tip him anyway, in a coin that has not been minted yet.*

*The 3rd of Deepwinter: the clocktower forgets noon. Noon does not return until someone weeps for it.*

Small things. Domestic impossibilities. A century of them, each dated, each precise as a debt.

And then his eye caught the date at the top of the page he had fallen open to, and the counting-house went very quiet around him, quiet the way a held breath is quiet.

Tonight's date.

Not similar. Not close. *Exact* — the 22nd of Ashfall, in the year the guild reckoned by the death of its founder, a numbering used nowhere else in the world. And beneath it, in that same loosening hand:

*A girl arrives carrying tomorrow. Her arms are a hedge. Do not ask her name twice.*

Marguerite read it three times. Then a fourth, because the fourth time he noticed the handwriting.

He knew that handwriting.

He saw it every day. It curved up from the page in fair, narrow strokes; it crossed its sevens; it made its *r*s with a small impatient flick, as if the letter had somewhere better to be. It was the hand in which he had just recorded a bottle of thunder. It was his own.

But the ink was a century old. The ink had dried before his grandfather's grandfather had learned to hold a quill. He turned the page with fingers that did not feel entirely his own, and the next entry was tomorrow's date, and the next, day upon day upon day, marching forward through weeks he had not yet lived — and then, further on, the dates stopped being days he recognized at all. They ran past the edge of any calendar the guild kept. And every entry after a certain point — he counted, and recounted, because surely he had misread — every entry after a certain point ended in the same phrase, repeated like a refrain, like a tolling bell:

*a country that no longer exists. a country that no longer exists. a country that no longer exists.*

The candle nearest him guttered, though there was no draft.

"Ledger-keeper."

Marguerite closed the book too fast. It made no sound, which was worse than a slam. The under-steward, Pellam, stood at the end of the aisle, a lantern in one hand and a manifest in the other, his face the color of porridge.

"There's — " Pellam stopped. Swallowed. Started again, as though the words needed coaxing out of a difficult crate. "There's a girl at the door."

"It's near midnight," Marguerite said. "The guild receives no one after the ninth bell."

"I know what the rules are." Pellam's lantern shook. "I've copied the rules out nine times for saying so. But she won't leave, and she says—" He glanced over his shoulder at the dark of the stairwell, and lowered his voice to something barely above the candleflame. "She says she knows what happens here tomorrow. All of it. She says she's already seen it. She says—" His throat worked. "She says she remembers us, master Amberdale. Remembers you. From a time that hasn't happened."

The book lay shut under Marguerite's palm. Through the leather he felt it — or imagined he felt it — warm as a held coin.

"Where is she now?"

"The threshold. She won't cross without being asked. Says she knows better than anyone what happens to things that enter where they're not invited." Pellam wet his lips. "Master Amberdale, her arms—"

"I know," Marguerite heard himself say. "Her arms are a hedge."

Pellam stared at him. "How could you possibly—"

"Fetch the guildmaster," Marguerite said, and stood, and took the prophecy book with him, because leaving it behind suddenly seemed more dangerous than any rule he might be breaking.

* * *

The front door of the Guild of Impossible Things was deliberately plain. It was oak, uncarved, iron-hinged, the sort of door belonging to a tax office or a maker of coffins, and this was policy: the impossible enters best through the ordinary, wearing its shoes wiped clean. Beyond it, the night of Ashfall pressed against the city — coal-smoke and river-damp, the far-off argument of gulls, the gaslamps hissing their yellow vigil along the Embankment.

The girl stood exactly on the threshold, one foot on the guild's flagstone and one on the street, as though she understood that the line between inside and outside was a border requiring papers.

She was perhaps seventeen, perhaps younger; hunger and road-dust made ages unreliable. She wore a coat too big for her, patched at the elbows with thread that didn't match, and her hair — dark, rain-frizzed despite the dry night, escaping its braid in every direction — was threaded with thorns.

That was the second thing Marguerite noticed, after the strangeness of her stillness. Thorns. Actual thorns, pale as new horn, growing out of her scalp the way barberry grows out of a wall, woven through the black hair so that at first glance they might be ornaments, bone pins, some foreign fashion. They were not ornaments. Where they emerged from her skin, the skin was raised and reddened, old scarring layered over newer breaks, and Marguerite — who had spent six years cataloguing the impossible and had therefore lost the ability to flinch — felt his stomach tighten all the same.

Her arms were worse. Sleeves rolled to the elbow, she stood with her forearms bare to the cold, and they were a hedge entire: thorned branches running from wrist to inner elbow like the veins of a leaf, some grey and dormant, some green and viciously alive, and here and there — he would swear to this later, before witnesses, before Inquisitors — here and there, small white blossoms, open, breathing.

"You're the ledger-keeper," she said.

Her voice was roughened by roads but steady. Steadier than Pellam's, steadier than Marguerite's own pulse.

"I'm the ledger-keeper," he agreed.

"You balance accounts for things that shouldn't exist." Not a question. "Bottled thunder. Borrowed years. Last month you took delivery of a widow's grief, traded to her by her late husband through a medium in Saltrow, and you filed it under *miscellaneous sorrow, domestic*, because the category *grief, conjugal, posthumous* wasn't created until the following week." She tilted her head. "You'll create it Thursday. You were very annoyed about the filing."

The candles in the entry hall bent toward her, all together, like grass under wind.

Marguerite looked at her for a long moment. Behind him he heard Pellam's lantern tremble; ahead of him, at the top of the stairs, the darkness gathered itself into the shape of the guildmaster, descending with the unhurried silence of a woman who had made an art of arriving exactly when she was inevitable.

"Ask me my name," the girl said to Marguerite. Only to him. "Go on. Ask me twice."

"Why twice?"

"Because the first time I'll lie."

Something in the way she said it — flat, unashamed, a fact reported like weather — unlocked a door in him that had been locked six years. He knew that tone. He had used that tone, once, across a table in a bright room, while men in grey robes wrote down everything he confessed to and nothing he was. There is a particular loneliness in being a thing that must be handled with instructions attached. He recognized it the way you recognize your own handwriting.

"Then I'll ask once," he said, "and wait."

The girl blinked. For the first time, the stillness cracked, and underneath it she looked — briefly, terribly — young. Tired. Far from anywhere.

"That's not how it goes," she said quietly. "In the version I remember, you ask twice. You always ask twice."

"And in the version you remember," Marguerite said, "what do I do after?"

"You turn me away." Her eyes went to the prophecy-shaped weight under his arm, though she could not have seen the book's spine from where she stood. "Every time. You turn me away, and it goes worse."

Above them, the guildmaster reached the bottom of the stairs. She was small and grey and neat as a folded letter, and her eyes were the color of ledger-paper, and when she spoke it was in the voice of a woman reading aloud terms already agreed.

"She's not a guest," the guildmaster said. "Guests cost us hospitality, and hospitality is a liability. She's cargo. Received, weighed, stored, and accounted for." She extended one hand, palm up, toward the girl. "Manifest?"

The girl hesitated. Then, slowly, she drew something from inside her oversized coat: a scrap of paper, soft with handling, and laid it on the guildmaster's palm.

The guildmaster read it. Whatever crossed her face crossed it quickly and was gone, the way a fish crosses a pond.

"Received," she said. "One girl, self-delivered, provenance disputed. Contents: tomorrow." She folded the paper away into her sleeve. "Ledger-keeper. Enter her."

Marguerite's quill-hand itched. "Under what classification, mistress?"

The guildmaster was already turning back up the stairs. Over her shoulder, in the tone of a woman dictating a line she had read long ago and merely waited for the world to catch up to:

"*Arrivals, prophesied, unscheduled.*"

And Marguerite, opening the ledger to a fresh page, writing the date at its head in his own fair, narrow hand — the hand that was also, impossibly, a century old — understood with cold and perfect clarity that the girl on the threshold had stepped across it at last, and that the book upstairs had known she would, and that somewhere in its century-deep pages, in handwriting like his own, the account of what happened next had already been balanced.

The girl paused beside him as Pellam showed her in. Close, he could smell the thorns — green sap and iron, and beneath it something like the air before a storm.

"For what it's worth," she murmured, not looking at him, "in none of the versions do you believe me this fast."

"I don't believe you at all," Marguerite said.

"Good." Almost a smile. "That's how it starts."

And high overhead, faint through the floors, the guildmaster's bell began to chime — and struck ten, though it was not yet half past nine, and no one moved to correct it.
