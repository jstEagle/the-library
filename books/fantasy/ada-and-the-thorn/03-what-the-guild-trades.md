## 3. What the Guild Trades

The guildmaster's key was made of a sound.

That was the first thing Ada understood about the Guild of Impossible Things: nothing here was what it pretended to be, and everything pretended beautifully. The key the guildmaster pressed into her palm was warm as a held breath, and when she closed her fingers around it she heard — faintly, from somewhere inside the metal — the creak of a door opening in an empty house. The guildmaster called it *the note of entry*. She said every lock in the counting-house answered to one sound or another, and that Ada should learn hers quickly, because down in the vaults, hesitation could be expensive.

"Expensive how?" Ada had asked.

The guildmaster had smiled with all the warmth of a ledger line. "You'll see."

So now Ada followed her down, past the counting-house where Marguerite Amberdale bent over his columns by candlelight, pretending very hard not to watch her go. She had felt his eyes on her back all morning — careful eyes, guilty eyes, the eyes of a man reading a book he already knew the ending of. She had decided, in the way one decides which stones to step on crossing a river, that she would let him keep his secret a little longer. Everyone in this building was carrying something. She could hear it, almost, under the floorboards: a low murmur like a hive, like a thousand small voices saying *remember, remember* to no one at all.

The stairs went down farther than the building stood tall. That was the second thing she understood.

"Mind the third landing," the guildmaster said, lifting her skirts with the ease of long practice. "It remembers being a different staircase and occasionally behaves accordingly."

Ada stepped over the landing. For half a heartbeat her boot sank into something soft and green — moss, and beneath the moss the smell of rain on stone, a smell she knew the way you know a word you've never spoken aloud — and then the stair remembered itself and was only wood again.

Her thorns stirred. They did that now, more and more since she'd arrived at the guild's door two nights ago, soaked and starving and insisting, to anyone who would listen, that tomorrow the sky would fall upward in the market square and no one would believe her until it did. The thorns grew along her forearms in pale spirals, fine as wire, and they had opinions. They bloomed when she lied — small white flowers opening along her skin like accusations — and they ached, deep and dull, whenever she came near a truth too large for her body to hold.

She was aching now.

"The vaults," the guildmaster announced, unlocking a door that was painted onto the wall so convincingly that Ada's hand passed through the paint before the wood appeared beneath it, "are where we keep the things the world has misplaced. Or mislaid. Or, in some regrettable cases, *disowned*."

* * *

The first vault smelled of other people's childhoods.

Ada didn't know how else to describe it. It was a sweetness like cut grass and woodsmoke and someone's mother calling from a doorway, and it made her eyes sting before she'd seen a single thing. Shelves rose into darkness on every side, and on the shelves sat jars — thousands of them, tens of thousands — each one glowing faintly with a light that moved, that *lived*, that pressed against the glass like a moth.

"Memories," the guildmaster said, with the brisk pride of a woman showing off a wine cellar. "Withdrawn voluntarily, I assure you. People come to us when remembering costs more than forgetting. A widow who cannot bear her wedding day. A soldier who keeps hearing his own orders. We store them safely. Some are reclaimed. Most are not." She lifted a jar and turned it so the light inside swirled. "This one is a first snowfall, witnessed by a girl of six, in a country whose name I am not permitted to say."

"Why not permitted?"

"Because the name is also in storage." The guildmaster set the jar down with a click that echoed far longer than it should have. "Next vault."

Ada walked between the shelves slowly. In one jar, a lullaby hummed to itself in a language she almost knew. In another, something laughed — a child's laugh, bright and sudden — and she caught herself smiling at it before she caught herself grieving. Her thorns had begun to prickle, a fine heat crawling up her arms, and she pulled her sleeves down over them out of habit.

At the end of the row, one shelf stood empty except for a single jar turned face-down, its label scraped away, its glow leaking out around the edges like light under a door.

"What's that one?"

"That one," the guildmaster said, "is none of your business, and if you are wise, it will stay that way." But her voice had changed — flattened, careful, the voice of a woman stepping around a hole in the floor she'd known about for years. And Ada saw, with the strange certainty that lived in her thorns rather than her mind, that the guildmaster was afraid of the empty shelf. Not of the jar. Of the *space* around it, as though the space itself might remember something.

* * *

The second vault held borders.

They stood on a gallery above a vast dark hall, and below them lay countries — actual countries, folded like cloth. Ada watched a river unroll itself across a stretch of floor and then fold again, patient as laundry. Mountain ranges hung from hooks like coats. A coastline breathed slowly in a corner, tide coming in and going out against nothing at all.

"When a treaty fails," the guildmaster said, "or a war ends badly, or a king decides a people are inconvenient, the border does not simply move. Borders are agreements, and agreements can be *withdrawn*. We hold the withdrawn ones. Safely. Neutrally." She gestured at the folded shapes below. "Somewhere down there is a border that used to enclose a great deal of orchard country. Apple orchards, mostly. Bell towers. A road that ran beside a river the color of hammered tin."

Ada's mouth had gone dry. "Which one?"

"I couldn't say. They aren't labeled by name anymore. Only by weight." The guildmaster smiled again, and this time the smile did not reach anywhere near her eyes. "You'd be surprised how much a country weighs, once you take it out of the world."

The ache in Ada's arms had become a burning. She pressed her palms flat against the gallery rail and made herself breathe. Below, among the folded territories, one shape was smaller than the rest and darker, and it seemed to her — though the gallery was dim and her eyes were not to be trusted — that the dark shape was shaped like grief. Like a wound in the shape of a place. Like something she had walked through once, in a life she had never lived, with bells ringing and apples falling and a storm coming up the valley like the end of the world.

"You're staring," the guildmaster observed.

"I'm looking," Ada said.

"A distinction without a difference, most days." The guildmaster turned away. "Come. There is one more thing to show you, and then we must discuss your cargo status. You understand, of course, that the guild cannot harbor you as a guest. Guests have rights. Cargo has *value*. I trust you find the arrangement acceptable."

Ada found it unacceptable, and said nothing, and hated herself a little for saying nothing, and noticed her thorns staying quiet — because it wasn't a lie, exactly, to keep silent. Silence, she was learning, was the one country where her thorns had no jurisdiction.

* * *

The third vault had no name on its door.

Every other door in the vaults bore a small brass plate: MEMORIES. BORDERS. YEARS (BORROWED). WEATHER (ASSORTED). NAMES, RETIRED. This door was bare wood, old and pale, worn smooth around the handle as though a thousand hands had touched it and a thousand hands had thought better.

"And this?" Ada asked.

"This," said the guildmaster, "is the reason the guild exists."

She did not open it. She stood before it with her head slightly bowed, the way people stand before altars, or before the doors of rooms where relatives are dying, and Ada understood that whatever was behind the unlabeled door, the guildmaster did not control it. The guildmaster *served* it. The realization settled over Ada cold and complete, like water finding the seams of a coat.

"It doesn't open for me," the guildmaster said quietly. "It hasn't opened for anyone in this building for a hundred years. But it's been restless lately. Creaking at night. Warm to the touch. As though it's waiting for something." She looked at Ada then, really looked, and her gaze snagged on Ada's sleeves, on the pale spirals visible through the fabric, and lingered there a moment too long. "As though it's waiting for *someone*."

"I wouldn't know anything about that," Ada said.

The lie bloomed.

She felt it happen — the fine heat along her forearms, the tight unfurling, and then the sting, sharper than she had ever felt it, wet warmth sliding down her wrists. She looked down. Three white flowers had opened along her left arm, and from their centers seeped thin lines of red, welling up and running down to drip from her fingertips onto the vault floor, where each drop hissed softly, like water on a hot stove.

The guildmaster stared. Then, slowly, she took out a handkerchief — clean, pressed, monogrammed with a letter Ada didn't recognize — and offered it without a word.

"Thorns," the guildmaster said at last. "Living ones. I've read of such things. In books older than this building." She tilted her head. "You told me you were cargo. Fleeing a debt in the eastern provinces. Ordinary trouble."

"I did."

"And just now you told me you knew nothing about the door." The guildmaster's voice was gentle, which was worse than if it had been cruel. "And your arm bled for it. How curious, that your body punishes you for kindnesses."

"It doesn't punish me for kindnesses." Ada pressed the handkerchief to her arm and felt the flowers close, reluctantly, like fists unclenching in sleep. "It punishes me for lying."

"Mm." The guildmaster studied her a moment longer, and Ada watched her file the moment away in whatever vast interior ledger she kept — watched her write it down in ink that would not fade. "Well. Cargo that bleeds truth is still cargo. Perhaps more valuable than most. Come along. We're done for today."

But as they climbed back up through the murmuring dark, past the folded rivers and the humming jars, Ada looked back once at the unlabeled door, and she would have sworn — though swearing was a dangerous business for a girl with her particular affliction — that the door was warmer than the wall around it. Warmer, and waiting, and patient in the way of things that have waited a hundred years and can wait a hundred more.

* * *

Marguerite was waiting for her in the counting-house, ostensibly reconciling a column of borrowed years against a shipment of bottled thunder, actually watching the stairwell door with the taut attention of a man listening for hoofbeats.

"You're bleeding," he said, before she'd fully cleared the doorway.

"It's nothing."

He crossed the room in four strides and took her wrist — gently, the way one handles a bird — and turned back her sleeve, and Ada watched his face as he saw the three white blooms and the red lines beneath them. Something moved through his expression that she recognized because she had seen it in mirrors on her worst nights: recognition, and fear, and underneath both, guilt wearing the mask of concern.

"They bloom when you lie," he said quietly. "Don't they."

"Everyone knows that about me within a day. You're getting faster."

"Ada." He lowered his voice further, glancing at the clerks at the far tables, at the candles guttering in their drafts. "What did she show you?"

"Jars of other people's childhoods. Countries folded up like winter blankets. A door with no name that makes the guildmaster bow." Ada pulled her arm free, not unkindly, and wrapped the bloodied handkerchief around it. "Why? What did *you* expect her to show me?"

For a moment he said nothing. The candle between them fluttered, steadied. Somewhere overhead, the guild's roof creaked like a ship, and beneath the floorboards the murmur went on — *remember, remember* — patient as rain.

"I expected her to show you nothing," Marguerite said finally. "I expected her to keep you in the guest quarters and lose the key." He rubbed his eyes, and when his hands came down he looked older, and tired, and honest in a way that cost him visibly. "There are things in those vaults that shouldn't be in jars. There are things in this building that I—" He stopped. Started again. "If anyone asks you about me, Ada. If anyone asks you what I do at night, or what I was before I came here, or whether I've ever mentioned a country—"

"Which country?" Ada asked, very softly.

His face did something complicated and closed, like a book snapping shut. "No country. Forget it."

And here was the moment. Here was the fork in the stair, the stone in the river. Because Ada knew — had known since the prophecy book, since the date written in handwriting like his own, since the look on his face when she'd said *tomorrow the sky falls upward* and he had gone white as paper instead of laughing — that he was hiding a country. That the word *Vhalen* lived somewhere behind his teeth the way her thorns lived under her skin, and that speaking it would cost him something terrible.

The guildmaster would ask. Ada was certain of it now; she had seen the hunger in the woman's eyes, the ledger-keeper's instinct for entries that didn't balance. Sooner or later, someone in this building would ask Ada Thorn what the exiled man whispered in his sleep, and her body would answer for her unless she learned to lie better than her own blood.

So she practiced. Right there, in the candlelight, with Marguerite watching her.

"If anyone asks," she said clearly, "I'll tell them you're a ledger-keeper who works too many hours and talks to his columns. I'll tell them you're boring. I'll tell them—" and here she smiled, and it hurt, and the thorns stayed quiet because for once she meant every word down to the root, "—I'll tell them you're worth protecting. Whatever you did. Whoever you were."

Marguerite stared at her. "You don't even know what you're offering to cover."

"No," Ada agreed. "But my arm does. It's been aching since I walked into this building, and it ached worst down on that empty shelf, and it ached worst of all when you flinched at the word *country*." She held up her bandaged forearm between them, the small red stain already spreading through the guildmaster's pressed linen. "My thorns know things before I do. And right now they're telling me that you and I are standing in the same story, and that the story is going to get much worse before it gets better, and that the only thing I can choose tonight is whose side I bleed for."

The candle flame leaned. Outside, far off, thunder rolled across a sky that had promised clear weather all week — a low, wrong-sounding roll, like a drum played underwater, like weather remembering an old argument.

Marguerite opened his mouth to answer.

And that was when the bell above the guild's front door rang — a single hard note, silver and cold, nothing like the warm creak of the guildmaster's key — and every clerk in the counting-house went still as a held breath, and the guildmaster appeared at the top of the stair with her face drained of all its ledger-line composure, and said, in a voice that tried very hard to be calm:

"Gentlemen and cargo — we have an Inquisitor at our door. His warrant is signed Corvane. And he is asking, by name, for the keeper of dates."
