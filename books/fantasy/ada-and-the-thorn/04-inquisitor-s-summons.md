## 4. Inquisitor's Summons

The bell above the counting-house door had a voice like a cracked coin, and Marguerite had spent three years learning to ignore it. It rang for clerks with ledgers to file, for merchants trading in bottled thunder and borrowed years, for the small gray traffic of a guild that made its living on things that should not exist. It did not ring for Inquisitors. Inquisitors did not knock.

So when the bell spoke at half past the ninth candle, and the room's warmth drained away as though someone had opened a window onto winter, Marguerite knew before he looked up.

He kept his eyes on his columns. That was the first discipline of the counting-house: *the numbers do not flinch, and neither do you.* Quill moving, ink black as a held breath, he finished the entry he was making — three hours of someone's childhood, sold at market rate, buyer anonymous — and only then raised his head.

The man in the doorway wore the dove-gray of the Inquisition, unadorned, without braid or sigil, because the Inquisition had learned long ago that fear needed no decoration. He was neither tall nor short, neither old nor young, a face built to be forgotten by anyone who saw it, which was itself a kind of signature. His boots were dry. Outside, the rain had been falling since dawn, the ordinary downward kind that Vhalen still permitted itself, and yet not one drop had touched him. The air around him simply declined to be weathered.

"Marguerite Amberdale," the man said. Not a question. A reading aloud, like an entry in a ledger.

"We're closed to unscheduled trade," Marguerite said. "You'll want the front office. Hours are posted."

"I am Corvane." The name landed in the room like a stone dropped into still water; Marguerite felt it ripple through him, touching something old and drowned. "I have a warrant."

Inquisitors always said it that way — *I have* a warrant, never *I carry* one — as if the paper were not an instrument of law but a thing they had grown, the way a tree grows fruit.

Behind Marguerite, in the shadowed alcove where the prophecy book lay open beneath a cloth, tonight's date sat written in ink a century old, in handwriting like his own. He did not look at it. Looking would be confession.

"Then you'll want the guildmaster," he said. "I keep accounts. I don't keep the guild."

"No." Corvane stepped inside and closed the door behind him with a click soft as a moth settling. "You keep dates."

* *

The counting-house of the Guild of Impossible Things occupied what had once been a chapel, back when Vhalen had believed in chapels. The vaulting overhead still arched like cupped hands; the windows were glass now instead of saints, but the light came through them greenish and forgiving, the way light does through water. Shelves rose along every wall, crammed with the guild's inventory: jars of thunder with their corks waxed blue, stoppered hourglasses labeled in faded script (*borrowed, return by spring*), boxes of borders — actual borders, cut from maps and folded flat, humming faintly when two of them touched. And everywhere, everywhere, ledgers. Thousands of them. The guild wrote everything down. It was the guild's single act of faith: that a thing recorded could not wholly die.

Corvane walked the central aisle slowly, and Marguerite watched the inventory react to him. Jars dimmed. An hourglass's sand stopped mid-fall, grains hanging like a swarm of golden flies. Even the borders went quiet, their hum flattening to nothing, as if the shelves themselves were holding their breath.

"You've been here three years," Corvane said. His voice was mild, unhurried, the voice of a man reading a menu. "Ledger-keeper, second class. Punctual. Unremarkable. You balance accounts that cannot balance, and you do it well enough that no one asks how a storm-caller learns bookkeeping."

Marguerite's quill stopped. He set it down with exaggerated care, aligning it with the desk's edge, because his hand wanted to shake and he refused to let it.

"My exile is a matter of record," he said. "Stripped of the gift, struck from the rolls. I'm no more a storm-caller than this desk is."

"The gift can be stripped. The calling cannot. It's in how you listen." Corvane turned from the shelves. "When thunder comes over the hills at night, you count the seconds between the flash and the sound. Don't you. Measuring its distance. Old habit."

Marguerite said nothing. It was true, and they both knew the silence would confirm it faster than any word.

Corvane produced the warrant then, and this was the moment — Marguerite would remember it afterward, in the terrible clarity that horror keeps for itself — this was the moment the dread became specific. Because the warrant was written in a fine slanted hand, letters leaning forward like runners at a starting line, the *d*'s looped high, the crossbars struck through with a single impatient flourish.

It was the handwriting from the prophecy book.

His own handwriting, or near enough that his own mother — if he had ever had a mother, if he had ever been allowed to remember having one — could not have sworn the difference.

"The ledger of dates," Corvane said. "A book of prophecies, century-old binding, vellum of Vhalen make. It came into this guild's keeping. I am instructed to recover it. You will tell me where it is."

"And if I don't know?"

"Then you'll tell me that instead, and we'll both pretend to believe it, and I'll search anyway." Corvane laid the warrant on the desk, squaring its corners with the same precision Marguerite used on his quills. "I'd rather not search. Searching is slow, and slow is cruel, and despite my reputation I dislike cruelty that serves no purpose."

*And despite your reputation,* Marguerite thought, *you are standing in a room full of impossible things and none of them will look at you.*

"The guildmaster keeps the seals," he said aloud. "Anything in the vaults is hers to release, not mine."

"This isn't in the vaults." Corvane's eyes — gray, forgettable, patient — moved past Marguerite's shoulder, toward the alcove, and stopped. Not on the book. On the space where the book was, the way a man looks at a wall and knows there is a hollow behind it. "This is in a room where a clerk sits alone at night with a candle and a conscience. Where else would a book of dates be kept but with the man who balances time for a living?"

The candle between them guttered, though there was no draft.

* *

Panic, Marguerite discovered, was not a storm. He had expected panic to feel like his old gift — vast, howling, lifting rooftops. Instead it was small and precise, a needle sliding under the skin of the moment, and everything narrowed to the width of its point: the alcove, the cloth, the book beneath it, the twelve steps between his desk and that shelf, and the gray man standing four steps closer.

Think. The guild taught its clerks one prayer above all others: *a thing recorded cannot wholly die, and a thing hidden in the record cannot be found.* There were ways. There were ways, because the guild hid things constantly — from tax assessors, from rival houses, from kings who wanted their purchased years back.

There was the false spine trick, too crude; Corvane would read the shelf like scripture. There was the folding, tucking the book into folded space between two moments, but that required the guildmaster's seal and she was three floors up and summoning her would bring her into this room, into this conversation, and Marguerite had seen the guildmaster lie and had liked her better for how well she did it, but he did not want to learn what she would trade to an Inquisitor to keep the guild safe.

Which left the third way. The ugly way. The way he had sworn, three years ago, weeping over his exile-oath, never to touch again.

Memories could be sold. Everyone knew that; it was half the guild's trade. What fewer people knew was that memories could also be *written into* — that a broker with skill and cruelty enough could fold a foreign object into the architecture of a recollection, so that the thing existed nowhere in the world except inside a person's past. The memory's owner carried it without knowing, the way a body carries a swallowed key. And the owner could not retrieve it, because retrieval required remembering that there was anything to retrieve.

Three years ago, broke and bitter and wanting to feel less, Marguerite had sold Sef the memory of a single afternoon: a hillside outside a city whose name he could no longer say, white blossoms, someone laughing, wind coming up the slope like a promise. It had been the gentlest thing he owned. He had sold it precisely because it was gentle, because he could not bear to keep it and could not bear to lose anything harsher.

Sef owed him a favor. Sef owed everyone favors; it was how brokers stayed alive.

Twelve steps. Four of them belonged to Corvane.

"I'll need a moment to consult the index," Marguerite said, rising. His voice came out level. He was almost proud of it. "Our holdings are catalogued by—"

"Sit down, clerk."

Marguerite sat. The candle flame stood perfectly upright, as if presenting arms.

Corvane came around the desk. Up close he smelled of nothing at all, which was somehow worse than incense or ash; a man should smell of something. He lifted the cloth from the alcove shelf with two fingers, and beneath it the prophecy book lay open to tonight's date, and Marguerite watched the Inquisitor read his own doom in a dead century's ink.

For a long moment Corvane did not move. Then he said, quietly, almost to himself:

"It's the same hand."

"You recognize it?" Marguerite asked, before wisdom could catch the words and pull them back.

"I would." Corvane straightened. Something passed across his forgettable face — not expression exactly, more the place where an expression had been removed and the wall behind it not yet plastered. "I've been reading it for eleven years. Every warrant I've ever served was written in it. Every order, every seal, every sentence of judgment handed down from the Chancery above. I used to think it was the Grand Inquisitor's hand. Then I thought it was the crown's. Now I find it in a book of prophecies older than either." He looked at Marguerite, and for one unguarded instant the gray man seemed almost young, almost lost. "Do you know what it is to spend your life obeying a handwriting?"

"No," Marguerite said honestly. "But I know what it is to spend three years balancing accounts in a country that's forgetting how to stay real. We're both employed by something larger than ourselves, Inquisitor. Perhaps we should compare employers sometime."

Something flickered — amusement? warning? — and was gone. Corvane replaced the cloth over the book with a gentleness that frightened Marguerite more than violence would have. Men who handled dangerous things gently had handled them many times before.

"The book stays where it is," Corvane said. "For now. I am not authorized to seize it tonight. I am authorized to confirm its location, which I have done, and to inform you—" he paused, and the pause had weight, had grain, had texture, "—that the Chancery has taken an interest in the entries after tomorrow's date."

"There are no entries after tomorrow's date," Marguerite said. "The book ends."

"The book ends in a country that no longer exists." Corvane buttoned his gray coat with unhurried fingers. "Or perhaps, clerk, the country ends where the book ends, and no one has thought to ask which came first. Consider that. Consider whether you want to be holding the pen's property when the pen comes back for it."

He walked to the door. The bell did not ring for him either; the door simply opened and admitted the night, and the rain beyond it fell in ordinary, obedient lines.

At the threshold he stopped, without turning.

"One more thing. The girl with thorns in her hair — she arrived at your guild two nights ago. Cargo, according to the manifest. She is not cargo. Whatever she told you about tomorrow, whatever she remembers that hasn't happened—" his voice dropped, and Marguerite heard, beneath the Inquisitor's iron calm, something that sounded horribly like pity, "—believe her. I have read the final page of that book, Mr. Amberdale. My name is on it."

The door closed. The bell hung silent, cheated of even that.

* *

Marguerite sat alone in the counting-house while the candles burned down around him, and the needle of panic worked deeper, and then — slowly, the way frost becomes resolve when the sun refuses to rise — it changed.

They would come back. Tonight had been confirmation, not seizure; Corvane had said so himself, and Inquisitors, whatever else they were, did not waste words. Tomorrow, or the day after, men in dove-gray would return with authorization and take the book, and read the entries after tomorrow, and follow the handwriting wherever it led — into the guild, into the vaults, into the unlabeled places Marguerite had spent three years not asking about.

Unless the book was somewhere no warrant could reach.

He took the prophecy book from the alcove and held it a long time, feeling its century-old weight, its vellum gone soft as skin. *Tonight's date,* written a hundred years ago. *Tomorrow's date.* And after that, page after page, a country dissolving off the edge of its own record.

Then he wrapped his cloak around his shoulders, put out the candles one by one, and went out into the rain-wet streets of Vhalen to find a memory broker named Sef — to sell, or to beg, or to bury a book inside the last gentle afternoon he had ever owned.

The rain followed him down, faithful and blind, and did not know — yet — that soon it would be asked to fall upward.
