## 5. The Country That Isn't

Ada had learned the guild's rhythms in three nights: the way the counting-house candles guttered at the same hour each evening as though the building itself exhaled; the way the clerks moved through the vaults with their eyes down, never looking at what they shelved; the way Marguerite Amberdale's pen scratched against ledger paper like a small animal trying to dig its way out of a cage.

She had not learned how to sleep here. The guild did not permit it, exactly. Dreams were inventory too — she had seen them racked in jars on the third sublevel, each one labeled in a hand so fine it might have been spider-silk — and so the beds in the dormitory were shallow and narrow, built for bodies that were only passing through.

On the fourth night she gave up on sleep entirely and went down to the vaults.

She told herself it was curiosity. She was cargo now, accepted into the guild's keeping the way one accepts a crate of knives — carefully, with gloves, with an eye always on where the sharp parts pointed. The guildmaster had said as much over the signing of her intake slip: *We will house you, girl, but understand that everything under this roof is either owned or owed, and you are presently neither.* Ada had signed anyway. A person with nowhere to be does not haggle.

The vaults answered to no lock she could find. They answered instead to wanting — this was the trick of the place, she was beginning to understand. You walked the counting-house stairs down past the level of ledgers, past the level of bottled weather, past the level where years were kept in long blue vials like preserved eels, and the stairs simply went on as long as you wanted something badly enough, and stopped when you didn't.

Ada wanted answers. So the stairs went down a very long way.

* * *

The thorns woke before she understood why.

They lived in her arms — had lived there as long as she could remember, which was itself a strange phrase, because her memory did not behave like other people's. It arrived in pieces, whole and vivid, of things that had not happened yet. A market square full of upward-falling rain. A door made of unmaking. A man with storm-grey eyes standing in a country that burned without fire. And when the memories pressed close, the thorns pressed close too, blooming through her sleeves in pale white flowers that smelled of rain on hot stone.

Now they bloomed all at once, both arms, dozens of blossoms opening like small gasps.

She stopped on a stair that had no business existing — the walls around her were old stone, older than the guild's brickwork, carved with letters in a script that hurt slightly to read — and looked down at her own forearms glowing faintly in the dark.

"Not yet," she whispered to them, the way one whispers to a dog. "Not here."

But the thorns were never wrong. When they bloomed, it meant a memory was near — one of hers, one of the futures folded up inside her skin like letters in a drawer. And there was something near her now, in the dark below the guild, that knew her.

She went down.

* * *

The room at the bottom was round, and empty, and wrong.

Wrong first because it was lit by no candle and yet she could see everything clearly, in the flat grey light of an overcast noon that existed nowhere in the night above. Wrong second because the walls were covered in maps — hundreds of them, layered like feathers, nailed up over one another until the stone beneath had vanished entirely. Wrong third, and worst, because every map showed the same country, and the country had no name.

She knew that was impossible. Maps named things. That was most of what maps *did*. But these — she stepped closer, thorns whispering against her sleeves — these showed coastline and rivers, mountain ranges drawn in confident ink, cities marked with little crowns and cathedrals, roads stitched from border to border like veins in a leaf. A whole country, complete, beloved, detailed by someone who had walked every mile of it.

And where the name should have been, in the cartouche at the bottom of each map, there was only a smooth blank space, as if someone had taken a knife and pared the name away — not crossed out, not blotted, but *removed*, the parchment itself healed over the wound.

Except the newest map, hanging alone on the far wall, apart from the others.

That one still had its name. Faint. Fading even as she watched, the letters going grey and then pale and then nothing, like breath fading off glass:

**VHALEN**

The thorns screamed.

Not in pain — in recognition. Every future-memory she carried lit up at once, and for one unbearable moment Ada stood in all of them simultaneously: rain falling upward over a market square, citizens pausing mid-word with their mouths still open; a bell tower ringing a name over and over because no one could remember whose name it was; a road that surfaced only beneath the feet of someone remembered; and above it all, always, a sky the color of a bruise, and lightning answering a man's raised hand —

— and the man had Marguerite's face.

"No," Ada said aloud, to the empty room, to the maps, to herself. "No, he's a ledger-keeper. He balances accounts. He counts other people's thunder."

But the memory would not be argued with. It unfolded in her the way her futures always did, patient and total, and she saw it now with terrible clarity: a city of white towers on a river bend. A prison. Rebels in the cells below, rebels who had helped her once — no, not her, someone else, someone she had been or would be or remembered being — and soldiers coming for them at dawn, and a man on the tower top calling down the storm because it was the only thing left, because the alternative was watching them die.

And the storm had come. Oh, how it had come. Not the polite thunder of the guild's bottled shelves but a storm with intent, a storm that tore the white towers down and drowned the garrison and cracked the river open — and then kept going. Because he had called it and could not uncall it. Because a storm-caller's gift, once given its head, takes miles to rein in, and the crown's answer had already been moving by then: not armies, not reprisal, but something quieter and far more thorough. Scholars in grey robes with a contract in their hands. A guild that traded in impossible things, agreeing to perform the most impossible one of all.

*Erase it,* the crown had said. *Not destroy it. Erase it. Unwrite Vhalen from every map, every mind, every mouth. Let there have never been such a country.*

And the guild — needing the storm-caller's guilt, his grief, his *name*, as fuel for the working — had taken those too. Had written him into exile inside his own erasure. Had let him keep his life and his pen and nothing else.

The memory closed like a book. Ada found herself on her knees on the cold floor, thorns bleeding — they bled when she lied, and apparently they bled also when the truth was too large — thin lines of red running down her forearms and dripping onto the newest map, onto the fading name of Vhalen.

Where her blood fell, the letters darkened. Held. Came back for a moment, sharp and black: **VHALEN**.

Then faded again.

"Oh," Ada said softly. "Oh, you poor country."

She understood, sitting there in the grey light with her bleeding arms, why the thorns had brought her down. She was not only a girl with impossible memories. She was — the thought arrived whole, the way her futures did — she was what happened when a country refused completely to be forgotten. Some last scrap of Vhalen, some final unkillable remembering, had gone into flesh rather than accept the blank space on the map. Her thorns were the country's insistence. Her futures were its memory of what was about to happen to it, held the way a dying woman holds her children's faces.

And the man upstairs, balancing impossible accounts with his careful pen — the man whose handwriting matched a century-old prophecy — was the reason.

* * *

She found him at dawn in the counting-house, which surprised her; she had half-expected to need to search. He sat at his desk with the green-shaded lamp burning low, the great ledger open before him, and he was not writing. He was staring at his own right hand as though it belonged to a stranger.

"You're up early," he said, without looking around. He always knew where she was; she had stopped finding it uncanny and started finding it companionable. "Or late. Down here the two get confused."

"Marguerite." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "What was Vhalen?"

The pen stopped.

It was a small thing, the stopping of a pen. But in the silence after, Ada heard the whole building hold its breath — the candles, the coals in the grate, the bottled thunder on its shelves two floors up, all of it listening.

"I don't know that word," Marguerite said.

"Yes, you do." She came forward into the lamplight and turned her arms over so he could see the thorn-blooms, the blood. "Look at me. Look at what I am. I've been having memories since before I can remember having them — futures, places, people. And last night my thorns took me down to a room under the vaults where there are maps of a country with no name, and the name is still dying on the newest one, and the name is *Vhalen*, and I know — I know the way I know tomorrow's weather — that you are the man who called the storm that gave the crown its excuse."

He rose slowly from his chair. In the lamplight his face did something complicated — confusion first, genuine and total, and then a flicker of something underneath, some animal instinct that recognized danger before the mind caught up, and then confusion again, papered over the top like fresh plaster over a crack.

"Ada," he said gently, and the gentleness was worse than anger would have been. "You came here remembering things that haven't happened. I accepted that. I *defended* you to the guildmaster. But this—"

"Is true."

"—this is a story with me in it, and stories with us in them are the hardest ones to see straight." He set the pen down with exaggerated care, the way a man sets down a knife during an argument. "I'm a ledger-keeper. I balance accounts. Bottled thunder, borrowed years, the occasional smuggled dream. Before the guild I was—" He stopped. His brow furrowed, and Ada watched him reach for his own past and find the shelf empty. "Before the guild I was..."

"Go on," she said quietly.

"I was exiled," he said, and the words came out flat, recited, a fact worn smooth from handling. "By the Inquisitors. For — for crimes I don't—"

He stopped again. This time the silence had a different texture. Ada watched his eyes move, quick and helpless, searching a room in his mind that had been swept clean, and she felt the terrible tenderness rise in her throat like sickness. This was the cruelty of it. He had not hidden his crime. He did not *have* his crime. Someone had taken it out of him the way you take a tooth, and left the ache.

"Sit down," Ada said.

"I'd rather—"

"Sit down, Marguerite." She pulled the second chair around to face him, close, knee to knee, the way you sit with the dying. "Because I'm going to tell you what you did, and you're going to want to stand up and leave, and I need you to hear all of it. My thorns bleed when I lie. Look at them now." The blood had dried in dark threads down her wrists. "Every word I say to you tonight is true, and I am so sorry for every one of them."

He sat.

"There was a country called Vhalen," she began. "White towers on a river bend. Bells that rang the hours in harmony instead of sequence, so the whole city sounded like one chord. Orchards that fruited twice a year. A king who was mostly harmless and a parliament that was mostly not. It had songs. It had a word for the smell of rain on limestone that doesn't exist in any other language. And eleven years ago — or it will be eleven years ago, I can't always tell which direction my memories run — a man stood on the tallest tower and called a storm to save forty rebels in the cells below."

"Ada—"

"And the storm saved them," she went on, relentless, gentle, awful. "And then it took the garrison, and the bridge, and the eastern quarter, and it would have taken the whole city if the man hadn't collapsed trying to pull it back. And the crown looked at what one man could do and decided no country that produced him should be permitted to continue. Not conquered. *Erased.* And the Guild of Impossible Things sold them the means, and the price the guild charged was the storm-caller himself — his name, his past, his crime, everything except his hands and his pen. They wrote him into exile and called it mercy."

The lamp flame leaned. Somewhere above them a bottle of thunder shifted on its shelf with a sound like a held breath.

Marguerite had gone very still. Not the stillness of disbelief — Ada had expected disbelief, had prepared for it — but the stillness of a man standing in a doorway while something enormous passes on the other side, feeling the wind of it, unable to see it.

"If that were true," he said at last, and his voice was careful, stripped, "then there would be — evidence. Records. A warrant. Something."

"The warrant exists. You'll see it soon — an Inquisitor named Corvane carries it, and it's written in your handwriting, which will make more sense later and less sense before that." She reached out and took his hand. It was cold. "The records exist too. They're in the room under the vaults, on the walls, with the name cut out of every one. That's what erasure *is*, Marguerite. There's nothing left to show you. That's the whole horror of it. The country isn't destroyed. It's *unwritten*. And everyone who ever knew it forgot it, including — especially — the man who broke it."

"Then how do you know?" The question came out raw. "If everyone forgot, if even I forgot — how does a girl with thorns in her arms remember?"

And there it was: the question she had hoped he wouldn't ask, because the answer was the last thing she had left to give and she hadn't decided whether he deserved it. She looked at his face — storm-grey eyes, the careful pen-callus, the hollow where a life used to be — and the tenderness won, the way it always won, the way it was already costing her.

"Because somebody has to," she said. "Because a country that's unwritten can't mourn itself. Because the erasure wasn't perfect — it never is — and what was left over went somewhere. Into flesh. Into thorns. Into a girl who remembers a future she never lived, in a country that's currently forgetting itself street by street, with nine days left on the clock." She tightened her grip on his cold hand. "I'm what's left of Vhalen, Marguerite. And I am telling you that you did this. And I am telling you that I watched you do it for the best reasons in the world. And I am telling you — this is the part that matters — that the guild didn't just erase a country. They wrote a prophecy while they were at it. A century ago. In your handwriting. And tonight's date is in it, and every entry after tonight ends in a country that no longer exists."

For a long moment Marguerite Amberdale said nothing at all. The lamp burned. The thorns on her arms trembled like leaves.

Then he withdrew his hand — not cruelly, but completely — and stood, and walked to the window, and looked out at the sleeping city, and Ada saw his shoulders change. Saw the ledger-keeper's careful posture break along some old fault line and re-form into something else. Something that had been waiting eleven years, or would wait eleven years, for exactly this weight to be set upon it.

"I believe you," he said quietly, to the window. "That's the worst part. I believe every word, and I remember none of it, and I believe you anyway." He turned. His eyes had the flat bright look of weather about to happen. "There's a sealed ledger in the guildmaster's office. I've walked past it for six years and the oath in my blood walks me past it too. Tonight I'm going to break a small law and read it."

"And if it tells you what I told you?"

"Then," Marguerite said, and for the first time since she had known him his voice carried, faintly, distantly, the sound of a storm still caged but awake, "then the guild and I are going to have a conversation about the difference between keeping accounts and writing them."

Above them, on its shelf, a bottle of thunder rolled over in its sleep.
