## 6. Storm Without Weather

The counting-house had never felt so much like a cage.

Marguerite sat at his desk long after the candles had burned down to their collars of wax, the prophecy book open before him, Ada's words still ringing in the hollow places of his skull. *You called it down. You called it down and it took everything.* He had not answered her. He had not been able to answer her, because when he reached inside himself for the truth — for the memory she claimed to carry like a splinter of glass — there was nothing there but smooth, polished absence.

So he did what ledger-keepers do. He reached for numbers.

The guild's accounts were always strange, but tonight they were stranger than strange; they were wrong in a way that made his teeth ache. The column of bottled thunder showed a surplus where there should have been a deficit. The borrowed years — three hundred and eleven of them, lent at interest to a duchess who wished to outlive her enemies — had been repaid early, which never happened, because no one who borrows years ever wants the lending to stop. And in the margin of the thunder page, in ink so fresh it still gleamed, someone had written: *Balance pending. Vhalen account remains open.*

Vhalen.

He said the word aloud just to hear what it sounded like in his own mouth, and it sounded like nothing. Like a coin worn blank. Like a name spoken in a language he had once known and deliberately drowned.

*You called it down.*

He closed his eyes and reached for the storm.

It was an old gesture, older than his exile, older than the oath. Somewhere behind his sternum there had always been a pressure, a gathering, a dark green weight of cloud waiting on his word. As a boy in the hill country he had summoned rain for farmers' fields and been paid in bread and gratitude. As a young man he had learned that weather could be a weapon, though the learning had cost — he could not remember what the learning had cost, and that absence too now seemed suddenly suspicious, a door painted over rather than bricked up.

He reached.

And found the cage.

It was not pain, exactly. It was the absence of pain where pain should be — the way a severed hand does not hurt but *itches*, phantom and furious. His gift was still there. He could feel its shape, vast and slow-turning, a cathedral of pressure somewhere below the floor of himself. But between him and it stood the oath, and the oath was not a wall. Walls can be climbed. It was a law, written into the marrow of him by his own voice, sworn in a room whose walls he could no longer picture, before judges whose faces had been sanded out of his memory like figures from a fresco left in the rain.

*I renounce the calling. I renounce the sky's obedience. I renounce—*

Renounce what? The third clause slid away from him every time, greased and slippery, and every time it slipped he felt something small and essential go with it, like water through fingers.

"Damn you," he whispered — to whom, he wasn't sure. To the oath. To the judges. To himself, most likely, because whoever had stood in that room swearing away the sky had been Marguerite Amberdale, and Marguerite Amberdale had done it willingly enough to make the binding hold.

The candle guttered. In its light the prophecy book lay open, and he looked at it again with new eyes — or with old ones, with the eyes of a man who has just discovered the floor of his house is hollow.

Tonight's date, inscribed a century ago. Every entry after it ending in a country that no longer existed. And the handwriting — he had told himself it merely resembled his own, the way all trained hands resemble one another after enough years of ledgers. But looking now, truly looking, he saw the tell he'd been avoiding: the way the descender of the *g* hooked left instead of right. His father had scolded him for it when he was nine years old. No forger would have thought to copy a flaw so small. No one but Marguerite Amberdale wrote that *g*.

Which meant either the book was a forgery made by someone who knew his hand intimately —

— or he had written it.

A century ago. Before he was born. Which was impossible, except that Ada remembered tomorrow, and the guild traded in borrowed years, and somewhere in this building was a door with no label, and impossibility, Marguerite reflected grimly, was the guild's entire stock-in-trade. He worked for the merchants of the impossible. He should have stopped flinching at it years ago.

*Balance pending. Vhalen account remains open.*

There was only one person in the Guild of Impossible Things who kept a private ledger. Only one set of books sealed under wax and law, kept in the master's study on the third floor, behind a lock that answered to blood-oath as well as key. Guildmaster Ysolt's ledger. The one book Marguerite had sworn — along with every soul who signed the guild rolls — never to read.

He had signed that oath eleven years ago, in this same room, in this same chair. He could not remember signing it. That fact, which had once seemed merely the ordinary fog of exile, now sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

Ada's face rose in his mind — the thorns in her hair moving like something alive, because they were; the terrible gentleness with which she had told him his own crime. She had wept while she accused him. He had seen it. She had bled from her arms while she spoke, thorns blooming red-petaled along her scars, and she had not stopped speaking, because whatever she was, she was honest the way wounds are honest.

She deserved better than a man who hid behind arithmetic.

Marguerite stood, took the candle, and went up the stairs he had spent eleven years not thinking about.

* * *

Ysolt's study smelled of cedar and cold iron. The guildmaster herself was absent — gone since dusk to some meeting Marguerite was not important enough to be told about, though lately he suspected he was far more important than anyone wanted him to know. The corridor outside her door was dark, and the dark here had texture, the particular velvety darkness of a place warded against casual eyes. Marguerite's candle shrank in it, a bead of gold trembling on a black sea.

The lock was brass, old, shaped like a coiled serpent swallowing its own tail. Beside it, in letters worn nearly smooth, the guild law he had agreed to without reading closely, because everyone agreed to it, because refusing meant the street and the street meant the Inquisitors:

*What the master keeps, the master keeps alone.*

His hand shook. He noticed it with detached interest, the way he noticed errors in columns — there is the discrepancy, there is the tremor, both shall be recorded. Eleven years of obedience sat on his shoulder like a tame bird, whispering that whatever was in that ledger, Ysolt had earned the right to keep it, that she had sheltered him when the world would not, that a man with no past owed his keeper everything.

But the tame bird had been fed on lies, hadn't it? Sheltered by the institution that — if Ada was right, if the margins were right — had held the pen while his country was erased. You did not owe loyalty to the hand that fed you if the food was your own flesh.

"I'm sorry," he said aloud, to Ysolt, to the corridor, to the boy he had been. "I'm sorry. I have to know."

He pressed his palm to the serpent lock.

For a moment nothing. Then the brass warmed, and he felt the oath rise in him to meet the lock — felt it like a second skeleton, rigid and lawful, the shape of every promise he'd made stacked spine to spine. The lock tasted his intent and found it wanting. Found it criminal. A thin voice that was his own voice, recorded somewhere deeper than memory, said: *Turn back, ledger-keeper. This is not your book.*

"No," Marguerite said. "I think it might be exactly my book."

He did not force the lock. Forcing was a storm-caller's solution, blunt and loud, and his storm was caged besides. Instead he did the thing he was actually good at. He thought about accounts.

Every ledger balances. That was the first law of the counting-house, the law beneath all laws: nothing appears from nowhere, nothing vanishes into nowhere, and every debt finds its debtor. If the guild's books showed a surplus of bottled thunder — thunder that could only come from a storm-caller, and there was only one storm-caller within a hundred leagues, caged or not — then somewhere a corresponding entry existed. An outflow. A payment. And payments, in the guild's double-entry world, always ran through the master's private ledger, because the master's ledger was the trunk of the tree and every other book was a branch.

Which meant the lock did not need his permission. It needed a balanced entry.

His hands moved before his courage could object. From his sleeve he drew the slip of paper he'd taken from the counting-house — the thunder surplus, transcribed in his own hooked-g handwriting — and pressed it flat against the serpent's coils. "One entry," he said quietly. "Bottled thunder, three measures, source: the exile Amberdale, drawn against prior account. Entered for balancing. Nothing created, nothing destroyed."

The lie inside the truth was small — he had not drawn the thunder, only found it unaccounted — but the lock was a creature of bookkeeping, not conscience, and bookkeeping cares only that the columns meet. The serpent uncoiled. The door opened onto darkness that smelled, faintly and impossibly, of rain.

Marguerite went in and shut it behind him.

* * *

The study was smaller than he expected, and stranger. No bed, no comfort — Ysolt slept elsewhere, or perhaps did not sleep at all. Shelves rose to the ceiling, but they held no books, only jars: hundreds of them, thousands, each stoppered with wax and labeled in a cramped hand. He lifted the nearest to the candle. The label read *the smell of a specific morning, 4th of Thawmonth, someone's childhood.* Inside, the jar appeared empty, but when he uncorked it — carefully, barely breathing — the smell of cut grass and woodsmoke and his mother's bread rolled out with such violence that he had to sit down on the edge of Ysolt's desk.

They weren't storing smells. They were storing the containers smells came in. Memories, whole and stoppered. The guild's true inventory, and it filled a room.

*Later,* he told himself, corking the jar with hands that would not quite steady. *Grieve later. Read now.*

The master's ledger lay on the desk itself, which surprised him; he had imagined it hidden, chained, buried. Instead it simply lay there, plain as bread, bound in grey leather, its clasp already undone — as if Ysolt had expected him. As if she had left it out the way one leaves a confession on a pillow before going out to face the firing squad.

The earliest pages were a century old. The same cramped hand throughout — and Marguerite's stomach dropped through the floor, because it was not Ysolt's hand. It was his. The hooked g. The left-leaning descenders. Page after page after page, dated across a hundred years, in the handwriting of a man who had, as far as he knew, existed for thirty-nine.

*Entry the first. The Guild of Impossible Things requires a country to erase. Candidates submitted: four. Vhalen selected for the following reasons: its borders are already disputed, its king already paranoid, and its one great asset — a storm-caller of unusual talent — is young, idealistic, and unknown to himself.*

Marguerite read it three times. The candle flame stood perfectly still, as if even fire was afraid to move.

He turned pages with fingers gone clumsy. The ledger laid it all out with the dispassionate clarity of a merchant's inventory — that was the horror of it, that was the thing that would wake him sweating for the rest of his life: it was written *as bookkeeping*. Costs and returns. The rebels Marguerite would shelter: assets, useful for provoking the crown. The storm he would call to save them: the trigger, scheduled. The crown's retaliation — the erasure, the unwriting of Vhalen from maps and minds and memory itself: the guild's fee, collected in full. And what the guild purchased with that fee, in the great market of impossible things, was written plainly on the final line of the century-old entry:

*Consideration received: the Guild's continued existence. The Inquisitors' warrant, quashed. Our charters renewed in perpetuity. We survive by selling the one thing no one else will trade: a whole country's place in the world.*

And beneath it, in fresher ink, in Ysolt's hand at last, a marginal note added perhaps a decade ago:

*The storm-caller must be exiled, not killed. A dead asset draws questions. An exiled one draws none — and may yet prove useful when the account comes due. Bind his memory. Bind his gift. Let him count our bottles and never ask what fills them. Balance pending. Balance always pending.*

Marguerite set the ledger down very carefully, the way one sets down something that might otherwise be thrown through a window.

The cage around his gift — his own oath, sworn in a room he couldn't remember, before judges whose faces had been sanded away. The gaps in his past, neat as trimmed hedges. The prophecy book in his own handwriting, a century old. They had not merely erased Vhalen. They had taken the erasure and folded it into *him*, made him the container of his own crime, the vault where the evidence slept. He was not the guild's ledger-keeper.

He was the guild's ledger.

And Ada — Ada with her thorns and her memories of days that hadn't happened — Ada had walked into the counting-house carrying the one thing the guild could not bottle: a future that refused to balance. No wonder Ysolt had accepted her as cargo. Cargo could be stored. Cargo could be sold.

The candle had burned low. Outside the study's single window, over the rooftops of the city, the sky was doing something Marguerite's body understood long before his mind caught up: the clouds were gathering wrong, stacking themselves in that bruised green-black architecture he knew the way other men knew their mothers' faces. A storm was coming. Not his — he checked, reflexively, and found his cage intact, his gift sleeping its drugged sleep behind the law — but a real one, a natural one, the first honest weather he had felt in eleven years.

Let it come. Let it wash the streets. He had a different kind of storm to raise.

Because here was the thing the ledger had taught him, the thing that hardened in him as he read, cooling from anguish into something with edges: the guild had made one mistake, the same mistake every arrogant bookkeeper eventually makes. They had assumed the exile would stay broken. They had bound his memory and caged his gift and given him columns of numbers to count, believing that a man who cannot remember cannot grieve, and a man who cannot grieve cannot fight.

But he was remembering now. Piece by piece, entry by entry, Ada's accusations and the ledger's confessions fitting together like a lock accepting its key. And grief, he discovered, was not the opposite of purpose. Grief was the fuel of it. Every erased street, every forgotten name, every jar on those shelves holding somebody's stolen morning — they were entries in an account, and the account was wildly, catastrophically unbalanced, and Marguerite Amberdale had spent eleven years becoming the best damned balancer of accounts in Vhalen's capital.

Time to call in the debt.

He took the master's ledger. He took, after a moment's hesitation, three of the jars — the ones labeled in his own century-old hand, the ones marked with dates from a country that no longer existed. Evidence. Ammunition. He wrapped them in his coat like contraband, which was precisely what they were.

At the door he paused, candle high, and looked back at the wall of stoppered memories glittering in the dark like a cellar full of trapped stars. Somewhere in those shelves, he was certain, were the missing pieces of himself — the third clause of his oath, the faces of the judges, the shape of the country he had doomed. The guild had bottled him too, in pieces, and filed him away.

The first drop of rain struck the window. Then another. Then the sky opened, and the sound that came down was vast and clean and utterly indifferent to the affairs of guilds and Inquisitors, and Marguerite found, to his surprise, that he was smiling.

Below, in the entrance hall, the front bell rang — deep, iron, urgent — and he heard the night-warden's boots, and a voice pitched low and hard, a voice he knew from eleven years of nightmares:

Inquisitor Corvane. At the gate. Demanding entry, and demanding the ledger of dates.

Marguerite blew out his candle, tucked the stolen centuries under his arm, and started down the back stairs into the dark.
