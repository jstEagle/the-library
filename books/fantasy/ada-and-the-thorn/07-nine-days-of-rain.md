## 7. Nine Days of Rain

The rain began falling upward on the third morning.

Ada stood in the doorway of the counting-house and watched it go — droplets lifting off the cobbles of Copperlane Market in long silver threads, rising past the awnings, past the bell-tower, into a sky the color of old pewter. It did not splash. It gathered. Puddles shrank as she watched them, pulling themselves up out of the stones like threads being drawn through cloth. Somewhere above, she supposed, there was a cloud growing heavier with everything the city was about to lose.

*Nine days,* the prophecy had said. She had read it over Marguerite's shoulder by candlelight, her thorns prickling so hard along her forearms that she had to bite her lip to keep from crying out. *Nine days before Vhalen finishes unexisting.* The words had been written a century ago in handwriting like his own, and beneath them, every entry after — every date, every small predicted thing — ended in a country that no longer existed. Not *was destroyed*. Not *fell*. Simply: no longer existed. As if the ink itself had forgotten how to hold the shape of it.

"You're staring," Marguerite said behind her. He came to stand at her shoulder, sleeves rolled, ink still staining the webbing between his fingers. He smelled of candlewax and ledger-dust and, faintly, of storm — though he would not have known it, and she had decided not to tell him.

"The rain's going the wrong way," she said.

"Then we're going the right way." He said it lightly, but his hand found the doorframe beside hers and gripped it, knuckles pale, and she understood that the lightness was a coat he wore over something with teeth.

Below them, the market was waking into its strangeness. A fishwife argued with a cooper about the price of barrel-staves, and halfway through her sentence she stopped — mouth open, hand still raised mid-gesture — and looked around at the stalls as though she had never seen any of them before. Then she laughed, embarrassed, and started her sentence again from the beginning, and this time she finished it. But Ada had seen the gap. The little white space where a thought should have been, papered over with a laugh.

"It's starting," she said quietly. "The forgetting."

"I know."

"Not out there. In here too." She pressed her palm flat against her sternum, where the thorns slept beneath her skin like sleeping birds. "I can feel them moving. Like they're trying to remember something for me and can't reach it."

Marguerite turned to look at her then, really look, and she saw the fear in him rearrange itself — not for himself, but for her. That was new. That was three days old. She held onto it the way a drowning woman holds onto driftwood, and hated herself a little for needing it.

"Then we don't wait," he said. "The map. Tonight."

* * *

They went down into the vaults while the guild slept its uneasy sleep, which was not sleep at all but a kind of collective pretending — two hundred keepers and clerks and brokers lying in their narrow beds telling themselves that tomorrow the ledgers would balance, that the accounts of bottled thunder and borrowed years would come out even, that nothing in the counting-house had changed since the century began.

Ada knew better. Her thorns knew better. They had bloomed twice already since the night she'd read the prophecy — once when the guildmaster asked her, smiling, whether she remembered anything else from tomorrow (she said no; the thorns split her sleeve; the guildmaster's smile did not waver, which was worse), and once when Marguerite asked her why she had really come to the guild, and she gave him half an answer, and felt the blood bead along her arms in the dark like dark berries ripening.

She was tired of bleeding for half-answers. So when they reached the third landing of the vaults — the one where the air changed texture, thickening like broth — she stopped and took his wrist.

"Before we go further. I have to tell you something, or I'm going to open a vein down here."

His eyes were very green in the lamplight. "Tell me."

"The guildmaster knows what I am. Or suspects. I lied to her and my thorns bled, and she watched them bleed and said nothing, and Marguerite—" her voice caught, steadied, "—she *let me in*. Cargo, she called me. But you don't take cargo into your house unless cargo is useful. And I think what makes me useful is that I remember a country that everyone else has agreed to forget."

The words hung between them. Above, muffled through stone, the upward rain ticked against the windows like fingers asking to be let in.

"Vhalen," Marguerite said. The name came out of him strangely — half-swallowed, like a word spoken in a dream. He frowned, touched his own mouth as if checking that it had made the sound. "Why did I say—"

"Because it's true, and some part of you is still honest even if the rest has been sold off." She hadn't meant it cruelly, but it landed cruelly, and she saw it land. She softened her grip on his wrist. "I'm sorry. But we don't have time for gentle. In nine days there won't be a Vhalen left to be gentle about."

"Then show me where the map is," he said, "and be quick about the rest."

The map was kept, according to the guildmaster's own index — which Ada had read upside-down across a tea tray, a skill learned in a childhood she mostly didn't talk about — in the Vault of Unlisted Things, behind a door with no label. Every other door in the vaults bore a brass plate: MEMORIES, SOLD IN BULK. BORDERS, GENTLY USED. YEARS, BORROWED AGAINST. The unlabeled door had only a keyhole shaped like a closed eye.

Marguerite produced a key from inside his coat. Of course he had one; he was a ledger-keeper, and ledger-keepers were trusted with everything except the truth. The lock turned with a sound like a sigh.

Inside, the lamp found shelves, and the shelves found Ada's breath and took it away.

Maps. Hundreds of them. Rolled, folded, pinned flat under glass — and all of them wrong in the same way. Coastlines that curled inward like leaves drying. Rivers that ended in mid-air. Cities drawn in confident ink and then, partway through, abandoned, the cartographer's lines trailing off into blank parchment as though the pen had fallen asleep. And in the corner of each one, stamped in violet wax, the seal of the Guild of Impossible Things.

"They drew these," she whispered. "Or they *kept* them. After."

"After what?"

"That's what we're going to find out." She pulled the largest roll from its shelf — heavy vellum, cold as riverstone — and carried it to the lamp. Unrolled, it covered the whole floor, and they both had to kneel on its edges to hold it flat.

It was a map of nowhere. No — she corrected herself, heart hammering against her thorns — it was a map of *somewhere*, somewhere with mountains like a spine and a river delta like an opened hand and nine cities named in a script that hurt to read, the letters writhing gently, refusing to be remembered. Along the border ran a single line of annotation in the same handwriting as the prophecy:

*ERASED BY AGREEMENT. ALL PARTIES COMPENSATED. SEE LEDGER 114.*

"Ledger 114," Marguerite repeated. His face had gone the color of the parchment. "That's the sealed one. The guildmaster's own. Nobody reads Ledger 114."

"Then nobody will miss it tonight," Ada said, "when we do."

He looked at her across the map of the dying country, and something passed between them that had no name in either of their vocabularies yet — not trust exactly, trust was too slow a word, too patient for what the ninth day was going to demand of them. Something more urgent than trust. An agreement, signed in fear, countersigned in want.

"Together, then," he said.

"Together," she said, and her thorns stayed quiet, and she took that for a blessing.

* * *

They almost made it out.

The trouble was the fishwife — or rather, the fishwife's husband, who turned out to be Ferren, the guild's night-broker, coming up the vault stairs with a lantern and a yawn just as Ada and Marguerite came down them, a rolled map under Marguerite's coat and Ledger 114 under Ada's shirt, where it lay against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

Four people on a staircase built for two. The lantern swung. Ferren's eyes went from the map's edge poking from Marguerite's coat to Ada's face, and Ada watched him assemble the story of what he was seeing, and watched him arrive at the wrong conclusion, and thanked every thorn in her body for it.

"Smuggling," Ferren said, with the weary relish of a man who had waited years to catch the great Marguerite Amberdale at something. "You. Smuggling. Out of *our* vaults."

"Ferren—" Marguerite began.

"No, no, don't explain. I'm taking you to the guildmaster. Both of you. Right now." He grabbed Marguerite's elbow, and Ada's heart dropped through six floors of stone—

—and then Ferren stopped. Mid-step. Mid-word. His mouth stayed open around the shape of *guildmaster*, and his eyes went soft and unfocused, and the lantern trembled in his grip, and for three full seconds the man was simply *not there*, standing on the stairs, breathing, present in every way except the one that mattered.

When he came back, he looked at Marguerite with polite puzzlement, the way one looks at a stranger encountered in a corridor.

"Beg pardon," he said. "Were we going somewhere?"

Ada's thorns bloomed all at once, a hot red rash from wrist to shoulder, and she pressed her arm against her side to hide it, and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the erasure was not waiting politely outside the walls. It was in the stairwells. It was in the brokers. It was eating the guild from the bottom up, one forgotten sentence at a time, and in nine days it would eat the stairs themselves, and the stone, and the idea of stone.

"We were going to bed," Marguerite said smoothly, steering Ferren gently aside. "Long night ahead of us. Goodnight, Ferren."

"Goodnight," said Ferren, and continued up the stairs toward a conversation he would never remember wanting to have.

Neither of them spoke until they were out the guild's plain front door and into the upward-raining street. The droplets rose around them, catching the lamplight, thousands of small bright things leaving the world the way tears leave a face — without permission, without ceremony, without any promise of returning.

"He forgot," Ada said. "Mid-sentence. Just like the fishwife."

"Yes."

"Marguerite. If it can take Ferren, it can take us. Our names. Each other." She heard her own voice crack and did not care. "In nine days I could walk past you in this street and not know you, and neither of us would ever know what we'd lost, because losing requires remembering, and—"

"Then we won't waste the nine days." He took her hand — the unbloodied one — and his grip was steady, and warm, and terrified, and she loved him for the terror as much as the warmth. "We find Ledger 114's meaning. We find the Door of Unmaking. We get out of Vhalen before Vhalen gets out of us. And if the forgetting comes for us anyway—" his thumb moved once across her knuckles, "—then we write things down. Everything. Dates, names, debts. You and I are good at records, Ada. Let's make a record they can't erase."

Above them, the last of the evening's rain rose into the clouds, and the clouds grew heavier, and somewhere in the vaults below the city a sealed ledger lay against a girl's ribs, waiting to be read.

"Where do we start?" she asked.

"With the man who sold me my own past," Marguerite said. "There's a memory broker in the Shambles who deals in recollection. Sef, they call him. And I need to know what I paid him to forget."

Ada's thorns stirred — softly, warningly — and she said nothing, and walked with him into the rising rain.
