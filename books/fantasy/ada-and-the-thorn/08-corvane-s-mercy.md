## 8. Corvane's Mercy

The rain had stopped an hour before Corvane found me, and the silence it left behind was worse than the storm.

I heard him on the stairs of the Halfway House — not his boots, which were soft as a priest's confession, but the way the house itself went quiet around him. The inn that existed on no road had creaked and muttered all night, its beams settling like old men in their sleep, its hearth popping with wood that smelled faintly of orchards that no longer grew anywhere. But when Corvane climbed toward the third floor, every sound in the building leaned away from him, the way water leans from oil. Even the candle on my table bent its flame sideways, as if bowing to someone it hoped would pass by.

I had one advantage: I knew he was coming. Ada's thorns had told us — she'd woken at midnight with her arms burning, the black blooms along her foreheads opening like small mouths, and she'd said, *He's close, Marguerite. The man who smells of ink and cold iron.* We'd had time to plan nothing better than this: I would stay. She would go down through the window into the garden of forgotten roads, where the ferryman kept his boat moored to a fence post, and wait for my signal or my absence.

So when the knock came — three measured raps, courteous as a summons — I sat alone at the little table with a cup of tea gone cold and the guildmaster's sealed ledger hidden beneath the loose board under my chair, and I said, "It's open."

Corvane entered the way frost enters a room: gradually, and then all at once.

He was not what the word *Inquisitor* had taught me to expect. No towering shadow, no mask of office. He was a lean, grey-templed man in a travelling coat the colour of wet slate, with ink stains on his right cuff that no amount of washing had ever fully conquered. His eyes were pale — not cold, exactly, but *rinsed*, as though they had looked at so many terrible things that they had been washed too many times and lost some essential colour of themselves. He carried a leather satchel and a lantern he did not light, because the candle was enough for him, because he wanted me to see his face clearly.

"Marguerite Amberdale," he said. "Or should I say — ledger-keeper second class, Guild of Impossible Things, currently in arrears."

"You can say whatever you like," I answered. "Everyone else does."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. The memory of one, perhaps, filed away long ago in some drawer of himself he rarely opened.

"May I sit?"

"It's your inn," I said. "Insofar as anyone's anything is theirs anymore."

He sat across from me, set the satchel on the table between us with the care of a surgeon laying out instruments, and folded his hands. Up close I could see the fine tremor in them — barely there, a vibration like a struck string still ringing somewhere far below hearing. I had seen that tremor before. I saw it every morning in my own hands when I thought no one was watching.

"You've been busy," Corvane said. "Nine days ago you were a man who balanced books. Now you're a man who steals maps, breaks seals, and runs through a country that is trying very hard to stop existing. I'm curious what happened. I find, lately, that curiosity is the only appetite I have left."

"The warrant," I said. "You came with a warrant. Written in the same hand as the prophecy. You came to take the ledger of dates."

"I came with a great many things." He unbuckled the satchel slowly, letting me watch each motion. "Warrants. Manacles of the old design, the kind that hold not just wrists but names. A writ of extraction, should you prove difficult. And—" he drew out a folded document, heavy paper, sealed with grey wax, "—an offer."

I did not touch it. "The Inquisition doesn't make offers. It makes examples."

"The Inquisition makes examples," Corvane agreed. "I make offers. There is a difference, though I admit it has narrowed over the years." He turned the document so it faced me but did not push it across. "Testify against the guild. Give the Crown everything — the vaults, the trades, the ledgers of borrowed years and bottled thunder, the whole obscene inventory. Name the guildmaster. Name her accomplices. In exchange, your exile is dissolved. Your name is returned to you. Not the name they gave you here — *Marguerite Amberdale*, a made thing, a convenience — but your true name. The one that was taken. The one Vhalen remembers."

The room tilted, very slightly, the way a ship tilts when something enormous passes beneath it.

My true name. I had not heard it spoken aloud in eleven years. I had not been permitted to remember it — the exile-oath had reached into me and folded it away like a letter into a drawer, and though I knew the drawer existed, I could not open it. When I tried, I found only smoothness, the way a tongue finds the socket of a missing tooth. For eleven years I had been a man shaped like a hole.

And here was a washed-out-eyed hunter offering me the tooth back.

"You're lying," I said, because it was easier than trembling.

"I never lie," Corvane said. "It's a professional deformity. Liars must keep two sets of books, and I have spent thirty years auditing other people's. I haven't the strength for my own." He tapped the document once. "Read it. Every clause is real. The Lord Chancellor signed it himself, which surprised even me — he does not usually sign things that mention Vhalen."

"Why?" My voice came out lower than I intended. "Why would the Crown want the guild broken? The guild holds its borders. The guild holds its *secrets*. You said it yourself — the guild co-authored—"

I stopped. Too late. Corvane's rinsed eyes brightened by a degree, the way ice brightens before it cracks.

"Co-authored," he repeated softly. "So you know. Or half-know. The guildmaster's sealed ledger — yes, we've known about that book for years. We could never reach it. Sealed against us, sealed against everyone, sealed perhaps against her own conscience." He leaned back. "You want to know why the Crown turns on its own pen? Because the prophecy is finishing, Marguerite. Nine days. Eight now. And when a prophecy finishes, everything written in it comes due — including the parts the guild wrote to protect itself. Including the parts—" and here his voice did something I had not expected; it faltered, a hairline fracture in dry stone, "—including the parts *I* am written into."

The candle flame stood very straight. Outside, in the garden of forgotten roads, I imagined Ada listening through the dark, her thorns blooming or closing, and I prayed silently that she would stay hidden, because what Corvane said next was not for her ears.

"Show me the last page," I said.

"That is not in the offer."

"Then there is no offer."

We regarded each other across the cold tea and the unlit lantern and the folded document neither of us touched. Somewhere below us the inn groaned — a long, wooden, almost human sound, like a sleeper turning over in a dream of better days.

Corvane exhaled. Then, with the air of a man stepping off a cliff because the path behind him has already crumbled, he opened the satchel again and drew out a slim book bound in grey leather — the same grey as his coat, the same grey, I realized with a lurch, as the wax on the warrant, as the sky over Vhalen these nine days past. The Book of Dates. The prophecy itself. I had seen its twin in the counting-house; I had found tonight inscribed in it a century before tonight occurred, in handwriting like my own.

"This is the Inquisition's copy," he said. "There are three. One belongs to the guild. One belongs to the Crown. One, legend says, belongs to whoever wrote it first, and I have begun to suspect that person is dead, or worse than dead, or standing in front of me pretending to be a bookkeeper." He opened it to the final page and turned it toward me, and his hands were shaking now openly, the tremor gone from subterranean to plain sight. "Read it. I have read it four hundred times. I can recite it. But I need someone else to read it aloud, because in four hundred readings it has never once changed, and I need to know if it changes for you."

I read.

*On the last day of Vhalen, when the Door stands open and the erasure turns upon its makers, the Judge shall stand in the dissolving street where he sentenced ten thousand names, and he shall be given back every verdict he ever rendered, all at once, and he shall accept them freely, and the street will take him, and the country will be done with judges.*

Below it, in the margin, in a hand I recognized with a sickness rising in my throat — the hand from the counting-house, the hand like mine, the hand that might be mine — someone had added a single line:

*He already knows.*

I looked up. Corvane was watching me with an expression I had last seen on the face of a condemned man I passed once, years ago, in the courts of the capital — before my exile, before my name was folded away — a look of terrible relief braided into terror, the look of someone who has finally been told the date of his own death and finds that the waiting is over.

"Chief Judge," I said quietly. "Of Vhalen."

"You knew."

"Ada suspected. She said the man who hunted us smelled of ink and cold iron, and that her thorns remembered a courtroom." I closed the book gently, the way you close the eyes of the dead. "She didn't know your face. Faces were the first thing the erasure took."

"Faces, roads, bells, harvests." Corvane recited it like liturgy. "Names last. Names always last — a country holds onto its names the way a drowning man holds onto rope, even after the rope begins to burn." He laughed, briefly, horribly. "Do you know what it is to audit the destruction of a nation? To sign the instruments of erasure — I signed them, Marguerite, forty-one warrants, each one removing a parish, a river, a family line from the record of the world — and then to watch the prophecy inform you, a century in advance, that your own signature is the last thing Vhalen intends to collect?"

* * *

For a while neither of us spoke. The candle burned. Downstairs, faintly, the ferryman's bell rang once — the Halfway House ringing for guests who arrived by no road — and I thought of Ada in the dark garden, and of Sef the broker, and of the memory I had sold and could not retrieve, and of how many people in this unraveling country were carrying drawers inside themselves that they could not open.

"Why show me?" I asked at last. "The page. Why does it matter if it changes for me?"

"Because if it changes," Corvane said, "then the prophecy is not fixed, and I have four hundred readings of hope ahead of me. And if it does not change—" he spread his hands, an auditor presenting a balanced account, "—then I want to spend my remaining days usefully. Which brings me back to the offer."

He pushed the document across the table at last. I picked it up. The Chancellor's seal was genuine; I had verified enough seals in eleven years of ledger-work to know the weight of wax, the depth of impression, the particular arrogance of the royal crest pressed into grey. The clauses were as he said. Testimony against the guild. Full confession, full cooperation. In exchange: dissolution of exile, restoration of name, safe conduct beyond the borders of the erasure — *beyond*, I noted, the word chosen carefully, meaning out of Vhalen entirely, out before the last day closed like a book.

A name. A self. A door out of the burning house.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then in eight days you are erased with the rest of us, and the guild's secrets burn unread, and the machine that traded your exile for its survival goes on trading — with the next storm-caller, and the next girl with thorns in her hair, and the next country that grows inconvenient." He said it without heat, without rhetoric, the flat delivery of a man reading figures from a column. "I am not threatening you. I am showing you the arithmetic. I have spent my life believing that arithmetic was justice. I no longer believe that. But I still believe in showing people the numbers."

I set the document down. My hands were steady, which surprised me; inside, something was swinging like a bell in a high wind.

Because here was the truth of it, the truth I could not say aloud in front of this man: the offer was *good*. That was the obscenity of it. Testify, and I walked free with my own name in my mouth. Refuse, and I burned with a country that hated me — that had every reason to hate me, though I still could not remember why, though the guilt sat in me like swallowed stone with no shape to grieve. And threaded through both futures ran the same question, the one Corvane's rinsed eyes were asking without asking: *what is a name worth, bought from the same crown that erased ten thousand of them?*

"You appear in the prophecy," I said slowly. "On the final page. As the judge who accepts his verdicts. And yet you serve the Inquisition. You hunt the guild's fugitives. You hunt *me*. Why does a man written into his own execution spend his last days doing his executioner's paperwork?"

"Because the paperwork is the only place left where I understand the rules." For a moment the rinsed quality left his eyes and something older looked out — something drowned. "Do you know what the erasure feels like from inside, Marguerite? Not for Vhalen. For us. The instruments. The ones who held the pen." He touched his own temple, lightly, as though checking for a wound. "Every morning I wake and there is a country-shaped hole where my certainties used to be. I remember sentencing rebels whose faces are gone. I remember a wife whose name is gone — I know I had one; the wedding ring fits; the indentations on my finger say decades. I remember loving her. I cannot remember her. And every night the prophecy tells me how it ends, and every morning I get up and file reports to a Crown that also cannot remember what it destroyed, and the reports are the only solid thing, and so I file them, and I hunt, and I follow the rules, because a man falling needs something to hold, even if what he holds is the thing that pushed him."

The candle guttered. Neither of us moved to shield it.

"So no," Corvane said, recovering himself, the frost returning to his voice like a tide coming back in. "I do not serve the Inquisition out of loyalty. I serve it the way a drowning man grips a spar — and I am telling you this, fugitive, traitor, storm-caller, because I think you are drowning too, and I think you know it, and I think the guild's offer of shelter has rotted through, and I think you deserve to hear the numbers from someone who has audited them honestly." He stood. He buckled the satchel. He left the document on the table between us, and the grey book beside it, open to the final page, deliberately, like a man leaving a door unlocked. "You have until tomorrow's dusk. Sign, and meet me at the Counting-House steps. Refuse, and I come back with the manacles and the writ, and we both pretend the mercy never happened. That is the arrangement. I find arrangements are all that keeps either of us human."

At the door he paused, one hand on the frame, and did not turn around.

"One more thing, ledger-keeper. The handwriting in the prophecy. The marginal notes. The hand like yours." His voice was very quiet. "Four hundred readings, and I never told a soul what I concluded. But you should know that I checked. Against the guild's records, against the Crown's, against samples of script going back two hundred years." Now he turned, and the candlelight made his rinsed eyes gleam like coins at the bottom of a well. "It isn't *like* your hand, Marguerite. It is your hand. Every entry. Every date. Every verdict. Whoever wrote that prophecy a century ago wrote it in the hand you will write in tomorrow — and the last line, the one in the margin under my sentence, the one that says *he already knows*?"

He opened the door onto the dark stairwell, where the house had resumed its creaking, its muttering, its ordinary impossible life.

"It isn't about me," Corvane said. "It's about you. You already know how this ends. You simply haven't let yourself remember yet."

And he was gone, down the stairs that leaned away from him, out into the rainless dark, leaving the grey book open on my table and the unsigned offer beside it and the candle burning straight and small, like a held breath.

From the garden below, faint through the floorboards, came the sound of the ferryman's bell — twice now, urgent — and beneath it, almost too soft to hear, Ada's voice calling my name in the tone of someone who has just watched thorns bloom all down her arms and knows exactly what they mean.

I looked at the open page. At the margin. At the words in my own unwritten hand.

*He already knows.*

God help me — I was beginning to.
