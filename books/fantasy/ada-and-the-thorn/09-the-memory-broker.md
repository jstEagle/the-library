## 9. The Memory Broker

The rain had not stopped since the market forgot itself, and by the time they reached the Salt Quarter it had learned new tricks — falling sideways through doorways, pooling on ceilings, hanging in the air like beads on an invisible string. Ada walked beneath a chandelier of suspended droplets and thought: *this is what the end of a country looks like from the inside.* Not fire. Not flood. Just weather losing its manners, and people pausing mid-sentence to wonder what they had been saying.

Marguerite walked ahead of her with the stolen map folded against his chest like a second heart. He had not spoken in an hour. She understood why. The map of erased places was drawn in ink that moved — streets sliding into one another, coastlines breathing — and every time he looked at it, his face did something complicated and then went carefully blank, the way a man's face does when he is carrying something breakable.

"Left here," she said.

"There's no left here."

"There will be in three steps."

There was. A street surfaced out of the fog between two shuttered dye-houses, cobbles still wet as though it had never gone anywhere at all. Marguerite stared at it. Ada watched him stare, and felt the thorns along her forearms shift under her sleeves — a slow, restless stirring, like cats turning over in sleep.

They were worse today. She had noticed it in the night, lying awake in the Halfway House of Forgotten Roads while Marguerite slept the sleep of a man who had just learned his own name was a wound. Every thorn she carried was a memory of a future she had never lived, and the future was fraying now, day by day, as Vhalen finished unexisting. When a thorn-memory faded completely, the thorn died. She had found one dead this morning, grey and brittle as old bone, and she had not told him. There would be time for telling later. Or there wouldn't, which amounted to the same thing.

The broker's shop sat where the map said it would: a narrow building squeezed between a candle-maker's and nothing at all, its sign reading simply SEF in letters that rearranged themselves when she wasn't looking. *FES. EFS. SEF.* A name trying on different clothes.

"You're certain about this?" Marguerite asked.

"No," Ada said honestly, because her thorns bloomed otherwise — a faint green flush along her wrists, tiny white flowers opening like small confessions. It was the curse and gift of her body: she could not lie without wearing the lie. "But certainty is for people with more country left than we have."

She knocked.

* * *

The inside of Sef's shop was larger than the outside, which Ada had expected, and warmer, which she had not. Shelves rose into darkness overhead, and on the shelves stood jars — thousands of them, tens of thousands — each one holding a curl of light that moved like a fish in shallow water. Some lights were gold. Some were the grey-green of pond water. One, near the door, flickered red at intervals, like a memory of a fire that had never quite decided to happen.

"Memories," Sef said, appearing between the shelves as though he had always been standing there and had merely allowed them to notice. He was small and neat, with spectacles that had no glass in them, and hands so clean they looked newly made. "Bottled recollection. I buy, I sell, I occasionally lend at reasonable interest. You are the storm-caller." He looked past her at Marguerite. "And you are the girl who remembers tomorrow. Word travels fast in a dying city. Faster than usual."

"We want to buy back memories," Marguerite said. "Mine. They were sold years ago. Through the guild."

"Ah." Sef removed his empty spectacles, polished them on his sleeve, and put them back on, which changed nothing about his eyes at all. "Guild stock. That's awkward. Guild memories are held in trust, you understand. Collateral against debts. I can't simply hand a man his own childhood back because he asks nicely."

"I'm not asking nicely," Marguerite said. "I'm asking desperately. There's a difference, and I suspect you deal in it."

Ada watched the broker's face do a small, pleased thing, like a lock recognizing its key. Brokers, she thought, liked desperation. Desperation meant the price would be paid.

"The guild holds your sealed ledger-memories," Sef said, drifting down the aisle, trailing one finger along the jar-lights, which leaned toward his touch like plants toward sun. "But memories move, storm-caller. Memories are the most liquid commodity in any country, including a country that is currently being unmade. Yours passed through my hands some years ago. I kept copies. Copies are my vice." He smiled thinly. "Well. One of them."

"How much?" Marguerite asked.

Sef stopped before a shelf and lifted down a jar in which a single light coiled, silver-grey, restless as weather. Even through the glass, Ada felt it — a pressure in her ears, a taste of lightning on the back of her tongue. Marguerite made a sound she had never heard him make, low and involuntary, the sound of a man seeing his own face in an unexpected mirror.

"That one is yours," Sef said gently. "I believe it is the afternoon you first called thunder. You were nine. Your mother was angry. You were not sorry." He turned the jar so the light pressed against the glass toward Marguerite, straining, and Ada saw the storm-caller's hands tremble. "The price," Sef said, "is not coin."

* * *

They sat in the back room, which contained a table, two chairs, and a third chair that existed only sometimes. Sef poured tea that tasted of other people's birthdays. Ada wrapped her hands around the cup and waited, because she already knew — had known since the shop door opened, had known perhaps since the day she walked into the guild with tomorrow stuck in her hair — how this would go.

"The guild took your memories as part of your exile-oath," Sef said to Marguerite. "Standard practice. But the oath has begun to rot with everything else, and the memories have become... available. I can restore all of them. What you did. What was done to you. Why Vhalen is dying." He set down his cup with a click. "In exchange, I require payment in kind. Memory for memory. And yours—" he inclined his head toward Ada, and her stomach dropped through the floor of the world— "yours are the only ones in this room worth anything at all."

"No," Marguerite said immediately.

"They're not even mine in the ordinary way," Ada began.

"No." He said it harder. "She doesn't pay for my sins. Whatever's in her head, those thorns, that future — it's hers. Find another price."

"There is no other price," Sef said, with the mild patience of a man stating the hour. "Coin buys objects. Years buy time. Only memory buys memory. And hers are—" he paused, choosing the word with a connoisseur's care— "unprecedented. Memories of days that have not happened. In four hundred years of trade, I have never stocked such a thing. Their value is beyond reckoning. One thorn-memory would buy back everything the guild holds of yours, with change remaining."

"Then we leave," Marguerite said, standing. "We find another way."

"There is no other way. There is me, and there is the dark, and the dark is closing at a rate of one street per day." Sef laced his clean fingers together. "Take the night to consider. My shop keeps its own hours during an erasure; you may stay until morning."

Marguerite looked at Ada, and his face was open in a way that hurt her to see — all the careful blankness gone, replaced by something fierce and protective and utterly certain that he knew best. "We'll find another way," he told her, as if promising. As if it were his promise to make.

Ada smiled at him and said, "All right," and her thorns stayed quiet, because it was not a lie yet. It would become one by morning. Lies, she had learned, were mostly a matter of timing.

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat by the shop's high window and watched the upside-down rain climb past the glass, and she took inventory.

Her thorns numbered thirty-one that morning. Thirty living, one dead. Each one was a memory of the future, and the futures were going out like lamps — not all at once but guttering, dimming, as the days they remembered were erased before they could arrive. She had lived none of them. That was the cruelty of her condition: she remembered a life she had never led, a Vhalen with bells and orchards and a storm-caller who laughed more easily, and none of it had ever been real, and soon it would not even be rememberable.

She closed her eyes and walked the garden of herself.

There was the memory of a festival she had never attended, lanterns rising over a river she had never seen, Marguerite beside her in the crowd, older, easier, his hand finding hers without ceremony. There was the memory of a winter kitchen, bread and argument, the smell of rosemary. There was the memory — she flinched away from this one, then made herself look — of the ending. The Door. The price. Whose life the Door would ask for. She had carried that knowledge since before she met him, and she had built her whole strange loyalty around keeping it, and if she sold it—

If she sold it, someone else would read it. Sef would read it. Sef, who bought and sold and lent at reasonable interest, would hold the ending of everything in a jar on his shelf, and brokers talked, and Inquisitors listened, and Corvane was still out there in the dissolving streets with his warrant written in prophecy-hand.

So not that one. Never that one.

But the others. The lanterns. The kitchen. The long ordinary happinesses that had never happened and now never would — those she could pay with. They were dying anyway. Better they should buy something than fade to nothing on the vine.

She got up quietly, because quiet was a skill the guild's vaults had taught her, and went to find Sef among his shelves.

He was waiting, of course. Brokers always were. He had two jars ready on the counter, and the silver-grey light inside them strained toward her like dogs toward a whistle.

"One," she said. "The smaller jar. And you tell no one what you read in it. Swear it on your vice."

"On my copies," Sef said solemnly, hand over whatever served him for a heart. "No one reads a broker's stock but the buyer. You have my word, which is worth slightly more than my spectacles."

"And he learns nothing of what I paid until it's done. Not tonight. Not ever, if I choose."

"He will know the shape of the transaction. Men notice when their memories return."

"He'll know I paid. He won't know what." She reached up, past her ear, into her hair, where the thorns grew densest, and found the lantern-festival memory by its warmth. It came away with a sound like a rose being cut — a soft green tearing — and pain bloomed down her scalp and neck, and she did not let her breath shake, because Marguerite was asleep two rooms away and men who had lost their countries deserved at least one whole night's sleep.

The thorn lay in her palm, still flowering, tiny white petals opening and closing like a mouth trying to speak. She set it in the jar Sef offered, and the light inside rose to meet it, and the memory sank into the silver-grey storm-light like sugar into tea.

"There," Sef said softly. "Paid in full."

The absence was immediate and strange — not emptiness but a smoothness, a place in her where a shape used to be. She would never see those lanterns rise. She had never seen them rise. Both of these were true, and both hurt in different directions.

"Will it hurt him?" she asked. "The restoration?"

"Truth usually does." Sef capped the jar. "But I will tell you something for free, girl-with-thorns, because free things are my second vice." He looked at her over his empty spectacles, and for a moment his neatness fell away and she saw something old and tired underneath, something that had handled ten thousand other people's yesterdays and missed having any of his own. "The memory you sold me — I will shelve it and never open it, as sworn. But know this: a memory of the future is a heavy thing on a shelf. It presses on the jars around it. Things leak. Nothing in my shop stays entirely where it is put." He smiled, thin as paper. "Especially endings."

Cold walked up Ada's spine. "What do you mean?"

"I mean that I know what you kept back. I mean that brokers see the shape of a jar even when they don't drink its contents." He turned the jar of Marguerite's storm-childhood in his clean hands. "Come. Fetch your storm-caller. Let him have his yesterdays back, and may whatever god keeps broken countries have mercy on you both."

* * *

She woke him with a hand on his shoulder, and he came up out of sleep already braced, already reaching for a storm that wasn't there, and she said, "It's done. Sit down. Drink your tea."

He stared at her. "What's done?"

"You were going to refuse all night and find another way that doesn't exist, and we don't have a night." She kept her voice level. Her thorns were quiet. That was the terrible trick of it — she could say almost anything true-adjacent and her body would hold its peace. "I paid. One memory. One I could spare. Don't ask which one, because I won't tell you, and don't argue, because it's spent."

"Ada." His voice cracked on her name. "What did you give him?"

"A yesterday that was never going to happen anyway." She pushed the teacup into his hands. "Yours are coming back. All of them. Everything the guild took. You'll know what you did, and what was done to you, and why the maps have a hole in them shaped like home." She made herself smile. "Drink the tea. Sef says it helps."

"It tastes of birthdays."

"Someone else's. Drink it anyway."

He drank. And Sef brought the jars — six of them, seven, a dozen, each holding a coiling light that strained toward Marguerite like children at a gate — and laid them out on the table in a ring, and said, "When you are ready. It is better seated. Truth arrives faster than the body expects."

Marguerite looked at the jars, and then at Ada, and she watched him try to work out what she had paid, watching her wrists, her hair, her eyes, hunting the missing piece the way a man hunts a missing tooth with his tongue. She met his gaze and gave him nothing but steadiness. Loyalty outpacing honesty, she thought. Let him be angry later. Later was a luxury the dying country still sold cheaply.

"I'm ready," he said.

Sef uncapped the first jar.

The light rose out of it like smoke with intent, hung for a heartbeat in the lamplight — and poured into Marguerite through his open mouth, his wide eyes, every seam of him at once. He made no sound. His hands gripped the table's edge. The second jar opened. The third. Ada counted them down with her heart, and with each one his face changed — confusion, recognition, horror arriving in tides — and when the last light entered him he sat very still for a long moment, breathing like a man surfacing from deep water.

Then he said, in a voice she barely recognized, flat and far away:

"I remember the storm."

And outside Sef's high window, the upward-falling rain stopped mid-air — every suspended drop hanging motionless above the Salt Quarter, the whole drowned sky holding its breath — as if the weather itself had paused to listen to what its maker would say next.

Ada's hand found his across the table. Her thorns, all thirty of them, bloomed at once — a silent garden of white flowers opening in the dark of the broker's shop, though she had told no lie at all.

Some truths, she was learning, cost the same as lies.
