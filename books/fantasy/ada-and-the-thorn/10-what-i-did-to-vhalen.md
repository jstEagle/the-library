## 10. What I Did to Vhalen

The memories did not return the way rain returns to a dry field — gradually, soaking, gentle. They returned the way a dam breaks.

Sef had pressed the vial into his hands with the ceremony of a man handing over a live coal, and Marguerite had drunk it standing in the broker's back room among shelves of other people's yesterdays, and for three heartbeats nothing happened at all. Then the world tilted sideways and every year he had sold came home at once.

He was on his knees before he knew he had fallen. The floorboards of Sef's shop became the flagstones of the Chantry of Storms; the smell of dust and old paper became incense and ozone; Ada's voice calling his name became the voice of a woman named Ilse Vay, who had taught him that lightning was not a weapon but a language, and that the sky spoke it only to those willing to be answered.

*Marguerite.* Her hand on his shoulder, her sleeves singed at the cuffs from twenty years of calling weather down onto demonstration-fields. *You have more storm in you than any student I've taught. That is not a compliment. It is a warning.*

He remembered Vhalen.

Not the word — he had known the word, a country-shaped absence in every atlas, a name people's mouths slid past like a tongue finding a missing tooth. He remembered the *place*. The terraced vineyards above the Sorrel River where the grapes went blue-black in autumn. The capital, Cerulein, with its nine bridges and its bell-tower that rang the hours in chords instead of single notes because the founders believed no moment should sound alone. The harbor towns where they burned salt-wood lamps green against the fog. The mountain passes where the snow sang — actually sang, a thin harmonic the wind drew from the ridgelines, so that travelers crossing at dusk walked through music.

And he remembered why the crown had wanted it dead.

* * *

The rebels had come to him in winter, in the twelfth year of King Aldous Meric's reign, when the levies had grown so ruinous that farmers in the eastern provinces were burning their own orchards rather than surrender them. Marguerite was twenty-six, newly master of his gift, arrogant in the way only the very talented are arrogant — certain that power was a tool and tools could be held without cutting the hand.

Their leader was a stonemason named Breccan Hale, a broad quiet man with mortar still in the creases of his knuckles, who knelt in the mud of a roadside shrine and did not ask Marguerite for a miracle. He asked for rain.

*"The king's granaries sit full while we starve,"* Breccan said. *"We mean to take them back. Not burn them — take them, and open them, and feed the provinces through winter. We need one night of storm so black that no garrison can see our hands moving."*

One night. One storm. Marguerite had called storms before — for festivals, for drought-broken harvests, once to drive off a pirate fleet at the request of a coastal magistrate. Weather was a muscle. This would be exercise.

He told himself that all the way up to the ridge above the granary town of Vell's Crossing, and he told himself it as he raised his arms, and he told himself it as the sky began to answer.

But here is what no teacher warns you: a storm is not a thing you hold. It is a thing you *invite*, and invitations cannot be recalled. He reached for darkness and the darkness came with teeth. He asked for wind and the wind arrived hungry, tearing roofs loose across three districts, and the rain fell like nails and the lightning — the lightning he had meant to keep high, a curtain of glare to blind the watchtowers — struck low, again and again, striking the granary itself, striking the oil-store beside it, and by midnight Vell's Crossing was burning in sheets of falling water and the rebels' careful plan was ash along with the winter stores of forty thousand people.

Breccan Hale died in that fire. So did two hundred others whose names Marguerite had never learned and would now never forget, because forgetting was exactly what everyone else had been made to do.

* * *

Marguerite surfaced from the memory gasping, and found himself not in Sef's shop but in the half-ruined parlor of the Halfway House of Forgotten Roads, laid out on a bench beneath a window that looked out on a road that existed only when walked. Rain ticked against the glass. Someone had covered him with a blanket. Ada sat nearby, very still, her thorn-marked arms wrapped around her knees, watching him with an expression he had no name for.

"You screamed," she said quietly. "For a long time. You said names. Dozens of names."

"I remember." His voice came out sanded raw. "Ada, I remember everything."

"Then you know." She unfolded herself and came to kneel beside him, and he saw that her thorns had bloomed — small white flowers opening along her forearms like frost forming in reverse. She was telling the truth, then. She was always telling the truth when she bloomed. "Vhalen wasn't destroyed. It was *erased*. And the storm at Vell's Crossing was the reason."

"Not the reason." He sat up slowly. The blanket slid away. Every joint ached as though the memories had been carried in his body all these years, heavy as ballast, and had only now been set down. "The excuse. Listen — I need to say all of it, or I'll lose the shape of it. When I broke the storm, I went to the crown myself. I confessed. I stood in the throne room of Aldous Meric and I offered my life, my gift, my name — anything. And the king's chancellor smiled at me the way a merchant smiles at a shipment, and said the crown had a better use for me than my death."

"The guild," Ada said.

"The guild." The word tasted like copper. "I thought I was being spared. I thought — God help me, I thought mercy. They took me to the counting-house I've worked in for eleven years, and there was a table, and on the table was a book, and the book was blank except for one line already written: *the storm-caller will serve*. And they explained, so reasonably, so gently, what the alternative was. Not execution. Erasure. The crown had recently acquired — through the guild, through a trade I now understand was older and vaster than I ever guessed — the art of unmaking. A country that harbors rebellion does not need to be punished, the chancellor said. It needs to be *forgotten*. Rebellion lives in memory. Kill the memory and you kill the rebellion root and branch, and no martyrdom, no songs, no sons rising up twenty years later to avenge fathers. Nothing rises from nothing."

"And you agreed." Ada's voice held no accusation. That was somehow worse.

"I signed." He heard his own voice go flat and strange, a stranger's voice reciting a stranger's sin. "They needed a storm-caller's signature — a binding written in weather, because erasure is a kind of weather, did you know that? It has pressure systems. It has winds. The guild wrote the ledger of unmaking but the sky had to countersign, and the sky would only listen to me. So I stood on the tower of the Chantry of Storms — my own chantry, my own city — and I called down the calmest, most obedient storm of my life, and I aimed it at my own country, and I let it erase everything. Cerulein. The nine bridges. The singing snow. The vineyards. Two million people who woke the next morning scattered across neighboring kingdoms with new names in their mouths and no idea what had been taken from them, grieving losses they couldn't name, weeping at empty horizons for reasons they'd never understand."

His hands were shaking. He watched them shake as though they belonged to the boy on the tower, the boy who had believed he was trading one guilt for a smaller one.

"They told me it was painless," he whispered. "They told me the erased feel nothing — that unmaking is gentler than fire. And I *believed* them, because believing them was the only way I could survive the signing. Eleven years I've balanced their impossible ledgers and told myself I was a penitent, that exile was my penance, that keeping their books honestly was some small service against the great wrong. And the whole time—" His breath caught. "The whole time the proof was sitting on my own desk. Tonight's date. Written a century ago. In my handwriting. Because I wrote the prophecy, Ada. Or the guild wrote it and used my hand, my storm-signature, the way they used everything else. My exile wasn't punishment. It was *storage*. They kept me the way they keep bottled thunder — a component, shelved until the trade required it."

* * *

He stood. His legs held, barely. The room seemed too small to contain what was in him now; his skin felt like the skin of a drum stretched over thunder.

Ada rose with him. "Marguerite—"

"Don't." He turned away, toward the window, toward the road that wasn't there. "Don't comfort me. If you comfort me I'll fold it up and put it away, the way I folded up everything else, and I cannot — I will *not* — put this away again. Do you understand? For eleven years I have been furniture in the house of the people who unmade my country, and I dusted their shelves, and I thanked them for the roof."

"Fury is a fuel," Ada said. "It burns fast, though. Grief burns longer."

"What do you know about grief?" The words left him like a lash and he regretted them before they finished leaving, because she flinched — actually flinched, and her thorns shivered though they did not bloom, which meant she was holding something back, which meant she was *lying by omission*, and he was in no state to be gentle about it. "You walk around with tomorrow in your head and flowers on your arms and you know how this ends, don't you? Some part of you has always known. And you've been kind to me anyway. Why? What did I ever do to earn kindness from the memory of the country I killed?"

She was quiet a long moment. Outside, the upward-falling rain of the dissolving world ticked against the glass and slid toward the sky.

"Because," she said finally, "when Vhalen was erased, everything in it was scattered — the people, the roads, the bells, the names. And some of it, the part that couldn't be scattered, the part that refused — it grew roots instead. In flesh." She lifted her arm, and the white blooms trembled. "I am not *from* Vhalen, Marguerite. As far as I can tell, I was never born anywhere. I am what Vhalen remembers of itself, wearing a girl's shape. Which means every cruel thing you just said about yourself, you said to the country you think you killed. And the country is telling you—" her voice caught, steadied, "—the country is telling you it doesn't want your death. It wants its *streets back*. Its bridges. Its bells. It wants to exist. And you're the only storm-caller alive who knows how unmaking works from the inside."

He stared at her. The fury in his chest did not die — it changed state, the way water changes to ice, the same substance in a harder form. Purpose.

"The Door of Unmaking," he said slowly. "The unlabeled door in the guild vaults. The guildmaster keeps it locked, and Corvane wants the ledger of dates, and the prophecy runs out in—" he counted, and his stomach dropped, "—nine days. Nine days and Vhalen finishes unexisting, and every entry after tonight is a country that isn't."

"So we go back," Ada said. "To the guild. To the door."

"To the guildmaster." Marguerite's voice had gone quiet, and he recognized the quiet — it was the quiet before a storm, the hush Ilse Vay had warned him about, the stillness in which the sky gathered its answer. "Eleven years ago she stood at my elbow while I signed my country away, and she told me it was mercy. I want to hear her explain it to my face. And then—" his hand closed around the memory of the sealed ledger upstairs, the guild's true accounts, the trades written in his own coerced hand, "—and then I am going to read every line she ever hid from me. All of it. Whatever it costs."

Ada studied him. "Fury," she said again, softly. "Fuel."

"Yes."

"It burns fast."

"Good." He pulled on his coat, and the coat smelled faintly of candle-wax and old paper, of the counting-house, of the cage he had mistaken for a sanctuary. "Fast is what we have."

Outside the Halfway House, the road that existed only when walked lay waiting under a bruised sky, and somewhere beyond it, nine days were already dying, one by one, like bells rung in a country that no longer remembered it had bells — and Marguerite Amberdale, storm-caller, traitor, penitent, component, stepped out into the rain to go home and burn the house down.
