## 14. Corvane's Last Page

The rain had stopped falling upward by the time we reached the Sighing Bridge, and that frightened me more than the upward rain ever had. Weather that defied itself was a country in crisis. Weather that simply stopped — hung in the air like beads on a string, each drop waiting — was a country holding its breath.

Ada felt it too. She walked with her hand pressed flat against her sternum, where the thorns grew thickest beneath her coat, and every few steps she glanced back the way we'd come, as if the streets might be closing behind us like water behind a boat.

"They're not dissolving," she said quietly. "They're *waiting*. Look."

I looked. The market square we had crossed an hour ago, all its stalls abandoned mid-flight — a wheel of cheese still rolling, a bolt of silk unspooling into the gutter — now stood perfectly still. Not frozen, exactly. Nothing so honest as frozen. The cheese hovered an inch above the cobbles. The silk lay coiled as though someone had wound it there with great care. The whole city had the quality of a sentence interrupted, every word hanging unfinished in the air.

"The freed memories," I said. "They're reaching everyone at once. The whole country is remembering itself, and it's stopped to listen."

"Then we should keep moving before it remembers *us*."

We crossed the bridge at a pace just short of running. Below us the river Vhal moved like poured glass, its surface so still it held our reflections perfectly — and then imperfectly, because my reflection's face was older than mine, lined around the eyes, and Ada's was younger, a child's face, thornless.

I did not tell her what I saw. She did not tell me either. We had learned, in nine days of catastrophe, which silences were mercies and which were wounds.

Corvane was waiting for us at the far end of the bridge.

He stood beneath the last lamp that still burned in Vhalen — a squat iron thing fed by bottled thunder from the guild's own stores, irony upon irony — and he had his hands folded behind his back like a man admiring architecture rather than laying a trap. Four Inquisitors flanked him, grey-cloaked, their warrant-seals glowing dull red at their belts. The light turned their faces into masks.

"Ledger-keeper," Corvane said. His voice carried strangely across the dead-still air, without echo, as if the country were leaning in to hear. "And the cargo you stole. You've made this very easy, and very difficult, and I haven't yet decided which troubles me more."

Ada's hand found mine. Her fingers were cold, and through her glove I felt the faint prickle of the thorns stirring — she was afraid, and when she was afraid they woke.

"Marguerite Amberdale," Corvane continued, "you are under warrant for theft of guild property, sedition against the Crown-in-Exile, and — " here he paused, and something flickered across his face, some small tectonic shift beneath the official calm — "and crimes against the historical integrity of Vhalen. That last one is new. It wasn't on the warrant this morning."

"It wasn't on the warrant an hour ago," I said.

"No," Corvane agreed. "It wrote itself onto the warrant while I was reading it. Which is, I grant you, a novel experience even for the Office of Inquiry." He unfolded his hands. "You're going to come with us. Both of you. And you're going to explain how a century-old book came to be rewriting itself faster than my clerks can copy it."

* * *

They marched us not to the guildhouse but inward, toward the old administrative quarter — the Hall of Records, a building I had passed a thousand times without once looking at, which should have told me something about myself long ago. Its doors were bronze, taller than two men, worked with scales and quills and the crowned eye of Vhalen's judiciary. My reflection slid across them as we approached: the older man again, the lined eyes, the mouth set in a line of habitual judgment.

The doors opened before anyone touched them.

Inside, the Hall of Records was drowning. Not in water — in ink. Every shelf stood open, every ledger fallen wide, and from their pages rose a slow black mist, curling along the ceiling like weather in a bottle. The freed memories, pouring out of everything the guild had ever written down. The words themselves were leaving the pages, drifting up in ribbons of darkness, and where they touched the Inquisitors' grey cloaks the fabric began to change — greys becoming colors, plain wool blooming with embroidery, hems stitching themselves into patterns no Vhaleni tailor had made in a hundred years.

One of the four Inquisitors made a sound I will never forget. A young man, barely older than Ada. He pulled off his glove and stared at his bare hand, which was suddenly brown-skinned and scarred across the knuckles, and he said, in a voice cracking with wonder and horror both: "*I remember orchards.*"

"Steady," Corvane said. But his voice had changed too. There was a hairline fracture in it now, and through the fracture I could hear something enormous pressing.

They put us in the center of the hall, beneath the dome, where a great seal of the judiciary was inlaid in the floor — the crowned eye again, half-eaten by rising mist. Corvane dismissed three of his men to the doorway and kept only the young one who remembered orchards, perhaps because the boy seemed least likely to obey any further orders at all.

"Now," Corvane said. He drew the warrant from his cloak and held it up between us, and I saw that the parchment was still changing, lines of text surfacing and sinking like fish under ice. "You will tell me what you know. You will begin with the prophecy book. And you will begin, Ledger-keeper, with why your handwriting appears in it."

"I've never written in that book."

"I know your hand. I have confiscated enough of your ledgers to know it the way a priest knows scripture. The prophecy is in your hand, Amberdale. Every date. Every entry. Including—" he turned the warrant over, and his jaw tightened— "including the final page."

"We don't know what's on the final page," Ada said. "It's blank. It's always been blank."

"It isn't blank anymore." Corvane's voice had gone very quiet, the way a room goes quiet before glass breaks. "As of this morning it reads: *'And the Chief Judge of Vhalen shall stand witness at the Door of Unmaking, having judged himself, and shall find the sentence just.'*" He let the warrant fall to the floor between us. "There has been no Chief Judge of Vhalen for a hundred years. The office was abolished when the country was — whatever it was that happened to it. So either the prophecy is mad, or—"

"Or you were him," I said.

The words left me before I understood them, and the moment they were spoken, the hall confirmed them. The mist surged. From the shelves, from the drowned ledgers, from the ink-thick air itself, memories came home to roost.

I saw it the way everyone in the hall saw it, because the freed memories did not distinguish between those who owned them and those who merely stood nearby. They simply *played*, the way light plays on a wall.

I saw a courtroom. High windows, winter light, a bench of dark wood worn smooth by decades of elbows. And on the judge's bench sat a man with lined eyes and a mouth set in a line of habitual judgment, and the man was Corvane, though younger, though wearing robes of deep green embroidered with silver bells — the judicial colors of a country I could not name and grieved for anyway.

I saw him pass sentence on rebels. Nine of them. Farmers and printers and a schoolteacher, condemned for the crime of insisting their country existed. I saw him sign the order with a steady hand. I saw him deliver the prisoners to the Crown's sorcerers with the observation that execution would only make martyrs, and that there were cleaner methods.

I saw him recommend erasure.

Not in malice. That was the unbearable part. I saw the memorandum in his own careful hand — *the rebels draw strength from belonging; remove the belonging and the movement dies; a country is only a consensus, Your Majesty, and consensuses can be revised* — and I saw the crown accept the recommendation, and I saw the guild approached as the instrument, because the guild alone knew how to trade in borders and memory, and I saw the price negotiated, and I saw—

I saw me.

Younger. Standing in the guildmaster's counting-house with rain in my hair and lightning in my fists, offering the only thing I had: my storm, my gift, my name. *Take them,* I heard myself say, in a voice raw with grief for the nine condemned, whom I had tried and failed to save with weather. *Take everything. Just make sure the country survives what comes next.*

And the guildmaster — patient, terrible, kind the way a knife is kind — saying: *Oh, Marguerite. The country won't survive it. But we will. That's the trade.*

The vision broke. I was on my knees on the crowned eye of the judiciary, and Ada was beside me, and her thorns had burst through her sleeves and were blooming white — she was telling the truth, or witnessing it, or both — and across the hall Corvane stood exactly where he had stood, except that he had aged twenty years in the space of a breath, or perhaps he had simply stopped pretending otherwise.

"So," he said.

His voice was almost steady. Almost.

"I wrote the sentence," he said. "My page in the prophecy. I wrote it a hundred years ago and the guild sold it forward, the way they sell everything, and it has been waiting for me ever since." He looked down at his own hands — the same hands, I realized, that had signed the death warrants, that had drafted the memorandum, that had shaken mine across a bargaining table neither of us remembered. "Having judged himself. And finding the sentence just."

"Corvane—" I started.

"Don't." He raised one finger, and it was the gesture of a judge calling a court to order, and it was also the gesture of a man holding shut a door inside himself. "Don't offer me absolution, Amberdale. You're not qualified. You were the weapon. I was the hand on the pen. There is a difference, and the difference matters, and I have spent a hundred years — however many years these have been — building a career on not knowing it."

The young Inquisitor by the door had sunk onto a step, cradling his bare scarred hand, whispering the names of orchards.

"What happens now?" Ada asked. Her voice was thin but level. "You have your warrant. You have us."

Corvane looked at her for a long moment. Then at me. Then at the warrant lying on the floor, its text still shifting, the final page of the prophecy bleeding through the parchment like a bruise.

"Do you know what the Office of Inquiry actually is?" he said. "Everyone thinks it enforces history. Keeps the record straight. Punishes those who tamper." He laughed — a short, awful sound with no humor in it anywhere. "The Office of Inquiry exists to prevent exactly this. Exactly this moment. The moment when the erased things come home. Because the Office was founded by the same signatures, funded by the same bargain, staffed — generation after generation — by people who did not want to know what they were keeping quiet. I am not an officer of the law, girl. I am a lock on a door, and I have just discovered that I built the door myself."

He bent and picked up the warrant. For a moment I thought he meant to arrest us after all, to complete the ritual, to be the lock until the end. Instead he held the parchment over the nearest brazier — the hall's lamps had gone out, but a clerk's coal-brazier still glowed in the corner, stubborn as a heartbeat — and let it catch.

The prophecy's final page burned green, then gold, then was gone.

"That's evidence," I said stupidly.

"That's a sentence," Corvane said, "and sentences are mine to pass. It seems I'm the only judge left in Vhalen, so I suppose I'll have to do." He straightened, and drew himself up, and for one moment he wore the green robes and the silver bells, or I dreamed he did, or the hall remembered him that way — and he pronounced, in the formal cadence of a court that had not convened in a century:

"In the matter of the Crown versus the Country of Vhalen: the court finds the Crown guilty. Sentence: dissolution of all offices derived from the crime. Effective immediately." He paused. His voice cracked clean through. "So ordered."

Then he took off his grey cloak and dropped it on the floor, and beneath it he wore ordinary clothes, a clerk's clothes, a citizen's clothes, clothes that belonged to no office at all.

"Go," he said to us. "The Door. Whatever you're doing, do it quickly — the country can't hold its breath much longer, and when it exhales, I don't know whether what's left will be Vhalen or nothing." He turned away from us toward the great bronze doors, toward the streets beyond, where the stillness waited. "As for me, I have a sentence to serve. I judged myself, and I find it just."

"Where will you go?" Ada asked.

Corvane considered. Through the high windows, the interrupted city hung motionless, every drop of rain suspended, every unfinished word waiting.

"To the Ninth Ward," he said at last. "Where the rebels lived. Where the orchards were." The young Inquisitor looked up sharply, his scarred hand pressed to his chest. "Someone should walk those streets while they still exist. Someone should remember them out loud. Consider it — " and here the ghost of something crossed his face that might, in another life, have become a smile — "community service."

He walked out through the bronze doors into the held-breath city, and we watched him go, this man who had destroyed a country and then spent a lifetime guarding its grave, choosing at the end to be its mourner instead. He did not look back. Judges never do, I thought, and then corrected myself: judges never used to. This one had learned.

The doors swung shut behind him with a sound like a book closing.

* * *

"He's going to die out there," Ada said. "When the country finishes — whatever it's doing — he'll be standing in the middle of it."

"Yes."

"You could have stopped him."

"With what?" I asked gently. "A warrant?"

She didn't laugh. Neither did I. But something in her shoulders loosened, a fraction, the way a bowstring eases when the arrow is finally loosed.

We stood in the drowning hall a moment longer, amid the rising ink and the sleeping ledgers and the boy on the step whispering orchard-names like prayers. Somewhere above us, through the dome, the suspended rain hung in its long golden rows, and beyond the rain, faint as a vein beneath skin, I could feel the road — the road that remembered, the road that had begun, since the burning of the ledgers, to surface wherever someone Vhalen remembered set their feet.

Three days. Ada had said three days, and she had known it the way she knew all the terrible things, from a future she'd paid away and couldn't fully explain.

"Come on," I said, and took her hand, thorns and all. "We have a Door to open, and I think I finally understand what it wants."

She looked at me — really looked, the way she did when she was deciding whether to lie to protect me — and her thorns stayed pale and quiet, and that frightened me more than anything Corvane had done.

Because it meant that whatever she was about to say next would be true.

"Marguerite," she said, "before we take another step — there's something I paid Sef that you need to know. And you're going to hate me for knowing it."
