## 15. Three Days Left

The rain had learned to fall sideways by the third morning, and Ada watched it through the window of the Halfway House as though it were a letter she could not bring herself to open.

Three days. The number sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. She could feel it in the thorns along her forearms, which had grown restless since Sef's back room — a low, itching hum beneath the skin, as if they too were counting down. Outside, Vhalen continued its slow forgetting. The market bell rang at odd hours for no one. A baker on the corner had stopped mid-loaf yesterday and stood holding his flour-dusted hands in front of him like a man who had found them in the road and did not know whose they were. By evening he was sweeping a shop that sold nothing, whistling a tune from a country that no longer existed, because to him it never had.

Ada knew how it ended.

That was the sentence she kept turning over, the way Marguerite turned his ledger-pen when he was thinking, the way Corvane must once have turned his warrant-seal. *I know how it ends.* She had known it since Sef's back room, since she had pressed one thorn-memory into his palm and walked out lighter than she had ever been, and the lightness had been the worst part — the terrible buoyancy of a woman who has set down something heavy and cannot pick it up again without telling everyone why it was heavy.

She had decided whose life the Door would take. She had decided it in the space of a heartbeat, before the memory was even fully gone, the way you decide to catch a falling child before your mind has finished forming the word *falling*.

And now there were three days left, and Marguerite was downstairs sharpening a knife that would be useless against what waited for them, and the ferryman was humming in the kitchen, and the twin sisters were arguing over which of them had dreamed the other, and Ada understood that she could not carry this alone any longer. Not because she was weak. Because carrying it alone was a kind of lie, and her thorns bloomed for lies, and she was so tired of bleeding.

She went down the stairs that creaked in the wrong places — stairs in an inn that existed on no road had earned their right to be strange — and found him at the long table by the fire, the map of erased places spread before him, weighted at the corners with a candlestick, a teacup, a horseshoe, and a stone that hummed faintly when touched. He looked up when she came in. He always looked up. That was the thing about Marguerite Amberdale: he had been exiled, erased, emptied of his own past, and still he looked up whenever she entered a room, as if she were weather worth watching.

"You've been quiet," he said. "Quieter than usual, even."

"I've been counting."

"Counting what?"

"The days." She pulled out the chair across from him and sat, and put her hands flat on the table between them, thorns first, so he could see them. They had come out overnight — small white blossoms along the green scars, five of them, six, seven, opening like tiny accusing mouths. "Marguerite. Look at them. I'm going to tell you something true, and I'd rather you see it happen than wonder later why my arms are bleeding."

He went very still. She had learned that stillness about him — the ledger-keeper's stillness, the stillness of a man who had spent years balancing accounts where the numbers moved when you weren't looking. "Ada."

"No. Let me say it all the way through, or I'll lose my nerve, and then we'll walk into the Door of Unmaking with me knowing and you not knowing, and that's its own kind of erasure, isn't it? Deciding things for people. The guild did it to you. The crown did it to Vhalen. I won't do it to you."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs, one of the twins laughed at something the other hadn't said yet.

"When we went to Sef," Ada said, "you think I paid him with a memory of tomorrow. A small one. What the market would look like, whether the ferryman would make his crossing. Something cheap." Her voice held. She made it hold. "It wasn't. I paid him with the end. I paid him with the last page. I gave away my memory of how this story ends, Marguerite — all of it — and I bought yours back with it, and I have regretted it every hour since, not because I want the memory but because you deserved to know, and I took that from you the same way everyone else has taken things from you."

Marguerite did not move. The candle flame leaned toward him as if listening.

"But here is what I remember *around* the hole," she went on. "You can't sell a memory whole. Sef said so. It comes out in pieces, and some pieces stick. So I know the shape of what I lost. I know the Door demands a life — I've known that since the halfway house, since the ferryman told us what happened to the last man who knocked. And I know—" her breath caught, and she pushed through it, thorns trembling, one blossom opening red at her wrist— "I know that in the ending I remember losing, someone walks through that Door willingly, and it isn't you. It was never going to be you. I decided it before I ever met you, maybe. Before the thorns. When I was still just Vhalen's memory wearing a girl's body, I think some part of me already knew what it would cost to put a country back together, and I already knew whose hands were steady enough to pay it."

"Ada." His voice came out rough. "Stop."

"No."

"*Stop.*" He was on his feet now, and the candles all leaned away from him, and for one moment she saw it — the storm under the ledger-keeper's skin, the thing the exile-oath had caged, pressing outward like weather against a window. "You sold the ending. You sold *the ending*, and you've been walking beside me for three days with that hole in you, deciding — *deciding* — that you know best. That your life is the price and mine is too precious to spend. Do you hear yourself? Do you hear what the guild used to say?"

That struck her like cold water. She stood too, slowly, thorns whispering against her sleeves.

"That's not fair."

"It's entirely fair," he said, "and it's cruel, and I'm sorry for both. But you don't get to do this. You don't get to trade yourself away in secret and call it love. I spent ten years as a line item in someone else's ledger, Ada. Ten years being *traded*. My exile for the guild's survival. My memories for their safety. My name for their silence. And now the person I—" he faltered, and the falter was worse than shouting— "the person I care most for in this world proposes to do exactly the same thing, only kinder. Only with better reasons. It's still a ledger. It's still a price paid on someone's behalf without their consent."

"And what would you have done?" she flared. "If you'd known? If you'd carried the ending instead of me? You'd have done the same thing! You'd have decided it was you! You'd have walked into that Door with your storm-gift and your guilt and your beautiful stupid nobility and you wouldn't have told me either!"

"Yes," he said simply. "Probably. Which is why I'm not angry that you decided. I'm angry that you decided *alone*."

The words landed in the room and stayed there. The fire settled. Upstairs, the twins had gone quiet, listening, the way everything in the Halfway House listened — the walls, the humming stone, the roads outside that surfaced only for the remembered.

Ada sat back down. Her legs simply declined to hold her anymore. The blossoms on her arms had opened fully now, seven white flowers, seven truths told, and they did not hurt the way lies made them hurt; they ached the way healing aches.

"Then tell me what you're proposing," she said. "Because there are three days left, and the country is coming apart at the seams, and I can feel it, Marguerite. In my thorns. Every hour, another street forgets itself. Yesterday the orchard road was just — gone. Not erased. *Unremembered.* There's a difference, and the difference is dying."

He sat across from her again. For a long moment he looked at his own hands, the hands that had called the storm, the hands that had balanced impossible ledgers, the hands that had burned the guild's books and freed ten thousand stolen memories into the rain.

"I'm proposing," he said slowly, "that we decide together. Whatever the Door asks. Whoever it takes. We walk in knowing, both of us, and neither of us carries it alone, and neither of us buys the other's future in a back room while they sleep." He looked up, and his eyes were wet, and he did not hide it. "I'm proposing that if the price is a life, then it's *our* life. Ours. Whatever shape that takes."

"There may not be a shape that takes," she whispered. "The ferryman said—"

"I know what the ferryman said."

"And Sef warned us. After we took the memory back. He said both endings are worse than we fear. He read it, Marguerite. He read my ending, and he looked at me like I was a grave he'd dug himself."

"Sef reads endings for a living," Marguerite said. "Of course they frighten him. But listen to me." He reached across the table and took her hand — the thorned one, the scarred one, the one that had bled for him in the vaults — and he held it the way you hold something you are afraid of breaking and more afraid of losing. "A prophecy is just a story that got written down too early. I've seen what happens when stories get rewritten. I burned the ledgers myself. So we rewrite this one. Together. Out loud. No secrets, no trades, no one waking lighter than they slept."

Ada laughed — a broken, wet sound, half sob. "You're a ledger-keeper proposing we falsify the accounts."

"I'm an exiled storm-caller proposing we *burn* the accounts," he said. "There's a difference. One is a crime. The other is a correction."

Outside, the sideways rain thickened, and far off — so far it might have been imagination, might have been the country dreaming — a bell rang a name. Not a note. A name. Two syllables, worn soft, spoken in bronze. Ada's thorns shivered, and she realized with a jolt that she recognized it: it was the name of the baker on the corner, the one who had forgotten his own hands. The country was calling its children home before the door closed. Or perhaps it was reading a roll, marking who remained, tallying what was left to save.

Three days. Maybe less. The prophecy did not negotiate, but then, neither, it seemed, did she.

"Say it plainly," Marguerite said. "Whatever you remember around the hole. All of it. Even the ugly parts. Especially those. If we're doing this together, I want every piece of you that stuck — every splinter of the ending you sold. I want to know what you're afraid of."

And Ada, who had lied to protect him and bled for it, who had traded tomorrow for his yesterday, who had carried the last page of the world alone through three days of falling-upward rain — Ada looked at the man she loved, and began, at last, to tell the truth.

"The Door doesn't just ask for a life," she said. "From what I can still feel of it — from the shape the memory left — it asks for a life *given freely, by the one who owes the debt.*" Her hand tightened in his. "And Marguerite — the debt is yours. The storm was yours. The erasure was yours. By every law the Door keeps, it should be you who pays."

His face went white as ledger-paper.

"So you see," she said softly, "why I couldn't let you decide alone. Because I already know what you'll choose. And I have three days to find a way to make the Door break its own rules — or to become a debt it cannot collect."

Far away, the bell rang again. This time it rang no name at all.

This time, it rang theirs.
