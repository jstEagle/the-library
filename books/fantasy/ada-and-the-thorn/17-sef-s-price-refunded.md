## 17. Sef's Price Refunded

The broker's shop did not exist until you needed it.

Ada had heard this said three times now — once by Marguerite, once by the ferryman with the borrowed face, and once, strangely, by her own thorns, which had whispered it against her scalp in the grey hour before dawn as they walked down through the drowned quarter of Vhalen. The rain still fell upward there, in slow silver threads that rose from the cobbles and vanished into a sky the color of old pewter. It made the streets look like they were weeping into heaven instead of being washed by it, and Ada found she could not stop watching, because every rising drop was one more small thing leaving the world.

Three days left. She carried the number like a stone behind her ribs.

Sef's shop stood — when it chose to stand — at the end of Rope-Walk Lane, where the houses leaned together like conspirators and the gutters ran with water that remembered being wine. It was narrow as a coffin and twice as deep, its frontage crowded with hanging signs: *Memories Bought. Memories Lent. Memories Restored, If You Can Prove They Were Ever Yours.* The last sign was newer than the others, the paint still tacky, and beneath it someone had added in smaller letters: *No refunds.*

Marguerite stopped at the threshold. In the upward rain his face looked hollowed out, the bones standing sharp beneath skin gone pale as ledger-paper. Three days of walking erased roads had worn him to something lean and burning, and Ada had learned to read him the way she read weather: the set of his shoulders meant guilt, the silence meant grief, and the way he kept touching the empty place at his hip where the prophecy book used to be meant he was afraid.

"You don't have to come in," she said.

"I sold my memories here." His voice was flat. "I should be the one to ask for them back."

"But you're not asking for yours." Ada pushed the door. "You're asking for mine."

* * *

Inside, the shop smelled of dust and lavender and something older underneath — the smell of attics, of things shut away so long they had begun to dream. Shelves rose on every wall, but they held no books. They held jars.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. Each jar glowed faintly with its own interior light: amber, blue, a bruised violet, a green like the first day of spring. And inside each jar, if you looked too long — Ada made herself look — something moved. A shape like a wedding. A shape like a funeral. A child's laughter, coiled at the bottom of the glass like a sleeping eel. A whole summer afternoon, pressed flat and pickled, turning slowly over and over.

"Mind the third shelf," said a voice from the dark at the back. "Those are jealous."

Sef emerged the way smoke leaves a doused candle: not walking so much as assembling, piece by piece, out of the shadows between the shelves. He was small and neat and ageless, with a bald head polished as an egg and eyes the color of tea left too long in the pot. His fingers were stained to the knuckle with ink of no color Ada could name — not black, not brown, but the shade of forgetting itself.

"The storm-caller," Sef said, inclining his head toward Marguerite without warmth. "And the girl who pays with her own future. I wondered when you'd come crawling back."

"We've come to buy back what she sold," Marguerite said. "Name your price."

"Oh, I don't think you understand the transaction." Sef drifted along the shelf, trailing one stained finger across the jars, and where it touched, the lights inside dimmed and flinched. "A memory isn't a coat you take off and put back on. When your girl sold me her ending, she didn't leave a hole. She left a *wound*. And wounds heal. They close over. They forget they were ever open." He turned, smiling with all the sincerity of a knife. "To give it back, I'd have to cut her open again. And I charge for the cutting."

"How much?" Ada asked.

Sef looked at her then, really looked, and something in his tea-colored eyes shifted — a flicker of something that might, in a better man, have been pity.

"That depends," he said softly, "on whether you want the truth with it."

* * *

He led them past the shelves into the back room, where a single lamp burned under a glass bell, and the light it gave was the light of a memory of lamplight — warmer than real, steadier than real, and somehow unbearably sad. There was a table there, and on the table, alone among all the thousands of jars in the shop, sat one jar of plain clear glass with nothing glowing inside it.

Empty. Or seeming so.

Ada's thorns knew it before she did. They stirred against her arms, blooming white and cold, the way they did when truth came near — and she understood, with a lurch, that the jar was not empty at all. It was full of something so quiet the eye slid off it. Her memory sat in that glass like a held breath.

"I read it," Sef said.

Marguerite went very still. "You *what*?"

"It's my trade, storm-caller. Do you imagine I buy futures to keep them pretty in jars? I bought a memory of how a story ends; of course I read it." Sef folded his stained hands. "I've read ten thousand endings. Most of them are foolish. Some of them are cruel. A very few are true." He paused. "Yours is all three."

"Then tell us," Ada said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her, because everything behind her ribs was shaking. "Tell us what happens."

"No."

The word dropped into the room like a stone into a well. Marguerite took a step forward, and the lamp-memory flickered, sensing anger. "You will tell us, broker. We have three days. The country is coming apart around us. People are forgetting their children's names mid-sentence. If her memory holds the way to—"

"It doesn't hold the way." For the first time, Sef raised his voice, and the jars on the walls all dimmed at once, a thousand memories flinching from him. "That is precisely what I am trying to tell you, you magnificent, doomed fool. Her memory does not hold the way. It holds the *cost*. And having read it, I am telling you — I, who have watched men trade their mothers' faces for a night of sleep, I who know every shape grief can be bottled into — do not take it back."

"Why?" Ada asked.

"Because knowing is not the same as surviving." Sef's voice dropped again, gentle now, and that gentleness was worse than any threat. "There are two endings in that jar, girl. Two doors, two prices, two griefs. In one of them, you lose him. In the other, he loses everything else — and you lose him anyway, differently, worse. I have read it four times hoping I misread it. I did not misread it." He looked at her, and his tea-colored eyes were ancient and tired. "You sold this memory to save him. Keeping it sold is the only mercy left in this shop. Walk away. Let the wound stay closed. Go to your Door ignorant, and let whatever happens happen *to* you instead of *inside* you."

The lamp under its bell burned steadily. Somewhere on the shelves, a jarred summer afternoon turned slowly over, over, over.

Ada looked at the plain glass jar. She thought about the inn on the road that existed nowhere, the ferryman who had forgotten his own river, the twin sisters sharing one name between them like a coat shared against cold. She thought about Marguerite in the counting-house, balancing accounts of bottled thunder, finding tonight's date written a century before he was born. She thought about the guildmaster unraveling like ink in water, saying *burn nothing until you understand*, and about Marguerite choosing, in the end, to burn.

She had spent her whole strange life being a thing that remembered what never happened. Thorns in her hair, thorns in her arms, thorns that bled when she lied. Everyone had told her what she was: cargo, omen, memory given flesh, the country's past wearing a girl's body. Everyone had decided for her what she could bear.

Sef was deciding now. Kindly. Carefully. Deciding for her.

"No," Ada said.

"Girl—"

"I'm not doing this because I'm brave." She reached for the jar. "I'm doing it because everyone keeps trying to protect me from my own life, and I am *tired*."

* * *

Sef did not stop her. That was the strange part, the part she would turn over later, lying awake under a sky that fell the wrong way. He could have — she saw his stained fingers twitch toward the shelf, saw some mechanism of the shop hold its breath — but he only stepped back, and watched, and let her lift the plain glass jar with both hands.

It was warm. That undid her more than anything. Not cold like the other jars, not humming with stored light, but warm as a living hand, warm as something that had been waiting to come home.

"There's a price," Sef said quietly. "There's always a price. The cutting costs. One honest memory of your choosing — something you'll feel the loss of. That's the fee for reopening the wound."

"Take the market," Ada said at once. "The morning my mother—"

"Not that one." Sef's voice cracked like a whip, and the lamp-memory guttered. "Not the ones you'd offer up cheaply, girl, thinking pain makes them fair payment. An honest memory. Something you *live in*. Something that, when it's gone, you'll reach for every day and find only smooth glass where it used to be."

Ada closed her eyes. She walked down the long corridor of herself, past the thorn-memories of futures never lived, past the erased orchards and the bells ringing names, and she looked for the thing she loved most that was truly hers — not Vhalen's, not the prophecy's, not the story's.

She found it. Of course she found it. It was the first thing she had ever been given.

"The night Marguerite found me at the guild door," she said. "Rain falling wrong. Him holding the lantern low so it wouldn't frighten me. Him saying—" her voice caught, steadied— "*you look like someone the weather owes an apology.* Take that one."

Behind her, she heard Marguerite make a small sound, half protest, half prayer. She did not turn around. If she turned around she would see his face, and she would keep the memory, and she would walk to the Door carrying comfort instead of courage, and comfort was not enough. Comfort had never unmade anything.

Sef uncorked the jar.

What came out was not smoke or light or sound. It was simply *absence* — a small, precise hole in the air, shaped exactly like a rainy night and a lowered lantern and a voice saying words she suddenly, desperately wanted to hear again before they were gone. It poured into her like cold water finding a crack, and somewhere inside her chest a door she hadn't known was open swung shut, and the night Marguerite found her became a smooth glass wall with nothing behind it.

She knew it had happened. She could not remember it. Both things were true, and both things hurt, and she stood very straight while they hurt.

"There," Sef said softly, and stoppered the empty jar. "It's done. The wound is open. The future is yours again." He hesitated — and then, in a voice stripped of all his broker's silk: "Last chance, girl. Leave it corked. Carry it to the Door unopened. Some knowledge is a stone, and stones drown."

Ada pulled the stopper.

The memory of the ending came home.

It didn't flood. It *settled* — the way snow settles, silent and total, covering everything. She saw the Door. She saw the price named. She saw the two endings, branching like a road splitting around a dead tree, and in one branch she lost him and in the other he lost everything and her either way, and she saw the exact moment, the exact word, the exact gesture that decided which branch the world would take — and it was so small, so terribly small, a single choice that either of them could make without knowing they were making it—

Her knees hit the floor. The thorns on her arms bloomed black as spilled ink, every one of them, a garden of midnight, because she was not lying, she was telling herself the whole truth at last, and the truth was worse than fear.

"Ada." Marguerite was kneeling beside her, hands hovering, afraid to touch. "Ada, what did you see?"

She lifted her head. Through the shop's grimy window, the upward rain climbed silver into the dark, three days' worth of drops leaving the world one by one.

"I saw how it ends," she said. "Both ways. And you're right, Sef — it's worse than we feared." She gripped Marguerite's wrist, hard, feeling his pulse hammering against her fingers like something trying to get out. "But I also saw the moment it turns. One moment. One choice. And it isn't mine to make."

She got to her feet, pulling him up with her, already moving toward the door, toward the drowned streets, toward the Door of Unmaking waiting at the heart of a country that was running out of time to exist.

"It's yours," she said. "And we have to get there before you make it without knowing."
