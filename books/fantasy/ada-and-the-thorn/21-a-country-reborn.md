## 21. A Country Reborn

The silence after the storm was not silence at all.

Ada stood in the doorway of the Door of Unmaking, and the silence was full of bells. Somewhere far off — or perhaps everywhere at once — a bell was ringing a name, and the name rang through the stone of the ruined city, through the wet grass pushing up between the cobbles, through her own ribs, where it settled like a bird finding its roost.

*Marguerite.*

Not her voice. Not the Door's. The country's.

She had heard bells ring names before, in the thorn-memories — a hundred futures she had never lived, all of them ending in this square, all of them ending with him standing where he stood now. But in none of those futures had the bells sounded like this: not triumphant, not mournful. Simply *glad*, the way a bell is glad when it has been silent a long time and is permitted, at last, to speak.

"Marguerite," she said aloud, because the bells had said it first and it seemed rude not to answer.

He turned. He was pale as paper left out in rain, and he swayed like a man who has set down a burden so heavy that his body has forgotten how to stand without it. But he was smiling. That was the thing she would remember later, when everything else had gone strange and soft — that he was smiling, and the smile was the smile of a man who has finally put down something he had carried so long he'd mistaken it for his own spine.

"Did it work?" he asked. His voice came out scraped thin. "I can't — I can't feel it. The storm. It's like reaching for a hand and finding my own."

Ada looked past him.

The Door was closing. Not slamming — closing the way a book closes when the last page has been read and the reader is satisfied. Its surface, which had been a wound in the world, a blackness with edges like torn paper, was becoming simply wood. Old wood, grey with weather, set into a wall that had no building around it. In a moment it would be a door in the way any door is a door: an invitation to walk through, and nothing more.

Beyond it, where the erasure had been, Vhalen was *happening*.

That was the only word she had for it. Streets were stitching themselves out of rumor — she could see it, actually see it, cobbles rising like bread, plaster spreading across timber frames like frost blooming on glass in reverse. An orchard unrolled itself down the hillside in a long green sigh, trees thickening from sapling to fruit-heavy in the space of a breath, apples dropping into grass that had never been mown. A river remembered itself into its banks. Chimneys began to smoke. Somewhere a dog barked, and the bark had the startled quality of a dog that has just remembered it exists.

"It worked," she said. And then, because her thorns were doing something she had never felt them do: "Marguerite. Look at my arms."

* * *

The thorns were falling.

Not all at once. They were letting go the way leaves let go in autumn — reluctantly, one by one, each with a small shiver of surrender — and where they dropped, they did not leave wounds. They left scars. Ordinary scars, pale and silvered, the kind a girl might carry from falling out of an orchard tree at nine years old, from a kitchen knife that slipped, from a life that had simply happened to her the way lives happen.

Ada turned her arms over in the grey morning light and watched a thorn the length of her forefinger loosen, tremble, and drop into the grass. Where it had grown out of the meat of her forearm there remained only a thin white line, curved like a question mark.

She had worn the thorns so long she had forgotten they were not her.

They had come when she was small — she had never learned exactly when, only that she woke one morning with them budding along her arms like the world's most terrible roses, and that they bloomed when she lied, and bled when she lied to protect someone, and that no healer in the guild's employ had ever been able to say what they were. *A curse,* some had said. *A blessing,* others, less usefully. *Cargo of unusual quality,* the guildmaster had said, which was the most honest of the three.

But she knew now. She had known since the Halfway House of Forgotten Roads, since the ferryman with half a face and the twin sisters sharing one name between them, since the terrible arithmetic of that night: *she is Vhalen's memory given flesh.* The country had been erased — from maps, from minds, from the tongues of everyone who had ever spoken of it — and something of it had refused to go. Something had rooted itself in a child and grown outward, thorn by thorn, every lie she told a small act of rebellion against a world that had decided to forget.

And now the world had decided to remember. And the thorns, their work finished, were letting go of her the way a mother lets go of a hand on the first day of school.

"Does it hurt?" Marguerite asked. He had come to stand beside her, and his hand hovered near her shoulder without landing, the way it always did, as if he were asking permission of the air.

"No," she said, and the thorns did not bleed, because it was true. "It feels like — do you know the feeling when you take off boots you've worn all day? Like the ground is suddenly very close and very strange?"

"I haven't worn boots in a year," he said. "Guild ledger-keepers go barefoot in the counting-house. It's in the rules. Chapter nine, probably."

"You're making that up."

"I am," he admitted, and she laughed, and the laugh came out of her sounding like someone else's — someone lighter, someone who had never carried a country in her skin. A last thorn, small as a fingernail, dropped from the back of her wrist. She watched it fall and did not pick it up.

* * *

They walked down into the city as it rebuilt itself around them.

It was like walking through the inside of a story being told well. Ada had spent her whole life among the guild's impossible inventory — bottled thunder, borrowed years, borders folded like linen — and she had thought she understood what impossibility looked like. She had been wrong. Impossibility, it turned out, looked like an old woman opening a shutter and leaning out to beat a rug, and the rug was there, and the shutter was there, and neither of them seemed in the least surprised to exist.

The people were coming back too. Not arriving — *remembering themselves*. They condensed out of the morning like dew reversing, a woman at a well, a boy chasing a hoop down a street that was still deciding whether to be cobbled or packed earth, a man in a ferryman's coat who looked up as they passed and touched his hat, and his face was whole, and Ada stopped dead in the street because she knew that face.

"You," she said. "From the Halfway House. You were — you were only half —"

"Ferryman of the Vell," he said, and his voice was whole too, both halves of it, and he smiled with a mouth that had been a suggestion for a hundred years. "I remember the inn. I remember you, thorn-girl. You sat by the fire and wouldn't touch the stew." His eyes went past her to Marguerite, and something moved through his face — not anger, not quite. Recognition, deep as a well. "And you're the storm-caller."

Marguerite went very still. "You know me."

"We all know you," the ferryman said, simply, and Ada's heart turned over, because she heard it — heard what the sentence carried, and what it didn't. "The whole country knows you. The bells have been ringing your name since dawn. *Marguerite Amberdale, who called the storm, who paid the price, who gave the country back its own name.*" He said it the way a man recites something he has known since childhood, a catechism, a street name, the words of a song every child learns wrong and loves anyway.

"Then—" Marguerite's voice cracked on the word. Ada saw his hands flex, the way they did when he was reaching for the storm and finding only air. "Then it's over. The exile. The erasure. I'm — I'm *remembered*."

The ferryman looked at him for a long moment. And then the look changed, and Ada saw it change, and she understood what he was going to say a half-second before he said it, the way she had always understood things half a second before they happened — the thorn-gift, or whatever it had been, giving her one last courtesy on its way out.

"Am I wrong?" Marguerite said. "Tell me I'm not wrong."

"You're not wrong," the ferryman said gently. "The country knows your name. It rang from every bell this morning." He paused. "But ask yourself, storm-caller — do the bells ring it the way they ring a neighbor's name? Or the way they ring a name in a story?"

* * *

They found out at the well.

The woman there was drawing water, and her bucket was real, and her shawl was real, and when Ada asked her — casually, too casually, the way you ask when you already dread the answer — what she knew of the man who had saved Vhalen, the woman smiled and told the story beautifully.

She told it *as a story*.

*Marguerite Amberdale, the storm-caller, who loved this country enough to break it and loved it more to mend it.* The woman's hands moved as she spoke, shaping the tale, and around her other people gathered the way people gather for stories in markets, and their faces had the soft shine of faces hearing a beloved legend. They knew every turn of it. The rebels in the hills. The crown's terrible answer. The guild's pen. The Door. The gift poured out. They knew it the way Ada's thorn-memories had known the future — completely, and from the outside.

"Ask them my mother's name," Marguerite whispered to Ada. His face had gone the grey of old paper. "Ask them what street I grew up on. Ask them anything *small*."

Ada took his hand — the first time she had ever done it, and she would think about that later, in the middle of everything else — and asked the woman, sweetly, where the storm-caller had been born.

The woman's smile widened, warm and untroubled. "Why, in the story, of course. In the telling. Where all the best people are born."

* * *

He sat down on the steps of a fountain that had remembered itself into being not an hour before, and Ada sat beside him, and the city went on rebuilding itself around them with the cheerful industry of a place that had no idea it had just broken a man's heart.

"The Door," he said at last. "It took more than the storm."

"Yes."

"I thought — when it said the price, when it said *a storm-caller's gift, freely surrendered* — I thought it meant the weather in me. The calling. And it did. That's gone, I can feel the hole where it was, it aches like a pulled tooth." He turned his hands over, looked at them as if they belonged to a stranger. "But the gift was never just the storm, was it. The gift was that I *belonged* to the weather, and the weather belonged to this country, and so I belonged to it too. That was the thread. That was how I was held in the world. And when I poured it out—"

"The Door kept the thread," Ada said. "It unmade the erasure from within. It needed the part of you that was *in* Vhalen. So it kept that part. And the rest of you—" she gestured at the market, at the woman at the well, at the children who were already, horribly, beginning to skip rope to a rhyme with his name in it — "the rest of you is a story now. A good one. Everyone will know you forever."

"Forever," he said bitterly, "and never."

She had seen him in despair before — in the counting-house, the night he found the date; in the vaults, when the memories came back and he learned what he had done. But this was different. This was despair wearing gratitude's clothing, because he *had* saved them, and the country *was* reborn, and the bells *were* ringing, and every single thing he had paid for had been bought. He was not allowed to grieve. He had gotten exactly what he wanted, and what he wanted had turned out to be a country that would love him the way it loved its own legends: loudly, gratefully, and at a distance no living man could cross.

"You could stay," Ada said. "Invisible. A ghost at your own festival. Every year on the anniversary they'd ring the bells and you could stand in the square and no one would see you."

"Charming."

"Or you could walk out." She said it steadily, though the words scraped her throat like thorns. "Through the guild's plain front door, or the ferryman's boat, or any road at all. Out of Vhalen, into the places that don't know the story. Where you're not a legend. Where you're just—" she hesitated, "—just a man with no gift and no name and no country, starting over."

"Thank you," he said dryly. "You've made exile sound *irresistible*."

"Marguerite." She turned to face him fully, and took both his hands, and made him look at her. "I spent my whole life as a country's memory wearing a girl's skin. I know what it is to be a thing everyone knows and no one *sees*. It's not living. It's being a page that other people read." Her voice dropped. "You told me once — the night we stole the map, in the rain that fell upward — you said the only thing worse than being erased was erasing yourself to fit a story someone else was telling. You don't get to forget you said that just because this time the story is *kind*."

He looked at her for a long time. The bells had stopped ringing his name; in the silence, the city made its small rebuilding sounds, hammer and trowel and the splash of the remembered fountain, and somewhere a child's skipping rhyme came and went on the wind like weather.

"The prophecy," he said slowly. "The book in the counting-house. Every entry after tonight ended in a country that no longer exists. But Vhalen exists now. Which means the prophecy is wrong. Or finished. Or—"

"Or unwritten," Ada said. "From tonight forward. No page. No ending. No one — not the guild, not the Door, not the thorns, not me — knows what happens next."

Something moved in his face then that she had never seen there: not hope exactly, hope being too grand a word for it. More like the first loosening of a knot pulled tight for years. The look of a man standing at the edge of a map that has, at last, run out.

"There's a road out of the city," he said. "The west road. It goes through country that was never Vhalen and never will be. No bells. No stories. No one who knows my name." He swallowed. "It's a long road."

"I know," Ada said. "I've seen it."

He looked at her sharply. "You've — Ada. What did you pay Sef? What was the memory you sold him? What did you see?"

And Ada, whose thorns were gone, whose scars were only scars, whose body had finally stopped keeping score of her lies, opened her mouth to answer — and found, for the first time in her life, that the truth was the harder thing to say.

"The west road," she said. "I saw the west road. And Marguerite — I saw who was walking it with me. And I saw that we don't both reach the end."

The fountain splashed. The child's rhyme came back on the wind, faint and cheerful, a name in it that no longer belonged to anyone.

"When?" Marguerite asked quietly. "When do we not reach the end?"

Ada looked west, where the road ran out of the city through the orchard of impossible apples, and the morning light lay on it like an unwritten page.

"Tomorrow," she said.
