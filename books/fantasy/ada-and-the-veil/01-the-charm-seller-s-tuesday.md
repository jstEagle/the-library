## 1. The Charm-Seller's Tuesday

There is a particular kind of peace that comes from being nobody, and I had spent twelve years perfecting it the way other men perfect swordsmanship or wine-making or lying to their mothers. My version required a folding table, a strip of faded blue canvas, and forty-one luck-knots arranged in rows so precise that even the most suspicious Ministry clerk would see only what Greyhollow wanted to see: a tired man selling string to superstitious fools.

Greyhollow was a city that had done magic the courtesy of dying quietly. No ley-lines sang beneath its cobbles. No hedge-witches kept cats that argued back. The Ministry of Unmaking had swept through a century ago, burned what wouldn't bend, and left behind a population so aggressively mundane that they'd made dullness a civic virtue. It was, in other words, exactly the sort of place an exiled storm-caller might hide — provided he hid well.

I hid excellently. I want that on the record.

My stall sat at the mouth of Tanner's Row, where the evening market gathered like sediment: the pie-wife with her steam-crusted trays, the knife-grinder who charged by the argument rather than the blade, old Marta selling candles she swore were blessed, though by whom remained a theological question she deflected with impressive skill. I'd been among them long enough that they'd stopped seeing me. That's the trick of invisibility — not concealment, but repetition. Be so reliably unremarkable that the eye slides off you like rain off oiled leather.

"Evening, Halloran." The pie-wife again, nodding at my table without looking. "Cold one coming."

"Cold ones usually do," I said, which was the kind of wisdom people expect from charm-sellers and the kind I could deliver all night without effort.

The truth was more complicated, as truths tend to be. Cold *was* coming. I could feel it in my teeth, in the faint static itch along my forearms where the storm used to live before I'd buried it under twelve years of wards and small commerce. Weather-sense is the last magic to go, they say. They say a lot of things, "they," those great authorities on the lives of ruined men.

I was re-threading a knot — the seventh knot of the third row had gone crooked, and crooked knots sell badly to people who believe crooked knots invite crooked luck — when she arrived.

You can always tell the customers who are trouble. Not by their faces. Trouble wears every face eventually. You tell by how they look at the goods. Honest buyers glance. Hopeful buyers linger. But trouble *reads*, the way a locksmith reads a door, cataloguing mechanisms, weighing what's inside against the cost of getting there.

This one was young, hooded despite the mild evening, with ink-stained fingers and the restless eyes of someone who'd climbed through a window recently and intended to climb through another one soon. She drifted past the candle-stall, past the knives, and stopped at my table with the deliberate casualness of a cat pretending it wasn't stalking anything.

"Luck-knots," she said, reading the sign I hadn't needed in six years.

"That's what the sign says. I find repetition builds trust."

She smiled like she was filing the smile away for later use. "Which one's strongest?"

"They're all equally lucky. Different flavors of it." I tapped the rows. "Red thread for fortune in money matters. Blue for safe travel. Green for—" I hesitated a half-second too long, and hated myself for it. "Green for finding lost things."

Her eyes came up sharp as a pick. "Lost things."

"Keys. Rings. Relatives who owe you money." I held out a green knot between two fingers, keeping my pulse bored. "Three coppers."

She didn't take it. She looked at it the way the locksmith looks at the door, and something in my chest began, very quietly, to knock.

"You know," she said, "there's a story going around the archive district. About a prophet who died up on Vellum Hill. Old Corvane. His house finally got sold, and the new owners found a cellar behind a false wall, full of books nobody's read in a hundred years."

"Fascinating. Three coppers."

"One of the books is a ledger of prophecies. Dates and names." She picked up the green knot now, turned it over, let it dangle. "And tonight's date is in it. With a name beside it."

The market noise continued around us — the knife-grinder's wheel singing, Marta hawking her blessed-and-legally-distinct-from-blessed candles — but for me the sound went distant, the way it does when lightning is deciding whether to strike. I kept my hands flat on the table because hands that tremble have opinions, and mine had been trained into stillness by men far less patient than the Ministry.

"Whose name?" I asked, and made it sound like idle curiosity, the question of a man humoring a madwoman to move her along.

"I don't know yet. The page's been torn out." She set a copper down, then a second, then a third, precisely, like punctuation. "But whoever tore it left a shadow. Ink transfers, if you know the trick. By tomorrow I'll have the impression of whatever was written there." She pocketed the knot. "Funny thing about prophecies. They're never wrong. Just early."

She walked off into the crowd, and I watched her go with the expression of a man watching his own funeral procession take a wrong turn.

* * *

I closed the stall early, which Marta noted aloud, because Marta noted everything aloud; discretion in that woman would have been a genuine miracle requiring papal investigation. I told her I felt a headache coming. This was truer than she knew. Headaches are what storms leave behind when they've been locked in a cellar too long.

My rooms above the chandler's shop on Wick Lane were exactly what a charm-seller's rooms should be: narrow, tidy, smelling faintly of tallow and dried lavender, decorated with the aggressive blandness of a man who has nothing to hide. Underneath the blandness — under the floorboard beneath the bed beneath the false bottom of the trunk beneath the winter coat I never wore — were the things I did have to hide. But we'll come to those. Or rather, we won't, if I have any say in it, which historically I have not.

First, the wards.

Every hiding place needs a heartbeat, something that tells you it's alive. Mine was woven into the window frame, the door lintel, and the chimney — three threads of storm-light spun thin as spider silk and dimmed down to almost nothing, humming a note only I could hear. For twelve years that note had been steady as a monk's chanting. Tonight, when I pressed my palm to the window frame and listened—

The note stuttered.

Not broke. Stuttered. Like a singer losing the beat, catching it, losing it again. I stood there in my narrow tidy room with my hand on the wood and felt the hair rise on my arms, and not from cold, though the cold was coming too, rolling down off the mountains the way I'd promised the pie-wife.

Wards don't fail on their own. Wards are like promises: they only break when something pushes against them.

I checked the others. Door lintel: stuttering. Chimney: stuttering worse, a flutter like a moth caught in a jar. Three points of failure, evenly spaced, moving inward. Something was walking a slow circle around my life, touching the edges of it, measuring.

Here is what I did not do: panic. Panic is a luxury for people whose secrets are survivable. Here is what I did instead: I lit a lamp, sat at my table, and took stock the way you take stock when the ship is listing — calmly, thoroughly, and with one eye on the boats.

Assets: forty remaining luck-knots, none of them containing actual luck. A false identity twelve years deep and weatherproof as slate. A face the city had forgotten how to see. Liabilities: a name in a century-old book, a date that was today, a thief with ink-stained fingers who'd just told me to my face that she was coming for the truth by morning, and a set of wards failing in a pattern I recognized.

That was the worst part. The pattern. Whoever circled my rooms wasn't sweeping the city at random. They were hunting the way I would hunt — patiently, geometrically, closing a net one knot at a time. And there are perhaps four people alive who hunt magic that way, because there are perhaps four people alive who learned it in the same school I did, in the court beneath the mountain, before everything burned and everyone scattered and I sold the only thing I had left worth selling.

I poured myself the last finger of plum brandy and didn't drink it. Some nights the brandy is for holding, not drinking, and this was shaping up to be one of those.

* * *

The vision came at midnight, and it came through the customer.

That sounds mad. Let me explain, because explaining is the last dignity available to a man in my position. Luck-knots are not entirely fraud. There's no luck in them — I'm a storm-caller, not a miracle-worker, and even at my height I couldn't conjure fortune out of red thread. But thread holds intention the way wax holds a seal, and when you tie ten thousand knots over twelve years, some of your attention soaks into the work. My knots carried a trace of me. A fingerprint in fiber. Harmless. Invisible. I'd checked a thousand times.

The customer came near closing — a broad, sweating man with a carter's shoulders and the desperate politeness of someone about to ask for something enormous. He bought a blue knot for his daughter, who was traveling to the coast, and when his fingers closed around the thread, the fingerprint in the fiber touched whatever he was carrying, and the vision hit me like a wave hitting a seawall built by an optimist.

Storm-light. Everywhere. Not greyhollow-grey, not the polite drizzle-light of a magic-dead city, but the real thing, the mountain thing, violet-white and vertical, pillars of it marching across a sky the color of a bruise. Beneath the light, an army — I knew it was an army by its silence, armies being loudest when they stop pretending to be anything else. Banners I recognized from a hundred years ago and from a future I'd hoped never to see. And in the middle distance, walking toward the light with her hood down and her ink-stained fingers bare, the thief from my stall, looking directly at me across a distance that was not measured in miles.

Then it was gone, and the carter was saying, "—three coppers, right? Sir? You've gone pale as milk."

"Fine knot," I heard myself say. "For safe travel. Tell your daughter to mind the tide tables."

He left pleased. Customers always leave pleased; it's the merchant's curse. I stood alone in the shuttered dark of Tanner's Row with my heart slamming like a shutter in wind, and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the thief walked away.

A torn page. Today's date. A failing ward-circle drawn by a hand I knew. An army in storm-light wearing century-old banners, marching on free cities that still believed themselves safe. And a memory-thief who'd looked at my little table of string and seen, somehow, a door.

Twelve years ago I had made a bargain in the dark beneath the mountain, and I had paid for it with the one currency that cannot be counterfeited, and I had told myself — every day, twelve years of days — that the debt was settled, sealed, buried deeper than the false floorboards, deeper than the winter coat, deeper than the man I used to be.

Tonight the wards stuttered. Tonight a dead prophet's ledger named a storm-caller. Tonight the storm itself reached through a carter's purchase and showed me its face.

I blew out the lamp and sat in the dark and listened to my heartbeat slow, and somewhere out beyond Wick Lane, beyond the sleeping, smugly ordinary sprawl of Greyhollow, something finished drawing its circle and began, softly, to pull it tight.

Tomorrow, the thief would have her ink impression. Tomorrow, she'd come back to my stall with a name.

I already knew whose it would be.

It was, after all, written in the book. And prophecies, as the lady said, are never wrong.

Just early.
