## 2. A Date Written in Dust

There are three rules to thieving memories, and I learned all of them the hard way, which is the only way anyone learns anything worth knowing.

One: never take a memory from someone who's looking at you. The eyes remember being watched even when the mind forgets what it saw, and you'll spend the rest of your short career wondering which door that flinch in the mirror came from.

Two: never take a memory you can't put down. Grief, love, the taste of a specific soup on a specific winter night—some things stick to your fingers like pitch, and no amount of scrubbing gets them clean.

Three: never, ever work a job on a day that already knows your name.

I broke rule three on a Tuesday, and I'd like it noted for the record that I didn't know I was breaking it. Ignorance is the whole architecture of rule-breaking. You can't be blamed for walking into a trap you didn't know was baited.

Though I'll admit—sitting in that cellar with the dust settling around me like the world's oldest snowfall—I did feel, for one long and deeply insulting moment, that the trap had known *me* first.

* * *

The archive belonged to a dead prophet, which sounds dramatic and was, in fact, dramatic, but not in the way you'd think. Prophets in Greyhollow die like everyone else: badly, alone, and owing rent. This one—Maren Voss, if the faded brass plate outside her townhouse was to be believed, and brass plates are the only honest historians left—had been dead eleven years, and her house had been sealed by the Ministry of Unmaking for ten of them.

The Ministry seals things the way a miser seals letters: thoroughly, joylessly, and with an eye toward the day they'll need to be opened again. Their wax carries a charm that tastes the air for magic. Bring anything live into a sealed room and the wax wakes up, screams to its masters, and you spend the rest of your life explaining yourself to people whose job title contains the word *Unmaking*.

Which is why I don't steal magic. I steal memories. Memories are the one thing in the world that's completely inert until someone remembers them, and dead prophets, bless their moldy hearts, remember nothing at all.

The lock on Voss's cellar door was a joke—a three-tumbler with a rusted spring, the kind of lock you pick with a hairpin and a mild sense of insult. The real security was the seal on the doorframe, a ribbon of grey wax stamped with the Ministry's eye. I'd spent three evenings watching the house from the widow's loft across the lane, and I'd watched a rat cross the threshold on the second night without the wax so much as twitching. Dead house. Dead magic. Dead prophet.

The wax let me step over it the way a sleeping dog lets you step over it—grudgingly, with one eye half-open, deciding I wasn't worth the trouble.

The stairs went down into a dark that had been undisturbed so long it had developed opinions. My lantern found shelves first: row after row of ledgers, journals, bundles of letters tied with string gone soft as cloth. Prophet's paperwork. The unglamorous sediment of a life spent seeing the future and still having to buy turnips.

I wasn't there for the prophecy. Let me be clear about that, because the record matters to me and the record is the only thing I own outright. I was there for the *memories*. Maren Voss had been a famous woman once—famous enough that people paid her to remember things for them, famous enough that when she died, her clients' secrets died with her. Or so everyone believed. But memories leave residue. They soak into the things around them the way lamp-oil soaks into a table, and a memory-thief with a good kit and a patient hand can press a page, a chair, a ring of keys, and pull up what the owner left behind.

Dead prophets' residue is worth more than live merchants'. A living man's memory can be contested. A dead woman's is provenance itself.

I set up my kit on her worktable—a slate slab scarred with candle burns and, I noticed with professional appreciation, at least one scorch mark in the shape of a hand, as if someone had slammed a burning palm down mid-vision and kept writing anyway. I pressed my first sheet of impression-ink against the spine of a ledger near the top shelf. Nothing. The second. Nothing. The third gave me a flicker: a woman's voice, dry as the dust, saying *no, that's not the shape of it, start again.*

Prophets. Even their leftovers were bossy.

I worked the room for an hour, and the room worked me right back. That's the thing about cellars—they hold the cold the way a miser holds coin, and they give it back to you all at once, with interest. By the time I'd worked through two shelves my fingers had gone clumsy and my breath had started showing, and I was telling myself the same lie I always tell myself on jobs like this: *one more shelf, then out, then hot wine, then sleep in a bed I paid for with someone else's forgotten afternoon.*

That's when I found the ledger with my face in it.

* * *

It wasn't on the prophecy shelf. That's what I want on the record too. It was on the *accounts* shelf, wedged between a grocer's bill and a bundle of letters from a woman in the free city of Vail whose handwriting got progressively drunker over the years. A plain ledger, leather gone the color of weak tea, no title on the spine. I pressed it almost as an afterthought, the way you check a coat pocket you're sure is empty.

The impression came up hot.

Not warm. *Hot.* My ink sheet curled at the edges like a leaf in a fire, and for a heartbeat I was somewhere else entirely—rain on stone, a taste like copper and ozone, the feeling of standing too close to a door that opens onto weather. Then it settled, and I was looking at a page in Maren Voss's own handwriting, except the impression doesn't show you pages, it shows you *moments*, and the moment was this: Maren Voss, alive, quill moving, writing words I could not read in the impression but whose *weight* I could feel, the way you feel a stone in your pocket without looking at it.

I put the sheet down. My hands were not entirely steady, and I noted that with the same detached interest I'd give a stranger's symptom.

Then I opened the ledger with my eyes instead of my ink, because some things you have to read the slow way.

The early pages were accounts. Tallow. Ink. A debt to a chandler that Maren had clearly been avoiding with the dedication she brought to everything. I flipped forward, past the middle years where the accounts gave way to something else—entries that weren't accounts at all, just dates, and beside each date a few words in her cramped, furious hand.

*Third of Frostmonth. The cart. The grey-eyed man lies about the rain.*

*Last of Emberfall. The door in the mountain remembers who knocked.*

*Midwinter. The thief counts what she stole and finds one thing too many.*

I read those and felt the specific chill of a professional recognizing a colleague's work in an unexpected place. *The thief.* Fine. Prophets collect thieves the way gutters collect rain; we're everywhere, we're useful, we're forgettable.

Then I turned the page and found Tuesday.

Not *Tuesday*. She'd written a date. A day, a month, a year—the year being this one, the month being this one, the day being, and I counted twice because the first count felt like a misprint, *today*.

Beside the date, two words: *the storm-caller.*

And beneath the words, a sketch.

Now. I have been described in police broadsheets. I have been described, less flatteringly, in the private correspondence of three separate magistrates. I know what my face looks like rendered by strangers: usually the nose is wrong, sometimes the mouth, and the eyes are always either too fierce or not fierce enough, depending on how much the artist disliked being robbed.

This sketch was not wrong anywhere.

It was me. Not a likeness of me—*me*. The set of my jaw, the scar through my left eyebrow that I got falling out of a window in Vail at nineteen, the way my hair does that thing at the temple that I have spent years and a small fortune in hairpins pretending it doesn't do. Maren Voss had drawn my face with the confidence of someone sketching a person sitting across the table from her, and she had been dead for eleven years, and the paper was older than that, and the ink had gone brown in a way that ink only goes when it has had a very long time to be brown.

I sat down on the worktable's stool. It wobbled. I noticed it wobble. I noticed everything with the horrible clarity of a person whose mind is trying very hard to be busy so it doesn't have to be *afraid*.

Here is what my training said, in the flat professional voice I keep for exactly these occasions: impressions can be faked. Ledgers can be forged. A clever forger with access to my face—wanted notices, a magistrate's portrait, the broadsheets—could produce this. The date was the hard part, but dates can be guessed; prophets who name dates are wrong constantly, and the wrong ones get remembered precisely because being wrong is rare enough to be news.

Here is what the rest of me said, in no voice at all, just a cold spreading feeling under the sternum like water finding the low places in a floor:

*She drew the scar.*

Nobody copies a scar that accurately from a broadsheet. Broadshets flatter. This didn't flatter. This was done by someone who had *looked*.

I did what any sensible thief does when the job goes sideways. I stole it.

The ledger went into my satchel, along with three bundles of letters I grabbed at random for cover—prophets' correspondence makes excellent reading and better insulation against the suspicion that you went into a sealed archive and took only one thing. I pressed one last impression sheet against the worktable's scorched handprint, on the theory that a woman who slammed burning palms into her desk mid-vision probably left something interesting in the wood.

Then I climbed the stairs, stepped over the sleeping wax, and let myself out into the Greyhollow evening, which was doing its usual trick of pretending to be an ordinary city in an ordinary world where faces do not appear in dead women's ledgers on the days they're dated.

* * *

I read the rest of the ledger in my rooms above the chandler's on Wick Lane, with the shutters closed and the lamp turned low, and the more I read the less the professional voice had to say and the more the cold water under my sternum had to say instead.

The entries clustered. That was the first thing. They weren't scattered across forty years of a prophet's life; they bunched, the way storm-clouds bunch, in the last two years of her writing. And they circled. Around a mountain—*the door in the mountain remembers who knocked*—around a court, around a man she called by a dozen names and never the same one twice: *the grey-eyed man, the liar about the rain, the one who sold weather, the storm-caller.*

And around me.

She never called me a thief in the late entries. She called me *the vessel*. She wrote it the way you'd write a diagnosis. *The vessel carries what was sold. The vessel does not know. The vessel must not know until the mountain, or the knowing will spoil the walking.*

I read that sentence four times. *The vessel carries what was sold.*

Sold. Not stolen. I want to be very precise about the difference, because my whole trade lives in that difference. Stolen things are taken by force or craft from someone who wanted to keep them. Sold things are given up willingly, for a price, by someone who had the right to give them. I have stolen hundreds of memories. I have never once stolen a soul. I wouldn't know how. I'm not sure souls *can* be stolen—I'd always assumed they were like virtue or a good reputation, things you could only lose by giving them away.

So whatever the vessel carried, it hadn't been taken from it.

It had been *sold to it*.

By someone.

I got up and walked around my room, which is small enough that walking around it is less exercise than pacing, and I made myself think like a professional instead of a person. A professional would ask: what does a prophecy actually do to you? Nothing, on its own. Words in a dead woman's ledger have no more power over my Tuesday than words in a grocer's bill. The danger isn't the words. The danger is the *other people* who might believe the words, and what they'd do to the woman in the sketch to make the words come true.

A dead prophet's archive, sealed by the Ministry, holding a prophecy naming a date and a face. If the Ministry believed it, they'd come for me with writs and iron and that polite terrible courtesy they use right before the Unmaking. If anyone *else* believed it—anyone with an army, say, or a throne, or a grudge a century old—they'd come for me with worse.

And if the prophecy named a storm-caller on the same date it named my face, then somewhere in this city of smugly magic-free ordinary Tuesdays, there was a man who called storms, and he and I were now, whether I liked it or not, a matched set.

I sat back down. I turned to the last written page, the final entry, and I'll copy it here because I have it by heart anyway and writing it out is the only way I know to make a thing lose a little of its weight:

*The storm-caller comes to the market with his lies in his pockets and his truth in his skin. The vessel comes to the archive with her face already on the page. They will find each other on the day I wrote at the top of this leaf, and they will hate it, and they will do it anyway.*

*After that, everything is weather.*

*The vessel should check her own house. The vessel should count what she carries. One thing in her was never hers, and it is heavier than she thinks, and it is patient, and it is waiting for the mountain.*

I put the ledger down very carefully, the way you put down a sleeping animal you've suddenly realized might be something else entirely.

*The vessel should count what she carries.*

I want to tell you that I laughed. I want to tell you I did the sensible thing—burned the ledger, changed my name, took the coach to Vail and a new life stealing memories from people whose faces weren't in dead women's books. That's what the professional voice recommended, right up until it ran out of arguments and went quiet.

Instead I did the thing I do when a memory doesn't sit right. I went looking in my own head.

Every thief audits her own stock. It's rule two's ugly little cousin: know what you're carrying, because the things you carry carry you. I have a map of my own mind the way cartographers have maps of coastlines—rough in the interior, detailed at the edges where I do my work. I know my memories like a jeweler knows her tray. The window in Vail. My mother's kitchen and the specific rattle of its door. The first face I ever took, a customs officer's, and how sick I was after. The soup. The winters. The jobs.

I walked the map, shelf by shelf, the way I'd walked Maren Voss's cellar.

Everything was where I'd left it.

Except.

There was a door.

I want to describe it properly, because the describing matters, and because putting it into words is the closest I can come to making it behave. At the back of my own mind—behind the kitchen, behind the winters, behind all the stock I'd taken and catalogued and owned—there was a door I had never built. Not a locked memory; I know what those feel like, I've built enough of them, they're *mine*, they have my fingerprints in the wax. This door was older than my work. It was *before* my work. It was there the way the ground is there, underneath everything, humming.

Humming. That's the word my mind offered and I'm sticking with it. The door hummed, low and constant, a note I felt in my teeth more than heard, and the note was warm the way a body is warm, and it was *light*—I don't know how else to say it—the way storm-light is light, that green-white color the sky goes in the last second before the world breaks open.

I did not open it.

I want that on the record too. I stood in my own head, on my own map, in front of a door I had never made, humming with a light I had no name for, and every instinct I have—and my instincts are the best-paid instincts in Greyhollow—every one of them said *not yet, not alone, not in the dark.*

So I came back up. I opened my eyes. The lamp had burned low and the chandler's cart rattled past outside on its early round, and the world went on being an ordinary city on an ordinary morning, the lie so thick and comfortable you could spread it on bread.

I took out the ledger and looked at the sketch of my face one more time. Eleven years dead, and she'd drawn me looking *tired*, looking like a woman at the end of a long day who has just found out the day isn't over. I resented how accurate it was.

*They will find each other on the day I wrote at the top of this leaf, and they will hate it, and they will do it anyway.*

The storm-caller. Selling something at a market, she'd written. Lies in his pockets, truth in his skin.

Greyhollow had one market worth the name, and it opened at dawn, and it was already open.

I put the ledger in my satchel, and the impression sheets in my coat, and the hairpins in my hair, and I went down the stairs past the chandler's boy who tipped his cap to me like every other ordinary morning, and I walked out into the street to go find a man I had never met, whose name I did not know, whose face was not on the page—

—and I hated it.

And I did it anyway.
