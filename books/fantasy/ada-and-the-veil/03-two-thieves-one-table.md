## 3. Two Thieves, One Table

*Ada*

The charm-seller's stall was exactly the kind of place a storm-caller would hide, if storm-callers were stupid.

It sat at the crooked end of Tallow Row, wedged between a candle-dipper who watered his wax and a pie-shop that had been "closing soon" for eleven years. The stall itself was a marvel of deliberate shabbiness: a striped awning gone grey as old dishwater, a folding table scarred with ring-stains from a hundred mugs he'd never drunk, and a hand-painted sign reading *FAIRWEATHER CHARMS — LUCK KNOTS, WARDING THREADS, LOVE TOKENS (RESULTS NOT GUARANTEED)*.

Results not guaranteed. That was the genius of it. A real hedge-witch would promise you the moon and charge you for it. This man promised nothing, which meant either he was honest or he was very, very good.

I'd spent two days watching him before I approached. I'd learned his rhythms the way I learned everything—by taking them. Tuesday evenings he sold to servants heading home with their masters' coin-strings. He joked with the candle-dipper's boy. He overcharged the rich widow on the corner by exactly enough to be forgettable, not enough to be resented. And every time someone brushed too close to the table's edge, his left hand twitched toward the drawer beneath it—the way a man touches a knife, or a secret.

I bought a luck-knot first. Cheap red cord, tied in a pattern any child could copy. He smiled at me with a face built for smiling: pleasant, forgettable, forty-ish, with laugh lines that were working overtime and eyes that weren't laughing at all.

"For the road?" he asked. "Or the heart?"

"The archive," I said, and watched the smile hold its shape while everything behind it went still. "The prophet Maravel's archive. The one that burned last night."

"Terrible thing," he said. "Old paper. Goes up like a wick."

"Mm. Except the fire started in one room. The room with the prophecy ledgers." I set the luck-knot down between us and leaned in, dropping my voice to the register I used for picking locks in crowded rooms. "And except that whoever was in that room left this behind." I produced the scrap of vellum I'd cut from Maravel's ledger—the page with the date, the name, and the sketch of my own face—and laid it flat on his table like a winning card.

The name written above my face was *the storm-caller of Greyhollow.*

He looked at it for a long moment. Long enough. Then he laughed—a warm, easy, market-day laugh—and said, "Lady, half this city thinks they're in an old book somewhere. My mother swore Maravel predicted her second marriage. It didn't take a prophet to see Uncle Doss coming."

"You're not in the book," I said. "You're *writing* in it. Or something is writing through you. Storm-light under your skin, Halloran. I saw it when you handed me the knot. Your veins lit up like a thunderhead at dusk."

And there it was—not fear, exactly. Something older than fear. The particular exhaustion of a man who has been waiting twelve years for someone to say that sentence, and had hoped, privately, to die first.

"I think," Silas Fairweather said pleasantly, "you've had too much sun."

"It's raining."

"Then too much rain. Very dangerous, weather."

He reached across the table to slide the vellum back to me, and that was when I took his memory.

Not all of it—I'm a thief, not a butcher. Just the top layer, the freshest hour: the vision in his stall yesterday, the customer whose purchase triggered it, the storm-light crawling up his own arms while he gripped the table and lied through his teeth to a frightened woman about the quality of his warding threads. I pulled it out light as a pickpocket lifting a purse, and I expected the usual: images, sounds, the texture of another person's fear.

What I got instead was lightning.

It went through me like a hook through the cheek. For half a heartbeat I wasn't in my body at all—I was standing inside a storm that lived in a man's chest, and it was *vast*, and it was *old*, and it knew I was there. It turned toward me the way a sleeping bear turns toward a sound. Somewhere far away, a voice that might have been mine said, *oh, you absolute fool,* and then I was back in my chair on Tallow Row, gripping the table edge, rain dripping off the awning onto my knuckles.

Silas was staring at me. His easy market face was gone. What was underneath was worse: recognition.

"You felt that," he said quietly.

"I don't know what you mean."

"You reached into my head and touched something that bit you." He wasn't angry. He sounded almost gentle, which was infinitely more alarming. "Nobody's done that in twelve years. Nobody's been able to." He glanced up and down Tallow Row—empty, raining, the candle-dipper shuttered—and then, with the weary air of a man opening his own prison door, he lifted the false bottom out of the drawer beneath the table.

Inside lay a single object wrapped in oilcloth. Even through the cloth I could feel it: pressure, like the air before a strike.

"Come round the back," said the storm-caller of Greyhollow. "We shouldn't discuss prophecy in the open. Also, if you're going to rob me again, do it where there's tea."

* * *

*Silas*

She drank her tea like a cat drinking water: constantly alert, as though the cup might be a trap, which to be fair it could have been, because I'd considered poisoning it for approximately four seconds before deciding that murder was bad for business.

Her name was Ada Vey. She'd told me that much without being asked, which told me she wanted me to know it—which meant it was probably true, which meant she was either honest or the best liar I'd ever met, and I'd met the Broker, so the bar was subterranean.

"So," she said, perched on the one stool in my back room like she owned it and was considering evicting me. "Twelve years."

"Twelve years what?"

"Since somebody touched your magic. Since you let anybody touch it. You flinched when I did it—no, not flinched. You *grieved*. Half a second of grief, then the wall came up." She sipped. "That's not the reflex of a man hiding from inquisitors. Inquisitors you hide from with anger. That's the reflex of a man hiding from himself."

I have been complimented on my charm-work, my knots, my prices, and once, memorably, my jawline. No one had ever complimented my walls before. It was deeply unpleasant how much I liked it.

"Memory-thief," I said. "That's the trade, yes? You take recollections for coin. Which means you've already taken mine, tonight, so let's skip the part where we pretend otherwise. What did you get?"

"A vision. Storm-light. A customer buying a warding thread and your whole body lighting up like the sky had a grudge." She tilted her head. "And something else. Something big, underneath. Like standing next to a cathedral and only being shown the door."

"The door's plenty," I said. "Trust me."

"Why hide?" The question came out fast, almost careless, but her eyes weren't careless at all. They were doing arithmetic. "Greyhollow's the safest city on the coast for your kind precisely because there's no magic here to find. The Ministry wrote it off decades ago. You could have hidden anywhere. Why build a life selling *luck knots*?"

Because luck knots are almost-magic. Because tying them kept my hands remembering the shapes of power without ever holding it. Because a man who has sold one soul learns to keep his hands busy or go mad.

"Low overhead," I said.

"Silas."

"Felix. Fairweather. Please use the shop name; I'm very attached to it."

"*Silas.*" She set the cup down with a click that ended the conversation's previous life. "Maravel's ledger named you. Not Felix Fairweather, charm-seller. *The storm-caller.* With a date. Yesterday's date. And my face drawn beside yours in ink that's a hundred years old, by a prophet who died before either of us was born." She pulled the vellum from her coat and smoothed it on the table between us, and there we were, the two of us, sketched in a dead man's hand: her sharp-eyed and wary even in charcoal, me a smudge of storm-shadow beside her. Underneath, in Maravel's cramped script:

*Two thieves, one table. The bargain that breaks the mountain.*

I looked at it for a long time. Rain ticked against the roof. Somewhere under my skin, the old storm turned over in its sleep, restless, like a dog dreaming of running.

"That line," I said finally. "'The bargain that breaks the mountain.' Do you know what it means?"

"No. Do you?"

"No." Which was true, and also the most dangerous kind of true, because I knew what it *might* mean, and I'd known since last night, and I had been sitting here hoping the feeling would pass like a fever.

Ada Vey studied me the way I imagined she studied locked rooms: patiently, cataloguing exits, weighing what was worth taking. Then she said, "Propose your bargain. You've been drafting it since I sat down. I can tell because you keep glancing at the oilcloth bundle."

I almost laughed. Almost. "You're right. I have. Here it is. You found the ledger page; I know things about Maravel that aren't in any ledger—including where he buried the parts of the prophecy he didn't want found. There's a cellar under his old house in the Sump. Sealed. Warded, if the wards have lasted, which they won't have, because wards need feeding and dead men are notoriously bad at upkeep. We open it together. We read what's there together. And then—" I spread my hands. "Then we decide what two people do when a hundred-year-old book insists they matter."

"And until then? Trust?"

"Gods, no." I said it warmly, and she almost smiled, and something in my chest did a small unhelpful flip that I chose to ignore. "Trust is for people with clean histories. Call it... mutual hostage-taking. You know my secret. If I vanish, you sell it. I know your face is in a prophet's book; if *you* vanish, I'll assume the worst and start asking loud questions in places you'd rather I didn't."

"Loud questions," she repeated. "From a man pretending to sell love tokens."

"I'm very convincing. Results not guaranteed."

That got the smile—brief, real, quickly filed away, but real. And I understood, sitting there in my shabby back room with the rain coming down and a century-old sketch of the two of us lying on my table, that I was in trouble. Not Ministry trouble, not Broker trouble. The other kind. The kind where you look at a stranger and feel the horrible vertigo of recognizing someone you haven't met yet.

She stood, pocketing the vellum. "The Sump. Tomorrow, after dark. If you're followed, I'll know, and I'll leave, and you'll never see me again."

"If you're followed," I said, "same terms."

"One more thing." At the door she paused, backlit by the guttering lamp in the alley, and for a moment the wariness dropped and I saw what was under it: a young woman genuinely frightened, holding the question carefully so it wouldn't spill. "When I touched your memory. The thing underneath. The big thing." Her voice stayed level. "Is it *you*? Or are you carrying something too?"

And there it was—the question I'd been dreading, arriving on the first night, ahead of schedule, wearing rain and lamplight.

Because I knew what she carried. Had suspected since the moment she laid that vellum down and I'd felt the echo of it through her grip on my mind: a vault of soul-light, humming, familiar as my own guilt. I'd sold a soul once. Souls don't stop existing when you sell them. They wait. And sometimes, apparently, they find their way into the heads of memory-thieves who break into prophets' archives.

If I told her now—if I said *I think you're carrying what I sold*—she'd run, or worse, she'd stay out of pity, and either way the prophecy's first line would strangle itself in the cradle. Two thieves, one table. The bargain comes first. Maravel hadn't written *the confession that breaks the mountain.* He'd written *the bargain.*

So I lied. Small, smooth, practiced, the twelfth-year vintage.

"It's just me," I said. "Storms and poor decisions. Nothing borrowed."

She held my eyes a long moment. Memory-thieves, I'd guess, make a career of knowing when they're being lied to, and I braced for her to call it.

Instead she nodded slowly and said, "Everyone in this city is carrying something. Fine. Tomorrow, after dark. Don't be late, storm-caller."

The door shut. The rain went on. I sat alone at my table with two cold cups of tea and the oilcloth bundle, and under my skin the storm stirred, and I could have sworn it was laughing.

Because here's the thing I haven't said yet, the thing I only admitted later, much later, when honesty stopped being optional: when she asked if I was carrying something too—

I said no.

And the storm inside me, old and vast and tired of waiting, answered *yes* so loudly that I'm fairly certain she heard it anyway.
