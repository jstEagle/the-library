## 4. What the Prophet Buried

The cellar door was hidden behind a false wall of brick, which was the first thing I disliked about it. Honest hiding places don't bother with masonry. A man who seals a room behind brickwork isn't hiding something—he's burying something, and he wants whoever finds it to know they had to work for the privilege.

"Prophets," I muttered, working my fingers into the mortar. "Even dead ones have to be dramatic."

Ada had gone back to her own lodgings an hour after our bargain, with instructions I still wasn't sure she'd follow: *don't tell anyone, don't sell anything, and if anyone asks about me, lie badly enough that they stop asking.* She'd looked at me like I'd suggested she take up gardening.

"You realize," she'd said, "that telling someone to lie badly is the most suspicious thing you could possibly say."

"It's called misdirection."

"It's called being terrible at this." She'd paused at the corner, and something in her face had shifted—less mockery, more arithmetic. "The prophecy had your face, Halloran. Not a description. Your face. Whoever wrote that knew you before you were worth knowing. Think about what that means."

I had thought about what it meant. All night, in fact, which is why I was now on my knees at dawn behind a tanner's row on Cinder Lane, picking apart a dead man's wall while the smell of rendered fat hung over everything like a curse.

The prophet's name had been Odile Marchetti. She'd died eleven years ago, officially of age, unofficially—the way these things went in Greyhollow—of having opinions. The city tolerated charm-sellers and luck-peddlers because we were harmless, decorative, a superstition tax on the gullible. But a woman who published pamphlets claiming the Ministry of Unmaking was a puppet of something older and hungrier than itself? That got you a quiet funeral and a sealed house, and everyone agreed not to wonder why the house stayed sealed.

I'd bought three luck-knots from her once, back when I first came to Greyhollow. She'd taken my coin, looked at my hands a moment too long, and said, "You'll want to come see me again before you think you need to." I hadn't understood it then. I was starting to.

The last brick came free with a sound like a tooth leaving a jaw. Behind it: darkness, cold air, and the particular silence of a room that has been waiting.

I lit no lamp. Old habit—light draws eyes—but also, I'll admit, fear. Storm-callers see well enough in the dark; the same sense that tastes weather a hundred miles off reads the shape of a room by its pressure, its drafts, its weight of dust. The cellar was small. Dry. And along the far wall, shelved like preserves, sat forty years of a dead woman's certainty.

Jars. Dozens of them, wax-sealed, each labeled in a fine, cramped hand. Not prophecies—*preparations*. That was the word on the nearest jar, and beneath it, a date. I turned it in my hands. Inside was nothing but paper, rolled tight, dry as a husk.

She'd known someone would come digging. Of course she had. That was rather the point of her.

I broke the seal on the oldest jar and unrolled the paper with the care of a man defusing something. Her handwriting was beautiful in the way that only the truly impatient manage—all economy, no ornament.

*To the one who comes late: yes, it is you. It is always someone. Sit down.*

I sat down on a crate that had been placed there, I now realized, specifically for that purpose. Eleven years of dust and she'd still arranged furniture for my arrival. I found I respected her enormously and resented her slightly more.

*You are looking for the storm-caller,* the letter continued. *You may be him. You may be hunting him. In either case, what follows will ruin your week.*

Understatement, as it turned out, was the one art she practiced poorly.

*There is a court beneath the mountain. This is not folklore. There is a throne beneath that court, and a true heir who should sit upon it, and a usurper who has held both for a century by keeping the heir's soul locked in a vault of flesh—a living vessel, walking the surface world, carrying the rightful bloodline without knowing it. The usurper cannot kill the vessel. The soul would simply flee to another. So instead the usurper waits, and hunts, and prays the vessel dies quietly somewhere useful.*

My hands had gone very still. The kind of stillness that precedes either lightning or vomiting.

*Here is the part that matters. The vault can be opened. The soul can be returned to the heir's body, which sleeps beneath the mountain, kept alive by machinery of oath and stone. But a return requires two things: the vessel must come willingly, and the one who performs the rite must be the same soul-dealer who sold the heir into the vessel's line in the first place. The magic remembers its maker. The Broker does not release what he has sold except to the hand that sold it.*

*I do not know who sold it. I know only that the sale happened twelve years ago this autumn, in defiance of every law of the deep court, and that the price paid was mortal—a life spared in exchange for a life bound. If you are reading this and you made such a trade, then I am sorry, and also, you are the only key this world has left.*

I stopped reading.

Not because I wanted to. Because my body refused to continue, the way a leg refuses weight when the bone beneath it is broken. Twelve years ago this autumn. A life spared in exchange for a life bound. Wren, coughing blood into her cupped hands in a rented room in Vail, and me running through rain to the one dealer who answered, and the bargain struck in a voice like wet gravel: *a soul for a soul, storm-caller, and you'll not choose whose—I choose the vessel, you choose the price.*

I had thought—I had *told myself*, twelve years of telling myself—that I'd sold some stranger's soul. Some nameless peasant from the deep court's census rolls, some life so far beneath my notice that the arithmetic didn't sting. The Broker had chosen the vessel; I'd assumed the vessel was the whole of it. A girl, maybe. A child. Someone I'd never meet, never have to picture.

But the letter kept going, and my eyes, traitors, kept following.

*The armies are already moving. The usurper's vanguard reached the Ashford waystation last month, though the free cities' councils have been told otherwise. They will reach Greyhollow's walls by winter unless the heir returns. And the heir returns only if the seller confesses—not to a priest, not to a page, but aloud, to the vessel, before the rite. The magic demands it. The truth is not decoration here. It is the load-bearing wall.*

*Confess, and lose everything you built to survive. Stay silent, and lose everything everyone else built to live.*

*Choose quickly. The dust on my jars says you won't have long.*

I read it twice more. Then a third time, because the mind does that when it's trying to find the seam in a nightmare—the place where you wake up.

The vessel. The vault of flesh. A living person walking around with the rightful heir of the deep court folded up inside them like a letter never delivered, and I—

No. No, I hadn't. I'd sold a stranger. I'd sold *a stranger*. The Broker had named no names, had shown me nothing but a ledger and a line of ink, and I'd signed because Wren couldn't breathe without blood coming up, and a man will sign anything at that bedside.

Except.

Except that Ada Vey had walked into my market square four days ago with a sketch of my own face in a century-old book, and the date beside it had been *yesterday*, and I had felt—when she stood across my stall with her thief's flat gray eyes—something under my skin answering her like a tuning fork. I'd told myself it was recognition of danger. Hunter and hunted, predator and prey, all the comfortable categories.

It hadn't felt like prey. It had felt like *debt*.

Twelve years ago this autumn. And how old would a stolen soul's vessel be? Young. Walking the surface world. Doing work that involved taking things from other people's heads, because a soul that doesn't belong to you leaves cracks, and cracks let other people's memories seep in, and a girl like that would grow up strange and skilled and hungry—

"No," I said out loud, to the cellar, to the jars, to the patient dead woman. "That's coincidence. That's just—"

But even as I said it, I was remembering the way lightning had flickered under my skin when she grabbed my wrist at the stall, and how it hadn't sparked *against* her. Magic answers kinship. Every storm-caller knows it. My power had recognized something in Ada Vey that belonged to the same deep places I'd traded with—and I had filed that away as evidence of her being a threat, because the alternative was unthinkable, and I had gotten very good at unthinkable over twelve years. It was practically my trade. Charms and denial, sold side by side.

I sat on Odile Marchetti's crate in her buried cellar and did the thing I'd spent a decade perfecting: I took the guilt out, looked at it, and put it back in its box. Except this time the box wouldn't close. This time the guilt had a face—gray eyes, quick hands, a laugh she deployed like a weapon—and the face was currently sleeping three streets away, trusting me exactly as far as a thief trusts anyone, which is to say barely, which is to say more than I deserved.

Because here was the arithmetic Odile had laid out with her beautiful impatient hand: the armies reach the free cities by winter. The heir returns only through me. Me—the seller, the key, the coward. And the rite requires confession, aloud, to the vessel. To Ada. *I sold you. Before we ever met, I sold you. Everything you are standing on, I undermined it for my sister's heartbeat.*

And if I told her—if I said those words—she would leave. Any sane person would leave. She'd walk out of my life with my crime tucked under her arm like a stolen purse, and she'd be right to, and the world would burn by winter anyway, because the key to salvation would have talked himself out of a vessel.

Unless she didn't leave. Unless—

No. I wasn't allowed to hope that. Hoping that was worse than the crime itself, because it meant part of me wanted her to stay *despite* knowing, which meant part of me valued my own comfort over her right to the truth, and I had done quite enough of that for one lifetime.

I rolled the letter closed with hands that weren't quite steady. Around me, the jars waited—forty years of preparations, dates and seals, a dead woman's entire life spent getting ready for a conversation she'd never have. She'd known. Maybe not my name, but the shape of it. *A life spared in exchange for a life bound.* She'd written my sin down before I committed it, sealed it in wax, and left it in the dark for me to find, and somehow that was the worst part. Not the guilt. The *witness*. Somewhere in the earth, all along, my secret had already been written down, and the universe had merely been waiting for me to catch up to my own file.

I climbed the ladder, reset the bricks as best I could—badly; I'm a charm-seller, not a mason—and emerged into the grey morning light of Cinder Lane feeling like a man who'd gone looking for a map and found his own arrest warrant.

One task remained before I could decide anything, and I dreaded it more than the Ministry, more than the Broker, more than the armies marching in Odile's pages. I had to look at Ada Vey and ask her, casually, idly, over whatever breakfast thieves eat, where she'd been twelve years ago this autumn. Where she'd grown up. Whether she'd ever been sick as a child in a way that medicine couldn't touch. Whether she'd ever dreamed of a mountain.

Whether she'd ever dreamed of a throne.

And I had to ask it while knowing that if the answer was yes—if the vault in her head was the vault I'd filled—then every kindness I'd shown her since the stall was built on the same foundation as everything else I'd built: a lie, load-bearing, and due to collapse.

I started walking toward her lodgings through the waking market, past stalls opening their shutters, past the luck-knots and love-charms and bottled courage of my former trade. My wards prickled at the edge of perception—faint, failing, the taste of iron before a storm. Someone was still hunting magic in Greyhollow. Winter was coming whether I confessed or not.

And ahead, leaning against a doorway with her arms crossed like she'd been waiting all morning and would deny it, stood Ada Vey—carrying, though neither of us knew it yet, the single most valuable thing in the world inside her skull, and the debt of my whole life owed to the person wearing her face.

She raised an eyebrow. "You look terrible, Halloran."

"Good," I said. "Then the day's honest so far."
