## 5. The Weight She Carries

There is a craft to stealing memories, and the first rule of the craft is this: know your own house before you burgle anyone else's.

My master—old Corvessa, may she rest badly—taught me that on my first day. *You are a thief of interiors, girl,* she said, tapping my temple with one yellowed nail. *And a thief who doesn't know her own rooms will hang herself on her own tripwires.* So every night before sleep, I walk my own halls. Every memory catalogued, shelved, dusted. It's how I know when something's been taken from me, and it's how I've never once been robbed.

Which is why what I found tonight has me sitting upright in a rented bed at the Drowned Lantern inn, at an hour when even the drunkards have gone home, with my heart going like a rabbit in a snare.

* * *

Let me back up.

After I left Silas Halloran's stall—after he lied to my face so smoothly that I almost admired it—I went back to the archive district. The prophet's book was still there, or rather, the copy I'd made was still tucked against my ribs where I'd hidden it, because the original had gone strange in my hands and I didn't trust what it might do to a building full of irreplaceable paper. My sketch of the page sat in my room like a guest who wouldn't leave: *the storm-caller walks unhooded*, dated today, and beside the words, a face drawn in faded ink that was unmistakably mine.

I'd spent the afternoon telling myself reasonable things. Faces in old prophecies are generic. Everyone looks like everyone if you squint. The prophet was mad; prophets usually are. And charm-sellers named Silas are common as pigeons.

But there was a second thing on that page, under the main text, written smaller—the kind of small writing people use when they're ashamed of what they're saying:

*She carries what was sold. She does not know she carries it.*

I read that line maybe forty times over the course of the evening, and each time, my eyes slid off it a little too easily. Like water off oiled cloth. Like a word in a language you almost speak.

That's when I stopped being reasonable and started doing my actual job—on myself.

* * *

The technique is called *walking the halls*, and no, I won't teach it to you, because you'd be terrible at it and probably lobotomize yourself. But here's the shape of it: you close your eyes and you lay your memories out the way a jeweler lays out stock. Not reliving them—that's a rookie mistake, you'll drown in your own feelings—but *touching* them. One fingertip to each. Checking weight, checking edges, checking for seams.

A stolen memory has seams. A tampered memory has seams. Even a good thief leaves them, because memory isn't cloth you can reweave without a tell. There's always a stitch out of place. A smell missing. A shadow falling the wrong way.

I started with yesterday. The archive. The book. The vision of storm-light that had cracked across my mind when I touched the page—wait.

No.

I touched that memory and flinched, because it wasn't right. Not wrong exactly. *Thicker.* When I'd lived it, the storm-light vision had been a flash—a heartbeat of white and ozone and then done. But the memory as it sat in my head now had depth to it. Layers. Somewhere underneath the flash there was a hum, low and constant, like a bell that never quite finishes ringing.

Memories don't hum.

I sat very still on the edge of the bed and did not panic, mostly because panicking requires a plan and I didn't have one. Then I went further down the stack.

Last week. The job in Candlewick Row, lifting a dead merchant's last argument with his brother for the grieving widow who wanted to know if she'd been loved. Clean work. Seams neat. Fine.

The month before. The ferry crossing. The rain. The—

There.

It wasn't a seam this time. It was a *door.*

In the middle of an ordinary memory—me on the ferry deck, watching gulls fight over a fisherman's scraps—there was a patch of dark. Not shadow. Shadow falls *on* things. This was more like a hole cut in the world of the memory, neat-edged, deliberate, about the size of a coin held at arm's length. And around its rim, faint as breath on cold glass, ran a pattern. Geometric. Interlocking. Deliberate.

A lock.

Someone had built a lock inside my memory. Inside my *head*. And they'd done it well enough that I'd walked past it every day of my life without ever seeing it, which meant either they were better than me—and I do not say that lightly—or they'd done it so long ago that the lock had grown into the walls like roots into masonry.

I did what any sensible professional would do.

I poked it.

* * *

Here is what I can tell you about what happened next, and I'll tell it slowly because I've now told it to myself eleven times and it hasn't gotten less insane.

Locks in memories are just structures. Thought-stuff shaped into barriers. Any competent memory-thief can pick one, because thought-stuff is our native material—we live in it the way fish live in water. So I set my picks, so to speak. I found the tumblers. They were old and heavy and they turned like millstones, but they turned.

And the door opened onto a room that should not exist.

Not empty. That would have been strange enough, finding a void inside myself, some amputated chunk of my own past. But it wasn't empty. It was *lit.* A soft gold light with no source, filling a space that had no walls, and in that light—

Weight. That's the only way I can describe it. Something in that room had mass the way grief has mass, the way a name you haven't said aloud in years has mass. It pressed outward against the lock I'd opened, gentle but absolutely certain, the way water presses against a dam—not angry, not trying, just *true.* It knew it belonged somewhere and this wasn't it.

And threaded through the light, faint as a watermark, I felt something else. Attention. Not hostile. Curious, maybe. Or patient. The attention of something that had been asleep a long time and had just felt a key turn in its door, and was now—slowly, the way dawn is slow—waking up.

I slammed the lock shut.

I don't mean I chose to. I mean my whole self flinched closed like a hand around a candle flame, and the tumblers crashed back into place, and I was sitting on the bed in the Drowned Lantern with sweat soaking through my shirt and my hands shaking hard enough to rattle the cheap headboard.

The gold light stayed with me anyway. Behind my eyes. A afterimage burned into the dark when I blinked. Humming.

* * *

I got up. I lit every lamp in the room, which is expensive and which tells you exactly how rattled I was. I drank half a cup of the inn's terrible wine, which is also expensive, and worse.

Then I made myself think, because thinking is the tool I actually trust.

Fact one: I have a locked space in my mind that I did not build. I know every hall of my own head the way Corvessa taught me, and that room is not mine. Its architecture is wrong—it doesn't match anything else in me. It's a foreign body. A swallowed stone.

Fact two: whatever's in it is old. The lock's edges had weathered, softened, grown-over. Years. Maybe many years. Which means it's been riding along with me through everything—every job, every city, every close call—and I never noticed.

Fact three: a hundred-year-old prophecy says, in small ashamed handwriting, *she carries what was sold. She does not know she carries it.*

Sold. Carried. By *she*, whoever she was, whose face the prophet drew wearing mine.

I'm not a woman given to dramatics. Dramatics get thieves hanged. But I will confess that at this point I stood up, walked to the room's grimy mirror, and looked at my own face for a long time, looking for—what? Someone else? Some heir, some stranger, some ghost wearing my cheekbones like a borrowed coat?

Just me. Tired, pale, wine-breath, freckles going autumn-brown at the edges. Just Ada Vey, memory-thief, currently unemployed and increasingly unwell.

But the humming didn't stop. If anything, having found it, I couldn't stop feeling it now—the way you can't unhear a clock once you've noticed it ticking. All those years of headaches I'd blamed on bad lamplight. All those nights I slept twelve hours and woke tired anyway, like I'd been working a second shift somewhere I couldn't remember. All those moments—small ones, forgettable ones—where I'd said something in a voice that didn't quite feel like mine, or known a street I'd never walked, or dreamed in colors that don't exist above ground.

Vertigo. That's the honest word for it. Not fear—fear has a direction, fear points at a thing. This was the floor itself becoming unreliable. Because here's the truth of being a memory-thief: your memories are the only property you actually own. Coin gets stolen, names get forged, bodies age. But the interior—the halls, the shelves, the stock—that's supposed to be sovereign territory. Mine, inviolate, the one place in Greyhollow or anywhere else where I am the law.

And I had just discovered a locked room in my own sovereign territory, built by someone else, holding something that hummed like a struck bell and pressed against its prison like it *knew* it belonged elsewhere.

Whose head have I been living in all these years? Mine, mostly. But not entirely. Not entirely.

* * *

I tried one more thing before I let myself sleep, and I want it noted that it was a stupid thing and I did it anyway, because curiosity is the other thing I trust, and between the two of them, caution rarely gets a vote.

I went back to the lock. I didn't open it. I just laid my palm against it—metaphorically speaking, though honestly by that point the distinction had gotten thin—and I listened.

And the thing behind the door listened back.

Not waking, exactly. Not rising toward me. More like... turning over in sleep. And in the moment of its turning, one image slipped through the seam, thin as thread under a door:

A mountain. Seen from below, from *inside*, lit by veins of gold running through black stone. And a sound—no, a *feeling*—of vast doors, doors the size of cathedrals, standing open somewhere far beneath the world.

Then it was gone, and the lock was shut, and I was on my knees on the floorboards with tears on my face that I had no memory of starting.

The mountain. The court beneath the mountain. The place every child in the free cities learns about in songs and then learns to stop believing in.

The prophecy hadn't just sketched my face. It had told me where I was going. What I was carrying. And who, presumably, would come to collect it.

* * *

Dawn came grey through the Drowned Lantern's one window. I hadn't slept. I'd sat in the lamplight and taken inventory instead, the way you do after a burglary—not of my goods, but of my options.

Option one: ignore it. Keep the lock shut, keep walking halls around it, pretend the humming is tinnitus. I gave this option a full five minutes of honest consideration, and then I caught myself pressing a hand flat against my sternum like I could hold the door shut from the outside, and I admitted that ignoring it was already failing.

Option two: find someone to open it properly. A surgeon of minds, a deep-craft worker, someone with more skill than sense. Except every such person I know sells their findings to whoever pays, and "there's a soul-shaped thing in Ada Vey's head" is worth a great deal to somebody, I'd bet my kit on it.

Option three: the charm-seller who is definitely, absolutely, certainly not a storm-caller, no matter what century-old books and the lightning I saw flicker under his skin might suggest.

He'd lied to me. Smoothly, warmly, with the practiced ease of a man who's had years to polish a single lie. I should write him off. A smart thief writes him off.

But the prophecy put us on the same page—literally, his date and my face—and whatever's locked inside me woke up curious the same week I met him, and I have survived twelve years in this trade by respecting patterns over preferences.

Besides. He looked at me yesterday, when he thought I wasn't watching, with an expression I recognized professionally, because I wear it myself on jobs: the look of a person standing in a house full of valuables, agonizing over the one thing they've come to steal.

Silas Halloran knows what's in my head. I'd stake my best lockpick on it.

So. Option three. Which meant today I was going to walk down to the market, buy a luck-knot I didn't need, and have a long, pleasant, mutually lying conversation with a man who smelled faintly of thunderstorms.

I washed my face. I counted my coin. I checked my halls one last time—all present, all accounted, except for the one room that was never mine at all.

Behind its door, faint and patient, the humming went on.

Like something waiting to be carried home.
