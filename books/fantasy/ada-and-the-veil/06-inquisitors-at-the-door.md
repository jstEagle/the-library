## 6. Inquisitors at the Door

The trouble with a small life, I've learned, is that it fits so neatly in your hands that you forget you're holding it.

I was thinking something like that on the morning the Ministry came—though not with any great prescience. Mostly I was thinking about tea. There's a woman three stalls down, Maren, who sells roasted chestnuts and chicory brew, and she waters her tea like a miser watering wine, but she gives me the dregs of the strong pot because once, years ago, I sold her a luck-knot that actually worked. She doesn't know why. She thinks it's because I'm charming. I let her think it. That's most of what charm-selling is, when you get down to it: letting people believe the world is kinder than it is, and charging them two coppers for the privilege.

So there I was, hands around a chipped cup of stolen-strength tea, watching Greyhollow wake up around me the way it did every morning—the fishwives arguing over eels, the lamplighter making his rounds home, a boy chasing a hoop past my stall of knotted thread and painted bone—and I thought: *this is fine. This is enough. Twelve years, and no one has ever looked at Silas Halloran the charm-seller twice.*

I should have known better than to say even that much to myself. The universe has excellent hearing.

* * *

Ada Vey arrived before the trouble did, which I suppose should have been my first warning. Trouble and Ada travel together like old married couples; they finish each other's sentences.

She came down the market row with her hood up and her walk too casual, the way people walk when they're trying very hard not to look like they're looking for someone. She stopped at my stall, picked up a luck-knot, turned it over with those quick clever fingers, and said, "Your wards are leaking."

"Good morning to you too."

"I'm serious." She set the knot down and leaned across my counter, close enough that I could see the sleepless bruising under her eyes. "There's a smell on this street this morning. Ozone and burnt sugar. That's you, Halloran. Your magic is sweating through whatever you've wrapped it in, and things with noses are going to come."

"Things with noses," I repeated. "You make it sound like we're being hunted by dogs."

"We might be." Her jaw tightened. "I went back to the prophet's cellar last night. Someone else had been there. Fresh boot-prints in the dust, and wax from a Ministry candle—grey tallow, smells of iron. They know the prophecy exists. They know it names a storm-caller. And every archive rat and fence I've talked to in the last two days says the same thing: the Ministry of Unmaking has agents in Greyhollow asking after magic."

I kept my face pleasant and stupid, the face I'd worn for twelve years. Inside, something cold began walking up my spine one vertebra at a time.

"The Ministry doesn't come to Greyhollow," I said. "Greyhollow is a dead city. No ley-lines, no wellsprings, nothing worth unmaking. That's why I picked it."

"That's why *they* picked it too," Ada said. "Dead cities are where dead things hide."

I looked at her for a long moment. She looked back, unblinking, and I realized—with the particular weariness of a man who has been caught lying by someone who lies professionally—that she had already decided I was the storm-caller, and that everything I said now was just theater she was politely enduring until intermission.

"You could leave," she said quietly. "Today. Before noon. Walk out the east gate and don't stop for three days."

"And go where?"

"Away from me. The prophecy names both of us. Whatever's coming, it comes through us, and anyone standing near us when it lands—" She stopped herself. Something flickered behind her eyes, some memory she didn't share. "I've seen what happens to people who stand near prophecy. It isn't pretty, and it isn't quick."

"Maren," I said, nodding toward the chestnut stall, "has stood next to me for eleven years."

"Then Maren should be worried."

"She waters her tea."

"What?"

"Never mind." I drained the cup and set it down. My hands were steady. I'd practiced that for twelve years too. "Ada. If I were what you think I am—if I were half of what's written in that dusty book—you'd be the last person I'd want standing next to me. You're a thief. You steal memories. You're a walking scandal waiting for a confession."

Something hurt crossed her face, fast as a fish under water, and was gone. "Yes," she said. "I know what I am."

And I thought: *well done, Halloran. Twelve years of careful living, undone by one morning of cruelty.* Because I hadn't said it to wound her—I'd said it to push her away, which is the same thing only less honest—and she knew that too, probably, because thieves always know when they're being handled.

She straightened up off my counter. "Fine," she said. "Die alone, then. But when they come—and they will come, Halloran, today or tomorrow or the day after—remember that I offered you the door before it became a window."

She walked away down the row, hood up, shoulders squared, and I watched her go and told myself the cold feeling in my chest was relief.

It wasn't. But I was very good at lying to myself. I'd had practice.

* * *

They came at midday, when the market was thickest.

I heard them before I saw them—the way the noise of a crowd changes shape. A market sounds like water, all day, all its life: chatter and haggling and laughter running in little channels between the stalls. What happened at noon was the sound of water hitting a dam. The channels stopped. The whole flow of the place backed up and went silent, and then a single voice cut through it, amplified by some artifice I didn't want to look at too closely:

"BY ORDER OF THE MINISTRY OF UNMAKING. ALL PERSONS WILL REMAIN WHERE THEY STAND. CONCEALMENT OF LIVE MAGIC IS A CAPITAL CRIME. COOPERATION WILL BE REWARDED. OBSTRUCTION WILL BE UNMADE."

*Unmade.* Not punished. Unmade. The Ministry had a way of saying it that made the word feel like a physical thing, a hand reaching into the crowd to pluck out threads.

I didn't run. Running is the first thing they look for. Instead I did what I'd rehearsed in my head for twelve years without ever believing I'd need it: I started destroying my own life.

The charms first. Each one I'd ever made carried a thread of me—that's how charm-work functions, even the weakest of it; you knot your intent into the object and the object remembers you. To an Inquisitor with a resonance-lens, my entire stall would glow like a bonfire. So I pulled the drawer of finished luck-knots and held a taper to them, right there, casually, like a man lighting his pipe, and watched twelve years of careful small work curl and blacken.

Thread-knots I'd dyed myself. Bone charms carved from river-cow vertebrae, each one sanded smooth over long evenings while Wren's face swam in my memory and I tried not to think about why I was sanding bones in a basement. A wind-chime of silvered rings I'd made for Maren's granddaughter and never delivered. All of it, burning. The smoke smelled of ozone and burnt sugar, exactly as Ada had said. My magic, sweating out of me even as it died.

"Fire!" someone shouted, and then the crowd's noise broke back in, panic-shaped now, and I used it. Under cover of the stampede I swept the ashes into my coin-box, dumped the box into the gutter, and smashed the false bottom of my counter where I kept the truly dangerous things: three storm-glass beads, a hairpin woven from captured lightning, and a letter I'd never sent to a sister who might not even remember me.

The beads I swallowed. Don't ask. It's an old smuggler's trick and it works, and I'll spare you the details except to say that swallowing lightning is exactly as pleasant as it sounds, and that for about ten seconds afterward I was fairly sure my teeth were going to come out through my ears.

The hairpin I drove into the wooden post of my stall, deep, where it would ground itself out and read as nothing more than a nail.

The letter—

The letter I couldn't burn. Twelve years, and I still couldn't burn it. I shoved it inside my shirt instead, against my skin, and felt it there like a coal.

By the time the Inquisitors reached my end of the row, I was just a charm-seller standing amid the wreckage of his own stall, coughing on smoke like everyone else, wearing the expression of a man whose livelihood has just burned down, which required almost no acting at all.

There were six of them. Grey robes, iron-grey masks with no eye-holes—just smooth blank faces with a single vertical seam—and the one in front carried a resonance-lens: a disc of smoked crystal on a chain, and when he raised it over the crowd, the whole market lit up for him in shades of grey and silver. Ordinary folk glowed faintly, the way warm bread glows. Charm-sellers' goods glowed a little brighter, cooling now as the magic burned out of them.

And me. I stood in the middle of my ruined stall and prayed to every god I'd ever pretended not to believe in that swallowed storm-light reads as indigestion.

The lens passed over me.

Paused.

Moved on.

I exhaled about a year's worth of air.

Then the masked figure stopped, turned back, and raised the lens again.

"No," I whispered.

"Vendor," the Inquisitor said. Its voice came out flat and sexless through the mask. "Your wares are burning."

"Yes," I said, with a little hysterical laugh. "Yes, I noticed. Terrible accident. Lamp must have caught the—"

"Charm-sellers do not light lamps near their stock." The lens rose higher. Through the smoked crystal I could see my own reflection, warped and grey, and behind it, faintly, like lightning behind cloud, something in me was glowing. "Vendor. Hold still."

And that's when Ada Vey fell into him.

Not walked—fell, staggering, clutching her arm, blood—real blood, gods knew where she'd gotten it, maybe her own—running down her wrist, screaming at the top of her considerable lungs: "HELP! Please! He pushed me—he knocked me down—the man with the lens, he shoved past me and I fell on the brazier—"

Every head in the vicinity turned. The Inquisitor turned. And in the chaos of a bleeding woman collapsing against a Ministry agent—which, I would later learn, is roughly equivalent to tackling a priest during communion—Ada's other hand, quick as a pickpocket's dream, slipped the resonance-lens off its chain and palmed it.

What she did with it in the next two seconds I will never fully understand, though I have theories involving sleight-of-hand and a hollow heel. What I know is this: when the Inquisitor recovered his lens and raised it again, the market glowed ordinary. Warm bread. Cooling charms. One charm-seller with what the lens presumably registered as a stomachache.

"Move along," the Inquisitor said finally, to everyone and no one, and the grey line moved on down the row, leaving ruin behind it. Two stalls down, they found a hedge-witch's salves that weren't well-hidden enough. What happened next took four minutes and I made myself watch, because I owed it to her to watch: they unmade her. Not killed—unmade. One moment a stout woman with flour on her apron, the next a smear of grey light and a silence where a person used to be, and her stall empty, and her name already fading from the minds of everyone who'd ever spoken to her, including mine, which is the cruelest part—I stood there trying to remember who I was mourning and couldn't.

That's what waited for me. That's what I'd been hiding from for twelve years, and today it had come within a lens-length of finding me, saved only by a thief's hands and a thief's nerve.

I found Ada three streets away, sitting on a loading dock behind a tannery, wrapping her arm with a strip of shirt-sleeve. The blood was real. The wound, I suspected, was older than the performance.

"You came back," I said.

"Don't flatter yourself. I came back for the lens." She tossed it to me. It was lighter than it looked, and colder. "Keep it. Consider it a gift. Or a leash. I haven't decided which."

"Why?" I asked. "Why risk that? I told you this morning to stay away from me. I was cruel about it."

"You were," she agreed. "You were also wrong, and scared, and lying, and all three of those things look different when you've spent your life reading people." She tied off the bandage with her teeth. "Besides. Prophecy put us at the same table, Halloran. I can hate the meal, but I'm not going to pretend the table isn't set."

I sat down beside her on the dock. Below us, the canal ran green and slow. Somewhere behind us, Greyhollow was learning to live with a hole in itself where a hedge-witch used to be.

"They'll come back," I said. "With a better lens. With a description. That Inquisitor paused on me, Ada. Paused and turned back. Next time there won't be a bleeding woman to trip them."

"I know."

"So this is it, then." I heard my own voice go strange and quiet. "Twelve years. Do you know what twelve years looks like? It looks like a stall. It looks like knowing which floorboard in your room creaks and stepping on it anyway because the creak means you're home. It looks like Maren giving you the strong tea. It looks like—" My voice cracked, traitor that it is. "It looks like a letter you never send, because sending it would kill the person it's addressed to."

Ada said nothing. She was watching me with that thief's attention, cataloguing, I supposed, every crack in the performance.

"I built a life out of knots," I said. "Little tied-off things. Safe things. And I just burned all of them in a gutter, and do you know the worst part? The worst part is that it was always a lie anyway. Every safe day was borrowed. Every friend was a friend of a mask. I didn't lose a life today. I lost the *costume* of one, and the body underneath turns out to be exactly what I was afraid it was: a storm-caller with a debt."

"It wasn't all a lie," Ada said.

"How would you know?"

"Because the tea was real." She shrugged. "Maren gave you the strong tea because of who you were to her, not what you were hiding. Masks don't pour tea, Halloran. People do. Whoever you were under there—it was real enough to be loved. That part doesn't burn."

I looked at her, this woman I'd known four days, who had just thrown herself under a Ministry agent for a man she had every reason to walk away from, and I felt something in my chest shift its weight from one foot to the other. Dangerous. Everything about her was dangerous, and the prophecy hadn't even gotten to the dangerous parts yet.

"Where do we go?" I asked.

"East," she said. "Toward the mountain. We follow the prophecy instead of running from it. It's the only direction they won't expect—prey running toward the wolf." She stood, slung her pack over her good arm, and offered me her hand. "Well, storm-caller? Door or window?"

I took her hand. Swallowed lightning and grief and the taste of burnt thread, and stood.

"Window," I said.

We went east, out through the tannery yards, past the last walls of the only home I'd allowed myself in twelve years. At the gate I didn't look back. Looking back is how masks fall off.

But I'll tell you a secret, since we've come this far together: I did look back. Just once, from the crown of the eastern hill, at the smoke still rising thin and grey from the market district, at the city that had held me like a cup holds water—carefully, and only because it didn't know what I really was.

And far below, at the gate we'd just passed through, a grey-robed figure stood in the road, motionless, its blank mask tilted upward.

Watching us go.

It didn't follow. It didn't need to. I understood, with a certainty that settled into my bones like cold rain, that somewhere in Greyhollow a report was already being written, and that my name—not Silas the charm-seller, but the other name, the true one—was in it.

The hunt had begun. And we were running, exactly as the prophecy intended, straight toward the mountain.
