## 7. Road Beneath Rain

### Ada

The rain started an hour out of Greyhollow and did not stop, which I took personally.

I had stolen a raincoat once. Well—*stolen* is a strong word. I had borrowed a raincoat from a man who would not remember owning it, because that was the nature of my trade. Memory-thief. Recollection-for-hire. You want to forget your dead husband? Done. You want to remember where you buried the money you hid from yourself? Also done, though that one costs extra, because giving memories back is harder than taking them. Taking is like lifting a coin from a pocket. Returning is like putting the coin back and hoping the pocket doesn't notice the shape of your fingers.

Silas Halloran walked beside me through the downpour with his hood up and his secrets closer, and I was fairly sure he didn't know either of those things about my trade yet.

"So," I said, for the fourth time since the city gates. "Exile."

"Charm-seller," he said, for the fourth time. "Retired."

"Nobody *retires* from selling luck-knots. There's no pension. There's no guild. You tie string in a pattern and charge people who can't do arithmetic."

"Then perhaps I retired from arithmetic."

He had a way of deflecting questions the way water deflects off oiled canvas—all surface, nothing soaking in. I'd watched him do it with the customs clerk at the gate, with the farmer whose cart we shared for six miles, and now with me. It should have annoyed me more than it did. Instead I found myself cataloguing his deflections like a collector cataloguing forgeries: *ah, the humble shrug, very fine work, note the self-deprecating pause.* The better the forgery, the more certain I became there was an original somewhere underneath.

"Your hands are shaking," I said.

"It's cold."

"Your hands were shaking in the sun outside Greyhollow, when that Ministry rider passed us on the road. You put them in your pockets so fast I thought you'd dropped something."

"I'm a cautious man."

"You're a *terrified* man pretending to be a cautious one. Those are different professions entirely."

He glanced at me then—a quick, sidelong look, the kind of look a man gives a mirror he suspects of lying—and said nothing, which was its own kind of confession. We walked. The rain kept up its argument against the world, and lost, as usual.

Here's what nobody tells you about memory-craft: everyone thinks it's about the past. It isn't. It's about attention. A memory is just a moment that someone bothered to hold onto, and most people hold onto all the wrong ones. They keep the insult from nine years ago and lose the smell of their mother's kitchen. My craft—the one I'd learned from a woman named Odile who charged me three years of my childhood for the lessons, and I've never decided if I overpaid—was the art of deciding what deserved keeping.

And Silas Halloran, walking beside me in the rain, was a man who had decided that *everything* deserved keeping, and had buried the keeping so deep even he couldn't find it anymore.

I could see it from here. That was the thing he didn't understand about traveling with a memory-thief: I could read him the way a jeweler reads a stone. Surface thoughts sat on him like weather—worry about the road, worry about the Ministry, worry about the price of oats in the next town. But underneath the weather there was a cellar. And something in that cellar was loud.

"Teach me something," he said suddenly.

I blinked rain off my lashes. "What?"

"You're a thief of memories. We have a long road and worse weather. Teach me how it works. If I'm going to be robbed, I'd rather know the technique."

"I'm not robbing you."

"Not yet." He almost smiled. It was the first almost-smile I'd seen on him that wasn't for sale at a market stall, and I noted it the way I noted everything: carefully, and against my better judgment.

* * *

### Silas

She taught me while we walked, which I later understood was the first lesson: her craft doesn't live in quiet rooms or candlelit studies. It lives in motion, in distraction, in the ordinary noise of a life.

"Everyone has a door," she said. "The front door of their mind. Most people leave it open because they've never been robbed. First rule: never be the open door."

"And how does one close it?"

"You don't close it. Closing invites breaking. You fill it." She plucked a wet leaf off a hedge as we passed and held it up between two fingers. "You know how a market crowd works? A pickpocket doesn't go for the man watching his purse. He goes for the man arguing with a fishmonger. Attention is finite. Fill a mind with enough living and there's no room left for stealing."

"That sounds less like magic and more like common sense."

"Common sense is what they call magic that works." She tucked the leaf into her sleeve—I would learn she collected small useless things, and I would learn why, much too late—and looked over at me. "Your turn. Tell me about the exile."

"There's no exile."

"Second rule of doors," she said pleasantly. "When someone asks what's behind yours and you say *there is no door*, every thief in a hundred miles hears the lock click."

I laughed. It surprised me coming out—it had been years since laughing cost nothing—and the sound seemed to surprise her too. She stared at me for half a stride like I'd done a card trick.

"What?" I said.

"Nothing. You have a dishonest laugh."

"Most of me is dishonest. The laugh is the honest part. It gets out before I can stop it."

"Hm." She walked a few paces in silence, rain drumming on her hood. "That's the truest thing you've said all day. Which means it slipped out. Which means—" she pointed at me with a dripping finger, delighted, "—you're bad at this. You're actually bad at hiding things. You've just spent so long doing it that everyone around you got bored of looking."

"They haven't," I said quietly. "Someone's looking right now. Someone's always looking."

The words came out heavier than I meant them to, and I felt her attention sharpen like a blade being turned toward the light. So I did what I do. I pointed at a milestone and asked whether she thought the next village sold dry boots.

### Ada

He was bad at it. That was the discovery of the afternoon, and honestly, it improved my whole opinion of the prophecy.

Because here's what I'd expected from a storm-caller in hiding: a man of iron discipline, wards within wards, a soul like a locked archive. What I got instead was a charm-seller who flinched at Ministry riders and lied with the smoothness of a man reciting lines he'd rehearsed twelve years ago and never once updated. His lies had *dust* on them. Nobody dusts a lie for over a decade; they just take it down off the shelf whenever someone visits and blow on it.

We stopped under a leaning barn roof where a shepherd had once, apparently, stored hay and then forgotten about hay entirely. Silas built a small fire with the efficiency of a man who'd built ten thousand small fires, and I watched his hands do it—long fingers, careful, a faint tremor he thought I couldn't see.

"Show me a theft," he said. "A real one. On purpose."

"Why?"

"Because you offered to teach me and then spent four miles teaching me how to avoid being robbed by you, which is either generosity or the most elaborate sales pitch I've ever heard."

It was, I admit, a little bit both. But mostly it was this: I wanted to show him. Not the ugly thefts—the grieving widows, the men who paid to forget their own names—but the good ones. The ones Odile never mentioned because they didn't pay.

"Give me your hand," I said.

He gave it. Hesitated first—that half-second where a man decides whether to trust you and loses the argument—but gave it.

"Now think of something small. Something from today. The milestone. The boots."

He thought of the boots. I felt it arrive like warmth off a stone: gray leather, a shopkeeper's voice, the specific disappointment of a man who owns one pair of socks. I didn't take it. I just *touched* it, the way you touch a soap bubble, and let him feel me touching it.

His breath caught. "I felt that. I felt you—how did I feel you?"

"Because I let you. That's the trick nobody teaches. Any thief can lift a memory from a sleeping mind. The craft is doing it to someone awake, gently enough that they feel it and aren't afraid." I released his hand. "Odile called it *reading aloud*. You don't steal the book. You just open it where they can see you looking."

"Who's Odile?"

"My teacher. She took three years of my childhood as payment. I've never gone back to check which three." I said it lightly, the way you say the heavy things when you don't want a stranger weighing them. "Some days I think it was worth it. Some days I try to remember being eight and come up with fog."

Silas looked at me across the fire, and his face did something complicated—grief and recognition fighting for the same ground.

"That's theft," he said finally. "Not craft. Theft. She stole years from a child."

"She bought them fair and square. There was a contract."

"A contract with a child."

"Yes, well." I fed a stick to the fire. "Neither of us was in a position to negotiate. That's usually how contracts with children go."

He was quiet a long time. Rain ticked on the barn roof. When he spoke again his voice had changed—lower, rougher, like something dragged up from a deep shelf.

"If someone took years from you," he said, "and you could get them back—would you want them? Even the bad ones?"

"That depends."

"On what?"

"On what I'd have to lose to get them." I looked at him straight. "Why? Do you know someone who's missing years, storm-caller?"

The name landed. I watched it land—watched his shoulders lock, watched the firelight flicker along his jaw in a way that had nothing to do with the fire. For one heartbeat the air tasted like the minute before lightning.

"I told you," he said. "There's no storm-caller."

"Mm." I leaned back against a hay bale that smelled of mouse and mildew. "Third rule of doors, Silas. When you say *there is no door*, some thieves stop asking questions." I closed my eyes. "I'm not some thieves."

* * *

### Silas

She frightened me, and I mean that as a compliment.

Twelve years I had built Greyhollow Silas: the charm-seller with the ready smile and the mediocre stock, the man so deliberately unremarkable that even the Ministry's informants skimmed past him like weather. Twelve years of practicing smallness until it fit me like skin. And in two days this woman had found the seams.

Worse—she made me *want* to show her. That was the real danger, and I knew it, and I walked straight into it anyway, because a man who has been alone with a secret for twelve years will follow anyone who looks at the secret kindly.

We walked the next morning through country getting steadily steeper, the mountain showing itself between clouds like a patient animal. She talked. Gods, she talked—about Odile, about jobs she'd pulled, about a duke who'd paid her to forget his mistress and then, a year later, paid her again to forget that he'd ever forgotten. She was funny in a way that snuck up on you. Her humor came sideways, disguised as complaint, and if you weren't paying attention you missed it, and I found myself paying attention with an attentiveness that should have alarmed me.

At midday we crossed a stone bridge over a flooded stream, and she stopped in the middle of it, rain streaming off the parapets, and said: "Do the thing."

"What thing?"

"The storm thing. I saw it at your stall. Lightning under your skin, when I grabbed your wrist. I've been thinking about it for two days and I want to see it properly."

"Ada—"

"I'm not asking you to call down the sky. I'm asking for a spark. Like showing a scar." She held out her hand, palm up, rain pooling in it. "You've seen inside my head. Fair trade."

And that was the trap, wasn't it. Because she had seen inside my head—or rather, I had felt her *not* taking anything, which was somehow more intimate—and fairness is a currency I've always been poor in.

I looked at her open hand. I looked at the empty road behind us. Then I let a little of it rise—not much, a thread of it, the smallest coin in my purse—and lightning moved beneath the skin of my forearm, pale gold, branching like frost, and leapt the gap between us to die harmlessly in her cupped palm with a sound like a struck match.

She didn't flinch. She laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her, bright as the spark itself—and shook her stinging hand and said, "Again."

"You're supposed to be impressed, not entertained."

"I'm both. Again."

So I did it again. And again. And somewhere around the fifth spark, with rain hammering the bridge and this impossible woman holding out her hand like a beggar at a festival, I forgot—for maybe thirty seconds—to be afraid. I made the lightning dance between my knuckles, a trick Wren had loved when we were children, and Ada clapped like the crowd at a hanging, and I laughed until my ribs hurt.

It was the first laughter I'd had in twelve years that wasn't for sale.

I realized that afterward, walking on, and the realization sat in my chest like swallowed ice. Because here was the ledger, and I could read it plainly: every mile of this road was a mile she'd lose. Every joke, every spark, every lesson in doors and thieves—if the prophecy held, if the return had to happen, then the vault in her head would empty itself of exactly these days. I was spending time we would both remember and only I would keep.

"Silas." She'd fallen into step beside me. "Back on the bridge. When you laughed—you went somewhere after. Your face did a thing. Where'd you go?"

"Nowhere."

"There's no nowhere," she said softly. "Remember? Rule of doors."

I looked at the mountain, gray and enormous in the rain, and I said the truest false thing I owned:

"I was thinking that I hope this road stays long."

And the worst part—the part I'll carry past the end of everything—is that it wasn't even a lie. I just didn't tell her *why*.

Behind us, far back along the valley road, a column of smoke stood up against the clouds, thin and black and wrong. Too far away to be sure. Too straight to be accidental.

Ada saw it a heartbeat after I did, and neither of us said a word, and we walked faster.
