## 8. The Sister Who Waited

The detour was Ada's idea. I want that on the record, carved somewhere in stone, so that when historians ask how the whole disaster began, they'll know it wasn't the storm-caller's fault for once.

"There's a village off the east road," she said over breakfast, which was road-bread and a hard cheese she'd charmed out of a farmer by borrowing his fondness for his late wife. "Millbrook. You went white as whey when I mentioned it yesterday."

"I did not."

"You dropped your spoon into the fire and then stared at it burning like it owed you money."

"That's called thinking."

"That's called guilt with a spoon in it." She tore her bread in half with the efficiency of someone who'd never once been sentimental about food. "Whoever you're avoiding lives there. Which means whoever you're avoiding matters. Which means we're going."

"We have a prophecy to chase and an army ahead of us eating waystations like a locust with a grudge."

"Exactly. So we can't afford ghosts rattling around your chest the whole way." She stood, brushed crumbs from her coat, and looked down at me with those grey eyes that had already picked three locks this morning and were now working on a fourth. "I steal memories for a living, Silas. I know what unopened doors do to people. They get heavier every year until one day you can't carry anything else."

I should have lied. I had twelve years of practice at lying; I could have done it in my sleep, and often had. But she'd said *unopened doors*, and something in me — some starved, stupid animal part of me that had been waiting since the night I signed my sister away — sat up and whined.

"Wren," I said. "My sister. She lives in Millbrook. Or lived. Or—"

"Or is the reason you flinch whenever anyone mentions family," Ada finished. "Pack up. We're walking."

* * *

Millbrook hadn't changed, which felt like an insult. Twelve years, and the mill still turned its lazy wheel, and the baker still burned his first batch of the morning because he was arguing with his wife instead of watching the oven, and the smell of woodsmoke and wet grain hung over everything like a blessing nobody had asked for.

I had left this place at nineteen with storm-light under my skin and a plan. Come back rich. Come back safe. Come back with the kind of life that could hold a little sister without crushing her.

Instead I'd come back once, at twenty-four, in the dead of night, having sold a soul to save hers, and found the house empty and a single line chalked on the doorframe: *Don't look for me. Please.* The *please* had undone me more than the rest of it. Wren didn't say please. Wren said *move* and *faster* and *I dare you*. A *please* from Wren was a scream wearing its Sunday clothes.

So I hadn't looked. Twelve years of not looking. It turns out not looking is a full-time occupation; it takes everything you have.

"Which house?" Ada asked quietly. She'd gone careful since we entered the village, reading my face the way she read locks — finding the tumblers, testing each one.

"The one that isn't burning down," I said. "That narrows it to most of them."

She gave me the flat look. We walked past the well where Wren had dared me to swim in February when we were children. Past the fence I'd broken and blamed on a goat. Past the spot under the alder tree where our mother had taught us both letters, before the fever took her and left just the two of us holding on to each other like the last two buttons on a coat.

The house at the end of Tanner's Row had new shutters. That was the first wrong thing. Wren would never have chosen new shutters; she believed paint was a scam invented by people who feared weather. The second wrong thing was the garden — neat, ordered, herbs in rows like soldiers standing inspection. Wren gardened the way storms happened: enthusiastically and without any plan whatsoever.

The third wrong thing was the man across the lane, mending a net that had no business being near a mill town, who watched us pass with eyes too patient for a fisherman.

"Company," Ada murmured.

"I see him."

"You see one. There's another by the chapel pretending to pray, and he's been doing it badly for ten minutes. And a third on the roof of the cooper's, unless that chimney sweep has developed a taste for crouching."

Three watchers on a quiet house in a quiet village. My stomach performed a slow, cold revolution. Leverage doesn't stay leverage if you leave it untended. Someone had been tending.

Ada touched my elbow. "We can walk away. Right now. We turn around, we take the south road, and whatever's behind that door stays behind that door."

For one shining moment I considered it. Then I thought about the word *please* chalked on a doorframe twelve years ago, and how heavy an unopened door gets, and I pushed open the gate.

* * *

She was smaller than I remembered. That's the thing nobody tells you about absence — it doesn't preserve people. It pickles them in your memory at whatever age they were, and then reality goes and ruins them by continuing to exist.

Wren Halloran, twenty-six years old, stood in her kitchen stirring a pot with the same wooden spoon our mother had used, and she was thin, and her hair was cropped short in a way that spoke of practicality rather than fashion, and her eyes —

Her eyes were the worst part. I'd braced myself for anger. I'd rehearsed fury, accusations, maybe even a thrown spoon. I had not prepared for eyes that looked at me the way you look at weather you've decided to stop fighting. Resigned. Tired. Already counting the cost of my arrival before I'd said a word.

"Hello, Silas," she said. "You shouldn't be here."

"Hello to you too. Twelve years and that's the greeting?"

"What would you prefer? Confetti?" She set down the spoon. Her hands were steady, which somehow frightened me more than shaking would have. "Sit down. Both of you. And keep your voice below a shout, because the man across the lane reads lips and the one on the roof has very good ears."

Ada slid onto the bench beside me, all casual grace, though I caught her hand resting near the knife at her belt. "Your brother's told me almost nothing about you," she said pleasantly. "But I'm guessing you're the smart one."

"She's the survivor," Wren said. "There's a difference, and it's wider than you think." She ladled out three bowls of stew as if feeding fugitives were a Tuesday chore, and maybe it was. Maybe it had been for years. The thought arrived like a knife between ribs: *how long has she been performing normalcy for watchers?*

"Eat," she told me. "You look terrible."

"You look—"

"Don't." Her voice cracked, just once, a hairline fracture in stone. "Don't tell me what I look like. I know what I look like. I see her every morning."

"See who?"

Wren looked at me for a long moment. Then she laughed — a small, awful sound with no floor under it.

"You really don't know. Twelve years, Silas. You sold whatever you sold, you ran wherever you ran, and you never once asked what the other side of that bargain looked like from here." She sat down across from me, folded her hands, and became very still, the stillness of someone who has practiced being still. "They come every month. The agents. Two of them, sometimes three. They sit where you're sitting and they ask me questions about you. Where you'd go. What name you'd use. Whether you've written. And every month I tell them nothing useful, and every month they smile and remind me what happens to sisters of traitors when their usefulness runs out."

The stew turned to lead in my mouth. "You could run. Greyhollow took me. Plenty of places—"

"Run where? They let me believe I could run for the first two years. Let me get as far as the crossroads before they collected me back. It was educational." She smiled, and it was her old smile, sharp as a fishhook, except now it was aimed inward. "Then I understood. I'm not a prisoner, Silas. Prisoners have hope of release. I'm a *holding*. Like land. Like coin in a vault. As long as you're alive and unfound, I'm worth keeping alive and found. The day you stop mattering—" She shrugged. "The day I stop mattering."

Ada had gone very quiet beside me. When I glanced at her, her face had that locked-down look I'd learned meant she was feeling something enormous and intended to feel it privately.

"So don't matter," Wren said, leaning forward, and now her eyes had fire in them after all, banked and desperate. "That's why you can't be here. That's why you have to go back to being nobody. Whatever you're doing — whatever brought you out of whatever hole you crawled into — stop. Be a charm-seller. Be a ghost. Be *boring*, Silas, for the love of all the indifferent gods, be so boring that they forget I exist."

"They won't forget."

"Then make them wish they had!"

"It doesn't work that way anymore." I heard my own voice go low and rough. "There's a war coming, Wren. The usurper's armies are weeks from the free cities. There's a prophecy with my name in it and a soul that needs returning and a girl beside me who—" I stopped, because saying it aloud made it real, and real things could be taken.

Wren stared at me. Then, slowly, horribly, she started to laugh again, and this time there were tears in it.

"A prophecy. Of course. Of *course* it's a prophecy. You always did need the universe itself to tap you on the shoulder before you'd admit you mattered." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand. "Listen to me. Listen carefully, because I've had twelve years to think about this and you've had twelve minutes. If you do this thing — this grand, stupid, prophesied thing — they will know. The moment you surface anywhere near that mountain, they will know, and the first thing they'll do is collect their collateral." She reached across the table and gripped my wrist, hard, her fingers thin as bird bones. "So I am begging you. Me, Wren Halloran, begging. Walk away. Let the world burn its own cities. Let the usurper keep his stolen throne. Buy yourself a little shop and sell luck-knots until you die old and dull in a bed. Because I would rather live one more year as their hostage than spend the rest of my life knowing I was the reason you died."

"And I'd rather die knowing you were free."

"Well, that's the problem with you, isn't it? Always has been." She released my wrist and stood, turning to the window, shoulders rigid. "You'd trade anything for the people you love. Anything. And you never once ask whether the people you love wanted to be paid for."

The words landed exactly where she'd aimed them, in the soft place beneath my ribs where the memory of a bargain lived. *A soul traded. A sister spared.* Except spared wasn't the word, was it? Spared implied rescue. Wren hadn't been rescued. She'd been *purchased*, and she'd spent twelve years as merchandise on a shelf, dusted monthly by men with patient smiles.

I had told myself for twelve years that I'd saved her. Standing in her kitchen, watching her stand guard at her own window like a soldier in a war no one had declared, I finally understood that I hadn't saved her at all. I'd just changed which cage she lived in, and then congratulated myself on the carpentry.

Ada rose quietly. "I'll check the lane," she murmured, and slipped out, giving us the room the way she gave people space — completely, and only because she chose to.

Wren and I stood in silence while the stew went cold. Outside, the mill wheel turned its lazy circle, indifferent as ever.

"When I was little," she said finally, not turning around, "you told me storms weren't dangerous. You said they were just the sky deciding what it wanted to say. That everything loud was honest." A breath. "Was any of it honest, Silas? Any of what you did? Or was I just the price tag?"

The question had no good answer, so I gave her the true one. "I don't know yet. But I'm going to find out. And when I do, I'm coming back here, and I'm going to be so boring you'll beg me to leave."

She turned. For half a heartbeat — half a heartbeat, no more — I saw my sister underneath the hollow eyes, the girl who'd dared me to swim in February, fierce and laughing and unafraid.

"If you come back," she said, "come back boring. Promise me."

"I promise nothing. I'm a known liar."

"Yes," she said softly. "You are. Now go out the back before the roof man gets curious. There's a gap in the hedge behind the woodshed. And Silas—" She caught my sleeve as I passed, and pressed something into my palm: a small tin whistle, dented, the one I'd carved her when I was eleven and couldn't yet whistle myself. "If you ever need me truly, send a boy with this. Not a letter. Letters get read. Whistles get remembered."

I closed my hand around it. It was warm from her pocket, and it weighed nothing, and it was the heaviest thing I'd carried in twelve years.

* * *

Ada was waiting in the hedge-shadow, and she read my face in about four seconds, which was three seconds longer than usual.

"Well?" she said, as we cut through the woodshed and out toward the south fields. "Do you feel lighter? Doors opened, ghosts faced, all that?"

"No."

"No," she agreed. "You never do. That's the swindle of it. Everyone tells you facing things sets you free, and then you face them and discover they have *friends*." She walked a few steps in silence. Then, without looking at me, in a voice deliberately light: "The agents. The ones who visit her. Did they ever mention what you sold? What the bargain actually was?"

The whistle pressed against my palm inside my fist. *A soul,* said the truth. *The heir's soul. Sold to a broker beneath the mountain. And the girl walking beside you carries it without knowing.*

"A stranger's soul," I said. "It doesn't matter anymore."

Ada was quiet for three steps. Four. Five. Long enough for me to notice, and long enough for her to know I'd notice.

"Right," she said at last, and her voice was pleasant and unreadable and utterly wrong. "Doesn't matter."

She walked on ahead of me down the south road, and I watched her back and told myself the lie had landed clean, the way I'd told myself for twelve years that Wren was safe.

Behind us, in a kitchen on Tanner's Row, a woman stood at her window watching two figures shrink into the distance — one carrying a storm, one carrying a soul — and I would think about that image later, in darker hours, and wonder which of us she pitied more.

What I didn't see — what I couldn't, walking behind her with my fist closed around a child's tin whistle — was the way Ada's eyes flicked sideways as we walked. The way her gaze lingered on my clenched hand, on my face, on the exact shape of the silence I was holding.

Memory-thieves, I would learn much too late, don't need you to open your doors.

They just need you to stand close enough to the lock.
