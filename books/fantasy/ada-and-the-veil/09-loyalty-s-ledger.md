## 9. Loyalty's Ledger

There are three rules to stealing a memory, and I invented all of them, so I can tell you they're good ones.

One: never take from someone you love, because you'll flinch, and flinching is how thieves get caught.

Two: never take from someone who's watching you, because eyes are anchors and anchored memories tear ugly.

Three: never take more than you need, because a mind is a house and every theft leaves a door open behind you.

I'd broken rule three exactly once in my career, and the man had wept for an hour over a dead dog he could no longer name, and I had carried that dog's name around in my head ever since like a stone I couldn't put down. Biscuit. His name was Biscuit. I didn't even like dogs.

Rule one I'd never tested, because I didn't love anyone, which I had always considered a professional advantage.

Rule two was about to become a problem, because Silas Halloran watched me constantly. Not obviously — he was too careful for obvious — but I'd caught it a dozen times on the road beneath the rain: the way his gaze snagged on my hands when I flexed my fingers, the way he went quiet when I talked about my craft, the way he laughed a half-beat late at my jokes, like a man translating them into another language before deciding they were funny.

He knew what I was. Of course he did. He'd seen me work back in Greyhollow, before the Ministry burned everything, and he hadn't run, which meant either he trusted me or he thought he had nothing left worth stealing.

Both possibilities interested me enormously.

* * *

We made camp in the ribs of an old watchtower, the kind the free cities had built back when they still believed the mountain court might come calling with armies instead of trade delegations. The stones were black with old soot. Rain came through the roof in threads, silver in the firelight, and Silas sat with his palms out toward the flames like a man warming himself on something he didn't trust.

"Your turn," he said. We'd been trading lessons since the second day of travel — he taught me to read weather the way I read faces, and I taught him the grammar of memory: how recollections stack, how false ones are mortared in, how to spot a wall that's been moved. "Show me the difference between a memory that's been lived and one that's been given."

"You already know this."

"I know it the way a fish knows water. Show me how it looks from outside."

I sighed, because he was doing the thing where he made curiosity sound like pedagogy, and because I wanted him to keep talking. His voice had gotten easier to listen to over the miles. That was its own kind of warning sign.

"Fine. Give me your hand."

He hesitated. I watched the hesitation happen — a flicker across his face, quick as heat lightning, there and banked. Then he set his hand in mine, palm up, calloused from rope and coin-strings, warm past what the fire explained.

"Now," I said, "I'm not going to take anything. Watch." I let my talent rise to the surface of my skin, that familiar warmth like standing near an open oven, and I turned it outward instead of inward — a lantern, not a hook. "This is a reading light. It shows me the shape of what's there without lifting anything. Every thief learns it first because it's how you case a house before you rob it."

"And what do you see?"

The question was casual. His pulse under my fingers was not. It had picked up, quick and shallow, like a rabbit hearing a hawk think.

I looked.

I want to be honest about this part, because honesty is the whole point of what happened next, or rather the whole point of what failed to happen next. I looked because he asked me to look, and then I kept looking after he stopped asking, because the surface of his mind was lit up like a market street at festival time and I have never once in my life walked past a lit window without pressing my nose to the glass.

Surface memories sit shallow. They're the top layer of sediment — today, yesterday, last week. Most people's surfaces are boring. Breakfast. Aches. A song stuck in the skull. Silas's surface should have been the road, the rain, the watchtower, me.

Instead it was storm-light.

Not the storm overhead — this was older, pressed flat like a flower in a book, and it took me a moment to understand I wasn't seeing weather at all. I was seeing a room. A beautiful room, wrong-shaped, carved out of the dark underneath a mountain, its ceiling lost in shadow, its walls hung with banners the color of deep water. And in the middle of it, a table. And at the table, a figure made of ledger-pages and patience, and —

And a soul.

I don't know how else to say it. I've held a thousand memories and none of them prepared me for what a soul looks like from the inside of someone else's guilt. It was small. That's the thing that will stay with me. All the songs make souls out to be suns and storms and burning rivers, and this one was small, a knot of pale light cupped in two hands, trembling like a caught bird, and the hands holding it were younger than the hands I was touching now, and they were shaking.

*Twelve years ago,* said the memory, in the wordless way memories speak. *Twelve years ago, in the court beneath the mountain.*

The Broker's voice came through like a bell struck underwater: *One soul, freely given, against one life, freely spared. Do you consent, storm-caller?*

And the younger Silas — thinner, wilder-eyed, storm-light crawling visibly under his skin the way it never quite did now — said, *I consent,* and the hands opened, and the small light rose off his palms like a spark leaving a bonfire, and somewhere behind him a girl's voice cried out — Wren, it had to be Wren, the sister he never talked about except in the tense of someone talking about a grave — and the light went into a vessel, a shape I couldn't see clearly, a silhouette wrapped in shadow at the edge of the memory, and the Broker smiled with a mouth full of arithmetic, and —

I pulled back.

My hand was shaking. So was his, though I don't think either of us had noticed until that moment, and we sat there in the ruined watchtower with our joined hands between us like something neither of us wanted to claim.

"Well?" he said. His voice was steady. His eyes weren't. "What does a lived memory look like, from outside?"

"Lived memories have edges," I said. "Given ones are smooth all the way through. Yours—" I swallowed. "Yours are very lived."

Something went through his face. Relief, I thought at the time. Relief that I'd seen nothing, learned nothing. I catalogued it as relief and filed it away and hated myself a little for the filing, because rule three says never take more than you need, and I had just taken far more than I needed, and I hadn't even used my hands.

* * *

Here is what I saw, laid out plain, because I spent the whole night laying it out plain and it refused to come out any other way:

Silas Halloran sold a soul to the court beneath the mountain twelve years ago. He sold it to buy his sister's life. The soul went into a vessel — a carrier, a keeper, someone the Broker bound it into — and Silas walked away with Wren breathing and the light gone out of his own hands, and he had been living in the dark ever since, selling luck-knots in a city that forbade the very thing he was.

That was the story. Clean, terrible, finished twelve years ago.

Except.

Except that prophecies don't finish. Except that a dead prophet had sketched my face beside the words *storm-caller* and a date, and except that for three nights running I had dreamed of a room with a ceiling lost in shadow, and except — and this was the part I kept circling like a tongue finding a cracked tooth — except that when the soul rose off young Silas's palms, I had felt something answer it.

Inside me. Behind my eyes. In the locked vault I'd found in chapter — no. In the locked vault I'd found in my own head, weeks ago, the one I hadn't made, the one that hummed at night with a light I told myself was imagination.

The vault had hummed when the memory-soul passed by. Like recognizing like. Like a dog hearing its own name in a stranger's mouth.

No, I told myself. No, that's absurd. You're a memory-thief; your head is full of other people's echoes; of course things resonate in there. The soul in Silas's memory went into some vessel in the mountain court, some poor conscripted keeper, and it's been there twelve years, and the reason the prophecy has my face in it is coincidence, or mistake, or the prophet being deliberately obscure the way prophets are contractually obligated to be.

The alternative was not thinkable. The alternative was that I was the vessel. That I had been carrying a stolen soul — *his* stolen soul, the one he sold, the debt he owed — without knowing it, and that every mile I'd walked beside him, every joke, every lesson, every hour of rain-shared silence, had been a lie wearing the costume of accident. That he hadn't found me at his stall by chance. That the wary bargain wasn't wary at all but choreographed. That I was not a person to him but a parcel, and the journey to the mountain was not a quest but a delivery.

I lay awake under the broken roof and listened to the rain and turned both stories over, and both of them fit, and that was the worst part. Guilty men and innocent men look identical from the outside. That's why I steal memories instead of trusting faces.

So why not just take it? Why not lean over in the grey half-light, while he slept with his back against the soot-black stone, and lift the truth out of him whole? I'd done harder extractions on sleeping strangers. He'd handed me his palm once already; the door was mapped. One clean pull and I'd know whether the vessel in the memory was me or a stranger, whether his kindness on the road was real or logistics, whether I was traveling with a man or a courier.

I got as far as sitting up.

And here is the thing I keep coming back to, the thing I'll be turning over long after this road ends, whichever road that turns out to be. When I looked at him sleeping — rain-threaded firelight, hollow cheeks, hands curled loose around nothing the way powerless men's hands learn to curl — I found that I didn't want the truth if the price of it was taking it from him.

Which was new. Which was alarming. Rule one exists because love makes thieves flinch, and I had always been safe from rule one, and now here I was, flinching, and I didn't even know yet what to call what was happening to me, only that it made my hands unsteady and my craft unreliable and my ledger unbalanced.

Because that's what it came down to, in the end. Loyalty keeps a ledger. So does hunger. The hunger said: *take it, know it, protect yourself.* The loyalty — new, unwitnessed, embarrassing — said: *he gave you his hand open, and whatever he's hiding, he's hiding it at cost, and a thief who robs a man of his secrets while he sleeps is just a burglar with better tools.*

I lay back down. I did not take it. I told myself it was strategy — better to let him confess on his own schedule, better to watch, better to keep my knowledge hidden — and maybe some of it was strategy. But some of it, the part I didn't examine too closely, was simpler than that.

I said nothing.

All the next day I said nothing. I laughed at his impression of a Ministry clerk, I let him win an argument about which pass through the hills was less haunted, I taught him to spot a false memory by the way it catches light, and every time his hand brushed mine passing the waterskin I felt the ghost of that small trembling soul and said nothing, nothing, nothing.

It was nearly dusk when we crested the ridge above the valley road and saw the smoke.

A waystation stood at the bottom of the pass — the kind of place that sold bad stew and worse beds to anyone with coin, that had never been anything to anyone except shelter. It was burning. Not smoldering — burning, whole and eager, flames standing off the roof like banners, and the road below it was full of soldiers in colors I didn't recognize and movement, organized movement, the kind that means schedules.

Silas went very still beside me. "They're ahead," he said quietly. "Whoever's marching — they're days ahead of where they should be."

And then, from the smoke and the crowd below, a single rider broke away from the column and started up the ridge toward us. Unhurried. Deliberate. Carrying something white on a pole, the way you carry a flag when you want to talk before you want to fight.

Silas's hand found my arm. Under his fingers, in the vault behind my eyes, something ancient and patient began, very softly, to sing.
