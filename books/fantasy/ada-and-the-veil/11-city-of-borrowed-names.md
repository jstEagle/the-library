## 11. City of Borrowed Names

The city had no name on any map, which was the first thing I liked about it. The second was that nobody asked questions. The third, discovered within an hour of walking through its crooked gates, was that half the people in it were carrying something that wasn't theirs.

I felt them before I saw them. That was new.

It started as a hum at the base of my skull, the same frequency as the vault—that locked room in my head I'd been trying very hard not to think about since the prophet's cellar. But out here the hum wasn't just mine. It echoed. Every few steps, another voice joined it, faint and off-key, like a choir tuning up in a cathedral with terrible acoustics.

"Stop staring," Silas muttered beside me. He'd pulled his hood low and his shoulders higher, doing his best impression of a man who sold luck-knots for a living and had never once called down lightning. He was almost convincing. Almost.

"I'm not staring."

"You're staring like a woman reading a ledger over someone's shoulder."

"I'm a memory-thief. Reading ledgers over shoulders is my entire profession." I turned to look at him properly. "There are dozens of them, Silas. People with other people's things tucked behind their ribs. Can't you feel it?"

He could. I knew he could, because his jaw did the thing it did when he was swallowing a truth whole—worked once, twice, went still. "Refugee cities collect strays," he said. "People running from the mountain's wars carry what they could grab. Sometimes what they could grab wasn't theirs to grab."

"And nobody minds?"

"Nobody *knows*. That's rather the point of this place." He gestured at the street: leaning houses stitched together with rope and prayer, market stalls selling soup and secrets in equal measure, children chasing a cat that had clearly won every previous encounter. "Everyone here is someone they aren't. It's the most honest lie in the free cities."

I should have felt at home. A city of borrowed names, of stolen goods worn like secondhand coats—I'd been in that business twelve years myself. Instead the hum in my skull grew louder with every step, until by the time we found lodging above a dye-shop—the landlady charged us in coins and didn't ask why our hands shook—it felt less like a sound and more like a hand pressing against the inside of my forehead.

*Let me out*, said nothing, in no words at all.

I didn't sleep that first night. I lay on a straw mattress listening to Silas breathe in the next room—he insisted on separate rooms, insisted loudly, in the way of men who insist about things that don't matter—and I pressed my palm flat against my temple and listened back.

Something listened with me.

* * *

The woman who changed everything ran a stall near the well, selling buttons.

That sounds like nothing. It was everything. Her buttons were arranged by feeling instead of color—grief in one dish, joy in another, a third dish labeled only *regret* in handwriting that trembled. And when I stopped to look, she looked back, and her eyes did something eyes don't do: they flickered, quick as a struck match, with light that wasn't hers.

Old light. Deep light. The kind you find at the bottom of wells.

"You feel it too," she said. Not a question. She spoke the way people speak in sickrooms, quiet and direct, past all pretense. "Sit down, girl. You've got the look of someone carrying more than she packed."

"I'm just browsing."

"Nobody browses grief-buttons at dawn." She nudged a stool toward me with her foot. "Maren," she said. "Formerly of the court beneath the mountain. Formerly a great many things. Sit."

I sat. Behind me I heard Silas shift his weight, heard the particular creak of a man deciding whether to intervene, and then—wisely—the sound of him pretending to examine a fruit cart instead.

"Maren," I repeated. "And what do you sell besides buttons?"

"Company, mostly. For people like us." She tapped her own temple. "I've carried a piece of my grandmother's soul since the mountain fell to the usurper's grandfather. She gave it to me the night the courts burned, so some part of her would survive outside their reach. Forty years now, she rides along. She hums when I'm happy. She weeps when I lie." Maren smiled, and for a moment the light behind her eyes flared bright and old and *sad*. "You've got something bigger than a grandmother's keepsake. I can tell from here. Your passenger doesn't hum. It *waits*."

My mouth went dry. "My what?"

"Don't insult us both." She sorted buttons with fingers that never quite touched them. "There are carriers in this city—a dozen, maybe two. We meet at the tannery on rest-days, those of us who know what we are. You should come. Because whatever you're carrying, girl, you're carrying it wrong, and if you keep carrying it wrong, it will carry *you*."

* * *

The tannery meeting was nothing like I expected, which is to say it was nothing at all like thieves, smugglers, or conspirators. There were eleven of us, seated on benches among the reeking vats, passing around a kettle of chicory like the world's most haunted sewing circle.

A ferryman who carried the last breath of a drowned king. Twin sisters sharing a single stolen name between them, each half-hoping the other would claim it. A boy of maybe fifteen who had swallowed a god's boredom and couldn't stop yawning. An old soldier with a stranger's courage lodged under his breastbone like a second heart—"it beats before mine does," he told me, tapping his chest, "every time there's trouble. Saved my life six times. Cost me every friend who wondered why I flinched before the arrows came."

They talked about their passengers the way farmers talk about weather. With respect. With resignation. With the occasional dark joke.

"So," I said, when the chicory had gone around twice. "How do you know? That it's a soul, and not just... a thing you stole? I steal memories. Memories sit still. They don't—"

"*Wait*," three of them said together.

The ferryman leaned forward. He was missing teeth and unbothered by it. "Memories are dead things, love. Beautiful dead things, like pressed flowers. A soul's alive. You'll know because it does three things. It dreams through you. It wants through you. And it grieves through you." He held up fingers. "Dreams first. Tell me—since you took whatever you took—have your dreams gotten strange? Not nightmares. Strange. Big. Old."

I thought of the dreams I hadn't told Silas about. Halls of black stone lit by veins of gold. A throne carved from a single tooth of the mountain. Faces turning toward me in crowds, faces I somehow knew, faces that *mourned* me. A bell tolling somewhere far below, and everyone in the dream falling silent to listen, because the bell meant—

"The bell means a death in the royal line," the old soldier said quietly, watching my face. "Your passenger has been showing you its home."

"It's not a passenger. It's not anything. I'm a thief, not a—"

"A thief knows stolen goods," Maren said gently. "Look at it honestly. Is it a purse of coin? A bundle of papers? Or is it a *cell*?"

The word landed in my chest like a dropped stone.

Because I knew. God help me, I'd known since the prophet's cellar, since I'd put my hands against the walls of that locked place in my mind and felt—not storage, not a cache, not a thief's tidy hoard—but bars. A door that locked from the *outside*. Something behind it, pacing.

"When did you receive it?" the ferryman asked. "Do you remember taking it?"

And there it was, the vertigo, the floor dropping out of my own skull. "No. That's the thing. I have no memory of acquiring it. Twelve years of thefts and scores and jobs, and there's just... a gap. A door I never made, locked from a side I can't reach."

Silence around the kettle. Eleven carriers looking at me with pity, and pity was worse than horror.

"Someone put it in you," Maren said softly. "Someone used you as a hiding place. Or a prison. Or—" she hesitated—"a vessel. Carriers who don't remember the taking are rare, girl. And they're never accidents."

* * *

I walked back to the dye-shop through streets gone golden with evening, and the hum in my skull had changed key. It wasn't waiting anymore.

It was *hoping*.

That night I didn't fight the sleep. I lay down, closed my eyes, and opened the inner door as wide as I dared—which was barely a crack, but a crack was enough.

The dream took me like deep water takes a stone.

Black stone halls. Gold veins pulsing slow as heartbeat in the walls. And people—hundreds, thousands, kneeling in tiers down a vast cavern-court, all of them silent, all of them facing a throne of mountain-tooth where a figure sat wrapped in shadow and starlight both.

Not me. Not quite me. Someone wearing my posture, my restlessness, my way of holding their chin when thinking. The heir. The true heir of the court beneath the mountain, alive behind my eyes for twelve years, dreaming of a home that had been stolen twice—once by the usurper, and once by whoever had hidden its soul inside a memory-thief who couldn't even remember the crime done to her.

The heir turned. Looked at me across the impossible distance between us—soul to thief, prisoner to cell.

And didn't rage. Didn't beg.

The heir *thanked* me. Bowed, actually, the way you bow to a stranger who has carried your child through a storm without being asked—gratitude so heavy and undeserved it buckled something in my chest.

*I'm sorry*, I tried to say. *I didn't know. If I'd known—*

The dream shifted. Showed me the throne again, and the shadow on it thickening, coiling, wearing a crown that didn't fit. Showed me armies massing in the dark below the free cities. Showed me a deadline written in dust and blood:

*Winter.*

Then the heir did the cruelest, kindest thing. It showed me a memory—not its own. A human memory. A market stall in Greyhollow, twelve years ago, and a young man with storm-light already crawling under his skin, kneeling before something vast and hungry, trading away a soul he had no right to trade, for a life he had no right to buy.

I woke with tears on my face and dawn in the window, and I didn't know whose grief I was crying—mine, the heir's, or the boy in the memory's.

Downstairs, Silas was already awake. I could hear him not-asking through the floorboards, the careful silence of a man sitting on a secret so large it must ache.

I stood at the window a long time, watching the borrowed-named city wake below, and I made myself say it aloud, to the stranger behind my eyes:

"I see you. Whoever you are—whatever was done to both of us—I see you. And I'm going to take you home."

The hum answered. Not in words. In warmth. In the unmistakable feeling, ancient and patient and unbearably sad, of someone who has waited twelve years finally letting themselves believe the wait might end.

Then, softer, threading through the warmth like a needle pulling thread: an image. A face. Young, storm-eyed, kneeling.

*Him*, the heir said, without words, without sound, in the oldest language there is.

*I know him.*

*I know what he traded.*

*Ask him.*
