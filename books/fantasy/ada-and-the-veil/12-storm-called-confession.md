## 12. Storm-Called Confession

There is an art to lying, and I was once very good at it. Twelve years of practice will do that to a man. You learn the small things first: how to hold your hands still, how to let your eyes go tired instead of shifty, how to answer a question with a question so the asker ends up defending themselves for having asked. Charm-selling helped. Nobody suspects the man selling luck-knots of harboring a hurricane behind his ribs.

But Ada Vey had spent her life walking into other people's heads and taking what she wanted, and I began to suspect that somewhere along the road beneath the rain, while I was teaching her how to salt a memory so it wouldn't rot, she'd been learning something about me too.

She cornered me at dawn on the third day out of the waystation ashes.

We'd made camp in the lee of a fallen watchtower — one of the old border forts from before the Ministry, back when magic and men shared a table without knives coming out. The stones still held a faint warmth, some residual enchantment the Unmaking never bothered to scrub this far from civilization. Ada sat across the dead fire from me, turning a strip of dried apple over in her fingers like it owed her money, and she didn't do any of the things people do before they ask a hard question. No throat-clearing. No circling. She just looked at me with those grey eyes that saw too much, and said:

"Silas. Whose soul did you sell?"

The morning went very quiet. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

I want it noted, for the record of whatever gods keep ledgers on men like me, that my first instinct was not to lie. My first instinct was to run. There's a difference, though neither would have gotten me anywhere. Running, at least, has the decency to be honest about what it is.

"Good morning to you too," I said.

"You talk in your sleep." She said it flatly, no accusation in it, which somehow made it worse. "You've been talking in your sleep since the waystation. Names. Numbers. A price. And last night you said *the court beneath the mountain* like you were reading off a receipt."

"I say a lot of things in my sleep. Once I recited an entire charm-catalogue. Very dull listening, I'd imagine."

"You said *forgive me*." Her voice didn't rise. That was the thing about Ada — she never rose to anger, she descended into it, calm as deep water. "You said it eleven times. I counted."

I looked at the fire pit. Cold ash. I looked at the horizon, where the mountain we were walking toward stood up against the pale sky like a broken tooth. I looked everywhere except at her, which was, I realize now, the first mistake of many, because avoidance is its own confession to someone who reads faces the way I read weather.

"Ada—"

"Don't." She set down the apple strip. "Don't do the thing where you make a joke and then deflect and then make another joke until I get tired. I've watched you do it for two hundred miles. I'm not tired. I've got nothing but time and a very specific kind of patience, and you should know that of all the trades I could have learned, I chose the one where people tell you the truth because they can't help it."

*Because they can't help it.* She said it lightly, but there was iron under it. Memory-thieves don't always need their craft. Sometimes they just need a campfire, a cold morning, and a man who's been carrying a secret long enough that it's started to leak.

I thought about lying well. I really did. I ran through the whole repertoire — the partial truth dressed as full disclosure, the tragic backstory with the incriminating details sanded off, the noble self-sacrifice gambit where I confessed to something smaller to keep her from digging toward something larger. Twelve years of craft, all of it lined up and ready.

And then I remembered the herald's blood cooling on the road behind us, and the way I'd felt when I killed him — not sick, not shaken, but *certain*, certain in a way I hadn't been certain of anything since Greyhollow. I had crossed a line for her. I had burned a man's life out with storm-light in front of witnesses. If I was going to be a man who did that, then perhaps I owed her more than craftsmanship.

Perhaps. In theory. In the clean bright country of good intentions.

What I actually said was: "It was a stranger's."

* * *

Here is how a lie works when it leaves your mouth. It doesn't come out whole. It comes out in pieces, and each piece has to find its footing, and you stand there watching it walk around the campfire wearing your voice, and part of you — the part that used to be a storm-caller, the part that knows every bolt of lightning carries a lie inside it, a bright fork claiming to be the sky's only opinion — that part waits to see if anyone notices the seams.

"It was twelve years ago," I said. "Before Greyhollow. Before any of this." I kept my hands flat on my knees, the way I'd taught myself. Steady hands. Honest-looking hands. "My sister was dying. Not sick-dying. Cursed-dying. A debt came due that our family had been running from for three generations, and the collectors don't take coin. They take... weight. Something heavy enough to balance the ledger."

"And a soul is heavy," Ada said quietly.

"A soul is the heaviest thing there is." That much was true. Gods, that much was true enough to choke on. "The court beneath the mountain keeps them. Vaults them. They call it the Deep Treasury, and they pay for what they take — protection, cures, favors. Whatever the seller needs most. And I needed my sister to live."

Wren. I said her name in my head and immediately wished I hadn't, because saying it even silently made the next lie easier, and lies are like storms that way — the second bolt always follows the first along the same scorched path.

"So you sold a stranger's soul to save her." Ada's brow creased. "Whose? How does a man even come to possess a stranger's soul?"

And here — here is where shame does its cleverest work. Because the true story was so close to the false one. The same year. The same desperation. The same Broker with his smile like a ledger-line. All I had to change was one name, one face, one single word, and everything else could stay honest, and honesty makes such excellent mortar for holding a lie together.

"A client," I said. "Back when I wasn't selling luck-knots. Back when I was..." I gestured vaguely at the sky, at the storm I wasn't naming. "People trusted me with things. Precious things. And one of them — a dying old woman, or so she claimed — asked me to carry her soul to the Treasury herself. Said she was done with it. Said living had become a chore and she wanted to trade it for a peaceful end rather than a lingering one. And I carried it. And on the way, I learned what Wren's collectors actually wanted, and I..." I spread my hands. "I made a substitution. One soul for another. Hers for hers. The Treasury can't tell the difference once they're vaulted. Souls all weigh the same in the dark."

It was good. It was *very* good. It explained my guilt, my exile, my nightmares, my flinching at the word *Treasury*. It gave Ada a villain who was already dead and a sin that, while ugly, had a certain terrible nobility to it — a man trading one death for one life. It even explained why the prophecy might name me: the storm-caller who sold a soul to the court beneath the mountain. The words fit the prophecy's shape perfectly.

They fit because I'd built the lie directly over the truth, the way you build a stage-set over a real house. Same walls. Different paint.

Ada was silent for a long moment. The wind moved through the ruined tower above us, whistling through arrow-slits like the fort itself was trying to remember how to breathe.

"And the old woman?" she asked finally. "The one whose soul you took instead?"

"Gone," I said, and this part cost me nothing, because it was true — just not in the direction she thought. "Whatever she wanted that soul for, she got it. Or she didn't. Either way she walked away free and I walked away owing the Treasury a debt I've been paying in hiding ever since."

"You stole from a dying woman to save your sister."

"Yes."

"And you've hated yourself for it for twelve years."

"Yes." That word came out rougher than I intended. True things do that; they catch on the way out.

Ada nodded slowly. She picked up the apple strip again, considered it, put it down again. When she looked up, her expression had softened into something I recognized and dreaded in equal measure: pity. Warm, generous, entirely misplaced pity.

"That's why you flinch when I touch your memories," she said. "That's why you won't let me near the older ones. You're afraid I'll find her. The old woman. Find out what she really looked like when you took what was hers."

"Something like that," I said, and smiled, and the smile held, because I have had twelve years of practice at that too.

* * *

She believed me.

I want to be clear about what that moment felt like, because I suspect it's the closest I'll ever come to understanding drowning. Relief first — enormous, vertiginous relief, the whole sky opening — and then, underneath it, cold water closing over my head. Because she believed me, and being believed by Ada Vey turned out to feel less like absolution and more like theft. She handed me her trust across that dead fire the way she'd hand a coin to a charm-seller, and I took it, and somewhere in the Deep Treasury beneath the mountain there is a vault with a soul in it that knows exactly what I am, and souls, unlike sisters and unlike liars, cannot be taught to keep quiet forever.

We packed camp. We walked. The mountain grew by degrees, the way guilt grows — slowly enough to ignore, then suddenly too large to see around.

For most of the day she was lighter. I could see it in her stride. The question that had been sitting between us since the waystation had been answered, filed, closed. She even laughed once — at my impression of a Ministry inquisitor trying to look menacing in market-day mud — and the sound of it went through me like a hook through soft flesh, because I knew now what I was laughing on top of. Every easy hour we'd bought with that lie was borrowed against a payment I couldn't avoid. The prophecy didn't care about my craftsmanship. The Broker didn't either. And the heir — the heir whose soul rode behind Ada's eyes, dreaming her dreams, watching me through her gaze — the heir knew the truth the way stone knows rain.

Twice that day she caught me looking at her strangely. Twice I covered it. The second time, she held my gaze a beat longer than the joke required, and I saw something flicker there — not suspicion, not yet, but its distant cousin. The instinct that tells you a floor is rotten before your foot goes through.

Memory-thief. Of course she'd feel it. I hadn't stolen a memory from her, but I'd stolen something adjacent. I'd stolen the truth, and the truth is just a memory that hasn't happened yet.

Near dusk we crested a ridge and saw it: the mountain's shoulder, black against a bruised-orange sky, and below it, faint as breath on glass, a light. Not firelight. Not lantern-light. Something colder and older, rising out of the rock itself, and beside me Ada went still in a way I'd learned to read.

"It's beautiful," she whispered. Then, quieter, almost to herself: "Why does it feel familiar?"

And there it was. The heir, stirring. Reaching up through her like a swimmer reaching for the surface, recognizing home.

"The Gate of Teeth," I said, because saying anything else required a courage I'd already spent that morning. "Tomorrow we reach it. After that, no more lies will fit through — the Gate strips travelers down to what they are. Disguises, guises, false names. It takes them all."

Ada glanced at me. Something in her face was unreadable, and for a man who reads weather in skin, unreadable is the worst forecast there is.

"Lucky thing," she said lightly, "that neither of us is wearing one."

I laughed. It came out steady.

And far below us, in the dark under the mountain, in a vault where all souls weigh the same, something that was not a stranger's soul waited — patient as stone, patient as prophecy — to be brought home by the man who sold it, whether he confessed the truth or not.
