## 13. The Gate of Teeth

*Silas*

The mountain did not look like a mountain so much as a wound that had healed wrong.

I'd seen it once before, twelve years ago, from the back of a debt-wagon with my wrists bound and my sister's name in my mouth like a prayer I couldn't afford to finish saying. Then it had been a silhouette at the end of a long gray valley, a black molar against a bruised sky. Now, standing at the valley's mouth with Ada beside me and three days of cold rain in our boots, it was worse. Closer. Mountains are supposed to be distant things, the kind of thing you point at. This one pointed back.

"That's it?" Ada said.

"That's it."

"It looks like a tooth."

"Everyone says that." I shifted my pack higher on my shoulder. "The locals used to call it the Molar. The court beneath calls it something older that translates roughly to *the place where oaths go to be digested.*"

"Wonderful. And we're walking into its mouth because—"

"Because the prophecy says so, and because every other road leads to an army, and because you asked me not to lie to you anymore, and the truth is I don't have a better plan." I said it lightly. Lightness is a skill, like charm-selling. You learn to make the cheap thing gleam.

Ada looked at me for a long moment. She had gotten good, these last weeks on the road, at reading me — which was inconvenient, because I had spent twelve years becoming unreadable, and she was dismantling the work one glance at a time.

"You're scared," she said.

"I'm *cold*. There's a difference. Fear has better posture."

She almost smiled. Then she looked past me, up the valley, and the smile died before it finished being born.

The gate was moving.

I don't mean doors opening. I mean the mouth of the mountain — the great cleft in its face, tall as a cathedral and twice as hungry — was *chewing*. Stone ground against stone in slow, patient cycles, like a jaw working through something it intended to swallow eventually no matter how long it took. The sound reached us a heartbeat later, a deep tectonic grinding that I felt in my back teeth and, less pleasantly, in the storm under my skin, which woke and pressed against my wards like a cat against a closed door.

"The trials start here," I said. "Everything past this line gets... examined."

"Examined by a rock."

"By what the rock *is*, technically. It's not stone all the way down. It's judgment all the way down. The stone is just what judgment looks like when it's tired."

Ada gave me the look she gave me when I was being evasive, which was most of her looks lately. "And our disguises?"

I touched the charm at my throat — the one I'd carved myself, the finest work I'd done since Greyhollow, a knot of ash-wood and river-silver that held my storm-light down the way a lid holds steam. Twelve years of craft in a pendant the size of a thumbnail. "We'll be fine," I said, and heard immediately how it sounded, and added, "probably," which made it worse.

"You know what you told me in the waystation," Ada said quietly. "About lying. About how the small lies are just big lies wearing coats."

"I remember."

"So let's not wear coats into the teeth."

I wanted to argue. Instead I took out the charm, turned it over once in my fingers — feeling the faint hum of the thing caged inside me shift toward my hand like a dog hearing its name — and put it away in the inner pocket where I kept the things I couldn't bear to lose and couldn't bear to use.

"No coat," I said.

We walked into the mouth of the mountain together.

* * *

*Ada*

Here is what nobody tells you about being eaten: it's mostly waiting.

The passage behind the gate was wide enough for six abreast and lit by nothing — not torches, not crystals, none of the pretty light sources storybooks promise you underground. It was lit by *attention*. The walls paid attention to us. I could feel it the way you feel someone reading over your shoulder: a pressure on the back of the neck, a prickle between the shoulder blades, the distinct sense that somewhere a very old ledger had opened and a very dry quill had begun to write.

Silas walked ahead of me, shoulders set, hands loose at his sides. He'd been quiet since the gate. Not his usual quiet — he had at least four kinds of quiet, and I'd been cataloguing them the way I catalogue anything I intend to steal later. There was seller's quiet, when he was calculating. Guilt quiet, when he thought about his sister. There was the new quiet, the one that had started after the herald, after the blood, after he'd stood over the body shaking and said *I crossed a line and I can't uncross it* — that one I didn't poke at yet. And now there was this: fear quiet. He wore it badly. His whole life he'd apparently worn fear badly, which was somehow the most endearing thing about him.

The first trial found us forty paces in.

It came as a smell. That's how it starts, Silas had told me — the mountain doesn't announce itself, it *asks*. The smell was woodsmoke and wet wool and something sweet underneath, like honey left too long in a hot room, and with it came a memory that wasn't mine.

I stopped walking.

A kitchen. A woman's hands kneading dough, flour to the elbows, humming a song in a language I didn't speak but understood anyway, the way you understand warmth. A child at the table. The memory was soft-edged and generous and it was offering itself to me like a coin, like a purchase: *take this, keep this, this is yours now, all you have to do is stay.*

"Ada." Silas's voice, low. "Don't take it."

"I know." My voice came out strange. "It's a bribe."

"It's a test dressed as a gift. The mountain offers you the life you might have had if you'd never come here. If you turn back now, it lets you keep the feeling of it forever." He wasn't looking at me. He was looking straight ahead, jaw tight, and I realized the air around him smelled different — rain on hot stone, ozone, the sharp green scent of a storm about to break. The mountain was talking to him too. Offering him something. And whatever it was, he was holding it at arm's length with both hands and visible effort.

"What's yours?" I asked.

"Nothing," he said, which was a lie, and then, because he'd promised, because we'd shaken on it under a leaking roof two weeks ago: "My sister. Alive. The version where I never sold anything, never left, never—" His breath went ragged. "It's very persuasive. Don't listen to it. Keep walking."

So we walked, side by side through a corridor made of temptation, each carrying our own private kitchen, our own private smoke. At some point my hand found his. I don't remember deciding that. Some things the body knows before the thief does.

The second trial was honest work, at least. It stripped us.

There was no warning. One moment the passage was dark and attentive; the next, light came from everywhere at once — white, total, merciless, the kind of light that has opinions — and I felt it go through me like a hand going through a drawer.

It took my traveling cloak first. Not off my shoulders — *out from under me*, unmade, unwoven, returned to the idea of cloth. Then my knife. Then, horribly, my face.

I felt my own features loosen. The little adjustments every thief makes without thinking — the softened jaw, the forgettable eyes, the way I'd worn *nobody in particular* like a second skin for fifteen years — the light peeled them away like bark. For one vertiginous second I stood in a corridor of judgment wearing nothing but my actual self, the face my mother gave me, the one I hadn't seen since I was nine years old, and I nearly wept, because I'd forgotten her. I'd forgotten what I looked like when I wasn't performing.

Beside me, Silas screamed.

Not loudly. That was the terrible part. It was a small sound, bitten off, the sound of a man swallowing a scream and choking on it, and when I turned, the light was pouring *out* of him.

Storm-light. That's the only word for it, though it's a bad one — there was no storm above us, no clouds, no rain, just Silas, standing in the white glare with lightning living under his skin like veins of fire in dark glass. It crawled up his throat and lit his jaw and blazed in his eyes until they weren't eyes anymore, they were weather. The charm in his inner pocket glowed through the fabric like a coal in a closed fist. The mountain's light pulled at him, peeling back layer after layer of the careful gray man I'd met at a market stall — the charm-seller, the wry voice, the human proportions of him — and underneath, revealed in flickers, was something vast and barely leashed and *beautiful* in the way avalanches are beautiful.

He was on his knees. I hadn't seen him fall.

"Let it see you," I said. I didn't know where the words came from. Maybe from the heir — the locked vault in my head had been humming louder ever since we entered the valley, a low note of recognition, of *homecoming*, that terrified me more than any trial. "Stop fighting it. Let it look."

"If it looks," he gritted out, "it takes. That's what it does. That's what it's *for*—"

"Then let it take what isn't you."

Something moved in his face. Trust, maybe, arriving late and limping but arriving. He stopped fighting.

The light went through him like a blade through water — and passed. It found the storm and knew it, weighed it, wrote it down somewhere in that dry old ledger. It found the guilt and the grief and the twelve years of small gray lies and it weighed those too, and I watched him endure the weighing with his head bowed like a man at sentencing. And then the light withdrew, all at once, like a tide going out, and left us kneeling on warm stone in ordinary darkness, stripped of everything except ourselves.

Which, it turns out, was the point.

"Well," Silas rasped. Lightning still flickered faintly along his forearms, dying slowly, reluctantly, like applause fading. He looked down at his own glowing hands with an expression I couldn't read — shame and relief fighting to a draw. "You've seen it now."

"I've seen it."

"The real me. The part I sold pieces of and hid the rest of." He laughed, hollow. "Twelve years keeping it under a bushel, and a rock strips me in four seconds."

"Silas." I waited until he looked at me. His eyes were still faintly lit, storm-colored, and looking into them felt like looking into the sky before hail. "It's the most alive thing I've ever seen. Including me."

That landed somewhere he wasn't guarding. I saw it hit.

* * *

*Silas*

Past the third trial — the one where the mountain asks your name and you must answer with the name you'd want carved on your grave, and Ada answered *Vey, Ada Vey, daughter of a baker, thief of things worth keeping,* and I answered *Halloran* for the first time in twelve years and the word tasted like blood and bread — past that, the passage changed.

The attention lifted. The chewing of the distant gate faded behind us. In its place came music, far below: strings and drums and voices, the sound of a court that had been dancing for a hundred years without stopping, because stopping would mean admitting what it had done to earn the throne.

We stood at the top of a stair cut into the living rock, descending into gold light and the smell of incense and old magic, and Ada's hand was still in mine, and neither of us had mentioned it, and I was holding on like a drowning man because I knew — I had always known, since the prophet's cellar, since the price the Broker would ask — that every step down was a step closer to the day she'd forget this hand, this road, this rain, this laugh.

Below us, the court beneath the mountain glittered like a lie told beautifully.

"There it is," Ada murmured. Her other hand drifted up, unconsciously, to the center of her chest — where the vault hummed. Where the heir stirred. I saw her pupils widen, storm-dark, and when she spoke again her voice had a doubled quality, hers and something older underneath, harmonizing. "*Home,*" she said. Or it said. Or we both did.

The stair began, gently, irresistibly, to pull us downward.

I gripped her hand tighter and followed the heir home, carrying my crime like a lantern, into the light of the court that owned my soul's best piece — and where, I now understood, the Broker was already waiting. He had sent word up the stair, written in letters of silver frost on the black rock:

**THE SELLER RETURNS. BRING THE VESSEL. THE PRICE HAS NOT CHANGED. IT NEVER DOES.**
