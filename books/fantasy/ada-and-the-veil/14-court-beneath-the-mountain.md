## 14. Court Beneath the Mountain

The mountain's mouth had teeth. We'd already met them at the gate—trials that peeled disguises off travelers like bark off wet wood—but the descent itself was a different kind of hunger. The stair went down farther than any stair has a right to go, spiraling into the dark with the patience of something that had been waiting a very long time to be walked.

I counted steps for the first thousand. Then I stopped counting, because the numbers started feeling like a prayer, and I've never been sure anyone down here would appreciate the theology.

Ada walked ahead of me, torch held high, her shadow swinging along the walls like a second traveler who kept trying to overtake us. The heir's soul-light hummed in her—faintly visible now, even to my ruined senses, a gold thread under her skin that pulsed when she spoke.

"You're doing the thing," she said, without turning around.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you count your sins instead of your steps. I can hear it. It sounds like breathing through your teeth."

"I'm breathing normally."

"You're breathing like a man rehearsing an apology he hasn't written yet." She paused on a landing where the stair widened into a shelf of black stone, and turned to look at me. Torchlight made her face half-gold, half-ruin. "Whatever's waiting down there, Silas—you've been carrying it since Greyhollow. Lighter if you set it down."

"Charm-seller wisdom," I said. "I'll sell you a knot for it."

"You'd charge me? After I fought a door made of teeth for you?"

"The door was mostly teeth-shaped, not actually toothed. And yes. Especially then. Business has been slow since I burned my entire inventory."

She laughed—the short, sharp version, the one she gave me when she didn't want me to know the real laugh was coming later. I'd learned to wait for it. That was the trouble with twelve days on a road with someone: you learn their laughter the way you learn weather, and then you can't unlearn it.

We descended.

* * *

The court announced itself before we saw it, the way grief does. First a warmth in the air, wrong for this far underground, like standing near a forge you can't find. Then sound—a low, vast resonance, as though the mountain were humming to itself a song it had forgotten the words to. Then light: not torchlight, not stormlight, but something older, seeping up out of the stone in veins of amber and deep blue, until the cavern opened around us and I forgot, briefly, how to be wry about anything.

It was a city inside a throat of rock. Towers grown, not built—spires of living stone that curved like ribs, hung with bridges of woven silver that swayed without wind. Thousands of windows glowed warm. Figures moved across those high bridges, small and distant, going about whatever business a buried court conducts when the world above believes it dead.

And everywhere, threaded through everything, the soul-light. Gold in the walls. Gold in the air. Gold in the veins of the people we passed as we came down the last stair into a great concourse, where merchants sold light itself in glass bottles and children chased something luminous that squeaked.

"They're alive," Ada said quietly. "Everyone up there thinks this place is a story."

"Stories are just places nobody's checked recently," I said. But my voice came out strange. Because I knew these streets. Not from prophecy. From memory—twelve years old and buried so deep I'd built a whole life on top of the grave.

I knew which bridge led to the Broker's hall. My feet knew. They carried me left at the fountain of falling water while Ada watched me do it, and said nothing, and filed it away—I could see her filing it—in whatever ledger she kept behind her eyes.

* * *

The Broker's hall sat at the heart of the city, a dome of blue stone veined with gold, its doors tall enough to admit a grief-stricken giant. Two guards flanked them—tall, gray-robed, faces smooth as river stones—and they did not stop us. They looked at me, and something in their smoothness flickered, and they stepped aside.

"They know you," Ada murmured.

"Everyone knows everyone down here. Small town."

"Silas."

"Ada." I stopped under the doors' shadow. The soul-light in her was brightening, responding to proximity the way a compass responds to north, and I could see it bothering her—her hand kept drifting to her sternum, pressing flat, as if holding a door shut. "When we go in. Let me talk first."

"Why?"

"Because the Broker deals in trades, and I'm the one who's traded here before."

She studied me for a long moment. Torchlight guttered between us. "You said you sold *a* soul," she said carefully. "A stranger's. To save someone."

"That's what I said."

"And is that what happened?"

The honest answer sat in my chest like swallowed glass. "Let me talk first," I said again, softer. "Please."

Something moved through her face—not belief, not forgiveness, but a decision to postpone judgment, which in Ada's economy was nearly the same as trust. She nodded once. We went in.

* * *

The Broker received us in a chamber at the dome's center, beneath an oculus of pure gold through which no sky showed but which glowed anyway, softly, from somewhere else entirely. He sat—or rather, he pooled—behind a desk of black glass. I say *pooled* because the Broker had never quite committed to a shape in all the years I'd known him. Tonight he wore the aspect of a lean man in merchant's gray, kind-eyed, forgettable, the face of every clerk who ever stamped a terrible document without reading it. Only his hands gave him away: too many knuckles, moving independently, like something learning fingers from a description.

"Storm-caller," the Broker said warmly. "Twelve years, four months, eleven days. You look terrible. Sit. Both of you. The girl especially—she must be tired, carrying what she carries."

Ada's hand went to her sternum again. "You can see it."

"I can *weigh* it, dear. Seeing is a courtesy." His many-knuckled hands folded. "Silas Halloran. Exiled. Disguised. Diligent. You've come to complete the transaction, I assume. Return the heir's soul to the court." He smiled with exactly the right number of teeth. "How wonderful. Everyone gets what they paid for."

"Not yet," I said. "First I need to know the price. Of return. All of it. No fine print."

The Broker's smile widened by one tooth. "Ah. You've grown cautious. Good. Cautious customers live longer." He laced his impossible fingers. "Very well. The terms are simple, as the oldest terms always are. A soul cannot simply be lifted out, Silas. It grows roots. Twelve years of roots, in this case. The heir's soul has been living in your companion's mind the way a tree lives in soil—it has drawn nourishment, and it has given some back, and the boundary between guest and ground has blurred past surgical repair."

"So don't be surgical," I said. "Pull it."

"If I pull it, she dies. Roots, Silas. Pull the tree, the garden comes with it." He spread his hands, apologetic, monstrous. "No. The soul must be *invited* out. Released willingly, by its carrier, in the throne hall, with the old rites. And when it goes—" he paused, savoring it, the way he savored everything, a connoisseur of costs— "it takes its roots with it."

"Meaning what?" Ada asked. Her voice was steady. Her hands weren't.

"Meaning," the Broker said gently, "that everything the carrier has lived *since receiving the soul* belongs to the transaction. Every memory formed while the heir slept within her. The soul grew through those years; they are its tissue as much as hers. When it returns to the throne, it takes them home. She will not remember them. Not dimly. Not as dreams. At all."

The chamber was very quiet. Somewhere above us, the golden oculus hummed.

"How long," Ada said, "has it been in me?"

The Broker consulted nothing, needing no ledger for other people's losses. "By my reckoning—twelve years, four months, and some days. Though I understand it traveled before reaching you. There was a seller."

I did not look at him. I looked at the floor, at the gold veins in the blue stone, and thought about how a vein of gold is just a wound the rock learned to live with.

"Twelve years," Ada repeated. "So whoever I was before—my childhood, my mother, however I got into the trade—all of that stays. Everything since goes." She laughed, one short note, awful. "That's convenient. That's practically tidy. I lose exactly the part of my life I'd want to keep."

"You'd want to keep *this*?" I heard myself ask before I could stop it. "Cold camps and inquisitors and a herald's blood on your boots?"

"Yes," she said, simply, and it went through me like a spear through wet paper. "Yes, Silas. That's the part worth keeping. That's the only part I chose."

The Broker watched us with the contentment of a man watching interest accrue.

"There must be another way," I said. "There's always another way. You deal in alternatives—that's the whole rotten business."

"I deal in *prices*, and alternatives are just prices wearing different coats." He rose, unfolding taller than the room should have allowed. "But since you ask. There is one alternative. The rites require a soul of equal weight to anchor the return—a counterweight, so the heir doesn't tear free and take more than its due. Yours would do, Storm-caller. Your magic. Your name. The storm you were born carrying. Offer that up alongside, and the extraction is cleaner. The carrier keeps—" he tilted his head, calculating— "perhaps a third of what she'd otherwise lose. Fragments. Warmth without the story."

"And what happens to you?" Ada asked me. "If you do that?"

"I become what I've been pretending to be for twelve years," I said. "Nobody. A charm-seller with no charms. It's a small step. I've been rehearsing."

"No," Ada said.

"It's not your decision."

"It becomes my decision the moment you make it about me." She stepped between me and the Broker, and the gold light under her skin flared, and for a moment she seemed lit from within by someone else's sunrise. "I didn't come all this way so you could pay my bill. I came so *we* could settle it together. That's what 'together' means. Look it up."

The Broker made a soft sound that might have been a chuckle or might have been the mountain shifting. "Oh, I *like* her. Sentiment is such excellent collateral." He returned to his desk, pooling back into the shape of a patient man. "Take the night. The rites require preparation regardless—the throne hall must be opened, the loyalists summoned, the seals broken. Dawn, or the hour after it. Come to the hall when you've decided who pays, and how much." His kind, forgettable eyes settled on me. "You'll decide correctly, Storm-caller. You always do. It's why I've never worried about you defaulting. Guilty men pay their debts. It's the innocent ones who run."

* * *

They gave us a room in a guest-tower of the court—a round chamber with a window of thick crystal looking out over the buried city, gold light rising through the stone like heat-shimmer in reverse. A bed, a basin, a brazier that burned without fuel. Luxury, by the standards of the road. A cell, by the standards of what waited at dawn.

Ada stood at the window a long time, watching the bridges sway and the small figures cross them.

"Say it," I said finally. "Whatever you're not saying. Say it, because tomorrow you might not remember saying it, and I'd like to have heard it at least once."

She turned. The soul-light pulsed in her collarbone, slow as a sleeping heartbeat.

"When did you sell it?" she asked. "Whose soul? You told me a stranger's. You told me you saved someone." She crossed the room slowly. "And the Broker knew your face, Silas. The guards knew your walk. You took a left at the fountain like a man going home. So before I stand in that hall tomorrow and let twelve years be pulled out of me like a splinter—tell me the truth. Whatever it costs. Because I am about to lose every mile I ever walked beside you, and I would very much like to spend my last night owning them honestly."

The words were all there. Twelve years of them, stacked and rotting behind my teeth. Wren in the usurper's cell. The Broker's ink drying. The vault of a stranger's soul pressed into a dying thief's daughter because she was dying anyway and the trade required a vessel and I told myself that made it mercy.

I opened my mouth.

And the door chimed—a soft, crystalline tone—and a gray-robed servant entered bearing wine and bread, and the moment broke like ice underfoot, scattering, gone.

Ada looked at the servant. At me. At the space between us where the truth had almost been.

"Dawn," she said quietly, and there was something in her voice I hadn't heard before—not anger, not fear. Dread. As if, somewhere behind her eyes, the heir had stirred in its sleep and shown her the shape of what was coming. "Don't waste it, Silas."

She took the wine to the window and drank alone, and I sat on the edge of a bed in a city I had betrayed, counting the hours until dawn, knowing that whatever I chose to tell her, the telling would cost more than the silence ever had—

—and knowing, with absolute certainty, that I was going to tell it anyway.
