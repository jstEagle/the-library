## 15. The Heir Speaks Through Her

I have been robbed before. Professionally, I mean—I've done the robbing—but I've also had the experience done to me, twice, by colleagues who thought I wouldn't notice and one husband who thought I'd forgive him. So I know what it feels like to have something taken from inside your own head: the hollow ache where a memory used to sit, the way you keep reaching for a name like a tongue probing a missing tooth.

What I did not know, until tonight, was what it feels like to be the vault instead of the thief.

Sleep took me the way it had been taking me all week—fast and greedy, dragging me under before I could finish counting the coins in my purse or the lies in Silas's eyes. We were camped in the last fold of foothills before the mountain's mouth, close enough now that I could see the teeth of rock against the stars, and Silas had built a fire small enough to hide from anything with eyes. He sat across it sharpening a knife he never used, because that was apparently his idea of relaxation, and I remember thinking—my last waking thought—that he looked like a man rehearsing a confession in front of a mirror made of steel.

Then the dream came for me.

* * *

It was not my dream.

That sounds like madness when I say it aloud, even now, but there is no better way to put it. My dreams have a certain flavor. They are cluttered, mercantile things, full of locked doors and half-remembered faces of people whose memories I've sold, full of staircases that go down into other people's childhoods. Mine are crowded because I am crowded. A hundred stolen lives rattle around in me like coins in a cutpurse's pocket.

This dream was not cluttered. This dream was *cold*, and *vast*, and it smelled of stone and candlewax and old gold, and I knew within three heartbeats that none of it belonged to me.

I stood in a throne hall carved out of the mountain's living bones. Pillars rose around me like the ribs of some god who had lain down here to die a thousand years ago and changed his mind halfway. The ceiling was lost in darkness, but the floor—the floor was the thing that undid me. It was a map. The whole world beneath my feet, rendered in veins of silver and lapis, the free cities marked in mother-of-pearl, and at the center, directly beneath where a throne should have stood, a mountain rendered so precisely I could see the very pass we'd climbed two days ago.

And on the throne sat an old man wearing a crown of black iron, and he was weeping.

Not loudly. Not dramatically. He wept the way stone weeps water—a slow seep, a darkening at the corners of his eyes, as though grief were something geological happening to him over centuries. He was looking at something across the hall, and I turned to follow his gaze, and that was when I understood whose dream this was.

Because the man standing below the dais was not old at all. He was young—younger than Silas, younger than me—with storm-grey eyes and hands that couldn't stop trembling, and I knew those hands. I had watched them tie luck-knots for a hundred gullible customers in Greyhollow market. I had watched them make tea. I had watched them hover, once, near my shoulder on a cold night on the road, wanting to touch me and deciding against it.

Silas. Twelve years younger. Kneeling.

He wasn't kneeling yet in the moment the dream showed me—he was standing, arguing, his voice cracking with a desperation I had never heard from the careful, wry charm-seller I knew. But the dream was generous, or cruel, and it gave me everything. It rewound and replayed like a merchant demonstrating a music box, and each time it showed me more.

* * *

The court beneath the mountain, a century after its darkest hour—or perhaps only twelve years after; time moved strangely here, folding like cloth—and the old king on the iron throne was dying. That much came through clearly, in the heir's own voice, which was not a voice at all but a pressure behind my eyes, a knowing that arrived pre-understood:

*My father is dying. The Broker circles like a carrion bird. And somewhere above us, the usurper's agents are already buying.*

The usurper. Even in the dream, the name arrived without a face—deliberately, I think, scrubbed clean by whoever had imprisoned this soul. There was only the shape of the coup: a cousin of the blood, ambitious and patient, who had spent decades doing what usurpers do—not fighting the court but purchasing it. Buying loyalties one at a time. Buying silence. Buying, through intermediaries whose names were struck from every record, the one thing no army could seize and no assassin could steal.

The heir's soul.

"You cannot sell what cannot be bought," the dying king had said, in the dream's long ago, "and so they will try to buy the vessel instead."

The vessel. The word landed in me like a hook finding flesh.

The dream shifted. I saw the coup itself—not the whole of it, but flashes, the way trauma preserves things: torchlight in corridors that had never known fire. Loyalists dragged from their beds. The mother-of-pearl map on the floor cracked down the middle by someone driving a sword into it, point-first, as if killing the world itself. The old king dead on his iron throne, still seated, arranged there deliberately so the court would find him that way. A message in corpse-language: *the seat is occupied now.*

And through it all, running like a thread of lightning through black cloud, the Broker.

I saw her clearly for the first time. She was not what I expected, which I suppose is the point of Brokers. She was small and neat and grandmotherly, with silver hair pinned up and kind creases around her eyes, and she wore an apron like a baker, and when she smiled at people she was about to ruin, she meant it kindly. That was the horror of her. She genuinely believed every transaction was a mercy.

"The child carries it," she said, in the dream's memory, to someone I couldn't see. "A girl-child, born under a bad star, sold by parents who needed winter bread. The soul settled into her like a swallow into a chimney. It will sleep until she's grown. Until then, she's just a girl with a hole in her where a life should fit."

A girl-child. Sold by parents who needed winter bread.

I felt the dream pause around that sentence, the way a room goes quiet when someone says your name in another conversation. The heir was watching me watch this. I had the distinct, vertiginous sense of being read—of being a page someone else was turning.

*I know what you're thinking,* the pressure behind my eyes said, almost gently. *Keep watching. You need to see how it ends.*

* * *

How it ended was Silas.

Twelve years ago. A cellar that smelled of candlewax and cold stone—this same hall, or near it, fallen further into disrepair since the coup, the loyalists scattered, the true line reduced to hiding its most precious thing in the body of a child nobody would look at twice. The Broker's shop-within-the-court, all brass scales and jars of preserved light. And Wren.

I saw Wren before I ever saw the bargain. A girl of maybe fifteen, grey-faced, lips going blue, some wasting sickness eating her from the inside while her older brother held her hand and was told, by every healer in three cities, that nothing could be done. Nothing mortal, anyway.

So Silas Halloran—storm-caller, exile, a man already stripped of his home for the crime of having power in a place that feared it—went looking for something immortal.

The dream showed me his descent. It showed me the trials at the mountain's mouth, the Gate of Teeth stripping away his disguises until he walked into the court raw and human and desperate. It showed me the Broker receiving him with that terrible baker's smile, pouring tea, letting him beg for a full hour before naming a price.

"Everything has a price," she said, in the dream's remembered voice, warm as fresh bread. "Yours happens to be convenient. There is a soul in my keeping that must be moved. Quietly. Permanently. Into a carrier who will never come looking for it, because she will never know it's there." She slid a slip of paper across the counter. A name. An age. A city.

I knew the city. I had picked pockets in its markets at eight years old.

"I can't do that," young Silas said. His voice was hoarse. "I won't hand a person over to be—be a prison."

"It isn't a prison," the Broker said, and here her kindness became truly unbearable, because she seemed to believe it. "It's a sanctuary. The usurper's men hunt this soul. In your court, in any court, it will be found and destroyed, and then the true line dies forever. In a girl who steals memories for coin? Who would ever think to look?" She folded her flour-dusted hands. "You would not be caging it, storm-caller. You would be hiding it. There is a difference. Ask any bird whether it prefers the aviary or the wild, and then ask whether it survives the winter."

"And the girl?" His knuckles were white on the counter. "What happens to the girl?"

"Nothing," said the Broker. "She lives her life. She never knows. When the time comes—if the time comes—we will ask for it back, and she will give it up the way you give up a coat you didn't know you were minding. A year of her dreams, perhaps. A little strangeness. Nothing that matters."

Nothing that matters.

I watched him sign. That was the worst of it—not the bargaining, not the desperation, but the actual signing, the scratch of his pen, the way he did it fast, the way people do when slowing down would let them feel what they're doing. He traded the heir's soul—the sleeping, exiled heart of the true court—for Wren's cure, and the Broker wrapped the transaction in words like *sanctuary* and *mercy* and *nobody need ever know*, and he let her, because he needed to let her, because the alternative was going home to bury his sister.

And then the dream showed me the part I will carry longer than any of it.

It showed me the transfer. The soul leaving the Broker's jar of preserved light and entering—me. A girl of nineteen, asleep in a rented room, exhausted from a job, dreaming about nothing, dreaming about *nothing*, because that was the year the dreams went quiet. I had always wondered about that year. The year my craft stopped working right. The year I started feeling like a house with a room I couldn't find the door to.

I watched myself sleep while a stranger's soul was poured into me like wine into a cup that hadn't been asked if it wanted holding.

And I watched Silas stand over my sleeping body and look at me for a long, long moment—young, guilty, already beginning the twelve years of silence that would become his life—and I heard him whisper, to me, to himself, to whatever gods still answered exiles:

"I'm sorry. You'll never know. That's the mercy of it."

* * *

The dream released me the way deep water releases a drowning swimmer—all at once, violently, upward into light.

I woke sitting bolt upright with the fire burned to coals and Silas on his feet across the ashes, knife in hand, alarm all over his face.

"Ada." Careful voice. The charm-seller voice, the market-stall voice, the one he used for customers and liars. "You were crying out. Talking in your sleep. Are you—"

"Don't." My own voice came out flat and strange, like a stranger borrowing it. Which, I supposed, was fitting. "Don't do the voice. Don't do the concerned-stranger voice. Sit down, Silas."

He sat. Slowly. Watching me the way you watch a trap you suspect you've stepped in.

The coals ticked. Somewhere up the slope, wind moved through scree with a sound like distant applause. I made myself breathe. I made myself be precise, because precision was the only thing standing between me and screaming, and I have never once in my life given anyone the satisfaction of seeing me scream.

"Twelve years ago," I said, "you came down into the mountain to buy a cure for your sister. The price was a soul. The Broker told you it would go into a girl who would never know, and you signed, and you told my sleeping body that I'd never find out, and you called that a mercy." I watched his face do something complicated and terrible. "Her name was Wren. She lived. You've been lying to me since Greyhollow. Since the stall. Since the day you looked me in the eye and swore you weren't a storm-caller while lightning sat under your skin like a swallowed star."

"Ada—"

"I'm not finished." I was shaking now. Fine tremors, rage and something worse than rage. "Do you want to know the funny part? Do you want to know what makes this almost bearable? I'm a memory-thief, Silas. Stealing what people keep hidden is the one thing in this world I am *good* at. And you—" my breath caught, and I hated it, hated the crack in it, "—you hid it in the one place I never thought to rob. Yourself. All I ever had to do was look at your surface thoughts, and I read them weeks ago. A soul traded. A sister spared. And I told myself it was some stranger's soul. Some nameless thing. Because the alternative was believing that the man I—" 

I stopped. That sentence had a cliff at the end of it, and I was not going off it in front of him. Not tonight.

Silas had gone very still. Stiller than a man, stiller than a statue—stiller than a storm, which is a thing I now knew storms could do, hold themselves in one clenched fist of grey light and refuse to break. When he finally spoke, his voice was ruined.

"How much do you know?"

"Everything." I stood, because sitting across from him had become unbearable, because my legs wanted to run and I refused to let them. "The coup. The dying king. The Broker with her flour-dusted hands and her mercies. The girl-child sold for winter bread. The heir sleeping behind my eyes for twelve years, dreaming through me, watching me live a life that was never entirely mine." I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "You know what the heir showed me last, before it let me wake? It showed me your face when you signed. And do you know what I felt, Silas? Not hate. *Understanding.* That's the cruelest trick anyone's ever played on me. I understand you completely. I understand why you did it, I understand why you lied, I understand every step of the road that brought you to my sleeping body with your apologies. Understanding is supposed to be my trade, and it turns out it's worthless. It doesn't fix anything. It just means I can see exactly how I was betrayed, in perfect detail, from every angle."

He rose too. His hands were open at his sides, empty, the knife abandoned in the dirt—an offering, or a surrender. Storm-light flickered beneath the skin of his throat, visible now, unmistakable, the disguise burned away by my knowing.

"There's more," he said quietly. "There's a part you don't know yet. About what returning the soul costs. About what it takes from you." His jaw worked. "And I swear to you, Ada, whatever you decide to do with what you know—hate me, leave me, put a knife in me yourself—I will tell you the rest of it. All of it. Tonight, if you want. No more vaults."

I looked at him for a long time. At the liar. At the man who had saved his sister by imprisoning a stranger. At the fool who had followed me across half the free cities carrying his guilt like a stone in his boot, flinching every time I got too close to the truth, and staying anyway. Staying anyway, all this way, all these miles.

The heir stirred behind my eyes—not speaking, just present, a warmth at the back of my skull like a hand resting there. Waiting to go home. Waiting to see what I would do with the last hours of being its unknowing keeper.

"No," I said.

Silas flinched. "No?"

"Not tonight." I turned toward the mountain's teeth, black against the stars, the Gate of Teeth waiting for us both. "Tonight you walk in front of me, where I can see your hands. And tomorrow, at the gate, you tell me the rest—the cost, the erasure, all of it, every piece you've been choking on since Greyhollow. Because we're going in, Silas. I'm taking your stolen soul home." I glanced back at him once, and let him see exactly what was in my face. "But understand this. I am not walking beside you anymore. I am walking *behind* you. And the difference between those two things is the entire distance between what I felt yesterday and what I feel now."

He nodded. Picked up his pack. Walked in front of me toward the mountain, shoulders straight, hands visible, a man marching toward his own reckoning.

Behind my eyes, softly, the heir began to sing.
