## 16. Twelve Years of Lies

*Ada*

The gilded halls of the court beneath the mountain did not echo. That was the first thing I noticed after the shouting stopped—no echo at all, as though the walls drank sound the way I once drank other people's memories, greedily and without permission.

Silas stood in the middle of the corridor with his hands half-raised, the way a man stands when he's been caught with something in them and can't decide whether to drop it or hide it. Twelve years of practice at being nobody, and he still hadn't learned how to look innocent.

"Say it again," I said. "Slowly. And this time don't arrange it so you come out looking like the tragic one."

"Ada—"

"You said *stranger*. In the rain, on the road, when I asked you what you sold. You said you sold a stranger's soul." My voice came out flat, which surprised me. I'd expected it to shake. "And last night the heir showed me her own coup. Her own murder. And she showed me a young man kneeling on a marble floor with storm-light coming off him like heat off a forge, trading something that wasn't his to trade."

He flinched. Good. I wanted the flinch. I wanted all of it.

"That was twelve years ago," he said quietly.

"I know. She told me. She told me everything, because apparently the only person in this whole mountain who thought I deserved the truth was the dead girl living behind my eyes."

* * *

*Silas*

There is no honest way to tell this story. I've spent twelve years learning every dishonest way, and when the moment finally came, they all deserted me at once like hired soldiers whose pay had run out.

So I gave her the truth in the order I'd buried it, which is to say backwards, which is to say badly.

"Wren was dying," I said. "Not sick—dying. The Hollow Fever had her lungs, and the physicians in Vaelmoor wanted three hundred crowns for a treatment that might not work, and I was twenty-two years old with a talent for storms and no coin worth mentioning. And then a man came to my door. Silk gloves. A voice like a ledger being balanced. He said the court beneath the mountain paid well for souls, and mine was worth more than most, because storm-callers are rare now. But I said no. Of course I said no."

Ada didn't move. The lamps along the corridor burned without flickering, and I understood dimly that even the fire down here was loyal to someone else.

"He offered an alternative," I went on. "A soul already held in trust. An heir's soul, taken during the coup a century past, kept in the vaults because no one dared destroy it and no one dared use it. He said if I sold mine, they would release hers into a carrier—a vessel—and the vessel would walk out of the mountain carrying everything they needed to keep the true line from ever reclaiming the throne. And Wren would live. Fully. No fever. They keep their bargains, Ada. It's the one thing about them that's decent."

"And the vessel," Ada said. Her voice was very controlled now, which was worse than shaking. "The vessel who carried the heir's soul out of the mountain. Who was that?"

I looked at the floor. Gilded marble. Even the floor down here was richer than anything I'd ever owned honestly.

"A child," I said. "An orphan from the river districts. Memory-thieves were cheap back then—the guild took children who could hold things without breaking. I never knew her name. I signed nothing. I took the money and I ran, and I told myself for twelve years that whoever she grew up to be, she'd have lived a hard life anyway, and at least Wren was alive, and at least—" I stopped. There was no *at least* left. I'd used them all up on the road, in the rain, in every deflection she'd let me get away with because she trusted me.

"And at least," Ada finished for me, in a whisper that was somehow louder than shouting, "*at least she wouldn't remember.*"

* * *

*Ada*

Here is what nobody tells you about learning your whole life has been arranged by a stranger's guilt: it doesn't feel like betrayal first. It feels like vertigo. Like standing on a stair that turns out to be one step lower than your foot expected, over and over, forever.

Every memory I had of the road—that was real. The market stall, the prophecy, the waystation fire, the herald, the gate of teeth where I saw the storm-light under his skin for the first time and thought, *oh, there you are*, like I'd been waiting for him without knowing it. All real. All mine.

But the vault in my head—the locked room humming with borrowed light—I'd carried that since I was six years old. Since before I could form memories worth keeping. Which meant every single thing I had ever been, I had been *around* it. Every choice I'd ever made, I'd made with a stolen soul sitting in the back room of my mind like a lodger who paid rent in dreams.

And he'd known. From the charm-seller's Tuesday onward, he'd known what I was carrying, and he'd looked me in the eye and lied about it, and I had defended him. To myself. In my own head, where I keep the only property I've ever actually owned, I had defended him.

"The erasure," I said. "Tell me about the erasure. Tell me exactly what happens when the soul goes home."

He hesitated, and I watched him hesitate, and I realized he was calculating—not whether to tell me, but how much of it I already knew, the way I used to calculate how much a mark already suspected before I lifted their childhood off them.

"Don't," I said. "Don't manage me. Just say it."

"When the soul returns to the heir," he said, "the carrier loses every memory formed since receiving it. Everything since the mountain. Since the transfer." His voice cracked on the last word, and some small vicious part of me was glad. "Since she was six years old."

"So not just you." I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "Not just our few miserable weeks on the road. My mother. Whoever raised me. Every job, every city, every friend, every enemy. The entire shape of my life. Gone. And you've known that since chapter one, haven't you? Since the day you read my face in a dead prophet's book and decided I was your redemption walking around in a coat."

"Ada—"

"And you were going to do it anyway." Not a question. I saw the answer land on him like weather. "You were going to stand there while they emptied me out, and afterward you were going to be sad about it, beautifully, at length, and maybe write me a letter."

His face did something terrible then. Because I'd guessed the letter. I hadn't seen it yet—I wouldn't find it until later, folded in his coat—but I'd guessed it, and the guess fit him so perfectly that he couldn't hide the confirmation.

"You were going to give me back my life," I said slowly, "by taking away the only part of it I chose for myself."

* * *

*Silas*

She was right about the letter. That's the detail that undoes me even now. She stood in that corridor with the heir's grief still wet in her eyes and deduced the letter, and I felt it against my ribs like a second heartbeat, the confession I'd written by lamplight two nights ago, planning for a version of her that wouldn't exist.

"Yes," I said. Because there was nothing else. Twelve years of lies, and the only currency I had left was the plain ugly truth, spent all at once.

"Then say the rest," she said. "All of it. The prophecy. Wren. What you're planning to offer the Broker. Don't curate it. I want the whole rotten ledger."

So I gave her the ledger.

Wren, alive but hollow-eyed, kept as leverage in a house outside Vaelmoor, begging me through a window not to come for her. The prophecy naming me and this date and her face sketched beside mine in a dead man's dust. The price the Broker quoted: my storm-magic, my name, my nature—all of it, collateral for the soul's release. And underneath all of it, the thing I'd never said aloud, not to her, not to Wren, not to the god I'd stopped believing in somewhere around the third year of selling luck-knots:

"I chose you," I said. "Or I chose the version of events where you existed. When the Broker put the vessel question to me—whether I cared what happened to the child—I said no. I said, *not my concern.* I signed away a six-year-old's whole life to save my sister, and then I built a small safe lie of a life on top of the grave of yours, and I called it penance because penance was comfortable and restitution would have cost me everything." I swallowed. "It's costing me everything now. It should have cost me everything then."

For a long moment she said nothing. The corridor held its breath. Somewhere far above us, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone, the usurper's vanguard was making its opinions known, and neither of us turned toward the sound. The battle could wait. Some debts can't.

"Do you know what the worst part is?" she said finally. Her voice had gone quiet and precise, the voice she used when she was working a lock. "It's not the sale. It's not even the erasure. It's that on the road—in the rain, when you laughed, when you taught me to call that little static spark off your knuckles and I laughed so hard I cried—I was happy. Genuinely happy. And the whole time, you were watching me the way a man watches a candle he's decided to burn down for light. Waiting for it to be useful. Waiting for it to be over."

"No," I said. "No, that's not—"

"It's exactly what it was." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, angry at the tears, furious at them. "You want credit for confessing. You want me to stand here and admire how heavy your guilt is. But guilt you carry for twelve years isn't love or honor, Silas. It's just another thing you refused to put down because putting it down meant facing someone you'd hurt."

* * *

*Ada*

He didn't follow me. That, at least, I'll grant him.

I walked. The gilded halls unspooled ahead of me, gold and bone-white and lit by flames that bent slightly toward me as I passed, as though even the fire recognized what I carried. Loyalists pressed themselves into alcoves as I went by—old courtiers in threadbare finery, soldiers with rusted honors, servants of a household that had been waiting a century for a coronation. They bowed. To me. Or to what was in me. I genuinely could not tell anymore, and I was so tired of not being able to tell.

The heir stirred behind my eyes. Not speaking. Just present, the way a hand rests on your shoulder when someone wants you to know they're there. She had shown me everything—the coup, the murder, the kneeling boy with lightning in his veins—and I understood now that she hadn't done it to wound me. She'd done it because she was tired too. A hundred years in a vault, twelve years in a thief's skull, and the one person who could free her had been walking beside me the whole time, lying to me with the specific tenderness of a man who thinks kindness is a substitute for honesty.

I stopped in a gallery hung with portraits of the old court. Kings and queens and heirs, all of them painted before the usurper's grandfather learned to smile. In the center hung a girl of perhaps nineteen, storm-grey eyes, a crown of iron and glass. The heir. Before.

*I'm sorry,* I said to her, inside, where no one else could hear. *I'm sorry it was me. I'm sorry it took this long.*

*You chose to come,* she answered, in that voice like distant thunder deciding whether to be gentle. *That was always yours. The rest of it was never.*

And that was the thing, wasn't it. That was the whole horrible arithmetic of it. If the soul went home, I lost everything I'd chosen. If it stayed, the true heir stayed imprisoned and the usurper's armies reached the free cities by winter and everyone I'd never met died for my convenience. There was no version where I kept my memories and the world kept its chance. He'd known that too. He'd known it and he'd walked me toward it anyway, mile by mile, letting me fall in—

Letting me fall in whatever it was we'd fallen into. I wasn't going to name it. Naming it would make losing it worse, and I was going to lose it either way: to the erasure if I stayed, to my own walking-away if I didn't.

Except.

Except there was a third door, and I'd seen it open in the heir's dream-memories, and I'd bet my last stolen thought that Silas had seen it too. The Broker accepted collateral. The Broker *delighted* in collateral. And there was one piece of collateral in this mountain that the Broker wanted more than any soul, more than any throne—a storm-caller's power, freely given, the last of a dying line.

He was going to offer himself. I'd watched him calculate it on the road, watched him go quiet whenever the Broker came up, wearing the expression of a man pricing out his own funeral. He'd trade his magic—his name, his nature, the storm under his skin—for my soul's release, and he'd do it without telling me, and he'd call it the only honest thing he'd ever done, and he'd be almost right, and it would still be one more decision made about my life without me in the room.

Well. I was a memory-thief. Deciding things in rooms I hadn't been invited into was supposed to be my trade.

I turned back toward the throne hall, and the gilded corridor stretched ahead of me, and above us the mountain shuddered with the first blows of the usurper's ram against gates that had not opened in a hundred years.

Whatever he was planning, he'd have to go through me to do it.

And this time, I intended to be in the room.

* * *

*Silas*

I found the Broker's antechamber empty, which frightened me more than finding him in it would have. The lamps burned low. On the black table between the two chairs, a fresh contract waited, unsigned, the ink still shining.

My name was already written at the top.

Behind me, soft as a closing door, I heard footsteps in the corridor—and they weren't Ada's.
