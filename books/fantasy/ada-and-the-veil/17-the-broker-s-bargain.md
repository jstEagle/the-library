## 17. The Broker's Bargain

The Broker's chamber smelled of old ledgers and older secrets, a scent I recognized the way you recognize a grave you dug yourself.

Twelve years since I'd stood in this room. Twelve years since I'd knelt on this same floor of black marble veined with something that glowed faintly, like the mountain's own slow heartbeat, and traded away a piece of a stranger's soul to buy my sister's breathing. I had told myself a hundred stories since then. That it was mercy. That it was arithmetic. That the heir to the court beneath the mountain was already dead in every way that mattered, and what I took was only the residue, the leftover light, the thing nobody would miss.

All lies. I had known they were lies when I told them, and I knew it now, standing in the amber light of a hundred hanging lamps while the Broker poured tea I would not drink.

"You've aged," the Broker said, settling into a chair that seemed to have grown out of the wall, all dark wood and bone-white inlay. "Storm-callers usually don't. The weather in you keeps you young, I've found. All that electricity scouring the pipes." The Broker's face was a thing I had never managed to describe to myself afterward—pleasant, unremarkable, the kind of face you forgot while you were looking directly at it. Only the eyes stayed. Eyes like coins left in a fountain: polished, faceless, and always, always watching the water line. "And yet you've come back looking older than a century of prophecy. Grief does that. Or guilt. In my experience they weigh the same on the ledger."

"I'm not here to trade," I said.

"Everyone who says that is here to trade." The Broker smiled, and the smile was worse than the eyes. "Tea?"

"I'm here to make an offer."

"An offer *is* a trade, Master Halloran. Or should I say—" the coin-eyes glittered, "—Master *Threnody*, once of the Storm Court, exiled by his own hand, currently trading in luck-knots at a stall that smells of cheap copper and cheaper hope. Names are such interesting currency. You've spent so many of them."

I kept my hands still. Somewhere under my skin, the storm that had lived in me since birth—the thing that had made me a weapon at nine, a courtier at fifteen, a traitor at twenty-two—shifted and pressed against the walls I'd built around it, like a dog hearing its name after years of silence. *Don't*, I told it. *Not yet. Not until the bargain's struck.*

"Ada Vey," I said. "The woman carrying the heir's soul. I want it released. Returned to the court. Fully, cleanly, with no debt attaching to her."

The Broker set down the teapot with a small ceramic click that sounded, in that silent chamber, like a lock turning.

"Now *that*," the Broker said softly, "is an interesting request."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about bargaining with the Broker: the danger is not the price. The danger is that the Broker will give you exactly what you ask for, in exactly the words you use, and the words will turn out to have been the whole trap. I had watched a courtier once ask for "a little more time" and receive a pocket watch that ran backward and ate a year of his daughter's life to fuel itself. The courtier had wept. The Broker had expressed sympathy. The ledger had not cared.

So I had spent the walk down—the long descent through the gilded halls where courtiers of glass and memory watched me pass with faces like frosted windows—choosing my words the way a sapper chooses where to put his feet.

"The soul was sold to you," I said. "Sold by me. The sale was lawful under the old covenants. But a sale can be *undone* by the seller. That's in the covenants too. Clause nine."

"Clause nine," the Broker repeated, delighted. "You've been reading."

"I've had twelve years."

"Twelve years," the Broker mused, "in which you could have come back at any time. The offer was always open. The heir's soul sitting in my vault like a lamp no one lights, and you—free, alive, your sister breathing, your debt paid. You could have walked in on any Tuesday and said *clause nine* and walked out whole." The coin-eyes tilted. "So why now? Why today, the exact date some dead prophet scribbled in a book? Why come back now, when the price of coming back is so much higher?"

"Because now I know who carries it."

"Ah." The Broker poured a second cup of tea no one had asked for. Steam curled up, and in the steam I saw—briefly, the way you see things in storm-light—images: Ada's face, the road, the rain, her laughter at something I'd said at a burned waystation, her hand steady on a rope in the dark. The Broker's tea showed you what it was pricing. "The memory-thief. The vessel. The girl who has been walking beside you for—what is it now? Six weeks? Seven? Long enough that the heir inside her has started dreaming your face." The Broker sipped. "Long enough that you've started dreaming hers."

"I want her out of the bargain," I said. "Every clause, every lien, every interest accrued. The soul returns to the court, and Ada Vey walks out of this mountain owing nothing to anyone. Clean."

"And in exchange," the Broker said, "you offer...?"

That was the moment. I had rehearsed it on the road, in the rain, at the gate of teeth where the mountain had nearly peeled my disguise off and Ada had seen the storm-light crawling under my skin and looked at me with awe and fear braided together, and I had thought, *she doesn't even know the worst of it yet.* I had rehearsed it again while she slept, watching her breathe, knowing that when this was done she would not remember any of the weeks she'd spent learning to trust me. The rehearsing hadn't helped. There is no rehearsal for selling yourself.

"Me," I said. "All of me. The storm-magic. The name. The exile. Every drop of what I am."

The chamber went very quiet. Even the mountain's heartbeat in the floor seemed to pause, listening.

* * *

"You should understand," the Broker said, and for the first time the pleasant voice took on the cadence of a lecture, a merchant laying out wares, "exactly what you're offering. Because I find that sellers who don't understand their goods always feel *cheated* afterward, and cheated sellers make such tedious ghosts."

"Understood," I said.

"Humor me. I've been alone with ledgers for a long time." The Broker rose and drifted to one of the hanging lamps, and the light bent toward the Broker's face like it was eager. "Storm-magic is not a talent, Master Threnody. It is not a skill you learn, like the memory-craft your friend practices, all clever fingers and locked doors. Storm-magic is *ancestry*. It is the sky's own blood in your line, going back to the first courts, when the weather had opinions and the weather's children walked among us. It cannot be taught. It cannot be given. It can only be *spent*." The Broker turned. "And once spent, it does not return. Not in this life. Not in any life the covenants recognize. You would be, in every way that matters to the deep laws, an ordinary man. A man of no court, no craft, no calling. Rain would fall on you and mean nothing. Lightning would pass you by like a stranger."

"I know."

"You would lose the wards. The little charms you've been hiding behind for twelve years—those were never charms, were they? Those were you, leaking. Your own power knotted into luck-strings because you couldn't bear to be entirely nothing." The Broker's smile widened by a degree. "You would lose the *name*. Threnody, storm-born, last of a line that called the lightning down on the Usurper's grandfather and lived to be hated for it. Gone. Struck from the deep ledgers so thoroughly that even I would not be able to tell you who you were. You would walk out of this mountain a man with no past that the magic recognizes. Do you understand? Not a man with a new past. A man with *none*. The world would have to write you from scratch."

"I said I understand."

"And your sister?" The coin-eyes went soft, which was the most frightening thing they had done yet. "Wren. Kept all these years as leverage, hollow-eyed, begging you not to come for her. The bargain that bought her life was paid with the heir's soul—but the *maintenance* of that bargain, the thing that keeps the usurper's agents from finishing the job, is your existence. Your name is the hostage's ransom note. Spend your name, and the note is void." The Broker let that sit. "Wren dies."

The word went through me like the cold at the center of a storm cloud, and for a moment I couldn't speak, and the Broker watched me not speak with the patience of someone who has watched a thousand men not speak.

"I know," I said finally. "I've done the arithmetic. She told me not to come. She's been dead inside for twelve years—what the usurper's people left of her is a woman waiting in a room, and the room is the whole of her life. If I pay, the room closes. If I don't pay, the usurper's armies reach the free cities by winter, and every room in every city closes, hers included, everyone's included." I made myself look at the coin-eyes. "I'm not saving her. I'm choosing the size of the grave. That's all any of us have been doing for twelve years. I'd like to stop."

The Broker was silent for a long moment. Then the Broker laughed—a real laugh, startled out of that pleasant face, brief and almost human, and somehow that was worse than everything else.

"Oh," the Broker said, wiping the corner of one coin-eye, "oh, you *have* changed. Twelve years ago you knelt on this floor and bargained like a courtier—hedging, framing, asking for the sale to be called something gentler. *A guardianship*, you called it. *A safekeeping.* As if words could keep the ledger clean." The Broker sat back down, and the chair received the Broker like water. "And now you walk in and say *I'd like to stop*, as if self-annihilation were a holiday."

"It's not annihilation," I said. "It's the first honest thing I've done since I was twenty-two."

* * *

The Broker drew out a ledger.

I had seen it before, twelve years ago, from my knees: a book bound in something that was not leather, its pages not paper but a material like pressed fog, and when the Broker's fingers moved across it, the fog darkened into ink that was not ink. The whole room leaned toward it. The lamps swung on their chains though there was no wind, and the mountain's heartbeat in the floor quickened, and I understood that this book was not *in* the chamber; the chamber was built around the book, the way a wound is built around a blade.

"Terms," the Broker said, all business now, the delight folded away into something more terrible: precision. "The seller, Silas Threnody, called Halloran, invokes clause nine of the covenants of sale, returning the soul of the true heir of the court beneath the mountain to its rightful custodianship." The fog darkened line by line. "In consideration: the entirety of the seller's storm-heritage, being his power, his name, his standing in the deep ledgers, and his recognition by the old laws. To be spent in full. Irrevocable. Non-refundable." The Broker glanced up. "You'll note I'm not saying *sacrificed*. I dislike that word. It implies nobility, and nobility is just a price tag worn on the outside."

"Write it," I said.

"Two conditions of my own." The Broker held up one finger. "First: the vessel. The memory-thief, Ada Vey. When the soul leaves her, it takes with it every memory formed since she received it. That is not my price—that is the soul's nature, the heir's grip on its own history, pulling free. I cannot soften it. But I can tell you the truth of it, and I am telling you: she will not remember you. Not the road, not the rain, not the waystation, not the laughter. She will walk out of this mountain a stranger to you, and you will be a stranger twice over—once to the world, once to her. Are you prepared to pay that as well? Because it attaches to the transaction whether you consent or not, and I would rather have your consent on the ledger than your scream in my chamber."

I thought of Ada's face in the gate of teeth, storm-light reflected in her eyes, fear and awe braided together. I thought of her voice on the road: *teach me to be honest, storm-caller, you're so bad at lying it's almost a craft.* I thought of how she had looked at me in the gilded halls, before she walked away alone—heartbreak and understanding arriving in the same breath, and the understanding somehow worse.

"I consent," I said. My voice came out steady, which surprised me. Somewhere far above, I felt the storm in me go still, the way the air goes still before the sky decides.

"Second condition." The Broker's second finger rose. "The return must be performed by you. The seller's hand. It cannot be delegated, deferred, or done by proxy. When the usurper's vanguard breaches the mountain—and it will breach it, they are three days out and moving faster than the prophecies promised—you will stand in the throne hall, in the middle of a battle, and you will give the heir's soul back while the ceiling comes down. And then—" the coin-eyes gleamed, and there was something in the gleam that was almost, almost pity, "—you will be an ordinary man holding a line against an army. With no magic. No storm. Nothing but a body that has spent its whole life being more than a body."

"I know how fighting works," I said.

"You know how fighting works *with lightning in your fists*." The Broker shook its head slowly. "I have watched storm-born die, Master Threnody. They die badly when the storm leaves them. Not because they're weak. Because they've never once, in all their lives, had to be only themselves."

"Then it's good," I said, "that I've had twelve years of practice being only myself. I just didn't know I was rehearsing."

The Broker looked at me for a long moment. The lamps stopped swinging. The fog-pages waited.

"Sign," the Broker said.

* * *

There was no pen. There was never a pen. The Broker simply held out the ledger, and I understood that I was to press my hand to the page, and the page would take what it took.

I thought about Wren, twelve years old in my memory still, laughing in a rainstorm because she thought the lightning answered her when really it answered me, and how I had never told her that. I thought about my father's court, and the usurper's coup, and the long gray years of Greyhollow, selling luck-knots to people who needed luck because the world had stopped making any. I thought about a woman with quick fingers and quicker eyes who had broken into my life the way she broke into everything—through a window I didn't know was open—and who had taught me, without meaning to, that honesty was not a thing you confessed but a thing you *practiced*, daily, badly, until your hands learned it.

She would not remember. That was the price, and I had consented, and the consent was the whole point. Because a sacrifice you make in secret, hoping no one finds out, is just a lie with better posture. I wanted the ledger to know. I wanted the mountain to know. I wanted *her* to know, someday, if the world was kind, that every mile of our journey had been paid for, not stolen.

I pressed my hand to the page.

The fog drank. It drank the storm first—I felt it go the way you feel a tooth leave, a wrongness and then an appalling lightness, twelve years of held breath finally released and taking everything with it. It drank the name *Threnody*, and I felt the word become a stranger's word. It drank the exile, the court, the bloodline back to the first thunder. It drank and drank and the lamps burned brighter and the mountain's heartbeat steadied like a patient whose fever breaks, and when it was done I stood in the Broker's chamber as a man of no court, no craft, no calling, and the rain that fell forever somewhere above the rock would never again mean anything when it touched me.

"Well," the Broker said, and closed the ledger with a sound like the end of a sentence. The delight in that pleasant face was naked now, unhidden, the joy of a merchant who has just made the trade of a century. "It has been a very long time since anyone paid *in full*." The Broker rose, and for a moment—only a moment—the coin-eyes showed depths, and in the depths something that might have been respect, or might have been hunger wearing respect's coat. "The soul will be released to your hand at the appointed hour. The vessel will forget. You will be ordinary. And the usurper's vanguard will breach the outer gates in—" the Broker consulted nothing, and knew anyway, "—rather less than three days. You should rest, Master... ah." The smile widened. "Forgive me. I've already forgotten what to call you. How *wonderful*."

I turned to go. At the door, the Broker's voice followed me, soft as ink:

"One courtesy, since you paid so handsomely. A piece of advice, free of charge, which should tell you how much it's worth." A pause, savoring. "The heir is awake now. It has been watching you through her for weeks, and it saw what you just did, and it has an opinion. When it speaks through her—and it will, soon, in the throne hall, at the worst possible moment—listen to what it says about the woman who carried it. Because I think, storm-caller—" the coin-eyes glittered, "—I think you'll find she left something behind that the soul couldn't carry out. And I think it's going to change everything."

The door closed behind me, and I stood alone in the gilded hall, an ordinary man with ordinary hands, listening to the mountain prepare for war—and somewhere above me, faint as a memory she hadn't lost yet, I heard Ada's voice, raised, calling my name.
