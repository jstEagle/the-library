## 18. Usurper's Arrival

The mountain woke me by screaming.

Not a person screaming—stone itself, a low groan that rolled up through the floor of my guest cell in the court beneath the world and set the water in the basin trembling into rings. I had slept badly anyway. The heir's dreams had been circling all night like gulls over a fish market, showing me corridors I'd never walked and faces I'd never earned, and I'd finally given up on sleep around the hour when even insomniacs concede defeat.

So when the stone screamed, I was already dressed, already armed with two knives and a truly impressive inventory of other people's memories, and already deeply annoyed.

"Of course," I told the empty room. "Of course he arrives *now*. While Silas is off bargaining with the Broker and I'm stuck here wearing borrowed silk."

The silk was the court's idea of hospitality—a gown of grey spider-woven thread that some well-meaning chamberlain had left folded on my cot. It fit perfectly, which unsettled me more than anything else that day. Clothes that fit meant someone had measured me while I slept. In my trade we called that reconnaissance. In polite society we call it tailoring.

I was still deciding whether to wear it or burn it when the second scream came, closer, and with it a sound I knew from waystations and border towns and one memorable night in a refugee city: the particular wet crunch of a gate that was never designed to be broken being broken anyway.

The vanguard had reached the mountain.

* * *

Let me back up, because I know how this sounds.

Three days ago I'd walked out on Silas Halloran in the middle of the gilded halls of an underground kingdom, having just learned that the man I'd shared a road, a tent, a dozen bad meals, and something dangerously close to trust with had sold the soul living behind my eyes to save his sister twelve years before I ever met him. I'd walked away because walking away was the only honest thing left in my body. Every other part of me—the part that remembered him laughing at his own terrible jokes, the part that remembered his hands shaking as he taught me to read storm-pressure in the clouds—every other part wanted to forgive him, and forgiveness bought that cheap is just lying with better manners.

So I walked away. And then I spent three days not walking anywhere, because where exactly does a memory-thief go when she's carrying a stolen crown inside her skull?

I'd taken a room—or been given a room; the distinction mattered less each day—in the wing of the court where the forgotten loyalists lived. That's what they called themselves, the ones who stayed after the coup a century ago: old families with old names and no power, keeping small ceremonies alive in small rooms, tending lamps for a king who would never come home. They were polite to me the way you're polite to a funeral. They could feel it, most of them. The soul-light. The heir hummed under my skin like a struck bell that wouldn't quite go silent, and every loyalist who passed me in a corridor did a small involuntary bow and then looked mortified about it.

I hated it. I want to be clear about that. I have spent my whole life taking things from people's heads, and there is a special circle of irony reserved for a thief who discovers she's been the vault all along.

But three days of their company had taught me things. Old Maren of the Lampwrights' line had shown me the secret sigils stitched into servant livery—marks of the true court hidden in plain sight for a hundred years. A stonemason named Corvel had walked me through passages that appeared on no usurper's map, because the maps had been drawn by men who only counted the rooms they could tax. And the heir—through dreams, through that patient pressure behind my eyes—had been showing me everything. The coup. The betrayal. The Broker's smiling arithmetic. And Silas, younger, kneeling, trading a soul for a sister's breath.

I knew this mountain's history better than its current masters did. I carried it, literally, in my head.

Which is why, when the gates broke, I didn't run for the exits like a sensible person.

I ran for Maren.

* * *

She was already in the lamp-hall, along with maybe forty others, huddled around the great flame that had burned unbroken since before the coup. The flame was guttering. I hadn't known it could gutter. Watching it flicker was like watching your grandmother's pulse stutter—some things you assume are simply permanent, and their impermanence rearranges your understanding of the world.

"They've breached the Third Gate," Maren said. Her voice was steady the way old people's voices are steady: not from calm but from having used up all her panic decades ago. "Corvel says soldiers in grey and iron. Usurper's colors. They're moving for the throne hall."

"Of course they are." I looked at those forty frightened faces, and then at the flame, and I felt the heir stir behind my eyes—not speaking, never speaking in words, but leaning forward the way you lean toward a window when something important is happening in the street. "And let me guess. Nobody down here has fought anything in a hundred years."

A uncomfortable silence. Somewhere a lamp hissed.

"That's what I thought." I climbed onto the offering table. Yes, the offering table. If the gods of this place objected, they could take it up with the soul in my head, who I was increasingly sure would side with me. "My name is Ada Vey. Some of you have heard what I carry. Some of you have bowed at me in hallways and pretended you didn't. I'm going to say the thing nobody wants said: the heir is real. She's real, she's *here*, and the usurper's army is coming to cut her out of me like a pearl from an oyster."

That got their attention. Fear will do that; it's the oldest audience in the world.

"You don't have to believe me," I went on. "Believe is a big word and I haven't earned it. But I can *show* you."

I closed my eyes and did the thing I'd been practicing alone in my cell for three days, the thing the heir had been teaching me in dreams without ever once using language: I opened the vault. Not all the way. Just a crack. Just enough.

Memory poured out of me into the hall like light through a door.

They saw what I saw. The coronation a century past, the true queen's crown, warm from her brow. The coup—iron men in the throne hall, the Broker standing behind them with his ledger open, writing names. The loyalists scattered like seed grain. The lamps kept lit. The secret sigils sewn into collars. And the last dream-image, the one that made an old woman near the front press both hands over her mouth: the heir herself, alive, laughing, wearing a crown of mountain-flowers the summer before everything ended.

When I opened my eyes, forty people were looking at me like I was a miracle, which was uncomfortable, because I knew exactly how much of the miracle was mine. None of it. I was a delivery mechanism. A very sarcastic delivery mechanism.

"The heir remembers you," I said, and my voice cracked on it, damn it. "Every name. Every lamp you kept. She's been dreaming you for a hundred years. And now the people who took everything are coming back to finish the job, and the only question in this room is whether you're the family that waited—or the family that watched."

Maren stood first. Of course she did. Then Corvel. Then others, hesitantly, then less hesitantly, and within ten minutes I had something I'd never expected to have in the court beneath the mountain: a following.

Gods help us all.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about rallying an army of lamp-keepers, masons, cooks, and one extremely fierce ninety-year-old archivist: they don't fight like soldiers. They fight like people defending their homes, which is either much worse or much better depending on the hour, and I had maybe an hour.

We couldn't hold the gates—the gates were already lost. We couldn't hold the corridors; iron and numbers beat enthusiasm every time. But Corvel knew the mountain's bones, and the heir knew its heart, and between the two of them we built a defense out of the one material the usurper's soldiers had never learned: a century of secrets.

We doused the great lamps in the processional halls and lit the sigil-marks instead—those hidden signs of the true court—and in that strange violet glow, the invading soldiers found a palace that seemed to shift around them. Servant passages opened behind them. Staircases they'd mapped led somewhere new. Maren's people had spent a hundred years learning every inch of this place out of pure stubborn grief, and grief, it turns out, makes excellent cartography.

I won't pretend I was a general. I was a thief with a crown in her head and a talent for knowing what people were afraid of—which, in fairness, is half of generalship. I moved through the loyalists touching shoulders, borrowing courage the way I'd once borrowed memories, giving it back amplified. And everywhere I went, the heir leaned closer to the surface, and the loyalists saw her in my face, and stood straighter.

"You should rest," Corvel said at one point, catching me swaying against a wall.

"I'm fine."

"You're glowing."

"I'm *motivated*." I pushed off the wall. "There's a difference. Mostly."

The truth was the soul-light burned brighter every time I drew on it, and every time it burned brighter, I felt a little more of myself thinning at the edges. I'd asked the Broker's price question sideways, days ago, and gotten the answer I already knew: when the soul goes home, everything I'd lived since receiving it goes with it. Every mile of road. Every bad meal. Every laugh. Silas.

I hadn't forgiven him. I might never forgive him. But the thought of losing the memory of him anyway—losing it to the return rather than to choice—sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. So I fought. Fiercely, spitefully, spending myself like coin, because if these were the last days of my remembering, I intended to remember *this*: the violet light, the old woman with the lamp-pole turned spear, the mountain refusing to die quietly.

* * *

They reached the outer ring of the throne hall at dusk, by the deep clocks. We heard them before we saw them—the synchronized tread of professional soldiers, the creak of siege-plate, and above it all a voice amplified by some charm of office, announcing to the court:

"By decree of the rightful sovereign of the deep throne, surrender the vessel and the pretender's remnant, and the household shall be spared."

*Vessel.* That was me. *Pretender's remnant.* That was everyone I'd spent all day bleeding for. I'd been called worse things by better people, but hearing a hundred years of loyalty dismissed as *remnant* made something in me go very quiet and very cold.

"He's lying about the sparing," I told the assembled loyalists. "I've read enough soldiers' minds to know how mercy works in an occupying army. It works until it's inconvenient."

"How do we know you're not lying?" someone asked—an honest question, bravely asked, and I respected it.

"I lie professionally," I said. "This isn't that. You learn the difference."

Then the doors came down.

I'll give the vanguard this: they were good. Grey-and-iron, disciplined, moving in the tight formations of troops who'd done this in a dozen free cities. What they weren't good at was fighting underground in a palace full of angry archivists, because Maren's people dropped lamp-oil from the galleries and Corvel's masons collapsed a gallery on the second rank, and suddenly the professional formation was a scrum, and scrums favor the side that knows which walls fall and which don't.

I fought too. Two knives, low stance, dirty habits—I'm a thief, not a duelist; I win or I run, ideally in that order. At some point the heir's reflexes bled into my hands, movements I'd never learned arriving fully formed, sword-forms from a dead queen's training yard, and I stopped questioning it and started using it. My body was becoming a shared document. We were editing each other in real time, the heir and I, and if that thought terrified me, terror is excellent cardio.

We held the outer ring. Barely. Bodies on both sides, the flame in the lamp-hall visible from the throne hall doors, burning steady again as if it had merely been clearing its throat.

And then the crowd of soldiers parted, the way crowds do, and the herald walked through.

I recognized him from the heir's memories, which is a strange sentence to write and a stranger thing to live. Not the same man—a century apart—but the same office, the same silver mask shaped like a serene face, the same voice that had announced the coup. He stopped ten paces from me, in the churned mud and blood of the throne hall's threshold, and inclined the serene metal face politely.

"Ada Vey," he said. "The sovereign extends terms. The vessel walks to the throne willingly, the soul is extracted cleanly, and the household below lives. Refuse—" the mask tilted, courteous as a knife, "—and we excavate. We have done it before. Ask the cities above."

Behind the mask, behind the voice, I felt something else. A thread of attention, cold and patient, running up out of the hall and away into the dark of the tunnels like a fishing line. The Broker's line. The Broker was watching. The Broker was always watching; it was practically his whole personality.

And behind *that*, fainter, from deeper in the mountain—from the direction of the Broker's vault, where a certain storm-caller had gone three days ago to offer heaven knew what for heaven knew why—a flicker. Pressure. Like weather building behind stone.

Silas.

He was coming. Or something of him was. I could feel it the way you feel a storm before the horizon shows it: a change in the air, a taste of ozone, the hair on my arms lifting. Whatever bargain he'd struck down there in the dark, it was coming due, and it was coming due *now*.

The herald waited for my answer. Forty loyalists waited, bloody and breathing hard. The heir waited behind my eyes, closer to the surface than she had ever been, so close I could almost feel her heartbeat separate from mine, almost hear her—not words yet, but nearly, nearly—

"Tell your sovereign," I said, "that the vessel declines."

The serene mask regarded me for a long moment. Then, softly, almost kindly, the herald said:

"So be it. He comes himself, then."

And far above us, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone, the mountain began to scream again.
