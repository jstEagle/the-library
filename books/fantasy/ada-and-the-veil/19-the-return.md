## 19. The Return

**Silas**

There is a particular kind of silence that comes just before a storm breaks — a hush so complete it feels like the sky holding its breath. I had spent my whole life chasing that silence, calling it down, bending it to my will.

I had never once stood inside it knowing what I was about to do.

The throne hall of the court beneath the mountain was built for gods and got usurpers instead. Pillars of veined black stone rose up into darkness that no torch seemed able to reach, and between them, the Broker's people had strung their pale lanterns like a funeral that couldn't decide on its flowers. The heir's empty throne sat at the far end, draped in cloth the color of old bone, and before it stretched a floor of polished obsidian that threw back every face in the hall like a mirror that held grudges.

And across that floor came the usurper's vanguard.

They poured through the great doors in armor the grey of grave-ash, maybe two hundred of them, shields locked, boots striking the obsidian in unison until the whole hall drummed like a heart under a physician's hand. Behind them, borne on a litter by eight soldiers, rode a man in white — the usurper's own herald-replacement, some cousin or creature whose job tonight was simple: take the soul, take the throne, kill everyone who'd already seen too much.

"Silas." Ada's voice, at my shoulder. Steady. Which frightened me more than screaming would have, because I knew what steadiness cost her right now. "The loyalists are holding the east gallery. Barely."

"Barely is a word I've built a life on," I said.

She almost smiled. Almost. There was a version of this night where she smiled all the way, where we walked out of here with the war won and a hundred small jokes banked for the road home. I had done the arithmetic a dozen times since the Broker named his price, and every sum came out the same: that version of the night did not exist. It had never existed. Every mile we'd walked together was already spent currency; I was only choosing when the ledger closed.

"You're doing the thing," she said. "Where your jaw does the thing."

"I don't have a thing."

"You have an entire catalogue of things." Her hand found my sleeve — not gripping, just resting there, the way you touch something to confirm it's real. "Whatever you're about to do, do it *after* we've won. Promise me that much."

The lie I could have told her. *I promise.* Two syllables, cheap as a luck-knot, and she'd have carried them into whatever blankness waited for her like a coin sewn into a coat lining.

"I promise," I said, "that after this, you won't have to wonder about me anymore."

It wasn't even a good lie. She frowned at it, opened her mouth—

—and the doors at the west end burst inward.

Not opened. *Burst*, torn from their hinges by something the vanguard had brought with them: a siege-engine of a man wrapped in iron bands, wearing a helmet shaped like a snarling mouth, the Gate of Teeth given legs. He came down the center aisle taking crossbow bolts in his plating like a man shrugging off rain, and behind him the shield wall broke stride and became a charge.

"Now," said the Broker, materializing at my elbow as he always did, smelling of cold candlewax. "Or never, storm-caller. The rite requires the throne. The throne requires a corridor. And corridors are made of corpses."

He was right, which I resented. The rite of return needed three things: the carrier standing in the light of the true throne, the seller's blood freely given, and the seller's power surrendered entire. The first two were mine to arrange. The third —

My third was the last of me. Twelve years of hiding, of selling charms and luck-knots and small mercies, of being nobody in a city that rewarded nobody — all of it had been a shell around one live coal. Give it up and I was just a man. A tired man with bad knees and a talent for knots.

Give it up or watch her die carrying something that was never hers.

"Ada," I said. "Get to the throne."

She looked at me. In the lantern-light her eyes were the exact color they'd been the day she'd confronted me at my stall, suspicion and fascination braided together, and I understood suddenly that I was memorizing her — that some part of me had been memorizing her for weeks without permission, cataloguing the tilt of her head, the way she said *storm-caller* like an accusation she'd grown fond of.

"Silas—"

"*Go.* I'll be right behind you."

She went. Of course she went. That was the cruelty of Ada Vey: she trusted exactly as far as she chose to, and she had chosen me, and I was about to spend that trust like the last coin in a burning house.

* * *

**Ada**

Here is what nobody tells you about carrying a stolen soul: in the final hour, it stops being quiet.

All the way beneath the mountain it had murmured — dreams of a coup a century old, a boy kneeling before a Broker, a trade struck in storm-light. But now, as I ran the length of the throne hall with arrows whispering past my ears and the heir's presence rising in me like floodwater, it wasn't murmuring. It was *singing*. A single sustained note of recognition, of homecoming, of a prisoner hearing the key turn in a door it had stared at for a hundred years.

*I'm coming,* I thought at it, absurdly. *Hold on.*

The steps to the dais were slick with blood already — the loyalists had paid for this ground in the east gallery and were paying still, Wren among them, hollow-eyed and fierce, fighting like a woman who had nothing left to lose except the brother she'd begged Silas not to come for. I put my shoulder against a wounded spearman and shoved him upright, and then I was climbing, and then I was standing in the light of the true throne.

It wasn't light, exactly. It was older than light. The air above the throne bent around something invisible and vast, the way water bends around a dropped stone, and when I stepped into that bend, the singing in my chest became a roar.

And underneath the roar, quieter, a memory that was not mine but lived in me now as if it were: *a younger Silas, kneeling, saying* take it, take the heir's soul out of her, and let my sister live.

I had known since the dream. I had known for two days and said nothing, because saying it would make it real, and because some stubborn animal part of me believed that if I didn't say it, he might yet find a way to make it not matter.

But I knew what he intended now. I saw it in the way he'd promised me nothing while promising everything. The rite needed the seller's power. All of it. Forever.

"No," I said aloud, to him, to the hall, to the universe generally. "Absolutely not. We'll find another—"

The Gate of Teeth reached the dais steps.

He came up them like a landslide deciding to be vertical, and the first loyalist who met him simply ceased to be a problem, and the second, and then the iron mouth of his helmet was ten feet from me and closing, and there was no time for another plan, another anything.

There was only Silas, arriving at the base of the steps with his hands full of storm.

I had seen his magic before — flickers, leaks, lightning under skin. This was different. This was the whole man unlidded. Storm-light poured off him in sheets, blue-white and howling, and when he raised his arms the air itself screamed, and the Gate of Teeth stopped.

Everything stopped. Two hundred soldiers, a battle, my heartbeat — all of it hung in that pre-storm silence I'd heard him describe once, drunk on road-wine, with something like homesickness in his voice.

Then he brought his hands together, and the world went white.

* * *

**Silas**

I have called storms over oceans. I have split a hillside in the Verge to bury an army's supply train. I once, at nineteen and showing off, dragged lightning out of a clear summer sky just to see if I could.

None of it was this. This was not weather. This was *me*, all twelve years of buried me, torn out through the palms of my hands and hurled down the throat of the usurper's vanguard.

The shield wall folded. The Gate of Teeth went down like a felled tree, iron bands glowing cherry-red, the man inside them making a sound I will hear for the rest of my life — however long that turns out to be. The litter-borne cousin in white took one look at the horizon of my fury and ordered a retreat in a voice cracked clean through the middle.

And I stood at the base of the dais steps, emptying myself, and felt the last of it go.

That's the thing about spending yourself entirely: it doesn't hurt, at first. It feels like relief. Like setting down a pack you forgot you were carrying. The storm-light guttered, thinned, went out — and what remained was just Silas Halloran, charm-seller, forty-some years old, knees aching, hands trembling around absolutely nothing.

Which was when I remembered the rest of the rite.

Blood, freely given. Power, surrendered entire. And the words — the old words, the ones the Broker had pressed into my mind like a seal into wax, the ones I'd sworn I wouldn't need to think about because thinking about them meant admitting—

"Silas!" Ada, on the dais, both hands pressed flat over her sternum, the heir's light bleeding out between her fingers like dawn through shutters. "Do it *now* — I can feel it — it's coming loose—"

I climbed the steps. My legs worked, which surprised me. Everything about my body working right now surprised me; I'd half expected the surrender of my magic to take the ordinary machinery down with it.

At the top step I stopped, because she was looking at me, and I wanted the last thing in her eyes to be something other than confusion.

"Ada Vey," I said. My voice came out wrecked. Charming. Very heroic. "Before I do this. I need you to know—"

"Don't." The word came out strangled. The light under her skin was flaring now, gold-white, and I could see her fighting it, could see her *choosing* to stand there anyway. "Don't you dare give me a speech I won't remember. Say it after. Say it when I can keep it."

"There is no after. Not for—" I swallowed. "You'll forget the road. The rain. The waystation. You'll forget teaching me to read a memory, and the night we laughed until the fire went out, and—"

"I know what I'll forget." Her eyes were wet and furious and utterly certain. "I've known since the dream. I've been waiting for you to tell me yourself, you cowardly, self-sacrificing, *terrible liar* of a man, and since you won't — yes. Do it. Do it now, before I talk myself out of being this generous."

The Broker drifted up beside the throne, candle-cold, delighted in the way a vulture is delighted. "The words, storm-caller. Freely given, freely returned. Speak."

I took out the knife. Took her hand — she gave it, palm up, steady as a table. And I said the words.

They weren't in any language I knew. They came up through me like water through a well-rope, old and heavy, and with each one the heir's light rose higher in Ada, and with each one I felt something else leave me — not magic this time, but memory's twin: anticipation. Whatever came next, I would carry it alone, unwitnessed by anyone who knew what it cost.

The last word left my mouth.

The light left her body.

* * *

**Ada**

It did not hurt. That was the obscene part. It felt like exhaling after holding your breath for a hundred years — like something enormous passing through you and out, grateful, grieving, *free*.

For one impossible moment I contained two lives. Hers — the heir's — a century of exile and dreaming, a throne lost, a court betrayed, a name I now knew the taste of. And mine: Greyhollow dust, a prophet's archive, a stall full of luck-knots, rain on a road, laughter, a letter I hadn't read yet but somehow already knew the weight of.

I watched them separate. I felt them separate. And I understood, in the last second I owned either, exactly what was happening, and I did the only thing left to do.

I grabbed his collar and kissed him.

It was not graceful. His lip was split, my hands were shaking, the heir's departing light was streaming out of my mouth and eyes like smoke. It was, objectively, the worst-timed kiss in the history of the court beneath the mountain.

I remember choosing it. I remember meaning it.

I do not remember it.

* * *

**Silas**

She kissed me, and then she let go, and then she stepped back, and I watched the journey leave her.

It went gently — that was the Broker's mercy, if mercy it was. Not torn away but dissolved, like frost off glass in morning sun. I saw the road go first: her eyes losing the map of it, the miles folding up like a chart being rolled. Then the craft — the memory-work she'd taught me, gone. Then the waystation, the refugees, the city of borrowed names.

Her face during it was the worst thing I have ever seen and I once watched my sister's soul get priced.

Because Ada didn't look afraid. She looked *curious* — even at the end, even as herself poured out of her, she was watching it happen with that tilted-head fascination that had been the first thing I ever noticed about her. As if forgetting were just one more lock to pick.

Then her gaze slid off me and found me again, and there was nothing in it.

Nothing. Not suspicion, not trust, not the accusation she wore like jewelry. Just a stranger's polite alarm — a woman standing on a dais in a battlefield, surrounded by armed strangers, one of whom was holding her hand and crying, which must have seemed, from where she stood, completely insane.

"Let go of me," she said. Flat. Careful. The voice of someone doing rapid, sensible arithmetic: *unknown man, unknown place, known danger.* "Who are you? What — why is everyone dead?"

"Not everyone." My voice didn't work properly. I made it work. "The loyalists hold the hall. You're safe. You're — Ada, you're safe."

"How do you know my name?"

Twelve years I'd hidden my name. One sentence, and I'd have traded every year of it to unsay hers.

The vanguard was broken but not gone — the retreat had become a rout, and routs regroup, and somewhere beyond the great doors the usurper himself was learning that his prize had slipped his fingers. The loyalists were re-forming, Wren shouting orders in a voice scraped raw. The heir — the true heir — stood blinking on the dais steps in Ada's body, crownless, memoryless, alive.

And I stood beside her with empty hands, defenseless, drained to the last spark, the only person in the hall who remembered everything and the only one who'd never be able to prove it.

She was backing away from me, one careful step at a time, toward Wren, toward the known. Sensible. Smart. Everything I'd taught her to be, except I hadn't taught her anything, had I? She'd learned it all before me, without me, and every lesson we'd shared was frost on glass now, gone with the sun.

From the ruined western doors came a sound that stopped the whole hall's breath: not the vanguard returning.

Something bigger. Something with a voice like a mountain clearing its throat.

The usurper had come for his throne personally — and the only soul in the room who knew how to fight him had just forgotten she'd ever held a knife.

Ada turned toward the sound. Squared her shoulders. And though she didn't know why — I'd swear it, I'd swear it on every storm I ever called — her hand went to her belt and found the knife I'd given her on the road, in a mile she no longer owned.

She drew it.

She looked at me.

"Why," she said slowly, "do I trust you?"
