## 20. What Remains When Memory Burns

The crown was heavier than I expected.

Not the weight of gold—I'd lifted heavier purses, heavier locks, heavier doors in my years of taking things that weren't mine. This was a different kind of weight. The weight of a hundred faces turning toward me in the throne hall of the court beneath the mountain, every one of them certain they knew me, and not one of them right.

"Your Radiance," said the old chamberlain, bowing so low his beard swept the obsidian floor. "The mountain has quieted. The deep galleries report stillness for the first time since the siege began."

"Good," I said, because it seemed like the thing to say.

He smiled at me—warm, crinkled-eyed, the smile of a man greeting someone he'd mourned and buried and dreamed of for a century. "You always hated being called that. Your mother used to say you'd squirm out of your own shadow if it bowed to you."

I had no memory of any mother. I had no memory of this man, whose name I didn't know but whose eyes were wet when he looked at me. Somewhere behind my ribs, where a soul had lived as an uninvited guest for who knew how long, there was only a hollow now—a clean, swept emptiness, like a room after the tenant has gone and taken everything, even the dust.

"I'm sorry," I heard myself say. "I don't remember you."

The chamberlain's smile didn't falter, which somehow made it worse. "No," he said gently. "But she does. Did. The memories are hers, kept in the vaults below. When you're ready, we can—"

"Not yet." The words came out faster than I meant them to. "Please."

He bowed again and withdrew, and I stood in a hall full of people who loved a girl I had never been, wearing a crown that fit a head that wasn't entirely mine, and I thought: *this is what it feels like to be robbed.* I should know. I'd made a living at it long enough.

* * *

They gave me chambers in the high gallery—*her* chambers, technically, though the heir's soul had gone wherever souls go when they're finally allowed home, and I was just the vessel left standing after the pouring. The rooms were beautiful in the way of things preserved too long: fresh flowers replaced daily by servants who wouldn't meet my eye, a bed turned down each night as though its owner might return from a walk.

I couldn't sleep in it. I sat instead by the window—an actual window, cut into the living rock and warded with glass that showed the grey light of the world above—and took inventory.

This was my craft, before all of it. Before prophecies and storms and a charm-seller with lightning under his skin. You want to know what's missing from a room, you look for the shapes things leave behind. A rectangle of unfaded wallpaper where a picture hung. A ring of clean wood on a table where a cup always sat. Grief leaves shapes too. So does love.

My chambers were full of shapes. A hairbrush with long dark strands still caught in it—mine, or hers, or both; we'd shared a skull, after all. Books with pressed flowers marking pages. A half-finished embroidery hoop tucked in a drawer, the stitches trailing off mid-flower, abandoned the way you abandon something when you expect to come back tomorrow.

And on the desk, weighted under a river-stone that had no business being in an underground palace, a letter.

I knew it was a letter the way you know a locked box holds something valuable—the shape of the waiting. My hands were steady. Twelve years of thieving teaches you that much: the moment before the opening is the moment you can still walk away.

I opened it.

* * *

The handwriting was terrible. Cramped, slanting, the script of a man who'd spent twelve years forging luck-knots and never once practicing his letters. It began without salutation, crossed itself out, and began again.

*I don't know how to write this, so I'm going to write it badly and hope you forgive the messenger.*

*By the time you read this, you won't remember me. That was the price, and I paid it knowing, which makes it worse than theft—it makes it a trade. I've stolen from people my whole second life. Small things. A first kiss here, a grandmother's face there, sold to collectors who pay well for other people's joy. I told myself it was different from what the Ministry does because I asked first. Mostly.*

*What I did to you, I didn't ask first.*

*So. The truth, in order, because you deserve order even if you'll never remember receiving it:*

*Twelve years ago I was Silas Halloran, storm-caller of the northern reaches, and my sister Wren was dying of a wasting the healers couldn't touch. The court beneath the mountain offered a cure. The price was the true heir's soul, which had been hidden in the world above, and I found it, and I sold it. I told myself a dying girl was worth more than a stranger in a prophecy. I have never once stopped telling myself that, and I have never once believed it.*

*Wren lived. The usurper got the soul—or thought he did. It slipped his brokers' chains and went looking for a carrier, and it found you. You've been carrying a stolen kingdom behind your eyes, Ada, and you never knew, and that wasn't your crime, it was mine.*

*Then I met you. And here is the part I keep circling like a man afraid to step off a ledge:*

*I knew what you carried from almost the beginning. Chapter and verse, I could have walked you to the mountain in three days. Instead we took the long road. I taught you nothing about where we were going and let you teach me your memory-craft, and when you laughed at my terrible card tricks on the road beneath the rain, I thought: I could live in this. I could spend the rest of my life earning the right to tell her the truth.*

*I ran out of road. I lied to you twice—at the waystation, when I said the soul was a stranger's, and every day since, by letting you believe I was worth trusting. The herald's blood is on my hands and I'd do it again. The lie in the cellar is on my conscience and I'd take it back.*

*Here is what I know will happen: you will wake in a hall of strangers who call you by a dead girl's name, and you will grieve, and no one will be able to tell you what you're grieving for, because the answer is a version of yourself that no longer exists—the one who walked beside me and laughed at my tricks and trusted me against all evidence.*

*I am not asking you to forgive me. I am asking you to believe one thing, the single thing I have earned: whatever you feel when you look at me—anger, grief, nothing at all—there was something real underneath it. Not the heir's borrowed longing. Mine. Yours, maybe. I'll never know now, and that's fair. That's the fairest thing I've ever gotten.*

*The Broker took my storm-magic as collateral. I'm nobody now. A charm-seller with no charms, in a city with no sky. If you ever want to find me—which you won't, and shouldn't—I'll be the man at the worst stall in Greyhollow, selling nothing, waiting for nothing, owing everything.*

*Yours in debt,
S.H.*

*P.S. — You cheat beautifully at cards. Don't let anyone down there tell you otherwise.*

* * *

I read it four times.

The first time, I read it as a stranger's confession, and my hands shook. The second time, I read it as a thief, cataloguing: *he watched you, he chose the long road, he learned your tells.* The third time, I read it as the woman who'd woken in a throne hall surrounded by loving strangers, and I understood that the loneliest person in the mountain wasn't me.

It was him. Up there in the grey city, selling nothing.

The fourth time, I didn't finish it. Because halfway through, somewhere around the line about laughing at card tricks on the road beneath the rain, something happened that I had no craft for and no defense against.

I cried. Not the heir's tears—I checked, the way you check a purse for tampering, reaching inward for that humming vault that had lived behind my eyes. It was empty. Swept clean. Whatever rose in me then came up from the bare floorboards of my own self, from whatever the soul hadn't touched, from whatever the journey had written on me so deeply that even erasure couldn't find the seams.

I remembered nothing. But my body remembered. When I read *the long road*, my feet ached. When I read *rain*, I felt it on my face, warm rain, and heard someone's terrible singing under it. When I read *you cheat beautifully*, my mouth did something it had no business doing in an empty room in a mountain full of mourners.

It smiled.

That was the grief, then. Not for the memories themselves—you can't miss what you can't recall, I know the mechanics better than anyone alive. The grief was for the shapes. For the ring on the table where the cup used to sit. For a love I could prove had existed, in ink, in a stranger's terrible handwriting, and could not feel, except in the places feeling lives deeper than recollection—in the ache of feet, in the warmth of rain, in a smile arriving uninvited like a thief through a window I'd forgotten to bar.

* * *

They came for me at dawn, or what passed for dawn down here—a slow brightening of the veinwork in the walls, gold seeping through stone. The chamberlain again, with a delegation: generals, priests, a woman in broker-grey with rings on every finger who looked at me the way I used to look at unlocked houses.

"The loyalists ask when you'll address the court," the chamberlain said. "The restoration requires—"

"I know what it requires." I was surprised by my own voice. Harder than I'd meant. Or exactly as hard as I'd meant. "And I'll give it. But I have conditions."

The broker-woman's rings clicked as she folded her arms. "Conditions. From the vessel."

"From the crown," I said, and watched her flinch, and decided I liked this new hard thing in my voice. "First: the memories in the vaults below. Hers. Mine. All of them. No one touches them until I say. Not the priests, not the archivists, not you."

"Your Radiance, those are state records of the—"

"They're a life," I said. "Mine, or hers, or ours. Nobody reads my life while I'm still writing it."

Silence. Then the chamberlain, softly: "She always negotiated like this. It drove the old king mad."

Something twisted in me—grief again, wearing its newest mask. I pushed past it. "Second condition. There's a man in Greyhollow. Charm-seller. Worst stall in the market, if his letter's honest, and I think it is, because liars write prettier."

"You would bring the storm-caller here?" The broker-woman's voice dripped. "The man who sold you?"

"He didn't sell me." I heard it come out of me before I could examine it, fast and flat and certain, rising from that same sub-basement where the smile had come from. "He sold her. He saved me from carrying it alone, at the end, when it counted. Those are different crimes and I'll be the judge of both."

I didn't know if that was true. I didn't know anything true anymore except the letter and the ache in my feet and the rain on my face. But I knew this: everyone in this mountain wanted to hand me a finished story—heir restored, usurper broken, the vessel emptied and crowned. Everyone wanted me to be the ending of their century-old tale.

One man in Greyhollow was the only person alive who wanted me to be the beginning of mine.

"Send no escort," I said. "Send no summons. If he comes, he comes because I asked, and I haven't asked yet. Third condition—" and here I paused, because the last one wasn't politics, and I suspected it was the only one that mattered.

"Yes?" the chamberlain prompted.

"When the courts above send envoys—when the free cities send their congratulations, when the usurper's remnant sends its spies—I want every document that mentions a prophecy. Every copy, every fragment, every burned scrap the archivists recovered. The prophet's book. I want it on my desk within the week."

The broker-woman frowned. "Why? The prophecy is fulfilled. The heir returned, the armies broken—"

"Prophecies are like contracts," I said, and my mouth curled, and I recognized the expression this time. It was the one from the letter. His. "There's always fine print. And I've just learned that the people who write them are always hiding something."

Because I'd read the letter a fourth time, half-finished, and there was a line I couldn't stop turning over:

*I'll never know now, and that's fair.*

Except that Silas Halloran, exiled storm-caller, liar, thief of small joys, had spent twelve years proving he didn't know how to leave a debt unpaid. And I had spent twelve years proving I didn't know how to leave a lock unopened.

Somewhere in the vaults below this mountain lay a hundred years of the heir's memories, including—if the heir had dreamed truly, as the court whispered she had—the night a younger Silas knelt in a gilded hall and made his terrible trade. And somewhere in the prophet's buried book, in the fine print of a fulfilled prophecy, there might be a clause about what happens to the vessel after the pouring. About whether the emptying takes everything. About whether some small sealed room of memory might survive, the way a smile survives, the way an ache survives.

I was a memory-thief. It was the only trade I'd ever mastered.

And I had just been handed the largest archive of stolen recollections in the world, a crown no one could refuse me, and a grief shaped exactly like a man.

"Within the week," the chamberlain repeated, bowing. "Will there be anything else, Your Radiance?"

I looked out the warded window at the grey light of the world above, toward a city where a powerless man was waking to sell nothing at the worst stall in the market.

"Yes," I said. "Bring me ink. I'm going to write a letter."
