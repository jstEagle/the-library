## 21. The Choice of the Unremembering

*Ada*

The letter had been in my pocket for three days, and I still couldn't tell you why I kept reading it.

Not couldn't—wouldn't stop. There's a difference. I'm a thief by trade, or I was, before the mountain and the crown and the strangers who bowed to me like I'd earned something. Thieves know the difference between can't and won't. Can't is a locked door. Won't is a door you keep opening even though it hurts your hand.

The letter hurt my hand every time.

*I sold a soul that was never mine to sell,* it said, in handwriting that slanted left like a man walking into wind. *You carried what I should have carried. You gave back what I stole. If you are reading this and you do not know my face, then the return took everything, exactly as I promised the Broker it would. I will not ask you to remember me. I only ask that if the mountain shakes again, you get out.*

Get out. As if I would. As if anyone with a spine would read those words and walk away from the man who wrote them.

I didn't know his face. That was the maddening part. Everyone else in this gilded dark knew me—the chamberlain wept when I passed, a one-eyed guard named Brakka called me "little storm" like she'd been saying it for years, and a cook pressed honey-cakes into my hands and told me I'd once traded her a memory of her mother's kitchen for nothing but a smile. They remembered me. I remembered none of them. And Silas Halloran—storm-caller, traitor, penitent, whatever he was—stood at the edge of every conversation like a man waiting to be dismissed, powerless now, gray-eyed, ordinary, watching me the way you watch a fire you started and couldn't put out.

I didn't remember him. But my body did something embarrassing whenever he entered a room. It turned. Like a compass needle. Like a dog hearing its name.

So when the horns began—that low, grinding bellow from the mountain's throat, the sound of the usurper's vanguard breaking through the outer gates—I didn't run for the shelters. I ran toward him.

He was already in the great stair hall, hauling a barricade into place with hands that used to call lightning and now just blistered. He looked up when I came skidding around the pillar, and his face did three things at once: relief, terror, and guilt, all fighting for the same real estate.

"You're supposed to be below," he said. "In the deep vaults."

"I don't take orders from people whose faces I don't know."

"That's usually considered an argument *for* taking their orders."

"It's considered an argument for taking nobody's," I said, grabbing the other end of the barricade. "But here I am anyway. Explain that."

He couldn't. I watched him try—watched him open his mouth and close it twice, a man drowning in everything he wanted to say. Then the gates above the stair boomed again, stone cracking in a voice like the end of the world, and he stopped trying to explain anything and started throwing tables.

* * *

*Silas*

She fought like someone remembering rather than learning.

That sounds like poetry. It isn't. I mean it mechanically: she'd never held a court-blade before—her grip was wrong, her stance was a street-brawler's crouch—but every time a usurper's soldier came down the stair, she moved *before* they did. Read the feint before it was thrown. Stepped inside the thrust as if she'd rehearsed it. The heir's soul had lived behind her eyes for years; some of what it knew had soaked into her bones the way rain soaks into chalk, invisible until you press on it.

Or—and this was the thought I kept choking on—she'd fought beside me on the road, and her body remembered what her mind had burned away. Muscle keeps its own ledger. Memory is not the only thing that survives a fire.

"Left!" I shouted, and she went left without looking, blade coming around in a short ugly arc that dropped a soldier in half-plate. She glanced back at me, breathless, and there was blood on her cheek and a question in her eyes she didn't ask out loud: *why did I trust that? Why did I trust you?*

Because I spent six weeks teaching you which way I'd shout before I shouted it. Because you laughed at my jokes on a road under rain, and I have no right to know that, and I know it anyway, and you don't.

"Silas." She backed against the barricade beside me, shoulder to shoulder. Below us, the stairwell was a churn of torchlight and steel—the vanguard had broken the outer gate, but the loyalists were holding the second landing, barely. Above, more boots. We were the filling in a very unpleasant sandwich. "The letter said you'd be defenseless. It said—"

"The letter said a lot of things."

"It said you gave up your storm." Her voice cracked on the word, and I realized she was angry—not at the enemy, at *me*, furious in the specific way of someone who has been handed a debt she cannot repay. "You gave up everything, for someone you claim you'll never ask anything of. Do you understand how unbearable that is? To owe a stranger your whole—"

The wall above us exploded.

Not broke—*exploded*, stone and dust and a herald's banner unfurling through the smoke like a wound opening silk. The usurper himself came through it. I'd seen his likeness on wanted-leaflets twelve years ago, back when I still had a name worth printing: Vaskarion, the Hollow King, tall in that wrong way tall men are when they've bought their height with someone else's years. His armor drank the torchlight. His eyes found Ada first, then me, and his mouth curved.

"The vessel," he said, "and the seller. The prophecy delivers itself. I do love an efficient enemy."

"He's not efficient," Ada said. "He's rude. He interrupted me mid-sentence."

And she attacked a king.

* * *

*Ada*

Here is what I knew about fighting Vaskarion, in the moment I was doing it: nothing. Here is what my body knew: everything. Where his guard would open. When he'd overcommit. The half-second hitch in his rhythm when he shifted weight to his bad knee—a knee he'd injured a century ago, in a coup, in a throne room, in a memory that belonged to a soul that used to live in my head and had gone home.

I fought a hundred-year-old tyrant on borrowed instinct, and it was almost enough.

Almost. He was stronger than sense allowed. My blade rang off his pauldron and numbed my arm to the elbow; his counterstroke caught my ribs and threw me across the hall into a pillar. The world went white at the edges. Through it I heard Silas screaming my name—no, not screaming, *roaring*, a sound too big for a powerless man's throat—and I saw him do something impossible.

He stepped between me and the king. Empty-handed. No magic, no blade, no wards. Just himself, arms slightly out, a man made entirely of stubbornness.

"Vaskarion," Silas said, and his voice shook, and he said it anyway. "Twelve years ago I sold a soul because I was afraid. You're about to lose your throne because you are. I know which of us I'd rather be."

"You have nothing," the king said. "No power. No army. Nothing."

"I have her." He didn't look at me when he said it. He looked at the king, which somehow made it worse, or better, or both. "And she doesn't even remember why she's standing up for me. That's the part you should be afraid of. Loyalty that doesn't need a reason doesn't need a *you*."

The king raised his sword.

And the mountain answered.

Not Silas—he had nothing left to answer with. It was the deep places, the old veins of the world, the storm the heir's return had rekindled in the roots of the rock itself. Lightning came up through the floor in a web of white, wrapping Vaskarion's sword-arm, and where it touched him his stolen height began to fail—years draining out of him like water from a cracked jug, the century of borrowed time finding its way home. He screamed. The sound was smaller than I expected. Most tyrants are, at the end.

The loyalists came up the stairs behind him then—Brakka with her one eye blazing, the cook with a cleaver, a hundred forgotten names with forgotten grievances—and the king who had owned the mountain for a hundred years was pulled down by the people he'd erased.

It was over in less time than it takes to tell. Most terrible things are.

* * *

*Silas*

Afterward there was shouting and weeping and a great deal of blood being mopped by people who deserved better jobs, and Ada found me sitting on the broken barricade with my blistered hands in my lap, feeling precisely as useful as furniture.

She sat down next to me. Didn't speak for a while. The mountain groaned overhead, settling into its new ownership, shedding a fine gray dust like snow.

"The lightning," she said finally. "That wasn't you."

"No."

"But it came when you stood up." She turned and looked at me full-on, and her eyes were the exact color I remembered from a market stall in Greyhollow, from a road under rain, from a hundred moments she no longer owned. "The mountain moved for you. A man with no magic. So either this place has a sense of humor, or—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I read your letter eleven times. It tells me what you did. It doesn't tell me who you are. And I keep waiting for that to matter, Silas. I keep waiting to feel like a stranger next to you. And I don't. I feel like I've known you longer than I've known myself, which is literally true now, and I hate it, and I sat down next to you anyway before I decided to."

"That's loyalty," I said. "The kind that doesn't need a reason."

"Don't quote yourself at me. It's insufferable." But her mouth twitched. "One question. Just one, and then I'll let you be insufferable in peace. On the road—you taught me things. I can feel the shape of lessons I don't remember. Did I laugh? Out there, with you. Did I ever laugh?"

My throat closed. Twelve years of lies, and this—this small, stupid, holy question—was the hardest thing I'd ever been asked.

"Three times that I counted," I said. "Once at my expense. You had excellent taste in what was funny."

She nodded slowly, absorbing it, filing it somewhere the fire hadn't reached. Then she stood, brushed the dust from her knees, and looked down at me with the expression of a woman arriving at a decision she'd been circling for days.

"The chamberlain says the coronation is tomorrow. The heir needs a court, and apparently I'm the heir, which means I need people around me who aren't afraid of me." She paused. "There's a position open. Advisor to the crown. Terrible pay. Constant danger. You'd be working for someone who doesn't trust you yet—but watches you turn every time you enter a room, and wants to know why."

"Ada—"

"It's not forgiveness," she said. "I want to be clear about that. Forgiveness requires memory, and you burned mine. This is something else. This is me choosing you the way I apparently chose you before—without the reasons. Call it an experiment." She held out her hand, blistered palm up, mirroring mine. "Well, storm-caller? Do we begin again?"

Outside, far above, dawn was finding its way down the mountain's throat for the first time in a hundred years. I took her hand.

And somewhere in the deep vaults, in a sealed case the Broker had surrendered without a fight, a luck-knot I'd woven in another life began—very softly—to glow.
