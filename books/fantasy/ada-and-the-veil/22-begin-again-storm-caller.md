## 22. Begin Again, Storm-Caller

### Silas

There is a particular kind of silence that follows the end of a war. Not the silence of absence—battles are loud enough that their absence rings—but the silence of people deciding, all at once and everywhere, that they can stop holding their breath.

Greyhollow had that silence now. Or something like it. The city had never been quiet exactly; it was built on commerce and complaint, and both continued apace. But there was a looseness to it, a slackening in the shoulders of the crowd, that told me the Ministry's writ no longer ran here. The Unmaking had been unmade itself, in the end. There was poetry in that, if you squinted.

I was trying very hard not to squint at anything else.

Three months since the mountain. Three months since I stood in a throne hall with nothing left in me but breath and honesty, watching Ada Vey look at me with the polite blankness of a stranger. Three months since she read my letter—the one I'd written by candlelight while she slept, knowing she would wake and not know why her hands ached from gripping a sword, or why the sight of rain on a road made her laugh for no reason she could name.

She hadn't spoken to me for six weeks after that. Then she'd appeared at the edge of the refugee camp outside Greyhollow, arms crossed, and said, "You owe me a story. Start at the beginning."

I'd started at the beginning. All of it. Wren, the sale, the Broker, the prophecy, every lie in order of commission. She listened without interrupting, which was worse than shouting, and when I finished she'd nodded once and walked away again.

That had been two months ago. Since then: letters. Careful ones. Then less careful ones. Then one where she called me *storm-caller* instead of *Halloran*, and I sat on the floor of my rented room and felt something in my chest come loose that I'd been clenching since I was nineteen years old.

Now I stood in Greyhollow's market with a folding table, a bolt of cord, and absolutely no magic whatsoever, selling luck-knots to people who believed in them honestly, which was more than I ever had.

The irony was not lost on me. It rarely is. Irony and I are old acquaintances—we've shared lodgings for years.

"Two coppers," I told a boy who wanted a knot to help him pass his apprenticeship exam. "And study. The knot helps, but it can't read for you."

"That's what my mum says."

"Your mum sounds like a wise woman."

"She says charm-sellers are mostly frauds."

I tied the knot—a simple overhand weave, three passes, the same pattern I'd sold for twelve years—and slid it across the table. "Your mum," I said, "is right about most charm-sellers."

He grinned and ran off, and I turned to restock my cord and found Ada Vey standing at my stall like she'd always been there, like she belonged in the picture, wearing a grey traveling cloak and an expression I couldn't read.

My hands did not shake. I want that noted for posterity. My hands were perfectly steady, and only the rest of me was trembling.

"You're back," I said. Brilliant opening. Twelve years of double lives and I opened like a first-year clerk.

"I'm back." She looked at the table, the knots, the little tin box of coppers. "This is it? This is the famous enterprise?"

"It's honest work."

"It's *cord*, Silas."

"Honest cord." I set down the spool. "How was the mountain?"

"Loud. Crowded. Full of people who bow to me now, which I hate." She paused. "Wren sends her love. She's teaching the archivists to swear properly. They're scandalized. She's delighted."

Something in my chest went warm and complicated. Wren. Alive, whole-ish, laughing in the deep places where I'd once traded her future away. She'd forgiven me before I'd forgiven myself, which was either grace or a character flaw, and I suspected the latter.

"And you?" I asked. "How are you? Really?"

Ada studied me for a long moment. I have been studied by inquisitors, by Brokers, by a usurper's herald with a knife at my throat, and none of them looked at me the way she did—as though I were a text she was checking against a translation.

"I remember everything you told me," she said. "Every version. The partial confession on the road. The full one in the court. The letter." Her voice didn't waver. "I've spent three months deciding what to do with it."

"And?"

"And I'm still deciding." She reached across the table and picked up one of my luck-knots, turning it in her fingers. "But I decided I'd rather do the deciding somewhere with decent bread. So."

So. That single syllable contained more hope than I had carried in twelve years of exile. I held very still, the way you do when a wild thing lands near you and you don't want to frighten it off.

"There's a bakery on Tanner's Row," I said carefully. "If you're interested in bread as a strategic priority."

"I'm interested," she said, "in buying a charm."

I blinked. "From me?"

"From you." She set the knot down and folded her arms. "A real one this time. None of your honest-cord nonsense. I want a luck-knot from a man who used to call storms, sold by a man who doesn't anymore, bought by a woman who can't remember why any of that matters to her—but keeps feeling like it does."

The market noise went distant. Somewhere a fishmonger was arguing about herring; somewhere a cart wheel squeaked its eternal complaint. And here, at a folding table worth perhaps four coppers, Ada Vey—memory-thief, soul-bearer, heir to a court beneath the mountain, though she'd abdicated half the title and kept the interesting parts—was asking me to sell her magic I no longer had.

"I can't," I said quietly. "It wouldn't be real. There's nothing left in me to put in it."

"I know." Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, but the ground where a smile might build. "Sell it anyway. Charge me full price. And then—" she glanced toward Tanner's Row, toward the ordinary afternoon sun, toward whatever came next— "come tell me the story of how it's made. From the beginning. Take your time. We have time now. That's rather the point of winning."

I took out a length of cord. Red, because red was for courage and she'd always struck me as someone who could use a spare supply.

"Full price is two coppers," I said.

"Robbery."

"Market rate."

"Fine." She slapped the coins into my palm like we were settling a duel. "Make it good, storm-caller."

I made it good. Three passes, overhand weave, the pattern my mother taught me before either of us knew what I was. And when I tied it around her wrist—because she held it out, wrist up, trusting, which undid me more thoroughly than any battle—I let my fingers linger half a second longer than commerce required.

"For luck," I said.

"No," she said. "For a start."

### Ada

Here is what nobody tells you about getting your memories burned out of you: the space they leave isn't empty. It's shaped. Like a glove turned inside out—you can't see the hand anymore, but you can feel exactly where the fingers went.

I lived inside those shapes for months. The shape of a road in the rain that made my throat tight. The shape of a laugh I kept almost catching. The shape of a man's voice saying *I sold a soul, and it was yours,* and the terrible vertigo of hearing it twice—once as memory, once as confession—and knowing both times that he meant it, that he'd carried it, that he'd finally chosen truth over the safe lie even when the truth cost him everything he had left.

The heir's soul was gone. Restored, crowned, seated on a throne of stone and root, dreaming the mountain steady again. What remained in me was mine—all of it, the childhood in the smuggler's rows, the first theft, the thousand small betrayals of my trade. Mine, plus the shapes.

I could have hated the shapes. For a while I tried. But hatred requires a target, and the shapes weren't Silas—they were just the negative space where loving him had been, and you can't punch negative space. Believe me. I considered it.

So instead I did what I do best. I stole.

Not memories—those I gave back, every one, to every person I'd ever lifted them from. That project took weeks and cost me several apologies I'm still not finished making. No, what I stole was something smaller and harder: a decision. I stole it from myself, from the version of me that wanted to walk away clean, and I've never committed a better theft.

Which is how I ended up standing at a market stall buying cord from a powerless storm-caller, and then walking beside him to a bakery on Tanner's Row, our shoulders not quite touching, the whole afternoon open in front of us like unwritten paper.

"You're staring," Silas said.

"I'm observing. It's different. Staring has no craft to it."

"Forgive me, your observation-ship."

"Don't make me regret the shapes," I said, and he laughed—that startled, rusty laugh, like a door opening in a house long shut—and something in my chest fit itself into one of the empty spaces so neatly it was almost frightening.

The bakery smelled of yeast and woodsmoke and the particular optimism of morning bread sold in the afternoon. We got a loaf and a bench by the window, and he tore the loaf in half and handed me the bigger piece without thinking about it, and I thought: *there. That's the shape. That's what the letter meant when it said he asks nothing.*

Because that was the thing. He asked nothing. Not forgiveness, not remembrance, not even companionship—he'd written it plainly, in ink gone slightly blotched where the candle guttered: *You owe me nothing. You never did. If some part of you wants to know who I was, I'll tell you everything. If no part does, I'll be glad simply to know you're free.* A man who sells luck-knots and expects nothing. It should have been easy to dismiss. Instead it lodged under my ribs like a splinter made of hope.

"So," I said, around bread. "Tell me about the knot."

"The knot?"

"The one on my wrist. Its story. You promised."

He looked out the window for a moment, gathering it, and I watched him decide—watched him choose honesty the way other men choose weapons, deliberately, with respect for what it could do.

"My mother taught me the weave," he said. "In a village that doesn't exist anymore, near a river whose name I've forgotten, which feels like a betrayal. She said the pattern was older than the words for it. Three passes: one for what you've survived, one for what you're carrying, one for what you're becoming." His thumb moved over his own bare wrist, where the storm used to live. "I sold charms for twelve years telling customers it was just cord. And it was. But the pattern was true the whole time. I think that's why I kept making it. Some part of me knew I'd need it again—for what I'd survived, what I carried, what I was becoming."

"And which pass am I?" I asked.

He turned and looked at me fully, and there was no flinch in it, no apology hiding behind his eyes. Just a man at a bench, honest and ordinary and unbearably brave about it.

"All three," he said. "Obviously."

I looked down at the red cord on my wrist. One for what I'd survived: a stolen soul, a burned mind, a war. One for what I carried: the shapes, the splinter-hope, the decision I'd stolen from myself. One for what I was becoming: unknown. Unwritten. *Ours*, maybe, if I was brave enough to say it.

"Silas Halloran," I said, "former storm-caller, current fraud-adjacent cord merchant, confessed soul-seller and terrible liar—"

"Noted and accurate."

"—I'm going to steal something from you."

His jaw tightened, just slightly. Old reflex. "What?"

"A tomorrow," I said. "All of them, actually. Every one you've got. I'm going to take them one at a time and you don't get them back, and in exchange I'll give you the same. It's the worst bargain I've ever offered and I've robbed a bishop."

He stared at me. Then he started to laugh—really laugh, helpless, head back, startling the baker—and when he caught his breath his eyes were wet and he wasn't hiding it.

"That's the worst contract I've ever heard," he said. "No exit clause. No terms. Infinite liability."

"Take it or leave it, charm-seller."

He took my hand—the one with the knot on it—and pressed his thumb to the weave, counting the passes like a man reading scripture.

"One for what we've survived," he said. "One for what we carry. One for what we're becoming." He looked up. "Deal."

Outside, Greyhollow went about its afternoon: carts and complaints, herring arguments, a boy with a luck-knot running toward an exam he'd pass on merit alone. Ordinary life, purchased at a price neither of us would ever stop counting. But the light through the bakery window was gold and unhurried, and the bread was good, and the man across from me was learning to be real, and so was I.

Tomorrow, I thought, stealing it early. And the one after that. And the one after that.

Mine. Ours.

Beginning now.
