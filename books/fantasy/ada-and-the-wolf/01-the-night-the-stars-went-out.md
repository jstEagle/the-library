## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The cold on Thornfell had teeth that night.

Edmund Lockridge felt them through his coat as he made the evening round of the gate — left pillar first, always left, then the arch, then the right pillar with its hairline crack he'd been meaning to report for eleven years and never had, because reporting it would mean explaining what the crack was in, and no one alive could answer that question honestly. The stone of the Thornfell gate was not granite. It was not limestone or basalt or anything a mason would recognize. It was older than the word *stone*, and it drank warmth out of the air like a drunk drinks wine, and after thirty years Edmund had stopped asking why.

He ran his gloved hand along the left pillar anyway. Ritual. Habit. The closest thing to prayer he owned.

"Evening," he said to the gate, because he said it every evening, and because thirty years alone on a mountain pass will make a man talk to architecture.

The gate did not answer. It never had. But tonight, when his palm pressed flat against the pillar, he thought he felt something faint beneath the surface — a tremor, quick as a bird's heartbeat, there and gone before he could be sure. He stood very still, breath smoking in the blue dusk, listening with his hand instead of his ears.

Nothing. Just the mountain's old silence, deep as a well.

He told himself it was the cold getting into his bones. He was fifty-eight years old; his knees had opinions about weather now, and lately so did his hands. Thirty years keeping the Thornfell gate, following the same circuit at the same hours, lighting the same lamp against the same dark. His father had kept it before him, and his grandfather before that, back into a line of keepers that the family records — such as they were, a single water-stained ledger kept in a box under the floorboards — simply declined to count. *Keeper of Thornfell* was not a job you applied for. It was a thing you were, the way a river is a river, and Edmund had never once, in three decades, asked himself what the gate was actually *for*.

That was the question he'd been trained not to ask. He only understood this later. That night, walking his rounds under a sky thick with stars, he was still a man who believed the world made sense.

The pass below the gate was empty, as it always was. Smugglers used to run this road, the story went, generations ago, until the wardens closed it and the mountain swallowed the shortcuts. Now nothing came up the switchbacks but wind and snow and the occasional lost shepherd, whom Edmund fed and pointed downhill and never spoke of twice. The wardens' last inspection had been nine years past. They had looked at the gate, looked at him, written something in a book, and left without a word. He'd learned not to find that strange either.

He lit the lamp at the gate's foot — oil, wick, match, the small domestic ceremony of it — and climbed the keeper's path to the watchstone above the arch, where he took the sky's measure each night. This too was ritual: stand, look up, count the constellations by their absence rather than their presence. His father had taught him the trick of it. *You don't watch for stars, boy. You watch for gaps.* Edmund had spent forty years watching for gaps and finding none, and had come to believe that the whole exercise was folklore, an old man's superstition dressed up as duty.

At the hour when the mountains turned from grey to black, he looked up, and every star in the sky went out at once.

Not dimmed. Not clouded over. *Out* — all of them, in the same instant, the way a candle goes out when a door slams somewhere in the house. One moment the heavens were sewn with light, hundreds upon hundreds of them, the Wheel and the Shepherd's Crook and the long bright river of the Milky Road spilling down toward the southern peaks. The next moment: nothing. A sky like the inside of a closed coffin. Black so total it seemed less like an absence of light than a presence of something else — a vast attention, pressing down.

Edmund did not move. Some animal part of him understood that stillness was survival, the way a hare freezes under a hawk's shadow. His breath came out in slow white ribbons. The lamp flame at the gate's foot bent sideways, though there was no wind, and burned low, and steadied.

"Father," he said quietly, to no one, "what am I supposed to do?"

His father had been dead nineteen years. The sky did not answer. But the gate did.

It began in his chest.

There was no other way to describe it, though he would try, in the years afterward, many times: it began behind his sternum, in the place where dread lives, and it sounded like glass — fine glass, crystal, the glass of a cathedral window — beginning to crack. Not all at once. In a spreading web. *Tick. Tick-tick. Tick-tick-tick,* each fracture a little colder than the last, each one running deeper into him, and with every crack he felt something *loosen*, some great weight that had been resting on him since birth shifting its grip, and he understood — with the dumb, absolute certainty of the body, which knows things before the mind consents — that the sound was not coming from the gate behind him.

It was coming from inside him. From whatever had been put inside him.

Edmund dropped to one knee on the watchstone and pressed both fists against his breastbone as if he could hold the shattering together by force. The cracks were coming faster now, a continuous splintering, and threaded through it — God help him — threaded through it was *light*. Not the light of the vanished stars. A different light, gold-green, the color of shallow water over sea-glass, blooming behind his ribs and leaking out through his skin, through the seams of his coat, throwing his own shadow huge and wavering across the snow. And with the light came fragments. Not thoughts exactly. Memories.

A harbor where the water glowed like spilled honey. — A woman's voice singing words he didn't know and somehow knew perfectly, every syllable, a lullaby in a language with more vowels than air. — Stairs cut into a cliff, descending into green fire. — A name, spoken in that voice, tender and warning at once: *Eadmund. Eadmund, little anchor, hold fast.*

He had never seen a harbor like that. He had never heard that song. He had never been called Eadmund — his mother had named him plain Edmund after her own father, a farmer, a man of fields and fences and absolutely no glowing harbors.

And yet the memories fit him like his own hands.

"*No,*" he said aloud, to the memories, to the cracking, to the starless sky. "*No, that isn't mine—*"

The wards broke.

He felt it happen the way a man feels a bone break — a wrongness, a giving-way, followed by a silence in which the body understands the damage is done. Somewhere in the deep architecture of the world, something that had held for centuries let go, and the recoil passed through the gate behind him, through the mountain, through Edmund Lockridge kneeling in the snow, and out into the night like a bell struck once and never again. The lamp at the gate's foot guttered and died. The gold-green light under his skin flared, flickered, and sank back into his chest, leaving him gasping, hollowed, cold in a way that had nothing to do with the weather.

Above Thornfell, the sky stayed empty. No stars. No moon — he realized with a fresh crawl of horror that the moon was gone too, and he could not say when it had gone, whether tonight or years ago, whether he had ever truly seen it at all. The dark overhead was complete and patient.

Edmund got up. He did not remember deciding to. His legs simply carried him down the keeper's path, past the dead lamp, to the gate itself, because if the world was breaking, a keeper went to his post. Whatever else had cracked open tonight, that much remained: thirty years of habit holding up the ruin of everything else like scaffolding around a collapsed house.

He put his hand on the left pillar.

The stone was warm.

In thirty years it had never once been warm. It drank heat; it gave none back; his father had warned him about frostbite from the pillars themselves. Now warmth moved under his palm like blood under skin, and far down in the gate's foundations he felt a slow pulse, one beat, two, arrhythmic and enormous, the heartbeat of something waking.

"*Hold fast,*" said the memory-voice in his skull, in the singing woman's language that was also somehow English, also somehow his. "*Little anchor. Hold.*"

"I'm holding," Edmund whispered. "I don't know what I'm holding, but I'm holding."

* * *

He built a fire in the keeper's hut and sat beside it wrapped in two blankets, drinking tea he did not taste, and tried to assemble the night into something survivable. Stars gone. Wards broken — he knew now that the word *wards* was true, knew it the way he knew his own name, knowledge arriving in him like water filling a dry channel, and that frightened him worse than the darkness did. Something had been sealed behind the Thornfell gate, and the sealing had lived partly in the stone and partly in *him*, and the part in him had just shattered, and the shards were cutting loose memories that belonged to a country he had never visited.

Vaelmoor.

The name surfaced while he stared at the fire, unbidden, complete, heavy as a stone dropped into a well. Vaelmoor. He said it aloud and the fire leaned toward him. He said it again and the hut's timbers creaked, though the wind outside had gone still. There was no Vaelmoor. He was certain of this and simultaneously certain of its opposite: there was a Vaelmoor, there had always been a Vaelmoor, a kingdom of harbors and green fire and songs with too many vowels, erased — and here his mind skidded away from the thought like a boot on ice — erased from every map, every record, every *memory*, except the ones buried in the blood of certain families, in the stone of certain gates, in the keeping of—

Of what? Of whom?

The tea had gone cold in his cup. Outside, the dark pressed at the shutters like a tide against a seawall. Edmund banked the fire, took down the lantern, and did the thing his father had drilled into him across a hundred childhood winters: when the sky is wrong, walk the bounds. Count the gaps. Know your ground.

The snow had stopped falling an hour ago, and the pass lay smooth and blue-white under a light that came from nowhere — no moon, no stars, yet the snow held a faint luminance, as if the darkness itself had begun faintly to glow. Edmund noticed this and filed it away in the growing drawer of things he could not afford to think about yet. He walked the bounds. Left pillar. Arch. Right pillar, hairline crack. Down the switchback road a quarter mile to the cairn that marked the old boundary, and back. Everything as it should be. Nothing come up the mountain. Nothing—

He stopped.

There were tracks in the snow.

They came up the switchback road from the south, from the direction of the lowlands, and they were wolf tracks — large ones, larger than any wolf he'd seen in thirty years of mountain winters, each print crisp-edged and deliberate, a steady unhurried stride. They came up the road, past the boundary cairn, past the place where the smugglers' shortcuts had been swallowed, straight through the drifts, directly to the gate.

And they stopped there. Ten feet from the left pillar. Where Edmund was standing.

But here was the thing that turned his blood to well-water: the tracks led *in*. They did not lead out. There was no second set. Whatever had walked up that road had arrived at the gate and had not walked away — and yet nothing stood between the last print and the place where Edmund himself stood, nothing but unbroken snow, smooth as a bedsheet, glowing faintly with its sourceless light.

Unless.

Very slowly, Edmund raised the lantern.

The wolf was standing in the snow where no tracks led in. It had not approached; it had not appeared to move at all; it was simply *there*, the way a fact is there once you finally see it — ten feet away, at the edge of the lantern's reach, sitting on its haunches in the drift with the composure of a creature that has been waiting a very long time and does not mind waiting longer. It was grey and silver and storm-dark, big as a pony, and its eyes caught the lantern light and threw it back the wrong color: not yellow, not green, but that gold-and-sea-glass shade that had bled out of Edmund's own chest an hour ago.

Every instinct he owned told him to run. Thirty years of solitary mountain life had honed those instincts fine as wire, and every strand of them screamed.

He did not run. He stood in the snow with a lantern shaking in his fist and looked at the impossible wolf, and the wolf looked back at him with an expression he had no right to recognize and recognized instantly — patience, and sorrow, and underneath both, unmistakably, *recognition*. As if it knew him. As if it had crossed a drowned kingdom and a starless night and thirty years of sealed road for the sole purpose of standing in front of this one tired man.

"You're late," said the wolf.

The voice was a young woman's voice. It came from the wolf's mouth and from the snow and from somewhere behind Edmund's ribs, all at once, and it was — God forgive him — familiar, threaded through with vowels from a lullaby he had never been sung.

The lantern shook. The starless dark leaned close. And Edmund Lockridge, keeper of the Thornfell gate, last of his line, heard himself answer in a voice gone quiet and strange:

"Late for what?"

The wolf rose, shook the snow from its coat, and looked up the pass toward the gate, toward the mountains beyond, toward the country that was not on any map.

"That," she said, "is going to take some explaining."
