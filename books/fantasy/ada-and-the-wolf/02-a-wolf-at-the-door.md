## 2. A Wolf at the Door

The stars did not come back.

Edmund stood in the doorway of the gatehouse with his hand still on the latch, watching a sky that had been scoured clean, and told himself — the way he had told himself for thirty years, every time something at Thornfell went wrong — that there was an explanation. Cloud cover too high to see. Some trick of the aurora. A night like any other night, only darker.

But the wards were broken. He had felt them go, felt them shatter behind his sternum like a window struck by a stone, and thirty years of tending the gate had taught him that the wards did not break. They frayed. They thinned. They asked for mending, season after season, the way an old roof asks for shingles. They did not *break*, all at once, with a sound only he could hear.

And yet here was a wolf standing in his snowfield where no tracks led in.

He had not imagined it. He had checked twice, walking the perimeter of the light spilling from the gatehouse windows, lantern held high, breath steaming. The snow lay unbroken in every direction — a smooth white page, six inches of fresh fall, and not one print on it anywhere except his own. The wolf simply was, sitting at the edge of the lantern-light as though it had been sitting there for hours, or years, waiting for him to notice.

It was a large wolf. Larger than any he'd seen in the borderlands, its coat the grey of slate before rain, its eyes catching the lantern flame and holding it — amber, patient, unnervingly intelligent. It did not crouch. It did not bare its teeth. It sat the way a traveler sits at an inn door, waiting to be invited in out of the cold.

Edmund's hand found the iron key at his belt without his deciding to reach for it. The key to the inner lock, the one that had never once been turned in his lifetime. His thumb fit into the worn hollow of the bow, and the metal was warm, which it should not have been, because the night was cold enough to crack stones.

"You're not real," he said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "The wards are down and things are coming through, and you're one of them."

The wolf tilted its head. And then it spoke.

"I've been called worse than unreal." The voice was a young woman's voice — clear, dry, faintly amused, arriving not from the wolf's mouth but from somewhere just beside his ear, the way voices come in dreams. "Though usually by people who were about to be wrong."

Edmund did not move. Every instinct he owned was screaming at once, and beneath the screaming, absurdly, was a thread of something else. Not fear. Recognition. The way you recognize a song you haven't heard since childhood, before you can place where you learned it.

"Things that come through when the wards fail don't talk," he said. "They howl. They hunt. I've cleaned up what they leave for thirty years."

"And what do you think those things were running from, keeper?" The wolf rose, unhurried, and took a single step toward the light. Its paws made no sound and left no prints. "Down. Into the dark places under the world. You've spent your life guarding a door and never once wondered what was knocking."

"I know exactly what's behind this gate." He heard the flatness come into his own voice, the tone he used on smugglers and officials and anyone else who thought the old man at Thornfell might be moved. "Nothing. That's what's behind it. Nothing, and my job is to keep it nothing."

"That's what you were told to know." The wolf sat again, closer now, near enough that he could see the frost melting off its fur in the lantern warmth — though the fur itself stayed dry, impossibly. "Tell me, Edmund Lockridge. When you were a boy, before they brought you here, before the training and the oaths and the long gray years — did you ever dream of mountains?"

The question landed harder than it had any right to. He kept his face still. "Everyone dreams of mountains."

"Not these mountains." The amber eyes held his. "Not the ones on maps. I mean the ones with no names on any chart drawn by any living hand. Shall I tell you some of them? Tell me if they're strangers to you."

She began to speak, and Edmund's blood went cold, and then, strangely, warm.

"The Hollow Vale," she said, "where the river runs backward three days out of seven, and the ferryman poles upstream against the current because going with it means going under. The Stair of Bells, cut into the cliff above the cloud line, nine hundred steps, each one tuned so the wind plays a different note and the monks below can tell the weather by the chord. The Glass Orchard, where the trees bear fruit like lanterns and you must pick them with bare hands or they go dark forever. The city of Vaelmoor itself, white-walled, seven-bridged, built on the bones of the first dragon, its lamps lit from a fire that never went out—"

"Stop." His voice cracked on the word.

Because he knew them. All of them. Not as names — he had never heard these names, would have sworn on his mother's grave he had never heard them — but as *places*. When she said Hollow Vale he could smell the wet stone of the ferry landing. When she said Stair of Bells he could feel the ache in his calves from climbing, could hear the wind striking the ninth step, a low bronze hum like a struck bowl. When she said Glass Orchard his palms remembered cold smooth fruit, remembered the specific care of lifting it free without letting his fingers tighten.

He had never been to any of these places. No living person had. They appeared on no map, appeared in no book, existed nowhere in the world except—

Except inside him. Folded away in some sealed room of his mind, dusty and intact, like furniture in a house whose owner never returned.

"What are you doing to me?" he whispered.

"Nothing," said the wolf. "That's the terrible part, keeper. I'm doing nothing at all. Everything I named, you already knew. I'm only saying it out loud."

He backed up a step, then another, until his shoulders met the doorframe of the gatehouse. Behind him the fire had burned low; the room beyond was all shadow and the glint of his few possessions — the kettle, the ledger, the whetstone worn to a smile's curve by three decades of the same motion. His whole life, laid out in a single glance. Small. Containable. Built, he suddenly understood, like the wall of a dam: solid, respectable, and designed for exactly one purpose.

"Who are you?" he asked.

"Ada."

Just that. She let it sit in the cold air between them.

"Ada what?"

"Just Ada. Names are heavier where I come from. We don't pile them on." The wolf's ears angled forward. "I've been waiting for you a long time, Edmund Lockridge. Since before you were born. Since before your father was born, if we're being honest, though he never knew it and neither did you."

"People don't wait thirty years for a gatekeeper."

"No," she agreed. "They wait for a key. You've been calling yourself a keeper your whole life. It was a kind lie. A gentle one. Whoever raised you, whatever order sent you here, they gave you the most important post on the border and told you it was the dullest, and you believed them, because believing it kept you safe and kept you *here*." Her tail moved once, slowly, through the snow it did not disturb. "The wards didn't fail tonight because something broke them from outside. They failed because their time ran out. Because everything runs out eventually, even bargains. And when they failed, the last lock in the chain — the one made of flesh and memory and thirty years of lonely duty — woke up. That's you. That's why your chest hurts. That's why you know the Stair of Bells."

"I don't believe you." But his hand had come off the key. He noticed this only afterward — noticed that at some point during her speech, his grip had loosened, the iron swinging easy against his hip instead of clenched for a fight.

"You don't have to. Belief is for priests and merchants." The wolf got to her feet and stood at the very edge of the lantern light, half her body silvered, half swallowed by the starless dark. "Come with me instead. Walk the wall with me tonight, the way you do every night. Only this time, look at what you're walking past instead of what you're walking toward. I'll show you the seams. Once you see them, you can't unsee them, and then you'll either send me away or you won't. Either way, it will be your choice, freely made. I've waited too long to take anything from you that isn't given."

For a long moment Edmund said nothing. The wind moved down off the peaks and stirred the snow at his feet, and somewhere far below, in the valley, a dog barked twice and fell silent, as if the whole ordinary world were holding its breath along with him.

Every rule he lived by said: bar the door. Report to the Order. Wait for instructions from men in gray robes who arrived twice a year with supplies and questions and never once looked him in the eye. Every rule said the thing at the edge of the light was a danger, an anomaly, a breach to be contained.

But the rules had never explained why he knew the sound of the ninth bell on the Stair. The rules had never explained why, when she said *Vaelmoor*, something behind his ribs had answered — not in words, but the way a tuning fork answers a note sung across a quiet room.

"If this is a trap," he said finally, "it's an elaborate one."

"All the good ones are." There was that dry amusement again, and beneath it, unexpectedly, something tired. Something old wearing a young voice like a borrowed coat. "Get your cloak, keeper. It's a long walk, and the night has already stolen our stars. I'd rather not give it anything else."

* * *

They walked the wall together, man and impossible wolf, and Edmund did not bar the door behind him, which was its own small surrender.

The wall was less a wall than a spine of fitted black stone running two miles along the pass, older than the Order, older than the road, older — the records hinted, in the careful way records hint — than most kingdoms. For thirty years Edmund had walked it at dusk and dawn, checking the ward-stones set into its crest, noting cracks, pouring the seasonal libations of oil and salt that kept the old magic fed. He knew every stone by touch. He could walk it blind.

Which was, he realized an hour into the walk, precisely the point.

"Here," Ada said, as they reached the third mile-marker. "Kneel. Look at the base of the marker. What do you see?"

"Mortar. Stone. Thirty years of weather."

"Look again. Not with the eyes you use for ledgers."

He knelt. He put his lantern down. And he made himself look — really look, the way he hadn't since he was a boy, before training had taught him to see only what reports required.

The mortar around the marker's base wasn't mortar. Up close, in the honest yellow light, it was writing. Thousands upon thousands of tiny characters, packed dense as ants, spiraling around the stone and down into the earth, and the characters were moving — slowly, imperceptibly, the way a clock's hands move if you stare long enough. Words, flowing in an endless slow circuit around the mile-marker and, he saw now, looking up, around every marker, along the whole spine of the wall, a river of script circling the pass in both directions at once.

"I've touched these stones ten thousand times," he breathed.

"With your hands, yes. Never with the rest of you." Ada paced a slow circle around him. "The wards weren't a spell cast on the mountain, Edmund. They're a story written around it. A very long, very careful story about what this pass contains — which is nothing, which is rock and snow and a dull old keeper, nothing to see here, move along. Everyone who comes within a mile of Thornfell reads that story without knowing they're reading it. Smugglers turn back because the pass feels unlucky. Officials forget to send inspectors. Even you—" the wolf's voice gentled, "—even you were given a life so quiet and so certain that you never once thought to ask why the Order posted a man alone at the least-traveled crossing in the range, and never once let him leave."

"They let me leave. Supply runs. Twice a year, to—"

"To Grayford. Yes. And what happens in Grayford?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. Searched, genuinely searched, for the memory of the market town two days south, and found — fog. Warm rooms, the innkeeper's face a blur, coins changing hands with someone whose features slid away whenever he reached for them. Twice a year, for thirty years, and he could not describe a single street in Grayford.

"Oh," he said. Very quietly. The word fell out of him like a stone down a well.

"There it is," Ada said softly. "I'm sorry. I'd have broken it to you more kindly if there were a kind way."

Edmund stayed kneeling a moment longer, his palm flat against the mile-marker, feeling the faint pulse of the moving script against his skin like a second heartbeat. Then he stood, and picked up his lantern, and looked east — past the wall, past the pass, into the high country where the mountains climbed into country no map had ever bothered to draw, because no mapmaker had ever come back from it.

Or so he had always believed.

"The Hollow Vale," he said. "If I walked east from here. How many days?"

The wolf's ears pricked forward. Something flickered in the amber eyes — surprise, quickly mastered, and beneath the surprise, unmistakably, hope.

"Four, if the river's running true. Three, if it likes you." A pause. "You know the way."

"I don't know the way," Edmund said. But even as he said it, his feet had turned east, and some compass deep in his chest — older than his oaths, older than his name — was already swinging, already pointing home. "I've never been anywhere. That's what they told me. That's what I told myself."

"And now?"

He looked down at the wolf. At the creature that cast no shadow in the lantern light, that waited in the snow without tracks, that spoke with a dead woman's patience and a living girl's wit. He should be afraid. He was afraid. But threaded through the fear, gaining strength with every beat of his heart, was the other thing — the recognition, the answering note, the sense of a door in himself standing open for the first time in thirty years with cold clean air pouring through.

"Now," Edmund Lockridge said, "I think I'd like to see what I've been forgetting."

Ada's tail swept once through the untouched snow. "Then we should hurry," she said, and all the amusement had gone out of her voice, leaving something raw underneath. "Because we're not the only ones who felt the wards break. And the ones coming for this gate next won't stop to talk."

Far to the west, down the valley road, a light appeared. Then another. Then a dozen, moving in perfect silence through the dark, in step, like a procession — or a hunt.

Edmund snuffed the lantern.
