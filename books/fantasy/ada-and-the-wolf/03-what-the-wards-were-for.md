## 3. What the Wards Were For

I had watched him for thirty years, and in all that time he had never once looked up at the gate the way a man looks at a thing he loves. He looked at it the way a jailer looks at a door — with respect, with vigilance, with the particular weariness of someone who has decided never to wonder what lies on the other side.

That was the tragedy of Edmund Quintana. Not that he didn't know. That he had been made *not* to know.

The snow was still falling when I led him inside his own watch-house, and he moved through it like a man moving through a dream he half-remembered from childhood — touching the back of a chair as if to confirm it was real, glancing at the cold hearth as if expecting fire. He had not invited me in. Wolves do not require invitations; that is a courtesy reserved for vampires and polite guests. But he had not barred the door either, and after thirty years of watching him bar everything, I counted that as progress.

"Sit," I said.

"I'd rather stand."

"You'll want to sit down for this." I settled onto the floor before the hearth, folding my legs beneath me, and let the wolf-shape fall away like a coat slipping off one shoulder. It is an old trick and an expensive one — every change costs me something, some small hoard of remembered light — but if I was going to unmake thirty years of his life, I would not do it as a beast crouching at his fire. I would do it as what I was: the memory of a dead country, wearing the shape of a young woman because that was how he had last seen me, three decades ago, in a dream he did not remember having.

He stared. To his credit, he did not reach for the iron poker.

"You," he said slowly. "You were in my mother's house. The night the fever took her. I was nine years old. There was a woman at the window, and the snow wasn't touching her, and—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening. "I told myself I dreamed that."

"You did tell yourself that. Every night, for years. You were very thorough about it." I fed a splinter of kindling into the hearth and coaxed it alight with a breath, because my hands were cold in this shape and I wanted them warm for what came next. "The wards did that, Edmund. They take the things that don't fit and fold them away into the dark. Dreams. Déjà vu. Songs you hum without knowing where you learned them. A woman at a window who cast no shadow."

"The wards." He said it flatly, testing the word. "The wards keep smugglers off the pass. Bandits. Things that come down out of the high country in winter. My father kept this gate before me, and his father before him, and none of them ever—"

"None of them ever knew. No. That's rather the point."

I reached into the folds of my cloak — a garment that was not entirely a garment, being woven partly out of a winter's night in Vaelmoor that no one else remembered anymore — and drew out the fragment.

It was small. That was all I could say for it. A shard of dragon-fire, no bigger than a thumbnail, and it burned without heat or light, a coal of pure remembering, red-gold at its heart like the last ember of a bonfire everyone else had already gone home from. In the old days there had been a dragon whose body was a furnace and whose breath was dawn, and when she died — when they *made* her die, which is a story for another telling — her fire had scattered across the kingdom like sparks from a struck flint, and each spark held a piece of what we were.

I set it on the stone hearth between us.

Edmund looked at it the way a starving man looks at bread he has been told is poisoned.

"What is it?"

"A memory. The last honest thing in this mountainside." I turned it gently with one finger, and it rolled, and the light inside it shifted like a living eye. "You asked me last night why the stars went out. You asked me what broke. So I'm going to tell you what the Thornfell gate actually is, and you're going to want to stand, and then you're going to wish you were sitting, and then you're going to wish you had never been born on this side of the mountain. And then, if you're the man I think you are, you're going to ask me what you can do about it. That last part is the only part I care about."

"Ada." My name in his mouth sounded strange to him, though he'd spoken it once already. "Whatever game this is—"

"It is not a game. Games end." I looked up at him, and I let him see all of it — the fading, the fraying at my edges that he couldn't quite perceive yet but felt anyway, the way people feel a draft from a door that isn't open. "Thirty years ago, the world made a bargain. Not your world — the world entire, the wide world, the world of kings and maps and harvests. And the bargain was this: there was a kingdom called Vaelmoor, and Vaelmoor was dying, devoured by a hunger that came out of the dark between stars, and the only way to save the rest of everything was to cut Vaelmoor loose. To seal it away. To erase it so completely that even its own survivors would forget it had existed — because a grief that is remembered can be followed, and a grief that is forgotten cannot."

His face had gone very still. Stillness, in Edmund, was not calm. I had learned that much in thirty years of watching. Stillness was the ground going hard before the earthquake.

"There is no kingdom called Vaelmoor," he said.

"No," I agreed softly. "There isn't. That's the bargain working exactly as intended. Say it again."

"There is no—" He stopped. His hand had drifted, without his noticing, to his own chest, pressing flat against the sternum as though something in there had knocked. "There's no such place. There's no such place, and yet I know the word. I know it the way you know a room you've walked through drunk — the shape of it, the turn of the door. Ada, what is happening to me?"

"The wards are breaking." I nodded toward the window, toward the black sky beyond, where no stars shone where stars should be. "And when they break, everything they swallowed starts coming back up. The memories first. Then the roads. Then the people — or what's left of the people. The gate doesn't keep smugglers out, Edmund. Smugglers were always a convenient story, a duty to give the keepers' line something to guard so they wouldn't ask questions. The gate keeps *Vaelmoor* out. Or rather—" I corrected myself, because precision mattered here, mattered more than kindness, "—it keeps the *memory* of Vaelmoor from bleeding back into a world that paid a terrible price to be rid of it. Every warden, every wardstone, every rune your family has renewed with their blood for six generations — all of it, one great forgetting, holding shut a door on a drowning country."

"And the stars?"

"The stars were part of the seal. Vaelmoor's sky was stitched into yours — a whole constellation, hidden among your heavens, disguised as empty dark. When the kingdom was sealed, its stars were taken too. Last night, whatever holds the bargain together finally failed, and the stitches tore out all at once." I smiled, and it was not a happy smile; I have very few of those left. "The stars didn't go out, Edmund. They went *home*."

For a long moment the only sound was the fire eating the kindling and the wind worrying at the eaves. Edmund stood with his hand still pressed to his chest, and I watched him fight it — watched the trained keeper in him reach for the familiar litany of duties, patrols, wardstones, weather — and watched all of it fail against the simple weight of what I had said.

Then he knelt. Slowly, joints cracking, a man of fifty-odd years lowering himself to the hearthstone across from me, until the little fragment of dragon-fire sat between us like a third guest.

"Show me," he said.

"Edmund—"

"*Show me.* If I'm going to lose my mind, I'd rather do it looking at the truth than hiding from it. Thirty years I've kept this gate. Thirty years I've done my duty without asking why, because my father told me asking why was the first step to failing. Well." His voice roughened. "I'm asking why."

So I gave it to him.

"Take it in your hand. It won't burn you. Fire remembers; it doesn't harm what it wants to be remembered by." I nudged the shard toward him across the stone. "But I warn you — the wards put walls around certain rooms in your head, and when the fire opens one, it doesn't open it gently. Whatever comes, let it come. Don't fight it. Fighting it will only make it worse."

He picked it up.

I have seen many souls touch dragon-fire. I saw a king weep over the memory of his daughter's christening. I saw a soldier laugh for an hour at a joke his dead brother told. I saw Sera, who you will meet, hold a spark to her tongue like a communion wafer and speak her grandmother's name for the first time in thirty years. Dragon-fire does not show you visions. It shows you *yours*.

Edmund's eyes lost their focus. His breathing changed — quickened, caught, steadied into something slower and deeper, the breathing of a man asleep or a man remembering. And then, in the firelight, I watched thirty years of careful forgetting crack down the middle like river ice in spring.

"Oh," he said. Just that. A single syllable, wrung out of him. "Oh — that's — *mother*—"

"Yes," I whispered. "Go on. I'm here."

"She's singing." His voice had gone somewhere younger. "There's a kitchen — no, not a kitchen, a *hearth-hall*, stone, the stones are black and the firelight makes them gold, and there's a cradle, and she's singing over the cradle, and the song is—" He frowned, struggling. "I don't know the words. I don't know the language. But I know the *tune*. I've hummed it my whole life. When I'm mending tack. When I'm walking the wall at dusk. I thought it was something my father—"

"It wasn't." My throat was tight. "It's a lullaby from the Vale of Sorrows, in the lowlands of Vaelmoor. It's older than your family's name. Mothers sang it there for two thousand years, and the last one to sing it was singing it over you."

"That's impossible." But he said it the way a man says *the ground is falling* while the ground is falling. "My mother was Marta Quintana. She was born in Harrowdale. She died of lungfever when I was nine. She never—"

"Marta Quintana was real. She loved you. She is not the woman in the song." I leaned forward. "Edmund. Look at the cradle. Look at the woman's hands."

He looked. I watched him look. And I watched the vertigo take him — that terrible, tilting moment when the floor of a life gives way and a man discovers the identity he has stood on all these years was a floor laid over a well.

"Her hands," he breathed. "She has a scar. Here—" he touched his own left wrist, below the cuff, where I knew — I had always known — a pale seam of skin lay that he had never been able to explain. A scar from a birth that happened nowhere, in a country that didn't exist. "The woman in the song has the same scar. Ada. *Ada.* Whose memory is this? Whose mother is singing?"

"Yours."

The word dropped into the watch-house like a stone into deep water.

"Your mother was Vaelmoori. Born under the hidden stars, in the last years before the sealing. Your family's line doesn't come from Harrowdale, whatever the parish records say — the records were written by the same hands that wrote the bargain. Your ancestors were chosen for one reason: the gate needs a key, and a key must be made of both worlds. Vaelmoor blood to fit the lock. Living-world blood to survive the turning of it. Six generations, Edmund. Six generations of Quintanas raised on this mountain, taught the patrol routes and the ward-litanies and the convenient story about smugglers, each of them born to be what you are — and none of them ever told, because none of them ever *knew*. The forgetting was bred into the keeping. You weren't guarding a secret, my love. You *were* the secret."

He was shaking. I could see it start in his hands and climb. The dragon-fire shard sat in his palm, glowing faintly brighter now, drinking in his recognition the way a plant drinks rain.

"My whole life," he said. The words came out sanded flat, stripped of everything. "My whole life was designed. My post. My duty. Even my loneliness — even *that* was part of the design, wasn't it? A keeper with no wife, no children, nothing to anchor him anywhere but the gate. They built me like a tool and never told me I was a tool."

"They built you like a *door*, Edmund. And doors get to choose whether they open." I reached across and closed his fingers over the shard, folding his grip around it. "Listen to me. This is the part that matters. Yes, your life was arranged. Yes, the lie runs all the way down. But the choice — what you do in the next few days, now that the wards are dust and the stars are gone home — the choice is the first thing in six generations that no one has designed for you. It's yours. Entirely yours. Do you understand? For the first time, the Quintana line gets to *decide*."

"Decide what?" He lifted his head, and his eyes were wet and furious and more alive than I had seen them in thirty years of watching. "What is there to decide?"

"Whether Vaelmoor dies forgotten, or comes back." I rose, because outside the window the eastern sky had begun to bruise grey, and grey meant morning, and morning meant the mountain would wake, and we had a long way to go and hollow-eyed men behind us. "Because the wards aren't just broken, Edmund. They were *broken*. Shattered from the other side, on purpose, by something that has been feeding on the erased for thirty years and has grown hungry enough to want the rest. And when it finishes with Vaelmoor's remains, it will come through the gate for everything else."

He stood too, slowly, the shard still clenched in his fist, its glow leaking red-gold between his fingers like a secret he was holding shut.

"Where are we going?" he asked. And I noted — I hoarded it away with my other treasures — that he said *we*, without hesitation, without even hearing himself say it.

"Down," I said. "Off the mountain, onto the roads your family used to move quietly and never spoke of. The smugglers' roads, Edmund. It seems the convenient stories had one true thread in them after all." I pulled the hood of my impossible cloak up over my hair and moved to the door. "We're going into a country that doesn't exist, to find the last embers of a fire that died before your grandfather was born. And we need to leave now, because the things your masters sent to silence the gatekeeper will be here within the hour."

He looked back once — at the watch-house, at the hearth, at the chair his father had sat in and his grandfather before him — and I saw him say goodbye to all of it in the space of a single breath.

Then Edmund Quintana, keeper of the Thornfell gate for thirty years, stepped out into the starless snow, following a wolf into the dark.

* * *

Behind us, in the empty watch-house, the fragment of dragon-fire he had left on the hearthstone pulsed once, twice, like a heartbeat remembering itself — and went dark.
