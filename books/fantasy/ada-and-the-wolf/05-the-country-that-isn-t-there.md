## 5. The Country That Isn't There

I had crossed the threshold a thousand times in the years since the sealing, and every crossing felt like walking into a house where someone had just died.

This time, Edmund came with me.

He stopped at the boundary the way a man stops at the edge of deep water — not afraid, exactly, but suddenly aware of how much of him was about to be somewhere else. The pass behind us was already closing, stone knitting over stone with a sound like a held breath released. Ahead, the road descended into a light that had no source: a violet dusk that never deepened to night, never brightened to morning, hanging over the mountains like a memory of evening that refused to end.

"That's not right," Edmund said quietly. "The sun set an hour ago. Behind us it's full dark."

"Behind us is the world," I said. "Ahead of us is what the world agreed to forget."

He looked at me then — really looked, the way he looked at everything, with the patient attention of a man who had spent thirty years watching a gate and learning its moods. Snow clung to his shoulders. His breath made small ghosts in the cold.

"You've been here before," he said.

"I've *been* here before." I let the words sit. "Every day for thirty years, Edmund. Walking these roads while no one saw me. Speaking to people who flickered like candle flames and couldn't hold my voice in their ears for more than a moment. This country and I have been keeping each other company."

"And now?"

"Now you're here, and they can see me." I nodded down the slope, toward the first rooftops showing through the twilight haze — slate roofs, chimneys, a bell tower leaning like an old man against the wind. "Because you remember them. Your blood remembers them. And where your blood goes, I am real."

He didn't answer. He started down the hill instead, boots crunching on snow that fell upward in places, drifting back toward the sky as though the land couldn't quite decide which way gravity ran. I paced beside him, four feet finding purchase on stone that remembered being a road even when it wasn't one anymore.

* * *

The town had a name once. It had three names, actually, because Vaelmoor was old enough that its towns wore their histories like coats, one layered over another. The oldest name meant *Where the Lanterns Rest*. The second meant *Harbor of the Low Clouds*. The last name — the one it wore when the world sealed it away — was Correnvale, and that was the only one anyone alive remembered, because remembering was my work, and I had kept it safe the way a mother keeps a child's first tooth.

The lanterns were still lit.

They burned in brackets along the main street, small blue-white flames that cast no heat and threw shadows in the wrong directions. Under them walked the people of Correnvale, going about the business of a town that had been dead for thirty years and didn't know it. A baker pulled loaves from an oven that hadn't held fire since the sealing. A woman beat a rug from a second-story window, and the dust drifted sideways into a wind that wasn't blowing. Two boys chased a hoop down the cobbles, laughing, and the sound reached us a half-second after their mouths moved, like thunder arriving late after lightning.

Edmund stopped in the middle of the street.

"They can't see us," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"The baker might. For a moment. If you stand where his son used to stand." I watched the man's hands work the dough, steady and sure, repeating a motion he had performed ten thousand times and would perform ten thousand more until the memory wore through. "Mostly they see through us. We're weather to them. We're the sound of the house settling at night."

One of the hoop-boys swerved, chasing his toy across our path — and stopped. Just for a heartbeat. His eyes found Edmund's face and widened, and for that single suspended moment he was fully, terribly *present*, a living boy looking at a living man across thirty years of silence.

"Mister," he said. "Mister, do you know if the ferry's running? Da said the ferry would run today."

Then the flicker took him, candle-flame guttering, and he was chasing his hoop again, laughing his delayed laugh, and Edmund stood very still in the street with the boy's question hanging around him like smoke.

"The ferry," he said hoarsely. "Ada, there's no river here. There hasn't been—"

"There was. There is. Both at once." I touched his arm with my muzzle, gently. "That's what this place is. Not a ruin. A *hesitation*. Everything Vaelmoor was, caught mid-breath, waiting for a world that promised to come back and never did."

We walked on. I showed him the market square where merchants had sold dragon-lit glass that glowed warm for a hundred years. I showed him the chapel whose bells rang at hours that no longer existed. I showed him the fountain at the center of everything, dry for three decades, where a stone wolf reared up with water frozen forever mid-leap — and Edmund stared at that wolf a long time, longer than he'd stared at anything else, and said nothing, and I didn't ask him what he was thinking, because I could see it in the set of his shoulders.

His blood knew this place. His blood had helped bury it. And some part of him, deeper than memory, deeper than guilt, was *home*.

* * *

Near the town's edge, where the roofs gave way to orchards heavy with fruit that never rotted and never ripened, an old woman sat on a bench beneath a pear tree. She was knitting something long and white — a scarf, perhaps, or a shroud; in Vaelmoor the two were often the same length. When we passed, she looked up, and her eyes were clear in a way the others' eyes weren't, and she said, in the old tongue that Edmund shouldn't have understood but did:

"*You brought him. The keeper's blood.*"

Edmund turned. "You can see us."

"I can see *you*, boy. Her, I've always seen." The old woman's needles clicked. "She walks past my window every evening. Every evening for thirty years. Never says a word. Tonight she brings a stranger who wears his face like a borrowed coat." She squinted at Edmund. "You have his jaw. The Builder's jaw. God rest him and rot him both."

"Do you know me?" Edmund asked.

"I know your face. Whether I know *you* depends on what you've come to do." She went back to her knitting, dismissing us, but her needles slowed, and she added, softer: "The pear tree still fruits. Did you notice? Nothing else grows here. Nothing changes. But the tree keeps trying. Stubborn thing." Her eyes lifted to mine, and there was pity in them, and I hated it. "Like her. Stubborn things, both. Holding on past all reason."

We left her knitting under her impossible tree, and Edmund was quiet for a long mile.

* * *

It was on the ridge above the drowned harbor — the sea itself had been unmade, replaced by a plain of grey mist that rolled where waves should have broken — that I finally told him.

He asked. That was the thing about Edmund; he always asked, even when the asking cost him. He sat down on a stone wall that was solid on one side and suggestion on the other, and he looked out at the mist-sea, and he said: "Tell me what you are. Truly. You've told me what you know. You haven't told me what you are."

I sat beside him. My haunches found the wall and held, because he believed I was solid, and in this country belief was architecture.

"When they sealed Vaelmoor," I said, "they didn't just hide it. They *unmade the knowing of it*. Every map, every song, every mind beyond the border. Mothers woke and couldn't recall their daughters' faces if those daughters lived here. Sailors forgot harbors they'd traded at for forty years. The kingdom didn't die, Edmund. It was *amputated* — cut away clean, and the wound closed so well the world never felt the pain."

"But something had to hold all of it. All that memory. All those lives." I looked out at the mist. "A seal that strong needs a vessel. Somewhere for the forgetting to go. So they made one. Or it made itself — the stories disagree, and I was there, and I still don't know. But everything this country was, everything it said and sang and suffered, poured into a single shape so it wouldn't bleed back into the world and drown it."

Edmund was very still. "You."

"I'm not a wolf wearing a woman's voice, and I'm not a woman wearing a wolf's shape. I'm Vaelmoor's memory given flesh. Every street we walked today, I remember being built. Every person we passed, I remember their christening and their wedding and their funeral — except they haven't had funerals, because they haven't been allowed to finish." My voice came out steadier than I felt. "I am a country wearing a skin, Edmund. And skins wear thin."

"What happens when yours does?"

I had known he would ask that too. I looked at the twilight sky, where stars should have been and weren't — where nothing had ever been, since the night of the sealing, because Vaelmoor's piece of heaven had been taken along with everything else.

"Memories fade when no one holds them," I said. "I have been holding eleven thousand years of a country alone, with no one to remember *me*. Thirty years ago I began to fray. Little things first. The smell of bread in Correnvale — gone, one morning, just gone, and I knew there had been a smell and couldn't find it. Then whole streets. Then names." I flexed my paws against the stone. "I am fading, Edmund. Slowly, but surely, the way a fire dies when no one feeds it. And when I'm gone, Vaelmoor doesn't sleep or wait anymore. It simply ends. Quietly. Completely. As if it had never been."

The mist-sea rolled against shores that weren't there. Somewhere below us, faintly, the chapel bell rang an hour that had been abolished.

Edmund reached out — hesitated — and then laid his hand on my head, between my ears, the way you touch something precious and frightened. His palm was warm. Warmth was rare here. I leaned into it before I could stop myself.

"Then we feed the fire," he said simply. "Whatever it takes."

And I wanted to tell him then. I wanted to say: *there is more, Edmund, there is a reason I found you specifically, there is a bargain I have not named, and when you learn it you may hate me.* The words climbed my throat like the upward-falling snow.

But the twilight flickered.

All of it — the sky, the mist, the town below, the pear tree with its stubborn fruit — shuddered like a candle in a draft, and for one terrible instant I saw through the world's skin: blackness, vast and hungry, pressing in from every direction, and within it a cold that was not temperature but *absence*, moving with purpose, moving toward us—

Then it was gone. Twilight again. Bell again. The baker baking, the boys laughing their late laughter.

Edmund was on his feet, hackles-raised tension in every line of his body. "What was that? Ada — what was *that*?"

I stared out at the grey sea, and my fading heart — the one piece of me that had never beaten until tonight — hammered against my ribs.

"Something has noticed," I said, "that the door is open."
