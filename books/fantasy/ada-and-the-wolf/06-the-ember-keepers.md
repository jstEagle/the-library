## 6. The Ember Keepers

The chapel announced itself before we saw it.

Ada stopped at the crest of a ridge of black glass — cooled dragon-ash, she'd called it, though I hadn't known such a thing could exist until she said it — and her ears went forward, and her whole body went still in that way wolves have when the world has just told them something.

"Listen," she said.

I listened. Wind through the ruins behind us, the thin whistle of it over broken streets. The flicker-people had thinned as we walked deeper into Vaelmoor's remains, fewer of those half-lit figures guttering in doorways, and I had told myself that was mercy rather than loss. Beneath the wind there was something else: a sound like a bell struck underwater. Once. Then again.

"Chapel," Ada said. "There are people there who don't flicker."

I didn't understand what she meant until we came down off the ash-ridge and saw them.

* * *

The chapel had been built to look small and then built again to look impossible. Its outer walls were low fieldstone, mossed and crumbling, the kind of ruin you'd pass on any mountain road without a second glance. But the doorway framed a space far larger than the walls could hold — a nave that stretched back into blue shadow, pillars rising toward a vaulted ceiling lost in gloom, windows of colored glass that showed no saints and no suns but only deep, patient darkness shot through with veins of ember-red, as if someone had painted the moment just after a fire dies.

And in that impossible interior, by the light of a brazier that burned without smoke, sat perhaps forty people who cast true shadows.

They were gaunt, gray-cloaked, their faces lined with a hunger that wasn't for food. They sat in rows on stone benches worn smooth by generations of sitting. And every one of them turned to look at us as we crossed the threshold — forty faces, old and young, turning together with the slow synchronization of people who have been waiting so long that waiting has become a discipline.

At the head of the nave, before the brazier, stood an iron cage no bigger than a bread box. Inside it, coals glowed. Not red, not orange — they burned white-gold, the color I had seen only once before, in the fragment Ada had pressed into my palm on the smugglers' road, the one that had shown me my mother singing in a language I shouldn't know.

Dragon-fire. The last of it, or near enough.

"You're not flickering," I said, because I couldn't think of anything wiser.

"No," said the woman who rose from the front bench. "We're what's left of the ones who refused to be forgotten."

She was old the way mountains are old — not bent by it, just weathered into permanence. Silver hair cut short and practical, hands like knotted rope, eyes the pale gray of ice under snow. She wore a key on a cord around her neck, an ugly functional thing, and I found my gaze snagging on it the way a boot snags on a root.

"I'm Sera," she said. "Last Keeper of the Ember. You've come a long way to stand in our chapel, gatekeeper."

"I'm not—" I started.

"You are." She said it without heat, the way you'd correct a child on a point of geography. "You walk like him. You stand like him. Thirty years I've prayed never to see that face again, and here it is, younger than I remember and wearing the same stubborn jaw." She descended the two steps from the brazier dais. The crowd parted for her without looking away from me. "Edmund Lockridge. Or should I say — Edmund Quintana? Which name did they give you?"

My mouth went dry. Quintana. My mother's name. The one word from her singing-language that Ada's ember-fragment had burned into me, the name I'd carried all my life like a stone in my boot and never once spoken aloud to another living soul.

"How do you know that name?"

Sera smiled, and there was nothing kind in it.

"Because we wrote it down," she said. "Every generation, we write it down. *When the keeper comes, he will bear this face.*" She stopped three paces from me, close enough that I could see the fine map of scars across her knuckles, burn scars, old ones. "We have waited a hundred and sixty years for you, Edmund. We drew your face from memory so our children would know it. Would you like to see how well we remembered?"

* * *

She led me down the side aisle while Ada paced at my heel, hackles up, a low sound in her throat that I felt more than heard. The crowd watched us pass. Some of them bowed their heads as Sera went by; some of them made signs against evil when I went by, quick and furtive, thinking I wouldn't notice.

I noticed.

The wall beside the eastern pillar was hung with cloth — tapestries, dozens of them, faded to the color of weak tea. Sera pulled one aside with a hand that did not quite tremble, and I understood why her voice had that edge of practiced steadiness. Behind the tapestry, carved directly into the living rock of Vaelmoor itself, was a frieze.

A man stood at a gate. He was tall, broad-shouldered, his hair tied back, and he held a chain of linked stars in both hands, drawing it closed across a doorway through which a burning country poured its light. His face —

His face was mine.

Not similar. Not a family resemblance. It was my face, line for line, the same heavy brows, the same crooked nose I'd broken falling from a watchtower at nineteen, rendered in stone by hands that had known him, or believed they knew him, well enough to carve his grief into his cheeks.

"That's not possible," I heard myself say. My voice sounded very far away. "That man is dead. He'd be — he'd be centuries old if he'd lived."

"He is dead," Sera said. "Dead a hundred and sixty years, and good riddance to him, though I'll grant he thought he was saving everything worth saving." She touched the carved face with two fingers, almost gently, and I hated how much that gentleness cost her. "Corvus Quintana. Warden-architect of Thornfell. Builder of the gate. The man who took a kingdom of two hundred thousand souls and folded it out of the world like a letter no one would ever read."

The floor seemed to tilt. I put a hand against the pillar. The stone was warm.

"My father was a shepherd," I said. "My mother — my mother sang songs in a language nobody spoke. I was born in a village four days' ride south of the gate. I've kept that gate for thirty years because my predecessor chose me and his predecessor chose him, and none of us ever asked—"

"None of you ever asked," Sera agreed. "That was the design. Corvus was thorough. He didn't trust strangers with his lock, so he built a line of keepers instead — blood of his blood, seeded out into the borderlands like seed corn, each generation raised ignorant, each chosen young, each bound to the gate by wards written into their own bones." Her pale eyes came up to mine. "You felt it shatter, didn't you? The night the stars went out. Like glass in your chest."

I said nothing. There was nothing to say. She had described it exactly — the shattering, the glass, the thirty years of certainty coming apart in a single breathless instant.

"The wards were woven through the keeper's heart," Sera went on, and now her voice hardened, the gentleness gone entirely. "So that when the gate finally failed, the last keeper would feel it die. So that he would come looking. So that he would walk into Vaelmoor on his own two feet, following the wolf that leads all Quintanas to their purpose." She glanced at Ada, and Ada met her gaze with yellow eyes that gave back nothing. "Even the wolf. Even her. Part of the architecture."

"That's a lie," I said.

"It's the truth, and you know it's the truth, because you've felt the shape of it since the night your sky went dark." Sera stepped closer. Up close, I could see that her eyes were wet, and that she was too proud to let the tears fall. "Two hundred thousand people, gatekeeper. Children. Grandmothers. A whole country with its own music, its own saints, its own way of saying *good morning*. And one man decided the world couldn't afford to remember us, so he erased us — and then he had the gall to leave his own face walking around above the seal like a signature on a theft."

"We didn't know," I said. "I swear to you, whatever he did, whatever was done through us — we were tools. We were kept blind."

"Yes." Sera nodded slowly. "Blind tools. That's exactly what you were. And now the blindfold is off, and the tool has walked into the workshop, and I am supposed to believe it came here for any reason but the one it was made for."

"What reason do you think that is?" Ada asked. Her voice, when she used the woman's shape of it, filled the nave the way cold fills a cellar.

Sera looked at her for a long moment. Then she laughed — a short, bitter sound with no humor anywhere in it.

"To finish the work," she said. "To close the seal properly this time. To make certain that what Corvus began, the Quintana line ends. Because that's what keepers *do*, isn't it? They keep things shut."

* * *

The accusation hung in the colored dark, and around me the Ember Keepers had risen from their benches, forty bodies between me and the door, and I understood with a clarity that felt almost calm that if they decided I was my ancestor's ghost come back to finish his crime, there was nothing Ada and I could do about it in this chapel that remembered better than it forgave.

"I came because the wards broke," I said. "I came because a wolf told me the truth my own life had hidden from me. I came because—" I stopped. Because what? Because I'd looked at the frieze of my own face and felt something worse than horror. Recognition. As if some buried part of me had always known the shape of the work I was born for, and had spent thirty years pretending otherwise.

"Because?" Sera prompted, merciless.

"Because I need to know what he stole," I said. "All of it. Every piece. And then I'll decide what to do with the knowledge — me, not him, not his design, not whatever script he wrote into my blood. I am done being a tool, Sera. If you want to hate me, hate me for my own choices. They're fresh. I promise you'll find plenty."

Silence. The brazier crackled, and inside its iron cage the dragon-fire coals pulsed once, twice, like a sleeping heart, and every face in the chapel turned toward that light with the same naked hunger, and I understood that these people had guarded a handful of embers for six generations because embers were all that remained of a sun.

Sera studied me for a long time. Whatever she was looking for, I couldn't tell whether she found it.

"Corvus said something like that too, at the end," she said quietly. "*I will decide.* Men of your line always decide alone, and the world always pays the bill." She turned away from me, toward the cage, toward the coals. "But you're right about one thing. The wards are broken, the stars are going out one by one, and something in the dark is eating what's left of the sky. We can't afford the luxury of hating you properly. Not yet."

She unhooked the key from around her neck and held it up. Iron, ugly, functional — and I felt it in my chest, in the place where the wards had shattered, a pulling sensation, like a hook set behind the breastbone.

"There are five embers left in the world," Sera said. "Four are here, in this cage, and we have killed to protect them, and we will again. The fifth is not here. The fifth is where Corvus hid it, in the one place in all of Vaelmoor even we dare not go." She let the key swing on its cord, catching the firelight. "You want to know what your ancestor stole, gatekeeper? Fine. Then take us to the Dragon's Throat, and open what only Quintana blood can open — and pray, boy, pray with everything you have, that when you do, you're not doing exactly what he designed you to do."

Behind me, Ada made a sound I had never heard from her before — low, urgent, afraid.

"Edmund," she said. "Don't touch the key."

But my hand was already moving.
