## 8. Ada's Bargain

I had told him the truth a hundred times in pieces. That is how memory keeps itself alive when it cannot afford to be whole — it fractures, it scatters, it hides in the spaces between words and waits for someone patient enough to gather the shards.

Edmund Lockridge was not a patient man. He was a careful one, which is worse for liars, and better for the truth, because careful men eventually ask the right question at the wrong moment, and then there is nothing left to do but answer.

He asked it on the third night in Vaelmoor, beside a fire that burned without heat, in a square where the fountain still played water that fell upward into a sky the color of drowned amber.

"Ada," he said. "Why me? Not the bloodline. Not *a* descendant of the builder. Why did you wait thirty years outside my gate? You could have gone to Sera. You could have gone to anyone with Vaelmoor in their veins." He turned his hands toward the fire, palms open, the way he did when he was trying to hold something steady. "You waited for *me*."

The firelight moved through me the way light moves through smoke, and I felt every year of my fading like a coat worn too long in the rain.

I could have lied. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger keeps accounts of wolves and women: I knew the lie. It sat on my tongue like a smooth stone. I could have said, *Because you are strong. Because you are kind. Because the gate answers to you.* All of those things were true. None of them was the truth.

"You already know," I said instead. "Don't you."

"I want to hear you say it."

* * *

Here is what I am, so that you understand what I stood to lose.

When the bargain was struck — when Edmund's ancestor spoke the words that folded Vaelmoor out of the world like a letter tucked into an envelope — everything the kingdom had been had to go somewhere. The land went into the fold. The people went into the fold. And the remembering — every song, every name, every grief and festival and small kindness done in the shadow of the dragon's light — all of that poured into a vessel that had been standing empty, waiting, because the bargain demanded a witness even as it forbade one.

I am the witness. I am Vaelmoor's memory given fur and breath and a voice borrowed from a girl who died before the sealing, whose name I carry the way a river carries the shape of its banks.

And here is the thing about being made of memory: memories fade. Every year the seal held, I thinned. Every decade, another street of Vaelmoor went dark inside me, another face smoothed over like writing on wet paper. The flickering people we had passed in the ruined squares — the candle-flame citizens who guttered when you looked at them too hard — they were my own substance wearing shapes. When they vanished, I vanished. I had been dying by inches for three hundred years, and no one had ever noticed, because noticing requires a rememberer, and I was the only one left to do the remembering.

Then the stars went out. Then the wards shattered. And I felt, for the first time in centuries, something other than erosion.

I felt *possibility*.

If Vaelmoor were restored — truly restored, unsealed, poured back into the world with its mountains and its harbors and its ten thousand souls — then memory would no longer need a vessel. It would have a country again. And I — I would no longer be a candle burning down in a locked room. I would be a woman. Real. Permanent. Flesh that scarred and healed, hair that grayed, a heartbeat that belonged to me alone. I would be able to forget things, Edmund. Do you understand what a gift that is? To be able to *forget*. Only living things can afford to forget. Memory-creatures must carry everything or become nothing.

That was my bargain. Not Vaelmoor's salvation. Mine.

* * *

"Say it," he said again, and his voice had gone quiet in the way that quiet frightens more than shouting, because quiet means the wound has already opened and the shouting is just the bleeding after.

I made myself look at him. His face was half firelight, half the amber dark of the dead kingdom, and he looked — this is the cruelty of it — he looked exactly like the man in my oldest memory, the builder, the one who had knelt in the snow three hundred years ago and wept while he sealed us away, because he loved us too much to let the star-eater have us and too little to die with us. Love and betrayal wearing the same face, generation after generation, until the face walked up to my gate and offered me his hand across the snow.

"I sought you," I said, "because restoring Vaelmoor would make me real."

The upward-falling water of the fountain made no sound. Nothing in Vaelmoor made sound unless I remembered the sound for it, and in that moment I forgot how silence should feel, and so there was nothing at all.

"Not to save the kingdom," I went on, because now that the stone had left my tongue I could not stop the words, they came like floodwater through a broken weir. "Not for Sera, not for the Ember Keepers, not for the flickering thousands. For me. I found the fragments of dragon-fire and I fed them to you and watched your eyes change because I needed a key, Edmund, and you were the key, and I told myself the wanting of my own life was a small thing next to the saving of a nation, but it wasn't small. It was the whole engine. Everything else was cargo."

"When?" His voice cracked on the word. "When did you decide? Before the gate? Before the wolf-in-the-snow performance?" A bitter edge came into his voice, and I flinched from it the way flesh flinches, which surprised me — I hadn't known I had enough substance left to flinch. "Was any of it real? The places I knew. The mother singing. Was that you showing me truth, or was that you *baiting* me?"

"The memories were true—"

"That's not what I asked."

The fire between us guttered. Around us, at the edges of the square, the flickering people had gathered — drawn, perhaps, by the first argument their kingdom had hosted in three centuries. A woman in a fisherman's oilskin. Two children holding hands, translucent as sea-glass. An old man whose face I knew, whose name I had carried for three hundred years and could suddenly not find. They watched us with the terrible patience of the almost-gone.

"The mother singing was true," I said. "Your blood remembers her. I only... uncovered what was already buried. But yes. I chose you before I ever saw you. I chose the bloodline, and then I chose the man, and then I waited thirty years in the snow outside your gate learning your habits, your loneliness, the way you talked to yourself on patrol because thirty years is a long time to be the only soul on a mountain." My voice dropped. "I learned how to be needed by you. That was not bait. That was strategy wearing bait's clothing, and I know the difference matters to you, and I am sorry, and I would do it again, and both of those things are true at once, and I don't know how to hold them both and neither do you."

He stood. Slowly, the way old men stand, though he was not old — thirty years of gatekeeping had put an old man's caution in his joints.

"Sera wants the truth restored no matter the cost," he said. "The wardens want merciful forgetting. And you—" He laughed, and there was no humor in it, only the sound a man makes when a floor gives way beneath certainties. "You want to *exist*. And I'm the door you picked the lock of." He looked down at me, and his eyes were wet, and that was the worst part, that he was grieving rather than furious, because fury I could have answered. "Did you ever once — even once, Ada — think of me as anything other than the mechanism?"

"Yes," I said.

"When?"

"The night the stars went out. When you found me in the snow and your first thought — I heard it, I was in your head before you knew I existed — your first thought was not *what is this thing*, it was *the poor creature has no tracks, she'll freeze*." I bared my teeth, which for a wolf is grief. "You pitied me before you feared me. No one has ever pitied me. Memories are not pitied; they are used or abandoned. So yes, Edmund. Once. At least once."

He closed his eyes. When he opened them, the wetness was gone, packed down behind thirty years of practice at keeping weather off important things.

"That's not enough," he said.

"I know."

"It should be. Gods help me, it should be enough — one honest moment in a lifetime of arrangement." He stepped back from the fire. "But I gave my life to a gate because I believed I was guarding something. I left my post — my post, Ada, thirty years, the only thing I ever chose — because you told me I was born for this, that my blood meant something. And it did mean something. It meant I was *built*. A key doesn't get to wonder whether the lock loves it back."

"Edmund—"

"Don't." He raised a hand, and the gesture was so exactly his ancestor's — I had seen it, in the oldest memory, the builder raising that same hand against a weeping crowd — that for a moment I could not breathe, if breathing is something I do. "Don't follow me. Don't call my name in that voice. Let me be, for one night, a man walking in a country that isn't there, and not a key, and not a plan, and not yours."

* * *

He walked into the flickering wilderness alone, and I let him.

Understand: I could have followed. I am a wolf; his tracks were plain in the ash-snow; his scent was written across the dead streets like ink. I could have circled ahead and been waiting at the pass with my tail low and my ears back, the picture of contrition, and by morning he might have forgiven me, because forgiveness is what careful men do with wounds they cannot afford to examine.

But he had asked me for one true thing, and I had given it, and the least I owed him now was the second true thing:

I stayed.

I stayed because following him would have been strategy again, and I was so tired of being strategy. I stayed because the flickering people drifted back to their squares and their upward fountains, and one of the sea-glass children looked at me and said, in a voice like wind through reeds, *"Are you going to save us, lady-wolf?"* and I could not answer, because the honest answer was *no, child, I was going to save myself, and I may have broken the only man who can save any of us in order to try.*

I sat in the empty square until the false dawn came up the color of old bruises, and I did what I had never dared do in three hundred years.

I stopped remembering.

For one hour, deliberately, I let Vaelmoor's streets go dark in me. The harbor. The bell tower. My own borrowed name. I let them slide away like silt in slow water, and I felt myself thin, and I did not fight it, because I wanted to know — I needed to know with the certainty of experiment — whether underneath the memory there was anything at all. Whether there was a *me* separate from the kingdom's remembering, some small stubborn kernel that was mine and not Vaelmoor's.

There was.

It was very small. It was shaped like a man walking away through ash-snow with his shoulders hunched against a cold that wasn't falling anymore. It was the memory of pity — *the poor creature has no tracks* — and it was the only coal in me that did not belong to a dead country.

So that was the answer, then. That was what I was, under everything. Not Vaelmoor's memory. A wolf who remembered being pitied once, and had built an entire treachery around the hope of earning it honestly.

I laughed. It came out as a sound halfway between a bark and a sob, and it echoed off buildings that were no longer there, because I had forgotten them an hour ago and not yet remembered them back.

And that was when I heard the horns.

Not Vaelmoor's horns — Vaelmoor's horns I remembered, and these were not them. These were thin, cold, deliberate sounds from beyond the kingdom's edge, from the passes of the living world, and I knew them because Edmund had described them on our long road: the wardens' hunting-call, the signal they blew when the quarry was sighted and the killing was sanctioned.

Three notes. A pause. Three notes again.

Coming from the direction Edmund had walked.

I was moving before the echo died, four legs hammering the ash, forgetting and remembering the roads simultaneously, the city flickering around me like a bad lamp — and far ahead, down in the white pass where the dead kingdom bled into the living snow, I saw torches. Many torches. And among them, small and upright and alone, a figure I knew better than I knew my own fading name.

They had found him. The hollow-eyed ones. The ones sent to silence the gatekeeper.

And I had spent the night learning what I was made of, while the men who wanted to unmake him closed like a hand around everything I had left.
