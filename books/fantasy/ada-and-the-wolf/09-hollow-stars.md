## 9. Hollow Stars

The wilderness of Vaelmoor did not forgive solitude.

Edmund had walked away from Ada with her confession still ringing in his ears — *I sought you because restoring it would make me real* — and he had told himself the hurt would burn off like morning fog once he put enough miles between them. It hadn't. Three days of walking through the flickering country, and the hurt had only settled deeper, the way a stone settles into mud. Every ruined street he passed reminded him that she had been made of these streets, made of everything the world had thrown away, and she had wanted — of course she had wanted — to be more than a memory wearing a wolf's shape.

He couldn't even blame her for it. That was the cruelest part.

So he walked alone through a land that flickered at the edges of his vision, past houses whose windows held candlelight that guttered when he looked too long, past a fountain where a woman drew water that fell upward, past a market square where the stalls were stacked with fruit that dissolved into grey ash when the wind touched it. The people — if they were people — nodded to him as he passed, and their faces were like faces seen through deep water, and he understood that they were not quite there, and that he, a solid thing walking through their twilight, was a kind of blasphemy.

The stars, when he looked up at night, were wrong.

He had noticed it the first evening and told himself he was imagining it. But by the third night there was no denying it: the sky above Vaelmoor was not empty the way a night sky is empty. It was empty the way a mouth is empty after the teeth have been pulled. There were sockets where stars should have been — dark places that had a shape, a hunger, a *history* — and when he stared at them too long, he felt something lean back against the inside of his own skull and stare at him.

He was thinking about that — about the way the dark places seemed to have weight — when the wardens took him.

They came out of a street that had not been there a moment before. He would swear to that afterward: one heartbeat he was walking through rubble, and the next there was a cobbled lane on either side of him and men in grey standing in it, six of them, seven, their faces the color of old tallow, their eyes hollowed out like the sky above him. They did not draw weapons. They simply closed, the way cold closes, and the leader — a tall man with a captain's broken insignia still pinned to his coat — said, in a voice like a door opening in an empty house:

"Keeper. You'll come with us."

Edmund's hand went to his knife out of thirty years of habit, and the nearest warden caught his wrist without looking at it, and the grip was not strong so much as it was *final*, like the grip of a closing book.

"You've been walking my country for three days," the captain said. "Leaving footprints. Leaving warmth. Do you know what notices warmth, in a place like this?"

"I'm not your keeper," Edmund said. "I left the gate. I abandoned my post."

"You are the gate," the captain said, and something in the way he said it made Edmund's chest go cold. "That's the whole tragedy of you, Lockridge. Come. It isn't safe to stand still, and it's worse to talk in the open. There's a place below the old customs house. There's a fire there that the dark doesn't like."

* * *

The old customs house was a shell, its roof open to the starless sky, its walls furred with a frost that was not cold. Below it, down a stair that had been carved by hands and then half-erased by forgetting, was a cellar lit by a brazier of coals that burned without smoke. Dragon-fire. Edmund knew it now on sight — a deep gold with a green heart, the color of the fragment Ada had once held before his eyes, the color of the memory of his mother singing in a language that had been erased from every living tongue.

There were perhaps thirty wardens in the cellar. Not the hollow-eyed things that had taken him — those had been outriders, he learned, men who had given up pieces of themselves to stay awake and moving in a country that ate sleep. The ones below were older, wearier, wrapped in coats patched with sigils Edmund half-recognized from the gate-wards. They looked at him the way drowning men look at driftwood: with hope, and with resentment that there wasn't more of it.

The captain — his name, he learned, was Vess — sat him down by the brazier and poured him something hot that tasted of rosemary and iron.

"You've met the wolf," Vess said.

Edmund said nothing.

"You don't have to confirm it. We felt her pass through the wards the night they broke. The whole country felt it — like a bell cracking." Vess turned his hands toward the coals. They were scarred hands, burned in old patterns, deliberate patterns. "We've been fighting a war out here for thirty years, Keeper. You've been standing at a door on the far side of it, oiling the hinges, and no one told you there was a war, because telling you would have meant telling the world."

"Tell me now," Edmund said.

Vess looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked up, at the ceiling, at the sky beyond it, at the hollow places in the dark.

"You saw the stars go out," he said. "From your side of the gate. All of them, at once, the night the wards fell."

"Every soul on the borderlands saw it. The whole sky went dark like a snuffed candle."

"Yes. And everyone on your side has spent the time since telling themselves a comfortable story about it. An eclipse. A weather. A sign." Vess's mouth twisted. "Here is the truth, Keeper, and I need you to hold still while I say it, because men have run from this cellar rather than carry it."

Edmund held still.

"The stars didn't go out," Vess said. "They were *eaten*."

The words went into Edmund like a splinter of ice. "Eaten."

"There is a thing — we call it the star-eater, because we are tired men and we name things plainly — that was born the night your ancestor made the bargain. Every kingdom that is erased, every name that is unwritten, every memory that is taken out of the world — it has to go *somewhere*, Keeper. The bargain didn't destroy anything. Nothing is ever destroyed. It was all poured into one place, and that place grew, and it learned, and it got a hunger the way a wound gets an itch." Vess's voice was flat, reciting, the voice of a man who has said this too many times to people who did not want to hear it. "For three hundred years it fed on scraps. Forgotten songs. Dead languages. The names of the drowned. And then, on the night your wards broke, it got bold. It reached up, and it ate the stars above Vaelmoor — every one — because stars are the oldest memories the sky has. Light that has been traveling since before there was anyone to see it. It ate them the way you'd eat the wax off a candle."

Edmund thought of the hollow places in the sky. The sockets. The way they had seemed to lean against the inside of his skull.

"And now," Vess said, "it is coming down."

"Coming down for what?"

"For the last dragon-fire." Vess nodded at the brazier, and at the thirty weary faces around it, and Edmund saw in them what he had refused to see in the hollow-eyed outriders: not evil. *Exhaustion.* "There is one ember left, Keeper. One coal out of all the fire that once lit this country. It sleeps in the Dragon's Throat, far south of here, and it is the only thing in existence the star-eater cannot digest — because dragon-fire is not memory, it is *meaning*, and meaning is the one thing it cannot swallow. As long as that ember burns, Vaelmoor's people cannot be fully erased, and the star-eater stays half-starved. When it goes out—" Vess spread his scarred hands. "Everything your ancestor hid, everything we've guarded, every soul in this twilight — swallowed whole. And then it will be strong enough to come for your side of the gate. For the borderlands. For every lit window in the world."

The cellar was very quiet. The coals breathed their gold-green light, and thirty faces turned toward Edmund, and he understood with a lurch that they were not looking at a prisoner.

They were looking at a key.

"You said the wards broke," Edmund said slowly. "The night the stars went out. Why did they break, Vess? Thirty years I kept that gate. The wards were sound. I'd have known."

"They broke," Vess said, "because the star-eater got strong enough to crack them. And they broke because—" he hesitated, and Edmund saw the first flicker of something other than exhaustion cross the man's face, something almost like pity. "Because a gate is only as strong as its keeper believes in it, Keeper. And something spent thirty years teaching you that you were only a servant of the wards, when all along you were the wards. Your blood. Your choice. The bargain was made by a Lockridge, and it can only be unmade or remade by one. The star-eater knew that. It's been pulling at you your whole life — every doubt, every lonely night at that gate, every whisper that your life was small. That was its breath on the back of your neck. It was softening the lock."

Edmund's hands had gone cold around the cup. *Every doubt. Every lonely night.* Thirty years of them, and suddenly they were not his own; they were weather made by a thing that ate light.

"The wolf," he said. "Ada. Is she part of it?"

Vess's face did something complicated. "She is the country's memory given flesh. The star-eater is what happens to what's forgotten when it's *abandoned*. She's what happens when it's *kept*. They're made of the same substance, Keeper — and they are not on the same side, and I genuinely do not know which of them is more dangerous to you."

* * *

They did not chain him. That was the thing that undid him, in the end, more than the capture itself. They gave him a blanket and a corner of the cellar near the coals, and the wardens slept in shifts, and Edmund lay awake listening to them breathe, and he could not make them into monsters.

He had spent three days walking through a dying country telling himself a story in which the wardens were the enemy — grey hunters sent to silence the gatekeeper, faceless and pitiless. And here they were: a one-eyed woman named Corrin who mended the others' coats and hummed a lullaby in the erased language, the same melody, Edmund realized with a jolt, that his mother had sung. A boy of maybe sixteen named Pell who had been born in the twilight and had never once seen a star, and who asked Edmund, in a whisper, whether the stars on the other side were really as bright as the old songs said. An old man who had been a warden for forty years and who had forgotten his own name and answered to whatever the others called him, and who was called, gently, *friend*.

*They're desperate,* Edmund thought, lying in the dark. *Not evil. Desperate.* They had been fighting a war no one knew about, on behalf of a country no one remembered, against a hunger no one had named, and their reward was to be hollowed out piece by piece, and still they kept the coals burning.

And Ada had known. She had known all of it — she was made of this country, she must have known about the star-eater, about the war, about what was coming for the last ember — and she had told him about the wards, about his blood, about the bargain. But not this. Not the thing in the sky with its mouth full of stars.

Or maybe she simply hadn't thought it mattered, next to the truth of what she wanted from him.

He turned that over in the dark, and it cut him both ways, and he got no closer to forgiveness than he had been on the road.

* * *

Near dawn — if the grey half-light that passed for dawn in Vaelmoor could be called that — Edmund was woken by silence.

Not quiet. *Silence.* The kind of silence that has eaten sound. The coals in the brazier had shrunk to a sullen red, leaning away from the stairwell as if from a wind, and the wardens nearest the stairs were on their feet, and their faces were the faces of men listening to something below the floor of the world.

Vess was beside him without Edmund hearing him come. "It's found us," he said quietly. "It's been following you since the road, Keeper. You're a lit candle in a dark house. Everything you do, it feels."

Above them — through the broken roof, through the hollow sockets in the sky — something pressed down. Edmund felt it the way he had felt the wards shatter: not in his ears or his skin but in his chest, in the place where the gate lived in him. A cold that was not temperature. A *pull*. His memories began to slide, gently, the way furniture slides across the deck of a listing ship — his mother's face, the smell of the gate-house, the sound of Ada's voice saying his name — each one tugged at, tested, *tasted*.

He grabbed for them. He held his mother's face the way you hold a door shut against a gale, and he felt the thing on the other side of it pause, and consider, and — withdraw. For now.

When he could breathe again, the cellar was in motion. Wardens were packing coals into iron lanterns, wrapping the brazier's heart in layered cloth, moving with the grim economy of people who had drilled for this. Vess pressed a lantern into Edmund's hands — the green-gold light of it steady as a heartbeat.

"You felt that," Vess said. It wasn't a question. "You held your memories against it. Thirty years at a gate, and one night of practice, and you held it." He gripped Edmund's shoulder, and his scarred hand was shaking. "The star-eater will reach the Dragon's Throat in six days. We cannot outrun it and we cannot fight it. But you — you're the key to the gate, and the gate is the only lock it ever feared. Whatever the wolf wants from you, whatever the Keepers want — none of it will matter if that thing swallows the last ember. Do you understand me? Everything converges at the Throat, Keeper. Everyone. The wolf will be there. Sera and her Ember Keepers will be there. And it will be there."

"And what do you want me to do when we all arrive?"

Vess looked at him, and for the first time the captain's hollowed-out composure cracked, and Edmund saw the man underneath: tired, terrified, and still standing.

"I don't know," Vess said. "I've been fighting this thing for thirty years and I don't know. But you're the first key it's ever had to chase. So I'm going to take you south, and you're going to walk into the Throat, and you're going to *decide* — because that's what you were born for, and it's the one thing none of us can do for you."

Above them, in the sockets where stars had been, the dark leaned closer.

And far to the south, Edmund felt — faint, flickering, unmistakable — the pull of the last ember, calling to the gate in his blood like a hearth calling home.

Six days.

Behind them, in the north, something was moving through the twilight with a purpose, small and grey and stubborn, and it did not stop walking when the roads forgot where they went.

It had his name in its mouth, and it was not letting go.
