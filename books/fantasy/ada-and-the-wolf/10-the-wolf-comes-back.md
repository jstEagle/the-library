## 10. The Wolf Comes Back

I felt him taken the way a hearth feels its fire carried out of the room.

One moment Edmund's thread ran bright through me — that stubborn, banked-coal warmth I had followed across three hundred miles of forgetting — and the next it was dragged sideways, down into the dark beneath the ash-roads, and frayed to a whisper.

I was lying in the ruins of the waystation where we had sheltered at dawn, my body folded wrong around itself like a book dropped spine-up. The wardens had not even bothered with me. Why would they? I was already half-gone. They had looked at the wolf-shaped thing breathing shallowly in the corner and seen nothing worth a rope.

They took him anyway. That was their mistake. Not the taking — the *not-taking*.

I tried to stand and learned what it means when a body is mostly memory. My foreleg went through the floorboards as if the boards had forgotten how to be solid under something so insubstantial. Outside, Vaelmoor's twilight pressed against the broken windows, that endless dusk we had walked through for days, the sky the color of a bruise deciding whether to heal. Somewhere beyond the hills, the hollow stars hung in their eaten constellations, and somewhere closer than that, men with lanterns and no light behind their eyes were carrying Edmund Quintana toward whatever hole they crawled out of.

*Get up,* I told myself. *You are the memory of a kingdom. You have carried cathedrals. You have carried harvests. Carry yourself.*

My legs answered — barely. I got them under me on the third try, and the world tilted, and I saw myself from outside for one sickening instant: a gray wolf gone translucent at the edges, my ribs showing through my own fur like fence rails through fog. When had that happened? I could not remember. That was the trouble. Lately there were whole hours I could not remember, whole seasons of myself sanded away, and I only knew they were missing by the smooth places where they should have been.

I put my nose to the ground and found his scent trail, faint but true — woodsmoke and iron and the particular cold-clean smell of a man who has kept a mountain gate for thirty years — and I followed it out into the dying country.

* * *

The wardens moved fast along the smugglers' road, faster than men should move, because they were not entirely men anymore. I had known that since the pass, since the first of them turned those lampless eyes on us and Edmund whispered, *they don't blink.* The bargain that erased Vaelmoor had needed hands in the living world, hands willing to forget why they served, and the forgetting had eaten them from the inside out until what remained was obedience wearing a uniform.

There were six of them now. They had Edmund bound at the wrists with cord braided through with iron filings — I could smell the iron burning him from fifty yards — and they moved him along the old toll-road toward the Hollow Ford, where the river remembered being deep and drowned travelers still walked its bed at night.

I kept to the ridgeline above them, and I did a thing I had not done since before the sealing: I opened myself.

Understand what I am. I am not a wolf who became a spirit or a spirit who became a wolf. I am what Vaelmoor made of everything it could not bear to lose. Every song sung twice so it would stick, every face studied by a dying mother so her child would keep it, every recipe and grievance and lullaby and lie — the kingdom poured all of it into me in its last hour, the way a man drowning will press his wedding ring into your palm. I am a country's memory wearing fur. And memories, unlike flesh, can be spent.

There is a fire inside me. It is not dragon-fire, though it is cousin to it; it is the heat of ten thousand minds refusing to let go. For three hundred years I have hoarded it, fed it, guarded it the way a miser guards coins, because I knew — I have always known, in the way you know your own name — that someday I would need every ember of it to make myself real. To step out of the twilight and into the world as something permanent. Something alive. Something that would not flicker at the edges when people stopped looking at her.

That was the whole architecture of my wanting. I had built myself around it like a cathedral around its relic.

And now I stood on a ridge above six hollow men and one bound gatekeeper, doing arithmetic I did not want to do.

To fight them, I would have to burn memory. Not a little — a great gout of it, a furnace-blast, enough to give this fading body weight and teeth and terror for the space of a battle. And whatever I burned would be gone. Not sleeping. Gone. A song I would never sing again. A face I would never see again. A reason —

I flinched away from the thought, and the flinch told me everything.

Because here is the truth I had been circling for days without landing on: I could no longer fully remember *why* I wanted to be real. The wanting was still there, huge and load-bearing, but the rooms behind it were going dark one by one. I knew I wanted permanence the way you know a house has a foundation — by the shape of what stands on it — but the original hunger, the night it was born, the voice that first cried *let me last* — smoothed over. Sanded away. Spent, maybe, in some moment I couldn't name, or simply rotted out of me by the long twilight.

So what was I protecting, exactly? A cathedral whose relic had vanished? An argument whose premise I'd lost?

I looked down at Edmund, stumbling between his captors, and watched him — even bound, even bleeding at the wrists — turn his head to check on the ridge where he'd left me. He couldn't see me. But he checked anyway. Thirty years alone at a gate, and the first friend he'd ever had vanishes wounded into the dark, and his first instinct is to look back up the hill.

The arithmetic finished itself without my permission.

*I don't remember why I wanted to live forever,* I thought. *But I know exactly why he matters.*

That was the whole ledger. That was everything on either side of the scale. And it was enough. God help me, it was enough.

* * *

I came off the ridge at the Hollow Ford, where the road dips between two shoulders of black stone and the river runs shallow and wrong, full of reflections that don't match the sky.

The first warden heard me too late. I hit him at the base of the skull with all the weight I had bought myself, and I paid for that weight in the same instant — a memory tearing loose like a root pulled from wet ground. My mother's kitchen. No — not my mother; Vaelmoor had no mothers, only the kingdom itself, but I had been given the *memory* of a kitchen, bread and steam and a woman humming, someone's borrowed childhood stored in me for safekeeping. It went into the blow. I felt it leave. The kitchen went white, then warm, then nothing, and the warden went down, and I was already moving.

They were fast. They were terribly fast. The second one had a blade of cold iron out before I landed, and it caught me across the shoulder, and where it cut, I didn't bleed — I *unraveled*. Little threads of gray lifted off the wound like smoke, like pages losing their ink. I felt a village festival go with it: lanterns on the water, someone's first kiss under paper light. Gone. I had been carrying that kiss for three centuries for someone who no longer existed, and now no one ever would.

It should have made me hesitate. Instead it made me furious in a way I had never been furious, because I understood suddenly what these men were: not wardens. *Spenders.* They unmade things. They were the bargain's collection agents, walking erasures, and every cut of theirs was a small version of what had been done to an entire kingdom.

*No,* I thought, closing my jaws on the second man's arm and shaking until the iron fell from his fingers. *Not again. Not one more thing.*

The third and fourth came together, and I stopped being clever.

I opened the furnace doors all the way.

What poured out of me, I cannot fully describe, because describing requires remembering and I was spending remembering like a drunkard spends wages. I gave them the archive of a thousand years of Vaelmoor's griefs — every funeral lament, every widow's vigil, every curse the kingdom had ever leveled at the bargain that ate it — and I wrapped all of it around those four men like a storm made of other people's sorrow. Hollow eyes are hollow because they have room in them. I filled the room. I filled it with the drowned king's last speech and the choir-school children's names, all forty of them, recited in order, and the taste of the honey-bread sold at the autumn fair, and the exact blue of a door on Copperlane that a girl had loved and never told anyone about.

The wardens screamed. Men with no light behind their eyes, screaming, because I had poured light into the hollow places and light, in a vessel shaped like forgetting, burns.

Two of them fell and did not get up. Two more broke and ran, and I let them run — let them carry a little of Vaelmoor back into the dark, a splinter of the kingdom lodged in their emptied heads, growing if it could.

The cost arrived all at once, the way a wave arrives after the ship has already shipped water.

I staggered. The ford tilted. And the memories kept going — the furnace, once open, would not close cleanly, and I felt whole *districts* of myself guttering. The harbor district. The library, all of it, every book. The name of the kingdom's last queen — I reached for it and found a smooth place, a polished absence, and I made a sound that was half snarl and half sob because I had just lost her *name*, I had held that name longer than anyone alive, and now it was smoke over black water.

By the time I reached Edmund, I was swaying on my feet, and I no longer knew how many years I had burned. Decades. Centuries. I had been old when the stars went out and I was ancient now, and thinner than thin, a wolf drawn in charcoal by someone losing patience with the drawing.

"Ada." His voice cracked on it. His wrists were raw where he'd worked at the cords — of course he'd been working at the cords, stubborn gatekeeper, thirty years of patience spent in an hour of quiet fury. "God's teeth, you're — you're *see-through*. What did you—"

"Hold still." My voice came out in tatters. I set my teeth to the braided cord and chewed, and the iron filings seared my tongue, and I did not care. "Don't talk. Don't thank me. If you thank me I'll remember this moment forever and I can't afford any more forevers."

He laughed — actually laughed, wet and shocked — and then the cord parted and his hands were free and he was pulling my head against his chest, which I had told him not to do, which I would remember forever anyway, damn him, because some things burn themselves into you whether you've budgeted for them or not.

"You came back," he said into my fur. "After what I said. After I walked out on you—"

"You didn't walk out on me. You walked out on a lie I told you. There's a difference." I pulled back and looked at him, and I saw myself reflected in his eyes — a ghost of a ghost, edges smoking. "Edmund. Listen to me, because I may not be able to say this twice."

The ford was quiet. The dead wardens were already dissolving, their uniforms emptying like shed skins. Far off, the eaten stars wheeled in their wrong constellations, and beneath us the drowned river murmured with voices of people walking its bed, patient as sediment.

"I built my whole self around becoming real," I said. "Around lasting. Around stepping into the world and staying. And I'm telling you—" my voice failed, steadied, failed again, "—I'm telling you I can't find the reason anymore. I've lost it. It's one of the smooth places now. I wanted it so badly and I cannot tell you *why*, and that should terrify me, and instead—"

Instead I pressed my forehead against his hand, because words had run out and the body knows older languages.

"—instead all I can find is you. You're the only thing in me that isn't smooth. You're the only want left with a shape to it. So whatever happens at the Throat of the Dragon, whatever I have left to spend — it spends on you. On them. On everyone your blood locked away. Not on me. I'm done bargaining for myself."

He knelt in the shallows of the Hollow Ford and took my ruined face in both his scarred hands, gatekeeper's hands, key-shaped hands, and he said the thing that I would carry into whatever was left of me:

"Then we'll remember you instead. Whatever you burn — you give it to us, and we hold it. That's the trade. That's the only trade I'll accept."

And I thought: *oh.* Oh, that was the answer, wasn't it. That had always been the answer. Memory doesn't survive by being hoarded. It survives by being *given*.

I was still learning how to believe it when I lifted my head, ears forward, and caught it on the wind coming down from the high passes — a smell with no source, a cold with no weather, vast and patient and hungry as an unpaid debt.

The star-eater had noticed the fire I'd spent. And it was coming to collect.

"Get up," I said. "Run. We have to reach the Dragon's Throat before it reaches us — and Edmund?"

He was already rising, already turning toward the mountains, and I loved him for how fast he moved.

"When we get there," I said, "remind me what we're fighting for. In case I've forgotten by then."

He looked back at me — at the smoke lifting off my shoulders, at the smooth places spreading — and his jaw set like stone laid by his ancestors.

"Then I'll remember for both of us," he said. "Every mile. Come on."

And we ran, wolf and keeper, up out of the Hollow Ford, into the country that was forgetting us even as we fled through it.
