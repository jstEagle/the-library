## 11. Roads Through Ash

Ada weighed nothing.

That was the first thing I understood as I lifted her from the wardens' trampled snow and settled her against my back — that a creature made of memory should have been heavy beyond bearing, dense with thirty years of my stolen past and three hundred years of a kingdom's grief. Instead she was light as a bundle of dry reeds, and when she breathed against my neck her breath came in shallow sips, like someone rationing what little of herself remained.

"Edmund." Her voice had gone thin, the young woman's timbre frayed down to something older and more worn. "The roads. They'll be going."

"I know."

"You don't. Not yet." Her chin hooked over my shoulder, and I felt her looking back the way we'd come, at the pass where the hollow-eyed wardens lay scattered and groaning, at the dark sky with its one stubborn absence where a star would soon need to burn. "Vaelmoor is forgetting us already. You'll see it before I do. Your eyes still work like eyes."

I adjusted her weight — such as it was — and started north.

* * *

For thirty years I had kept the Thornfell gate, and for thirty years I had told myself the smugglers' roads were mine by right of patrol: every goat track, every collapsed mine shaft, every fire-road the charcoal burners had cut and abandoned two centuries ago. I knew which ledges held frost until noon and which streams ran under ice you could trust. I had walked them alone so long they felt less like paths than like habits, grooves worn into me rather than into the mountain.

By midmorning of the first day, the mountain began taking those habits back.

It started small. A cairn I had passed ten thousand times — a stack of stones older than my grandfather, marking the fork above Cold Larch Hollow — was simply not there. I stopped, certain I'd miscounted my steps, and looked at the bare slope where it should have stood. No scattered rocks. No pale scar of disturbed earth. The ground was smooth as an unmade bed, as if no one had ever stacked stone there at all, as if stacking stones were an idea that had never occurred to anyone.

"Ada," I said.

"I know." Her arms tightened weakly around my shoulders. "Keep walking. Don't look for it. If you look for what's missing, it takes the missing thing's place in your head, and then it can take *that* too."

"What do you mean?"

"The land isn't erasing itself, Edmund. It's erasing our *knowledge* of itself. The cairn is gone because the road is gone, and the road is gone because somewhere behind us, the part of Vaelmoor that remembered this path just... let go." She coughed, a sound like ash settling. "A country that was never allowed to exist can't hold its own shape forever. It holds shape through being remembered. And everything that remembers it is either dead, fading, or carrying a wolf on their back toward a caldera."

"So we walk faster."

"We walk *smarter*." Her muzzle turned, and I caught the gleam of one amber eye, dimmed now to the color of old honey. "You said you were a smuggler once, before the gate took you. Before Sera's people found you walking out of the mountains with no history and gave you one."

"I wasn't a smuggler. I was a courier. There's a difference."

"There is. Smugglers move goods. Couriers move *certainty* — letters, debts, promises. Which means you know how to travel through country where the destination keeps changing its mind." She pressed her forehead briefly against my jaw. "So teach me, courier. How does a man find a road that no longer exists?"

I thought about it while the pines thinned and the slope steepened, and the honest answer was: he doesn't. He finds what the road used to lean against.

* * *

That was the trick of it, and I will set it down here plainly, because it saved our lives perhaps six times in three days, and because no one else alive knows it now but me.

Roads are not lines. Roads are agreements between a traveler and the terrain — and terrain forgets slower than maps do. A road may vanish, but the cut it left in the hillside stays a cut. The bridge may rot away, but the abutments remember the weight of it. Water always finds the lowest argument, and men building roads always argue with water. So you stop following roads and start following their bones: the drainage cuts, the retaining walls, the places where the slope has been bullied into gentleness.

And when even the bones were gone — and they went quickly, the higher we climbed, the world smoothing itself out behind us like a tide erasing footprints — there was one trick left, the oldest one, the one the first couriers learned before there were couriers.

You follow the places that hurt.

Every hard passage leaves a scar in the people who crossed it. The Widow's Step, where my father's cousin fell. The Kettle, where the wind screams through a notch all winter. I could not have drawn you a map of these places; I had never needed to. But when I closed my eyes and let my body remember fear instead of geography, the fears arranged themselves in order, each one pointing at the next, and I walked from terror to terror across the blank face of the mountain like a man crossing a river on stepping-stones.

Ada understood immediately, of course. She was made of remembering.

"That's why it chose you," she murmured on the second night, as I built a fire in the lee of a rockfall that I knew only because I had once been nearly killed beneath it. "Not your blood. Not the key in your veins. Any Vaelmoor-born could open the lock. But you're a keeper, Edmund. Keeping is what you *are*. You keep things the world wants to drop."

I fed the fire and didn't answer, because the truth in it sat too close to the other truth, the one I was trying not to look at: that keeping was also what I had been *made* for, designed for, bred toward across generations I was only beginning to count. My whole life a lock waiting for its moment. I carried that knowledge now the way I carried Ada — lightly, because the alternative was to be crushed.

She slept — or did whatever wolves made of memory do instead of sleeping — and I watched the sky.

There were no stars. There hadn't been since the night the wards broke, and I had stopped expecting them, the way you stop expecting a dead friend to write. But that night, for the first time, I noticed something worse than absence. The darkness overhead was not empty. It was *attentive*. It hung over the peaks like a held breath, and when I stared up into it too long, I felt a pressure behind my eyes, a slow soft tugging, as if something vast and patient were reading me the way a customs officer reads a face — checking my papers, weighing what I carried, deciding whether I was worth the trouble of swallowing.

I looked away. I banked the fire. In the morning, the rockfall sheltering our camp was smooth stone, unbroken, and I could not remember being afraid under it, and I had to take Ada's word — and my own aching shoulder — that I ever had been.

* * *

On the third day the road ate itself in front of us.

We had come down into a high valley the Ember Keepers called the Long Stair — a switchback trail descending toward the caldera country, carved, Ada said, by mule trains hauling dragon-coal when her kingdom still had a name. I could feel it under my boots: the graded terraces, the stone edging, the work of a hundred forgotten hands. And then, ahead of us, at the third switchback down, the valley simply *gave up*.

There is no better way to say it. The terraces below us softened, blurred, ran together like wet ink. The stone edging sank into grass that had never been planted. Where a mule train had labored for two hundred years, there was suddenly just a slope — a pretty, innocent, unremarkable slope, the kind of slope that has never known a road or wanted one. And I felt the knowledge go out of me as I watched. I had known the Long Stair. Now I knew only that I had known it, and then I knew only that something about this valley had once mattered, and then even that thinned to a rumor.

Beside me, Ada made a sound I had never heard from her — a low, cracked whine, the sound of a wolf watching its den collapse.

"It's faster," she whispered. "It's getting faster. The star-eater isn't waiting anymore. It's *feeding*, Edmund. Every road, every name, every grave — it's eating the last of Vaelmoor ahead of us, clearing its plate before the main meal." Her legs buckled. I caught her before she hit the ground, and she leaned into me, shaking. "If it eats the Throat before we reach it—"

"Then we reach it first."

"How? Look at it!" She flung her head at the erased valley. "There's no way down! There's no way down because there was never a way down, because soon there will have been never any—"

"Ada." I crouched so we were eye to eye, wolf-gold and human-tired. "Thirty years I kept a gate for a country that wasn't on any map, guarding a door that opened onto nothing, for people who couldn't remember why they paid me. Do you know how I did it?"

She was silent.

"Habit," I said. "Stubbornness. Getting up every morning and doing the thing anyway, especially when it seemed pointless. Especially *then*." I stood, and slung her across my back again, and looked down at the slope that had never been a road. "The stair is gone. Fine. The stair was only ever the easy way. Smugglers don't use the easy way — the easy way is where the tolls are. We go where the land forgot to look."

* * *

We went down the scree.

I want to be honest about what that cost, because I have earned the right to be honest about exactly one thing in my life, and it is this descent. Scree is stone pretending to be liquid. You do not walk it; you negotiate with it, step by lying step, trusting each slab of rock for precisely as long as it suits the rock to be trusted. A mule train would never have taken it. A sane man wouldn't either. But sane men were not racing an appetite the size of the sky, and so for six hours I surfed a gray river of broken slate with a fading spirit on my back, letting gravity do half the work and prayer do the rest, and twice the slope shifted under me and carried us both forty feet in a grinding rush before it thought better of it and let us stop.

Somewhere in the middle of it, Ada laughed.

It startled me so badly I nearly sat down permanently. It was a thin laugh, ragged, more breath than voice, but it was unmistakably laughter.

"What," I gasped, catching a ledge, "is funny."

"You are. You're *smuggling yourself*." Another crackle of that ruined laugh. "Down a mountain that's deleting itself, carrying contraband memories, evading the biggest thief in creation. Edmund Lockridge, the last courier of Vaelmoor, and the cargo is a wolf." She pressed her cheek between my shoulder blades. "My kingdom had knights. It had sorcerers, admirals, a queen who wrestled a river spirit and lost. And the one who finally carries its hope home is a gatekeeper on a scree slide, swearing under his breath in three languages."

"Two languages. I never swore well in Vaelmoori."

"You will. I'll teach you when—" She stopped. I felt the sentence die against my back like a moth against glass.

"When I'm real," she finished, very quietly. "When I'm real."

I didn't say anything. There was a time — two days, three days ago, a lifetime — when I'd have asked her what she meant, and she'd have had to tell me about the bargain, the selfishness buried in her devotion, and it would have torn at us both. But she had burned that confession out of herself along with the years of stored memory she'd spent saving me from the wardens. Whatever she had wanted once, she no longer remembered wanting it. She knew only that I mattered. Some cruel, merciful part of me wondered if that was a cleaner love than the original — and some harder part answered that it was bought at too high a price, and that I intended, somehow, to pay the price back.

So I just said, "Hold on," and kept sliding.

* * *

Near dusk, the land tried to take Ada.

I didn't understand it at first. We had reached the valley floor — black volcanic sand, flat as a frozen sea, the caldera wall rising ahead of us like the rampart of a drowned fortress — and I set her down to rest while I scouted the approach. When I came back ten minutes later, she was flickering.

Not glowing. Flickering. Like a candle in a draft, her outline stuttered — wolf, girl, wolf, a tall woman with river-dark hair, wolf again — and the ground beneath her was smooth and unmarked, though she had lain there long enough to leave a print. I had seen the flickering thousands in Vaelmoor's twilight streets, ghosts wearing down toward nothing. I had told myself Ada was different. Made of stronger stuff. Made, in fact, of the same stuff as the land, which should have made her *safer* here, not less.

But the land was spending itself now. Burning its furniture to keep the lights on. And Ada was made of the same wood.

"Get up," I said, and hated how my voice shook. "Get up, we're close, you have to—"

"I know." She staggered upright, and for a moment she was the girl — the shape she wore when she spoke, the shape I had begun, without admitting it, to think of as hers. She steadied herself against my arm, and her hand was cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature. "It's pulling at me, Edmund. The forgetting. It knows what I am. I'm not a traveler here. I'm *pantry*."

"Then we don't stop again."

"No." She looked up at me, and her eyes were still amber, still hers, and in them was something I had seen once before — in the mirror, the morning after the stars went out, before I knew what I was looking at. Dread and awe, sharing one face. "No stopping. But Edmund — listen. When we get to the Throat. If it comes down to the ember or me—"

"Don't."

"—you choose the ember."

"*Don't.*"

"You have to promise me, because I can't remember why I want to be real anymore, and that means I can't be trusted to choose rightly for myself, and *you* can." Her grip tightened on my arm, and for a heartbeat her fingers were strong, fierce, alive. "Promise me, courier. Deliver me to the end of the road, but don't deliver the world to nothing for my sake. That's the whole job. That's the *only* job."

I looked at her — this impossible creature who remembered everything and was now losing the thread of her own wanting — and I made the mistake of telling the truth.

"No," I said.

"Edmund—"

"I've spent my whole life keeping other people's bargains. My ancestor's bargain. the wardens' bargain. The world's bargain. Every one of them priced in somebody else's blood, and everyone always said *it's the only way*, and everyone was always believed." I lifted her onto my back, gentler than I felt, and turned toward the caldera wall, where a seam of faint red light pulsed in the rock like a heartbeat under skin. "So I'm done making promises in advance. I decide at the door. That's the last smuggler's trick I know, Ada: never commit your cargo until you see the buyer's face."

Ahead of us, the Dragon's Throat breathed its ember-light into the starless dark, and high above the caldera rim, the attentive darkness gathered itself and began, at last, to descend.
