## 13. Loyalty Is a Kind of Lie

The inn's fire burned without heat.

Edmund had noticed it the night before and told himself he was too tired to trust his own skin. Now, sitting on a bench by the hearth with a bowl of something that tasted like barley and like nothing at all, he held his hands toward the flames and felt only the memory of warmth — a suggestion of it, the way you remembered your mother's kitchen years after the house was gone. The firelight moved across the floorboards. It touched everything in the room except him.

Ada slept in the corner booth, if a creature like Ada could be said to sleep. She lay with her muzzle on her forepaws, her gray fur gone patchy at the shoulders where she had burned herself hollow pulling him out of the wardens' camp. When she breathed, the candle nearest her guttered in time with it, as though the room could not quite decide whether she was there at all. Half of her was spent. He had watched her spend it. He did not know yet what to do with that knowledge, so he carried it the way he carried everything else — silently, in the chest, next to the shattered wards.

Across the common room, Sera sat sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening.

She had been at it since dawn, or what passed for dawn in a country that existed only while someone remembered it. The whetstone whispered against steel in long, patient strokes, and every few moments her eyes came up — not to Edmund, not exactly, but through him, to some arithmetic she was running behind them. She was old the way driftwood is old: bleached, hardened, shaped entirely by what had been done to her. The Ember Keepers' coals rode in an iron lantern at her hip, and she kept one hand resting on it even now, even here, the way a miser rests a hand on a purse.

"You didn't sleep," she said, without looking up.

"I don't think this place lets you."

"It lets you forget, if you want." The stone whispered. "That's its whole trade. The innkeeper offered, didn't he? Last night, when we came in half-dead. Offered you a warm bed and a clean mind and a road home with no Vaelmoor in it." Her voice was not accusing. That was somehow worse. It was the voice of someone reading a ledger they had already balanced. "I saw your face when he said it. You wanted it."

Edmund set down the bowl. "I wanted to stop being afraid. Those aren't the same thing."

"Aren't they?" Now she looked up, and her eyes were the pale gray of ice over deep water. "Thirty years you stood at that gate keeping my country buried, Lockridge. Were you afraid the whole time? Or were you comfortable?"

The question went into him like the knife would have, and cut cleaner.

He thought about answering honestly, because honesty was all he had left that no one had designed for him. His name — chosen by ancestors who needed a keeper bred to the post. His memories of the mountains above Thornfell — not his own, but Vaelmoor's, seeded into his blood like a letter waiting thirty years to be opened. Even his loneliness, he suspected, had been engineered: a man with nothing else to love makes a reliable gatekeeper. He had walked out of his own life three days ago and discovered that the life had been a lock, and he had been the key, and neither had ever been asked what they wanted.

"I was comfortable," he said. "And then the stars went out, and comfort turned out to be a kind of lie I'd been telling myself with my eyes open. So no, Sera. I'm not going to pretend the offer wasn't sweet. But I've stopped confusing sweet with right."

"Have you?" She set down the whetstone at last, and the silence it left was enormous. "Then answer me this, keeper. My people are flickering. Did Ada tell you that? Did she show you? Out past the chapel there are thousands of us left — thousands — and every year more of us go out like candles. Not dead. Never dead. Just *unremembered*, thinned until there isn't enough left to hold a shape. A child I taught to sing, forty years ago in a country that officially never existed — last winter I reached for her and found smoke. And you sit there weighing options." She rose, and the lantern swung at her hip, and the coals inside threw light like small angry eyes. "There is one option. Open the seal. Let the truth come back. Let the world choke on it if it has to. Every year you deliberate, more of us become smoke. So weigh *that*, Lockridge. Weigh my child made of smoke against your borderlands' peaceful mornings."

"The world drowned in returned memory," Edmund said quietly. "You know what Ada showed me. Every soul in the living lands suddenly remembering a country that was stolen from them — grief like a flood, borders dissolving, wars—"

"Grief is honest!"

"So is mercy," said a voice from the doorway.

Edmund turned. The warden captain stood in the inn's entrance, and the room went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the heatless fire.

He should have been dead. Edmund had seen him die — or something like death — back in the hollow stars, before the star-eater had spoken through his mouth like a puppeteer testing strings. But here he was, solid enough, gray-cloaked, frost in his beard, his hollow eyes fixed not on Edmund but on Sera with something that looked almost like pity. Behind him, faintly, through the door, the night sky hung wrong — too dark, too hungry, the absence where stars should be pressing against the inn's threshold like a tide against glass.

"You," Sera said, and her hand closed on the lantern.

"Me." The captain did not step inside. Perhaps he couldn't. "Put the coals down, grandmother. I'm not here for them tonight. I'm here because the boy looks like a man who wants to think clearly, and you're making that impossible."

"He's not a boy. He's the Builder's blood."

"He's a man who learned three days ago that his whole life was a sentence someone else wrote." The captain's voice was low and even, and terribly tired. "I know how that feels. I've been dead for six years, Keeper. Did you know that? The order doesn't tell its wardens that the ones who fall at the gate get up again and keep walking. We walk because the thing that ate the stars walks us. And I am asking you — while I still have enough of myself left to ask — let him choose forgetting. It's kinder. For everyone. For your people most of all, because what you're asking for won't save them. It will feed it."

"*It* will—"

"The star-eater was born the day your kingdom was sealed." The captain's hollow eyes did not blink. "Every erased memory, every unmapped mile, every name struck from every record — it ate them. It has been eating Vaelmoor for three hundred years, one flickering soul at a time, and it grows, and it leans against the seal, and someday soon the seal breaks not because anyone opens it but because there will be nothing left inside worth containing. Then it comes for the living lands with a hoard of ghosts behind it. Unless—" and here his voice cracked, just slightly, on the word, "—unless the debt stays paid. Unless the last embers go into the dark willingly, and Vaelmoor finishes dying, and the hunger sleeps another age. Mercy, grandmother. Merciful forgetting. It's the only door that doesn't end in ruin. Ask your keeper. Ask him what the thing said through my mouth."

Edmund had already told them. In the wardens' camp, bound, he had listened to the star-eater explain itself with the patience of a creditor: *the bargain created me; the erasure feeds me; open the door and pay the debt, or I take the hoard.* He had repeated it to Sera and the Keepers around a fire that gave no heat, and he had watched Sera refuse to hear it, and he had understood then that she was not refusing the words. She was refusing the arithmetic. Because the arithmetic ended with her people becoming smoke forever, and she had promised them otherwise, and a promise is a thing a person will burn the world to keep.

"Sera," he said now. "Sit down."

She didn't. "You're listening to a corpse."

"I'm listening to everyone. That's the job nobody trained me for." He stood, and his knees ached, and he was suddenly, enormously aware of how tired he was — not body-tired, which sleep fixes, but the other kind, the kind that comes from carrying too many truths that cannot all stand up at once. "Sera wants the truth restored, whatever it costs. The living lands drown; maybe wars; maybe worse. But her people live, really live, for the first time in three hundred years. The captain wants merciful forgetting. The borderlands stay safe; the hunger sleeps; and Vaelmoor dies the rest of the way, quietly, forever, and Sera's singing child stays smoke. And Ada—" 

His voice caught on the name.

In the corner booth, Ada stirred. One ear lifted. She did not wake — or she pretended not to, which might be the same thing in a creature made of memory — and Edmund looked at her patchy fur and her slow candle-guttering breath and finished the sentence anyway, because she deserved to have it finished.

"And Ada wants to exist. Really exist. Permanent. Alive. And restoring Vaelmoor whole would make her real — and unmake everything else. And if we take the captain's mercy instead, she gutters out with the rest of them, and the only creature in creation that ever bothered to remember me properly goes into the dark." He laughed, and it came out wrong, thin and cracked. "Do you see it? Do you both see it? There is no side here. There is no clean choice. If I'm loyal to Sera, I betray every farmer between here and Thornfell. If I'm loyal to the wardens, I betray Sera's people — and Ada. And if I'm loyal to Ada—" he stopped, because saying it aloud made it real, "—if I'm loyal to Ada, I have to want the thing that destroys everyone else. Loyalty is a kind of lie. Every one of you is asking me to swear an oath that ends with someone bleeding, and every one of you is certain the blood is justified, and I am so *tired* of certainty."

The room was silent. The heatless fire moved. Somewhere upstairs, the ghostly innkeeper was sweeping a floor that existed only because Edmund remembered it, and the sound of the broom was the loneliest sound Edmund had ever heard.

Sera spoke first, and her voice had lost its blade. "Then don't choose a side, keeper. Choose a door."

"What?"

"You said it yourself — every oath ends with someone bleeding. So stop looking for the oath that doesn't. There isn't one. Three hundred years ago my ancestor's friend sealed my country to save the world, and it was the right thing, and it damned us, and both of those are true at once. That's what a choice *is*, Lockridge. It isn't picking the path with no cost. It's picking which cost you can carry, and then carrying it, and then letting the people you failed hate you for it." She picked up the lantern. The coals flared at her hip like small defiant hearts. "So carry something. Anything. But carry it on purpose."

The warden captain shifted in the doorway, and frost crackled under his boot. "Or," he said softly, "you put the embers in the dark, and no one has to carry anything, and everyone sleeps."

"No one sleeps," Edmund said. "Not after this. Not anymore."

He looked at the captain. He looked at Sera. He looked, last and longest, at the wolf in the corner, whose ear was still raised, who had heard every word and given him the grace of pretending otherwise.

Three doors. Truth, and mercy, and existence. Each one bought with someone's blood. And underneath them all, half-buried like a root under a road, a thought that had been growing in him since the hollow stars, since the star-eater had spoken of debts and doors:

*Open the door and pay the debt.*

The star-eater had said it like a threat. But threats and instructions wear the same clothes sometimes, and Edmund had spent thirty years learning the difference between a gate that keeps things out and a gate that lets things through — and a gate, any gate, was built to be opened from one side by the one who held the key.

What if the debt didn't mean giving the embers to the dark?

What if it meant opening the door — but only partway?

He didn't say it. Not yet. It was half-formed, dangerous, the kind of idea that dies in daylight if you speak it too soon. Instead he crossed the room, knelt by the booth, and laid his hand on Ada's shoulder, over the patch where her fur had burned away to show skin beneath — ordinary skin, mortal-looking, impossibly fragile.

Her eye opened. Gray as morning. Tired as his.

"You heard all that," he murmured.

*Every word,* she said, in the voice that was a young woman's and a wolf's and something older than both. *You did well, gatekeeper. You told the truth about loyalty. Most men never manage it.*

"It didn't feel like doing well. It felt like standing in a river with the banks washing away."

*That's what truth feels like from the inside.* Her breath gusted warm against his wrist — real warmth, the first true warmth the inn had offered him. *But you left something out, Edmund. Something you figured out and didn't say. I felt it in you just now, the way I feel weather coming.*

He met her eye. Outside, beyond the door, the starless dark pressed closer against the inn's walls, and somewhere far off in the caldera country, the last ember waited beside the bones of the dragon that had once lit a whole kingdom.

"There might be a third door," he said, very quietly, so that neither Sera nor the dead captain could hear. "One nobody's offering. And I need you well enough to help me find it."

Ada's tail moved once against the floorboards — a small, stubborn rhythm, like a heart deciding to keep beating.

*Then finish your barley, keeper,* she said. *We leave for the Dragon's Throat at first remembering.*
