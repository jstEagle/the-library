## 14. The Throat of the Dragon

I had forgotten what cold was.

Not the cold of snow, which is only water remembering another shape. Not even the cold of the wardens' hands, which was the cold of men who had been emptied and refilled with someone else's purpose. This was older. This was the cold that had lived at the bottom of Vaelmoor since before Vaelmoor had a name — the cold that waits inside every story for the moment someone stops telling it.

It began at the rim of the caldera, and it pressed against my mind like a thumb against a candle flame.

"Ada." Edmund's voice came from behind me, hoarse from three days of ash-wind and half-made roads. "Talk to me. What do you feel?"

*What do I feel.* I almost laughed. I was memory wearing the shape of a wolf; feeling was the only thing I had left in abundance. But he could not see what I saw, not yet, so I turned on the black glass of the ridge and let him read my face instead of my thoughts.

"It's here," I said. "It's been here a long time."

The caldera opened below us like the mouth of something that had once sung. That was the truth no map would ever carry: the Dragon's Throat was not a wound in the mountain. It was a throat. The dragon whose bones we had come to find had not died here — it had *settled*, the way an old singer settles into a chair, and the mountain had grown up around its shoulders the ivy grows around a door. When it finally went out, the land collapsed inward around the absence, and what remained was this: a bowl of obsidian two miles wide, ringed with cliffs like teeth, breathing slow plumes of grey light from somewhere far below.

And beside that light, when the plumes parted, I could see it. A coal no larger than a sleeping cat, burning the color of dawn before dawn — the last ember of dragon-fire in all the world, or all the world that remembered itself.

Edmund crouched beside me on the ridge, and I watched him look down into it. Thirty years he had kept a gate without knowing why. Now his face did the thing it always did when the truth arrived too large to hold all at once: it went very still, the way a lake goes still before ice.

"That's it," he said. Not a question. He had carried the ember-keeper's coals across half a country that kept trying to forget them; he knew dragon-fire the way you know a voice you heard in the cradle.

"That's it," I said. "And it knows we're here."

* * *

We went down the way the smugglers' maps said to go down, which is to say the way that should not have worked and did because Edmund refused to be surprised by anything anymore. A stair cut into the cliff face, each step carved with a word in Old Vaelmoori — *remember, remember, remember* — worn nearly smooth by centuries of feet that had believed the words mattered. Edmund read them aloud as we descended, haltingly, his accent terrible, and every word he spoke made the stair a little more solid under us.

That was the arithmetic of this place now. Attention was mortar. Forgetting was gravity.

I felt both pulling at me. I had burned so much of myself breaking Edmund out of the wardens' camp — years of stored memory poured out like blood — and what remained of me came loose at the edges in ways I tried to hide from him. Names went first. I reached for the name of the river where I'd first shown him his mother's song and found a smooth blank place where it should have been. Then small histories: the taste of the bread Sera's people baked over ember-coals, the exact blue of the door of the house where I had waited for Edmund to be born. Each loss was quiet. That was the cruelty of it. Nothing tore. Things simply were not there anymore, like rooms in a house you can no longer afford to heat.

But the ember — the ember I remembered. Whatever else the star-eater's cold took from me, it could not seem to touch that. Perhaps because the fire was the one thing in Vaelmoor that had never agreed to be forgotten.

Halfway down, the cold stopped being weather and started being company.

It came as a pressure behind the eyes first, then as a slowness in the tongue. I heard Edmund falter mid-word on the stair — he had been reciting the names of his patrol routes, a habit of his when frightened, forty gates and beacon-towers recited like a rosary — and I turned to see him standing with his mouth open, reaching for a name that had just been taken out of him.

"The third beacon," he whispered. "On the Thornfell road. It had a name. It had—"

"Harlow's Rest," I said quickly, pressing my muzzle against his hand. "Named for the warden who lit it first. You told me on the second night. Harlow's Rest."

He blinked, and it came back to him — I felt it return, the way warmth returns to a hand held near a stove — and he looked at me with an expression I will keep as long as I keep anything at all. Not gratitude. Worse than gratitude. Recognition. He understood then, fully, what was waiting for us at the bottom of the stair, and he understood that I was thinner than I had let him believe, and that every name I gave back to him cost me a name of my own.

"Don't," he said.

"Don't what?"

"You know what. Don't spend yourself on my mnemonics. We're nearly there."

"We are nowhere near there," I said, "and I will spend myself exactly as I choose. That is the whole point of being alive, Edmund. Even this much alive."

He didn't answer. He put his hand flat on my head, between the ears, the way you steady yourself against a friend in high wind, and we kept descending.

* * *

The floor of the caldera was a field of black glass, and walking on it was like walking on the surface of a thought that had frozen mid-formation. Beneath our feet, deep down, shapes moved — vast slow shapes, the shadows of things the caldera still half-remembered: processions, wings, the sweep of a tail longer than a river. The glass showed them for a heartbeat and then smoothed over, embarrassed, the way water does after a stone.

The skeleton rose out of that field at the center, and I am a creature made entirely of remembering, and even I was not ready for it.

It was not bones the way cattle are bones. It was architecture. Ribs arched overhead in curves of yellowed ivory tall as cathedral naves, and between them hung, still, after all these centuries, threads of something like silk and something like light — the last scaffolding of the glow that had once filled them, the light the old songs called *the kingdom's morning*. The skull lay half-buried at the eastern edge of the ribcage, jaws parted, and I swear to you — I know what I saw — the great eye sockets were not empty. They held a darkness that was watching us with the patience of the dead, which is the greatest patience there is.

Beside the skull, in a hollow of fused glass that might once have been the cup of a forepaw, the ember burned.

Up close it was smaller than it had seemed from the rim — hardly bigger than my two paws together — and it made no crackle, gave off no heat I could feel through my fur. It simply *was*, with the absolute self-possession of a thing that has outlasted everything meant to kill it. The light it cast did not fall on the bones so much as agree with them, filling the ribs from within until the whole skeleton glowed faintly, a lantern built in the shape of everything it had ever illuminated.

Edmund knelt at the edge of the hollow. He did not reach for the ember. He was a careful man; thirty years at a gate teaches you that some fires must be asked, not taken.

"We've come to carry you," he said softly, in that terrible accent of his, in Old Vaelmoori. "Or to wake you. Whichever you'll allow."

The ember brightened. Just slightly. Like a sleeper turning toward a familiar voice.

And that was when the cold came down on us like a lid.

* * *

I want to tell you what the star-eater's presence was like, and I find I must describe it sideways, the way you describe a hole by describing the edges of it.

It did not roar. It did not approach. There was no moment when it arrived, because it had never left — it had been soaked into this place for centuries, the way salt soaks into a shore, fed drop by drop on everything the bargain erased. Every name struck from every ledger. Every face unpainted from every portrait. Every song unsung. All of it had gone somewhere, and the somewhere was here, and here was now pressing inward on the last warm thing in the world the way night presses on a window.

It unmakes thought. That is the truest thing I can say about it. Not the body — the body it leaves alone, because the body is not where the treasure is. It comes for the *meaning* of things. Standing in that caldera, I watched Edmund try to remember his father's face and fail, and then try to remember that he had failed, and fail again, his brow creasing over a smooth white nothing, panic rising in him like water in a locked room. I watched him grip the ground with both hands and say his own name aloud — *Edmund, Edmund Lockridge, keeper of Thornfell* — and I watched the name go thin in his mouth, syllables coming apart like wet paper.

So I did the only thing left to me. I lay down against him, wolf-shaped and shaking, and I poured what remained of me along the places where the cold was drinking him.

I gave him the gate at Thornfell in winter, frost on the iron hinges. I gave him the smell of the wax he used on the ward-stones. I gave him Ada — not my name but the fact of me, the weight of a wolf leaning against his side, the specific warmth, the specific stubbornness — and I gave it to him *loud*, shouted into the interior of his mind the way you shout into wind. And where I held those memories against the dark, the dark could not get its teeth in. It slid off. Meaning, defended, holds.

But oh, the price. With every memory I lent him I felt the corresponding room in my own house go dark. I no longer know the color of the sky over Vaelmoor on the day the bargain was sealed. I no longer know whether I ever had a name before Ada. I am becoming a shape with fewer and fewer things inside it, and I continued anyway, because there was never any question of continuing. Love does not consult the ledger.

"Ada." Edmund's voice, when it came back to him, was ragged. His hands were buried in the fur of my neck, gripping hard enough to hurt, and I was glad of the hurt; pain is a memory the body keeps for free. "It's not attacking the ember."

I lifted my head. He was right.

The cold pressed everywhere — on us, on the bones, on the light — but around the ember itself it bent aside, curving away in slow invisible rivers, the way a stream bends around a stone. The star-eater, which had eaten every star in the sky above this country, which had drunk kingdoms dry, would not touch the last ember directly.

"Why?" Edmund breathed.

And I understood, in the way that understanding sometimes arrives all at once, like a door blowing open — because I was made of this kingdom's memory, and some knowledge lives deeper in me than my own fading.

"Because it can't," I said. "Dragon-fire isn't light, Edmund. It's *witness*. Everything the fire has ever shone on stays real. That's what the bargain feared. That's what the seal was built to bury. If it takes the ember, everything the ember remembers comes out of it — all at once, unhidden, into the world. It wouldn't be stealing a coal. It would be forcing the door open from the inside."

"Then it's waiting for us to take it," Edmund said slowly. "Waiting for us to open the way, so it can follow the memory out."

"No," I said, and my heart — the borrowed, flickering, mortal heart I had not possessed a month ago — went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the star-eater. "Worse. Look at the ribs."

He looked. And in the glow of the ember, in the spaces between the great arching bones, the darkness had begun to gather — not drifting, not random, but *collecting*, drawing itself together into a shape with edges, a shape with patience ending, a shape that was learning, from the cold of this place and the hunger of centuries, how to have a body.

Above us, at the rim of the caldera, small and distant against the starless sky, lights appeared. Lanterns. Many lanterns, moving in formation, descending the stair.

The wardens had found us.

And they were not coming for the ember.

They were coming to help it die.
