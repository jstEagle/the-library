## 15. What the Star-Eater Wants

The cold came before the voice.

Edmund had known cold all his life — the Thornfell winters that froze the iron of the gate, the breath-fog of thirty years of patrols, the deep chill of mountain stone in January. He knew cold the way a fisherman knows water. But this was different. This cold did not touch the skin first. It touched the *thought*, sliding beneath memory like water beneath ice, and where it passed, things went smooth and blank and gone.

He was kneeling in the caldera's throat with Ada half-conscious against his chest, her fur thin as frost on a windowpane, and he felt the cold arrive the way one feels a change in pressure before a storm — a stillness, a wrongness, an attention.

The dead warden captain stood at the mouth of the tunnel behind them.

Edmund had left him in the pass below Hollow Stars, two days and a lifetime ago. He remembered the man falling — remembered it clearly, because Edmund had not been the one to strike him, and the guilt of witnessing death without causing it had its own peculiar weight. The captain had been hollow-eyed even then, even alive, a man worn down to duty and nothing else. Now he was worse than dead. Now he was *occupied*.

The captain's body did not move like a body. It moved like something remembering, belatedly and badly, how bodies worked — a pause between each step, a tilt of the head held too long, eyes that reflected no light from the ember-glow far below. Frost bloomed across his lips as he walked, and where his boots touched stone, the stone went grey. Not darkened. *Greyed*, drained of color the way old photographs drain, the way memories drain when you try too hard to hold them.

"Edmund Lockridge," the captain said, and the voice was the captain's voice, but underneath it, threaded through it like wire through wax, was something vast and patient and terribly, terribly hungry. "Keeper. Key. Son of the seal."

Ada stirred against him. Her growl was barely a sound at all now, more vibration than voice, a candle-flame of defiance in a body running out of wax. "Don't listen," she breathed. "It lies. It only knows how to lie, because lying is just forgetting done on purpose—"

"I do not lie." The star-eater tilted the captain's head, considering her with something almost like tenderness. Almost like grief. "I am the most honest thing in creation, little memory. I am everything your bargain threw away, given appetite."

* * *

Edmund got his feet under him slowly, keeping Ada cradled against his ribs. His legs were shaking. He told himself it was the climb, the exhaustion, the altitude of the Dragon's Throat — anything but the truth, which was that every instinct he owned, every year of gatekeeper's discipline, was screaming at him to run, and there was nowhere in any world to run from this.

"You know my name," he said. His voice came out steadier than he deserved.

"I know all the names that were fed to me." The captain's frost-lipped mouth curved. "That is what I am, keeper. I am the ledger. When your ancestor struck his bargain — when he sealed Vaelmoor out of the world — the bargain required payment. Every map unmade. Every song unsung. Every name scraped from every tongue. And erased things do not simply vanish, whatever the wise would like to believe. Erased things must go *somewhere*."

The cold pressed closer. Edmund's thoughts began to snag, small losses he could feel: the name of the village where he'd bought his boots, gone. The taste of his mother's stew, gone. He gripped harder to what remained — Ada's weight, the ember's distant warmth, Sera's face twisted in accusation — anchors, all of them, driven into the ice of himself.

"So they went into you," he said.

"They went into *me*." Something moved in the captain's throat, a swallow of pride or hunger. "I was born in the moment of the sealing. I am the debt itself, given shape. A kingdom's worth of forgetting, coiled around a single wound in the world, growing fat on every scrap your ancestors' wards shaved away. For three hundred years I have eaten the crumbs of Vaelmoor. And I am still hungry, because a kingdom is a great deal to digest, and your family made certain I would never be allowed to finish the meal."

"The wards." Edmund's stomach turned over. "They weren't holding Vaelmoor out."

"No, keeper. They were holding *me* in. Or trying to." The star-eater laughed, and the laugh came out of the captain's dead mouth as a fall of frost crystals that shattered on the stone. "Your thirty years of faithful service, Edmund Lockridge. Your father's forty. His mother's fifty. Generations standing watch over a prison for a thing you never knew existed, while the prisoners you thought you were guarding flickered in their twilight streets, waiting for a door that would never come. You were not the jailer. You were the *lid*."

The words went into him like a blade finding the gap between ribs. Thirty years. Thirty years of patrols and ward-checks and the small proud litany he'd recited to himself on cold nights: *the border holds because I hold.* He had built his entire self on that sentence, and now the sentence was ash, and beneath it was the truth: he had been a cork in a bottle, and the bottle had been fermenting a monster.

Beside his heart, Ada whispered: "Now you understand why I couldn't tell you all of it at once. Some truths are doors too heavy to open alone."

* * *

"Why come to me?" Edmund asked. "Why speak? You could have let the cold take us on the road. You took the stars. Why talk to the man whose family built your cage?"

"Because the cage is broken, and the last meal is in reach, and yet—" the captain's head cocked, that awful avian tilt, "—there is a complication. There is always a complication, and the complication is always *choice*. It is written into the oldest bargains, keeper. Even the one that made me. I cannot simply take the ember. I cannot simply devour the last souls of Vaelmoor as they huddle in their dying country. The bargain that birthed me bound me as surely as it bound them. I may eat what is *given*. I may consume what is *abandoned*. But what is freely offered, freely chosen, freely carried across a threshold by a keeper's will—" the frost on the captain's lips cracked as the mouth widened, "—that is beyond my reach forever. So I need you to choose wrongly."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I wait. I am very good at waiting. I have eaten three hundred years of patience along with everything else." The captain spread his arms, and Edmund saw that the frost had reached the man's heart now, a white bloom spreading across his chest like lichen across a gravestone. "Here is what waits, keeper, so that you may weigh it honestly, as I said I do not lie. If you restore Vaelmoor whole — if you rekindle the dragon-fire and raise the drowned kingdom back into the world — the returned memory will flood the living lands like a dam breaking. Millions of minds, suddenly carrying three centuries of stolen past. Kingdoms will convulse. Borders drawn over Vaelmoor's grave will dissolve into war. The living world will drown in a truth it was sealed away to survive. That is Sera's hope, and it is a beautiful one, and it feeds me a kingdom's worth of chaos besides."

The cold crept nearer. Edmund's knees wanted to buckle. He thought of the borderlands — the farmsteads, the ferry towns, the children on the smugglers' roads — and felt the weight of every one of them settle onto his shoulders like snow.

"If you keep the seal," the star-eater continued, "if you mend the wards and walk back to your gate and stand your post until you die of old age as every Lockridge before you — then I remain. Fed forever on the slow drip of a fading country. The Ember Keepers will dwindle. The flickering thousands will gutter out one by one, and each soul that gutters comes to me, and I grow, and one day — a century, ten centuries — I will be large enough that no ward, no key, no bloodline can hold me, and I will step out into a world that has forgotten how to fight me. That is the wardens' hope. Merciful, patient, doomed. They think they are protecting the world. They are fattening its devourer."

"And the third way," Edmund said quietly. "You haven't mentioned the third way."

"Ah." For a moment — just a moment — something flickered through the captain's borrowed face that was not hunger. It looked almost like *weariness*, ancient and bottomless, the exhaustion of a thing that had never once been allowed to rest. "The third way. One door. One star. The people out — carrying their memories with them, paid for, accounted for, the debt honored rather than denied. Then the seal ends not in breaking but in *keeping of promise*. And I—" the captain's jaw tightened, frost spraying, "—I am unmade. Because I am made entirely of unpaid debt, keeper. Pay the debt, and there is no more me. No more hunger. No more cold. Do you understand what I am telling you?"

Edmund did. The horror of it opened under him like a crevasse.

"You want me to fail," he breathed. "All three ways but the third. You've spent three hundred years making sure the choice never comes right — corrupting the wardens, starving the Keepers, snuffing the stars — because as long as the debt stays unpaid, *you keep existing*. The monster isn't the price of the seal. The monster IS the seal. My family didn't just bury a kingdom. We built you."

"I am the interest," the star-eater said softly, "compounding on a loan your ancestor took out against the world. And now the note has come due, and the only one who can pay it stands in front of me holding the last warm thing in a dead country, and I am asking him — begging him, in the only voice I have left — to please, please choose anything else."

The captain's body finally failed. It folded at the knees like a coat slipping off a hook, and the frost rushed out of it in a rising tide of white, filling the tunnel, filling Edmund's vision, and through the whiteness came the voice no longer wearing any throat at all:

"*Choose, keeper. The cold is patient. Are you?*"

* * *

Then it withdrew — snapped back like a tide going out — and Edmund was on his knees in the dark with Ada gasping against his chest and the distant ember painting the caldera walls the color of a heartbeat.

His hands were shaking. His whole life was shaking.

*We built you,* he thought. *Every patrol, every ward, every faithful Lockridge dawn — we were feeding it.*

Ada lifted her head. Her eyes, dim as coals about to go out, found his.

"Now you know," she whispered. "And knowing, Edmund Lockridge — what will you choose?"

Far above, through the broken roof of the sky, where a single stubborn star might soon burn — the darkness waited for his answer.
