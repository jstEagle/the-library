## 17. Rekindling

The cold in the Throat of the Dragon did not bite. That was the horror of it. Cold that bites is a living thing, a thing with appetite and limits, and you can fight it the way you fight anything alive. This cold simply *was* — a stillness so complete it seemed to press against the inside of my skull like a thumb against wet paper, smoothing out thought the way one smooths wrinkles from linen.

I had been made of memory once. I knew what it was to be smoothed away.

Edmund walked ahead of me down the caldera's inner slope, and I watched him the way you watch a candle in a draft — not because you expect it to go out, but because you cannot help measuring how much of it remains. His breath came in white plumes. The blood on his left hand had frozen to a dark crust where he'd cut his palm on the lock-ward at the caldera's rim, and he did not seem to notice or care. Thirty years he had kept the gate. He was a man accustomed to paying with his body and calling it duty.

*You are doing it again,* I told myself. *Measuring him like a candle.*

But that was what I was, in the end. A measure. The kingdom's memory given flesh, and flesh that was running out.

The ember sat at the bottom of the world.

I do not know how else to describe it. The caldera descended in rings of black glass, each ring smoother than the last, as though the mountain had been swallowed and this was its throat's final narrowing. And there, in a basin of fused stone no wider than a hearth, lay the last coal of dragon-fire — no larger than a fist, dull red at its heart, breathing light the way a sleeping child breathes: unevenly, trustingly, as if certain someone would always be watching over it.

Beside it, curved around it like a mother's arm, lay the skeleton.

It was vast beyond sense. Ribs like the arches of a drowned cathedral, a skull longer than Edmund's cottage at Thornfell, the whole of it gone ivory-pale and shot through with veins of gold that still caught the ember's faint pulse. The dragon that had given Vaelmoor its light. The dragon whose fire Sera's people had carried in secret for three hundred years, coal by hidden coal, while the world above forgot them so thoroughly that even their own names had begun to slip.

I had been there when it died.

That memory was one of my oldest, and I held it now the way a starving man holds bread — not to eat, but to remember what food was. Fire filling the sky. The bargain being struck in the smoke. Edmund's ancestor, young then, his face exactly the face that walked beside me now, weeping as he spoke the words that would erase a country. *It is the only way,* he had said. *They will live, but no one will remember they lived.* And the dragon, dying, had looked at him with something that was not hatred — that was the terrible part, it had never been hatred — and said, *Then someone must remember for them.*

And so I had been made. Or made myself. The stories disagree, and I am the only witness, and I have forgotten which version is true.

"Ada." Edmund's voice came out flattened by the cold, stripped of echo. "Talk to me. Your voice is going strange."

"I'm here," I said, and heard what he meant. My voice had been a young woman's voice when we met — bright, quick, a little sharp-edged with old grief. It was thinner now. After the wardens, after burning years of stored memory to tear him free of them, after the roads that unmade themselves beneath our feet — I was thinner. There were holes in me. I had reached for the name of Vaelmoor's western harbor two days ago and found nothing, just a smooth blank place where a city with white towers should have been.

I was forgetting the country I existed to remember.

"The star-eater," Edmund said. He had stopped at the edge of the basin, and I saw his jaw tighten as the cold pressed harder there, close to the ember, close to the thing that hungered for it. "It's close. Can you feel it?"

Yes. I could feel it the way a hearth feels winter through a cracked window. Above us, beyond the caldera's mouth, the sky was wrong — not dark, for night is honest darkness, but *absent*, a hole shaped like everything the stars used to be. The star-eater had eaten the heavens and come for the last light below, and it had followed us here with the patience of a debt.

"It's waiting," I said. "It wants us to try. If we fail, the ember falls to it undefended. If we don't try—"

"—it wins either way." Edmund crouched at the basin's rim. In the ember's dim pulse his face looked younger and older at once, the way it had the first night, standing in the snow at Thornfell with no tracks leading to him. "So we don't give it the choice."

I came to stand beside him. Below, the skeleton's golden-veined ribs rose around the ember like cupped hands.

"There's something I should tell you," I said. "Before. While I still remember that I wanted to."

He turned his head. He had learned, in our months together, to read my silences, and now he read my voice, and whatever he found there made him go very still.

"After the inn," I said. "After I told you why I sought you. That restoring Vaelmoor would make me real. Permanent. Alive." The words came slowly; each one cost something, and not only in the way speech costs the dying. "You walked away from me. You were right to. It was a selfish reason, Edmund. Maybe the only reason."

"Ada—"

"Let me finish. I have less time than you do, and I've spent enough of it lying by omission." I knelt at the basin's edge, and the cold pressed up through my knees into my chest, and somewhere in me another small room of memory went dark — I could no longer recall my mother's face, though I had never had a mother, only the idea of one, borrowed from the kingdom's longing. "When I burned myself to save you from the wardens, I spent memories I can't get back. Years of them. Centuries. And do you know what I discovered?"

He said nothing. He was good at that. He knew when silence was the kinder gift.

"I couldn't remember why I wanted to be real." I laughed, and the laugh came out cracked, like ice on a pond in early spring. "All that longing, all those centuries of wanting permanence, wanting to *matter* in a way that lasted — and it's gone. Burned away with everything else. I sat in the ash-roads holding you, keeping you alive, and I searched myself for that hunger, and it wasn't there. Do you understand? The reason I gave you at the inn — the true reason, the selfish one — I can't even feel it anymore."

"What's left?" Edmund asked quietly.

"You," I said. "Only that. Only ever, now, that. I don't remember why I wanted to exist. I only know that you matter, and that I would spend every memory I have, down to my own name, to see you standing in the sun." I looked at the ember, at its slow patient heartbeat of light. "Perhaps that's what being real is. Not lasting. Choosing."

The cold pressed in. Somewhere far above, the absent stars leaned closer.

Edmund knelt beside me. He did not take my hand — his was crusted blood, mine was going translucent at the fingertips, and we had long ago stopped pretending our bodies worked like other people's — but he pressed his forehead to mine, briefly, the way Vaelmoor's people had greeted one another at festival time. I remembered that. I would remember it as long as anything remained of me.

"Together, then," he said.

"Together."

* * *

The working was older than the gate, older than the bargain, older than the words in any living tongue. It had been written in fire, and it could only be paid in fire — and in blood, and in memory, and in the one coin none of us had ever had enough of: willingness.

Edmund went first, because the lock required the builder's blood, and the builder's blood ran in him undiluted after thirty generations of keepers who had married carefully, waited faithfully, kept the line pure for a purpose none of them had understood. He knelt at the basin's southern edge, where the black glass bore an inscription worn nearly smooth — the same inscription that had been carved above the Thornfell gate, that had been carved, I realized with a vertiginous lurch, into the lintel of every door in Vaelmoor before the end. *What is given in love is never taken in vain.* The ancestors had built the whole world's architecture around this moment, around this man, and he opened his cut palm without hesitation and pressed it to the stone.

The mountain woke.

Not violently — that was the astonishing thing. I had expected thunder, expected the caldera to roar like the dragon's last hour. Instead there was a deep, slow warmth, spreading outward from Edmund's hand like dawn moving across a valley, and the golden veins in the skeleton's bones began to glow, one by one, rib by rib, until the whole vast carcass was lit from within like a lantern made of everything it had ever been. The inscription drank his blood. The stone drank his intention. And the lock of his ancestors — three hundred years of patience folded into a single mechanism of bone and glass and oath — turned with a sound like a great door unbarred at last.

"Now," Edmund gasped. His face had gone grey. The lock was taking more than blood; it was taking the years of his keeping, the thirty winters at Thornfell, the weight he had carried so long he'd mistaken it for his spine. "Ada — *now* —"

I stepped down into the basin.

The cold rushed at me, and I let it. That was the trick no one tells you about giving yourself away: you cannot hold back and give at once. You must open every door in yourself at the same instant and trust that what pours out will land somewhere worth landing.

I put my hands into the ember.

Dragon-fire does not burn the way wood burns, or the way flesh burns. It burns the way truth burns — it takes exactly what you are and makes it visible. And so as I poured myself into that small stubborn coal, I felt myself becoming visible, all the way down. Every century of watching. Every name of every flickering soul I had kept alive in the twilight country. The song Edmund's mother sang in a language he shouldn't know — I gave that back to him later, I promised myself, I would find it and give it back. The white towers of the western harbor — I clung to that one, I *refused*, I poured the towers in first so they would be at the heart of the flame where nothing could reach them. The wolf-shape I had worn since the beginning, the snow, the tracks that led nowhere, the night I had stood at a gatekeeper's door and known him before he knew himself.

It hurt. I want to be honest, whoever finds these words, whoever remembers me after: it hurt more than erasure, because erasure is nothing and this was *everything*, given freely, torn out root and branch, and I felt myself guttering like a candle in a doorway with the wind coming home.

But the ember drank, and grew.

It swelled from fist-size to heart-size to the size of a sleeping child curled up, and its color climbed from dull red to gold to a white so complete it cast shadows backward in time — I swear it, I saw the caldera as it had been, I saw the dragon alive and coiling up the inner slope with wings like storm-fronts, I saw Vaelmoor whole, harbors and orchards and bells, all of it reflected in the growing light like a country waking from a three-hundred-year dream.

"Edmund!" My voice was almost gone now. I was almost gone. What remained of me was a girl-shaped outline full of light, pouring and pouring. "The star — call it — it needs a name to climb toward—"

He understood. Of course he understood; he had been born for this the way I had been made for it. He raised his bleeding hand from the lock-stone and reached upward through the caldera's throat toward the hole in the sky, toward the absence where the stars had been, and he spoke the word his ancestor had spoken to seal the kingdom — but reversed, unmade, turned from a lock into a key:

"*Remember.*"

The ember leapt.

It left the basin like a bird leaving a cupped hand — a thread of white-gold fire climbing the caldera's throat, past the glowing ribs, past the ringed black glass, up and up through the wound in the mountain and out into the ruined sky. For one heartbeat the whole world held its breath. The star-eater's cold surged, furious, a tide of unmaking rushing in to swallow the spark—

—and the spark caught.

High above the mountains, in the exact center of the emptiness, a new star ignited. Small. Stubborn. No brighter than a lamp in a far window. But it burned with dragon-fire and keeper's blood and a memory-wolf's entire self, and it burned *defiantly*, and the darkness around it recoiled the way darkness always has, since the first morning, from even the smallest lit thing.

One star. Enough.

I felt it ignite the way you feel your own heartbeat — from inside, from the place where I had been. Because that was the last secret, the one I hadn't told Edmund, the one I had carried down into the basin: the ember needed a wick, and a wick must burn. I had not poured my substance into the fire. I had become the fire's passage, the fuse between the kingdom's hoarded light and the sky's empty socket, and what climbed the mountain's throat and caught and blazed was *me*, the last of me, every memory I had hoarded for three centuries finally doing the one thing memories are for.

I was on my knees in the basin. The light was fading out of me. I could see my hands — or rather, I could see the stone through them, the gold-lit ribs through them, the whole world through them, because there was very little of me left to obstruct anything.

"Ada." Edmund was beside me, catching me before I fell, and his arms closed around something that was mostly light and mostly grief. "No. No, look at it, look what you did — Ada, *stay*—"

"I'm here," I whispered. It was getting hard to shape the words. "I'm — hm. Isn't that strange. I can't remember the song anymore. Your mother's song. I gave it away, I meant to give it back to you—" A flicker, a gutter, and then, desperately, grasping at the last coals of myself: "Edmund. Listen. The door. When the door opens — the survivors — Sera — they'll need—"

"Don't talk. Save it. Save yourself, you've earned—"

"*Listen.*" My voice was smoke now, barely itself. "Someone has to keep the door. From the far side. You know that. You've known it since the inn." I tried to smile. I think I managed it; he was crying, and crying men see smiles generously. "Don't you dare argue with me. I'm the kingdom's memory. Arguing with me is arguing with history."

Above us, the new star pulsed — small, stubborn, enough — and far below, in the ruins of the gate at Thornfell, I felt something answer it. A door, waking. Dragon-fire threading itself through old stone, opening the way I was closing.

The exodus had begun. I could feel them coming — Sera and her Ember Keepers, the flickering thousands, a whole country walking out of oblivion carrying its memories home.

And I was guttering. Triumphant and guttering. The star burned and I went out with it, and both things were true, and both things were good, and I was so tired, and Edmund was holding what remained of me and saying my name over and over as if repetition could anchor a soul to the world—

—as if, I thought, drifting, as if anyone had ever been kept in the world by anything except being remembered.

The star burned on above the mountains.

And in Edmund's arms, the last embers of me sank toward darkness — but not, not quite, not yet — for somewhere in the deep of the dying light, one small spark refused. One spark remembered a promise I no longer could: that wolves come back.

The door was opening. And something of me was still falling toward it.
