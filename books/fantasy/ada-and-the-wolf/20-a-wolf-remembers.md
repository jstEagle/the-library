## 20. A Wolf Remembers

I was born twice.

The first time, I had no body at all — I was the remembering of a kingdom, a shape the drowned land wore when it wanted to weep. The second time happened in fire, in the caldera's heart, when I poured everything I was into a single coal and felt myself come apart like breath on winter glass.

I should have ended there. I had planned to end there. There is a peace in that kind of ending, a rightness — the memory given back, the debt paid, the wolf lying down at last in the snow of her own making.

But embers are stubborn things. So am I.

I woke on the slope below the Dragon's Throat with ash in my mouth and cold in my fingers, and the first thing I understood about my new life was how small it was. My heartbeat — I *had* a heartbeat now, a ridiculous fluttering thing — stumbled like a foal learning its legs. My lungs ached with every breath of thin mountain air. When I tried to lift my hand, it shook.

And my mind — oh, my mind was a house with most of its windows broken. I reached for Vaelmoor and found only edges: the smell of salt off a harbor I could no longer name, the first line of a song whose melody had fled. I reached for myself and found less. Why had I wanted to be real? Why had I crossed the smugglers' roads, fought wardens, burned centuries of stored memory in a single desperate night? The reasons were gone, scooped out of me like the flesh of a fruit.

But one thing remained, glowing at the center of all that emptiness like the last coal in a banked fire.

Edmund.

Not his face — though I could see his face, gray-eyed and stubborn, lined by thirty years of keeping a gate he didn't know he was born to keep. Not his voice, though I could hear it saying *Ada* in that careful way he had, as if speaking my name might break me. What remained was simpler than either. It was the shape his absence left in the world. It was the knowledge, bone-deep and unarguable, that somewhere behind me a door was closing and he was standing on the wrong side of it.

I got up. It took three tries. My legs were mortal legs now, weak and complaining, and the scree slid under my boots — I was wearing boots, someone had dressed this body in rough wool and leather, the ember's last gift or perhaps Sera's kindness — and each step up the mountain cost me something I could not name because I no longer knew what I was spending.

Above me, the sky was changing.

For twenty years — for thirty, for a hundred, for however long the bargain had held — the stars over these mountains had been eaten, swallowed one by one into the dark maw of the thing the seal had made. But now a single star burned above the peaks where no star had been. It was small. It was stubborn. It was the color of dragon-fire, gold shot through with red, and it did not flicker.

I knew whose work that was. I remembered that much, if nothing else. I remembered blood on an old lock, and hands steadying mine, and a voice telling me — when I was coming apart, when I was giving everything — *Stay with me, Ada. Stay with me just a little longer.*

I had stayed. And then the door had opened, and the survivors of Vaelmoor had poured through it carrying their memories like bundles on their backs, and Edmund had looked back at me through the closing fire with an expression I will carry into whatever grave this mortal body eventually finds him too late to share.

He had smiled.

That was the worst of it. He had known what stepping back through the threshold would cost him, and he had smiled at me anyway, as if to say: *this is not a tragedy, wolf, don't you dare make it one.*

The climb took hours. Mortal bodies are slow, and mine was slower than most, fresh from its second birth and half-starved for whatever it was that fed it now. Twice I fell. Once I lay in the snow — real snow, wet and cold and utterly indifferent to me, which was itself a kind of miracle — and considered simply stopping. The star-eater was dead, dissolved into ordinary night. The kingdom was saved, or the remnant of it was. No one needed a fading spirit who couldn't remember her own purpose.

But the coal at my center said otherwise. It said *up*. It said *him*.

So I went up.

* * *

The ruins of Thornfell gate looked different from the far side.

I had passed through them once before, in the old days, when the wards still hummed and the world still pretended Vaelmoor had never existed. Then they had been a wound in the mountain, a scar of black stone around a darkness that ate light. Now they were — I searched for the word and found it, one of the few words that survived my burning — *open*. The arch stood whole. Firelight moved within it, gentle and low, the color of the new star overhead. And through it I could see the borderlands: pine slopes silvered with moonlight, a valley with woodsmoke rising from chimneys, the ordinary blessed world going about its ordinary blessed night.

And in the arch itself, woven into the stone —

Him.

I stopped ten paces away, afraid in a way I had never been afraid as a spirit. Fear requires a body; I have learned that since. As a spirit I had known dread, awe, vertigo — but fear, the animal kind, the kind that makes your knees water and your throat close — that belongs to flesh, and my flesh delivered it to me in full measure as I stood there looking at what Edmund had become.

He was in the stone. That was the truth of it, and no gentler phrasing softens it. His outline showed through the rock of the arch like a figure seen through frosted glass — shoulders, head, the set of his jaw. Veins of gold ran through the stone where veins should have run through skin, pulsing slowly, in time with the star above. His eyes were open. They were the same gray eyes, and they were looking directly at me, and they were full of wonder and exhaustion and something so tender it hurt to hold.

"Ada," he said.

His voice came from everywhere and nowhere — from the stone, from the wind, from the firelight, from somewhere inside my own chest where the coal still glowed. It was Edmund's voice, but it had a resonance now, a depth, the sound of a man who had become a place.

"You're alive," I said. My own voice cracked on it. Mortal throats do that. "You're — I can see you. You're *alive*."

"I'm the gate," he said, and there was rue in it, the dry humor I remembered even if I'd forgotten why I remembered it. "Which is a kind of alive. Better than I expected, honestly. I thought it would be like drowning." A pause, and the gold veins pulsed. "It isn't. It's like being a window. I can see everything, Ada. Both sides. The valley. The mountain. You."

I walked closer. My legs shook. Ten paces became five became two, until I stood close enough to touch the stone that held him, and I laid my palm flat against it, and it was warm.

Warm as a living cheek. Warm as the ember.

"I couldn't cross," I said. "They told me to. Sera told me to — she carried me down the caldera, I think, or someone did, everything after the lighting is smoke. They said the door was open, that I'd earned a place in the world, that I should come through while it lasted." I pressed my forehead against the warm stone, next to where his hand would be, if he still had hands. "But I stood at the threshold and I tried to remember why I wanted to be real. And I couldn't. The wanting is gone, Edmund. Whatever I was before, whatever I burned to become — it's gone. All that's left is you. And you were on this side of the door."

"Ada—"

"So here I am," I said. "On this side of the door."

The wind moved through the pass, carrying pine-smell and snow-dust, and somewhere below us a nightbird called across the valley — the ordinary sounds of an ordinary world that had no idea how close it had come to forgetting itself entirely. Above us the new star burned on, steady as a promise.

"You should hate me," Edmund said quietly. "Or pity me. One of the two. Everyone else does — Sera wouldn't look at me when she crossed. She said the price was too high, that I'd cheated her of grieving properly by smiling about it." The gold pulsed, dimmed, pulsed again. "But you. You burned yourself hollow to save people you couldn't remember loving. You gave everything, and the reward was — this. A faint little mortal life in a body that barely works, with holes where your self used to be. And instead of walking through the door to heal, you climbed a mountain in the dark."

"Yes," I said.

"That's not devotion, Ada. That's madness."

"It's both," I said. "I've been both since the night the stars went out. So have you."

He laughed then — a real laugh, startled out of him, echoing strangely from the stone and the sky at once, and for a moment the figure in the arch looked less like a prisoner and more like a man leaning out a window on a summer evening. "Gods," he said. "Thirty years I kept this gate thinking duty was a wall around my life. Turns out it was a door. Turns out I was the door." His eyes found mine again, and softened. "What will you do? Truly. You can't live in the pass. Winter's coming, and you're — forgive me — you're built of sparks and stubbornness."

"I'm going to stay," I said. "Here. At the threshold."

"Ada—"

"Not *in* the gate. I know better than to ask for that. But beside it. The gate needs a keeper on the far side — you said so yourself, or the mountain did through you, or however it works now. Someone to tend the fire. Someone to greet the ones who come to remember. Because they'll come, Edmund." I looked past him, through the arch, at the valley below, where the survivors of a drowned kingdom were learning to sleep without flickering. "Sera's people will tell their children true stories. Travelers will hear those stories and get curious. Curious people always find their way to gates. And when they arrive, cold and hungry and full of questions, someone should be waiting. Someone who remembers — even if what she remembers is mostly you."

The stone was silent a long moment. When Edmund spoke again, his voice had gone rough.

"The winters are cruel up here."

"I was a spirit made of a kingdom's grief. I think I can manage a winter."

"There's an old warden hut, half a mile down the eastern trail. Stone walls. It held against thirty years of neglect; it'll hold against weather."

"I know the one," I said, and then blinked, because I did know it — a small square room, a hearth, a shelf of ledgers in a dead hand. A memory that wasn't mine, or wasn't only mine. Some of him was bleeding into me already, or some of me had always been his. "I'll fix the roof."

"Ada." My name again, in that careful voice. "Why? Truly. If the wanting is gone — if you don't remember what you were or why any of this mattered — why stay?"

I took my hand off the stone and looked at it — at the mortal fingers, the ash still ground into the knuckles, the faint gold shimmer beneath the skin where the ember's sparks had settled and refused to leave. And I answered with the only truth I had left.

"Because when everything else burned away, this didn't. I don't know what love is anymore. I don't know if I ever knew. But I know that you are the shape the world makes around its most important thing, and I would rather sit in the cold beside that shape than be warm and whole anywhere you aren't." I shrugged, mortal and awkward. "Maybe that's not devotion. Maybe it's just what's left when a wolf burns down to the coals. But the coals are yours, Edmund Lockridge. They've been yours since the night the stars went out and you found a wolf standing in the snow where no tracks led in."

The gold in the stone flared bright — bright enough to throw our shadows long across the snow, his cast from the arch, mine from the starlight — and when it settled, Edmund was quiet in the way of a man who has been given something too large to answer with words.

So I didn't wait for words.

There was a flat place beside the threshold, sheltered by the arch's shoulder from the worst of the wind, where the snow lay thin over bare stone. I arranged myself there the way I had arranged myself a thousand nights in another body, in another life — curled, nose to tail, facing the gate. Old habit outliving its reason. The wolf-shape was gone from me, probably forever; this body would never grow fur or fangs or four swift feet. But the posture remained, written deeper than flesh.

I lay down beside the threshold of the gate, and I watched the stars come out around the one that burned, and I listened to Edmund breathe in the rhythm of stone and season.

"Get some sleep, wolf," he said at last, gently. "Tomorrow there's a roof to fix."

"And ledgers to read," I murmured. The warmth of the stone seeped into my side like a hand. "You kept thirty years of records, keeper. Someone has to learn what you knew."

Above the mountains, the single star burned on — small, stubborn, gold-shot-red, enough. Below in the valley, woodsmoke rose from chimneys where a kingdom's children slept, dreaming in a language the world had almost lost. And between the two, at the meeting place of the living lands and the remembered ones, a faint grey-eyed woman with a broken memory curled up in the snow beside a man made of stone and starlight, and closed her eyes.

Somewhere in the broken house of my mind, behind a door I could not yet open, something stirred — a fragment of melody, a mother's voice singing in a language I shouldn't know but somehow did.

I fell asleep humming it.

And far below, in the valley, the first of the curious travelers was already lighting a lantern and turning his horse toward the mountains.
