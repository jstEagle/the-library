## 21. The Gate That Stays Open

The first thing I learned about being a mountain was patience.

I had thought I understood it. Thirty years at the Thornfell gate, walking the same circuit, lighting the same lamps, watching the same snow fall and melt and fall again — I had mistaken routine for patience, the way a man mistakes a cage for a home because he has stopped testing the bars. But that was the patience of waiting. The patience of stone is different. It is not waiting for anything. It is simply *being*, fully, in every grain and fault line, while the world moves through you like weather.

I have been the gate for eleven years now.

I know this because I count the springs. I feel them come up through my roots — the thaw moving down from the peaks like a slow green tide, the meltwater finding its old channels through me, the smell of loam waking in the valleys below. I count them the way I once counted stars, back when there were no stars to count, and I find that the habit of counting is one of the few things about myself I have managed to keep.

That is the bargain nobody warned me about. Not the stillness — I chose the stillness, walked into it with my eyes open while Sera wept on the far side of the closing fire. No. The bargain's true price is *drift*. A man who becomes a gate begins to forget he was ever a man. The mountain does not take your memories; it simply dilutes them, the way a river dilutes a drop of ink, until you cannot say for certain whether you remember your mother's face or only the shape of a face, any face, worn smooth by ten thousand years of rain.

So I hold on to things. Deliberately. Every morning — though *morning* is a courtesy of language, since I no longer sleep — I take up my memories the way a smuggler takes up his ledger, checking each entry against the last. My name: Edmund Lockridge. My trade: keeper, then key, then gate. My mother singing in a tongue that should have been foreign and was instead the first music I ever knew. Ada's voice saying my name the night the wards broke, half command and half wonder, as if she had been rehearsing it for centuries and still stumbled on the syllables.

And the wolf. Always the wolf.

She is here now. She has been here for eleven years, and if the mountain has taught me patience, Ada has taught me something harder: that presence can be a form of speech. She lies at the threshold where the door of dragon-fire used to stand, on a shelf of granite that I have — without quite deciding to — kept warm for her. Travelers who pass this way see an old grey wolf, scarred across the shoulder where the wardens' iron bit her, dozing in the sun or watching the road with amber eyes that miss nothing. They do not know they are looking at the memory of a kingdom given flesh, faded now to something small and mortal and stubborn.

They leave offerings anyway. People always have, at gates. But the offerings have changed.

* * *

In the early years they left fear-offerings: bread and salt at the threshold, averted eyes, coins pressed into crevices by hands that shook. The borderlands remembered what it was to live beside a sealed country, even if they could not remember why. Old habits outlive their reasons. A grandmother would hush a child pointing at the wolf — *don't stare, don't name it* — and I would feel the old wards' ghost ache in my stonework, the echo of thirty years of keeping things out.

Then came the year the Vaelmoor children started school in Thornfell village.

Sera crossed with the first of them, of course — Sera, who had spent a lifetime guarding coals and nursing grudges against my ancestor, and who stepped through the last door carrying the ember's final spark cupped in two wrinkled hands like a bird she refused to let die. She settled in the valley below my mountain, in a house with blue shutters that I can feel the warmth of on cold nights, and she made it her business to ensure that Vaelmoor did not become a story again. Not a lost story. Not a warning. A *true* one, told loudly, in daylight, to anyone who would listen.

"Memory is work," she told the village council, when some of them murmured about strange accents and stranger names. "It doesn't keep itself. You either tend it or you bury it, and buried things grow teeth. We've all seen what grows teeth."

They gave her the schoolroom. Of course they did. Nobody has ever won an argument against Sera, and I include the star-eater in that assessment, wherever its scattered cold has drifted.

Now the offerings at my gate are different. Children climb the smugglers' road — the real one, the one that exists again, mapped and named — carrying wildflowers in fistfuls, river stones painted with lopsided stars, ribbons tied to the hawthorn that grows from a crack in my western wall. One of them, a girl named Merrin with Sera's exact scowl, leaves written questions folded into boats of birchbark:

*Do you get lonely?*

*What does it sound like inside a mountain?*

*Is the wolf really a lady? (Ada says yes but Ada says everything funny so I don't know.)*

I answer what I can. This is the other thing I learned, the thing that makes the price bearable: those who remember me can hear me. Not always, not clearly — I am a voice in creaking timber and shifting shale, in the particular warmth of sun on stone, in dreams that linger past waking. But Sera hears me. The children hear me, better than the adults, because children have not yet learned to disbelieve the world's quiet voices. And Ada hears me always, perfectly, though she answers mostly by opening one eye and huffing, which in the old language of wolves — a language she taught me across a hundred miles of smugglers' roads — means nearly everything worth saying.

To Merrin's first question I sent back, through a tremor in the hawthorn's roots that bent three branches toward her boat: *Sometimes. But loneliness is just love with nowhere to go, and mine goes everywhere.*

She read it aloud to her whole class. Sera laughed until she cried, and told me later — shouting cheerfully at a crack in my stones during one of her visits — that I had become "a sentimental pile of geology." She visits every week. She is ninety-one now and climbs my road out of pure spite, which I admire more than I can say. She brings tea she cannot drink and pours it into a hollow in my granite anyway, because ritual matters, and because she knows I like the smell.

"What's it like today, then?" she asks, every time. Same question, eleven years.

*Warm,* I tell her, through the hollow's echo. *The glacier moved four inches. A fox denned in my north shoulder. The wolf caught a marmot and pretended she wasn't proud of it.*

"And you? What's it like being you?"

And that is the question I never answer honestly, because the honest answer changes. Some days it is peace. Some days it is a slow drowning in stone, and I cling to my ledger of memories until my knuckles — if I had knuckles — go white. Most days it is both at once, which I suspect is what being alive actually is, whether you are flesh or granite.

Today, though, today I answer truly, because today is different. Today is the eleventh spring, and the whole valley has come up my mountain.

* * *

They call it Founding Night now, though Sera insists on calling it Door Night, because "founding" sounds too tidy for a night when a dead kingdom walked out of a mountain through a hole in the dark. Eleven years ago tonight, a door of dragon-fire opened in these ruins, and the flickering thousands of Vaelmoor — the candle-flame people, the half-remembered ones, the bakers and weavers and soldiers who had endured a century of being unwritten — stepped through into the living world carrying everything they were.

The valley below is full of their grandchildren now. Full of their songs, sung in a language that was supposed to be forgotten, taught in a schoolroom with blue shutters down the hill. There are Vaelmoor names on the tax rolls of Thornfell. There is a bakery in the village that makes honey-bread according to a recipe no living person wrote down, because the woman who remembers it walked out of nonexistence carrying it in her hands, and she teaches it every autumn to whoever shows up. Memory as ongoing act of love. Sera's phrase. She'd thump my stones if she knew how often I repeat it to myself.

Tonight the crowd gathers at my threshold in the blue hour, hundreds of them, lanterns bobbing like a constellation someone decided to keep low to the ground. The children race the last stretch of road. The old ones walk slower, touching my walls as they pass, and I feel every palm like a small warm word.

Ada rises from her shelf of warm granite and stretches, grey muzzle silvered further now, movements stiff with honest age. The crowd parts for her the way water parts for a keel — not fear, exactly. Reverence with a practical edge. These are people who know what she burned to save their keeper, what years of stored memory she poured out like blood against the wardens, until she could no longer remember why she had wanted to be real and loved him anyway. Half-erased, and she chose the threshold over the open door.

Merrin, taller now, thirteen and all elbows and certainty, carries the lantern to the hawthorn and hangs it on the lowest branch, where eleven lanterns already hang from eleven years. Then the singing starts.

It is the song my mother sang. The one I heard in a fragment of dragon-fire before I knew whose son I was — the lullaby of a drowned kingdom, in a language erased from every map. Now it comes up the mountain in dozens of voices, rough and sweet and slightly out of tune, and I let it move through me the way the meltwater moves through me, and if mountains could weep I would be a river tonight.

Ada sings too. She has a human shape somewhere deep under the wolf — she wears it rarely now, saving her strength, and when she does it is faint, like a figure seen through fogged glass. But her voice carries the melody above all the others, thin and clear and utterly certain, and I understand that she is not singing to the crowd. She is singing to me. It is our conversation, the one we have had every Founding Night for eleven years, wordless and complete:

*I stayed.*

*I know.*

*You're still in there?*

*All of me. Every entry in the ledger.*

*Good,* says the wolf at the threshold, laying her chin on warm stone. *Because I'm not done with you yet.*

* * *

The crowd drifts down the mountain by moonlight, lanterns strung out along the switchbacks like a rosary being counted. Sera is the last to go, as always. She stands at the threshold a long moment, tiny and upright and ancient, patting my wall twice — her version of a blessing — and then looks down at the wolf.

"He talked in his sleep, you know," she says suddenly, to Ada, in the tone of someone continuing a conversation forty years long. "My grandfather. The builder. Talked about the gate like it was a person he'd wronged. Said it would want a keeper someday who chose it." She snorts. "Family gift for dramatic prophecy, the Lockridges."

Ada's tail moves once against the stone. *He chose it,* she says, in the voice only Sera and I can hear. *That's the whole difference. That's everything.*

"I know it is." Sera pulls her shawl tighter. "Eleven years, and the borderlands haven't drowned, the star-eater's cold hasn't come back, and the children learn two alphabets. He was right. The third way." She pauses, and her voice drops, and for a moment she is not the indomitable matriarch of two peoples but a very old woman on a cold mountain. "But Edmund. Love. Are you happy?"

The question lands in me like a struck bell.

I consider lying, out of kindness. I consider the full truth, out of respect. I settle for the truth that fits in a tremor of stone beneath her feet, gentle as a heartbeat:

*Ask me on a bad day and I'll tell you about the drowning. Ask me tonight, with their songs still in my walls and the wolf asleep on my warmest stone — ask me tonight, and I will tell you that I have been a keeper, a key, a smuggler, a son, and a gate, and that being a gate is the only one of those that lets me hold all of them at once.*

Sera listens. Old women who have survived impossible things are very good at listening to stone. Then she nods, briskly, blinking hard, and says, "Sentimental pile of geology," and starts down the road, and does not look back, because looking back is for people who doubt.

The mountain settles around me. The moon crosses. Somewhere in my northern shoulder the fox stirs in her den, and far below, the bakery's chimney breathes the first heat of tomorrow's honey-bread into the dark.

Ada opens one eye.

For eleven years she has slept at my threshold, mortal now, growing older in the slow honest way that wolves grow older, and I have watched her the way I once watched the empty sky — with dread, counting what remains. Wolves do not live forever. Even memory given flesh, even love given fur and teeth, runs on a clock. I have not said this to her. She has not said it to me. It sits between us like the last unopened entry in the ledger.

But tonight, as the moon sets and the valley goes dark and the single star I lit burns small and stubborn above the peak — my star, the one that opened one door and saved one people — Ada does not sleep. She stands, stiffly, and walks to the very edge of the threshold, and looks up at that star for a long time.

Then she speaks, aloud, in her young woman's voice, the one she keeps saved for things that matter.

"Edmund," she says. "Something's coming up the smugglers' road. It's been climbing since midnight, and it isn't one of ours."

I reach for the road through my roots, through the hawthorn's trembling, through the frost-hardened ground — and feel it. Footsteps. Slow, deliberate, unhurried as stone. And beneath them, faint as a dying ember, a cold I know.

The star-eater's cold. Or what's left of it.

Ada's hackles rise along her scarred shoulder, and her amber eyes catch the light of one small star.

"It's carrying a lantern," she says quietly. "And it's asking for you by name."
