## 22. One Star Burning

The child's name was Wren, and she had been born in the borderlands in the first spring after the door opened — the first Vaelmoor-born child in thirty years, though she would never know what that meant until she was old enough to carry it.

Ada knew. Ada counted everything now.

She sat with Wren on the flat stone above the village where the Ember Keepers had built their new homes, the girl wrapped in a blanket against the autumn cold, Ada's own hands stiff around a cup of tea she no longer drank for warmth but for the ritual of holding something. Her body was sixty years old, or looked it. She had chosen this shape deliberately, decades ago, when the ember's sparks rebuilt her: if she was going to be mortal, she would be mortal all the way through, gray hair and aching knees and all. There was no honesty in pretending otherwise.

"Which one?" Wren asked, as she asked every night.

Ada lifted her chin toward the sky. The stars were back — all of them, or nearly. The star-eater was gone, dissolved into ordinary night, and the heavens had slowly refilled themselves over the years like a wound closing from the edges inward. Astronomers in the southern cities wrote papers about the anomaly of 3021, the night every star vanished at once. They had theories. None of them were right, and that suited Ada fine.

But there was one star that had never gone out after that night, because it had never been eaten at all. It had been lit. It burned low over the northern peaks, small and stubborn and slightly wrong-colored — a gold that no other star shared, the color of dragon-fire banked in a chapel, the color of a door made of memory.

"Find me the one that doesn't belong," Ada said.

Wren squinted. She was seven, sharp-eyed, impatient with riddles she'd already solved twice. "The gold one."

"The gold one. And what do we call it?"

"The Keeper." Wren said it with the flat confidence of a child reciting something she considered beneath her. "Because a man became the gate so we could come through."

"A man," Ada agreed. "Not just any man. Edmund Lockridge. He kept a wall for thirty years before he understood what he was keeping. And then he understood, and he stayed anyway."

"Did you love him?"

Children asked these questions the way they asked about weather. Ada had learned not to flinch.

"Yes," she said. "I loved him before I knew what loving was. I was made of remembering, Wren. That's all I was — a kingdom's memory wearing fur. And everything I remembered, I remembered alone, because everyone who could have remembered with me was sealed away or erased. Then he came walking out of the snow with his lantern, and he didn't know anything, and I thought: here is someone I can teach. Here is someone I can give it all to." She smiled into her tea. "I was selfish, at first. You should know that about me. I wanted him because he could make me real. It took me dying once to learn the difference between wanting someone and loving them."

"What's the difference?"

Ada considered. Below them, lamplight kindled window by window down the village street. Somewhere a fiddle was being tuned badly. Sera's granddaughter was shouting at her brothers. The ordinary music of the living world.

"The difference is what you do when it costs you," Ada said. "Come here. Look along my arm."

She raised her arm and pointed, and Wren leaned against her side, following the line of her finger up past the black shoulder of Thornfell mountain to the single gold star burning low and steady above the gate.

"You see how it sits just above the pass? Not high in the sky like the others. It stays close to the mountain. That's because it isn't really a star. It's a lamp in a window. Someone is home behind it."

"Is he lonely?"

"He has company," Ada said. "A wolf sleeps at his threshold. A very old wolf, by now, even if she doesn't look it on the nights she walks."

Wren giggled. "You're the wolf."

"I'm the wolf."

"And you're also here."

"I'm also here." Ada set down the cup and drew the blanket higher around the girl's shoulders. "That's the last lesson of the sky, little wren, and it's the hardest one, so listen. Everything else up there is far away. Light that left its source ten thousand years ago, arriving late, arriving changed. But that one—" she tapped Wren's chin up toward the gold point "—that one is near. That one is a choice somebody made, still burning. When you look at it, you're not looking at the past. You're looking at a promise that hasn't finished being kept."

Wren was quiet for a moment, which for Wren was unprecedented.

"Will you teach my children this too?"

And there it was. The question Ada had been waiting for all autumn, arriving out of a seven-year-old's mouth without ceremony, the way the truest things always did.

"No," Ada said gently. "But you will. That's how it works. That's how it has always worked. Memory isn't a treasure anyone keeps. It's a fire everyone carries. You'll teach your children, and they'll teach theirs, and none of you will ever meet me, and I'll be in every word anyway."

"That doesn't make sense."

"It will when you're older. It took me three hundred years and two deaths."

* * *

Winter came early that year, the way it liked to, and Ada felt it settling into her bones with an intimacy she had never known as a spirit. Fading had been painless — a thinning, a going-transparent, a candle flame guttering in a draft. Mortality was different. Mortality was specific. Her left hip ached before rain. Her eyes needed candles brighter than they used to. She caught colds. She had never imagined she would be grateful for a cold, but the first time she sneezed, forty years ago, she had laughed until she cried, because only real bodies sneezed, and she was, impossibly, undeniably, real.

Sera found her on the stone steps of the chapel they'd rebuilt — not the ruined one in Vaelmoor, but a new one, timber and fieldstone, raised in the borderlands with the old embers enshrined at its heart. Sera was ninety-one now and walked with two sticks and refused, on principle, to use either of them indoors.

"You're slowing down," Sera said, lowering herself onto the step beside Ada with the groan of a woman who had earned every joint complaint she owned.

"You're one to talk."

"I'm allowed. I'm ancient. You're supposed to be a miracle." Sera's eyes, still sharp as awls, studied her sideways. "How long?"

Ada didn't pretend to misunderstand. They had been friends too long for pretense; Sera had accused her of treachery once, forgiven her once, and buried a husband and two children since, and there was nothing left between them that wasn't true.

"The healer says the winter," Ada said. "Maybe spring, if I'm stubborn."

"You're stubborn."

"Then spring."

They sat in silence while snow began to fall, fat slow flakes catching in Sera's white braids and melting on Ada's warmer hands.

"I used to hate you," Sera said conversationally. "Did you know that? Forty years, and I've never said it plainly. When you walked into that chapel with his face beside yours — his face, the builder's face, the face on every curse my grandmother taught me — I wanted to put a knife between your ribs. I thought you'd led him to us like a butcher leads a calf."

"I know. I could smell it on you."

"And then he chose us." Sera's voice went rough. "He stood in that caldera with every reason to run, and he chose us. And you — you poured yourself into the ember like water into sand. So I stopped hating you, and I never told you that either, because I'm a spiteful old woman and apologizing is for people with better knees." She reached over and gripped Ada's wrist, hard, her strength surprising even now. "Thank you. For my grandchildren. For the village. For the fact that my daughter's children speak Vaelmoori and don't have to hide it. Thank you."

"You're welcome," Ada said, and found, to her enormous irritation, that she was crying.

"Don't be dramatic," Sera said, and handed her a handkerchief, and held her hand while the snow fell, and neither of them said anything else for a long time, because some friendships are finished sentences.

* * *

On the last clear night of her life, Ada walked up to the gate.

It was a long walk now. She took it slowly, stopping to rest on the stones that travelers had placed along the path over the years — the offerings. Bread and salt, mostly, sometimes a carved wolf, sometimes a child's drawing weighted down with pebbles. Once, memorably, a perfectly good fiddle, which the villagers had debated for a week before deciding the mountain wanted it more than they did. The tradition had grown on its own, unbidden, the way true rituals did. People left things for the Keeper of Thornfell, and told their children why, and the story grew a little larger and a little truer with each telling.

The gate itself was no longer a gate. That was the thing visitors never quite understood until they saw it. There was no arch, no door, no wall. There was only the pass between the peaks, and the mountain standing over it, and if you knew how to look — if you had Vaelmoor blood, or if you had been loved by someone who did — you could see the shape of a man in the stone of the cliff face, arms spread, head bowed forward as if listening. His eyes were two points of gold light, and they were open, and they had been open for forty years.

Edmund. Part of the mountain, woven into stone and starlight, alive and aware and bound. He could speak to those who remembered him. He had spoken to Ada almost every night of those forty years, his voice rising out of the rock like warmth out of a hearthstone, and she had answered, and the wolf-shaped hollow beside the threshold was worn smooth by four decades of her sleeping there.

She climbed the last stretch on legs that shook, and lay down in the hollow, and looked up at him.

"Hello, keeper," she said.

*Hello, wolf.* His voice filled the stone around her, deep and unhurried as geology. *You walked far tonight.*

"Not so far as all that. You should see the view from the other side of forever. Now that's a walk."

He laughed — she felt it as a tremor through the ground, through her old bones, through the whole patient body of the mountain. *You've been teaching the Quill girl the stars.*

"Wren. Yes. She found your star in under a minute. She's insufferable. You'd adore her."

*I do. I watch her leave bread on the north stone. She talks to me. Last week she asked whether I get bored.*

"What did you tell her?"

*I told her boredom is a door, and I've had forty years to open every one of it.* A pause, and when he spoke again his voice had gentled into the register he saved for truths. *Ada. I can feel it. The same way I feel weather coming off the plains. This is your last night, isn't it?*

She had planned to lie to him. She discovered she couldn't. Forty years of honesty had worn the habit away entirely.

"Yes."

The mountain was silent for a long moment. Snow ticked softly onto stone. Far below, the village lights glimmered, and somewhere among them a baby was crying and being sung to in Vaelmoori, the old lullaby, the one about the drowned kingdom and the wolf who remembered — except it wasn't drowned anymore, and the song had new verses now, verses about a door and a star and a man who stayed.

*Ask me,* Edmund said at last.

"Ask you what?"

*Whatever you've been carrying up this mountain to ask me. I've watched you decide to ask it six times since the chapel steps. Ask it.*

Ada looked up at the stone face, at the gold eyes that had watched her grow old, and asked the question.

"When I die — when I'm done, truly done, when there's nothing left of the body and nothing left to burn — will you remember me? Not the way the world remembers, in stories that change. I mean the way you remember. The way the mountain remembers. Will I be in the stone with you? Or does the gate keep only its keeper?"

*Oh, Ada.* The tenderness in it cracked the air like ice on a pond in spring. *What do you think a gate is? What do you think I've been doing for forty years? Every soul that crossed that door passed through me. I hold them. Sera's husband. Her daughters. The flickering thousands. I am not a wall, my love. I never was. I'm a doorway, and a doorway is defined by what passes through it. You think the mountain keeps me?* The gold eyes brightened, and for a moment she saw them as they had been under a dead sky, young and terrified and certain. *I keep them. All of them. And there is room.*

"Even for a memory that learned to be a person?"

*Especially for her. She's the best part of what I keep.*

Ada closed her eyes. The snow settled on her blanket, on her hair, on the smooth-worn hollow of the wolf's bed, and it did not feel cold. It felt like being tucked in.

"Then I have one request," she said. "Bury me facing the gate. Facing you. Sera will argue about the plot — she'll want me up in the chapel yard with the Keepers — let her win that fight, it will do her good. But the grave itself. Face it east, toward the pass, toward the star. I want the first light of morning and the last light of your lamp to land on the same stone. I want whoever comes to visit me to turn around and find you. That's the whole prayer, Edmund. Let everyone who mourns me look up and see what I loved."

*It will be as you ask.*

"And Edmund? One more thing."

*Anything.*

"When Wren is grown, and she brings her children up here, and they ask about the wolf who sleeps in the stone — tell them I wasn't brave. Tell them I was afraid the whole time, from the first night in the snow to the last night on this mountain, and I did it all anyway. That's the inheritance worth leaving. Not courage. Courage afterward."

*Fearful and faithful,* he said. *The oldest kind of hero. Rest now, Ada. I'll keep the light on.*

She smiled, and pulled the blanket to her chin, and looked — for the last time with living eyes — at the single gold star burning low and stubborn above the pass, close to the mountain, a lamp in a window, a promise still being kept.

"I was made of remembering," she whispered. "And I am remembered. So I was never really in danger of ending at all."

The star burned. The mountain breathed. The wolf slept.

* * *

They buried her in the spring, in the place Sera had argued for, in the chapel yard among the Keepers — but the grave faced east, exactly as she had asked, and the mason cut the stone himself, an old Vaelmoori craftsman with tears freezing in his beard, and carved a single line in the old language and the new:

**ADA — WHO REMEMBERED US FIRST**

Wren came to the grave that evening, seventeen now and tall and fierce, carrying her own daughter on her hip — born in March, red-faced and furious, the second Vaelmoor-born child of the new world. Sera walked beside her, ninety-two and leaning on one stick now, having outlasted, as she cheerfully admitted, nearly everyone she'd ever argued with.

The sun was setting behind them, so the grave lay in shadow, but Wren turned, as every mourner would turn, as Ada had designed them all to turn — and looked east, up past the village roofs, past the offering stones, to the pass between the peaks where the mountain wore the shape of a listening man.

Above the gate, low and golden and utterly steady, one star was already burning, though the sky around it was still blue.

"There she is," Sera said, and patted the headstone with her knuckle, twice, the way she'd knocked on Ada's door for forty years. "Facing the gate. Stubborn to the last. Good."

The baby fussed, and Wren shifted her, and followed the old woman's gaze upward to the gold point of light.

"Who's that?" the baby wouldn't have understood, but Wren said it anyway, softly, the way you say a name into a room where someone used to be. "Who's that, little one? That's the Keeper. And the wolf beside him —"

She stopped. Because in the last slant of the sunset, where the shadow of the threshold met the stone, there was a hollow in the rock shaped exactly like a resting wolf, worn smooth, as if by centuries of sleeping —

— and it was warm.

Wren stood a long moment with her hand pressed flat against the stone, feeling the faint pulse of heat, like a hearth seen through a wall, like a candle carried in another room, like something nearby keeping watch. Then she kissed her daughter's forehead and turned toward home, where the lamps were being lit and the fiddle was tuning and the whole living world was waiting to be told, again, the story of the wolf who remembered and the man who stayed.

Behind her, above the pass, the star burned on — small, stubborn, enough — and would burn, the mountain promised, for as long as anyone remembered to look up.

No one who is remembered is ever fully gone.

And they remembered.
