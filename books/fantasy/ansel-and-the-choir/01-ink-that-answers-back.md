## 1. Ink That Answers Back

The scriptorium of Saint Vellum smelled of tallow, oak gall, and the slow despair of men who had spent forty years writing to a silence.

Ivo Quintana had been one of them for eleven years, since the age of nine, when the temple had taken her in with the charity it extended to all orphans of Thornfell: a bed in the dormitory, bread at dawn and dusk, and a stylus in her hand before her knuckles had finished growing. Her indenture was written on vellum the thickness of a fingernail and the permanence of a scar. It hung in the Abbot's office, as hers hung for every child the temple sheltered, and it said, in the beautiful legal hand the temple reserved for things that bound, that Ivo Quintana would serve the scriptorium until her twenty-first year, at which time her debt of shelter would be considered discharged.

*Considered.* The Abbot liked that word. It left room for consideration.

She sat at her desk in the third row of nine, beneath the high windows that let in the grey mountain light, and she copied the morning's petitions. That was the work, all of it, the whole of it: the faithful of Thornfell wrote their prayers on scraps of paper and dropped them into the lacquered box at the temple door, and the scribes copied them onto good vellum in the fair hand, and the fair copies were carried up to the reliquary and read aloud once a week to the saint's empty chair, and nothing happened. Nothing had ever happened. Saint Vellum had been dead for four hundred years, and whatever power the temple claimed to mediate had never once, in Ivo's living memory, so much as cleared its throat.

She had stopped expecting anything at around the age of twelve, and had made her peace with it at fourteen, and by nineteen she had refined the peace into something almost like craft. There was a satisfaction in the copying itself — in the discipline of a line held straight, in the small red flourishes she was permitted on the initial letters of particularly pious petitions, in the way the ink went down wet and shining and dried into something fixed, finished, incapable of change. Ink did not lie. Ink did not disappoint. Ink simply was, once it dried, and Ivo loved it the way other people loved a wall they could lean against.

The petition she was copying that morning was from a woman in the tanners' quarter named Maren Ostrey, and it read, in a hand that shook:

*Saint Vellum, who sees the ledger of all things: my son has not come home from the high pasture in six days. The snow took the path and the wolves took the rest, I think. I do not ask for a miracle. I only ask that someone write down that he existed, so that he is not lost twice.*

Ivo's stylus paused.

It was a strange petition. Most of them asked for things — rain, a good price for wool, the death of a neighbor's cow, the recovery of a husband's affection, the death of a different neighbor's cow. They asked the saint to *do*. This one only asked the saint to *witness*, which was, in Ivo's private taxonomy of prayers, the most honest kind. She copied it carefully, giving the initial letter a red flourish she did not strictly have leave to give, and set the fair copy on the drying rack with the others, and reached for the next petition in the stack.

Then she stopped, because the original was still lying on her desk, and the original had changed.

She did not see it change. She wanted to be clear about that, in the account she would give herself later, going over it a hundred times: she did not see the ink move, did not see letters crawl into place like insects. She had looked away — a moment, no more, the time it took to lay a fair copy on the rack — and when she looked back at Maren Ostrey's scrap of paper, there was writing on it that had not been there before.

It was written below her son's name, in a line of its own, in an ink that was not ink — darker than oak gall, darker than the black of a closed door, a color Ivo had no name for except that it was the color of looking down into very deep water. And the hand was not a human hand. It was not the crabbed scrawl of the tanners' quarter, nor the fair hand of the scriptorium, nor any of the seventeen hands Ivo had catalogued over eleven years of professional habit. It was a hand that seemed to have been designed by someone who had heard about writing from a great distance and was doing their best. The letters were too tall. The curves bent the wrong way, like a reflection. And yet it was unmistakably, impossibly, a *reply*:

*HE IS NOT LOST. HE SLEEPS UNDER THE ROOT ON THE NORTH SIDE OF THE STONE BARN. HE IS COLD BUT HE IS WHOLE. TELL HIS MOTHER TO BRING BROTH.*

Ivo sat very still.

Around her, the scriptorium went on with its morning: the scratch of forty styluses, the cough of Brother Aldous in the corner where he always coughed, the creak of the drying racks, the grey light moving by fractions across the floor. Nobody else had looked up. Nobody else had seen. And Ivo became aware, with the particular clarity of a person standing at the edge of a cliff they have only just noticed, that her heart was going like a drum at a festival, and that her hands were not still at all.

She looked at the page. The page looked back, insofar as a page can.

*HE IS COLD BUT HE IS WHOLE.*

There were rules for this. There were, in fact, an entire shelf of rules for this, in the *Codex of Proper Devotion*, which Ivo had copied out twice herself and knew better than the Abbot did. Rule the eleventh: *No petition shall receive answer, for the saint intercedes in silence, and silence is his speech.* Rule the twelfth: *Should any scribe observe aught unusual upon a petition, she shall deliver it at once to the Provost of the Scriptorium, that the matter be corrected.* *Corrected.* Ivo knew what correction meant in the scriptorium. It meant the page went into the stove, and the scribe went into a small room with the Provost, and came out having learned something about the difference between observation and report.

She looked at the stove. It was a squat iron thing in the corner, and it ate mistakes the way the temple ate years.

*Tell his mother to bring broth.*

It was such a small thing. That was what undid her, later, when she tried to make herself hand the page over — the smallness of it. If the reply had been thunder, if it had commanded the woman to sell her house and follow the mountain, if it had been grand and terrible and obviously the work of something with plans, then perhaps she could have filed it under the category of things the Codex had anticipated. But it was broth. It was a mother's boy under a stone, cold but whole, and somewhere in the tanners' quarter a woman had asked only that her son be written down so he would not be lost twice, and something beneath the world had bent down and answered her *in writing*, like a scribe, like Ivo, like the only kind of kindness Ivo had ever trusted — the kind you could hold in your hand.

The bell for the mid-morning office rang. The scribes rose, forty of them, in the rustling unison of people whose bodies belonged to a schedule. Ivo rose with them. And in the motion of rising — her sleeve passing over the desk, her hand flat on the wood for one half-second — the original petition was no longer on her desk. It was up her sleeve, against her wrist, warm as a living thing.

She never decided to take it. That was the truth she would return to again and again in the years that followed, when the whole shape of the world had changed and people asked her — people would ask her, eventually — how it began. She had not decided. The page had simply come with her, the way a spark comes with the tinder it lands on, and by the time the thought *I have stolen from the temple* arrived in her mind, her body had already finished the crime, and all that was left was to walk out of the scriptorium with her face arranged into the blankness of forty other blank faces, and go to the office where the petitions were read aloud, and listen to Maren Ostrey's prayer rise up to the empty chair and fall back down like a stone into a well.

* * *

She did not sleep that night. That was not remarkable; she often did not sleep. What was remarkable was where she spent the sleeplessness: not in the dormitory, but in the scriptorium, which she could enter after compline because she was the one who banked the stove, a small duty she had traded for from Brother Aldous years ago in exchange for copying his letters home. The temple trusted her with keys the way it trusted her with everything — completely, and without ever once wondering what she did with them. That was the nature of charity, Ivo had learned. It did not watch. Watching was for owners, and the temple had never quite understood that it owned her.

She sat at her own desk with one candle lit and the page flat before her, and she read it until she had the reply by heart, and then she read it again for the pleasure of the impossible thing, the way you might keep touching a sore tooth to confirm it still hurts, that you are still in the body where it hurts.

The ink had not dried. She discovered that around midnight. Every other line on the page — Maren Ostrey's shaking hand, the date, the smudge of the tannery soot — was dry and fixed and safe. But the reply remained wet, faintly, after twelve hours, after a day, glistening when the candlelight crossed it, as though it were still being written, as though somewhere below the world a pen was moving at the speed of stone and this line was only the part of it that had reached the surface. Ivo touched the corner of her stylus to it, very lightly, the way you touch a stream to know its temperature.

The ink took a little of the stylus. Just a little. Just enough that when she lifted it, the tip was stained the color of deep water, and did not wash out, and never washed out, and for the rest of her life her stylus wrote with a faint shadow in its black, and she never used another.

She should have been afraid. She catalogued the reasons for fear the way she catalogued everything, because cataloguing was how she kept the world from sliding: the reply was not from Saint Vellum, whose hand, per the reliquary samples, had been a fine human hand; the reply had come from nowhere the Codex permitted; the reply knew where a lost boy was, which meant it either watched the mountain or *was* the mountain; and she, Ivo Quintana, indentured scribe, had stolen it, which was a sin under temple law and a crime under town law and, if the thing that wrote it was what the Codex called *the old dark of Thornfell*, possibly a sin under older laws than either.

She was afraid. She noted it, and set it beside the other thing, which was this:

For eleven years she had written prayers into a silence, and the silence had been the whole of her theology, and she had built her peace on it the way you build a house on a grave. And now, in one morning, without ceremony, without thunder, the silence had spoken — and its first word to the world had not been a command or a threat or a demand for worship. It had been directions to a lost boy and a request for broth. It had been *kind*, in the awkward, over-literal way of something that had not spoken to a human in four hundred years and had perhaps forgotten the shape of small talk. It was a handwriting not her own, on a page that was hers by duty, and it had changed everything, because it meant the world was not what she had been taught. It meant the prayers had been going somewhere all along. It meant the silence had not been silence but *listening*, and the listening had ended, and she did not know what had woken it, and she did not know what it wanted, and she did not know why it had chosen her desk, of all the desks in the world, to answer on.

She banked the stove. She folded the page — carefully, along no existing fold, so as not to crease the wet line — and she did not put it in her desk, because the Provost inspected desks. She did not put it in the dormitory, because the dormitories were searched when anything went missing. She sewed it, that night, into the lining of her left boot, with thread from the mending basket, in stitches so small they were invisible, and she walked on it for three days before she could forget it was there, and then for three more before she could stop feeling it with every step, and then she stopped feeling it, the way you stop feeling a heart.

* * *

On the fourth day, Maren Ostrey's son came home.

Ivo heard it the way the whole town heard it, in the market, in the way the story moved from mouth to mouth faster than fish at a good price: the Ostrey boy, found alive under the stone barn on the north pasture, half-frozen but whole, and his mother had been carrying broth up the hill every day since the second day because — and this was the part that made the hair move on Ivo's arms — *she said she didn't know why, she just woke up one morning and knew*. Knew where he was. Knew he was cold. Knew, with a certainty she could not account for and did not try to, that she should bring broth.

The temple took credit, of course. The Abbot preached on it at the week's office — the intercession of Saint Vellum, the mystery of the saint's silence now and his mercy then, the proper humility of the faithful. Ivo stood in the choir of the congregation with her hands folded and her face arranged, and she listened to the Abbot explain the miracle in the language of a man closing a ledger, and she thought: *You don't know. You don't know what answered, and you don't know that it wrote, and you don't know that I have its handwriting sewn into my boot.* And beneath the thought, quieter, in the place where she kept the things that were actually true: *Neither do I.*

That evening she went back to the petition box. She told herself it was duty — the box was her responsibility on Thursdays, the sorting and the stacking — and perhaps it was. But her hands were slower than usual, and her eyes read every scrap twice, and when she reached the bottom of the box she sat back on her heels in the empty narthex and looked at the last petition for a long time.

It was from a child. The hand was a child's hand, round and uneven, the letters like sheep in a field that had not yet learned to stand in rows. It said:

*Dear Saint Vellum. I don't have a mother to ask for me so I am asking for myself. I have a song in my head that isn't mine and I don't know where it came from and it gets louder at night. Please write down that I am not mad. I just need someone to write it down.*

No name. The foundlings were not taught to sign their petitions, because the temple said a prayer from the nameless rose the same as any other, and Ivo had always privately thought that was a lie told to make the rule kinder than it was. But she knew the hand. She had seen it on the practice slates in the foundlings' classroom, on the chores roster, on the little paper hats the children made at midwinter. She knew it the way you know a voice heard through a wall.

It was the choirboy. Ansel. The one who sang like the mountain had taught him.

Ivo sat in the narthex with the child's petition in her hands, and outside the light was going the color of the ink in her boot, and somewhere above her, in the choir loft, a boy was humming — she could hear it faintly, through the floor, a line of melody that no one had written down anywhere in the temple's library, because she had checked, she had checked years ago out of the same restless habit that made her catalogue handwriting — and the candles along the narthex guttered. Not from wind. There was no wind. They guttered in time, all of them, all together, like a room full of small hearts beating to a song.

Ivo looked down at the petition in her hands.

And at the bottom of the page, below the child's round sheep-lettered words, in ink the color of deep water, in a hand too tall and bent the wrong way, a reply was drying.
