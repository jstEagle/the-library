## 2. The Choirboy Who Hums in His Sleep

Ivo did not sleep that night. She told herself it was the ink.

She had locked the page in the bottom of her copying chest, beneath three years of discarded drafts and a tin of dried-out pens, and then she had lain in her narrow cell above the scriptorium and listened to the temple breathe. Saint Vellum's was an old building that had been a granary before it was a church and a watchtower before that, and at night its bones creaked like an old man settling into a chair. She knew every sound. The drip of the cistern. The wind worrying the bell rope. Brother Aldous snoring through the wall with the regularity of a metronome, which was, Ivo had often thought, the only truly liturgical thing about him.

Tonight there was another sound.

At first she took it for the wind. Then she took it for a dream, hers, because the sound was a voice, high and clear and very young, rising and falling in a pattern that was almost a hymn and almost nothing she had ever heard sung in the temple of Saint Vellum, whose hymns were four hundred years old and sounded like it.

She got up. She stood at her window with the cold coming through the shutter seams, and she listened.

The voice came from the boys' dormitory across the cloister garth. It was not a song so much as the memory of one — the shape of melody with the melody missing, the way a footprint is the shape of a foot. It rose. It fell. It held a note that no child's throat should have been able to hold, long and silver and steady as a wire, and then it stopped, all at once, the way a candle stops.

Ivo stood in the dark for a long time, hugging her own elbows.

She had been at Saint Vellum since she was nine years old, indentured at her grandmother's death, her debt calculated to the copper by the Abbot's ledger and her freedom scheduled, if she was careful and the ink held out, for the winter she turned thirty. Nine years of copying. Nine years of prayers that went nowhere, that she had long ago understood went nowhere, that the temple itself understood went nowhere — because the whole architecture of Saint Vellum, the tithes and the candle-fees and the indulgences and the beautiful, expensive, hand-illuminated intercessions for the wealthy dead, was built on the comfortable certainty that no one was listening. Faith, the Abbot said, was a ledger. You made your entries. You did not expect the account to answer.

And now something had answered. In ink the color of deep water, in a hand too tall and bent the wrong way.

Ivo was, by temperament and by training, a person who did not run toward strange sounds in the night. She went back to bed. She lay with her eyes open until the grey came, and when the bell rang for prime she rose and dressed and did her hair in its severe temple knot, and she went down to the scriptorium, and she took up her pen, and she copied the morning's allotment of prayers with a hand so steady that Brother Aldous remarked on it, and she did not think about the voice at all, except constantly, in the place behind her sternum where she kept the things she refused to think about.

* * *

She found out whose prayer it was on the third day, and it was an accident, and the accident was a candle.

The scriptorium of Saint Vellum occupied the old granary's long upper floor, forty feet of oak and arrow-slit windows, and in winter it was lit by tallow candles set in iron rings along the desks. The temple bought its tallow cheap, from a renderer down in Tanners' Row who watered his fat, and so the candles guttered — all of them, always, in the draughts, in a random and democratic flicker that every scribe ignored the way one ignores one's own heartbeat.

Ivo was copying an intercession for a wool merchant's dead mother — *that her soul be weighed and found sufficient* — when she noticed that the candle at the end of her row had stopped guttering.

Not stopped burning. Stopped *flickering*. It stood up straight and still, a single unwavering needle of flame, while every other flame in the room danced.

And then it began, very slightly, to sway. Left. Right. Left. A slow pendulum, patient as tide.

She looked up. Across the scriptorium, at the boys' table where the foundlings sat under Brother Aldous's eye, a child was copying out his letters. He was maybe eleven, small for it, with a cowlick the temple barber despaired of and ink on his chin and his tongue between his teeth in concentration. Ansel. She knew his name the way one knows the names of furniture. He had been left at the gate six years ago with no surname and a blanket that had once been good wool, and the temple, which could not refuse a child without scandal, had taken him in and put him to work, and that was all Ivo had ever needed to know about him.

He was humming. She could not hear it across the room, but she could see it in his shoulders, in the small bob of his head, in the way his lips moved around a tuneless tune — and the candle swayed in time with him. Exactly in time. Left on the lift of his chin. Right on the fall.

Ivo put down her pen very carefully, as though it might go off.

She watched him for the better part of an hour, pretending to work, her own page filling with a wool merchant's mother in a hand so automatic it might have been written by a ghost of her. Ansel finished his letters, got up, carried his pages to Brother Aldous, was inspected and dismissed, and went out to the garth to fetch water, and as he passed beneath the candle-ring the flame leaned after him, the way a flower leans after sun, and righted itself only when the door closed behind him.

That night she went to the dormitory window again, and the voice came again, and this time she did not mistake it for wind.

* * *

"You knew him," she said to Brother Aldous, two days later, in the tone of a person making conversation, which was a tone she had practiced in front of the washbasin and still could not make sound natural. "The foundling. Ansel. Where was he found?"

Aldous looked up from his mending. He was sixty, rheumy, and had spent forty years listening to other people's confessions before his hands shook too badly for the altar, and he had the professional deafness of a man who had heard everything and been paid to forget most of it. "Gate step. Winter before last of the fever years. Why?"

"No reason."

"You've asked about him twice this week."

Ivo had, in fact, asked the cook once before, and had not realized Aldous had been within earshot, and this was the sort of small failure of craft that she despised in herself. "He hums in his sleep," she said, and immediately wished she hadn't.

Aldous threaded his needle. "All children hum."

"Not like that."

The needle paused. Aldous did not look up, but the whole quality of his attention changed, the way a room changes when a door opens somewhere. "Like what, then?"

"Like something remembering a song," Ivo said. "Not learning one. Remembering."

Aldous was quiet for long enough that she thought the conversation was over, and then he said, without looking at her, in the mild voice of a man discussing the weather: "In my last year hearing confessions, a woman came to me from the mountain villages. She said the old shrines up there — the ones the temple calls superstitions — she said they'd started to warm. Stones that had been cold for three hundred years. She asked me what it meant. I told her it meant she should tithe and stop listening to stones." He bit the thread. "I've thought about that woman lately."

"Why?"

"Because I told her the wrong thing," Aldous said, "and I've been telling this temple the wrong thing for forty years, and I am too old to start telling it the right one." He looked up then, and his rheumy eyes were not rheumy at all; they were very clear and very tired. "Leave the boy alone, Quintana. Whatever you've noticed — and I'd wager a month's tallow you've noticed something — leave it alone. Things that wake up want things. That's what waking up is *for*."

* * *

She did not leave him alone. She was not built for leaving things alone; she was built for following a line of ink to its source, and there had never, in her whole careful friendless life, been a line of ink like this one.

She began, in the small ways available to a scribe, to be near him. It was not difficult. The foundlings ate last, in the kitchen, off the temple's dented pans, and Ivo had taken her own meals in the kitchen for nine years because the refectory was for clerics and she was neither cleric nor guest but property, and property ate where the pans were dented. She had never once spoken to the boy across that scarred table. Now she began to.

It went badly, at first. Ansel was shy the way foundlings are shy — not timid, but *provisional*, a child who had learned that adults' interest was a weather that could turn — and when Ivo sat nearer to him than usual he went quiet and ate with his shoulders up around his ears.

"I'm not going to report you for anything," she said finally. "I don't report people. I'm not — " she searched for it — "I'm not one of them."

"You're a scribe," Ansel said. "You're temple."

"I'm a *debt*," Ivo said, before she could stop herself, and then was furious at having said it, and the boy looked at her with a sudden, startling directness, the way children look at you when you have accidentally told them something true, and something in her chest that had been locked for nine years turned over in its sleep.

"I like your hands," he said.

"What?"

"When you write. They go fast but they don't hurry." He demonstrated with his spoon, a small precise circling. "Brother Aldous says a scribe's hand should be like water finding its level. Yours does that."

Ivo looked down at her hands, which had never been praised by anyone in her life, which were inkstained to the second knuckle and chapped from the scriptorium cold, and found that she did not know what to do with her face. "Thank you," she said, stiffly, as though accepting a delivery of tallow.

He smiled. It was an unguarded smile, the whole face of him, and it did something to the light in the kitchen, and Ivo understood with a cold drop in her stomach that she was in danger. Not of the boy. Of the caring. She had arranged her life at Saint Vellum with the same care she gave her letterforms — nothing wasted, nothing attached, no one to lose — because the Abbot's ledger held her freedom and the Abbot's ledger could take things away, and the only thing it could not take was something she had never let herself have. Nine years of not-having. And now a foundling with ink on his chin had praised her hands, and the not-having had a crack in it.

* * *

It was the candle again, a week later, that made her certain. Or rather the lack of it.

She had taken to walking the cloister after compline — ostensibly to check the shutters, a task no one had assigned her and no one questioned, because Ivo Quintana was a person who did unassigned tasks and was therefore invisible. On this night the garth was full of moon, hard and white, and the dormitory window stood open to the warm edge of spring, and the voice came floating out over the cloister stones.

She stood beneath the window and listened properly for the first time. And she heard it.

The boy was not humming nonsense. He was humming the *petition*. The one she had copied, the one with the round sheep-lettered hand — *please I want to know if my mama can hear me if I sing to her down* — he was humming it, the cadence of the written words, the rise and fall of a child's sentence, set to a melody that no one had taught him because the melody had not existed until he made it, and the candles in the dormitory — she could see them through the window, a dozen little flames on the ledge — were guttering in time. In. Out. In. Out. Breathing with him.

And beneath his voice, so low she might have imagined it, so low it was less a sound than a pressure, something was humming *back*.

Ivo's mouth went dry. She thought of the page locked in her chest, the reply drying in ink the color of deep water, in a hand too tall and bent the wrong way. She had spent nine days telling herself it might be a trick, a test, the Abbot's little snare to catch a thieving scribe. She had spent nine days failing to believe it, because the ink had not been the Abbot's ink, and the hand had not been a human hand, and because — this was the part she admitted only now, alone in the moonlit garth, hugging her elbows against a cold that was not in the air — because when she had read the reply, before she had locked it away, before she had thought at all, some animal part of her had *recognized* it, the way you recognize a voice through a wall. Something old. Something that had been waiting a very long time to be addressed.

The humming beneath the boy's voice swelled, just slightly, and every candle in the window leaned toward him like grass toward wind.

The petition had been his. Of course it had been his. A foundling, motherless, singing to a mother who could not hear him — except that something *had* heard him. Something beneath the mountain, or beneath the world, or beneath whatever floor lies under the floors of the world, had heard a child sing into the dark and had answered, in ink, on a scribe's desk, in a temple built on the certainty that the dark never answers.

And the temple did not know. The Abbot did not know. She was the only one — her, Ivo Quintana, debt of Saint Vellum, friend to no one — who stood between this boy and whatever wanted him.

*Wanting* was the word that frightened her. Aldous had said it: things that wake up want things. And the voice in the dormitory window had gone on singing, sweet and unknowing, a child singing down a well with no idea how deep the well was or what had begun, patiently, at the bottom, to climb.

Ivo stood in the garth until the singing stopped and the candles guttered back into their ordinary democratic flicker, and then she went to her cell and did not sleep, and near dawn she made a decision that she would later understand was the first true choice of her life: she would not tell the Abbot. She would keep the boy's secret. Whatever it cost.

She had just blown out her lamp, resolved and terrified, when the knocking came at her door — three slow blows, unhurried, in the dark before prime, when no one in Saint Vellum had any honest business walking the cells.

She opened it. Serel, the Abbot's enforcer, stood in the corridor with a lantern, and behind his shoulder, down the stair, she could see the Abbot himself, robed and waiting, and in the Abbot's hand, unmistakable in the lantern light, was a page of vellum with a child's round sheep-lettered words at the top.

"Quintana," Serel said. "The Abbot wishes to discuss the ledger."
