## 3. The Abbot's Ledger

Ivo hid the page inside the binding of a psalter for three days.

Not the temple's psalters — those were counted, collated, and inspected with the same loving rigor the Abbot applied to everything that might reflect glory on Saint Vellum. She hid it in her own: a cheap octavo she'd bought at fourteen with her first wages, its spine cracked from years of being read in secret, its margins already crowded with her own small rebellions — pressed flowers gone to brown ghosts, a recipe for ink-thinner written sideways, one line of a smuggler's song she'd heard through the scriptorium window and never been able to forget.

The page fit there as if it had always belonged. That was the worst part. The answered prayer, with its second handwriting curling beneath her own careful petition like smoke rising off a candle, looked *at home* among her secrets.

She told herself she would destroy it. She told herself this every night, and every morning she took it out instead and read it again by the grey light before the bells, when even the most devout of the sisters were still snoring and God, presumably, was attending to other business.

*Saint Vellum, keeper of the unwritten word, hear your servant.* Her own hand, eleven years old in its habits, the letters upright and obedient. A prayer copied ten thousand times without variation, because variation was pride and pride was the first sin of scribes. *I ask nothing for myself. I only ask that someone is listening.*

And beneath it, in ink the color of deep water, in a hand that slanted leftward the way no living scribe was taught:

*We are listening. We have always been listening. Tell the little singer we know his name.*

Three days. On the fourth day, the Abbot sent for her.

* * *

She knew before she reached his study. There is a particular quality to silence in a house built on rules, and the silence of Saint Vellum's on that morning had teeth in it. The novices wouldn't meet her eyes. Brother Cassian, who owed her three favors and a forged dispensation, stepped out of her path with the exaggerated courtesy of a man stepping away from something about to fall. Even the candles in the corridor burned straight and still, as if they too were holding their breath.

The Abbot's study sat at the heart of the temple, behind two doors, the inner one oak banded with iron — because Saint Vellum's god had been a god of records before he was anything else, and records were kept behind iron. Ivo had entered that room four times in eleven years. Twice to be praised. Once to be warned. Once, when she was nine, to be told that her grandmother was dead, delivered in the same tone the Abbot used for inventory reports, which was how she had learned that grief in this place was a clerical matter.

Today the doors stood open, and the Abbot was not alone.

Serel leaned against the wall beside the window, arms folded, watching Ivo come down the length of the room with the patient attention of a cat watching a mouse that has not yet realized the door is shut. Serel was the Abbot's enforcer, though the temple called the position *Warden of the Exterior Works*, a title that meant whatever needed doing outside the walls that the temple wished to deny doing. Serel wore no habit. Serel wore good boots and a sword hilt worn smooth by use, and a face that gave away nothing except, occasionally, contempt.

On the Abbot's desk, weighted flat under a paperknife, lay the page.

Her page. Taken from the spine of her psalter, which sat open beside it, gutted, its broken binding exposed like a wound.

"Sit," said the Abbot.

She sat. Her hands, she noted distantly, were perfectly steady in her lap, and she hated them for it. Some part of her wanted to shake. Shaking would have been honest.

"You will tell me," the Abbot said, "where you obtained this."

"I wrote it." Her voice came out level too. "Eleven years ago. It's a standard petition. You'll find it in the—"

"I know what you'll find in the formulary, Quintana. I have the formulary memorized." He turned the page toward her with one finger, not touching it, she noticed. Not touching it, as though it might be hot. "What I do not find in the formulary is the answer."

"No," Ivo agreed. "You don't."

The silence stretched. Serel made a small sound that might have been amusement.

"Do you understand what you are holding?" the Abbot asked. "Do you understand what this means? In eleven hundred years of recorded devotion, Saint Vellum has answered perhaps seven prayers directly, and each of those occasions required a papal commission, three years of investigation, and the burning of at least one inconvenient scholar. And you — an indentured copyist with delusions of privacy — receive an answer in your *cell* and hide it in a *psalter*."

"It wasn't addressed to me." The words were out before she could weigh them. "It says — it names someone. 'The little singer.'"

Something moved behind the Abbot's eyes. Not surprise. Confirmation.

"Yes," he said quietly. "It does."

* * *

He rose then, and went to the iron cabinet against the far wall, and Ivo understood, with a lurch, that she had never once seen him open it. It was the cabinet where the temple kept its true ledger — not the ledger of tithes and candles displayed for the bishop's inspections, but the other ledger, the old one, the one the founding brothers had supposedly carried down from Thornfell's first chapel. She had assumed it was legend. Everything in this room, she was beginning to understand, was legend until it wasn't.

He laid the book on the desk beside her confiscated page. It was bound in leather gone black with age, its corners capped in tarnished silver, and it was thick — thick the way a stone is thick, thick with weight that had nothing to do with paper. When he opened it, the smell rose up out of it: dust and tallow and something older underneath, something mineral and cold, like air from a cave.

"This," the Abbot said, "is the Ledger of Obligations. Every debt this temple has ever incurred, every contract ever signed in Saint Vellum's name, every bargain struck on behalf of souls entrusted to our care. Do you know how many entries it contains?"

"No, Father Abbot."

"One hundred and forty-four." He turned pages with a care that bordered on reverence. "One hundred and forty-three of them are settled. Paid, forgiven, or expired with the death of both parties. One remains open." He stopped turning. "It was opened fifty-one years ago. It concerns a child. And the party who holds the counterclaim—" he looked up, and his eyes were tired in a way that frightened her more than anger would have, "—the party who holds the counterclaim is named Quintana."

The room did not tilt. Ivo was disappointed to note that it didn't; some treacherous part of her had expected the world to react, to crack along its seams the way it had cracked the night she'd found the answered page. But the candles went on burning, and Serel went on leaning, and the Abbot went on speaking in his inventory voice, and the world declined to acknowledge that anything had changed at all.

"My grandmother has been dead for twenty years," Ivo said.

"Debts survive their debtors. That is rather the point of debts." He rotated the ledger so she could see. The entry was written in a hand she almost recognized — a hand like hers but wilder, less obedient, the letters of a woman who had learned to write late and never fully submitted to it. *Maren Quintana, of Thornfell town, in consideration of certain services rendered and hardships averted, does convey unto the Court Beneath the Mountain one soul entire, newly born, in payment of—*

The rest was smudged, or eaten, or simply refused to be read; Ivo's eyes slid off the line the way water slides off oiled cloth. But the beginning was clear enough, and clear enough was a knife.

"A soul entire," Ivo said. "Newly born."

"The child sold was never named in the contract. Names, in such transactions, complicate matters." The Abbot closed the ledger gently. "But souls leave... residue. Traces. A soul that has been conveyed does not sit quietly in its new owner's keeping. It hums. It harmonizes. It reaches back toward the world it was taken from." He paused. "Three nights ago, the choirboy Ansel sang in his sleep, and every candle in the dormitory guttered in time, and Sister Verity — who is not imaginative — came to me white as whey. And yesterday, a prayer hidden in a psalter received an answer naming 'the little singer.' And today I learn that my copyist has been concealing evidence of a metaphysical breach for three days while sitting, every day, within arm's reach of the boy in question."

"I wasn't concealing—"

"You were concealing *exactly* that, and we both know it, and what interests me is *why*." He folded his hands. "You are not a sentimental woman, Quintana. You are careful, skeptical, and friendless by demonstrated preference. So either you concealed the page out of fear — plausible — or you concealed it because some part of you already suspected the truth and did not want it confirmed. Also plausible. Either way, the concealment ends now."

Ivo looked at the ledger. At the black leather and the tarnished silver and the single open entry that bore her family's name like a brand. Fifty-one years. Her grandmother had walked to the mountain's mouth before Ivo was born and signed away a newborn's soul, and the temple had known, had *recorded* it, had let the child grow up singing in their choir with the debt coiled around him like a root under a floorstone — and they had let *her* grow up too, copying prayers by the thousand into a silence everyone in this room apparently knew was not empty.

"Why keep him here?" she asked. Her voice sounded strange to her, flattened, far away. "If you knew what he was. Why not give him to them and be done with it? Why feed him and teach him and let him sing?"

For the first time, the Abbot looked genuinely uncomfortable. It lasted perhaps two seconds, and then it was filed away, and the inventory voice returned.

"Because a debt in default accrues interest, and a soul delivered prematurely can be reclaimed by parties other than the creditor. Because the Court Beneath the Mountain is not the only power that trades in souls, and Saint Vellum's protection has kept that child unclaimed for fifteen years. And because—" he hesitated, and Ivo would remember the hesitation all her life, would turn it over in the dark years afterward looking for the moment charity became arithmetic, "—because we hoped to negotiate. A soul held in trust is worth more than a soul seized. We intended to arrange terms. Terms require a guide."

"Terms," Ivo repeated.

"The Court has woken, Quintana. The shrines on the mountain are glowing. Things that slept for centuries are counting footsteps on roads that haven't seen honest traffic in a generation. If Ansel's soul is to be retrieved — if the debt is to be discharged and the boy freed — it must happen now, while the ways below are still passable, before powers greater than ours finish waking and set their own claims in order." He drew a folded document from the ledger's back cover and set it on the desk between them. "A retrieval party leaves within the week. Priests, guides, protection. They will need someone who knows the smugglers' roads under Thornfell — the ways that don't appear on any chart the temple owns. Someone who grew up on those roads. Someone whose family name, conveniently, appears in the Court's own records, and who may therefore be granted audience where strangers would be eaten."

The shape of it assembled itself in her mind with horrible clarity, block by block, like a wall going up.

"You're asking me to go."

"I am not asking." The Abbot's voice did not rise. It never rose; that was the terrible thing about him, that he had never once in eleven years needed to raise his voice, because the cage did the work for him. "Your indenture, Quintana. Eleven years of service rendered, four remaining. Refuse this duty, and I regret to inform you that the temple will have no choice but to declare your conduct insubordinate, extend your term accordingly, and remand you to the Warden of Exterior Works for discipline appropriate to—"

"That's not discipline. That's slavery with better calligraphy."

"Yes," the Abbot said, without a flicker. "Most contracts are. You of all people should appreciate the distinction; your grandmother's was considerably worse."

From the window, Serel spoke for the first time, voice dry as old vellum. "For what it's worth, copyist, I've read the alternative. The Court doesn't extend terms. It collects. Whole. You'd be doing the boy a kindness."

"You'll forgive me," Ivo said, "if I don't take moral instruction from the temple's boot."

Serel smiled. It was not a comforting sight. "Oh, I like her."

"Quintana." The Abbot waited until she looked at him. "I want you to understand one thing before you decide, because you will decide tonight and I will not offer this twice. You believe this temple caged you. You believe your indenture was charity, and charity was a chain. Perhaps it was. But understand what the chain was *for*. Your grandmother did not sell Ansel's soul to the Court. She sold it to buy something back — the entry is explicit, or as explicit as such entries ever are. And the remainder of her estate, the part that passed to you, is entangled in the same transaction. The Court's claim does not end with the boy. Read the seal at the bottom of the page sometime, when your hands are steadier than they are now. The debt-holder's name is Quintana. Both halves of it."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere above them, muffled by floors and doctrine, the choir was rehearsing, and threading through the ordinary hymn — thin as wire, unmistakable once heard — ran a harmony no one had taught anyone.

"Ansel doesn't know," Ivo said. It wasn't a question.

"He knows he dreams strangely. He knows the candles listen to him. Children accommodate far stranger knowledge than that." The Abbot closed the ledger and rested his hand upon it, and for one unguarded moment he looked not like a man of power but like a very old clerk guarding a very bad book. "You leave in six days. Serel will brief you on the party. Dismissed."

Ivo stood. At the door she stopped, because there was one question left and it had been sitting in her chest since the words *one hundred and forty-four*, and she was not, whatever the Abbot believed, a woman who left questions unasked.

"When my grandmother signed," she said, without turning around, "what did she buy back?"

Behind her, the pause stretched long enough to be an answer in itself.

"That," said the Abbot, "is not in the ledger."

She walked out past Serel, past the straight-burning candles, down the corridor where the choir's rehearsal seeped through the stones — and beneath the hymn, beneath the taught notes and the obedient harmonies, she could hear it now, plain as a signature: the second voice, the leftward-slanting voice, the voice of something that had been listening all along.

*Tell the little singer we know his name.*

Six days, she thought. Six days to decide whether to break a cage by walking deeper into it.

She went to find Ansel.
