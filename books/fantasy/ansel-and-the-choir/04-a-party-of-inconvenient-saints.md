## 4. A Party of Inconvenient Saints

The Abbot did not say *these are your companions*. He said, "These are the people who will be traveling with you," in the tone of a man introducing a disease to a healthy body.

Ivo stood in the temple's disused counting room — a chamber she had not entered since her novitiate, when she'd been sent to fetch candle-wax ledgers and had found instead a wall of small drawers, each labeled with a name and a sum. She had asked then what the names meant. The answer had been: *nothing you need to pray about.* Now she understood that the drawers had been debts. Everything in Saint Vellum was debts.

The four of them were arranged before her like an apology that had gone wrong somewhere in the drafting.

"This is Father Aldous," the Abbot said, gesturing to a gaunt man in a cassock two shades too fine for his current station, which was apparently no station at all. "Formerly of the parish at Hollowmere."

"Defrocked," Aldous corrected pleasantly. He had the voice of a man who had spent thirty years reading lessons aloud and could not stop making everything sound like scripture. "For the sin of asking questions during doctrine review. Also for the sin of answering them. The bishop felt the combination showed poor judgment."

"He is useful," said the Abbot, as if describing a tool with a chipped edge.

"I am extremely useful," Aldous agreed. "I know every rite of binding, unbinding, and polite refusal ever written in this province, including three that were declared heretical for being *too effective.*"

Ivo looked at him. He looked back with the frank, tired amusement of a man who had already survived worse company than hers. There was ink on his fingers — real ink, the old iron-gall kind that ate into skin — and she caught herself softening toward him by a single degree before catching herself. Friendless by design, she reminded herself. That was how the temple kept its scribes. A scribe with friends asked questions. A scribe with friends got answers like the one waiting in her sleeve, folded in eighths, warm from her body heat: a page of prayer answered in handwriting that was not her own.

She did not soften.

"The cartographer," the Abbot continued, "is Odile Vess."

Odile was small and brown and perhaps fifty, with a leather roll slung across her back the way other people wore swords. She watched the Abbot's mouth with total attention, and when he finished speaking she looked at Ivo and made a quick gesture — flat hand, two taps against her chin.

"She's deaf," Aldous supplied. "Reads lips beautifully, but only if you face her and don't mumble. The Abbot mumbles. It's the only entertaining thing about him."

The Abbot did not dignify this. "Vess has mapped more of Thornfell than anyone living. Including the parts we prefer unmapped." His eyes flicked to Ivo, and there it was again — the thing he wasn't saying aloud, the reason a deaf cartographer belonged on a journey through smugglers' roads. Odile had mapped the under-roads. Of course she had. Sound didn't matter down there; stone did. And a woman who lived by her eyes would have seen things in the dark that hearing people walked past singing.

Odile caught Ivo looking and produced, from some inner pocket, a stub of charcoal and a scrap of paper. She wrote in a quick, slanted hand: *You're the guide? You look like a librarian.*

*I am a librarian,* Ivo wrote back, because it was easier than explaining that she was an indentured scribe being blackmailed with her own grandmother's sins.

*Good,* Odile wrote. *Librarians find things. Mercenaries lose them.*

"Speaking of whom," Aldous murmured.

The mercenary was leaning against the counting room's one window, which was filthy enough to make him look like a saint in a bad fresco. He was enormous in the way of furniture rather than men — broad, dark, immovable — and he wore a mail shirt under a coat that had been good twenty years ago, and around his neck, on a cord, hung a small wooden token of a god Ivo recognized and wished she didn't.

"That is Brannoc Hale," the Abbot said. "He will handle anything that needs handling."

"Anything," Brannoc said. His voice was gravel poured slow. "Except miracles. I'm out of stock on those."

"You pray to the Ninefold Mother," Ivo said before she could stop herself.

The room went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when someone has said the true thing too early. Brannoc looked down at the token, then up at her, and his face did something complicated — grief wearing gruffness as a coat, buttoned wrong.

"Aye," he said. "And before you ask, girl, yes, I know she's dead. Eaten. Whatever word your Abbot prefers in his ledgers." He touched the little wooden figure with one huge thumb, gentle as a man touching a sleeping child. "I keep praying anyway. Habit. Or spite. Never worked out which."

Ivo knew the word. She had copied it forty times in the margins of forbidden folios: *consumed.* The court beneath the mountain had eaten the Ninefold Mother three generations ago, along with six lesser gods whose names the temple had quietly struck from the calendar so that no one would ask where the feast days went. Everyone in Thornfell knew the story the way you know a scar — without remembering the wound.

But nobody prayed to a dead god. Nobody except, apparently, the mercenary hired to walk into the thing's belly.

"You'll want to stand away from me when we get close," Brannoc told her, matter-of-fact. "Whatever's down there, it knows my prayers. Might come looking."

"Noted," said the Abbot, who had clearly known this when he hired him.

And then there was the fourth.

Serel stood apart from the others the way a blade stands apart from a table setting — present, accounted for, and not for eating with. Grey cloak, grey eyes, a face that had been handsome once and had since decided beauty was inefficient. She had not spoken since Ivo entered. She had not needed to. When the Abbot gestured to her, she inclined her head exactly the degree required by courtesy and no further.

"Serel acts as my hand on this journey," the Abbot said. "What she reports, I know. What she commands, you do."

"What she reports," Ivo repeated, because she wanted it in the open air where it could curdle properly.

"I report," Serel said. Her voice was level and cold and completely unbothered. "It is my function. So is keeping all of you alive, which you may find is the more demanding role." Her gaze moved over Ivo — cataloguing, weighing, filing. "The scribe. Quintana. You've walked the smugglers' roads."

It was not a question, but Ivo answered it anyway. "Since I was nine. My grandmother ran goods through the mountain before the temple bought her debt. I know the roads by heartbeat."

"By heartbeat," Serel repeated, and something almost like interest crossed her face, the way light crosses a closed door. "Then you understand pacing. Good. We move at dawn."

"We haven't even agreed—" Aldous began.

"There is nothing to agree," the Abbot said. "There is a boy in the infirmary who hums harmonies no one taught him, candles that gutter in time to his breathing, and a contract in my vault with a Quintana signature on it. You are going beneath Thornfell to retrieve what was sold. You are going because each of you owes this temple, or this province, or your own conscience, more than you can pay any other way." He gathered his sleeves, done with them. "Consider yourselves a congregation."

He left. Doors in Saint Vellum closed softly; even the architecture was trained.

* * *

They spent the rest of the day in the counting room, because it was the only room with a table big enough for maps and far enough from the cloister that no one would overhear. Ivo spread her grandmother's road-charts — oilcloth gone soft as cloth-of-gold with handling — and the party assembled itself around her the way iron filings assemble around a lodestone: reluctantly, and along lines they hadn't chosen.

Aldous read rites aloud to test their weight, discarding most. "Binding of the Threshold — no, requires a consecrated altar, and I doubt the court provides one. Litany Against Hunger — ha, no. We are walking *into* the hunger." He looked up. "Do you have anything? Scribes always have something."

Ivo hesitated. Then she took from her sleeve the folded page — the answered prayer — and laid it on the map between the Shrine of Nine Bells and the Weeping Stair.

The room changed. Even Serel leaned in.

The petition was written in Ivo's own careful hand, copied from the daily basket months ago: *a prayer for safe passage, for my brother lost on the mountain roads.* Below it, in a script none of them recognized — looping, bell-shaped, alive — someone had replied: *HE IS WARM WHERE HE FELL. THE MOUNTAIN KEEPS HIM KINDLY. COME AND ASK US YOURSELF.*

"It started answering in spring," Ivo said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Petitions left in the offering baskets. Not all of them. Maybe one in a hundred. The temple burned the first dozen replies. I kept this one."

"Why?" Brannoc asked.

Because the handwriting had been beautiful. Because it had been *kind.* Because in nineteen years of copying prayers into a silence, she had never once been spoken to, and the first voice that answered her god had sounded like her grandmother's kitchen sounded, before. She said none of this.

"Because burning evidence is how temples stay honest," she said instead, and Odile snorted — a silent laugh, shoulders shaking — and tapped the table twice, which Ivo was learning meant *agreed.*

Odile took the page, held it near the lamp, tilted it. Then she pulled her charcoal across paper in fast strokes: *Ink's wrong. Not written. Rung. Look — letters repeat like notes.*

Ivo looked. The looping script did repeat, in patterns, the way a refrain does. A prayer answered in music. In harmony. Like a choirboy humming in his sleep.

Ansel. The thought arrived whole and terrible: *the answered prayer was his.* Some thread of him, leaking upward through the temple's foundations, finding the petitions of the grieving and replying with borrowed grace. She folded the page away before her face could finish deciding what it felt.

"So the gods below write back now," Aldous said softly. "After three hundred years of silence. Right when a soul they own starts singing in our attic. Anyone else find that arithmetic suspicious?"

"Everyone finds everything suspicious," Brannoc rumbled. "That's why nothing gets done. I find it suspicious *and* I'm getting paid."

"You're getting paid?" Ivo said.

"Temple's forgiving a debt." He shrugged, mountains moving. "Not mine. My captain's. Dead now. Debt lives on. Debts always live on." He looked at her then, directly, with those weathered eyes, and added, quieter: "Yours too, I'd guess. The Abbot doesn't blackmail people who owe him nothing."

The firelight popped. Ivo kept her hands flat on the map and said nothing, and hated how well he'd read her, and hated more that being read felt, for one treacherous instant, like company.

* * *

Dawn found them at the postern gate, where the temple's holy ground gave way to the smugglers' paths Ivo had run since childhood. Mist lay in the hollows like spilled milk. Ansel was nowhere in sight — the Abbot had been explicit: the boy stayed behind, a coin in a vault, until they returned with the means to free him or failed and made the question moot. Ivo had tried to see him once, yesterday, and been turned away at the infirmary door by a novice with apologetic eyes. *He's sleeping,* she'd said. *He sings in his sleep now. It's lovely.* Lovely. As if a drain were lovely because the water went down smoothly.

"Quintana." Serel fell into step beside her as they passed the first boundary-stone, where the mist thickened and the temple's bells went quiet behind them. "Lead. Don't perform. If we die, it should be efficiently."

"Charming," said Aldous from behind.

"I'm not paid to charm."

"You're not paid at all, I suspect. People like you *are* payment."

Serel did not turn around, but her stride shortened by half a step — just long enough for Ivo to notice, just long enough to mean something — before resuming its metronome pace.

They went down through the birch scrub onto the old drover's path, and Ivo felt the mountain take notice of them. She couldn't have explained how she knew; it was a pressure in the ears, a change in the way her heartbeat sat in her chest, the sense of a great attention turning over beneath the stone like something in bed deciding whether to wake. The shrines began an hour later — roadside niches she had passed a thousand times, dead stone with rain-stains for faces — and every one of them was glowing. Faintly. Warmly. Like coals remembered.

Brannoc stopped at the third one. Took off his hat, which Ivo had not known he owned. Stood with his head bowed while the others waited, and the little wooden god at his throat seemed to drink the shrine-light, or maybe the shrine-light drank from it; at that distance Ivo genuinely could not tell.

"They're waking up," he said when he straightened. "All of them. Every god that thing swallowed." He looked south, toward the deep roads, toward the court beneath the mountain, and his voice dropped into something that was not fear exactly, but was fear's older, tougher sibling. "And they're counting us."

"Counting?" Aldous asked.

"Footsteps." Brannoc resettled his hat. "Something down there has been counting footsteps since the boundary-stone. I can feel it the way you feel a stare. One. Two. Three." He glanced back at Ivo, and for the first time the gruffness slipped entirely, and what was underneath was a man standing vigil over a grave. "Four of us, girl. It already knows."

Behind them, far above, faint through miles of stone and morning air, the temple bell rang the hour — and underneath it, threaded through it like a second voice holding a harmony no one had taught it, something sang.

Odile stopped. Turned. Wrote one word on her slate and held it up so they could all see:

*Five?*

Ivo counted. Brannoc, Aldous, Serel, herself. Four sets of boots on the drover's path, and a fifth set of footsteps keeping time with theirs, patient as a tollkeeper, coming down from the direction of the temple —

— from the direction of the infirmary, where a choirboy who sang in his sleep had just woken up alone.
