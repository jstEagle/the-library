## 5. The First Road Under Thornfell

The mouth of the first tunnel was hidden behind a waterfall that no longer fell.

Ivo stood at the lip of the dry gorge with her lantern raised and felt something in her chest go quietly wrong. The falls had been called the Weeping Vein by every smuggler who ever used them as a door — a thin silver thread of water sliding down black rock, loud enough to drown the sound of men and cargo passing behind it. Her grandmother had taught her its rhythm before she'd taught her letters. *Count to nine while it pours, girl, then slip through on the tenth breath.* Nine years of drought and drainage upstream had thinned it, but never killed it. Never once in Ivo's twenty-six years had she come to this gorge and found bare stone.

Now there was only a dark stain down the cliff face, like a tear track on an old woman's cheek, and silence underneath it.

"The map says water," Serel said behind her. Not accusing. Worse — neutral, the way a ledger is neutral.

"The map is five years old. My feet are not." Ivo crouched and pressed her palm flat against the rock where the water should have struck. The stone was cold and faintly damp, the way a face is damp after crying has stopped. "It went dry recently. Days, not months." She didn't say what she was thinking, which was: *or it was taken.*

Brannoc shifted his pack, and the mercenary's many small weapons made their quiet metal conversation. "Dry is good for us, isn't it? Less noise."

"Dry is good for crossing rivers," Ivo said. "This isn't a river. It's a lock." She got up, brushed grit from her knees, and led them along the ledge to the seam in the rock that only looked like shadow until you knew to walk into it.

Behind her she heard Odile's boots stop, and turned. The cartographer stood with her head tilted, one hand pressed to the stone wall, fingers spread. She was reading it, Ivo realized. Reading the mountain through her skin, the way she read everything — vibration, pressure, the deep grammar of things moving underground. After a moment Odile caught Ivo watching and signed something quick and economical: *Hollow. Big. Old.*

"I know," Ivo said aloud, because Odile read lips better than most hearing people listened. "I grew up inside it."

*It grew too,* Odile signed back, and smiled her small crooked smile, and Ivo decided not to think about that for several hours at least.

She took them into the dark.

* * *

The smugglers' roads under Thornfell were not one road but a body — capillaries and arteries and one or two great veins wide enough to drive a cart through, all of it carved over centuries by people whose profession required the world above to forget they existed. Ivo knew them the way other people knew prayers: not by study but by repetition, by heartbeat, by the particular way fear sat in the stomach at each turning. There was the Long Stair where her grandmother had once hauled salt up four hundred steps with Ivo asleep in a basket on her back. There was the Chamber of Hooks where the ceiling bristled with iron from some forgotten industry, and you walked beneath it bent double, superstitious, because old smugglers swore the hooks remembered hands. There were three places where you absolutely did not speak your own name, and Ivo had never asked why, because asking why was how you died in these passages.

She moved through them now with the specific pleasure of competence — the small hot satisfaction of being, for once, the person everyone else had to follow. Defrocked Father Ambrose wheezed behind her, muttering litanies under his breath that were half prayer and half complaint. Brannoc brought up the rear with the patience of a man used to walking toward things that wanted to kill him. Ansel came in the middle, where Ivo had put him, and he was humming.

He didn't seem to know he was doing it. That was the strange part. Every few minutes the tune would surface out of him like a fish rising, a thread of melody with no words, and the lantern flames would lean toward him — all of them, all together, a slow synchronized bow — and then right themselves when he stopped.

"You're doing it again," Ivo said, the fourth time.

Ansel blinked at her. He was fourteen and had the kind of face that hadn't yet decided what it would be when it finished growing. "Doing what?"

"Humming."

"Was I?" He looked genuinely baffled, and a little frightened, and Ivo felt the reluctant tenderness move through her chest like a splinter working deeper. She'd spent her whole life friendless by design — friends were leverage, leverage was debt, debt was the temple's favorite food — and this boy with his candle-bowing hymns was dismantling that design one note at a time without even trying.

"It's the same song," Ambrose said quietly. He had gone pale around the mouth. "Every time. It's the same song, and I know it, and I can't place it."

"Old?" Brannoc asked.

"Worse than old." Ambrose wiped his face with his sleeve. "Pre-temple. Something from before Saint Vellum's people came up the valley. I heard fragments of it in seminary, in the forbidden commentaries. They said it was what the mountain sang before anyone taught it words."

Nobody said anything after that. Ansel hummed, and the candles bowed, and Ivo led them on through her grandmother's roads, which were beginning to feel less like roads and more like the inside of a throat.

* * *

They made camp in the Salt Gallery, a broad chamber where the walls glittered with mineral frost and the air tasted faintly of tears. Ivo set the watches herself, checked the side-exits twice, and lay down on her bedroll staring at the ceiling of her childhood and feeling like a stranger in it.

That was when she noticed the shrine.

There had always been shrines. Smugglers were a pious trade — you spent enough time underground, you hedged your bets with whatever might be listening. Little niches cut into the walls, holding river stones, or coins, or knots of red thread, offerings to gods whose names the smugglers had mostly lost. Ivo had passed a hundred of them in her life and never seen one do anything but sit there being ignored, which was, she'd assumed, the natural condition of gods.

The niche forty feet down the gallery was glowing.

Not lit — glowing. A soft amber light rising out of the stone itself, out of the little heap of offerings, out of the carved shape above them that Ivo had always taken for a knot decoration and now, in that light, resolved unmistakably into a bell. The light pulsed. Slowly. Rhythmically.

Like breathing. Like a heartbeat. Like something counting.

She got up without waking anyone and walked toward it, and the glow brightened as she approached, warming her face, and she saw that the offerings had been rearranged. Recently. The river stones were stacked in a careful cairn. The red thread had been tied into a pattern she almost recognized — a loop-and-cross, loop-and-cross, the way you'd tie off a contract.

The way you'd tie off a soul-contract. She'd seen the seal on Ansel's page in the Abbot's ledger. Loop-and-cross.

*I'm not sleeping,* she told herself firmly. *Underground air. Mineral fumes. This is a hallucination with excellent production values.*

The bell-shape pulsed again, and this time she felt it — a pressure against her sternum, gentle as a fingertip, and beneath her feet, far below, something vast shifted its weight.

And counted.

She couldn't have said how she knew it was counting. It wasn't sound; it was more fundamental than sound, a rhythm in the floor, in the salt, in her own pulse, which had stopped being entirely hers. One. Two. Three. It was counting footsteps, she realized. Counting *their* footsteps, theirs and hers, keeping tally with the patient precision of a merchant at a ledger, and somewhere beneath the arithmetic of it was an attention so large and so old that her mind kept sliding off it like rain off slate.

*Ivo Quintana,* said nothing, in a voice that was somehow perfectly audible.

She stumbled back from the shrine. The glow dimmed — not extinguished, she noted with a fresh crawl of horror, just polite, the way a shopkeeper dims his lamps at closing but keeps watch through the glass. Behind her, a bedroll rustled.

"You felt it too." Brannoc sat up in the dark. His voice was carefully level, the levelness of a man standing very still on a high ledge. "Don't lie to me, scribe. You walked like someone following a voice."

"There's a shrine that's awake," Ivo said. "Down the gallery. And something below us is—" She stopped. How did you say it? *Something below us is doing bookkeeping on our souls?*

"Counting," Brannoc said.

She stared at him. In the dim reflected glow his face was all weathered planes and shadow, and his eyes had the flat shine she'd seen once on a man who had watched his whole village burn and decided to keep walking anyway.

"Yes," she said. "How—"

"Later." He lay back down, putting his arm behind his head, ending the conversation with the finality of a dropped portcullis. But he didn't close his eyes, and neither, for a long time, did she.

* * *

Dawn, such as it was, arrived as a change in the quality of the dark. Ivo roused them, distributed bread and the hard mountain cheese that Ambrose claimed was a penance God intended for the proud, and led them onward and downward, because the route to the court beneath the mountain only went one direction in any meaningful sense.

The second day, the shrines were everywhere.

Every niche glowed now. Every cairn was stacked, every thread tied, every coin polished and set face-up. As they descended, the offerings changed too — older, stranger. A child's shoe, mended with great care. A wedding ring. A tooth. A small clay figure of a woman with her arms open, worn smooth by centuries of handling. And in one chamber, a shelf of little bells, dozens of them, green with age, arranged in a perfect descending scale — and when the draft from their passage stirred the air, they rang, all together, one clear chord that hung in the salt air for far longer than physics should have permitted.

Ansel stopped dead in the middle of the chamber.

"That's my song," he said.

Everyone turned. The boy stood with his fists clenched at his sides, white-faced, staring at the shelf of ancient bells. "The thing I hum. When I sleep. It's — that's it. Exactly it." His voice cracked. "Why do I know a song that's older than the temple? Why do I know it *better than they do?*"

"Because it knows you first," Ambrose said softly, and then looked appalled at himself, as if the theology had escaped before he could vet it.

Serel, who had been silent for two days in the manner of a drawn blade, finally spoke. "Can we move? Whatever sings down here, we are not paid to appreciate it."

"We're not paid at all," Ivo said. "You're blackmailing me, remember. Keep your genres straight."

Something flickered across Serel's face that in a healthier person might have been humor. "Move, Quintana."

Ivo moved. But as she passed the shelf of bells, the lowest one — the deepest note, the grandmother of the scale — swung gently on its hook, though there was no draft anymore, and gave a single soft stroke, like a nod. Like recognition.

She did not look back. Looking back, her grandmother had always said, is how you tell the dark you're interested.

* * *

On the third day they reached the Crossing of Nine Names, the junction where seven smugglers' routes met beneath a vault so high their lanterns couldn't find its ceiling, and Ivo called a halt because ahead of her, the road was wrong.

She knew this junction by heartbeat. There was the fallen pillar you climbed, moss-slick on the left side. There was the trickle of true clean water, the only safe drinking source in three miles, coming out of a crack shaped like a mouth. There was the wall where generations of smugglers had scratched their marks — initials, sigils, warnings, jokes — a palimpsest of the profession going back past the temple, past the kingdom, maybe past writing itself.

The pillar was there. The water was there. The wall was there.

But the marks on the wall had changed.

Ivo stood before it with her lantern held high and her blood going slow and cold, because she knew this wall. She had traced these scratches with her fingers as a child while her grandmother bargained ten feet away. She knew the anchor sigil of the Vane family, the crude phallus some long-dead wit had carved beside the warning *DON'T SING PAST THE THIRD BEND*, the neat account-keeper's tally of a man named Ferro who had marked every trip he ever made, four hundred and eleven strokes.

Ferro's tally now read four hundred and twelve.

The new stroke was fresh. The stone dust still clung below it, bright as flour.

"Odile," Ivo said, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "Come look at this."

The cartographer came, laid her palm against the wall, and read it the way she read everything. Her fingers traveled slowly. Then faster. Then they stopped, and she pulled her hand back as if the stone had bitten her, and she signed, rapid and urgent:

*Something has been adding. Recently. Hours. It walks here. It touches the wall. It counts.*

"Counts what?" Brannoc asked, and Ivo translated with her hands as best she could, badly, because her own were not quite steady.

Odile answered by pressing her palm flat to the ground and holding up the other hand, fingers spread, then folding them down one by one. Five. Then she pointed at each of them in turn. Ivo. Brannoc. Ambrose. Ansel. Herself. Serel — six, and Odile frowned, folded a sixth finger, then shook her head and held up five again, and looked at Serel with an expression Ivo couldn't parse and didn't love.

Then Odile knelt, and smoothed the dust on the floor with her sleeve, and wrote in it with one deliberate finger:

*IT KNOWS WE ARE HERE. IT IS NOT ANGRY. IT IS PATIENT. THAT IS WORSE.*

Above them, in the vaulting dark where no lantern reached, something exhaled — a long, slow movement of air that smelled of incense and cold iron and, absurdly, of the temple scriptorium, of ink and vellum and the dry sweet dust of old paper. Of home. Of the smell of every page Ivo had ever copied.

One. Two. Three. Four. Five.

Six beats of silence, precisely measured, where a sixth footstep should have been.

Then, far below, deep in the roots of Thornfell, in whatever court kept its books and its stolen voices, something dipped a pen — Ivo would swear until her dying day that she heard the scratch of nib on parchment, vast and intimate as a heartbeat — and began, with terrible care, to write their names.

Ansel's hand found hers in the dark. Small. Cold. Trembling exactly as much as hers was.

"Ivo," he whispered. "It spelled mine right."
