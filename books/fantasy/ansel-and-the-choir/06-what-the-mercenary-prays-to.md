## 6. What the Mercenary Prays To

Brannoc Vael had killed men for less than what the Abbot was paying him, and he had buried gods for nothing at all.

He told himself that second part was a figure of speech. He'd been telling himself that for eleven years, and it had never once taken root, because figures of speech didn't wake you at the third bell of the night with your hand already closed around a sword hilt and a word — *Haleth* — dying on your lips like a man's name instead of a god's.

The fire in the waystation was low. Rain came down on the smugglers' road like the mountain itself had decided to weep and couldn't stop, and under the lean-to roof the party slept in the uneven breathing of people who trusted each other exactly as far as they had to. The defrocked priest, Cassian, snored with the self-satisfaction of a man who had lost his faith but kept his opinions. Odile the cartographer slept curled around her satchel of charts like a hen around eggs, silent as she was when awake, which unsettled Brannoc more than any noise would have. Serel did not appear to sleep at all; she sat against the far post with her eyes half-lidded and her hands loose in her lap, and Brannoc had fought enough soldiers to know that posture. It wasn't rest. It was a drawn bow.

And Ivo Quintana sat up past the fire, turning a scrap of paper over and over in her fingers, looking at Ansel where the boy lay asleep by the coals.

The boy hummed in his sleep. That was the thing nobody wanted to say out loud. He hummed harmonies to songs no one was singing, and the flames leaned toward him like wheat toward wind, and every soul in this party pretended not to notice because noticing would mean deciding something.

Brannoc noticed. Brannoc always noticed. It was why he was still alive after twenty years of selling his sword to people whose causes he wouldn't have defended for free.

He crossed the camp without hurrying. A big man moving slow reads as harmless; a big man moving fast reads as trouble, and he had no quarrel with the scribe. Yet.

"You're staring at the boy," he said, low, settling onto the bench across from her.

Ivo didn't startle, which he gave her credit for. "I'm watching the fire."

"The fire's watching him back." He nodded at the flames, which bent again as the boy's sleeping hum climbed a note. "You've seen it. Everyone's seen it. Nobody's saying it because the Abbot's enforcer is sitting right there pretending to be furniture."

"Serel isn't pretending," Ivo said. "She's listening."

"To what?"

"To whether we say anything worth reporting." The scribe looked up at him then, and Brannoc saw what he'd been seeing since Thornfell: a woman built out of ink and caution, thin as a ledger line, with eyes that had done all their living indoors and resented it. "Why are you here, mercenary? Truly. You pray to Haleth before every meal. Haleth is a god of the old road-shrines. The temple of Saint Vellum considers that superstition at best and heresy at worst. They don't hire men like you for escort work. They hire men like you when they expect blood."

"They hired me because I'm cheap and I don't ask questions."

"You're asking one right now."

He almost smiled. Almost. "So are you."

* * *

They walked out into the rain together an hour later, because there were some conversations you couldn't have under Serel's half-closed eyes, and because Brannoc had decided — in the way decisions came to him, all at once and usually too late — that the scribe was the only person in this party who might understand what he had to say.

There was a shrine two hundred paces down the road. He knew there would be, because there were always shrines on the old roads, moss-eaten and forgotten, little stone mouths that had once been fed. This one was a waist-high cairn with a hollow at its heart, sheltered by a slab of rock, and rain ran off the slab in a beaded curtain so that the hollow inside stayed dry.

Inside the hollow was a carving. Weathered nearly smooth now, but Brannoc could have traced it blind: a traveler's staff, a loaf, a flame. The marks of Haleth, god of the long road and the shared fire.

He knelt in the mud. Not gracefully. His knees cracked like green wood, and the rain got down his collar immediately, and he didn't care.

"You still do this," Ivo said behind him. Not mocking. Wondering.

"Eleven years," Brannoc said, "since anyone answered."

He heard her go still. Scribes, he supposed, understood silence the way soldiers understood ground.

"I was twenty-six," he said, to the shrine, because it was easier than saying it to her face. "Caravan guard, southern passes. There were nine of us and forty wolves' worth of winter coming down early, and we stopped at a shrine like this one — bigger, better kept, back when they were better kept — and I made an offering. Half my pay that month. Bread and a copper flame-lamp, real silvered wick, the works. And I asked Haleth for one thing. Not victory. Not gold. Just that the fire keep. That's his whole bargain, see? You feed the flame, the flame feeds you. Road-god. Hearth-god. The god of *enough*."

"And?"

"And the fire kept." His voice came out flat as a blade laid on stone. "Nine of us walked out of that pass alive, warm every night, and I thought: there. Proof. I'd been raised on the temple's line that the old gods were stones and stories, but I had proof, I had a *kept* fire, and I prayed to Haleth every day for six years after. Every day. Offerings when I could afford them, words when I couldn't."

Ivo crouched beside him, out of the rain's reach, close enough that he could see her hands — ink-stained, chapped, a scribe's hands wrapped around each other like they were cold.

"What happened?" she asked.

"You know what happened. Everyone in Thornfell knows what happened, they just don't say it plain." He turned and looked at her, and let eleven years of swallowed grief come up rough and unpolished, because gruffness was just grief wearing armor and he was tired of the weight of it. "The court beneath the mountain ate him. Eleven years ago, midwinter. I felt it happen."

"Felt it—"

"Like a tooth pulled." He tapped his own chest, hard, twice. "Here. I was sitting at a fire outside Harrowmere, eating, and I said the blessing — *Haleth keep the flame, the flame keep us* — and the words went out of me and hit nothing. Nothing. You've thrown a stone your whole life and one morning you throw it and there's no ground. That's what it was. The prayer fell and fell and never landed. And I knew — not believed, *knew*, the way you know a bone's broken — that somewhere under that mountain, something enormous opened its mouth, and my god went down it, and the world didn't even get quieter. That's the worst part, scribe. The world didn't notice. Fires still lit. Bread still baked. Men still swore by him in taverns, swearing by a corpse and not knowing it. I checked the shrines for a year afterward. Fed every one I passed. Nothing. Silence like a held breath that never lets out."

The rain filled the pause between them, drumming on the slab.

"Why tell me this?" Ivo asked quietly.

"Because you keep that page." Her face changed and he pressed on before she could lie. "Don't bother denying it. I watch hands for a living. Yours shake when you touch your coat pocket, left side, and they don't shake any other time. Whatever's written there, it matters, and the temple doesn't know you kept it, or you'd have lost your indenture already. So you're hiding something from the Abbot. And tonight I watched you look at that boy like you were trying to solve him. Same as me."

"He hums," Ivo said. "In his sleep. Harmonies no one taught him. And the candles—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening.

"The candles bend toward him. Yes." Brannoc rose off his bad knee with a grunt. "Now here's the part I've been chewing since we left Thornfell, and you're going to hear it whether it's comfortable or not. Why does the Abbot want the boy retrieved? Retrieval means bringing him back. But Serel rides like a woman carrying orders, not hope. And the Abbot sent a defrocked priest, a deaf mapmaker, and a hired sword on a holy errand — that's not a rescue party, scribe, that's a party you can afford to lose. I've been on the other end of that arithmetic too many times not to recognize the shape of it."

"You think Ansel is bait."

"I think bait is the polite word." He wiped rain from his beard. "I think whatever woke beneath that mountain eleven years ago has been waking further ever since — the shrines glow now, you saw them on the tunnel road, don't tell me you didn't — and the temple wants to know what's stirring before it decides what to do about it. And a choirboy whose soul was sold to the court, humming harmonies that make fire lean? That's not a boy to them. That's a sounding line. Drop him down the dark and measure how deep it goes by how loud he screams."

Ivo had gone very pale. But she didn't argue, and Brannoc had spent twenty years learning the difference between people who argued and people who recalculated, and this woman recalculated.

"There's a page," she said finally, barely above the rain. "A prayer I copied. It was answered. In handwriting that wasn't mine. The temple hid it. I kept it. And I think — I *think* — it was Ansel's prayer. Written in his hand first, copied through mine somehow. The old gods answered him, Brannoc. Something under there talks back."

Something in Brannoc's chest turned over, slow and painful, like a wagon wheel finding a rut.

"Talks back," he repeated.

"That's what I said."

"Then hear me, and hear me clear." He stepped closer, rain streaming off him, and lowered his voice to the register he used for deathbeds and last stands. "If something down there answers prayers — if my god's voice is in that thing, woven into it, the way thread is woven into cloth — then every prayer that falls into that dark lands somewhere. On someone. Maybe on something that remembers being Haleth, the way a man remembers being young." His throat closed. He pushed through it. "I signed on to guard a retrieval. Fine. But I'll tell you what I'll actually do, and you can decide if you're with me. Nobody uses that boy as bait. Not the Abbot, not Serel, not the gods themselves. If it comes to choosing between the temple's errand and the boy's neck, I choose the neck. I've served enough masters who spent people like coin. I'm done being the coin *or* the purse."

Ivo looked up at him for a long moment. Rain ran off the shrine slab between them, a curtain of small silver threads.

"You'd defy the Abbot," she said. "You'd forfeit your pay. Possibly your life — Serel doesn't leave loose threads."

"I've been loose thread my whole career. It's why I'm still here." He hesitated, then said the true thing, the thing he'd carried eleven years like a stone in his boot: "And if there's even a chance that going down there — doing this right, keeping the boy free — gives me back one word of my god's voice... then there's no pay in the world that weighs against it. I'm not a believer anymore, scribe. I'm worse. I'm a mourner. And mourners dig."

* * *

They came back into camp separately, ten minutes apart, the way careful people do. The fire had been fed; Cassian muttered in his sleep about vestments; Odile's eyes were open in the dark, watching Brannoc with the flat attention of someone who mapped everything, including him. He met her gaze and inclined his head slightly, and after a moment she inclined hers back, and he understood that she had heard everything anyway — that deaf cartographers read the ground of a conversation through their feet and their eyes better than hearing men ever managed through their ears. Good. One more ally who hadn't needed convincing.

Serel hadn't moved. But as Brannoc settled by the fire, he caught it — quick, practiced, gone in a breath. Her hand slipping inside her coat to touch something flat and folded. Paper. A report half-written, maybe, waiting for the next crossroads and the next messenger heading back toward Thornfell.

Let her write, he thought grimly. Let her carry every word back to her master. By the time the Abbot read it, the shape of the game would already have changed.

Across the coals, Ivo had moved too. She sat nearer to Ansel now, near enough to touch, and when the boy whimpered in his sleep — a small sound, a child's sound, and beneath it that impossible harmony threading upward like smoke — her hand hovered over his shoulder and then, deliberately, came to rest.

The fire leaned toward them both.

And far below — so far down that no ear could have heard it, so deep that only a man who had once known a god's voice in his chest could feel it — something stirred. Not a word. Not yet. Just a pressure against the inside of the world, faint and vast and patient, like breath against the far side of a door.

Like a flame, somewhere in the dark, remembering how to keep.

Brannoc closed his eyes and, for the first time in eleven years, prayed anyway — not asking, just speaking into the dark the way you speak at a grave, in case the dead can hear.

Tomorrow, the road bent toward the high villages. Tomorrow, the mountain began counting their footsteps in earnest.

Tonight, for the first time since midwinter eleven years ago, the dark felt less like a mouth.

It felt like a choir, holding its place, waiting for the downbeat.
