## 7. The Village That Forgot Its Name

The road down into the valley smelled of bread.

That was the first wrong thing. Mountain villages in late autumn smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and the sour tang of animals kept too close through winter. They did not smell of fresh-baked bread at midmorning on a day when no feast-day bell had rung anywhere in Thornfell. Ivo stopped at the lip of the pass with her hand raised, and behind her the party bunched up like sheep sensing a dog they couldn't see yet.

"Harvest's in," Brannoc said, sniffing. "They're baking the surplus before it spoils."

"Harvest was three weeks ago," Ivo said. "I've walked this road since I was nine. The barley comes in late here — the valley floor holds frost until nearly summer. They'd still be threshing."

Odile tilted her head, one hand flat against her cart's rail, reading the ground the way she read everything: through her palms, through the soles of her boots, through some private grammar of vibration that Ivo had given up trying to learn. After a moment she signed, quick and economical: *Too quiet. No dogs.*

She was right. Ivo listened past the smell of bread and found what should have been underneath it: geese, chickens, a dog or two, children. A village this size — forty roofs, maybe fifty, terraced down the hillside like crockery on a shelf — should have been audible from half a mile off. Instead there was only the wind, the creak of their own cart wheels, and beneath it all, faint as a pulse under a wrist, a hum. Low. Layered. Many voices braided into one sustained note, like the drone-pipe of an organ that never stopped.

Father Aldous heard it too. She saw it land on him — the defrocked priest went pale around the mouth and gripped his walking staff like a rosary he'd forgotten how to use.

"That's not singing," he said. "That's *holding*."

"What do you mean, holding?" Serel asked. The enforcer stood at the front of the party as she always did, positioned where she could see both the road and everyone behind her, and she said everything the way she did everything: without any visible expenditure of feeling.

"Holding a note." Aldous swallowed. "In the old rites — the ones Saint Vellum's people burned out of these mountains — a congregation would hold a single tone for hours. Days, sometimes. Not to praise anything. To *hold* something. Keep it in place. Like a lid on a pot." He looked down the hillside at the smoking chimneys, the golden fields, the bread-smell drifting up like an apology. "Someone is holding something down in that village. Or something is being held."

"Or," Serel said, "they've got food, warm fires, and full granaries, and we've got hardtack and a schedule. We go through, we resupply, we move on. That's what roads are for."

"We don't know what we'd be walking into."

"No," Serel agreed. "We never do. That's why we keep moving."

Ivo looked at the village and felt the hum settle into her back teeth. She had grown up two valleys over, in a village much like this one, and she knew its grammar the way she knew the smugglers' tunnels: which doors faced away from the wind, which fields were left fallow out of superstition rather than sense, where the shrine used to be before the temple men came through with hammers and a ledger book. This village had once had a name. Every village did. It would have been carved over the well or painted on the meeting-house beam, something old and local and slightly embarrassing, like *Bitterroot* or *Undermill* or *Nine Sisters*. And standing here now, straining for it, Ivo realized she couldn't remember ever hearing it. Not because she'd never known. Because somewhere between the pass and the valley floor, the name had slid out of her head like water off oilcloth.

"Ansel," she said quietly.

The choirboy looked up from the cart bed where he'd been dozing against the grain sacks. He was thinner than when they'd left the temple, sleepless in a way none of them discussed, with shadows under his eyes like thumbprints in dough. But his voice, when he answered, was steady. "Yes?"

"Do you know what that village is called?"

He frowned. He looked. Ivo watched him look — watched his lips move as if tasting a word that wasn't there, watched his brow crease with the particular frustration of reaching for a thing in a pocket that has been sewn shut. "It had one," he said slowly. "There was a sign. At the crossroads, down there. Painted letters. I can almost—" He shook his head. "It's like the word is behind glass. I can see the shape of it."

*Behind glass.* Ivo's stomach turned over, slow and cold. She thought of the page in the scriptorium, the prayer answered in handwriting not her own. She thought of the Abbot's ledger, the neat column of debts, Quintana written in ink that had dried decades ago. She thought of her grandmother, whom she could no longer picture clearly even before the Bridge of Signed Names took the last of her face — and now, after the toll, after the seal burned into the empty space where the memory had been, Ivo carried her grandmother the way Ansel carried this village's name: as a shape, a weight, a thing behind glass.

Debts, she was learning, were not paid in coin. They were paid in pieces, and the pieces were always things you loved.

"We're going down," she said.

Serel turned. For a moment the two women simply regarded each other, and Ivo understood — not for the first time — that Serel was not cruel. Cruelty required heat, and there was nothing hot about her. She was arithmetic wearing armor. "We have four days of food and a window that closes when the deep roads flood with meltwater. Every hour we spend—"

"You want to walk into a village that can't tell us who it is, take what we need from people who won't know they gave it, and leave?" Ivo kept her voice level. It cost her. "You want to add our names to whatever ledger did this?"

"I want Ansel delivered alive," Serel said, "which is the entire purpose of this expedition, and which becomes less likely every time we stop to feel things." Her eyes flicked to Ansel, then away, dismissive as a clerk closing a folder. "The boy is cargo. Cargo doesn't get a vote, and neither does the guide."

"The guide," Ivo said, "knows what happens to villages that trade with the court beneath the mountain. My grandmother's village traded. Do you know what we paid? Do you know what's *left* of what we paid?" She heard her own voice crack on the last word and hated it, and pressed on anyway. "You think a good harvest is cheap. It isn't. It's just priced in something you haven't learned to count yet."

For a long moment nobody spoke. The hum rose faintly from the valley, patient as water finding a level.

Then Odile stepped forward and set her palm flat against the cart rail, and signed one word, deliberate enough that even Serel could follow it:

*Stay.*

Brannoc cracked his knuckles, which was his way of voting. Aldous murmured something that might have been a prayer to a god who no longer answered, or might have been surrender. And Ansel — thin, sleepless, marked by every power in Thornfell like a tree struck twice by lightning — climbed down off the cart and stood beside Ivo, and said nothing at all, which was somehow the loudest vote of them all.

Serel looked at each of them in turn, tallying. Then she said, "One night," and turned down the road, and Ivo understood that the enforcer had not conceded anything. She had simply recorded the loss and moved the ledger forward.

* * *

The village welcomed them like family.

That was the second wrong thing, and it was worse than the silence, because it was warm. Doors opened before they knocked. Bread appeared — dark rye, dense with seeds, still warm — along with butter and honey and a cider so sharp it made Brannoc's eyes water. The villagers pressed food on them with both hands, laughing, touching their sleeves, asking nothing. Asking *nothing*. Ivo had traveled enough to know that hospitality in poor places was a transaction: kindness now, stories later, news of the lowlands traded for salt. These people wanted no stories. They didn't seem to understand that strangers *had* stories. They smiled at Ivo the way you smile at someone whose face you almost know from somewhere — a market, a funeral, a dream — and the smile never quite resolved into recognition, and it never quite fell away either.

An old woman sat Ivo down at her hearth and fed her soup and told her, twice, the story of the harvest. How the barley had come in heavy for the first time in living memory. How the apples had hung so thick the branches bowed. How the winter stores were full, fuller than full, fuller than the oldest songs promised, and wasn't it wonderful, wasn't it blessed, wasn't it—

"Grandmother," Ivo said gently, using the word the way mountain folk used it, for any elder, "what did you give for it?"

The old woman smiled at her. It was a lovely smile. It reached all the way to her eyes, and there was nothing behind it but warmth, and that was the most frightening thing Ivo had ever seen on a human face.

"Give?" the woman said. "The mountain gives. We thank it."

"How do you thank it?"

"We sing," she said, and touched her own throat with something like wonder, as if the gesture surprised her. "All together. One note. We hold it, and the mountain holds us. Everyone knows that song. Don't you know that song?"

And the terrible thing — the thing Ivo would carry out of that valley like a stone in her boot — was that for one dizzying heartbeat, she *did* know the song. The shape of it rose in her, the way the village's name had risen in Ansel: a melody with the middle scooped out, a tune made entirely of edges. She knew where her mouth would open. She knew the pitch. She knew that if she opened her mouth right now, the note would come out of her perfect and whole, and she would not be able to stop singing it until someone else decided she could.

She clamped her jaw shut and stood up so fast the bench scraped.

Outside, the light was going amber, and the villagers were gathering in the square by the well, and the hum was rising.

* * *

"They sing at dusk," Aldous reported, coming back from the well with his face gray. "Every dusk. All of them. The whole village, one note, held until full dark. I asked a man what happens if they stop." He wiped his mouth. "He looked at me like I'd asked what happens if they stop breathing. He didn't *know*. He couldn't remember why they started. None of them can. They just know they mustn't stop."

"Because if they stop," Ivo said, "the thing they sold gets taken back. With interest."

They stood in the loft of a barn the villagers had offered them — offered everything, offered the beds out from under themselves, and had to be physically prevented from doing it by Brannoc planting himself in doorways like furniture. Below, through gaps in the boards, they could see the square filling. Families arriving with lamps. Faces upturned. Forty, fifty people arranging themselves in rings, oldest at the center, youngest at the rim, with the unconscious precision of iron filings around a magnet.

Serel stood apart, arms crossed, watching the gathering with those cold ledger-keeper's eyes. "So the debt collects daily," she said. "One note a night. Cheap, as these things go."

"It's not cheap," Ivo said. "Look at them. Really look."

Serel looked. Ivo watched her look, and watched the looking find nothing — because on the surface, nothing was wrong. The villagers were healthy. Fed. Warm. Smiling. The debt had taken their memory of the transaction and left the profit visible, and to a mind like Serel's, trained by the temple to weigh ledgers and call the balance justice, it probably looked like a fair trade. Grain for a name. Apples for a few evenings of singing.

But Ivo had spent her life copying prayers, and prayers are made of names, and she knew what a name was worth. It wasn't a word. It was the handle by which a community lifted itself. Without it, the village couldn't tell its own story, couldn't grieve its own dead properly, couldn't teach its children who they came from — and she could see that already, see it in the way the young ones looked at the old ones: fond, obedient, and utterly unanchored. Children who would grow up knowing they belonged to this place without ever knowing *what* they belonged to. A root cut off below the ground, still green above, still bearing fruit.

Full barns. Empty shelves inside.

"They'll forget more," she said aloud. "This is year one, or maybe year five — I can't tell, and neither can they. But debts compound. Next it'll be the reason they settled here. Then the faces of their dead. Then—" she gestured at the gathering rings, the lamps, the upturned faces beginning, softly, terribly, to hum, "—then it stops mattering what they forgot, because they won't be able to miss it. You can't grieve a hole if you don't know it's a hole. That's the real price. Not the note. The *not missing*."

Something moved across Serel's face then — not softness, nothing so useful as softness, but a flicker of recalculation, like an abacus bead shifting. She said nothing. She turned back to the window.

Below, the whole village found the note.

It came up through the floorboards and into Ivo's chest, into her teeth, into the hollow behind her sternum, and it was beautiful, and that was the worst part. Fifty voices, one tone, held with the effortless unity of a single throat, and threaded through it — under it, around it, like wire through wax — the other sound. The court's sound. The sound of many souls woven into one singing thing, patient and vast and *satisfied*, drinking the evening in through fifty open mouths.

Beside her, Ansel had gone rigid.

Ivo turned. The choirboy stood at the loft's edge with his hands gripping the rail, and his lips had parted, and his chest had begun to rise for breath, and his eyes were wide and fixed on nothing — on something far below the village, below the valley, below the roots of the mountain itself.

"Don't," Ivo whispered, and caught his arm.

His skin was cold. His mouth shaped the first edge of the note — she could feel the harmony assembling in him, feel the pull of it through her grip on his sleeve, the same pull she'd felt at the well of her own memory, the court calling in a voice that sounded exactly like belonging. His soul was collateral. Of course it could hear the collection plate passing.

"*Don't*," she said again, sharper, and hauled him back from the rail, and held him by both shoulders in the lamplight, and watched the glaze recede from his eyes by degrees, like frost melting off a pane.

When he could speak, his voice came out small and scraped. "It knows me," he said. "Ivo. It wasn't humming at the village." His hands found her wrists and gripped hard. "It was humming at *me*. It's been humming since the pass. It's counting down to something, and I think—" He swallowed. "I think tonight, when they finish singing, I'm going to dream my way down again. And this time I don't think I'll wake up on my own."

Out in the dark, the village held its note, and the mountain held the village, and somewhere far beneath them both, something vast and patient turned over in its sleep and began, very softly, to harmonize.
