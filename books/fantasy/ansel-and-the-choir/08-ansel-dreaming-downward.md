## 8. Ansel, Dreaming Downward

Ansel had always known that sleep was a door. He simply hadn't known, until the night the candles began to gutter in time with his humming, which side of it he was standing on.

He fell asleep in the novices' dormitory with his boots half off and Ivo's stolen page folded inside his shirt — the prayer she'd copied out in her careful temple hand, the one that had answered itself back in handwriting like frost on a window. He wasn't supposed to have it. He wasn't supposed to know about it at all. But Ivo had pressed it into his hands behind the reliquary that afternoon with a look he couldn't read, and now the paper lay against his sternum like a second heartbeat, and sleep came for him the way water comes for a drain.

The door opened downward.

He knew this the way you know things in dreams: completely and without evidence. The floor of the dormitory became a stair, and the stair became a road, and the road went down through stone that was warm as skin. There were no walls exactly, only a suggestion of them, carved — or grown — with galleries of niches, and in each niche something glowed faintly, the way coals glow after the fire has been declared dead. He walked without deciding to walk. His feet made no sound, but the mountain heard them anyway. He could feel it listening, the way you can feel a hand hovering an inch above your hair.

*Counting,* said some patient part of his mind. *It's counting your footsteps.*

He looked down at his own feet to see if they were real. They were. His choir shoes, scuffed at the toes, one lace trailing. That detail steadied him more than any prayer ever had. Whatever this place was, it had let him bring himself into it — all of himself, down to the fraying lace — and a place that wanted all of you was either very kind or very hungry.

The road opened into a cavern so vast that his eyes refused its edges. Stalactites hung like the pipes of an organ built by something that had never seen an organ but had been told about music by someone weeping. And beneath them, filling the dark the way water fills a bell —

— the singing.

Ansel stopped breathing. Not from fear. From professional courtesy. He was, whatever else he was, a choirboy of Saint Vellum, and there are sounds before which even a trained throat goes still, the way a crowd goes still when the king enters. This was one. It came from everywhere and from one direction at once, a chord with no beginning, voices braided so tightly together that they had stopped being voices and become a single fabric of sound — a thing that sang the way a river runs, not because it chooses to but because it is what it is.

He should have been afraid. He noted, distantly, that he would need to be afraid later, and filed the feeling away like a boy setting aside his supper because there is work to do.

He walked toward it.

The galleries thickened as he went. The glowing niches resolved, and he saw what they held, and his stomach turned over slowly, gently, like a boat in a swell. Faces. Not skulls — faces, small as coins, each one lit from within, each one moving its lips. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. They lined the walls of the deep road the way saints line a cathedral, except that saints do not sing, and these did, every one of them, in a whisper pitched just below hearing, and every whisper was a thread in the great braid of sound ahead.

One face turned toward him as he passed. A man's face, seamed and tired, with a farmer's sunburn preserved in its glow. Its lips moved, and though the Choir swallowed most words before they could become words, this one reached Ansel whole, the way a single note can escape a cathedral through an open door:

*Cold,* it said. *It's cold where they keep my name.*

Ansel's step faltered. Another face caught at him — a woman, young, her glow flickering like a candle in a draft — and another, an old man whose lips shaped a child's name over and over, a name Ansel didn't know and somehow grieved. None of them could turn their heads. None of them could close their eyes. They could only sing, because that was what they were for now. They had been woven in.

*They're not dead,* he thought, and the thought arrived already knowing it was true, the way thoughts do here. *Dead would be kinder.*

Ahead, the cavern widened into the heart of the mountain, and the Choir was there.

He had expected — he didn't know what. A throne. A monster. Something with a face you could hate. Instead there was a shape made entirely of singing: a vast slow turning mass of light and voice, like a galaxy seen from inside, like a storm of moths if every moth were a soul and every wingbeat a note. It filled the chamber floor to ceiling and did not touch either. It turned, and as it turned, threads of it unwound and rewove, faces surfacing and submerging, harmonies folding into harmonies, and the whole of it was so beautiful that Ansel felt tears start down his face before he understood he was crying.

It was the most lovely thing he had ever heard, and every thread of it was a person who could not stop.

"Hello," Ansel said. His voice came out small and ordinary, a boy's voice in a cave, and the Choir faltered.

Not stopped — faltered. A ripple went through it, the way wind goes through wheat, a shiver of attention passing from thread to thread until the entire vast singing thing had turned, in whatever way a thing without a body turns, to look at him.

And then it *wanted* him.

He felt it the way you feel sunlight through glass — warmth arriving from somewhere you cannot go. Want rolled off the Choir in waves, and the want was not cruel, that was the terrible part. It was the wanting of a lonely thing. It was the wanting of a table set for ten thousand with only ten thousand chairs and always room for one more. It poured over him like warm water and said, in a chorus of every voice it contained, in a harmony that used his own pitch, his own range, his own sweet unbroken treble as one of its strands:

*Come in. Come in. We have been missing a note. We have been missing you since before you were born. Come in and be finished. Come in and never be afraid again. Here there is no cold and no hunger and no morning you must get up for. Here you will only ever be singing.*

And oh — oh, it was tempting. That was the horror under the horror. Ansel stood in the dark with tears on his face and felt the offer settle over him like a blanket in winter, and some worn-out corner of himself, the corner that got up before dawn to scrub the chapel floor, the corner that flinched when the Abbot's footsteps passed, that corner leaned toward the Choir like a plant toward a window.

*You wouldn't have to be Ansel anymore,* the Choir sang, tenderly, coaxingly. *Being Ansel hurts. We know. We remember hurting. Give us the hurt. Keep only the song.*

"No," Ansel said.

His voice cracked on it, which was fitting; everything in his life cracked on the important words. But he said it again, steadier. "No. Thank you. I'm — I'm wanted somewhere else too. Upstairs. Someone kept a page for me."

The Choir rippled. The warmth dimmed by a degree, and beneath it he felt something else move, something older and colder that lived deeper in the singing than the loneliness did — a patience like bedrock. The warmth came back immediately, smoother now, more practiced, and Ansel understood with a lurch that he had just watched a very large thing decide to be gentle on purpose.

*You don't have to choose tonight,* it sang. *We are very old. We can wait longer than you can stay awake. And you will come back, little note. You come back every time you sleep. Every night the door opens downward. Every night you walk a little farther. One night you will walk all the way, and you will not even notice you've decided.*

"That's not choosing," Ansel whispered. "That's just — wearing me down."

*Yes,* said the Choir, with no shame at all, the way a river has no shame about the sea. *That is how all deep things are done.*

He backed away. His heels found the road and he walked backward up it, past the niches, past the coin-sized faces, and the faces watched him go, and some of their whispers changed. He couldn't make out the words at first. Then, near the top of the gallery, one voice cut through the braid of the Choir the way a knife cuts cloth — one voice alone, ragged, urgent, a woman's voice gone rough with years and smoke and something that might once have been laughter.

*Boy.* It said. *Boy. Look at me.*

A niche at eye level held a face unlike the others. Where the rest glowed steady, hers guttered, fighting its own light. She was old — old the way trees are old, weathered and bent and still standing — and her eyes, the only eyes in all that wall that could still move, fixed on him with a ferocity that stopped his breath.

"I know you," Ansel said stupidly. "Do I know you?"

*You will,* she said. *Or you did. It's hard to keep it straight down here — they take the straight parts first. Listen to me, boy, and don't sing along, whatever it offers, don't you dare sing along. My name was Maren Quintana when I had one. I sold something at the mountain's mouth a long time ago, and the price came due wrong, and now there's a girl upstairs with my chin and my temper copying prayers for men who don't believe in them—*

"You're Ivo's grandmother," Ansel breathed.

Something moved through the old face that was almost a smile and mostly grief. *She told you? No — no, she wouldn't have. You guessed. Clever boy. Stubborn. Good.* Her glow surged, guttered, steadied. *Listen. It wants you because you're not only yours. There's a weave in you, a rite-woven soul, bought and paid for, and the paper is still wet after forty years because debts down here don't dry. As long as the contract stands, every dream pulls you one step lower. So run, boy. Wake up. Climb. And tell my granddaughter—*

The Choir noticed.

Ansel felt it happen — felt the great warm attention swing back toward the gallery like a searchlight, felt the temperature of the whole cavern drop by a degree that had nothing to do with air. The Choir's voice, when it came again, was still gentle. That was the worst thing yet. It was gentle the way a hand coming down on the back of your neck is gentle.

*Grandmother,* it sang, in a hundred stolen voices at once, in a fondness like syrup over teeth. *You know the rules. The threads do not speak to the unwoven.*

*I have never once,* said Maren Quintana, in a voice like a door slamming in a house that had been quiet for forty years, *followed your rules.*

Her niche flared white. For one impossible moment the whole gallery blazed — every face lit at once, every whisper rising off its leash into something that was almost, briefly, a shout — and in that blaze of borrowed rebellion the old woman spent herself like a candle spending its last inch, and her voice reached Ansel whole and burning:

*RUN.*

He ran.

The road unspooled upward, stone warm as skin, galleries streaming past, and behind him the Choir began to sing again — but differently now, no longer the lonely lullaby. This was a working song, a weaving song, and he could feel it taking hold of his sleeping body somewhere far above, threading into his breath, setting his heart to its tempo. His legs grew heavy. The stairs lengthened. Each step lifted him less. *Every night you walk a little farther,* it had said, and now he understood: it wasn't waiting for him to choose. It was reeling him in the way a fisherman reels, slowly, patiently, letting the line sing.

His grandmother's voice was fading behind him, thinned to a thread, and the last thing he heard of her was not a word at all but a hum — three notes, low and rough and familiar, a tune he suddenly realized he had been humming in his sleep for weeks, the harmony no one taught him. She had been teaching him in his dreams. All this time, down here in the dark, she had been slipping him the one song the Choir didn't own.

He grabbed it. He threw it up ahead of himself like a rope.

And woke.

* * *

He woke gasping in the dormitory, tangled in his blanket, dawn grey at the shutters, and the first thing he did was put his hand flat on his own chest to check that his heart was keeping its own time. It was. Barely. It stumbled like a runner crossing a finish line.

Around him the other novices slept on. Brother Aldous snored. Somewhere a rooster made its idiot announcement. The world upstairs, ordinary and chipped and smelling of tallow and floor soap, and Ansel lay in it shaking with relief and loss in equal measure, because he had left something down there. He had left her. He had left ten thousand of them.

*Ivo,* he thought. *I have to find Ivo.*

Because he remembered — dream-memory, whole and undissolved, which meant she'd gotten through, which meant it had cost her — the last of his grandmother's message, the part that had come after *run*, the part the Choir's weaving had nearly smoothed away before he caught it and held it:

*Tell my granddaughter the debt has two names on it. Tell her to look at the seal. Tell her the mountain keeps what's signed, and she is signed.*

Ansel sat up in the cold dormitory, hugged his knees, and did the bravest cowardly thing he knew: he stayed in bed, safe and awake, and waited for the one person in Thornfell who would believe him — because she already had the proof folded inside her shirt.

By the time the bells rang for prime, he had made a decision that would have horrified the Abbot: tonight, when the door opened downward again, he was not going to run.

He was going to bring a map.
