## 9. The Avatar at the Shrine of Nine Bells

The shrine announced itself before they saw it.

They had been walking since the false dawn through a corridor of the mountain that smelled of wet iron and old candle smoke, and then the air changed — thickened — the way air changes just before a choir inhales. Ivo felt it in her teeth first, then in the scarred calluses of her writing hand, which had begun, over the past days, to ache whenever something holy came near. A scribe's hand knows ink. It should not know divinity. And yet there it was: a low, resonant hum threading up through the stone, through the soles of her boots, through the marrow of her, as if the mountain itself had been struck like a bell and had not yet finished ringing.

"Nine bells," Odile signed, her hands quick and certain in the gloom. She could not hear the hum, but she could feel it — she had told them once, in the flat practical way she communicated everything, that the mountain's deep places were *loud*, that vibration was simply sound wearing different clothes. Her fingers stilled. Her eyes went wide. Then, slowly, she pressed both palms flat against the tunnel wall and stood there like a woman listening to a heartbeat through a door.

"What is it?" Brannoc asked. His voice was pitched low, the way it always was now, ever since he'd confessed what had happened to his god. He kept one hand on his axe out of habit rather than threat; Ivo had noticed he'd stopped sharpening it three days ago, as if some part of him no longer believed in the usefulness of edges.

Odile pulled back from the wall. Her signing was slower this time, deliberate, each word placed like a stone in a wall. *Bells. Nine of them. But they are not ringing separately. They are ringing as one voice.*

"That's not how bells work," said Cassian, the defrocked priest, who had spent the morning complaining about his boots and the afternoon complaining about the dark, and who complained, Ivo suspected, because silence let him think about the collar he no longer wore.

*That,* Odile signed, with something almost like wonder, *is exactly what I mean.*

The tunnel opened without warning. One moment they were walking between walls close enough to touch with both elbows; the next, the stone fell away and they stood at the lip of a cavern so vast that Ivo's scribe's mind, unhelpfully, began calculating how many pages it would take to describe it and concluded that all the vellum in Saint Vellum's scriptorium would not suffice.

The shrine sat in its center on an island of pale rock, and it was — at first glance — modest. A ring of nine bronze bells hung from a frame of black wood gone silver with age, each bell taller than a man, their mouths open toward the sky of the cavern where no sky should have been. Stalactites hung above them like the fingers of a buried giant, and water dripped from their tips in slow, patient intervals, though no drop ever seemed to reach the bells. The drops fell, and vanished, and the bells rang anyway — softly, softly, a sound less heard than remembered.

Around the ring of bells lay offerings. That was the word Ivo's temple-trained eye supplied, and then immediately rejected. Offerings at Saint Vellum were bread and coin and candles, things given by the living to gods who never took them. These were different. These were *faces*. Hundreds of them, pressed thin as communion wafers and laid in careful spirals around the bell-frame: faces of wax, faces of bark, faces of clay and leaf and riverstone, each one distinct, each one unmistakably someone. An old man laughing. A girl mid-song. A soldier with half his jaw shot away, rendered in gray clay with such tenderness that Ivo had to look away.

"They're votives," Cassian whispered. "Pre-temple votives. This is older than Saint Vellum. Older than the *faith* of Saint Vellum." He crossed himself with the old sign, the double-bar one the temple had forbidden two centuries ago, and did not seem to notice he'd done it.

Serel stood at the edge of the light, and Ivo watched her the way she always watched Serel: sideways, obliquely, the way one watches a blade on a table. The enforcer's face showed nothing. It never showed anything. But her hand rested on the hilt of her short sword, and her weight was forward on her toes, and Ivo had learned enough of Serel's grammar by now to read the sentence: *I do not like what I cannot arrest.*

Ansel stepped past them all.

He had been quiet for two days — quieter than even his usual dream-haunted quiet — and when he walked toward the bells, barefoot, having removed his boots without anyone seeing him do it, none of them moved to stop him. There was something in the way he moved that made stopping him feel like interrupting a prayer. The humming had started again, the harmonies no one had taught him, and as he approached the shrine the nine bells answered him, each one sounding a note that slid beneath his voice and held it up, the way a mother's hands hold up a child learning to stand.

"No," Ivo said, and her own voice startled her with its roughness. "Ansel. Wait."

He turned. In the bell-light — for the bells were glowing now, a warm bronze luminescence that had no source and needed none — his face looked older and younger at once, the way faces do in dreams. "It knows me," he said simply. "Ivo, it *knows me.*"

"That's what frightens me."

And then the avatar arrived.

There was no dramatic entrance. That was the worst of it. One moment the space among the bells was empty; the next, a woman stood there, as if she had always stood there, as if they had simply failed until now to notice her. She was tall and dressed in robes the color of tarnished bronze, and her face —

Her face was wrong. Not ugly-wrong. *Crowded*-wrong. As Ivo watched, the features shifted: a broad peasant jaw gave way to a fine-boned noble cheekbone gave way to the laughing old man from the clay spirals gave way to a face Ivo knew, a face that made her stomach drop through the floor of the world —

Her grandmother's face.

The avatar smiled with Ivo's grandmother's mouth. "Scribe," it said, in a voice made of struck metal and memory, of every bell that had ever rung in Thornfell layered one atop another. "You flinch. Forgive me. I forget whose faces I am wearing. They accumulate." The features rippled, settled, became something neutral and unfinished, a face like a page waiting for ink. "I am Vesh. Or rather, I am what Vesh can afford to send. The god himself is — thin, these days. Starved. Waking slowly, the way an old man wakes in winter."

"You're wearing my grandmother's face," Ivo said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her, because inside she was anything but.

"I am wearing nine hundred years of faces. Your grandmother's was among them. She prayed here once." The avatar tilted its borrowed head. "Did you know that? No. Of course not. The temple burned the records. The temple burns everything it cannot tax."

Brannoc moved up beside Ivo, axe forgotten, hands loose and ready. "What is it?" he asked her, quietly. "What are we looking at?"

"A god," said Cassian, and his voice cracked down the middle. "Or the skirt-hem of one. Saints preserve us, it's real, they're *real*, the old ones — we were taught they were stories the mountain told about itself—"

"We are real," the avatar agreed pleasantly. "And we are hungry. And we are waking. And you—" its unfinished face turned, taking in each of them in turn, lingering longest on Ansel, who stood among the bells with his head tipped back like a man listening to rain after a drought, "—have brought me the most interesting thing to enter this shrine in four hundred years."

Ivo's blood went cold. "We haven't brought you anything. We're taking him *back*. From the court beneath the mountain. His soul was sold—"

"Yes," said the avatar. "We know. We wrote the rite."

The words landed in the cavern and rang there, echoing off stone, off bell-metal, off the spiral fields of borrowed faces.

"The *what*?" Serel spoke for the first time. Her voice was flat as a ledger line.

"The soul-binding rite your temple uses — used — for its debts beneath the mountain." The avatar drifted closer to Ansel, and the bells leaned toward him, all nine, like flowers toward sun. "Your Abbot believes his church invented those contracts. Charming. The temple has been laundering our liturgy for centuries. Every soul sold to the court below was bound with *our* knot, sealed with *our* names, sung shut with *our* music. The court merely holds what we tied." Its voice softened, and the softness was worse than the grandeur. "The boy's soul is woven from our thread, scribe. Can you truly not hear it? He hums in his sleep because the Choir taught him the song before he had teeth. He is not merely sold to the deep places. He is *made* of them. Half-made. A door half-built."

"Door," Ivo repeated. The word tasted like copper.

"The old gods are waking, and we are locked out of our own mountain." The avatar spread its hands, and Ivo saw that its palms were stamped with seals — dozens of them, overlapping, contracts rendered in skin. "The court beneath took our voices, one by one, across the centuries. Bargain by bargain. Every farmer who traded his harvest-song. Every village that sold its name. Every soul your temple shipped downward to settle its accounts. They built themselves a Choir out of what they stole, and the Choir sings so loud, so *continuously*, that we cannot get a single note of ourselves back into the world. We wake, and we press against the door, and the door does not open, because the door is made of stolen things and stolen things only open inward."

"And the boy?" Brannoc asked. His gruffness had gone strange and careful.

"The boy is the hinge." The avatar's face shifted again — briefly, horribly, into Ansel's own face, young and open and terrified, before sliding away. "A soul woven partly from our rite, still wearing flesh, still *choosing* — do you understand what that is? He is a note the court does not yet hold. If he walks down into the court whole, he becomes theirs, another theft, another brick in the wall. But if he comes to us first — if he stands in this shrine and lets us finish what was begun — he becomes a door that opens *outward*. Through him, we return. Through him, the Choir hears our true song again, and the stolen voices remember they were once separate, once *free*, and rise."

"It sounds beautiful," Ivo said slowly, "when you say it like that."

"When I say it like that," the avatar agreed, "it is because it is largely true. Gods are not liars, scribe. We are worse. We are *sincere*."

"Then say the rest of it."

The unfinished face regarded her. Around them the bells had gone silent, all nine, hanging still as executed men.

"The rest of it," the avatar said, "is that the door swings both ways. To open outward for us, it must first open inward. The boy would be our passage — our vessel, our threshold. He would carry us into the world, and the carrying would change him. He would never be only Ansel again. He would be Ansel-and-the-choir-of-Vesh, forever, a house with a god living in the attic." A pause, delicate as a knife laid down. "He would belong to us instead of the court. We would be gentler owners. But we would be owners."

The silence that followed had texture. Ivo could feel it against her skin like wet wool.

She looked at Ansel. He stood among the bells with his arms wrapped around himself, and his face — his actual face, the one that was only his — was doing the thing it did when he was trying very hard to be brave in front of adults: chin up, eyes too bright, jaw set. Thirteen years old. Thirteen years old and everyone in this cavern, herself included, had been discussing him like a shipment.

"He doesn't want to be a door," Ivo said. "He wants to be a boy. He wants—" she groped for it, and found it, and it hurt, "—he wants someone to ask him."

"I am asking," said the avatar.

"You're telling. There's a difference. My grandmother—" the name caught in her throat, snagged on the absence the tollkeeper's bridge had left there, the hole where a face used to be, "—my grandmother taught me the difference, I think, though I can't remember her face anymore to ask her. Asking means you can take no for an answer."

For a long moment the avatar said nothing. The borrowed faces in the spirals around the shrine seemed, in the bell-light, to watch.

"You are more dangerous than your Abbot," it said at last, and there was something in the bell-voice that might have been respect. "He sees a commodity. You see a boy. That will make everything harder." It turned toward Ansel and inclined its head — actually bowed, slightly, a god's avatar bowing to a choirboy. "I will not take him. Not today. Not by force. Understand what that costs me, scribe — understand what it costs a starving god to leave food standing on the table. But hear this also, and carry it down into the dark with you: the court beneath the mountain knows you are coming. It has known since the night the boy first hummed. And the court does not ask. The court does not bow. Whatever you believe you are stealing him back *from* — ask yourselves, on the road, whether you are not simply thieves working for another thief. Whether any of you — any of *us* — has clean hands in this mountain."

The bells shivered. The avatar's outline frayed, thinned, became a column of resonant air shaped like a woman, and then shapes like nothing at all.

"I will be watching," said the voice of nine hundred bells, from everywhere. "So will the others. We are all awake now, little scribe. Pray you prove worth the watching."

Then it was only a shrine again — old bronze, dead faces, dripping stone — and the cold, and the dark, and the long road downward still waiting.

* * *

They made camp in the tunnel mouth above the shrine, because descending further in the dark felt like walking into a mouth, and even Serel conceded the point. No fire. Hard bread. The nine bells far below glowed faintly through the dark like drowned lanterns.

Ansel sat against the wall with his knees drawn up. Ivo went and sat beside him, because someone had to, and because — she admitted it to herself with the reluctance of a woman opening a bill — she wanted to.

"You could say yes to it," she said quietly. "To Vesh. It might be better than the court. It *said* it would be better."

"I know." He picked at the crust of his bread. "Everyone keeps telling me what I might be. Door. Vessel. Payment. Asset." He glanced at her, and there was an old-soul dryness in it that always undid her a little. "You're the first one who asked what I want."

"What do you want?"

"To go home," he said. "To the temple. Which is stupid, because the temple sold me, and the Abbot knew, and—" his voice wobbled and steadied, "—but Brother Tomas used to sneak me honeycakes, and the novices let me win at dice, and the pigeons on the scriptorium roof know my whistle. It's a cage. But it's got my friends in it."

Ivo looked out at the faint glow of the shrine. *Charity was a cage all along,* she had thought in the Abbot's study, and had believed it completely, and here was this child explaining that a cage with friends in it was still worth going home to, and she did not know which of them was right and suspected the answer was both.

"Maybe," she said, "we get you home, and then we burn the cage down around you and build something with a door you can open from the inside."

Ansel considered this with enormous seriousness. "Can it have honeycakes?"

"It can have honeycakes."

Across the campfire-less dark, she saw Serel rise silently, wrap her cloak around her, and walk back up the tunnel the way they had come — alone, unhurried, a woman with an errand. Ivo told herself the enforcer was checking their backtrail. She told herself it twice.

It didn't take either time.

Below them, faint and far, one of the nine bells rang — a single soft note, uninvited, like a knock at a door in a house where everyone is pretending to sleep.
