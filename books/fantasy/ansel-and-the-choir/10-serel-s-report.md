## 10. Serel's Report

The signal was a bell that did not ring.

Serel had learned it in her first year of service, when she was still young enough to believe the Abbot's smile meant warmth: a small bronze bell kept in the shrine alcove at Thornfell's edge, one that had been cracked on purpose so that its voice died in its own throat. When you struck it, only the mountain heard. The old gods heard too, perhaps — but they were asleep then, and the Abbot had built his whole career on their sleeping.

She struck it now, three times, and waited in the dark beneath the shrine while the sound went nowhere and everywhere.

The answer came as it always came: not a voice but a pressure, a change in the air, the particular hush of a man arranging his attention around you. Then the Abbot's presence unfolded out of the dark like a letter being opened. He could not be here — he was four days behind them, in his study, among his ledgers — and yet here he was, the way a debt is present in every room its debtor enters.

"Report," said the Abbot.

Serel knelt, because kneeling made the reports cleaner. Kneeling reminded her what she was: an instrument, and instruments do not bend themselves.

"The party crossed the Weeping Stair two days past," she said. "Quintana leads them through smugglers' roads — she knows them by heart, which is worse than knowing them by map. Brannoc watches her more than the road. The cartographer is deaf but hears everything that matters; she taps the stone and reads it like scripture. And the boy—"

"Yes." The word arrived soft as ash. "The boy."

* * *

Ansel hummed in his sleep. That was the first thing Serel had noted about him, years ago, before any of this began, when he was only a foundling in a choir robe too big for him and she was only the Abbot's shadow walking the cloisters at night. He hummed in his sleep, and the candles leaned toward him like flowers toward a window.

She had reported it then, too. She reported everything. That was the whole architecture of her life: she saw, she told, and in exchange she was fed, clothed, armed, and — this was the part she never said aloud, even to herself, even in the privacy of her own skull — forgiven. Whatever she had been before Saint Vellum took her in, whatever she had done at eleven years old that made her grandmother drive her from the house with a fire iron still warm in memory, the temple had agreed to unmake it. Not pardon it. Unmake it. There were no records of Serel anywhere in the world except the ones the Abbot kept, and the Abbot kept them in a locked box under his bed, and once a year he let her look at them without touching, the way a man lets a dog look at meat.

So when the party slept, Serel did not sleep. She sat up with her back against a root-buttress and watched Ansel breathe, and she counted.

That was her gift, if it could be called a gift. Other people saw faces; Serel saw sums. A man owed a debt, and a debt was a number, and a number could be moved from one column to another until the columns balanced. Her whole life was a ledger someone else had opened, and she had decided long ago that if she must live inside an accounting, she would become very good at arithmetic.

The boy was a sum. She had understood that the moment the Abbot told her what he was — a soul sold, decades ago, signed away with ink that had dried into something older than ink. Everything since had been a matter of determining who held the note.

* * *

"The boy sings down," Serel said now, to the pressure in the dark. "Not in his sleep anymore. Awake. Quintana caught him doing harmonies off the liturgy — harmonies that aren't in any book I've seen, and I've catalogued the scriptorium twice. The candles gutter in time with him. The stones hum after he stops. Whatever is under this mountain has its hand on him already, Abbot, and it's reeling him in like a fisherman who knows the hook is set."

"And Quintana?"

"Knows more than she says. She keeps a page hidden in her boot — a prayer with an answer written on it in a hand that isn't hers. I've seen her touch it at night like a woman touching a scar. She thinks no one knows. She thinks because she's quiet, she's invisible." Serel allowed herself the smallest breath of contempt. "Quiet people are the easiest to count. They don't move the numbers around."

"You admire her."

It was not a question, and that was the trap of it. The Abbot always asked things that were not questions, so that answering felt like volunteering a sin.

"I respect her usefulness," Serel said. "She'll get us to the court faster than any priest could, and faster than any soldier. After that, her usefulness ends."

"After that," the Abbot said, "her usefulness transforms."

Serel waited. In the dark, waiting was a skill, and she had mastered it the way other women mastered embroidery — stitch by patient stitch, until the pattern showed.

* * *

He told her on the third night, when the party camped in a hollow where an old shrine glowed faintly through the moss — nine bell-shapes carved into the living rock, worn smooth by centuries of hands that no longer existed. The others slept. Brannoc snored like a landslide arguing with itself. Odile lay curled around her satchel of charts the way a mother curls around a child. Quintana sat last watch, her back straight, her eyes on the tunnel mouth, and Serel noted — because noting was breathing to her — that the scribe's lips moved sometimes, shaping prayers to gods the temple insisted were dead.

Serel walked to the shrine and struck the cracked bell, and the Abbot came.

"He will be handed over," the Abbot said, without preamble, without the dark even settling properly around them. "At the boundary of the court. To Vesh's servants."

Serel kept her face still. Stillness was cheap; she'd bought it years ago at a fair price and it had never needed maintenance. But inside, the abacus clicked. *Handed over.* Not retrieved. Not rescued. The word the Abbot used for the mission, back in his study, with his cold hands flat on his ledger, had been *retrieval*. Words were also numbers. This one had just changed value.

"To Vesh," she repeated.

"The old god wakes. You've seen the shrines glowing; you know it better than I, you're closer to it than I am. Vesh wants a door back into the world, and the boy is that door — a soul already half-sold, already woven into the court's choir, already singing downward. The old gods cannot simply walk back in. The world locked against them centuries ago. But a vessel, freely offered—" the Abbot's voice did something terrible then, something almost tender, "—a vessel can be *invited*."

"And the temple receives?"

"The forgiveness of our own debt." For the first time, the pressure in the air shifted, and Serel felt rather than saw the shape of the Abbot's ambition, vast and patient as a cathedral. "You have read the deep ledgers, Serel. You know what Saint Vellum owes, and to whom, and how long we have paid interest on a principal no living hand signed. Four hundred years of tithes to keep a silence comfortable. If Vesh takes the boy willingly given, the court's claim on us dissolves. Every ledger in my study burns clean. The temple stands free for the first time since its founding."

"And the boy?"

The pause was brief. Brief pauses were the Abbot's honesty; long ones were his mercy, and he offered neither often.

"The boy was sold before he could walk. His soul belongs to powers beneath the mountain, and those powers are waking whether we act or not. The choice is not between his freedom and his servitude, Serel. The choice is between his servitude serving *us*, and his servitude serving nothing at all." A dry rustle, like pages turning somewhere far away. "One soul, weighed against a temple. Against every prayer that will ever be answered in that building again. Against the village harvests, the plague-shrines, the births we bless and the dead we bury. Arithmetic, Serel. It is only arithmetic."

*Arithmetic.* The word settled into her like a coin into a well-worn slot. She had been waiting her whole life for someone to say it plainly. Priests dressed their math in mercy; merchants dressed theirs in fairness; the Abbot alone had the courage to call a sum a sum.

"I will need to separate him from Quintana," Serel said. "She suspects me. Not consciously — she's too busy suspecting herself — but her instincts are good. And Brannoc has gone soft over the boy. He prays to a dead god and pretends it's nothing, but he holds his sword like a man guarding a grave. He'll fight."

"Then arrange it so fighting costs him dearly."

"And Odile?"

"Is deaf," the Abbot said, "and therefore hears nothing worth reporting. Leave her."

Serel almost smiled. Almost. Even alone in the dark with a voice made of pressure and ambition, she rationed her smiles the way a miser rations gold.

"There is one complication," she said. "Vesh sent an avatar. We met it at the Shrine of Nine Bells — a woman made of bell-sound, wearing borrowed faces. It spoke to Quintana mostly. It wants the boy too, but I don't think it wants him the way you want him. There was something in its voice when it looked at him. Hunger, yes. But also—" she searched for the word and found it and disliked it, "—*longing*. The old gods may intend to use him as more than a door. They may intend to use him as a first voice. A choir rebuilt around him."

"Then all the more reason to deliver him promptly," the Abbot said. "A thing longed for can be bargained upward. A thing merely wanted is sold cheap. We will offer Vesh what she hungers for, at the moment of her greatest hunger, and name our price accordingly." The pressure gathered itself, began to withdraw, then paused. "Serel."

"Abbot."

"When it is done — when the boy is delivered and the ledgers burn — your box comes out from under my bed. All of it. Into the fire. You will be no one's instrument, because there will be nothing left to forgive."

The dark held its breath.

She had known, of course. She had deduced it weeks ago, running the numbers the way she ran all numbers: her service, her silence, her sins in their locked box — the total had only ever pointed one direction. But hearing it spoken was different. Hearing it spoken was the difference between knowing a bridge would hold and standing on it while it held.

"Thank you, Abbot," she said, and meant it, and hated that she meant it, and filed the hatred away in a column marked *later*, a column she had never once been able to balance.

* * *

She walked back to the camp through the glowing moss-light, and the mountain breathed around her, slow and enormous, counting footsteps. Let it count, she thought. Hers were the lightest in the party. She had practiced.

At the edge of the firelight she stopped and looked at them a long moment, the way an accountant looks at a page before closing the book.

Brannoc, asleep with his sword-hand curled open, dreaming of a god that had been eaten. Odile, curled around her charts, mapping a world through her bones. Quintana on watch, whispering to dead gods, carrying a stolen page and a stolen inheritance she didn't yet understand. And Ansel, smallest of them, humming in his sleep — a thin thread of sound rising off him like smoke, threading up through the stone, threading down, joining something vast below that sang back.

Four lives. One temple. Four hundred years of debt against one boy's borrowed breath.

The columns balanced. They balanced perfectly, and that was the thing no one ever warned you about arithmetic: it always balanced, right up until the moment you noticed what you'd spent.

Serel sat down at the fire's edge, drew her whetstone from her belt, and began, quietly, to sharpen.

Below them, far beneath the stone, something changed key.

Above them, at the mountain's crown, the first bell of morning rang — and this one, though no hand touched it, was not cracked at all.
