## 11. The Bridge of Signed Names

The road ended where the mountain began to think.

Ivo had known this place since she was small enough to ride her grandmother's shoulders — the seam in Thornfell's flank where the smugglers' ways opened like a throat. But she had never seen it look like *this*. The last mile of track simply stopped, and beyond it hung a bridge of grey stone that had no business existing: no pillars, no foundations, just an arch of masonry flung across a chasm so deep that the air rising out of it was warm and smelled of candlewax and old paper.

"Someone built that," Brannoc said flatly. He stood with his arms crossed, the way he did when he wanted the world to argue with him first.

"No one built it," Ivo said. "It's older than building."

The bridge was not empty. At its near end, seated at a desk that could only be described as *insisted upon* — a slab of stone worn smooth as a lectern, an inkwell of black glass, a quill cut from something that had once been a wing — sat a figure. It wore robes the colour of fog over water, and it had no face worth remembering. Not because it was horrifying, though it was, but because every time Ivo's eyes tried to fix on its features they slid off like rain off oiled leather. She caught impressions instead: the suggestion of spectacles, the ghost of a clerk's stoop, hands stained to the second knuckle with ink that had dried centuries ago.

"It's a tollkeeper," she said. "My grandmother told me about them. The deep roads don't take coin."

"What do they take?" Serel asked. Her voice was exactly as level as always, which was how Ivo knew she'd already guessed.

"Memories." Ivo said it before she could decide whether to soften it. "One each. You sign for what you give, and you forget you ever had it. That's the toll. That's why nobody uses these roads twice if they can help it — you can't remember the way well enough to come back angry."

Cassian, the defrocked priest, made a sound low in his chest, half prayer and half curse. "That's not commerce," he said. "That's confession without absolution."

"That's a bargain," said the tollkeeper.

Its voice arrived inside their ears rather than through them, dry as a page turning. It rose from its stool with the creak of a man who had been sitting since before the temple of Saint Vellum laid its first stone, and gestured at the desk with terrible courtesy.

"One signature per traveler. Name the memory aloud or write it. The bridge will know if you lie. The bridge has been lied to before." A pause, delicate as dust settling. "We keep those liars."

* * *

They argued about it for an hour, camped in the lee of the mountain's mouth while the chasm breathed its warm paper-smell up at them.

"We could go around," Cassian offered, without conviction.

"Around is nine days through the Weeping Stair country," Ivo said, "and the Stair has its own opinions about visitors. This is one crossing, one memory each, and we're through to the deep roads by morning."

"And what do *you* give?" Serel asked. She sat apart from the fire, sharpening a knife she did not need to sharpen. "You've led us here. You'll cross first. What does the great guide surrender?"

Ivo looked into the flames and found, to her surprise, that she didn't know. That was the trap of memories: you couldn't inventory them. They were like teeth — you never thought about any particular one until it was gone, and then your tongue went looking forever.

"I'll know when I sign," she said.

Brannoc snorted. "That's either very brave or very stupid."

"I've been told they're the same thing with different lighting."

He almost smiled. Almost. Since the night he'd told her about his eaten god — since she'd seen him kneel in the dark of a smugglers' shrine and press his forehead to cold stone like a man visiting a grave — something between them had changed shape. Not trust, exactly. Recognition. Two people who had both lost something to the court beneath the mountain, and were only now learning the full size of the loss.

Odile signed first.

She did it without ceremony, which was somehow the most frightening thing any of them did all night. She walked to the desk, took up the quill in her quick cartographer's fingers, and wrote three words on the waiting parchment. Ivo craned to see and couldn't read them; the ink seemed to drink itself into the page even as it dried. Then Odile set down the quill, nodded once to the tollkeeper, and stepped onto the bridge.

She stopped halfway across. Ivo saw her go still — saw her hand rise slowly to her sternum, pressing, as though checking that something was still there. When she reached the far side she turned and looked back at the rest of them, and her face was composed and calm and missing something none of them would ever be able to name, because she no longer had it either.

She tapped her chest twice, then pointed back the way they'd come. *Worth it,* the gesture said. Or possibly *I can't remember what I lost.* With Odile it was often both.

* * *

Cassian went next. He named his memory aloud, formally, like a man reciting a penitential rite: "The day I was defrocked. The sound of my own name being struck from the rolls." His voice cracked on *struck*, but he finished the sentence, and when he came off the bridge he was weeping gently and did not know why, and kept touching his cheeks with an expression of mild scholarly interest, as though someone else's grief were leaking onto him.

"The body remembers," the tollkeeper observed, "even when the ledger doesn't. It's the great flaw in my system." It sounded genuinely regretful.

Brannoc planted himself at the desk like a man signing articles of war. "A memory," he growled. "Fine. Give me—" He stopped. Ivo watched him wrestle with it, watched him refuse to look at any of them, and understood that whatever he chose, he was choosing the least valuable thing he owned, and that the least valuable thing a mercenary owns is still a piece of his soul.

"The taste of my mother's bread," he said finally, gruffly, like a man spitting it out. "I haven't tasted it in thirty years anyway. It's just sitting there going stale."

He signed. He crossed. On the far side he stood flexing his jaw, working his tongue against his teeth, hunting for a flavour that was no longer anywhere in the world. Ivo saw the moment he realized he couldn't even remember what he'd been hunting for — saw the confusion, then the shrug, then the deliberate turning away from the gap in himself. Mercenaries were good at that. It was practically the trade.

Serel crossed without hesitation and without telling anyone what she'd given. She wrote her line, folded her hands, and walked the bridge like a woman crossing a courtyard she owned. Whatever memory she'd paid, she'd priced it beforehand and considered it cheap. Ivo hated her a little for that, and admired her a little too, and filed both feelings away where they wouldn't slow her down.

Which left Ivo alone at the desk with the tollkeeper and the black-glass inkwell and the quill made of something that had once flown.

"You've been dreading this," the tollkeeper said. Not unkindly. Clerks are never unkind; they are merely accurate.

"Everyone dreads it."

"No. Most travelers dread losing. You dread *choosing*. I can smell the difference. It smells like ink and indecision." It slid the parchment toward her. "Take your time. I have nothing but."

Ivo stared at the blank line. And here was the cruelty of it, the beautiful mechanical cruelty: she could not choose the safe thing, because there was no safe thing. Every memory she might surrender was load-bearing. Take away the night her grandmother taught her the smugglers' roads and she could not lead them tomorrow. Take away the answered prayer, the page she'd stolen from the scriptorium, Ansel humming in his sleep — take away any of it and the whole expedition collapsed like a roof with one beam pulled.

So she would have to pay with something structural. Something she could survive the loss of, the way a house survives the loss of a window.

Her grandmother's face.

Not the grandmother — not the lessons, not the roads, not the smell of tallow and wool, not the voice that had sung her to sleep with songs about the court beneath the mountain. Just the face. Just the particular arrangement of lines and light that meant *this woman, this one, mine*. She could keep everything else. People remembered loved ones by ten thousand things. One of them being gone would be like a missing tooth: an ache, a whistle in the wind, survivable.

Wasn't it?

"Name it," the tollkeeper prompted.

"My grandmother's face," Ivo said. Her voice came out steady, which felt like a betrayal of something. "What she looked like. Only that."

The quill was in her hand. She hadn't picked it up; it had simply arrived there, the way things did around ghosts. She wrote her name — *Ivo Quintana*, the letters small and cramped, a scribe's hand even under duress — and the moment the final stroke of the *a* left the nib, the parchment drank the ink, and the bridge drank the parchment, and the world drank something out of her.

There was no pain. That was the worst part. There was a sensation like a page being turned somewhere behind her eyes, and then—

Then she was standing at the edge of a chasm holding a quill, and her grandmother was a warm absence shaped like a person. She knew she had loved her. She knew the love the way you know a room you grew up in — blind, total, structural. But when she reached for the face, the way you reach in a dream for a door you've opened a thousand times, her hand closed on smoke. There had been a face. It had laughed. It had been *hers*. And now the space where it lived was smooth and blank as unwritten vellum, and the horror wasn't the loss, the horror was that she could feel the shape of the loss perfectly, a tooth-shaped hole in a smile.

"Oh," she heard herself say. Small. Very small.

"The body remembers," the tollkeeper repeated softly. "Even when the ledger doesn't."

She crossed the bridge. The stones were warm under her boots, faintly pulsing, like walking on the back of something asleep. Halfway across she stopped, because everyone stopped halfway across, and pressed her hand to her sternum the way Odile had.

And that was when she felt it.

Not a gap. Everyone else had gaps — she'd watched them find theirs on the far side, the little blind spots where lives used to be. What Ivo felt was not empty. Under her palm, beneath the breastbone, in the place where Odile had pressed and found nothing, Ivo felt a *burn*. Low and banked, like a coal wrapped in cloth, like a brand healing over.

And when she closed her eyes — she could not have said why, except that her body knew things her ledger-mind did not — she saw it.

A seal. Pressed into the soft dark behind her ribs the way wax takes a signet: a circle, and within the circle a mountain shown in section, its roots descending past the borders of the seal into darkness, and around the rim a ring of writing in a script older than Saint Vellum, older than the temple, older than the alphabet she'd spent her life copying. She could not read it. But she recognized it with a recognition that had nothing to do with memory, because it lived below memory, in the marrow:

She had seen this seal before. On the contract. On *the* contract — the Abbot's ledger, the debt signed decades ago in a hand that shared her blood. The court beneath the mountain stamped its claims, and the claim it had stamped was not only on Ansel.

It was on her.

*Ivo Quintana,* the seal said, in the wordless way seals say things. *Collateral. Inheritance. Ours.*

She opened her eyes. The others were waiting on the far side, small against the vast dark of the deep roads' entrance. Brannoc was watching her with the particular attention of a man who had learned to read faces for a living and was reading hers now, and not liking the book.

"You felt it too," he said when she reached them. Not a question.

"Felt what?" Serel asked sharply.

Ivo didn't answer her. She was looking past all of them, down the throat of the mountain, where the first of the deep-road lanterns had begun to glow — old shrines waking, one after another, like eyes opening — and thinking about the coal burning behind her ribs, and doing arithmetic she did not want to finish.

The toll had taken her grandmother's face. Fine. That was the price of passage, paid fair.

But the seal had not been taken. The seal had been *revealed* — uncovered by the removal, the way pulling a tapestry from a wall shows you the hook it hung on. The memory hadn't merely held her grandmother's face. It had been *covering* something. All these years, some part of her had carried the contract's mark, and some wiser part of her had buried it under the one memory she treasured most, and the tollkeeper's clean little transaction had stripped the covering away like skin off a fruit.

"Quintana," Serel said, and the name sounded different now, the way it had sounded in the Abbot's office — less like a person, more like a clause. "What did you see?"

Ivo pressed her hand once more against the burn beneath her breastbone, warm as a living thing, patient as a creditor.

"The family crest," she said.

And turned to lead them down into the dark.
