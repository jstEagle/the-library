## 12. Half a Soul

The toll did not take the memory all at once. That was the first cruelty of it — Ivo had expected a snip, a clean cut, the way a scribe trims a ragged edge from vellum. Instead it came apart like wet paper.

She was still standing on the bridge when her grandmother's face went.

One moment she was looking at it — that beloved topography of wrinkles and one crooked tooth, the laugh lines carved deep as riverbeds — and the next she was looking at *a shape*, a warm brown blur where a face should be, and her own hands were pressed to her mouth because she could feel herself trying to scream around something that wasn't there anymore.

*Grandmother.* The word remained. The word was all that remained. A name attached to an absence, like a label on an empty jar.

The tollkeeper ghost drifted back from the bridge's rail, its ledger open, its quill already moving. It had no face either — none of them did, here on the Bridge of Signed Names, where every crossing cost a piece of the past — but Ivo had the sudden, sick certainty that if it had possessed a mouth, it would have been smiling.

*One memory,* it wrote in the air, in ink that glowed like drowned stars. *Received with thanks.*

"Give it back." Her voice came out wrong. Thin. A child's voice. "I signed for passage, not — give it *back*—"

*The bridge does not sell,* the ghost wrote. *It only buys.*

* * *

Afterward — though "afterward" was a word she had to reach for, the way a swimmer reaches for a rope in dark water — they made camp in the lee of the bridge's far abutment, where the smugglers' road bent down toward the deep galleries. Brannoc built a fire without being asked. Odile sat close to the flames and watched Ivo with those steady, unblinking eyes that missed nothing, having never once needed ears to hear with.

Serel stood apart, arms crossed, watching the dark. She had signed her toll without hesitation — Ivo had seen it, had envied it even through her terror. Whatever memory Serel had surrendered, she had handed it over like a coin she was glad to be rid of.

"I can't see her," Ivo said. It came out before she decided to say it. "Her face. I know everything else. I know she kept goats and hated the temple bell and sang off-key on purpose to annoy the cantor. I know her hands. I remember her hands." She looked down at her own hands, ink-stained, grandmother-shaped. "But the face is just — a door painted on a wall. I keep walking up to it and there's nothing behind."

Brannoc grunted. He was turning something over in his fingers — a worn copper medallion, the sigil rubbed nearly smooth. His god's sigil, Ivo realized. The god that had been eaten. He carried it the way other men carried knives.

"Eat," he said, and shoved a tin plate at her. Hard bread, hard cheese, hard kindness. "Grief goes down easier with cheese."

"That's not grief advice, that's just cheese."

"Aye. And it works."

She almost laughed. Almost. The almost hurt worse than the not.

* * *

Sleep took her like a trapdoor.

And the mountain's mouth opened again — not the tunnel-mouth they'd entered by, but the true mouth, the old one, the one from stories: a cleft in Thornfell's southern flank wide as a cathedral door, ringed with stones that pilgrims had kissed smooth a thousand years before Saint Vellum taught anyone to kneel. Ivo knew it instantly, wholly, the way you know a room you dreamed in as a child. She had been here. She had been *small* here.

Frost on the grass. Breath hanging white. And her grandmother — the shape of her, the coat of her, the voice of her, everything but the face — standing at the threshold with a stranger.

No. Not a stranger. Something that wore a stranger the way a hand wears a glove. Tall, courtly, dressed in colors that didn't sit still — grey becoming green becoming the grey of ash — and when it turned its head, the frost crept *toward* it instead of away.

"You understand the terms," the thing said. Its voice was patient the way rot is patient.

"I understand them." Grandmother's voice. Steady. Terrible. Ivo had heard that steadiness only once before in her life — the day the temple's debt-collectors came for the goat shed, and Grandmother had stood in the doorway and read them their own contract aloud until they left. "Half. Not more. You take half and the village remembers itself again. Every name. Every grave. Every child born after."

"The harvest also."

"The harvest was never mine to bargain. Take the half."

"It must be given freely," the thing said, and here its patience thinned, showed teeth beneath. "That is the oldest law. Freely given, freely binding. Do you consent, Maren Quintana, to surrender half your soul to the court beneath the mountain?"

*Freely given, freely binding.* The words rang in Ivo like a bell struck against her spine. She knew them. She had copied them. Page four hundred and eleven of the Rites of Saint Vellum, in a chapter the Abbot called apocryphal and never let the novices study — *On Debts That Predate the Saint*. She had copied it forty times in her indenture and never once understood it until now.

"Yes," said her grandmother.

And then — this was the part that broke something in Ivo that has not yet mended — her grandmother knelt. Knelt in the frost at the mountain's mouth, and gathered up a small girl who had followed her out in the dark without permission, a girl of six or seven with ink already smudged on her fingers because even then, even at six, she'd been sneaking into the scriptorium.

"Ivo," Grandmother said, holding her face in those remembered hands. "Listen to me. Listen very carefully, little scribe. This is going to hurt me and it is going to pass through me and land on you, and I am sorry, I am so sorry, but listen — *it is not a punishment.* Do you hear? Whatever they tell you it is, whatever it feels like — it is the price of a whole village remembering its dead. There are worse things to inherit than a debt paid for love."

"But—" the small girl said, and her voice was Ivo's voice, six years old and cracking, "what are you selling?"

Grandmother smiled. And the smile — the smile Ivo could still see, the last surviving fragment of the face — was the worst thing in the world, because it was happy.

"Only the part of me that gets to rest," she said. "The singing part. They're collecting a choir down there, sweetheart. Let them have my verse. You'll carry the words."

The mountain breathed in.

* * *

She woke screaming, or she thought she did; in the fractured country between dream and waking she couldn't be sure the scream had ever left her throat. Hands were on her shoulders. Brannoc's, by the weight of them, and Odile's cool palm flat against her sternum, feeling — Ivo understood dimly — the vibration of her panic the way she felt the vibration of tunnels.

"Lantern," Ivo gasped. "Lantern, I need to write, I need to write it *now* before it—"

Odile thrust her journal into her hands before Ivo finished the sentence. Of course she did. Odile always knew.

Ivo wrote by lantern-light with shaking fingers, hunched over the page like a woman sheltering a flame from wind: *the mountain's mouth, the south cleft, the frost, the glove-wearing thing, freely given freely binding, Maren Quintana consents, half, the choir, the singing part, the words.* She wrote until the memory stopped sliding and pinned itself to the paper like a moth to a board. Then, and only then, she looked up.

They were all awake. All of them. Even Serel, who stood at the edge of the firelight with an expression Ivo had never seen on her cold, arithmetic face — something almost like disturbance.

"How much did you hear?" Ivo asked.

"All of it," Brannoc said. "You talk in your sleep like a town crier with a grievance."

"What did I say?"

"You said a name," Serel said quietly. "Hers. Maren Quintana. And you said *half*, over and over. Half. Half. Half." She stepped into the light. For once she did not look like the Abbot's knife walking around in a person. She looked like a woman doing sums she didn't like the total of. "Scribe. When we crossed the bridge, the tollkeeper examined you longer than any of us. Did you wonder why?"

"I assumed it was reading how much I was worth."

"It was reading what you were *already missing*." Serel crouched, brought her eyes level with Ivo's, and Ivo saw that her pupils were doing something faintly wrong in the lantern-light, some old discipline of focus the temple trained into its enforcers. "I've served the temple nineteen years. I have seen debt-contracts. I have seen soul-claims. The seal on Ansel's bill of sale — the one the Abbot keeps under triple lock — I have seen it twice." She paused. "It is a choir-mark. A joined seal. Two halves of one signature, cut from a single rite. One half bound to the boy."

Ivo's mouth had gone dry. "And the other half."

"You already know," Serel said. "You've known since the bridge. You signed away your grandmother's face and found the contract's seal burned into the scar it left. Didn't you."

Not a question. Ivo nodded anyway, because saying it aloud would make it real, and some cowardly clerk in her chest was still hoping to file it under fiction.

But Serel waited, and Brannoc waited, and Odile reached over and touched the back of Ivo's hand — once, firmly, the deaf woman's way of saying *speak, I'm listening* — and so Ivo said it.

"When the toll took her face, there was a mark underneath. Where the memory had been rooted. A seal. Two interlocking verses of music, drawn as one line." She swallowed. "It's the same seal that's on Ansel's claim. I've copied enough contracts to know a matched set when I see one."

"Then say the rest," Brannoc said, gentle as a landslide.

"The rest is that Ansel isn't sold to the court alone." The words tasted like well-water and iron. "He was woven in the same rite that took my grandmother's half-soul. The same night, maybe. The same ceremony. His soul and my inheritance were cut from the same cloth — which means the court doesn't hold one claim down here." She heard her own voice go flat, clerical, the voice she used for inventory. "It holds two. And they're braided together. Whatever happens to him happens along a thread that runs straight through me."

Silence, except the fire.

It was Odile who moved first. She pulled her leather tube from her pack — the one she guarded like a relic — and unrolled not her maps but a sheet of thin vellum covered in her private notation: rows of fine strokes, dense and sparse, rising and falling. Ivo had always assumed they were survey records. Now, watching Odile's finger travel down them, humming silently to herself, Ivo understood they were something else entirely.

*Music,* Ivo realized. *She's been writing down what the mountain sings.*

Odile tapped a cluster of dense notation near the bottom of the sheet, then looked up at Ivo with an expression of urgent apology — sorry, this will hurt — and pressed Ivo's hand flat against the vellum.

The strokes buzzed faintly under her fingertips. Ink mixed, Ivo would have sworn, with something older than ink. And the pattern of them — two lines intertwined, rising together, falling together, inseparable —

Two voices. Braided. One line.

Ansel's line. Hers.

"Oh," Ivo said. Very quietly. The way people speak in temples, or at gravesides. "Oh, no."

Brannoc put his medallion away. Serel turned her face toward the dark of the descending road, toward the court beneath the mountain, and when she spoke her voice had gone back to arithmetic — but the sums had changed.

"The Abbot sent us to retrieve one soul," she said. "He believes he is owed one soul." A pause, precise as a blade laid down on a table. "He has no idea the debt has been compounding for thirty years. Scribe — if the court learns what you are before we reach the Choir, they will not trade for the boy."

"They'll take the pair," Ivo said. "A matched set. A harmony."

"And every plan we have," Serel said, "is built on the assumption that you are the rescuer."

The fire cracked. Somewhere far below them, down roads that smelled of cold stone and older bargains, something began — very faintly, very patiently — to count footsteps.

Odile caught Ivo's eye across the flames, tapped the braided line on her vellum one last time, and then pointed down into the dark, toward the heart of the mountain, and began rolling out her charts.

There was another way in. Ivo could see it in her face before she drew a single stroke.

There was another way in, and it was one even the gods had forgotten.
