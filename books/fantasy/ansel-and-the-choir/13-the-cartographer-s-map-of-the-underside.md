## 13. The Cartographer's Map of the Underside

Odile had learned to read the world through her boots before she ever learned to read it through her eyes.

Her mother had taught her the first alphabet — the one written in stone and soil and the soles of the feet. *Gravel means water once ran here,* her mother would sign, hands moving in the firelight of their cottage above Lark's Hollow. *Clay means men dug here and gave up. A road that hums under your heel is a road that carries carts; a road that is silent is a road that wants to be forgotten.* Odile had been four years old, or thereabouts, standing barefoot on the packed earth of their yard, learning that silence was not an absence but a language, and that she was fluent in it while everyone around her was illiterate.

The temple had called her affliction. The smugglers had called it luck. Brannoc, when he'd first learned of it somewhere on the second day out from Thornfell, had called it nothing at all, which she had appreciated more than any word.

The truth was simpler than all of them: Odile heard everything. She simply heard it with her whole body instead of only her ears, and what her body told her was truer than anything ears could catch. Ears were fooled by echoes. Ears were fooled by distance. But the bones did not lie. The bones felt the mountain breathing before anyone else knew it breathed at all.

And for eleven days now, walking the deep roads beneath Thornfell with a party of people who spoke over her head and around her and occasionally — kindly, clumsily — *to* her, Odile had been feeling something in the bones of her feet that made no sense at all.

It had started as a wrongness in the rhythm.

Every road has a pulse. Cart-roads pulse slow and heavy, like a sleeping ox. Pilgrim roads pulse quick and light, like birds. The smugglers' roads Ivo led them down pulsed hardly at all — they were roads built to be forgotten, trodden soft by feet that never wanted to be counted. Odile had walked these roads twice before, years ago, running charts for a man who paid in salt pork and never asked why a deaf girl wanted maps of places no one mapped. She remembered their pulses the way other people remembered songs.

But these pulses had changed. Something underneath the roads was keeping time now. Slow. Patient. Regular as a heart that had been beating since before hearts were invented.

*Something below is counting footsteps,* Ivo had said on the first day, and Odile had signed back, without thinking, *Not counting. Singing.*

Ivo had stared at her. Then she had fetched ink and paper, and for three days afterward the scribe had carried Odile's notes like they were scripture, reading them by candlelight long after the others slept.

Odile let them think she was mapping the way cartographers map: with compass and chain and careful distances. She drew those maps too — honest ones, useful ones, the kind Serel could study and Brannoc could argue about. But every night, when the camp went quiet and the others wrapped themselves in bedrolls, Odile sat cross-legged on the stone with her palms flat against the floor and her eyes closed, and she listened with everything she had.

What she heard was a city.

* * *

She had known, in the abstract, that there was a court beneath the mountain. Everyone in Thornfell knew it the way everyone knows there is a bottom to a well — as a fact, not a place. The old stories said the court had been a kingdom once, before the new god came and the old gods went quiet, and that its people had not died but *changed*, folding themselves into the dark like letters folded into envelopes, waiting for someone to open them.

The stories did not say the court sang.

On the eleventh night, camped in a gallery where the ceiling rose into darkness and the walls were carved with processions of figures whose faces had been worn smooth by centuries of passing hands, Odile pressed her palms to the floor and finally understood what she had been hearing for eleven days.

It was not one song. It was thousands. Voices braided together so tightly that from a distance they sounded like a single note — the way a rope looks like one cord until you pull it apart. The voices rose through the stone the way heat rises through a floor, and each voice was distinct if you listened the way Odile listened: this one high and bright, this one cracked with age, this one stuttering like a child still learning its own name. They wove and unwove. They held chords that no throat should have been able to hold, because the throats holding them had stopped being throats a long time ago.

And threaded through all of it, thin as a silver wire pulled through grey wool, was a voice Odile recognized.

Ansel.

He was asleep ten feet away, curled in his bedroll with his breath going slow and even, his face in sleep looking younger than his years and older than his soul. But part of him was down there, in the braid, singing harmonies no one had taught him, and the Choir was drawing him in note by note the way a tide draws in a swimmer who has stopped kicking.

Odile sat very still for a long time.

Then she took out her charts — the real ones, the ones she kept rolled inside the false ones, hidden in the waxed canvas lining of her map case — and she began, by candlelight, to draw what she had heard.

* * *

The others discovered her at dawn, or rather they discovered the maps, spread across the gallery floor and weighted at the corners with stones.

There were nine of them. No — eight finished and one half-drawn, the ink still tacky. Each showed the same region of the undersides, but none of them looked like any map Odile had ever drawn for pay. There were no clean borders. Instead there were lines of varying thickness radiating outward from a central point like the rings of a felled tree, and along each line she had written, in her small precise hand, descriptions of what the vibrations carried: *water, cold, moving fast — old stair, broken, three flights — air that tastes of iron — many footsteps, none recent — SINGING, do not write how loud.*

At the center of the innermost ring she had drawn a small chamber, and in the chamber a shape like a knot, and beside the knot a single word: *HERE.*

Brannoc crouched over the maps with his arms crossed, his scarred knuckles white. He was a man who trusted steel and distrusted paper, but he had learned in eleven days of tunnels that Odile's paper was a kind of steel. "This is the deep court," he said slowly. "This is the actual — you've had this the whole time?"

"Not the whole time," Ivo said. She was standing at the edge of the spread, and her face had gone the particular grey it went when she was doing arithmetic she didn't like. "The last three days. Maybe four." She looked up at Odile, and her eyes were sharp and ashamed at once. "You've been sitting on the floor every night with your hands flat on the stone. I thought you were praying."

*I was listening,* Odile signed, and then, because Ivo was the only one of them who could read her hands quickly, she added: *Praying is just listening with your mouth shut. Someone told me that once. I don't remember who.* She paused, and her hands hesitated in the air, which almost never happened. *I don't remember a lot of things lately. The roads take tolls. You know that better than I do.*

Ivo flinched. Since the bridge of signed names, since the tollkeeper ghost had taken her grandmother's face, the scribe had been carrying something broken behind her eyes, and everyone in the party had learned to step around it the way you step around a cracked floorboard. Odile understood broken memory better than any of them. Her own childhood came back to her these days in fragments — her mother's hands, yes; the name of her village, sometimes; the sound of her own voice, never, though she supposed she must have had one once, before the fever at age six took her hearing and, apparently, took the memory of sound along with it.

That was the thing none of them understood, Odile thought, watching them stare at her maps. That was the thing she had never told anyone, because telling it made people look at her with pity, and pity was worse than being overlooked.

She had already lost everything the court beneath the mountain took from people. Memory of sound. The voice she'd been born with. Whole years of her early life, gone soft and featureless like footprints in rain. The tollkeeper ghosts, the memory-traders, the hungry Choir — they dealt in exactly the currency she had already spent. Whatever they threatened to take from her, they would be taking a copy of something already gone.

Which meant she was the only person in this party who could walk into that place without anything left to lose.

*The knot in the middle,* she signed to Ivo, pointing at the center of the innermost ring. *That's where the voices braid tightest. That's the heart of it. Ansel's thread runs straight there — I can feel it pulling even when he's awake. He hums and the floor hums back, and the two notes are getting closer together every day.* She let her hands fall, then raised them again. *And there's a way in that isn't on any of your roads. Even the gods have forgotten it. Especially the gods.*

"How?" Ivo asked aloud, and then caught herself and repeated it in signs, badly, her fingers stiff. Eleven days of practice and she still signed like someone writing with her off hand. Odile found she didn't mind. Effort was its own kind of fluency.

*Because the gods are loud,* Odile signed. *Vesh is bells. The Choir is a thousand voices. Everything down there announces itself. But there's a channel under the eastern galleries where the stone was quarried out and never finished — the workers stopped halfway, and the rock remembers stopping. Dead stone. No echo, no resonance, no song. It's a seam of silence running right under the heart of the court, and nobody uses it because nobody can hear it. It doesn't sing. It doesn't glow. It doesn't answer prayers.* Her mouth curved, dry. *It's exactly like me. Invisible to everything that listens with power.*

Brannoc grunted. It was, from him, applause.

Serel stood apart from the others, as always, at the gallery's mouth where the passage ran back toward the surface, and Odile watched her watch the maps with an expression Odile had learned to read from faces alone, in the years when reading faces was the only reading she had. Serel's face said *useful*. Serel's face always said *useful*, the way a knife's face says *sharp*. Odile had no reason to distrust her except that Serel had never once looked at Ansel the way the rest of them did — the way you look at something you're trying to save rather than something you're trying to spend.

She filed that away. Odile filed most things away. It was what cartographers did.

* * *

They spent the day preparing, and Odile spent it teaching.

This was the part she loved, though she'd have bitten her tongue bloody before admitting it. Ivo sat across from her with a slate, learning the vibration-marks — the little symbols Odile used for kinds of ground, kinds of hollow, kinds of danger. Brannoc learned faster than he pretended to, memorizing the hand-signals for *stop*, *down*, *run*, *quiet* with the grim efficiency of a man who had served under commanders who communicated less. Even Ansel joined in, sitting cross-legged with his chin on his knees, watching Odile's hands with the total absorption of a boy who had spent his life listening to a world that never quite explained itself.

"You feel it through your feet," Ansel said at one point. Not a question. "All of it. The singing."

*Through my feet, my spine, my teeth,* Odile signed, and Ivo translated, and Ansel nodded slowly.

"Does it hurt? When it sings?"

Odile considered lying. She considered it for about two seconds, and then she looked at his face — at the shadows under his eyes, at the way his fingers kept finding each other and pressing, as if checking that they were still his — and she decided he had earned the truth. Everyone else in this party got lied to gently, by circumstance if not by each other. The boy whose soul was the prize deserved better.

*Yes,* she signed. *It hurts like a hook behind my sternum. Like something reeling me in on a line I can't see. And every day the line is shorter.* She held his gaze. *You feel it too. Don't pretend you don't. I can see it in how you stand — like you're leaning toward something downhill.*

Ansel's face did something complicated. "It's beautiful," he said quietly. "That's the worst part. Down there, in the dreams — it's the most beautiful thing I've ever heard, and I can't hear anything anymore without wondering how much of me is already down there, enjoying it."

Ivo's hands stilled over the slate. Brannoc looked away, jaw working.

Odile reached out and touched the boy's wrist, two firm taps — her sign for *attention*, the one her mother had used — and when he looked at her, she signed slowly enough for Ivo to translate word for word:

*Beautiful things that eat you are still things that eat you. I'll get you to the heart of it. What happens there is yours to decide. But decide it while you still have a you to decide with.*

Ansel held her eyes for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, and something in his shoulders loosened — not much, but measurably, the way a rope loosens when someone takes a turn of the weight onto their own anchor.

* * *

They broke camp at dusk, because the deep roads were kinder in the dark and because Serel had argued for dawn and Odile had argued for dusk and Ivo, after a long look at Odile's maps, had sided with her. Small victory. Odile had learned to treasure small victories; they were the only kind an overlooked person collected reliably.

The seam of dead stone opened off the eastern galleries behind a fall of rubble that Brannoc moved stone by stone, quietly, over two hours, sweating in the cold. Beyond it the passage ran narrow and utterly silent — truly silent, in a way that made even the sighted ones uneasy. No drip echoed. No footfall rang. Their lanterns threw light that seemed to fall without sound, and Odile walked at the front with her bare palms brushing the walls, feeling the blessed nothing of it, the great muffled hush of stone that had given up on song.

Behind her, the party followed in single file: Ivo with her satchel of stolen pages and burned seals, Brannoc with his wounded pride and unwhetted axe, Ansel with his hands pressed flat against his own sternum as if holding a door shut, and Serel at the rear, silent as the stone itself.

They had gone perhaps half a mile into the seam when Odile felt it.

Not through her feet. Through the air — through the faint pressure changes against her skin, the ones she'd learned to read as a child when sound itself abandoned her. Someone behind her was signing. Not to her. Signing small, low, close against the body, the way you sign when you don't want to be seen signing.

She couldn't make out the words. The angle was wrong, the light was wrong. But she knew the cadence of it, the clipped military economy of the hand, and she knew that no one else in this party signed at all.

Odile did not turn around. She kept walking, palms brushing the dead stone, her face as smooth and unreadable as the walls around them, and inside her chest the hook behind her sternum twisted once, sharply, like a fish testing a line.

Ahead, faint through miles of rock, the Choir sang on. And somewhere in its braid of voices, Ansel's silver thread grew shorter, and shorter, and shorter.

She had one night, maybe two, before the map in her hands and the boy behind her stopped agreeing with each other. Whatever Serel was signing into the dark, Odile intended to find out — and the seam of forgotten silence, the one road even the gods couldn't hear, was suddenly the only place in the world where she might learn it first.
