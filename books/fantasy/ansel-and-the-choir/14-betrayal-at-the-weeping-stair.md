## 14. Betrayal at the Weeping Stair

The mountain wept before they ever reached the stair.

Ivo heard it first as rain and corrected herself — they were deep enough now that rain was a rumor, a story told by the tunnels about the world above. No, this was water seeping through a hundred hairline fractures in the stone, gathering in channels worn smooth by centuries, and falling in slow, deliberate drops onto a shelf of rock that rang like a struck bowl. Drip. Ring. Drip. Ring. The Weeping Stair had been named by someone with a poet's ear and a smuggler's patience, and Ivo had crossed it eleven times in her life without once thinking of it as anything but a shortcut.

Now, with the old gods awake and the court beneath the mountain listening, every drop sounded like a bell being rung for someone's funeral.

"Stop." Odile's hand came up, palm flat, and the party stilled. The cartographer could not hear the water — she had never heard anything — but she could feel it, had told them once in the shorthand of her hands and her slate: *the mountain's pulse is wrong here. Too fast. Like a heart before a fever breaks.* She crouched, laid two fingers against the wet stone, and frowned at the rhythm she read there.

Ansel stood close to Ivo, close enough that she could feel the warmth of him through her sleeve. He had not slept well since the Shrine of Nine Bells. None of them had, but the boy wore his sleeplessness differently — in the way he hummed under his breath without realizing it, harmonies that made the dripping water fall out of time, in the way he flinched when his own shadow moved wrong in the lamplight. The Choir was calling him. Ivo could not hear the call, but she could see the shape it left in him, the way you can see where a hand has gripped a shoulder by the wrinkle it leaves in the cloth.

"It's not far now," she said, and hated how gently her voice came out. She was not a gentle woman. She was a scribe, a smuggler's-road guide, a woman who had signed away her grandmother's face on a ghost's tollbridge and had not yet decided what that loss had made of her. But gentleness kept arriving in her mouth anyway, uninvited, whenever she looked at Ansel. "The boundary of the court. After the stair, there's a gallery of standing stones — the old boundary markers. We rest there, and then—"

"Then we do what we came to do." Serel's voice, from the rear. Cold, level, the voice of a woman reading a ledger aloud. She had been quiet for two days. Ivo had told herself that quiet was fatigue. She had been a fool, and she would have a long time to know it.

Brannoc grunted and shifted the lantern higher. The big mercenary moved like a man carrying a wound already — he had been favoring his left side since the tollbridge, since he'd signed away the name of his eaten god and discovered, Ivo suspected, that the memory had been load-bearing. "Do what we came to do," he echoed. "Aye. Lovely plan. Very detailed."

"Brannoc," Ivo began.

"Save it." But there was no edge in it. There was something worse — something tired and fond. He had stopped pretending, somewhere in the dark, that he was only in this for the coin. He'd told her about his god by firelight, in the flat voice of a man describing weather that had killed his family: *the court ate him. I felt it happen. Like a tooth pulled from the world's jaw.* He had joined her secret plan after that — the plan to keep Ansel from being used, the plan Serel did not know about, or so they had believed.

They moved down the stair in single file. The water rang beneath their boots. Ivo counted the turns the way her grandmother had taught her — *seven to the landing, three to the shelf, and never trust a tunnel that doesn't echo back* — and on the fourth turn, the echo came back wrong.

It came back doubled.

She stopped so abruptly that Ansel walked into her back. "Wait." She held up the lamp. The flame bent, not from wind but from something in the air itself, a pressure like the moment before a door opens in a sealed room. "The echo. Listen."

They listened. The mountain dripped. And beneath the drip, faint as a thread pulled through cloth, something answered each footfall a half-beat late — not their footsteps, but the *idea* of their footsteps, a choir of boots rehearsing their walking.

"They know we're here," Ansel whispered. His hand had found Ivo's sleeve without either of them deciding it. "They've known since the bells. Since—"

"Since someone told them," Ivo said.

She turned. Serel stood at the top of the last flight of steps, and she was not looking at the dark below. She was looking at Ansel, and her expression was not cruelty. That was the horror of it, Ivo would think later, turning it over like a page she couldn't stop rereading. Serel's face held the serene satisfaction of an accountant watching the columns finally sum.

"I'm sorry it's this stair," Serel said. "I asked for the bridge, but the Abbot insisted the boundary stones were cleaner. Fewer witnesses."

The world did a slow, sick half-turn. Ivo's hand went to her knife out of pure animal reflex and found, absurdly, that she was still holding the lamp in the other hand, that she was a scribe standing in dripping dark with a boy behind her and a liar above her, and that all her competence had just been spent as someone else's coin.

"Serel." Brannoc's voice had gone very quiet. Sheathed steel has a sound, when a big man lets you hear it. "Say the rest of it. Say it all, so I know exactly how deep to bury you."

"The temple has a debt older than the Abbot's office," Serel said, as if reporting the weather. "Vesh holds it. The Abbot has negotiated its forgiveness. The price is the vessel — delivered to the god's servants at the boundary, unharmed, willing or not. My part was the route. Your part," and here her cold eyes moved to Ivo with something almost like professional respect, "was making sure the vessel arrived. You did that beautifully. I only had to keep you from turning aside."

"The village," Ivo said. The word came out of her like a stone coughed up from the lung. "The village that forgot its name. You wanted to press on. I stayed to help them. That was you clearing the way."

"That was me following the Abbot's schedule." Serel stepped down one stair. Two. From the dark below the landing came a sound Ivo had heard once before, at the Shrine of Nine Bells, and had hoped never to hear again: the sound of many feet moving in perfect unison, a congregation of one thing. "The servants of Vesh are already here, Ivo. They have been following us since the shrine. Fighting them would cost Brannoc his remaining blood and Ansel his remaining self, and it would change nothing. The trade is made. The Abbot's seal is on it."

"The Abbot's seal isn't worth the wax," Brannoc snarled, "if the wax is on a lie—"

"It isn't a lie." For the first time, something moved behind Serel's eyes — not doubt, but the ghost of a justification, rehearsed so long it had worn a groove. "Sacrifice is arithmetic, Ivo. One boy, already half-belonging to the dark, against a temple's freedom. Against every debt the old gods hold over every parish in Thornfell. You've seen what the village lost. Multiply it. That is what the Abbot is buying back."

"And what are you buying?" Ivo's voice shook, and she let it. "What's your line in the ledger, Serel?"

Serel did not answer. She didn't have to. The unison footsteps reached the landing, and the servants of Vesh came up into the lamplight.

They had been men once — Ivo was nearly sure of it. Now they were dressed in vestments of gray silk that moved like smoke underwater, and their faces were borrowed, each one a patchwork of features that did not agree with its neighbors: one eye from an old woman, a young man's jaw, a mouth that had clearly sung for a living. Bell-sound lived in them the way blood lives in the living. When they moved, they chimed.

"Ansel," Ivo said, and did the only arithmetic she had ever believed in. She put her body between the boy and the stair.

What happened next happened in pieces, the way disasters do — each fragment whole and bright and separate, the connective tissue of time torn away.

*Piece one:* Brannoc moving. Not toward Serel, though she was the traitor and some tidy part of the story would have wanted that. Toward the servants, because they were the thing that could actually take the boy, and Brannoc had spent two days telling Ivo, in his gruff grief-disguised way, that he was done watching gods eat what they wanted. He came off the stair like a landslide with a sword, and the first servant's borrowed face opened in genuine surprise as the blade took it through the gray silk, and the thing came apart into a sound — a single deep bell-note that Ivo felt in her back teeth.

*Piece two:* Serel's hand closing on Ansel's arm. Not violent. That was the worst piece. She took the boy the way you take a child by the hand in a market crowd, and Ansel — who had flinched from his own shadow for days — did not flinch from her. He looked at her with something worse than fear. He looked at her with understanding. "You're the arithmetic," he said, in his cracked choirboy voice, and Serel's jaw tightened as if he'd struck her.

*Piece three:* Ivo's knife finding silk and finding nothing behind it, her lamp shattering on the stair, the dark arriving all at once, total, ringing.

*Piece four:* Brannoc's roar — not pain, not yet — and the wet heavy sound of a sword hilt connecting with borrowed bone, and a voice like a landslide saying, over and over, *run, girl, run, take him and run—*

*Piece five:* Light. Not lamplight. The boundary stones below the landing, the standing markers of the old court, glowing from within like teeth in a dark mouth, and between them a doorway of sound standing open, and Serel walking Ansel through it while the boy twisted in her grip and sang — sang, God help them, he *sang*, and his voice made the doorway shiver with want.

"No!" Ivo came off her knees with no memory of falling. She got three steps. A servant's hand — an old woman's knuckles on a young man's wrist — closed around her throat, and it was not strong, it was worse than strong, it was *resonant*. Her own voice came out of her throat as a bell-tone, stolen mid-scream, and the servant wore her scream like a scarf while its borrowed eyes looked at her with something that was almost pity.

Then Brannoc hit the servant like a cathedral falling.

They went down together. Ivo heard the crack of it — stone, or bone, or both. Heard Brannoc's sword ring off the standing stone and snap. Heard him laugh, short and awful, the laugh of a man whose god was dead and who had decided that was no reason to stop being what his god had made him. "Run," he said again, and this time it cost him, and this time she heard it cost him, a wet tearing sound beneath the words like a page ripping slow.

She ran. She hated every step. She would run those three steps backward in her mind for the rest of her life, and every version of her would run them anyway, because Ansel was already through the doorway and Brannoc was already down and the arithmetic of the moment had no other column to write in.

She caught Ansel's hand as Serel pushed him through — caught it, actually held it, felt his fingers grip hers with a strength that broke her heart, a child's grip, a drowning grip — and then the doorway of sound closed between them like water closing over a stone, and her hand was empty, and the boundary stones went dark, and the Weeping Stair dripped its slow funeral bells into a silence that had a boy's voice missing from it.

* * *

Brannoc was dying by inches, and being insufferable about it.

Ivo knelt beside him on the landing, her hands red to the wrist, doing the work her grandmother had taught her for the smugglers who came back opened up — packing the wound under his ribs, binding it, packing the one below his arm where a servant's bell-blade had gone in and, by some mercy Ivo did not believe in, come back out. He had killed four of the servants before they brought him down. Four bell-notes still hung in the stairwell, fading so slowly they seemed to be deciding whether to fade at all.

"Odile," Ivo said, and Odile was already there, slate in hand, her fingers moving fast. *The doorway. Where did it go.* Ivo read the cartographer's hands the way she read any text. "Down. Deeper than we've mapped. The boundary stones are a mouth, not a door — the court swallowed him through its own front teeth." She pressed down harder on the bandage than she meant to, and Brannoc hissed. "Sorry."

"Don't be." His voice was a ruin. He grinned through the blood on his teeth anyway. "You should see the other congregation."

"Brannoc." She kept her hands moving so they wouldn't shake. "You're not dying. I forbid it. I've had a very bad night and I am not permitting one more loss, do you hear me?"

"Never could refuse a scribe." He coughed, and the cough was bad, and all three of them heard how bad. Odile's hands stilled. Then, deliberately, the deaf cartographer took his huge bloody paw and pressed it flat against her own throat, against the place where her voice would have lived if she'd ever had one, and held it there. Brannoc went still. Whatever he felt — the pulse of her, the vibration of her breath, the wordless sentence she was spelling into his palm — it quieted him more than any prayer to any god, eaten or otherwise, had ever done.

"They'll take him to the Choir," Ivo said. The words came out flat because flat was the only way they would come out at all. "Vesh's servants. The god needs a door back into the world, and Ansel is the door, and the Abbot has sold him through it to buy the temple's debts. And I led him here. Every step. I counted the turns. I knew this road by heartbeat, and I delivered him like a parcel."

"You delivered him," Brannoc said, "to a liar with a ledger. Different crime. Lighter sentence." He tried to sit up, failed, and glared at his own body with the contempt of a soldier for unreliable equipment. "Ivo. Listen. The avatar. The bell-woman. She said the gods need a *willing* voice. That's why they want the boy — or why they say they want him. But Serel's trade hands them a slave. A bought thing. You felt it at the shrine — the avatar flinched when the Abbot's seal came up. There's a crack in that arithmetic. You're the one who reads contracts. Go find the flaw."

"The flaw is that I'm lying in a tunnel with my hands full of your blood and my heart full of nothing."

"The flaw," Brannoc said, and his hand found her wrist, grip still strong, grip of a man who had carried a god's name once and would not go out empty, "is that you're the only one of us they haven't figured out yet. They counted on the guide. They counted on the muscle. They counted on the traitor. Nobody counts on the half-souled scribe with her grandmother's contract burned into what's left of her." His grin came back, terrible and warm. "Go be uncounted."

Ivo looked at Odile. Odile looked back, and then down at her slate, and wrote one line and turned it around:

*The court's heart is where the vibrations braid. I can find it. Can you walk into it?*

Ivo looked at her hands. Red to the wrist. She thought of Ansel's grip on her fingers, the last thing the doorway had left her. She thought of a page in a scriptorium, a prayer answered in handwriting not her own, the first moment the world had shown her it worked differently than she'd been taught — and how she had kept the page, because she was a woman who kept things, who held on, who did not let the dark have the last copy.

"Yes," she said.

Brannoc laughed once, softly, and closed his eyes. "Then go," he murmured, already sliding down into the dark that was either sleep or the beginning of the long arithmetic of dying. "And Ivo — when you find the bell-woman—"

"Yes?"

"Ask her what the gods are really hungry for." His breathing slowed, ragged, stubborn. "And don't sign anything."

Ivo stood. Above them, the mountain wept its slow bells. Below them, through the standing stones, the dark of the court waited, holding a choirboy the way a ledger holds a name.

She picked up Odile's lantern, and they went down.
