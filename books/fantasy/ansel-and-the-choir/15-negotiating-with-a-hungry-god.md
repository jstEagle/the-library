## 15. Negotiating With a Hungry God

Ivo left them at the mouth of the ravine with instructions that were mostly lies.

*Stay here. If I'm not back by the time the second bell would ring in Thornfell — you won't hear bells down here, but count it anyway — drag Brannoc back toward the Weeping Stair and don't look for me.* Odile had gripped her wrist, hard, and pressed something into her palm: a sliver of chalk, worn to a nub, warm from the cartographer's hand. *Draw your way out,* her eyes said. Ivo nodded as if she understood, which was only half a lie too.

Brannoc was unconscious on a litter of smugglers' canvas, his breathing wet and uneven, the wound in his side bound with strips of Serel's abandoned cloak because it was the cleanest cloth they had. That was its own kind of joke. Ivo had stopped laughing somewhere around the fourth hour of carrying him.

Ansel was gone. Taken up the Weeping Stair by Vesh's servants — if servants was the word for things that moved like poured shadow wearing borrowed faces — and every minute that passed was a minute the choirboy spent dissolving into something that sang.

So she went alone, down the ravine the servants had come from, following a sound that was not quite sound: a resonance in her teeth, in the hollow behind her sternum, like standing inside a struck bell that hadn't finished ringing. The chalk sliver rode in her fist. Her grandmother's name rode in her chest, or what remained where the memory of her grandmother's face used to be — a shape with a hole in it, an absence she kept touching the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

The ravine opened without warning into a cavern that should not have existed this far under Thornfell, because it had a sky.

Not a real one. A ceiling so high and so dark it read as night, hung with points of pale light that might have been glowworms or might have been eyes, arranged in constellations no navigator had ever named. Below them stretched a plain of grey stone, and on the plain stood the Shrine of Nine Bells — which Ivo had seen once, years ago, on the mountain's sunlit shoulder, small and moss-eaten and ignored. This was not that shrine. This was that shrine the way a wolf is a picture of a wolf. It rose nine-sided and vast, its bronze bells each tall as temple doors, hanging silent in frames of black wood, and the silence coming off them was heavier than any ringing could have been.

She was expected. Of course she was expected.

The avatar stood between the first two bells, and Ivo's breath caught, because she knew that face.

It was her grandmother's face.

Not remembered — taken, rendered perfectly from whatever deep archive the court kept of traded things: the strong jaw, the laugh-lines, the small scar above the left brow from a kettle long ago. The woman wearing it wore it slightly wrong, the way a singer wears a song — expressive, inhabited, but not owned. Her dress was grey wool like a working woman's; her hands were folded like a woman waiting at a gate.

"You keep her face," Ivo said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "You've been wearing my grandmother."

"We wear what is given," the avatar said, and the voice was bell-sound shaped into words, harmonics stacked on harmonics until meaning rang out of them. "Nothing here is stolen that was not first sold. Your grandmother gave much to this court, Ivo Quintana. Is it such a desecration, to be remembered by the thing she fed?"

"Don't." The word cracked out of her before she could dress it in composure. "Don't use her voice to talk to me about giving."

"Very well." And the face changed — flowed like water finding a new level — into a face Ivo didn't know, then another, then a third, cycling through strangers, through the deaf cartographer's patient features, through Brannoc's battered ones, settling finally on a face with no particular history at all: young, plain, forgettable. A face designed to be argued with on neutral terms. "Better? You came alone, past your wounded mercenary and your maps. You came to negotiate. So let us negotiate honestly, scribe. Ask me anything, and I will answer it true. That is the oldest courtesy of the nine bells, and I am bound to it."

Ivo walked closer across the grey stone. Her boots made no echo. She noticed that and hated it — even her footsteps were being swallowed, filed away, added to some ledger.

"The Abbot," she said. "What did he promise you?"

"That Ansel be delivered to the boundary of the court, unresisting, whole in body." The avatar's borrowed face did not smile, but something behind it did. "In exchange, the temple of Saint Vellum's founding debt — four hundred years old, signed in blood and tallow by the first Abbot — is forgiven. The temple goes free. One boy's soul against an institution's freedom. He believes he has driven a hard bargain."

"And have you?"

"I have driven an easy one." For the first time the avatar moved, turning, gesturing up at the nine silent bells with a hand that caught the false starlight. "Do you know what we are, Ivo Quintana? Not the court beneath the mountain — the court is younger than us, a bureaucracy grown over our bones like ivy over a statue. I mean *us*. The old ones. Vesh and the eight who sang the mountain up out of the deep stone when the world was young enough to be sung to. Do you know why we slept?"

"No one taught us anything but Saint Vellum," Ivo said. "Saint Vellum says there are no gods worth the name under the earth. Only hunger wearing crowns."

"Saint Vellum," said the avatar, "was a man who came here begging, four hundred years ago, and was refused, and wrote his refusal into scripture." The bells shivered, though nothing touched them. "We slept because we starved. Do you understand what a god eats, scribe? Not blood. Not sacrifice — the altars of your temple, the smoke and slaughtered lambs, those feed nothing but priests' conceits. A god eats *voice*. Freely given voice. Praise that means something. Grief offered honestly. A mortal who sings to the dark because the dark is listening and the mortal *wants* to be heard. That is our bread. And your Saint Vellum built a religion on the principle that the divine must never be addressed directly — that all prayer must pass through intermediaries, liturgies, licensed intercessors. He walled off the well and called the thirst a sin."

Ivo thought of the scriptorium. Of decades of copied prayers, petitions written in her careful hand and sent nowhere, answered by nothing, filed in archives that gathered dust and silverfish. *Prayers that no god answers.* She had believed the gods were absent. It had never occurred to her that they were starving behind a wall men built.

"So you woke hungry," she said.

"We woke because the wall cracked." The avatar's face flickered — grief moving through borrowed features like weather. "A child began singing in his sleep. Down here, where we could hear it. Do you know what Ansel's humming sounded like to us, after four centuries of silence? It sounded like rain to a man dying of thirst in a desert. It sounded like *morning*." The voice dropped, and the harmonics in it went ragged. "We are old, Ivo. Old things, when they starve, do not wake gentle. The court beneath us — the Regent and its tollkeepers and its contracts — grew in our sleep like mold in a dark house, feeding on the only currency this mountain has ever traded: pieces of souls, sold and signed and securitized. And when we woke ravenous, the court learned quickly how to turn our hunger into profit. Bring the god a voice, the court said, and we will broker the transaction. For a fee."

"The fee being Ansel."

"The fee being everything, eventually. But yes. First Ansel."

Ivo wrapped her arms around herself. The false sky pressed down. Somewhere among those unnamed constellations, she suspected, things were watching this conversation and pricing it.

"Then take me instead," she said. "That's why I came. You want a mortal voice in your Choir — the Regent told me as much, or near enough. I'm offering mine. Freely. Let Ansel go."

And the avatar laughed.

It was not a cruel laugh. That was the worst of it. It was the laugh of someone who has watched a child reach for a hot stove with the best intentions, and it rang from all nine bells at once, filling the cavern, shaking grit from the ceiling of the false night.

"Oh, scribe. Oh, brave, foolish, *scribal* girl. You think the offer is the problem?" The laughter died and left the silence rawer. "Walk with me."

The avatar turned and led her along the face of the shrine, and Ivo followed, because there was nothing else to do. Up close she could see that the bells were not empty. Behind the bronze of each one, faint as figures behind frosted glass, shapes moved — or rather, *sang*, mouths open in profiles frozen mid-note, dozens of them, hundreds, layered like reflections in deep water.

"The Choir," the avatar said quietly. "What remains of the nine of us, and what we have gathered over ten thousand years. Every voice ever freely given to us lives here. When a mortal gives us their voice — truly gives it, chooses it — they do not die. They *join*. They become part of the singing that holds the mountain up, that keeps the deep roads passable, that turns the seasons over Thornfell like pages. Your harvests. Your mild winters. Your mountains that do not shrug their shoulders and bury your villages. All of it is the Choir, working. And the Choir has been thinning for four hundred years, Ivo. Voices fade when they are not renewed. We are running out of song. Another century and the mountain comes down on every head in Thornfell, and no one will even know why."

Ivo stared at the shapes behind the bronze. One of them, near the surface of the third bell, turned — or seemed to — and looked at her with an expression of infinite, patient sorrow.

"Then take my voice," she said again, and her voice shook now. "I'm choosing it. Right now. I choose it."

"And that," said the avatar gently, "is exactly what the Regent is counting on."

The words landed like cold water.

"Think, scribe. You are trained to think; it is the one virtue of your profession. Why does the court beneath us exist? What does a contract-keeper *eat*? Not voices — those are its inventory, its coin. It eats *transaction*. Every signature, every bargain struck, every debt incurred — the act itself feeds it. A freely given gift is poison to the Regent. A gift cannot be taxed, brokered, or resold. So when the old gods woke starving and the court needed leverage over us—" the avatar spread its borrowed hands, "—it found a way to make even our salvation a sale. Deliver the boy Ansel to the boundary, and the court will hand him to us as payment for debts owed. A *slave's* voice, purchased and transferred, entering our Choir under contract. And a contracted voice in the Choir is a hook in the god's own throat. Through him, the Regent would hold title — not to one soul, but to the Choir entire. To *us*. To the mountain. To every harvest and every season, forever, under lien."

The cavern seemed to tilt. Ivo put a hand against the cold bronze of the nearest bell and felt the trapped singers shudder at her touch.

"The Abbot isn't freeing the temple," she said slowly. "He's foreclosing on heaven."

"He is doing arithmetic," the avatar corrected. "Serel learned it from him; Serel is merely honest about it. Sacrifice, calculated. One boy, weighed against an institution. They believe the equation balances. They do not understand that the moment Ansel enters the Choir as property, the equation consumes *everything* — including the temple they meant to save. Including the gods they imagine they are bribing. We would become what Saint Vellum always claimed we were: hunger wearing crowns. He would be right, at last, posthumously, and it would be his descendants who made him so."

Ivo thought of Ansel asleep in the choir loft, candles guttering in time with his breathing. Thought of him on the road, sharing his supper with Brannoc, asking Odile earnest questions he'd write down phonetically because he couldn't hear her answers but wanted them anyway. A boy. A door. A slave.

"And if the trade happens," she asked, "can it be undone?"

"Everything can be undone," said the avatar. "That is the tragedy of contracts. Undoing is simply a more expensive transaction." Its face shifted again — and settled, deliberately, into her grandmother's, and this time Ivo did not flinch, because she understood now that the avatar was not mocking her. It was speaking to her in the only language of trust it had left: the faces of the given. "But hear the rest of it, Ivo. Hear why I tell you true. We need a voice in the Choir. A free one. Given, not sold — chosen awake, walking in with open eyes. Such a voice would burn through the Regent's claim like fire through rope. It would renew us for another age. It would set Ansel free as a side effect, the way dawn sets lamps unnecessary." The bell-sound voice softened. "We cannot take it. Taking is the one thing that ruins it. It must be *offered*. By someone who understands the price. That is why I answer your questions truly. That is why I wear the faces of the generous dead. We are not your enemy, scribe. We are a starving thing trying very hard, at the end of a very long famine, not to become the monster your scriptures say we already are."

Ivo stood a long time in the silence of the nine bells.

She understood it. That was the terrible thing — she understood the logic completely, the way she understood a well-drafted contract, clause by clause, and found it *sound*. The gods were starving. The Choir was failing. The mountain would fall. A free voice was the cure, and no one could be forced to be the cure, because force was the disease. Every word of it was true.

And every word of it was also, precisely, the argument a hungry thing would make.

*Understood too well*, she thought. That's the trap they never warn you about in the scriptorium — not lies, which a scribe can smell like smoke, but truths offered by the party that benefits. Her grandmother had understood that too, maybe. Had stood somewhere near this spot, or one like it, and weighed a truth against a price, and signed.

"What happens to the one who gives?" Ivo asked. "Truly. No courtesy required. The truth."

The avatar was quiet for a moment. The false stars burned overhead.

"They join the Choir," it said. "They sing. They are more alive than they have ever been, and they are not alive at all. They do not walk the sunlit mountain again. They do not finish their books or keep their promises or grow old. From outside, it will look exactly like a death. From inside—" the borrowed face, her grandmother's face, closed its eyes, "—from inside, it is the first day of a feast after a lifetime of fasting. I will not lie to you to win you. If I lied, you would be worthless to us anyway. A coerced voice is a poisoned gift. Only a scribe who has read every clause and signs anyway — *that* is the voice that saves us."

Above them, deep in the false night, one of the pale lights flickered and went out. Then another. The avatar looked up, and Ivo saw fear move through the borrowed face — real fear, ancient and undignified.

"The court knows you're here," it said. "The Regent has begun spending Ansel's soul to speed the transfer — drawing on the account, as it were. Every light that dies up there is a piece of him being consumed to bind the contract faster. You have until the last star fails, scribe. After that, the boy is paper, and paper can be filed anywhere."

Ivo's hand closed around Odile's chalk stub until it bit.

"How do I get to the heart of the court," she said, "before the sky goes out?"

The avatar smiled with her grandmother's mouth, and it was, horribly, comforting.

"Why," it said, "you take the road only a Quintana can walk. The one your grandmother bought and paid for, and that the court has been trying to forget it owes. Come. I'll show you the door — and then, scribe, you must run faster than a contract."

* * *

Behind them, high in the false heavens, a third star guttered, and somewhere far below, in a cage woven of harmony, a boy who was forgetting his own name heard the loss of it like a bell going silent — and, without knowing why, began to hum louder.
