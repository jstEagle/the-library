## 16. Descent Into the Court

**IVO**

The mouth of the deep road was not a door. It was a wound.

Ivo had known the smugglers' tunnels since she was nine years old, when her grandmother first walked her through them by candlelight and taught her to count her heartbeats between turns — *forty to the fork, sixty to the drip-stone, hold your breath past the cold*. But this was not that road. This was the road beneath it, the one the smugglers only whispered about and never named, and it opened out of the mountain's flank like a split in old leather, breathing warm air that smelled of tallow and turned earth and something sweeter underneath, something like incense burned down to its last quarter-inch.

She stood at the threshold with a lantern in one hand and Odile's map-case slung across her back, and she made herself look at what waited below.

Stairs. Hundreds of them, cut into living rock, descending in a spiral so wide that the far wall was lost in dark. And on every stair, carved shallow as a whisper, were names.

Thousands of names. Tens of thousands. They climbed the walls too, and the ceiling, overlapping, crowding, some worn nearly smooth by time and some so fresh they still held the shadow of the chisel. Ivo crouched and set her lantern close to the nearest one. The letters were careful, practiced — the hand of someone who had written his own name many times before he cut it here.

*Marrec, son of Marrec.*

"Signing," Brannoc said behind her. His voice was flat, but his hand wasn't; it kept drifting toward the axe at his back the way a tongue drifts toward a broken tooth. "They signed themselves in."

"They paid their way in," Ivo said. "Or they were paid for." She rose. Her knees ached. She was twenty-six years old and every joint felt seventy. "Either way, the court keeps receipts."

Odile touched her elbow. When Ivo turned, the cartographer held up her slate: *Feel it?*

Ivo listened. At first there was nothing — the mountain's ordinary silence, which was never truly silent, only patient. Then she caught it, low and rhythmic, rising up through the soles of her boots like a pulse heard through a floor. A hum. Many voices, blended past telling, singing something without words at a pitch just below hearing.

The Choir. Ansel was somewhere inside that sound.

*It's louder than my charts predicted,* Odile wrote. Her face, usually so composed it might have been carved from the same rock as the stairs, had gone careful. *The whole mountain is resonating. We're standing inside an instrument.*

"Then we'd best not give it anything to play," Ivo said, and stepped onto the first stair, over the name of Marrec, son of Marrec, and began to descend.

* * *

**BRANNOC**

Brannoc had fought in eleven wars, if you counted the border skirmishes, and he had learned one lesson so thoroughly it lived in his hands now rather than his head: *never go where the ground itself is against you.* He could read a battlefield the way Odile read stone. He knew the feel of a slope that wanted to kill him, the smell of an ambush before the ambushers knew it themselves.

This place smelled wrong in a way he had no word for.

Not rot. Not blood. It smelled like a church after the congregation has gone home — wax and old smoke and the ghost of singing — except the singing hadn't gone anywhere. It followed them down. Three hundred steps, four hundred, and always that hum beneath everything, patient as tide.

And the galleries began.

They came off the stairway at intervals, doorways cut into the outer wall, each opening into a chamber lit by no flame Brannoc could find. In the first gallery stood rows of objects on shelves of black stone: a child's shoe. A wedding ring. A wooden bird with half its paint worn away. A lock of hair tied with ribbon gone brown. Ordinary things, worthless things, arranged with terrible care, and around each one the air seemed slightly thicker, slightly warmer, as though the object were still being thought about by someone who no longer remembered why.

"Toll-keeper's stock," Ivo murmured. She didn't touch anything. Smart. "Memories people traded. The court doesn't keep the memory itself — it keeps the proof. The anchor. You can't take a memory out of a head cleanly; it leaves a splinter. These are the splinters."

Brannoc looked at the wooden bird a long moment. He didn't know why. There was nothing in it for him. Except — and he hated himself for the thought — he wondered whose hands had worn the paint off its wing. Some child's thumbs, worrying at it summer after summer. And now the child was grown, or dead, or walking these same stairs above them with a hole in their head shaped exactly like a wooden bird, smiling at strangers they couldn't recognize, the way that village had smiled.

His god had been eaten in a place like this. He had felt it happen — felt the prayer he was saying mid-sentence go suddenly nowhere, like shouting into a room where everyone had left. He had spent six years since then praying anyway, out of stubbornness, because a man's habits were the last thing anyone got to take from him.

He looked at the shelves and understood, finally, what he was looking at. Not trinkets. A larder.

"Keep moving," he said, and his voice came out rougher than he meant it to. "Don't linger near 'em. Things that get stored long enough—" He stopped. Started again. "In the wars, we had granaries. Rats always found them. Whatever feeds on this stuff knows where the stores are."

Ivo gave him a look — quick, sharp, grateful — and moved on. Odile squeezed his forearm once, brief, and fell in beside him.

The second gallery was different. No shelves. Instead, the walls were hung with mirrors, dozens of them, tarnished silver in frames of bone-white wood, and Brannoc made the mistake of glancing into one.

His reflection looked back at him. That was expected. What wasn't expected was that the reflection was praying. Lips moving, eyes shut, hands folded — a younger Brannoc, twenty years younger, kneeling at a shrine to a god whose name he could barely hold onto anymore, saying the evening prayer the way he'd said it ten thousand times.

He wrenched his gaze away. His heart was going like a drum.

*I saw mine too,* Odile wrote, when he told them. She hesitated, chalk hovering. *Mine was drawing. A map I never finished. I've been trying to remember it since I was twelve.*

"The corridors sing back your own voice," Ivo said quietly. "That's what the smugglers say. Now we know what tune."

* * *

**ODILE**

Odile had been deaf since the fever took her at seven, and she had spent the thirty years since learning what most hearing people never suspected: that silence was not empty. The world was full of vibration — footfalls in floorboards, thunder in window glass, a voice's shape pressed into the skin of a drum, into a table, into the palm of a hand laid flat against a wall. She had built her life on reading those tremors. She had built her maps on them. Other cartographers measured with chains and compasses; Odile walked a tunnel barefoot and let the stone tell her its history, its hollows, its lies.

The stone under the mountain was screaming.

Not loudly. That was the horror of it. It screamed the way a choir screams when the conductor demands a note no throat can hold — sustained, controlled, exquisite, and utterly beyond endurance. Every surface she touched thrummed with it. The stairs. The walls. The air itself carried it, a pressure against her skin like weather.

And threaded through the great sustained note were smaller ones. Individual voices. She could feel them the way you can feel individual threads in woven cloth if you know how to pull. Each one distinct. Each one *straining*.

Ansel was in there. Somewhere in that fabric, a thread that had been woven in recently, still rough-edged, still fighting the pattern.

She marked it on her chart as they descended — the charcoal stylus moving fast across waxed cloth, recording what her hands knew and her companions' ears could never gather. This was the thing about Odile that the temple had never understood, that the Abbot had dismissed, that Serel had sneered at: she did not need to hear the Choir to map it. In some ways she was the only one who could. Sound passed through hearing people and was gone. Vibration stayed in her hands. She could hold the Choir's shape while it sang, turn it over, find its seams.

And it had seams. Everything woven had seams.

*Here,* she wrote, at the fifth gallery, stopping them all with a raised hand. She knelt and pressed both palms flat to the floor, closed her eyes, and read.

The main note ran downward and inward, spiraling toward a vast hollow — the Choir's heart, the Regent's hall, whatever the court called its center. Everyone would assume the way in lay along that note, following the loudest song. But beneath it, almost buried, was another vibration: an old one, faint, dissonant, running sideways through the rock like a crack through porcelain. It didn't belong to the Choir's harmony. It predated it. It was the sound of the mountain before the court, a fault-line of raw unclaimed stone, and it led —

Her breath caught. Even deaf, even alone inside her own skull, she made a small involuntary sound, and both of them turned.

*There's a way in the gods forgot,* she wrote. Her hand shook, and she steadied it. *A seam in the mountain older than the contracts. The Choir doesn't guard it because the Choir can't hear it. It sings outside their key.*

Ivo read it twice. "Show me."

*It's not on any path. We'll have to climb down through a flooded gallery and along a vein of soft stone. It will be slow. And—* Odile paused, hating the next line, writing it anyway. *Once we're in the seam, the Choir won't be able to track us. But neither will we be able to hear it. If Ansel needs us sooner—*

"Sooner is a luxury," Ivo said. Her face in the lantern light was gray with exhaustion, and set like mortar. "We get one chance at this. If we walk in the front door, we arrive as trespassers, and this whole place runs on taking things from trespassers. We go in through the crack in the world. We go in the way nobody's ever gone in." She looked at Odile, and something passed between them that needed no slate — the specific respect of two people whom institutions had overlooked, holding the only true map between them.

Brannoc cracked his neck and spat, ceremonially, into the dark. "Lead on, then. I've crawled through worse for worse pay."

*You haven't,* Odile wrote, and was pleased when he laughed.

* * *

**IVO**

The flooded gallery was exactly as bad as Odile had promised. The water came to their thighs and was warm — body-warm, which was infinitely worse than cold — and it moved when they moved, swirling around them with a sluggish deliberateness that Ivo refused to think about. Things drifted in it. Small things. A button. A ring. Once, turning slowly, a page of sheet music, the notes blurred past reading.

She kept her grandmother's counting in her head. Forty steps to the ledge. Sixty to the vein of soft stone. Hold your breath past the cold — except there was no cold, there was only warmth, everywhere, the whole mountain cradling them like something it intended to keep.

Halfway along the ledge, the corridor began to sing back her own voice.

It started subtly — a hum at the edge of hearing that resolved, as they went deeper, into melody. Her melody. A lullaby her grandmother had sung, off-key, in the kitchen in Thornfell village, with flour on her hands. Ivo had not thought of that lullaby in years. She had certainly never sung it aloud here. But the corridor had it, note for note, flaw off-key in the same places, and then it added the next verse, the one Ivo had forgotten, and hearing it was like a hand reaching into her chest and turning over everything it found there.

"That's the toll again," Brannoc growled. "Ignore it."

"I can't." Her voice cracked. "It knows verses I don't remember knowing. It's singing me *back to myself*." She pressed a hand to the wall and felt, beneath the great choral scream, the corridor's small malicious music — and beneath that, faint as a heartbeat through a door, the seam-vibration Odile had found. Still there. Still outside the key.

She made herself listen to the lullaby all the way to its end. That was the trick, she realized. The corridor wanted her to flinch, to run, to cover her ears — because a memory fled from is a memory that tears, and torn memories leave splinters, and splinters were currency here. So she stood in the flooded dark and let her grandmother's voice wash over her, every botched note, and she *kept* it, deliberately, fiercely, the way you cup a match in wind.

When the song ended, the corridor was silent. Sulkily, she thought. She was almost certain it sulked.

*Well done,* Odile wrote, watching her face.

"It wants us to lose pieces," Ivo said. "So we arrive incomplete. Cheaper, maybe. Easier to file." She lifted the lantern. Ahead, the ledge narrowed to a crack in the rock — a vertical seam, barely shoulder-width, exhaling air that tasted of nothing at all. No incense. No tallow. Just stone and cold and age. The unclaimed dark.

She thought of Ansel asleep in the choir loft, humming harmonies no one taught him. She thought of her grandmother's hands signing a contract at the mountain's mouth, and of the seal she had seen burned into the ash where her memory of that face should be. She thought: *everything down here is a bargain, and I am done bargaining.*

"In single file," she said. "Odile first — she reads the stone. Brannoc behind her. I'll take the rear with the light." She managed something that was almost a smile. "And if anything in there offers you anything — a wish, a memory, your heart's desire, a fair price — you say nothing. You sign nothing. You sing nothing. Understood?"

Brannoc grunted. Odile saluted, dry as chalk.

Ivo looked back once, up the long spiral of signed stairs, at the thousands of names climbing into the dark above them — Marrec, son of Marrec, and all the rest, every one a person who had come down here believing they were making a trade.

Then she turned into the seam, into the oldest dark in the mountain, and the rock closed behind them like a book.

Somewhere far below, past galleries and flooded halls and corridors of borrowed song, the Choir changed key — and in the new chord, thin and strained and unmistakably fighting it, Ivo heard a boy's voice trying to remember its own name.
