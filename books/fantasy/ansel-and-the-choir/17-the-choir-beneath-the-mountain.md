## 17. The Choir Beneath the Mountain

The first thing Ansel lost was his fear.

He noticed it the way you notice a candle going out in a room full of other candles — a small darkness, quickly filled. Fear had been with him so long it had felt like a bone, something structural, something that held him up. When the Choir took it, he reached for it out of habit and found only smooth, singing silence where it had been.

*That's all right,* said the Choir, in a hundred voices that were also one voice, that were also his voice. *Fear is heavy. We carry nothing here.*

He had been in the court beneath the mountain for — he did not know how long. Time down there was not a river but a chord: everything sounding at once, past and future pressed into harmony. He had tried counting his heartbeats and lost the number somewhere past ten thousand, because his heart had begun keeping time with the Choir instead of for itself.

He sat — or floated; sitting was a courtesy the Choir allowed him — in a gallery of pale stone that curved away in both directions like the inside of a throat. The walls were veined with light the color of candleflame seen through milk. And everywhere, everywhere, the singing.

It was beautiful. That was the horror of it, and he clung to the horror the way a drowning man clings to a rock that is also grinding him to sand. It was *beautiful*, and every hour it was more beautiful, and every hour he was less able to say why that was wrong.

Because he remembered — he made himself remember, the way you press your tongue into a loose tooth — that beauty taken from you was not the same as beauty given.

*Who gave it to you?* the Choir asked, gentle, amused, the way an older sibling asks about a childhood superstition. *No one. You were sold before you could choose anything. Your whole life above was a waiting room. Here you are only arriving.*

"I have a name," Ansel said aloud, because saying it aloud was work, and work was resistance, and resistance was all he had left that was truly his.

*Do you?* A ripple of laughter moved through the gallery, soft as water over stones. *Say it, then.*

"Ansel."

The word came out of him and the Choir caught it, turned it over, sang it back to him in four-part harmony until it sounded like a word in a language he was only studying. *Ansel.* He heard it and felt the way you feel hearing someone else's name called in a market square — a half-turn of the head, then back to your own business.

His own business. What was his own business?

He had been a choirboy at the temple of Saint Vellum. He had slept in a narrow cell that smelled of tallow and stone dust. He had hummed in his sleep — Ivo had told him that once, embarrassed, as if confessing something intimate — and the candles had guttered in time, and Ivo had looked at him afterward with an expression he had never learned to read but had come, quietly, without deciding to, to rely on.

*Ivo.*

The name rang in him like a struck bell, and around the ringing the Choir's harmony thinned, just slightly, the way a fabric thins where you worry it between your fingers.

*Ah,* said the Choir, and its gentleness acquired an edge, the way honey acquires an edge when there is glass in it. *You keep a hook in yourself. We will draw it out. Hooks are how they get you. Hooks are how they make you theirs.*

"I was never theirs," Ansel said. "I was never anyone's. That's what being sold means."

*And here,* the Choir sang, swelling, warm, the note rising through the floor and up through his bones until his teeth hummed with it, *you will be everyone's. You will be carried in a hundred throats. You will never again be alone in the dark wondering if anyone hears you. Is that not what you prayed for, little vessel, all those nights in your narrow cell? To be heard?*

And that was the worst of it, the part he would not tell anyone if he ever got out, the part that shamed him more than fear ever had: it was what he had prayed for. Every night, face pressed to the cold wall, he had prayed *let someone hear me, let me matter to something larger than myself*, and the god who should have answered had been silent, silent, silent, and the thing below had heard every word and kept them, the way a merchant keeps letters of credit.

*You see?* the Choir murmured, tender now, terrible now. *We are not thieves. We honor our paper.*

* * *

He measured himself by what remained.

There was a game he played, or tried to play, though the Choir disapproved of games because games required a self that ended somewhere. He would sit in the throat-gallery and list things, silently, the way Brother Aldous had taught the novices to list their sins before confession. Not sins. Anchors.

*Tallow. Stone dust. The third stair from the top of the dormitory steps, which creaks even when no one is on it. Ivo's ink-stained fingers. The way she holds her pen too tight, like it might bolt. The answered prayer — my prayer — in handwriting not hers, the letters leaning left like a person walking against wind.*

Some anchors were already gone. He knew some were gone because there were holes in the list, gaps shaped like words, and when he probed them the Choir sang over the gap so smoothly he couldn't tell what had been removed. His mother's face — had he ever had one? There was a hole there, warm-edged, worn smooth, like a stone in a stream. Something about rain. Something about a window.

*Let go,* the Choir whispered, and its whisper was in his own voice now, which meant it had taken another piece without noticing, which meant — *letting go doesn't hurt. That's the mercy no one tells you. Above, everything hurts. Holding on hurts. Wanting hurts. Here there is only the chord, and the chord has no wants, and you will never ache again.*

"I want to warn Ivo," he said.

The chord stumbled.

It was a small stumble, a missed beat, but in a thing made entirely of harmony a missed beat is a scream. The gallery's milky light flickered. Somewhere far off in the winding dark, a door slammed — or a mouth.

*She cannot come here,* the Choir said, and all the tenderness was gone, and what was underneath was old and vast and hungry in the way mountains are hungry, patiently, for centuries, for the slow collapse of everything into themselves. *If she comes here, she pays. Everyone who enters pays. That is not cruelty. That is arithmetic.*

"Then I won't call for her," Ansel said. "Whatever happens. Whatever you do to me. I won't call."

*You will want to.*

"No," he said, and found, reaching down through the dissolving layers of himself, that this was still true, still solid, still *his*: "I'll want the opposite. I'll spend what's left of me wanting the opposite."

The Choir considered him for a long time. He felt it considering, the way you feel weather changing. Then it did the cruelest thing it had done yet, crueler than taking his fear, crueler than wearing his voice:

It laughed at him kindly.

*Little anchor,* it sang. *You think warning her is a gift. But you are the bait whether you speak or not. They are coming — we have counted their footsteps since the first road under Thornfell, one, two, three, four, the deaf one whose heart taps like a telegraph, the loud one who prays to a dead god, and the scribe whose blood carries our seal. They are coming for you, and every step they take downward is a step into debt. You cannot spare her by silence. Silence is just a slower signature.*

And Ansel understood, with the last clear part of himself, that the Choir was right, and that being right made it no less monstrous, and that this was how the court beneath the mountain had survived the fall of every older god: not by lying, but by telling truths that were also traps.

* * *

Time passed, or didn't. He dissolved a little more.

He could feel the edges of himself going soft, the way a chalk line goes soft when the choir loft is damp. His name — he held it like a coal in cupped hands. *Ansel.* Two syllables. He practiced the shape of them in the dark behind his eyes where the Choir's hearing was thinnest, because the Choir heard sound but not intention, not yet, not quite; there was one room in him it had not found, and he had built it himself, brick by brick, out of stubbornness, and in that room he was still a boy in a narrow cell humming to the candles.

Then, through the stone, through the chord, through a hundred miles of mountain's patience, he felt footsteps.

Not heard. *Felt* — the way he felt the candles gutter, the way he had always felt things slightly wrong and slightly early, which was the whole reason any of this had happened to him. Three sets of footsteps, uneven, descending. One limping. One moving with the precise, counted tread of someone reading the world through her feet. And one walking like a pen scratching — quick, angry, too tight, like it might bolt.

Ivo.

The Choir rose around him like a tide, exultant, and began to sing louder, and Ansel understood that it wanted him to call out. It was *arranging* for him to call out — loosening something in his chest, opening his throat the way you open a cage door, making the cry rise in him unbidden, because a cry freely given was worth tenfold a cry compelled, because everything in this place ran on the difference between the given and the taken, and they had learned to farm the given.

The cry came up his throat like water finding a crack.

He bit it.

He bit it the way you bite down on a scream in a nightmare, teeth locked, whole body shaking, and the taste of it was copper and candle-tallow and the third creaking stair, and he poured everything he had left into the room behind his eyes — *tallow, stone dust, ink-stained fingers, don't come, don't come, I am not worth the price, no one is worth the price, turn around, Ivo, turn around* —

And then, fainter than memory, thinner than the last candle-gutter of a dying evening, he felt something else arrive on the edge of his perception. Not footsteps. Not the Choir.

A vibration. Tapped, deliberate, patient, in a rhythm he almost knew — a rhythm like a hand on a table, like a code, like a cartographer speaking through stone.

And threaded through it, unmistakable, impossible, a voice that was not singing, that was refusing to sing, that was dragging itself down the corridors of the court against the current of the chord, hoarse and furious and absolutely certain:

"—ansel! ansel, if you can hear me, don't answer—"

She had come anyway. She had come knowing the price, and she was telling him not to answer, and the Choir surged toward her voice like a flood toward a cracked door, and Ansel stood in the throat-gallery with his name cupped in his hands like a coal, and understood that whatever he did next — call and damn her, or stay silent and lose himself alone — the choosing of it was the last thing that would ever be entirely his.

He opened his hands.

He looked at the coal of his name.

And he made his choice.
