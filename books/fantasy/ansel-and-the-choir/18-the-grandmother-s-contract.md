## 18. The Grandmother's Contract

The archive beneath the court did not smell like dust. That was the first wrongness Ivo noticed, and it took her a long moment to understand why it was wrong.

Every archive she had ever entered — the temple's own scriptorium cellar, the smugglers' paper-stash in the Hollow Stair, the mildewed chest where the village of Thornfell kept its tithe-records — smelled of dust and mouse and slow decay. Decay was what archives were *for*, in a way: they were places where time was allowed to happen to paper. But this place smelled of nothing at all. Of cold stone and cold air and the faintest suggestion of candlewax that had never been lit. The documents here were not permitted to age. They were not permitted to *change*. They were held exactly as they had been on the day they were signed, forever, the way a debt is held.

"Odile," Ivo whispered, though Odile could not hear her. She tapped the cartographer's shoulder twice — *stop* — and then drew in the air with one finger the sign they had agreed on: a circle, closed. *We hold here.*

Odile nodded. In the grey light of the memory-lamps — glass globes filled with something that swam and shifted like minnows made of fog — her face was calm and attentive, her eyes moving constantly, reading the room the way she read everything: through her skin, through the soles of her feet, through the bones of her jaw where vibration gathered like rainwater. She could not hear the archive. Ivo suspected the archive knew it, and resented it. Everything down here seemed to resent being unread.

Brannoc stood behind them both, one hand resting on the wall. He had not let go of a wall since they had entered the court. "It's humming," he'd said at the threshold, and his voice had been very quiet. "The whole mountain. Like a throat deciding whether to swallow." Now he said nothing, but his hand stayed where it was, as if the stone might carry him if the floor decided otherwise.

They had come down through corridors that sang back your own voice — Ivo had heard her own footsteps greet her from around corners before she made them, had heard a whisper of *Ivo, Ivo, Quintana-girl* in a register she did not possess — and past galleries of traded memories, alcoves where glass vessels held other people's yesterdays like preserved fruit. She had seen a jar labeled only with a child's name, and inside it, a summer afternoon turning slowly like a thing alive. She had not looked long. Looking cost something here. She was almost certain of it.

But this was the archive, and the archive was why they had come. Somewhere in these shelves — shelves that rose into darkness overhead, that ran back into darkness deeper than any temple nave — was the contract. Her grandmother's name was on it. And if she was going to stand in front of the Regent and bargain for Ansel's soul, she would do it knowing every word of what her family owed, down to the last drop of ink.

She walked the aisles slowly, trailing her fingers an inch above the spines. The documents were not books, exactly. Some were scrolls; some were tablets of black slate; some were sheets of vellum so thin they trembled when she breathed near them, eager, she thought, to be read. Each bore a seal. Most seals she did not recognize — spirals, teeth, a handprint, a knot. But the court's own seal appeared again and again, pressed deep: a ring of small figures holding hands, so worn by repetition that they had nearly merged into a single circlet of bodies, a crown of dancers, or a noose.

*Ivo.* Her name came from the shelf itself, faintly, the way a shell holds the sea. Not a warning. A *summons*.

She followed it three aisles deep, to a niche where a single document lay flat on a ledge of stone, unshelved, waiting. As if it had been placed there recently. As if someone had known she was coming.

Her mouth went dry. "Of course you did," she murmured. "You keep ledgers. You know who walks in."

The document was a contract, written in two hands. The upper portion was the court's — the same looping, patient script she had seen on the tollkeeper's bridge, each letter formed like a link in a chain. The lower portion was a different hand entirely: quick, slanted, impatient, the writing of someone who copied prayers for a living and resented every hour of it.

Ivo knew that hand. She had grown up trying to imitate it. She had been beaten — gently, with a ruler, by a patient temple tutor — for the crime of forming her letters too much like it.

*Maren Quintana.*

Her grandmother's name sat at the bottom of the page in that slanted, living script, and beside it, where a signature should have been, was a smear of blood pressed flat and brown with age — except that down here nothing aged, and the blood was still red. Still wet-looking. Still *fresh*, as if Maren Quintana had pricked her finger five minutes ago instead of fifty years.

Ivo read.

---

*Be it known beneath stone and star that Maren, daughter of Aldous, of the village of Thornfell, does hereby sell unto the Court Beneath the Mountain one half part of her soul entire, freely given, in exchange for the following consideration:*

*That the memory of Thornfell, sold by its elders in the winter of the bad harvest, shall be returned whole. Every name. Every face. Every grief and grudge and lullaby. The village shall remember itself, and its children shall be born knowing their grandmothers' voices, and no elder of Thornfell shall ever again smile at a stranger he cannot recognize.*

*This consideration is granted in perpetuity. This price is paid in perpetuity. The half-soul of Maren Quintana shall pass upon her death to her blood and bone, whosoever carries her line, and shall remain the property of the Court until such time as the Court releases it, which the Court does not intend to do.*

---

Ivo read it twice. Three times. Her lips moved over the words without sound, the old scribe's habit, tasting each clause like a coin bitten for gold.

*Freely given.* There it was, in the court's own careful language. Not taken. Not tricked — or no more tricked than any contract tricks the desperate. Her grandmother had stood at the mountain's mouth — Ivo remembered now, remembered with a lurch that felt like a stair missing in the dark: a cold morning, a shawl, a woman walking up the switchback road alone and telling the family she was going to sell eggs — and she had signed away half of herself to buy back a village's mind.

And that half had passed, upon her death, to her blood and bone.

To Ivo.

"That's why," she said aloud, and the archive repeated it back to her in her own voice, softer, from somewhere behind her left shoulder: *that's why, that's why.* "That's why the Abbot needed me. That's why the debt-holder's name was Quintana. It was never about my freedom. It was never even about Ansel, not first. It was about *this*. Half a soul, sitting in a temple scribe, unclaimed, unspent."

Half a soul. She had always known there was something wrong with her — everyone at the temple had known it, in the gentle, merciless way religious communities know things about you. She tired too easily. She dreamed in other people's colors. Prayers slid off her like rain off oiled cloth, and she had assumed, all her life, that she was simply faithless, simply broken, simply *less*. The Abbot had called it a trial. Serel had called it an affectation. Brannoc, once, drunk on smuggled plum brandy, had called it a hollow and asked her, kindly, what she was carrying that weighed so much.

Nothing, she had told him. I'm carrying nothing. That's the problem.

She had been wrong. She was carrying half of her grandmother.

And Ansel —

The thought arrived whole, terrible, luminous, the way the answered prayer had arrived in the scriptorium a lifetime ago: *the choirboy who hums in his sleep.* Ansel, the foundling, left at the temple gates in a basket lined with a shawl. Ansel, whose soul had been sold to the court decades ago — sold, the Abbot had said, by parties unknown, in a rite the temple had never fully explained to her. Woven. The word the Abbot had used was *woven*, and she had not understood it then.

But she understood it now, standing in her grandmother's handwriting, because the same phrase appeared in the contract's final clause, the clause she had skimmed past twice in her shock:

*Let it further be noted that the vessel prepared by the Temple of Saint Vellum — the child called Ansel, whose soul was wrought by the same rite, from the same loom, using thread drawn from the same account — is bound by sympathy to the half-soul herein described. What touches the one touches the other. The Court acknowledges this binding and finds it convenient.*

*Finds it convenient.* The court's idea of a footnote. Her grandmother's half-soul and Ansel's woven soul, spun from the same account, the same loom — twin threads out of one spindle. That was why the answered prayer had come to *her* desk. That was why Ansel's harmonies bent the candles toward her. That was why, on the Bridge of Signed Names, when the tollkeeper ghost had burned away her memory of her grandmother's face, the seal of the court had been revealed underneath, printed into the scar tissue of the loss like a watermark in paper.

She had thought the seal meant she was owned.

It meant she was *connected*. Bound by sympathy. What touched the one touched the other. Ansel's soul and hers rang together like two bells cast from the same pour of bronze, and everything that had happened since the night the candles guttered in time with a sleeping boy's humming had been the two halves of one chord trying to find each other across a room.

"Oh, Grandmother," Ivo whispered.

The archive hushed. Even the swimming lights in their globes slowed, drifting like fish settling into evening.

Because here was the thing she had not expected, the thing that broke her open in the cold of that lightless place: she had spent the whole journey down hating the contract. Hating her grandmother for signing it, hating the inheritance of it, hating the cage of debt that had shaped her life without her knowledge — charity as a leash, kindness as collateral, the temple's warm bread bought with a lien on her soul. She had rehearsed, in the dark hours between marches, the furious speech she would deliver to Maren Quintana's ghost: *you had no right, you spent me like coin, you priced me before I was born.*

But the contract did not say what she had feared. It did not sell Ivo. It did not spend her. It sold *Maren* — half of Maren, freely given — and the passage of it to her blood and bone was not a sale at all. It was inheritance. It was the ordinary, human, heartbreaking mechanics of an estate: what my mother leaves, I carry. What my grandmother gave, I hold.

Her grandmother had bought back a village's memory. Had looked at a town of smiling strangers — elders who couldn't recognize their own daughters, mothers who smiled politely at children they had birthed — and had walked up the mountain alone in the cold with a shawl and a needle for her finger, and had paid the only currency the court respected. Half of herself. Forever.

Not a cage. A *gift*. Mispriced, yes — priced terribly, ruinously, by a market that valued souls the way slavers value breath — but a gift. Given freely, in love, for strangers and neighbors and grandchildren unborn, of whom Ivo was one.

"I've been ashamed of the wrong thing," Ivo said, and her voice cracked on it, and the archive caught the crack and held it, replaying it softly, *wrong thing, wrong thing,* until she wanted to scream at the shelves to leave her grief alone.

Brannoc's footsteps came heavy down the aisle. He had followed her tapping-signs chalked on the stone, or perhaps he had simply refused to let her out of arm's reach since the Weeping Stair, since the bandage on his side where Serel's blade had opened him, since the party had become three. He stopped when he saw her face.

"What is it?" he asked. Then, because he was Brannoc, and because grief disguised as gruffness was the only language he trusted: "Bad news?"

"No." She laughed, and it came out wet and strange. "Yes. Both. It's my grandmother's contract. She sold half her soul to buy the village's memory back. All of it — every name, every face. That's why they know their grandmothers' voices in Thornfell. That's why the village exists at all." She held the page out to him, and her hand shook. "And the half she sold didn't die with her. It passed to me. It's been in me my whole life, Brannoc. The hollowness, the tiredness, the prayers sliding off — that's not faithlessness. That's a mortgage. I've been carrying her payment this whole time."

Brannoc read. He read slowly, his lips moving — the mercenary who prayed to a dead god, sounding out the grammar of souls — and when he reached the clause about Ansel, about the same loom, the same account, the binding by sympathy, his jaw tightened until she heard his teeth creak.

"So the boy's soul was spun from the same thread-pool as your half," he said. "Same rite. Same—" He stopped. Started again, carefully, the way he handled blades. "Then when Vesh's lot take him, they don't just get a choirboy. They get the other end of *you*. Whatever they weave him into, you're woven into it too. You'd feel it. You might—"

"Might dissolve with him. Yes." She had already done the arithmetic. Scribes are good at arithmetic; it is most of the job. "Or the reverse. If I give my half freely — if I sing it into the Choir the way my grandmother sold it, except *given* instead of *sold* — the binding might pull him loose. Two bells, one casting. Ring one true, and the other answers."

"You don't know that."

"No," Ivo said. "I don't know anything. I'm a scribe. I copy prayers that no god answers." She looked down at the contract, at the fresh-red blood-smear beside her grandmother's name, and felt something settle in her chest that was not despair. It was closer to the feeling of a key turning. "Except one did. One finally did. And it turns out it was answering through *her* all along."

Odile appeared at the aisle's mouth, moving fast, her hands signing before she arrived — urgent, clipped shapes in the lamplight. *Company. Coming. Many feet. And something else — a rhythm under the floor, faster than footsteps.* Her eyes went to the contract in Ivo's hands, and then to Ivo's face, and whatever she saw there made her signs soften. *Later. Move now.*

"The Regent knows we're here," Brannoc translated grimly, because three weeks on the road had taught them all Odile's hands. "Or the Regent *invited* us. Either way—"

"Either way, I have what I came for." Ivo folded the contract along its old creases, gently, the way one folds a letter from the dead, and slipped it inside her coat, against her heart, against the hollow where her grandmother's half-soul sat like a banked coal. "And I know the price now. All of it. Down to the last drop of ink."

She took one last look at the niche, at the empty ledge where the document had waited for her for fifty years, patient as a debt.

Behind them, far down the singing corridors, the court began to applaud. Slow, dry, countless hands, coming closer.

* * *

They ran.

The archive spat them out into a gallery of traded memories, and Ivo led by instinct and heartbeat, left at the alcove with the jarred summer, right at the shrine-niche where someone's childhood fear of wells glowed sullen green. Behind them the applause never quickened and never slowed, a metronome of possession, and beneath it — Odile signing frantically as they ran, Brannoc reading her hands mid-stride — the floor's rhythm was rising, resolving, becoming almost melodic.

"It's the Choir," Brannoc gasped. "It's singing toward us."

*No,* Ivo realized, and stopped so suddenly that Odile collided with her back. Because she knew that melody. She had copied it ten thousand times in a scriptorium that smelled of tallow and dust. It was a petition-hymn, a minor-key plea for safe passage, one of the thousand dead prayers of Saint Vellum's canon — and it was being sung *upward*, from the heart of the court, sung in a boy's clear unbroken voice with harmonies underneath it that no one had taught him.

Ansel. Alive. Singing. And singing *her* hymn — the hymn from the answered page, the prayer that had started everything — as if he knew, as if the binding-by-sympathy ran both ways, as if somewhere in the dark below, half a soul and a woven one were calling each other home across the shelves of everything that had ever been sold.

The applause stopped.

In the silence, every memory-jar in the gallery lit at once, thousands of stolen yesterdays burning like votive candles, and a voice that was many voices said, from everywhere, courteously, in the court's patient linking script made audible:

*"Granddaughter. Come down. We have kept your half warm — and we are so very curious to learn whether you have come to sell, or to buy."*

Ivo put her hand flat against her coat, over the folded contract, over the coal.

"To settle an account," she said, and walked toward the stairs going down.
