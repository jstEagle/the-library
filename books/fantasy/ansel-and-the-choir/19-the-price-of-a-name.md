## 19. The Price of a Name

The Regent's hall was built from the sound of agreements.

Ivo understood that now, standing on a floor of pressed slate that hummed faintly under her boots — the residue of ten thousand signatures, ten thousand bargains struck in this room and soaked into the stone like wine into bread. The walls were not walls. They were ledgers made vertical, shelves of contracts bound in vellum and skin and things she refused to look at too closely, each one breathing with the slow respiration of a debt still unpaid. The light came from nowhere and everywhere, a grey-gold luminescence like the last hour before dawn, and it fell most brightly on the table at the center of the hall: a slab of black glass, polished to mirror-depth, with a single quill resting upon it.

The quill was white. Bone-white. It had been white for a very long time.

"You've come far," the Regent said.

She had expected a monster. She had spent the whole descent rehearsing monsters — the Regent as a many-mouthed thing, a crowned horror, a choir given teeth. Instead the figure across the black table was almost disappointingly human: tall, robed in grey so deep it drank the light, with a face like a face remembered by someone who had never looked closely. Features present but uncommitted. Eyes the color of the space between two notes.

That was worse, somehow. A monster you could hate. This was a clerk. The universe's oldest clerk.

"I've come for Ansel," Ivo said. Her voice sounded small in the hall, and she hated that too. She was a scribe; she knew what words were worth. She would not let hers be cheap here. "The boy. The soul your court holds under contract."

"Under contract." The Regent smiled without any mouth moving. "How precisely you put it. Yes. We hold him. Or rather—" one long finger tapped the black glass, and somewhere far above, impossibly, Ivo felt the tap through the soles of her feet, through the mountain's bones, "—we hold the paper, and the paper holds him. That is how it has always worked. Paper is patient. Paper outlives flesh, regret, kingdoms. Paper is the only honest thing mortals ever made."

"I've read your paper," Ivo said. "My grandmother's signature is on it. Quintana. You'll have noticed the name matches mine."

"We noticed everything about you, Ivo Quintana, the moment you set foot on the first road under Thornfell." The Regent drew something from the folds of its robe — and there it was, the contract, her grandmother's contract, the one Ivo had found in the archive three corridors back, except this copy was different. Newer. The seal at its foot burned with a low red ember-light, and beneath her grandmother's faded hand, space had been left. Blank lines. Waiting lines.

"Then you know why I'm here," Ivo said. "You know what I am. Half-mine already. Ansel's soul was woven from the same rite — my grandmother sold half herself to buy back a village's memory, and that half passed down to me, and because of it the boy and I are tangled together like two threads off the same spool. So make your offer. I didn't walk through galleries of stolen faces to watch you enjoy the suspense."

The Regent regarded her for a long moment. When it spoke again, its voice had changed — softened, warmed, taken on a timbre Ivo recognized with a lurch that went through her chest like a hook finding purchase.

*Her grandmother's voice.*

"Child," the Regent said, in Maren Quintana's low smoke-and-honey tones, "you look so tired."

Ivo's hands shook. She clenched them behind her back where the thing could not see. *It reads us,* she thought. *It reads what we want and wears it.* Brannoc had told her, grimly, on the Weeping Stair while pressing his bleeding side: *Don't listen to anything down here that sounds kind. Kindness is bait in this country.* But knowing a trap did not stop it from being shaped exactly like the shape of your loneliness.

"I'm tired," Ivo admitted. "And you're going to tell me rest is for sale."

"Rest is never for sale here." The Regent laid the contract flat upon the black glass and smoothed it with both hands, tender as a nurse straightening a shroud. "Rest is *given*. That is the tragedy your temple never taught you, scribe. Above, they call us thieves. Below, we keep the only honest market in the world. Everything above is taken — labor, years, faith, freedom, all of it extracted and called duty. Here, we ask. Here, we offer terms. Here, a woman may trade half her soul and know exactly what she buys with it." The ember-seal pulsed. "Your grandmother knew. She signed with open eyes and walked out of this hall lighter than she came in, because she had turned grief into harvest. Do you imagine she regretted it?"

"She forgot my face," Ivo said quietly. "When she came home. She looked at me every day for eleven years and never once knew whose child I was. That was her harvest. That was what she bought the village's memory with — she bought their remembering by selling her own, and I was the price nobody counted." Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it. Let the Regent hear it. Let it think her broken, if brokenness bought her the next sentence. "So yes. I imagine she regretted it. Every single day she couldn't name."

Something flickered across the Regent's unfinished face — not guilt. Curiosity. The curiosity of an accountant encountering a number that refuses to sum.

"And yet," it said, "here you stand, offering to do the same."

The silence rang. Somewhere in the walls, a page turned.

"The trade is simple," the Regent continued, and now it was its own voice again, smooth as fresh ink. "Ansel's soul, whole and released, returned to his body before the Choir dissolves him past retrieval. In exchange: your half. The half you carry, the half that binds you to him, the half that has made your life a corridor of other people's prayers. Sign, and the boy walks free. Sign, and the tangle between you is cut clean. Sign, and you will feel—" it leaned forward, and its eyes were suddenly, terribly specific, suddenly seeing her, "—lighter. You said it yourself. Half a soul is half a weight."

Ivo looked at the blank lines.

They were beautiful. That was the obscene thing. She had spent her life among beautiful script — the temple's illuminated capitals, gold leaf and lapis, prayers no god answered — and these waiting lines had the same beauty, the same terrible invitation of empty space asking to be filled. Her fingers ached toward the bone-white quill the way a drowning man's fingers ache toward surface. She thought of Ansel above, caged in harmony, his name wearing thin as an over-copied page. She thought of his face when he slept, humming harmonies no one taught him, candles guttering in time. She thought: *half. Only half. I have been living on half anyway. What is one more subtraction?*

She reached for the quill.

Her fingertips touched the bone. It was warm. Of course it was warm. It had been warm for centuries, fed and fed and fed—

—and in the warmth she felt, distinctly, a heartbeat that was not hers. Slow. Vast. Patient as paper. And beneath the heartbeat, faint as a voice through a wall, singing: thousands of voices, braided, the Choir, and threaded through them one thread growing thinner by the hour, a boy's thread, fraying into music—

*Ivo.*

Not aloud. Not in the Regent's borrowed voices. In her own voice, inside her own skull, in the tone she used when she caught her own errors in a fair copy — sharp, unsparing, fond. Her grandmother's cadence worn by her own years of use. The voice of the half-soul itself, the piece of Maren Quintana that had lived in Ivo since before Ivo could talk, speaking up at last from the place where Ivo kept it: behind the ribs, left of center, next to everything she'd never said.

*Read the line before you sign it. You taught me that. Read the line.*

Ivo's hand stopped.

She looked down at the contract. Really looked, the way she had been trained to look, the way twenty years in the scriptorium had ground into her: not at the words but at the *structure*, the architecture of clauses, the grammar of the trap. And there it was. Clause nine. The clause she should have seen in the archive, would have seen if grief hadn't been ringing in her ears like a struck bell.

*All instruments executed within the Court are held in perpetuity by the Court, together with all instruments derivative thereof, unto the ending of debt.*

Derivative. Every contract signed here became the Court's property — and every contract *derived* from it, every future bargain, every amendment, every renewal, forever. Her grandmother's signature had not ended when Maren died. It had *compounded*. It sat in these walls right now, still drawing interest, still feeding. And if Ivo signed —

If Ivo signed, she would not be paying off the debt. She would be *renewing* it. Refinancing it. Giving the Court a fresh instrument, written in living ink, with a new Quintana's name at the foot — and the Court would hold that instrument in perpetuity, and through it hold a claim not just on her half-soul but on the *line*, on whatever came after her, on every prayer she ever copied, every choice she ever made that touched a signature. The Regent wasn't offering her a trade.

It was offering her a subscription.

"That's why you wanted me to come down here yourself," Ivo said. Her voice came out level. Cold. Scribe-cold, the voice she used on novices who forged their practice letters. "You could have taken the half from my body anywhere on the road. Serel would have delivered me gift-wrapped. But a taking isn't an instrument, is it? You need me to *sign*. Willingly. Freely. Because a freely given signature is the only ink that compounds." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged, half a sob dressed in armor. "All those galleries upstairs. All those traded memories. None of them were robbed, were they? Every single one *signed*. You never take. You just make the alternative look worse than the pen."

The Regent's face did not change, because it had no fixed face to change. But the light in the hall dimmed, fractionally, the way a room dims when someone closes a book.

"Everything ends in signature," the Regent said. "Yours will too. The only question is whether you choose the terms."

"No," Ivo said. "The question is whether I feed you."

She picked up the contract.

The Regent moved for the first time — a ripple through the grey robes, a tension in the unfinished face, and around the hall the shelved contracts rustled like a forest hearing wind. *Careful,* said the voice behind her ribs. *Careful, girl, it can't stop you from holding paper, but it can't let you leave holding it either—*

"You should know," the Regent said softly, "what happens to defaulters."

"I do know. I've seen the village that forgot its name. I've seen what's left when the Court collects." Ivo lifted the contract high, both hands, the way she had seen priests lift relics she no longer believed in. "But here's what *you* should know, clerk. Your whole edifice — the halls, the Choir, the galleries, the Regent himself — runs on one fuel. Not souls. Souls are just the currency. The fuel is *transaction*. The moment of signing. The instant a mortal decides that anything — love, memory, a child's future — can be priced. Every contract in these walls is a monument to someone deciding that giving was impossible, so trading must do. And I am not going to add my stone to your cathedral."

She tore the contract in half.

The sound was enormous. It was not the sound of paper. It was the sound of a held breath releasing after forty years — the gallery of memories sighing, the walls groaning, the ember-seal flaring bright red and then guttering like a candle pinched out. The two halves of her grandmother's contract hung in her fists, and where the tear ran, the vellum was crumbling, aging decades in seconds, the ink lifting off the page like smoke returning to a fire.

"You cannot—" the Regent began, and for the first time its voice cracked, split into a dozen voices, the Choir's confusion echoing up through the floor. "The debt persists. The debt *must* persist. Without the ledger there is no—"

"Then starve," Ivo said.

She dropped the halves, and before they finished falling she was already turning to the black glass table, to the bone-white quill, and she seized it — and it fought her, it writhed in her grip like a fish, warm and horrible and hungry — and she snapped it across her knee the way Brannoc had snapped a smuggler's blade on the second road, with her whole body, with a sound like a small bone breaking.

The hall screamed. Quietly. The way temples scream.

And then, in the ringing aftermath, in the dust of two destroyed instruments, Ivo Quintana stood in the heart of the court beneath the mountain with nothing left to sign with and nothing left to sell — and discovered that she was not empty at all.

Because the half-soul behind her ribs was singing.

It had always been singing, she realized. All her life — every prayer she'd copied unanswered, every night alone in the scriptorium, every time she'd hummed without noticing while she worked — it had been Maren Quintana's half, trying to harmonize with a world that kept refusing the tune. Sold once, inherited once, priced twice, and still, after everything, it had never once stopped wanting to be *given*.

Vesh had told her, at the Shrine of Nine Bells, in a voice made of bell-sound and borrowed faces: *We do not want the boy. We want a voice that comes to us free.* She had thought then that it was another bargain, another trap with softer upholstery. Standing here, in the wreckage of the only economy this place understood, she finally saw it plain.

A gift was the one instrument the Court could not hold. No signature. No terms. No perpetuity. A thing given freely belonged to the giver even after it was given — or to no one — or to everyone — but never, ever to a ledger.

Ivo opened her mouth.

What came out was not words. It was the prayer she had copied ten thousand times and never once believed — Saint Vellum's canticle, the one every indentured scribe learned first, the one about the voice that needs no paper — and she sang it into the dark of the court, unaccompanied, unpriced, unsigned, her half-soul rising through her chest like water finding its level, and the torn contract at her feet dissolved entirely, and the walls full of breathing ledgers went silent, listening.

Far below, through the mountain's roots, the Choir heard her.

And one thread in that vast braided harmony — thin, frayed, nearly gone — heard its own name sung inside the song, and remembered it was a boy.

* * *

The Regent was unraveling.

Ivo watched it happen as she sang, verse by verse, and understood that she was watching a creature discover it had been a contract all along. The grey robes thinned to smoke. The unfinished face tried on expressions — her grandmother's stern love, the Abbot's cold arithmetic, Ansel's sleeping wonder — and discarded each one, because each had been borrowed, and borrowed things could not survive a gift. The court's economy was breaking around her the way ice breaks on a spring river: not all at once but in long cracking reports, shelf after shelf of contracts going dark, going quiet, going *blank*.

She sang louder.

She sang until her throat burned, until the half-soul poured through her like light through a clean window, until she felt it begin to leave her — not torn, not taken, not traded, but *going*, the way a bird goes from an open hand, and she wept as she sang because it hurt, God, it hurt, it hurt like the best thing that had ever happened to her — and the song carried her grandmother in it, and the village's remembered name, and Ansel's candle-timed harmonies, and every unanswered prayer she had ever copied, all of it flowing down and down into the deep of the court where the Choir waited with its arms of sound.

The last thing the Regent said, as it came apart into ordinary darkness, was not a threat.

It was, in a voice suddenly, briefly, its own — young, and tired, and relieved:

"Oh. *That's* what it's for."

And then the hall was only a hall, and the black glass table was only stone, and Ivo stood alone in the ringing quiet with an emptiness behind her ribs that did not feel like loss.

It felt like room.

Above her — far above, through leagues of singing rock — she heard footsteps on the stairs. Three sets, uneven, hurrying. Brannoc's limp. Odile's silent certainty. And a fourth sound underneath them, a sound that made her knees give out with relief so violent she had to catch the dead table to stay standing:

Humming. Off-key. Stubborn. Entirely, gloriously his own.

Ansel was coming up the stairs, and he was singing *badly*, and it was the most beautiful thing Ivo Quintana had ever heard — because the Choir was behind him, waking, unfurling through the mountain like dawn through a valley, and every voice in it was singing *freely* now, and none of them, not one, had signed.

The door burst open. Light poured in. And Ivo turned toward it with wet cheeks and an empty chest and a whole soul she hadn't owned an hour ago, to see who the gods had sent to fetch her home.
