## 20. The Unbought Song

*Ivo*

The Regent's hall was built of signatures.

Ivo saw it clearly now, standing on the dais steps with the torn contract still smoking in her fist — the walls were not stone but vellum stretched taut over ribs of ink, and every surface was written on, layer upon layer, names stacked like sediment: *sold, traded, forfeited, inherited, owed.* The court beneath the mountain did not have an economy of voices. It *was* one. The Regent sat at the center of it like a spider at the center of a web made entirely of promissory notes, and its body — tall, gray, elegant, its face a smooth oval where features should have been — was woven from the interest accrued on ten thousand bargains.

It had offered her the trade with such courtesy. Ansel's soul for her half. A simple exchange between parties of standing.

She had almost signed. Her hand had been moving before her mind caught up — that was the horror of it, that the gesture of signing lived in her bones deeper than any prayer she'd ever copied, that she had been raised in a scriptorium and her body knew how to give itself away in ink better than it knew how to breathe. And then she had looked at the contract's seal, at the little burning sigil of the court pressed into the wax, and understood what Serel had never understood, what the Abbot had built his whole grey life upon and never seen:

Every signature fed it. Even hers. Especially hers. A trade was just another meal.

So she had torn it. And the sound the hall made when the contract ripped — a sound like ten thousand small gasps, like a congregation inhaling all at once — was still ringing in the vellum walls when she turned to the place where Vesh's avatar stood at the edge of the light.

The avatar had followed them down. Of course it had. A woman-shaped arrangement of bell-sound and borrowed faces, wearing tonight the face of Ivo's grandmother — because Vesh was not subtle, and hunger rarely is — and it watched her now with eyes that rang faintly when they moved.

"You tore it," the avatar said. Its voice came from everywhere, from the walls themselves, from the names. "Do you know how long it has been since anyone tore a contract down here?"

"I don't care."

"You should. It matters." The avatar drifted closer, and where it passed, the written walls dimmed, as if the names flinched. "The old gods are starving, scribe. We slept because there was nothing left to eat that wasn't already promised to this place. Every voice in Thornfell was spoken for before we woke. Do you understand? We woke into a world with no unclaimed anything." It spread hands that chimed. "And then you come down here carrying half a soul that no one has ever successfully collected. Half a soul your grandmother loved too much to sell whole. And you tear up the paper instead of signing it."

"I'm not giving you Ansel," Ivo said. "And I'm not letting you take me either. But—" and here her voice did something strange, cracked open along a seam she hadn't known was there, "—you said once that what you wanted was a mortal who chose you. Freely. So. Here is my choice, and I'm making it out loud, in front of everything, so there can be no mistaking it for a bargain."

She stepped off the dais. The floor hummed under her feet — Odile's map had shown them the way in through corridors that sang back their own voices, and even now the stones remembered her footsteps and gave them back with interest.

"I don't want anything in return," she said. "No absolution. No debt forgiven. No favor. I am going to sing my half-soul back into the Choir, and I am giving it, not selling it, and if your gods are anything other than what you claim, they will know the difference."

The Regent moved for the first time.

It rose from its seat of ledgers like a tide coming in, and the whole hall groaned, and somewhere far above them — or perhaps very near, distance meant nothing here — Brannoc shouted a warning, and Odile began hammering the floor in the sharp triple rhythm that meant *now, now, now.*

But Ivo was already singing.

*Ansel*

He had almost forgotten he had a name.

That was the thing about the Choir that no one could have told him, that he could not have imagined from outside it: dissolution did not hurt. That was the trap. He had expected torment — the Abbot's sermons had painted damnation as fire, as wailing, as teeth — but the Choir was warm. The Choir was *harmonious.* He had been inside it for what might have been hours or years, and his fear had gone first, worn smooth like a pebble in a stream, and then his loneliness, and then his name, and what remained was a boy-shaped region of music that was beginning, gently, sweetly, to stop believing it had ever been separate from the rest.

He knew there had been someone called Ansel. He held onto that the way you hold a note at the end of a long phrase — barely, on breath alone.

*There was a boy,* the Choir agreed around him, in ten thousand stolen voices, some of them kind. *There was a boy who hummed in his sleep.*

*There was a boy someone came for.*

That thought arrived like a crack in amber. Someone came for him. Someone was coming. And beneath the Choir's warm blanket of belonging, something small and stubborn and *his* turned toward the sound — because far away, at the edge of everything, a voice had begun to sing that did not belong to the Choir at all.

It was ragged. It was untrained. It missed intervals the way a cart misses ruts, jarring and recovering, jarring and recovering. And it was the most beautiful thing Ansel had ever heard, because it was *wrong* in a way nothing in the Choir was wrong — it was imperfect and unblended and unmistakably, gloriously *one single person*, and hearing it was like remembering that snow is cold, that bread has a crust, that a self is a thing you can be.

*Ivo,* he thought, and the name came back to him whole, and with it came his own.

He opened his mouth inside the Choir and did the one thing the Choir could not do, the one thing it had spent centuries teaching itself to forget:

He sang against it.

*Ivo*

She felt him the moment he answered.

Her half-soul — the piece her grandmother had sold at the mountain's mouth, the piece that had lived in her all her life as a hollow feeling, a sense of being slightly less present than other people, of watching the world through a pane of glass — that half-soul recognized the Choir the way a river recognizes the sea. It *leaned.* Every note she sang pulled at something below her sternum, unraveling her gently from the inside, and she let it, and kept singing.

She was not a good singer. She wanted that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger would survive this night: *Ivo Quintana, scribe of Saint Vellum, sang badly and it did not matter.* Because the old gods were not listening for beauty. She understood that now, standing in the heart of the court with her voice cracking on the high parts, tears running freely down her face while the Regent bore down on her like a collapsing archive. They were listening for the one thing this place had never contained.

Consent.

The Regent reached her in three strides that covered a hundred yards, and its gray face bent close, featureless, and where a mouth should have been the air simply said, in a voice assembled from every contract in the room: *"You cannot give what was already sold. Your grandmother's signature binds you. You are mine by inheritance."*

"No," Ivo said, and did not stop singing. "You never collected. Fifty years and you never collected, because you couldn't — because she sold only half, and the other half refused, and a soul divided against a sale stays contested forever. Ask your own law. It's written on your walls. I've read your walls, Regent. I've read everything you are, and do you know what I found?" She laughed, wet and wild, mid-verse. "*Nothing.* Not one line in this entire court says a gift is binding. You have no grammar for it. You never needed one. No one has ever given you anything."

The hall shuddered.

Because it was true, and the walls knew it was true, and the walls were made of truth-of-a-particular-kind, the truth of signatures, and they could not bear a sentence they had no ink to hold.

*"Take her,"* the Regent said to the dark, and the dark did not move, because the dark was the old gods, and the old gods were watching a mortal woman give herself away for free and finding, for the first time in an age, that they were *full.*

*Brannoc*

Brannoc had fought in eleven wars and buried two gods, counting the one that got eaten, and he had never once seen anything like this.

He stood at the mouth of the singing corridor with Odile beside him — her hand resting on his forearm, reading the battle through his bones the way she read the mountain through her feet — and he watched a skinny scribe with ink-stained fingers hold off the Regent of the deep court with nothing but a bad voice and a torn-up contract, and he felt his dead god stir in the place where his god used to live.

Not wake. Nothing so grand. Just... stir. Like a man turning over in bed because he smelled breakfast.

"Odile," he said, and tapped the words against her palm the way she'd taught him: *is it working.*

She tapped back, fast, urgent, and he felt her grinning through her fingertips: *the walls are falling. Look at the walls.*

He looked. The vellum surfaces were peeling. Names were lifting off them — not erased, not destroyed, but *released*, rising like sparks off a bonfire, thousands of them, tens of thousands, fifty years of harvests and bought memories and sold children drifting up through the dark of the mountain toward a sky that wasn't there yet but was thinking about becoming one. And each released name made a sound as it went. Small. Distinct. A voice remembering it was a voice.

The Choir was coming apart into a congregation.

*Ansel*

He sang against the harmony and the harmony, impossibly, began to make room for him.

That was the miracle he would try to describe for the rest of his life and never quite manage: the Choir did not fight him. It *yielded*, voice by voice, the way ice yields to spring — reluctantly at first, then all at once, a great groaning surrender, because every soul in it had been taken and none of them had ever been asked to stay, and being asked, even now, even wordlessly, even by one badly-sung line of Ivo's stubborn descending melody — being asked was enough to remind them all that they had once been people who could say no.

His grandmother's voice found him last.

He knew it was hers though he'd never heard it awake — he knew it the way he knew the answered prayer in the scriptorium, the way he knew candles guttering in time, the way he knew things that came to him sideways, in his sleep, from below. Her voice wrapped around his like a shawl.

*Run,* she had told him, in the dream. But that was before. Now she sang something else, four notes, simple, and he understood them without translation: *Stay yourself. Go home. Be a boy.*

The cage — he saw it now, had always been able to see it, a lattice of fine gold script hanging in the center of the Choir like a birdcage made of clauses — the cage opened. Not broke. *Opened*, like a door whose lock had finally been given the right key.

Ansel stepped out of the Choir and into his own body, which was lying on cold stone at the edge of the great hall with Brannoc's huge hand under his head and Odile kneeling at his side, and the first thing he did, before opening his eyes, was hum.

One note. His own. In key with nobody.

*Ivo*

The Regent unraveled quietly.

That surprised her most of all. She had braced for rage, for a final lunge, for the whole mountain to come down on their heads — but the Regent simply came apart the way a ledger comes apart when its spine is broken, page by page, name by name, its accumulated interest flowing backward out of it into the dark. As it thinned, its face grew features for the first time — eyes, a mouth, an expression — and the expression was relief. Whatever it had been, ages ago, before the bargains accreted, it had been waiting a long time to put itself down.

The last of it was a single line of handwriting, hanging in the air where the gray shape had stood, and Ivo read it before it faded, because she was a scribe and reading was the one prayer she had ever truly known how to make:

*account closed.*

Then the hall was only a hall, stone and shadow and drifting motes of freed light, and Vesh's avatar stood alone in the middle of it, ringing softly, wearing no one's face at all — just a smooth brightness, like a bell before it is engraved.

"Well," the avatar said, and its voice was smaller now, and warmer, and it sounded almost young. "That was not what we planned."

"No," Ivo said. Her legs chose that moment to inform her they were done, and she sat down hard on the dais steps, laughing and crying and holding her own ribs as if checking she was still all there. She was not all there. She could feel the absence where her half-soul had been — but it was a clean absence, an amputation healed over, nothing like the old hollow. She had given it away and somehow felt more whole than she had since childhood. "Your gods are fed anyway. Aren't they. Don't lie to me, bell-woman. I can hear it in you. You're *ringing* differently."

The avatar was silent a long moment. Around it, in the high dark of the cavern, other sounds were waking — not the hungry harmonies of the old Choir but something looser, call-and-response, voices finding each other the way travelers find each other at a fire. The old gods, fed on a gift, waking gentle.

"They are," the avatar admitted. "We planned for theft. We had rituals prepared for theft. There were... contingencies." It drifted closer and looked down at her with its blank bright face, and when it spoke again the chime in its voice had softened to something almost like awe. "We had no ritual for being *given to*. Do you understand what you've done, scribe? You've broken our hunger. Not satisfied it — broken it. A god that has been fed freely once cannot go back to taking. It changes the nature of the mouth."

"Good," Ivo said. "Then stay changed."

Above them, faintly, filtered through a mile of mountain, came the sound of morning bells from the temple of Saint Vellum — and every bell in the deep court, every shard of shrine-glass, every humming stone, answered it. In tune. Willingly.

*Ansel*

They climbed out into daylight that none of them had honestly expected to see, and Ansel stopped three steps from the cave mouth and just stood in it, letting the sun land on him like a hand.

He was thin. He was filthy. His voice, when he tested it, had a new register in it, low and resonant, a gift or a scar from the Choir — he hadn't decided which yet, maybe never would. Brannoc walked behind him with an arm bound and a grin like a landslide; Odile had already stopped at the threshold and was tapping the ground, mapping the sunrise by its vibration, and her face was doing the thing it did instead of crying, which was nothing at all, absolutely nothing, while her shoulders shook.

Ivo came out last.

She looked different. Lighter, Ansel thought — no, that wasn't right. She looked the same weight but differently *distributed*, like a scale rebalanced, like a page where something had been erased and something else written in its place. She caught him looking and raised an eyebrow, the exact expression she'd worn the day he'd first hummed in the scriptorium and she'd pretended not to notice while knocking over an inkpot.

"So," she said. "You're whole."

"So are you," he said. "Mostly."

"Mostly." She snorted. Then, quieter, looking back at the mountain's mouth, where the dark was no longer hungry but merely deep: "I lost my grandmother's face, you know. At the bridge. I gave it to a ghost for passage. I'll never remember what she looked like." She turned back to him, and her eyes were dry and fierce and free. "But I remember what she *did*. Turns out that was the part worth keeping."

Ansel didn't say anything. He just started to hum — the four notes his grandmother had sung him, the ones that meant *go home, be a boy* — and Ivo listened, and her face did something complicated and soft, and far above them, in the temple where the Abbot was about to discover that every ledger in his keeping had gone blank overnight, the morning choir began to sing.

And beneath the mountain, for the first time in five hundred years, something ancient and newly gentle sang back.

* * *

They came down off the mountain at dusk, four ragged figures on the pilgrim road, and ahead of them Thornfell's lights were coming on one window at a time — and in the temple square, visible even from here, a bonfire was burning papers.
