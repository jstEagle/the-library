## 21. Ledgers Burned

The last stretch of the road up was the hardest, because it was ordinary.

Ivo had expected the mountain to fight them on the way out — to seal its roads, to mourn, to reach after them with hands made of echo. Instead the tunnels simply let them go. The shrines they passed glowed a soft, even gold now, like banked hearths rather than watchfires, and when Brannoc limped past the Shrine of Nine Bells for the second time in his life, the bells did not toll at all. They hummed. Low and contented, the way a dog hums in its sleep when someone it trusts walks through the room.

"They're not hungry anymore," Odile signed, her hands quick in the lantern light. She had learned to sign to Brannoc over the weeks; she signed to all of them now, out of habit, so that Ivo found herself reading the cartographer's hands before her face. "Listen."

Ivo listened. Beneath the humming of the bells there was something else — a vast, slow sound like breathing, like a choir between songs, holding its breath not in hunger but in patience. Waiting, perhaps, for the next voice that came to them freely. There would be others. Word traveled strangely under Thornfell; word would travel strangely above it too.

Ansel walked beside her with his hood down. He had not stopped touching things since they surfaced — doorposts, tree bark, the strap of Brannoc's pack — as if confirming each one real. His soul sat whole behind his eyes. Ivo could feel it, faintly, the way you feel warmth from a stove through a wall: the half of herself she had sung away had been woven into him, or he into her, and the border between them was thinner than skin now. When he shivered, she shivered. When he laughed at nothing — and he laughed often, giddy and unsteady, a boy who had very nearly dissolved into harmony and come back with his name intact — something in her chest answered.

"Stop looking at me like that," Ansel said, without turning his head.

"Like what?"

"Like I'm a page you're checking for errors."

"You were full of errors," Ivo said. "You hummed in your sleep for eleven years and nobody thought to ask why."

He grinned. It was a good grin. It was going to get him in trouble someday, and she found she was glad she'd be around to see it.

The mouth of the smugglers' road opened onto the hillside above Thornfell in the grey hour before dawn, and the town lay below them with its chimneys smoking and its bells — the temple's bells, Saint Vellum's proper consecrated bells — ringing wildly, out of any pattern Ivo had ever copied into the office books.

"That's not matins," she said.

Brannoc squinted down the slope. "That's not bells. That's panic with a rope."

* * *

They came down into a town mid-boil.

Word had outrun them somehow — Odile swore later it was the mountain itself that told, vibrating the news up through every well and cellar floor in Thornfell until half the town woke knowing that the court beneath had changed hands, that the Regent was unraveled, that the old debts were ash. The temple square was packed. Priests stood on the steps of Saint Vellum's in their nightclothes arguing with each other. And in the center of it all, on the wide stone landing where the tithing carts used to queue, someone had built a fire.

Not a small fire. A bonfire, fed by armloads of paper, and Ivo knew what the paper was before she was close enough to read the curling edges. Contracts. Ledgers. Tithing rolls going back two hundred years, the great bound volumes with brass corners that the Abbot kept locked in the counting room, the books in which every soul in Thornfell's obligations were entered in red ink and black. Villagers stood around the fire throwing more onto it — farmers, fishwives, the cooper, old Mother Hesse who owed the temple four goats and a christening fee from 1811. They were feeding their own debts to the flames with the solemn joy of people burning a fever out of a house.

And standing apart from all of it, at the edge of the square where the shadow of the temple wall cut the dawn, stood Serel.

Ivo saw her before anyone else did. She couldn't help it; she had spent three weeks learning the shape of the enforcer's stillness, the particular way Serel held her weight as though the ground might be audited at any moment. Serel was not watching the fire. She was watching the crowd, cataloguing exits, and across her shoulder hung a satchel Ivo recognized — flat, leather-strapped, heavy with paper.

Their eyes met across fifty feet of burning debt.

Ivo started forward. Brannoc caught her arm. "Don't."

"She sold Ansel," Ivo said. "She nearly got him—"

"And she didn't finish the trade." Brannoc's grip was gentle for a man his size. "Look at her hands."

Serel's hand rested on the satchel's buckle. After a moment — deliberately, making sure Ivo saw it — she lifted the flap, drew out a single folded page, and held it up between two fingers. Even at that distance Ivo knew it. Her indenture contract. The one the Abbot had put in her hands in his study, the one with Quintana written in the margin of Ansel's soul-debt like a brand.

Serel let it fall into the fire.

Then she turned and walked into the alley behind the tanner's row, unhurried, and did not look back, and Ivo understood that this was the closest thing to an apology Serel's arithmetic would ever produce. Not sorry. Settled. Accounts balanced by her own strange ledger, and the ledger closed.

"Let her go," Ansel said quietly. "She gave up the only thing she valued to do it. That's penance enough for anyone."

"It wasn't the only thing she valued."

"No," Ansel agreed. "But it was the only thing she knew how to count."

* * *

They found the Abbot in his study, because Ivo insisted, because some doors you have to walk through yourself.

He was sitting behind his desk with the window open — the window, which had been painted shut since before Ivo's indenture began — and morning air moved through the room for the first time in years, stirring the dust motes like startled birds. He looked smaller. Not diminished exactly; reduced, the way a man is reduced when the scaffolding of a lifetime's certainty has been taken down around him and he hasn't yet learned which of his own bones will hold him up.

On the desk before him lay a single sheet of paper, blank except for a line of handwriting across the top that Ivo recognized with a lurch: it was the same hand that had answered the prayer in the scriptorium, all those months ago. The hand that had started everything. The old gods' writing, loose in the world again.

*Ivo Quintana,* it said. *Your account is closed. Come collect what remains.*

"I've been sitting here since midnight," the Abbot said, without looking up. "Trying to decide whether to burn it."

"What does the rest say?"

"Nothing. That's all of it. One line." He laughed — a dry, cracked sound, like a book whose spine has finally gone. "Do you know, I have spent forty years managing the accounts of heaven. Forty years of balances and remissions and interest accrued against souls too poor to pay. And when the gods finally wrote to me directly, they sent me one line about a scribe." He looked up then, and his eyes were wet and furious and, underneath it all, relieved in a way that frightened Ivo more than anger would have. "They never wanted me at all. All those prayers I forwarded. All those petitions I approved or denied. I was a clerk at a door that had been bricked shut, and I told everyone the silence was doctrine."

"Yes," Ivo said. Because he deserved the truth and no one else in the room would give it to him plainly. "You did."

"The debts are void." He said it like a man reporting his own death. "Every contract tied to the court beneath the mountain — voided when the Regent unraveled. Every obligation the temple held through that court — voided. The tithes, the soul-pledges, the harvest bonds. Two hundred years of bookkeeping, worthless overnight. The townspeople are burning whatever copies they can find, but it doesn't matter. Burning them changes nothing. They were already ash." He pushed the gods' letter an inch toward her. "Including yours. Your indenture, Ivo. It was collateralized against the temple's claim on the mountain's court. That claim is gone. You are—" his voice caught, and he mastered it, "—you are free. You have been free since the moment you sang."

Ivo picked up the letter. The paper was warm, faintly, like a stone left in sun. *Come collect what remains.* She thought of the half-soul she had given away, and of how it hadn't felt like losing anything — it had felt like setting down a weight she'd carried so long she'd mistaken it for her spine. What remained was hers. Whatever that was.

"What happens to you?" she asked.

The Abbot looked out the open window at the smoke rising from the square, at the townspeople dancing — actually dancing — around the ashes of their ledgers. "I don't know," he said, and it seemed to cost him everything, and it seemed, also, to be the first honest sentence he had spoken in decades. "Perhaps I'll learn to pray properly. Uncoerced. It appears that's the only kind they take now." He almost smiled. "Tell your grandmother's ghost she overpaid."

"She'd want a receipt."

For a moment — just a moment — the Abbot laughed, really laughed, and the sound was so human and so broken and so free that Ivo had to leave the room before he saw what it did to her face.

* * *

They stayed three days in Thornfell, because bodies need mending and Brannoc's had a great deal of mending owed to it. The wound Serel's blade had opened at the Weeping Stair had gone bad in the mountain's cold, and for one bad night Ivo sat by his pallet in Mother Hesse's front room listening to him pray — not to Vesh, not to the eaten god whose name he still wouldn't say, but to the mountain itself, to the new-woken choir, asking nothing, just talking, the way you talk to someone at a deathbed.

The mountain talked back. Everyone in the house heard it: a low chord through the floorboards, warm as bread, and by morning the fever had broken.

"That's showing off," Brannoc grumbled, propped on his elbows, grey-faced and alive.

"That's gratitude," said Odile, signing with one hand while the other changed his bandage. "You gave them a gift once. They remember."

"I gave them grief."

"Grief freely offered is a gift. That's rather the whole lesson, isn't it?" She tied off the bandage and looked at Ivo over his chest, and her eyes — sharp, dark, always three maps ahead of everyone else — were shining with something Ivo was surprised to recognize as mischief. "Besides. Someone has to stay and map what the mountain becomes now. The undersides are changing daily. Old roads reopening. Shrines waking in villages that forgot they had them. Do you know what a cartographer does with a landscape that redraws itself?"

"Retires?"

"Starts a life's work." Odile smiled her small, rare smile. "We're staying. Both of us. Brannoc can guard my survey camps and complain about the weather, which is the only prayer he's any good at anyway."

"I heard that," Brannoc said.

"You were meant to."

Ivo looked at them — the defrocked priest and the deaf cartographer, the mercenary who prayed to a dead god and the woman who had heard the whole world through her boots — and felt the specific ache of a thing she had never had before and was now, for the first time, in a position to lose: friends. Chosen ones. Companions who were for a road, and who had decided the road ended here.

"Some companions are for a road," she said aloud, testing the shape of it. "Not a home."

"Exactly," said Odile. "Which is why you'll write. Or better — you'll come back through, when your wandering takes you. The mountain likes you. It'll hold the road open."

As if to confirm it, the floorboards hummed.

* * *

On the third evening, Ivo went up to the scriptorium.

It was empty. The other scribes had abandoned their desks for the fires in the square, and the long room smelled of ink and candle smoke and the particular dusty holiness of a place where no one had lied out loud in years. Her desk was where she had left it. On it lay the page — the first page, the petition that had answered itself in a stranger's hand, the page she had stolen and hidden and carried through everything. She had left it here before descending, tucked under the blotter like a promise to herself.

She smoothed it flat. Read her own careful petition — *Saint Vellum, keeper of accounts, hear your servant* — and beneath it, in that wild, bell-toned handwriting: *Heard. Ask better.*

Ask better. She had spent her whole life asking badly, she realized. Asking the way the temple taught: on her knees, in the negative, begging not to be hurt rather than daring to name what she wanted. And the gods — the real ones, the hungry ones, the ones who woke — had never wanted kneeling at all. They wanted voices. Free ones. Chosen ones. A choir assembled not from debt but from desire.

Ivo dipped her pen.

She wrote slowly, carefully, in her best copyist's hand, the way you write something you intend to keep:

*To the Choir beneath the mountain, and to Vesh of the nine bells, and to whatever else is listening now that listening costs you nothing: I am Ivo Quintana, granddaughter of Maren, half a soul and wholly myself. I am not signing anything. I am not selling anything. This is not a contract. This is a letter. I am going to walk the pilgrim roads and guide the ones who come to you willingly, and I will tell them the truth about the price of voices, because I have paid it twice. If you want company, leave the lamps lit. If you want song, sing first.*

She signed it. No seal. No witness. No blood.

The candle flame leaned sideways, though the window was shut. And across the bottom of the page, in that handwriting like struck bronze, an answer bloomed:

*Sung first. Always, from now on, sung first. Walk well, Ivo Quintana. The lamps are lit.*

Outside, far off, the mountain hummed — and somewhere in the temple below, clear and bright and entirely his own, Ansel began to sing vespers for an audience of two worlds, and Ivo sat in the empty scriptorium with ink on her fingers and nothing owed to anyone, and wept, and laughed, and reached for a fresh page to begin telling the truth about the road.
