## 22. Ansel and the Choir

*Ansel*

The morning of the first proper choir practice after everything, Ansel arrived early enough to see his own breath in the cold of the nave.

He had not been early to anything in his life before the mountain. Foundlings learned to be exactly on time — early was presumptuous, late was theft. But now he came a full hour before Matins, and nobody scolded him, and he stood in the aisle with the dust motes drifting down through the clerestory like slow golden snow, and he let himself simply *be there*, unaccounted for, and it felt like standing in warm water.

The choir loft had been repaired. That still surprised him when he looked at it — the rail where Serel's men had splintered the wood hauling him over it, the flagstones below where Brannoc had bled. The masons had used new timber, paler than the old, so that if you knew where to look you could read the whole terrible month written in the grain of the railing. Ansel had decided he liked knowing. The temple wanted to sand the past smooth and varnish over it, but the mountain had taught him that things carried their histories in their bodies whether they wished to or not. He would rather be legible.

He climbed to the loft and took his place at the end of the second row, where the youngest voices went, and waited for the others.

They came in ones and twos: Brother Aldous with his ruined cough, the twins Pell and Perrin who sang like two halves of one throat, old Marta who had sung at this rail for fifty years and remembered when the god beneath Thornfell had last been awake, though she claimed she didn't. They nodded to him. Not the careful, frightened nodding of the weeks right after — *there goes the boy who was taken* — but ordinary nods, the nods you give someone whose place among you is no longer remarkable.

That, more than anything, told him he was going to be all right.

Brother Aldous tapped the rail for silence and gave them the morning's piece: an old setting of the Vellum litany, plain and square, the kind of music built to fill a room without ever once asking anything of the room. Ansel sight-read it easily. He had always sight-read easily; that had been the first small strangeness about him, back when strangeness was only a private thing he kept behind his teeth.

But now, as the choir found its footing and the litany began its patient climb, Ansel heard something underneath it.

It started low, lower than any of them were singing — a hum in the stone itself, or under the stone, rising through the foundations the way water rises through chalk. He knew that sound. He had walked inside that sound. It was the Choir beneath the mountain, the great woven voice of every soul that had ever been traded down into the dark — except that it was changed now, loosened, the way a braid is loosened when one strand is pulled gently free. It did not drag at him anymore. It did not say *come down, come home, dissolve*. It just... listened.

And when the choir reached the third phrase, the listening became something else. A harmony rose from under the floor, faint as memory, fitting itself under their melody the way a river fits under a bridge — supporting it, carrying it, asking nothing.

Ansel's voice caught. Brother Aldous frowned at him, and he shook his head, recovered, and sang on. But he understood what was happening, and understanding made his chest ache in a way that was not entirely pain.

The old gods were learning a new way to eat.

Not eating. That was the point. Vesh had explained it to him once, in her borrowed faces and her voice made of bell-sound, in the strange gentleness of a starving thing that has just discovered it can be fed by hand instead of by theft: *We were never meant to take, little door. We were meant to be given to. Every faith forgets this eventually, and then it builds temples like ledgers.* She had said it while Ivo stood beside him with her torn contract still smoking in her fist, and Ansel had thought: *she sounds like someone remembering how to breathe.*

So now, when the temple choir sang, the mountain listened. And when the singing was freely given — and it always was, now; Aldous had stopped docking wages for missed practices, which had turned out to matter more than anyone expected — the mountain answered. Quietly. Underneath. A harmony no one had taught them, rising up through the flagstones, and only Ansel could hear all of it clearly, because part of him had lived down there and come back whole, and whole things remember.

He sang for both audiences now. He had stopped trying to explain this to anyone. You sang for the room you were in, and you sang for the dark beneath it, and both of them deserved your truest voice, and neither of them owned you.

When the litany ended, the harmony lingered a moment longer, like a guest reluctant to leave, and then faded into the ordinary sounds of the morning — carts in the lane, the scriptorium shutters opening, someone laughing in the cloister.

Marta was looking at him sideways. "You hear it too," she said. Not a question.

Ansel considered lying. He had gotten better at honesty lately; it seemed rude to regress. "Yes," he said.

"Last time it woke," Marta said, folding her hands, "I was a girl, and my mother wouldn't let me out of the house for a season. People lost things. Names, mostly." She paused. "This time it feels different."

"It is different," Ansel said. "Someone taught it manners."

Marta snorted, which from Marta was practically a hymn of praise, and shuffled off to complain about the new timber in the railing.

Ansel stayed a moment alone in the loft. He put his hand flat on the pale new wood, and beneath his palm, faint and patient, the mountain hummed — not calling, just keeping company.

*I'm here,* he thought at it, the way you'd think at a friend across a courtyard. *I'm staying up here. But I'm here.*

The stone warmed very slightly under his hand, the way a cat leans into a palm, and Ansel smiled, and went down to breakfast.

*Ivo*

The pilgrims always asked the same question at the same bend in the road, and Ivo had come to love the question the way you love a song you've heard a hundred times and never once tired of.

"Is it true the mountain answers?"

She would stop, and turn, and let them see the whole grey shoulder of Thornfell rising above the pinewoods, its summit wrapped in cloud that moved against the wind — a small wrongness you could point to, proof that the world had seams. Then she would say: "It answers prayers that aren't coerced. Which rules out most of mine, historically, so mind what you ask it."

They laughed. Pilgrims always laughed. They were, by definition, people who had chosen to walk — nobody indentured themselves to a pilgrimage — and chosen laughter travels differently than obliged laughter. Ivo had spent twenty years surrounded by obliged laughter and she could taste the difference now the way a wine-taster tastes a corked bottle.

Her company this season was eleven strong: three widows from Lowmarsh walking for a sister dead of childbed; a young man with ink-stained fingers who claimed to be a scribe himself and kept glancing at Ivo's hands with professional envy; a pair of brothers who hadn't spoken to each other in nine years and had apparently decided a haunted mountain was neutral ground; and five others whose reasons she hadn't pried at, because reasons were tolls too, and she had sworn off collecting those.

They were camped at the Shrine of Nine Bells, which was no longer nine bells but eleven — two new ones cast that spring, paid for by public subscription, which Vesh had declared *hilarious* in her voice made of bell-sound. The avatar herself was not present, but the shrine hummed anyway, and the pilgrims kept glancing at it the way you glance at a sleeping dog.

"Ivo." The ink-fingered scribe — Tam, his name was — hovered at the edge of her fire. "Can I ask you something improper?"

"You can ask. Whether I answer depends on the impropriety."

"The indentures. The temple's. Everyone says they burned. All of them, all at once, the night the Regent fell. Is that true? Because I have—" he flexed his stained fingers, "—a certain number of years left on mine, and I confess I've dreamed about it."

Ivo fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. Her own indenture papers had burned in the Abbot's ledger-room along with everything else, and she had not gone to look at the ashes, because she had not needed to. She had known the moment the contract tore in her hands beneath the mountain — known it in the place where her half-soul used to sit, a hollow that ached in cold weather like an old break.

"It's true," she said. "Every debt the temple held was voided. The old gods don't recognize coerced contracts anymore, and the temple's whole economy was coerced contracts wearing vestments." She smiled at Tam's expression. "Go home afterward and check. If your paper still exists, it's a souvenir now, nothing more."

"And you?" Tam asked, with the delicacy of the young, which is to say none. "What do you do now? Now that you're—"

"Free?" Ivo supplied. "Half-ghost? Granddaughter of the most interesting woman in Thornfell's history?"

"All of those, I suppose."

"I walk," Ivo said. "People want to come to the mountain now — properly, willingly, to pray or apologize or gawk. Someone has to show them the roads that don't bite. Turns out I know those roads by heartbeat." She turned her hands palms-up to the fire. Old calluses from the scriptorium were fading; new ones from the staff she carried were coming in. "It's honest work. Nobody's soul changes hands. I get paid in actual coin."

Tam laughed, and then, because he was young and the fire was warm: "Do you miss it? The copying?"

Ivo considered telling him the truth, which was complicated — that she missed the smell of ink the way you miss a childhood house even after you learn what happened in its cellar. But what she said was: "I miss being good at something quiet. So I'm practicing."

She looked up at the mountain while the pilgrims settled toward sleep. Somewhere up there, past the weeping stair and the bridge of signed names and the galleries of traded memories — all of it open now, all of it emptied of its stolen goods — something vast and newly gentle was keeping watch. Some nights she swore she could feel it noticing her, the way you feel sunlight find you through a window. It never pressed. That was the miracle she still couldn't quite believe: it never pressed.

*Ansel*

Autumn came, and with it the Festival of Unbought Song, which had not existed as a festival until that summer and was already developing traditions at the alarming speed of all invented things.

The whole town walked up to the mountain's mouth at dusk — not into it, never into it anymore, that road stayed closed out of respect rather than fear — and stood in the amphitheater of stone below the entrance, and sang. Anyone who wished. Any song. Children sang nonsense; old men sang drinking songs; the widow Cattrell sang a lament for her husband that made half the crowd weep; and the mountain took all of it, harmonized with all of it, and gave it back warmer.

Ansel stood near the front with Ivo, because that was where they always stood now, side by side, the boy who had been sold and the girl whose grandmother had done the selling-adjacent buying, and neither of them had ever discussed why they gravitated together and neither of them needed to. Some friendships are signed in blood and grief and require no further paperwork.

"Do you think she'd have liked this?" Ansel asked. "Your grandmother. All these people, singing for free."

Ivo was quiet a moment. She had lost her grandmother's face to the tollkeeper ghost on the bridge, and everyone knew it, and everyone had learned not to look at her strangely when she spoke of the woman she remembered only as warmth and ink and the smell of bread. "She sold half her soul to buy a village its memory back," Ivo said. "I think she'd have liked anything where nobody had to sell anything." A pause. "I think she'd have heckled, though. Lovingly."

"She sounds like you."

"I'm given to understand it's genetic."

The singing started around them, ragged at first, then finding itself. Ansel opened his mouth and joined, and as always when he sang now, he sang twice — once for the crowd, once for the deep listeners — and as always, the mountain met him halfway. The harmony rose under the town's voices, and it was richer this year than last, fuller, because the Choir beneath was no longer a cage but a chorus, souls who had *chosen* to stay singing, souls who had been freed and found they loved the song, and somewhere in that great braided voice Ansel could pick out — he would always be able to pick it out — one strand that had joined only recently, freely, half of a whole, singing in a register that sounded uncannily like Ivo when she forgot anyone was listening.

He glanced at her. She was singing with her eyes shut, and her face was doing something complicated and private, and he understood that she could hear it too.

Neither of them said anything. There was a whole vocabulary they shared that consisted entirely of not saying things.

When the last song ended and the crowd began to drift homeward with lanterns bobbing, the mountain did its new trick: it held the final chord a breath longer than the singers did, and released it gently, like setting down something fragile. The whole crowd felt it. The whole crowd pretended not to cry. Tradition formed around them like frost.

"Walk me to the crossroads?" Ivo said. "I leave at dawn. There's a group from the coast wanting the long route — shrines, springs, the works. Two weeks' walk."

"You just got back."

"I know." She grinned, and it was the grin of someone who had discovered late that freedom has legs. "That's the best part."

They walked down through the pinewoods together, and behind them the mountain hummed its goodnight, and ahead of them the temple bells rang the hour — and for the first time in the long history of Thornfell, the two sounds were not rivals. They were parts of the same song, and everyone in earshot was welcome to sing it, and no one, ever again, would be made to.

At the crossroads Ivo squeezed his shoulder once — her entire vocabulary of tenderness, and it was enough — and turned toward the pilgrim road, and Ansel stood a while in the dark between the lantern-light of the town and the faint glow of the shrine, belonging everywhere, owned nowhere, humming.
