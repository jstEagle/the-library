## 1. The Date That Shouldn't Exist

The moon-iron sang to him all night.

Bram Amberdale had carried a great many things through the Thornfell passes in his nine years of exile — brandy, gunpowder, letters sealed with wax that melted if the wrong eyes touched them — but nothing hummed like moon-iron. It sat in the double-lined crate lashed to his back and it *sang*, a thin silver thread of sound only he could hear, the way dogs heard whistles. Old Wren said that was nonsense, that the metal was dead as any other metal, but Wren had never been struck by lightning twice before his seventeenth birthday. Wren didn't know what it was like to have the sky take an interest in you.

The storm had been following him since dusk.

He felt it the way he felt most weather now: not with his skin but somewhere deeper, behind the sternum, where the old scar tissue from the second lightning strike had healed into something like a second heart. This one was still two valleys east, dragging its skirts over the high crags, but it was coming this way, and it was angry, and Bram did not need a witch-finder's divining rods to know why. Storms were drawn to him the way iron filings were drawn to a lodestone. He had spent nine years learning to be small, to be quiet, to be a man who moved goods and asked no questions, and still the sky found him out every time.

So when the ruined chapel rose out of the snow like a broken tooth, he did not hesitate.

It was an old thing, older than the Crown, older maybe than the border wars that had redrawn every map for three hundred miles. Its roof had fallen in a century ago or more; its bell tower stood open to the sky like a ribcage; frost furred the stones where saints or kings or somebody had once been carved and had since been worn down to suggestions of faces. Bram shouldered the door — half its hinges gone, the wood swollen with ice — and stepped into a nave knee-deep in drifted snow, blue in the last light, silent as the inside of a held breath.

He set down the crate. He built a fire in the lee of the fallen altar, using splinters of pews that had probably been prayed on by people whose grandchildren's grandchildren were dust, and he told himself the tightness in his chest was just the altitude.

*Just the altitude,* he thought, as the first thunder rolled over the pass, distant and patient. *Just the cold.*

* * *

The peddler came an hour after full dark.

Bram heard the mule before he saw the man — a soft clop-clop-clop in the snow outside, unhurried, which was itself strange, because nothing traveled the Thornfell at night without hurry unless it had no fear of what hunted these mountains, and nothing had no fear of what hunted these mountains. Then the door scraped, and there stood a shape wrapped in a coat made of too many animals, face lost under a hood, snow melting off its shoulders in slow beads.

"Fire," the peddler said. Not a request. A statement, like naming the weather.

Bram's hand drifted toward the knife at his belt, an old habit worn smooth as river stone. "There's room enough if you keep to your side."

"I'll keep to my side." The peddler shuffled forward into the firelight, and Bram got his first look at the face beneath the hood: old, wind-scoured, unremarkable, the kind of face you forgot while you were still looking at it. Except for the eyes. The eyes were quick and black and they went straight past Bram's face to the crate against the wall, and stayed there a moment too long, and smiled.

"You're the iron-man," the peddler said.

"I'm nobody."

"Mm." The peddler lowered himself onto a fallen pew with the groaning care of the very old and began unbuckling a satchel. "Nobody carries moon-iron over the Widow's Tooth in November. Nobody's back is strong enough. Nobody's nerves are calm enough." He glanced up, and the firelight did something unpleasant in those black eyes. "And nobody else makes the barometer drop when he walks into a room."

Bram kept his voice flat. "You've mistaken me for someone worth robbing."

"Oh, I'm not going to rob you." The peddler laughed, a dry rustle like paper. "I'm going to *sell* to you. There's a difference, and the difference is consent." From the satchel he drew a book.

It was a poor-looking thing. Leather cracked and scuffed the color of old tea, corners rounded soft with handling, spine re-sewn at least twice with cord that didn't match. Bram knew books like that; smugglers carried them sometimes, ledgers and ciphers and the occasional forbidden prayer-book, because paper weighed little and sold well. But this one had no title on its cover. Just a stamped impression, worn nearly smooth, of a door.

"What is it?"

"A book." The peddler turned it over in his hands, fondly, the way a man handles a horse he means to be rid of. "A very old book. It belonged to a monastery that isn't there anymore. Then to a family that isn't there anymore. And now—" he shrugged "—it wants to belong to someone who *is*."

"I don't buy books."

"Tonight you do." The peddler set it on the pew between them, deliberate as a man laying down cards. "For firewood money. That's all I ask. A few coins. Enough to keep me in kindling till the low country." He smiled again, and Bram noticed his teeth were very even for a man who looked like he'd never eaten anything but hardship. "Consider it charity. I'm an old man, and it's a cold night, and I'd rather sell than freeze."

Everything in Bram said *no*. Nine years of exile had built that instinct fine-grained as good joinery: strangers on the mountain at night, strangers who knew too much, strangers whose prices were never what they seemed. His hand was already moving to his coin purse only because refusing outright would prolong the conversation, and every moment the peddler sat there, the storm outside rolled closer, and Bram wanted — needed — to be alone with it, to ride out its attention in silence, the way he always did.

"How much?"

"Whatever's in the left pocket."

Bram stared at him. Slowly, feeling like a man walking in his sleep, he reached into his left coat pocket and drew out the coins there — four coppers and a battered silver bit, exactly the firewood money, exactly what he'd budgeted for the fuel he'd need at the next waystation. He hadn't counted them in days. He hadn't known how much was there.

He put them in the peddler's palm. The peddler closed his fingers over them without counting, pushed the book across the pew, and stood up in one motion that had nothing old or infirm in it at all.

"Pleasure doing business," he said. At the door he paused, hood lifting slightly, and for one strange instant Bram could have sworn the man was looking not at him but at something just above his head, the way you look at weather coming. "Read the parts about the hands," the peddler said. "Those are the best parts."

Then he was gone into the dark, and the mule's footsteps faded, and the storm arrived overhead like a guest taking its seat.

* * *

Bram did not sleep.

He fed the fire and sat with his back to the wall and let the storm spend its temper on the chapel roofless bones, lightning strobing blue-white through the empty bell tower, thunder cracking so hard the old stones shed grit. It was a bad one. It was looking for him. He could feel it circling the ruin like a dog around a door, frustrated, hungry, and he did what he had taught himself to do in the first terrible years after the exile: he breathed slow, and he kept his fear small, and he gave the sky nothing to grip.

When the worst of it had dragged itself eastward sometime past midnight, he took out the book.

He told himself it was practical. A book with no title, bought from a stranger who knew too much, was either worthless or dangerous, and both kinds were worth knowing. He turned it in the firelight. The leather was warm. That was the first wrong thing — the night was bitter enough to freeze breath in the wool of his beard, and the book lay in his hands warm as a sleeping bird.

He opened it.

The pages were heavy stock, cream-colored, dense with a cramped brown ink. Not print — manuscript, every line hand-lettered by someone with a steady hand and no love of margins. Diagrams crowded the text: star-charts, genealogies, maps of coastlines Bram didn't recognize, and one recurring figure that appeared on page after page like a signature — a door, always the same door, drawn in careful section like an architect's plan, with annotations in a language he couldn't read and, beneath them, in smaller script, translations in trade-tongue. *The Door Opens Inward. The Door Opens From Within. The Door Remembers Being Open.*

Prophecy, then. Or the ravings of some monastery madman. Bram had met both, and they looked identical on paper.

He flipped faster, skimming, half his attention still on the settling storm. Dates ran down the margins like a ledger of appointments — names beside them, short entries, the whole thing arranged not as a story but as a schedule. *In the year of the salt famine, the grey-eyed queen weeps blood.* *In the year the river forgets its name, a traitor wears a friend's face.* Nonsense. Beautiful, patient nonsense, the kind of thing that convinced frightened people to give everything to whoever claimed to understand it.

Then the date caught his eye, and his thumb stopped moving.

He read it once. He read it again. Outside, the wind worried at the bell tower, and the fire popped, and Bram Amberdale sat perfectly still in a ruined chapel in the Thornfell mountains while his own heartbeat became the loudest thing in the world.

Today's date. Not approximately. Not a near-miss he could explain away with bad calendars or transcription errors. The day, the month, the year — written in brown ink by a hand that had been dust for a hundred years.

Beside it, a name: *Bram Amberdale.*

His name. Spelled correctly. The surname no one used anymore except tax rolls and witch-finder warrants, the name he'd buried along with his old life the day he walked out of the low country with nothing but a knife and a talent he'd have cut out of his own chest if he could have reached it.

"No," he said aloud, to the chapel, to the fire, to nobody.

He turned the page because his hands turned it, because dread has its own momentum, because a man falling does not decide to fall.

And there — filling the page in the same cramped, patient hand — a drawing.

Two hands, cupped together, palms up. Scarred hands. He knew the scars the way you know your own teeth: the long white seam across the left knuckle where a rusted cargo hook had opened him to the bone; the burn-pucker on the right thumb from the forge at Calder's Crossing; the small crescent below the index finger where his sister's cat had punished him at seven years old. Every mark. Every one, rendered precisely, lovingly, by someone who had never seen him and never would.

And rising out of the cupped palms, drawn in strokes so fine they might have been smoke, a storm. Lightning coiled like a living thing. Rain falling upward. The sky held in two scarred hands like water, like a gift, like a weapon waiting to be named.

Below the drawing, one line, in the translator's smaller script:

*On this day the storm-bearer shelters in the chapel at Thornfell and reads his own name, and does not yet believe.*

Bram looked up. The ruined nave gaped around him, snow-blue and silent. The bell tower framed a sky rinsed clean by the storm, scattered hard with stars, and beyond the broken wall the mountains went down and down into darkness toward the low country, toward the Crown, toward the witch-finders who had been hunting a storm-caller through these passes since autumn.

Somewhere far off, too far to be real, a bell rang. Once. The chapel had no bell. It had had no bell for a hundred years.

Bram closed the book gently, the way you close a door on a room where something is sleeping, and sat with his scarred hands flat on its cover, and began — very carefully, very reasonably, the way a man talks himself off a ledge — to plan how he would convince himself, by morning, that this was coincidence.

He almost managed it. Then he noticed the last line of the entry, the one his eye had skipped, written smaller than the rest, almost apologetic:

*He will not believe. But they are already on the pass behind him, and the woman with the maps has already chosen to lie.*
