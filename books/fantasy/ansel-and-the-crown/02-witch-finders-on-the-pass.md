## 2. Witch-Finders on the Pass

The waystation at Cold Saddle was the kind of place Bram Amberdale trusted precisely because it was worthless. A squat stone barn crouched against the mountainside, half-swallowed by scree, its roof sagging under a century of snow it had never been cleared of. The Crown's maps marked nothing there. That was the whole point. Everything worth having in Thornfell lived in the blank spaces — the moon-iron in his pack, the coin in Old Wren's purse, the names that didn't exist on any ledger.

He'd been at the fire for an hour when the dogs started.

Not barking. That was the first wrongness. Witch-finders' hounds didn't bark; they'd been bred out of it, the way they'd been bred to a silence that felt less like training and more like grief. What Bram heard instead was the fire itself — the flames leaning east, all of them at once, toward the pass road, the way iron filings lean toward a lodestone. His storms answered fear the way his pulse answered a knife at the throat, and the fire's behavior told him everything the dogs hadn't yet.

"They're coming," he said.

Tamsin looked up from the whetstone she'd been dragging along her sword's edge for the better part of an hour, more ritual than work. "Coming where?"

"Here. All of them." Bram was already moving, kicking dirt over the fire with a boot-heel, feeling the small heat die under his foot like something drowning. "Finders. Six, maybe eight. The fire can smell the iron in their badges."

Old Wren didn't argue; Wren never argued, which was how he'd lived to be old. He simply began sweeping his trade goods into their cases with the practiced calm of a man who had been raided before and intended to be raided again without loss. Pip was asleep in the loft with the mules, a knot of elbows and stolen scarves, and Tamsin went up the ladder two rungs at a time to wake her.

That left the cartographer.

She was sitting apart, as she had sat apart for three days now, wrapped in a grey traveling cloak that was too fine for the road and too plain for a court. She had a name — Sera — and a commission she'd shown them at the pass gate, wax-sealed, royal, the kind of paper that made smugglers polite. She was mapping the Thornfell passes for the Crown's winter campaign, she'd said. She'd paid for protection. Wren had taken her coin with a shrug, because coin didn't care whose face was stamped on it.

Now she was looking at Bram the way she'd been looking at him since the chapel, two nights back. Not with suspicion. With something worse. Recognition.

"You knew this would happen," she said. It wasn't an accusation. It was quieter than that, and it cut deeper for it. "The book. You read it and then you sat by that fire like a man waiting for a knock."

"I sat by the fire because it was cold."

"Bram." She said his name like she was testing the weight of it. "The finders don't raid waystations for moon-iron. They don't ride this high in the season. They came for something specific, and whatever it is, it's written down somewhere, and I—" She stopped. Her hand had gone to her satchel, the one with the sealed papers, and stopped there, hovering, as if the leather had gone hot.

From the pass road, faint but closing, came the sound Bram had been dreading: the creak of many boots moving in unison. Not soldiers. Soldiers clattered. These men had been taught to walk quietly, and the quiet was somehow more obscene.

"Loft," Bram said. "All of you. Now."

* * *

The loft smelled of mule and old hay and Pip's fear, which had a smell of its own, sharp and green like snapped stems. Bram pulled the loose board over the trapdoor and crouched in the dark with his back against the rafters, and he made the mistake he always made, which was to listen to his own heart.

Because his heart was doing something it shouldn't. It was calling.

He'd learned at fourteen what his blood could do, learned it in a border town that no longer existed, learned it in thunder and in the long silence after. Since then he had spent twenty years doing the opposite of calling — swallowing every storm that came near him, packing the pressure down behind his ribs like a man swallowing stones in a river so he wouldn't be swept off his feet. But fear loosened the seal. Fear was the river. And somewhere below him, eight men in grey were climbing the waystation steps with a name in their pocket, and his blood was answering them like a struck bell, and the pressure behind his ribs rose and rose, and outside the clouds that had been sitting fat and harmless over the pass all day began, very slowly, to turn.

*Don't*, he told himself. *Don't you dare.*

The door below banged open. Not knocked — banged, the way men do when the paper in their pockets means they don't have to knock.

"By authority of the Crown's Commission of Inquiry," a voice said, and it was a voice Bram would remember, level and dry as old paper, the voice of a man who had said these words so many times they had worn smooth as river stones. "This station is seized. Everyone down. Slowly."

Boots on floorboards. Wren's voice, easy and unctuous: "Officers. An honor. Can I offer you—"

"Where is the smuggler Amberdale?"

The silence that followed had a shape, and the shape was Bram's name hanging in the air of the room below him. He felt Pip's small hand find his in the dark. Her palm was ice.

"Amberdale," the dry voice said again. "Storm-caller. Exiled from the lowlands under writ of the old king, survived the purges at Harrow Fen, currently carrying—" a page turned, and Bram felt the turning in his spine, "—a quantity of moon-iron and a text of prohibited provenance. He was seen at a ruined chapel on the Thornfell road two nights past. He is here, or he was. The hounds say the storm is close."

*The hounds say the storm is close.* As if the storm were a separate animal. As if it weren't crouched in a loft trying very hard not to breathe lightning.

"We don't know any Amberdales," Tamsin said from below. Her voice was flat, bored, a deserter's masterpiece of nothing-to-see-here. "We know a Wren, a girl, and me. The cartographer's under our protection, Crown paper and all. You're welcome to read it."

A pause. Then footsteps, unhurried, crossing the floor — and stopping directly beneath Bram, so that he could see through the gap in the boards the crown of a grey hat and, below it, the top of a face he couldn't read. The man below him held something in his hands. A book. Old, cracked, swollen with damp.

Bram knew that book. He'd sold it for firewood money two nights ago to a peddler with clever eyes, and he'd told himself it was nothing, just a mad old thing of blank pages and bad ink, and then he'd found today's date in it, and his name, and a sketch of his own scarred hands holding a storm, and he had run.

"The text was recovered this morning," the finder said. "The peddler was very cooperative. They usually are, once we show them our methods." Another pause, and when the voice came again it was addressed to the room, mild as a man discussing weather. "The storm-caller is within forty feet of me. I can feel it in the iron. I will ask once more, and then I will stop asking, and my colleagues will begin their work. Where is he?"

And that was when Sera spoke.

* * *

Later — much later, on a glacier shelf under a sky the color of a bruise — Bram would ask her why. Why she did it. Why a royal cartographer with a sealed commission and a career ahead of her chose, in the space of one held breath, to lie to eight witch-finders with iron badges. And she would look at him with that expression she had, the one that was half apology and half defiance, and she would say: *I don't know. I've been trying to find the reason ever since. I think maybe the reason found me first.*

But in the moment, all Bram knew was this: the grey hat below him turned, and the dry voice said, "You. Cartographer. You've been surveying these passes for three days. Sight lines, shelters, camps. Where would a fugitive shelter within a mile of this station?"

And Sera said, in the clear, precise, unimpressed voice of a woman who had spent her life making men in authority feel foolish for asking: "There's a hollow under the scree field, east of the stream fork. Half-buried, dry, invisible from the road unless you know the angle. If your man is as careful as your text suggests, that's where he is."

Every word true. Every word useless — because the hollow was two miles east, on the far side of the stream, and by the time eight finders had searched it and the scree field and the stream fork in a rising storm, the waystation would be empty.

The silence below lasted three heartbeats. Bram counted them against the pressure in his chest, against the clouds outside grinding against each other like millstones, against Pip's hand crushing his fingers.

"You're certain," the dry voice said.

"I'm a cartographer," Sera said. "Certainty is my trade. Uncertainty is yours."

A sound that might have been a laugh, from one of the others. Then the dry voice again, and Bram heard something in it that made his blood run colder than fear ever had: interest. "Your name, cartographer."

"Sera. Of the Royal Survey."

"Sera of the Royal Survey. We'll speak again." Boots moving now, toward the door, and the dry voice trailing back like a hook left in the water: "The storm is close, friends. Storms don't hide well. They only wait."

The door closed. The boots crunched away down the pass road, eight sets of them, east, toward the stream fork, toward the hollow, toward two wasted miles of rising wind.

For a long moment nobody moved. Then Pip's grip went slack, and Tamsin's voice came up through the trapdoor, ragged: "Clear. For now. Get down here before your storm comes through the roof."

Bram came down the ladder on legs that didn't feel like his. Sera was standing by the dead fire, and she was holding her satchel against her chest with both arms, and her face was the color of the snow that hadn't fallen yet. She had just committed treason. She knew it. He could see that she knew it, in the way her eyes went to the door and then to him and then to the door again, calculating, and failing to calculate, because there was no sum that came out in her favor.

"Why," Bram said. Not thank you. He'd thank her later, if they lived. Right now he needed the answer more than the gratitude, because the answer would tell him what she was.

"I don't know." Her voice cracked on the second word, and she hated it, he could see her hating it. "They had your name in a book. A book that's a hundred years old, Bram. A hundred years old, and it has today's date in it, and your face, and I read the page they let me see and I—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "The hand that wrote it. I know that hand. I've been copying it my whole career. It's the hand the Crown writes its commissions in. Mine included."

The wind outside rose, all at once, the way it does when a storm that's been held too long finally gets permission. Bram felt his own pressure ease, slightly, shamefully, like a fist unclenching.

"The finders will search that hollow," he said. "And then they'll come back, and they'll remember your face."

"I know."

"And the Crown doesn't forgive its mapmakers for drawing wrong roads on purpose."

"I know that too." She lifted her chin, and there it was, the first hairline crack in the duty she'd been built from, running through her like a flaw through fine porcelain — invisible unless you knew where to look, and then all you could see. "So I suppose I'd better travel with the smugglers. At least until the passes close."

Old Wren, from the corner, without looking up from his ledger: "Protection costs double for fugitives."

"Triple," Sera said. "I've seen your books."

And Pip, from the loft, in the voice of a child delivering a verdict handed down by someone else entirely — someone old, and dead, and patient: "The ghost says we have to go tonight. She says the snow is coming early, and the door won't wait, and neither will the men in grey."

The fire's ashes stirred. Outside, the first flakes of the long winter's first storm came down through the dark like the mountain exhaling, and Bram Amberdale, storm-caller, smuggler, name in a dead book, looked at the strangers who were about to become the only family he had, and said the only sensible thing left to say.

"Then we'd better pack."
