## 3. A Company of the Unwanted

Sera had drawn maps her whole life, and she had never once drawn herself into one.

That was the thought that kept circling her as she walked east through the Thornfell passes with a smuggler, a deserter, a fence, and a child who talked to ghosts. She had spent eleven years in the Cartographer's Guild learning to render the world as it was — every ridge measured, every river named, every border inked with the Crown's authority. A map was a promise that the world would hold still. And here she was, moving through country that refused to be held, on a road that existed on no chart she had ever signed, in company she would have arrested three days ago.

The witch-finders had let her go because she had lied to them. That was the part she could not stop turning over, like a stone in her pocket. She had stood in the smoke of the waystation, watched them drag Old Wren's crates into the snow, heard the interrogator ask — *have you seen a storm-caller, a man called Bram Amberdale* — and she had pointed west, down the wrong valley, with a steady hand and a steady voice, and said *he went toward the Kell road, I saw his fire from the ridge.* She had never seen any fire. She had known nothing except that something in her chest had gone cold and certain at the same moment, and her mouth had moved without consulting her.

Duty did not do that. Duty did not misfire. So either she was broken, or duty itself was not what she had believed it to be, and both possibilities frightened her more than the mountains did.

Bram Amberdale walked ten paces ahead of her and pretended she wasn't there. He was good at pretending. He had a smuggler's flat walk, shoulders loose, eyes doing constant quiet work at the treeline, and if he resented the royal cartographer stumbling into his crew he kept it folded away somewhere behind his face. Only once, when the path narrowed above a drop, had he paused and offered his hand without looking at her. She had taken it. Neither of them mentioned it.

* * *

They made camp that night in the hollow beneath a leaning shelf of granite, where old Wren said the wind couldn't get its teeth in. Sera learned quickly that Wren's camp was a small economy of habit: the fire ring built from stones that had clearly been carried from elsewhere, chosen for how they sat together; the kettle hung at exactly the height that made no noise; Pip's bedroll placed between Wren's and Tamsin's like a coin set in a setting.

Tamsin was the deserter. Sera had guessed it before anyone told her — the way the woman sat with her back to the rock and her face to the open ground, the way her hands kept finding the absent weight of a sword belt she no longer wore. She was broad and quiet and moved like someone who had once been responsible for other people's lives and missed it terribly. When Sera introduced herself, Tamsin looked at her for a long moment and said, "I know what your guild's seals look like. I burned two of them off a supply wagon I wasn't supposed to burn." Then she'd shrugged. "You're here. Wren says you're here. Wren's usually right about people."

Old Wren himself was a fence — a buyer and seller of things that had fallen off wagons, out of windows, out of history. He was sixty if he was a day, thin as a winter branch, with spectacles mended twice with wire and a voice like dry paper. He had greeted Sera's arrival with neither surprise nor welcome, only a nod, as though royal cartographers wandered into outlaw camps every fortnight. "Sit," he'd said. "You look like a woman who hasn't eaten anything she chose for herself in years." It had been insultingly accurate.

And Pip — Pip was maybe nine, maybe twelve, all elbows and sharp dark eyes, with a coat three sizes too big that she clearly slept in. She had appeared at Sera's elbow within an hour of her joining them, walking backward so she could study her face.

"You're the map lady," Pip said now, dropping cross-legged by the fire with a strip of dried apple in each hand. "Wren says you draw countries."

"I used to."

"Did you ever draw Vaelmoor?"

The name went through the circle like a dropped knife. Tamsin's head came up. Wren's hands stilled over the kettle. Even Bram, who had been studying the dark beyond the firelight, turned.

"No," Sera said carefully. "Nobody draws Vaelmoor. It isn't on the maps."

"That's 'cause it's hiding," Pip said, with the enormous patience of the very young explaining weather to adults. "My ghost says so. He says it didn't die, it just shut its eyes, and everyone forgot where it was standing." She bit her apple. "He's very old. Older than Wren."

"Nothing is older than me," Wren murmured, but his eyes hadn't left the child.

"He talks funny," Pip went on, undeterred. "Like a letter being read out loud. He wears bells on his coat but they don't make noise unless something important's happening, and they've been making noise since we found you, map lady." She pointed the apple core at Sera. "Since the witch-finders. He says the door's waking up."

"Pip," Bram said quietly. It wasn't a reprimand exactly. It was the tone of a man asking a child not to carry something heavy.

But Sera leaned forward despite herself. "Your ghost. What does he call himself?"

Pip's face changed — sharpened, aged, became briefly something older than her body could hold. "Herald," she said. "First Voice of the Court of Vaelmoor. Last of the line." Then she blinked, and she was a child again, licking apple juice off her thumb. "See? Funny talk."

* * *

Later, when Pip slept and Wren pretended to, Sera took out her field kit and began, without quite deciding to, to sketch the hollow by firelight: the granite shelf, the kettle, the shapes of the sleepers. It was the first drawing in weeks that nobody had ordered. Her pencil moved and she felt something in her chest unclench by a single degree.

"You're drawing us."

Bram had settled across the fire, close enough to talk, far enough to leave. His scarred hands were wrapped around a tin cup he wasn't drinking from. In the light, Sera could see the old burn-marks across his knuckles and fingers — Lichtenberg figures, the ferning scars lightning leaves behind, though his were deeper, older, earned over years rather than in a single strike.

"I'm practicing," she said. "Drawing things that don't exist yet."

"And what's that supposed to be?"

"A company of the unwanted." She nodded around the circle. "A deserter. A fence. A pickpocket. An exile. A traitor." She heard herself say the last word and was surprised at how little it cost. "I pointed the witch-finders the wrong way, Amberdale. Whatever I am now, it isn't what I was yesterday."

He studied her for a long time. The fire cracked. Somewhere up-slope, wind moved through the pines with a sound like the sea rehearsing.

"Why?" he asked finally. Not hostile. Just tired, and honest, and wanting the truth the way a man dying of thirst wants water.

"I don't know." She set the pencil down. "I've been trying to find the reason and there isn't one I can write down. Their questions felt wrong. The way they said your name felt wrong — like they weren't hunting a man, they were retrieving something. Like you were cargo." She hesitated. "And then there's the book."

Something shifted in his face. "Wren told you."

"He showed me the page." She had turned the brittle leaves by lantern light while Wren watched her with those mild, terrible eyes. *On the third night of the Frostmoon, the storm-caller Bram Amberdale shall stand at the door of Vaelmoor,* the old hand had written, and below it, a sketch: two scarred hands cupping a storm like a bird. She had known the hands instantly. She had drawn hands professionally for eleven years. "It's a hundred years old, and it has today in it. That's not prophecy, Amberdale. Prophecy is fuzzy. That's a schedule."

"So I'm told." He turned the cup in his fingers. "Everyone keeps telling me what I am. The witch-finders. The book. Now your ghost-reading child." A humorless breath. "Do you want to know the worst part? The part I haven't told them?"

Sera waited.

"When I'm afraid," Bram said, "I can't stop calling storms. I don't mean I choose to. I mean it starts in my hands and my teeth and my bones, and the sky answers whether I want it to or not. Every time. Since I was a boy. My village exiled me for it — not the Crown, *us*, my own people, after the night I—" He stopped. His knuckles had gone white around the tin. "I drowned half the harvest. Two men died. I was fourteen and scared of my father, and the sky did it *for* me."

The fire popped. Sera realized she had stopped breathing and made herself start again.

"The prophecy wants a storm-caller at the door of Vaelmoor," he went on, quieter. "And everything in me that's afraid — and I am always afraid, cartographer, that's the whole engine of me — everything afraid in me wants to go. Because if there's a door, maybe there's a way to put it down. To hand it back. Or to finally be good at it instead of just dangerous." He looked up at her across the flames, and his eyes were the grey of a sky deciding. "So that's what you've thrown in with. A weapon that fires when it's frightened. Are you sorry yet?"

Sera considered the question honestly, because he had earned honesty.

"I grew up in the Guild," she said. "They teach you that a map is a kind of control. You draw the world small enough, you feel like you own it. I was very good at feeling that way." She picked up her sketch and looked at it — the huddled shapes, the fire, the child curled against the deserter's side. "Then I met four people the Crown doesn't want, sitting in a hole in a mountain sharing dried apples, and I realized I've never once drawn anything true. Everything I've ever inked was a lie the Crown paid for." She tore the page free and held it out to him. "So no. I'm not sorry. I'm terrified. But I think this might be the first real thing I've ever been part of, and I'd rather be terrified inside it than safe outside it."

Bram took the sketch. He looked at it for a long time — at the small faithful fire, at Pip's sleeping shape, at his own hunched figure rendered without flattery and without judgment. When he spoke, his voice was rough.

"It's a good drawing."

"It's a true one. There's a difference."

* * *

She woke near dawn to voices pitched low, and lay still, listening the way she had been trained to listen.

"—bells again," Pip was whispering. "He won't stop. He's saying *soon, soon, soon*—"

"Pip, love, it's the middle of the night—"

"No, Wren, listen. He says the snow's coming early this year. The long winter. He says if we don't reach the gate before it closes—" Pip's whisper caught. "He says somebody else is reading the book too. Somebody who isn't us. And they're already on the road."

Across the dead fire, Sera met Bram's eyes in the dark. His scarred hands were already flexing, once, twice — and high above the hollow, though the stars were hard and clear and there was not a cloud from ridge to ridge, she heard the first low grumble of thunder.
