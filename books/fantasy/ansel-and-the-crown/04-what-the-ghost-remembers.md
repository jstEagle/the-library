## 4. What the Ghost Remembers

Pip had learned early that grown-ups only heard what they wanted to hear, which was why she'd stopped telling them about the ghost by the time she was six. Grown-ups wanted to hear about bread and coin and whether the watch was coming, so that's what Pip gave them. The ghost she kept folded up inside her like the last sweet in a pocket, taken out only when no one was looking.

But tonight everyone was looking, because tonight the ghost was *loud*.

She sat cross-legged on a flat stone at the edge of camp, knees drawn up against the cold, watching the grey shape drift between the pines. It looked like a man made of the moment just before dawn — pale, thin, edges bleeding into the trees. He wore a coat with silver frogs down the front, the kind of coat that belonged in a painting of a city Pip had never seen, and he kept bowing. Bowing at Bram, at Sera, at Tamsin sharpening her knife, at Old Wren snoring like a landslide. Bowing at Pip most of all.

"He's doing it again," she announced, because there came a point where keeping a secret got heavier than carrying it, and Pip's back was small but not that small.

Tamsin didn't look up from her knife. "The weasel's talking to you."

"Not a weasel." Pip scowled. "He's a herald. That means he announces things. He's very sad about it."

Bram crouched down to her level, and Pip saw him do the thing he always did — the thing she'd decided meant he believed her even when he pretended not to. His eyes went past her shoulder, into the empty air, and stayed there a beat too long. Storm-callers could feel ghosts, maybe. Or maybe he was just kind.

"What does he want to announce?" Bram asked.

Pip listened. The herald's voice wasn't sound exactly; it arrived behind her eyes, like remembering something that hadn't happened to her. *The gate. The gate. I must reach the gate before the snow. A hundred years and I am still late.*

"He says we have to go north," Pip said. "Through the pass with the broken arch. He says—" She tilted her head, listening harder. "He says Vaelmoor is waiting, and it's been waiting so long it's forgotten how to stop."

Nobody laughed. Pip had expected Wren to laugh, or Tamsin to say something sharp about children who talked rot, but the fire crackled and Sera went very still over her maps and Bram looked at the north dark like it had called his name.

Which, Pip supposed privately, it probably had.

* * *

They walked for two days, and the herald walked with them, though only Pip could see him properly. He drifted ahead through the pines and turned back every hundred steps to make sure they followed, patient as a shepherd, sorrowful as rain. Sometimes he passed his hand through a snow-laden branch and the snow fell without a sound, and once — Pip checked three times to be sure — he stepped across a stream without disturbing the water, walking on it the way you'd walk on a floor.

"Does he have a name?" Sera asked on the second morning. She'd fallen into step beside Pip, her map case knocking against her hip. Sera asked questions sideways, Pip had noticed, like she was afraid of the answers but couldn't help herself.

"He says names were given away," Pip said. "All of them. When Vaelmoor went. He says he used to be called Master of Ceremonies to the Silver Court, but the court gave its name to the bargain, so now he's just—" she screwed up her face, reaching for the shape of the feeling behind her eyes, "—just the announcing part. The rest got spent."

Sera's boots crunched to a halt. "Spent."

"That's what he said." Pip shrugged. "Like coins."

Ahead of them, Bram had stopped too, and Pip watched the two of them look at each other over her head with an entire conversation happening that nobody said out loud. Grown-ups did that constantly. It seemed exhausting.

"The book said Vaelmoor was conquered," Bram said quietly. "A century ago. Crown armies crossed the passes and the country just— ended. Off the maps. Everyone says the people died or scattered."

The herald had drifted back to stand beside Pip, and he was shaking his head, slow and terrible, the way you'd shake your head at a grave.

"He says no," Pip translated. "He says that's the story the winners needed. He says—" Her voice went smaller. "He says Vaelmoor wasn't taken. Vaelmoor *left*."

* * *

The gate stood at the head of a valley that wasn't on any map Sera owned, and Pip knew this because Sera said so eleven times, checking her charts against the peaks with hands that weren't quite steady.

It shouldn't have been possible, the valley. The mountains around Thornfell were old and mean, all scree and black stone, but this one opened ahead of them like a held breath: walls of pale granite rising smooth as cut bread, and between them a floor of white grass gone silver with frost, and at the far end, half a day's walk distant, a gate.

Not a gate like a farm gate or a city gate. A gate like a decision. Two pillars of grey stone taller than any tower Pip had ever seen, carved so densely with figures that from a distance they looked like fur, like feathers, like weather made solid. And between the pillars — nothing. Not darkness. Nothing. A shimmer like heat off summer stones, except it was winter and cold enough to crack your lips, and the shimmer hung there anyway, patient as the herald himself.

The company stood at the valley mouth and stared.

"It's beautiful," Sera whispered, and then, in the voice of someone confessing a crime: "I've mapped these ranges. There is no valley here. There is *no valley here*. I would swear it on anything you asked me to swear on."

"There isn't," Pip agreed comfortably. "That's the trick of it. He says the valley agreed to be forgotten. All of Vaelmoor did. That was the price."

Bram had gone the colour of old ash. "What price? Price for what?"

Pip closed her eyes and let the herald pour it into her, and it hurt more than knowing usually hurts, because the herald's memory was a hundred years deep and cold as well-water, and carrying it up took something out of her she'd want later. But she carried it anyway, because that was the job now. She was the door the words came through.

"A long time ago," she said, "Vaelmoor's crown started eating. Not food. It ate the magic of the country. Kings wore it and it poured them full and poured them out again and what came out wasn't them anymore. The last queen saw what was coming — saw her daughter wearing the crown and not knowing her own mother's face. So the queen made a bargain. With the country itself, with the old magic under everything. She said: take our name off the world. Take us out of the maps and the songs and the remembering. Seal the crown and all its hunger behind a door that can't be opened from outside. And the country said yes, but the price was everything — every name, every grave, every bell. The whole country paid itself away to keep one crown from ever being worn again."

Pip opened her eyes. Everyone was looking at her, and none of them were looking at her like she was a child, which was new, and she didn't decide yet whether she liked it.

"The door can only be opened from inside," she said. "That's why the herald's been waiting. Somebody had to come who could open it. And the book says—" She looked at Bram, and felt bad about what she had to say next, the way you feel bad about medicine. "The book says it's you."

The wind moved down the valley, through grass that hadn't been walked on in a hundred years, and it sounded almost like voices. Almost like bells.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of the first pillar, close enough to touch the shimmer if they reached, though nobody reached. The carvings glowed faintly when the firelight caught them — figures in procession, thousands of them, marching toward the gate from both sides, and Pip traced them with her finger and understood, with a lurch, that they weren't decoration. They were a record. Every figure was someone who had walked in and given their name to the bargain.

"This one's a baker," she said, touching a squat figure holding a round loaf. "This one's a soldier. This one's—" She stopped. The figure next to the soldier was small, child-sized, holding a wooden horse, and the herald had come to stand beside her, and his sorrow came off him like cold off ice.

"She waited," Pip said softly. "Right here. She wouldn't go in until the very end, because she thought her mother might change her mind."

Sera wrapped a blanket around Pip's shoulders without asking, and Pip let her, because some things you don't argue about.

Later, when the others slept, Pip sat awake and watched the herald pace the length of the gate, bowing to it, pleading with it in his silent way. And she noticed what she should have noticed sooner, what the wonder had been covering up like snow covers a hole in the road.

The herald wasn't only sad. Underneath the sadness, threaded through it like wire through wax, was something else. Hunger, maybe. Or hope so old and starved it had turned into hunger. He wanted the gate open the way a drowning man wants air, and he had waited a hundred years, and Pip was suddenly, coldly certain that a hundred years of wanting does not care whose hands open the door, or what happens to them after.

"He says thank you," she told Bram, who had woken and come to sit beside her without a sound. "For coming. He says tomorrow, when the frost-ferns bloom on the pillars, the gate will hear you."

"And after that?" Bram asked. His scarred hands were wrapped around his knees, and in the firelight Pip could see the storm living behind his eyes, the one he couldn't put down. "After the gate hears me?"

Pip listened. The herald had gone still, facing the shimmer, and what came through was barely a whisper, barely a memory of a whisper:

*"The crown remembers its shape. Tell the storm-caller: whatever it offers him — and it will offer him everything — the country paid everything to bury it. Ask him if he means to dig up what a queen gave her daughter's name to hide."*

Pip pulled the blanket tighter. "He says be careful," she said, which wasn't all of it, but the rest could wait for morning. Some doors, once described, start opening on their own.

Beyond the pillars, the shimmer breathed — in, and out — and somewhere on the far side of a hundred silent years, bells that no one had rung began, very faintly, to remember how.
