## 6. The Winter Closes

The snow came down like a verdict.

Bram felt it before he saw it — a pressure behind his eyes, the old ache in the scar tissue of his palms, the mountain itself holding its breath. For three days they had climbed through Thornfell's high passes with the herald-ghost flickering ahead of them like a candle in a drafty room, and for three days the weather had held. Now it didn't.

"Early," Old Wren said, squinting up at a sky the color of unpolished pewter. The old fence stood hunched against the wind, her grey braid whipping loose from its cap. "Winter's a month early if it's an hour. That's not weather. That's policy."

"The Crown," Tamsin said flatly. She had her hood back despite the cold, her desertion tattoo — a broken spear, inked crooked over her left ear — bare and defiant as ever. "They've got storm-workers on retainer now. Bought up every hedge-witch and rain-priest between here and the coast. If they want the passes shut, the passes stay shut."

"They want *me* shut in," Bram said.

"No," Wren corrected gently, tucking her braid away. "They want you *found*. Shutting you in just makes it easier."

Pip laughed from somewhere near the rear of the column, where the child was walking backwards through the drifts to taunt the ghost, who was pretending — badly, Bram thought; ghosts were poor liars even after a century of practice — not to notice. "Then they're doing it wrong! We're still here!"

"We're still here," Bram agreed, and did not say what he was thinking: that being still here might be exactly what something wanted. That he had not opened the prophecy book since the chapel, and yet he could feel it in his pack like a second heartbeat, patient as a spider.

The pass narrowed above them into a defile the smugglers called the Throat — a slot of grey stone barely wide enough for two mules, which was why everyone used mules and no one used armies. Snow was already crusting the ledges. By tomorrow it would be chest-deep. By the day after, the whole high country would be sealed until thaw, and thaw, this year, might mean never. The long winters came when the Crown wanted them, and the Crown wanted one now. He could feel it in the storms massing beyond the peaks like wolves at the edge of firelight — storms that were almost, but not quite, natural. Storms with a hand on the tiller.

Someone else's hand.

His hands ached. They always ached when other people's magic was working nearby, as if his scars remembered what they used to be for.

"We make the Throat by dark or we don't make it at all," he said. "Move."

* * *

They made the Throat by full dark, which was worse.

The waystation on the far side was a smuggler's cache carved into the rock: a low chamber with a smoke-hole, a cold hearth, and walls lined with decades of contraband hidden in niches — bolts of moon-iron cloth, jars of lantern-spirits, bundles of letters that would hang their senders. It smelled of stone and old resin and, faintly, of all the frightened men who had waited out searches there before him. Bram got a fire going small and clean while Tamsin rigged tripwires across both approaches and Wren counted and recounted her ledger with the serenity of a woman whose accounts had survived three kings.

Pip sat cross-legged nearest the ghost, who had settled into the corner by the hearth where the warmth wasn't but chose to be anyway. The herald looked better here, closer to Vaelmoor's buried bones. More solid. Its face — a young man's face, courtly and sad — had been gaining detail all week, like a painting drying in reverse.

"It says the door knows we're coming," Pip reported, in the tone of a child relaying gossip. "It says the snow is early because the book is getting excited." She paused, head cocked. Then she frowned, and the frown made her look suddenly much older than ten. "It says I shouldn't have told you that."

Bram's hands went very still over the fire.

"Tell me the rest," he said.

Pip chewed her lip. The wind screamed down the throat of the pass outside, and under the wind — so faint he might have imagined it, except that Pip heard it too, and Wren's quill had stopped scratching, and Tamsin's head had come up like a hound's — there was another sound.

A bell.

Far off, deep under the mountain, ringing once, as though a century-old morning had finally decided to finish striking the hour.

"That's it saying hello," Pip whispered. "To you specifically."

Nobody slept well after that.

* * *

Two days later, the amnesty notices found them.

Not posted — nothing so public. They came the way the Crown did everything now: quietly, thoroughly, on paper good enough to last. A supply train bound for the border forts had been turned back by the snow, its drivers billeted in the lower waystation, and among the drivers was a witch-finder's clerk too bored and too cold to guard his satchel. Wren bought the satchel off him for the price of a warm meal and a warmer lie, and spread its contents on the waystation floor by lantern light: route schedules, garrison orders, and a sheaf of printed bills, dozens of them, each bearing the same blocky type.

BY ORDER OF THE LORD PROTECTOR'S OFFICE OF INQUIRY:

AMNESTY AND REWARD are offered to any subject of the Crown delivering to lawful authority the storm-caller BRAM AMBERDALE, wanted for HERESY, SEDITION, and CRIMES AGAINST THE NATURAL ORDER. Pardon of all prior offenses is granted. Land grants available. No questions asked.

Below the type, in finer print, was the detail that made Bram's stomach turn: *the said Amberdale is to be taken alive.* Alive, and delivered — the clerk's marginal notes helpfully specified — to a rendezvous point inside the closed borders of old Vaelmoor.

"They know about the door," Sera said quietly.

Sera had been quiet for two days, since the bell. She sat apart with her maps, as she always did, but tonight her compass lay untouched beside her and her eyes kept going to the amnesty bill as though it were a map of a country she'd never charted. "They don't just suspect. They're directing traffic. Every notice, every garrison order — they're herding him. Us." She looked up, and Bram saw something he'd learned to read on glaciers and ridgelines: the expression of a woman measuring a slope and not liking the angle. "This isn't a hunt anymore. It's a delivery."

"Then we go around them," Tamsin said.

"There is no around. There's the Throat behind us and Vaelmoor ahead, and between—" Wren tapped the route schedule. "Between is the Seventh Company of witch-finders, wintering at Graywatch, which sits on the only road that isn't a glacier."

"So we take the glacier."

"So we take the glacier," Bram agreed, "and we move tonight, before the snow buries the trail—"

And that was when Pip said, in a small voice, from the doorway:

"There's a man on our tripwire."

* * *

He was alone, which was the first wrong thing. Witch-finders traveled in threes at minimum; it was doctrine. This one stood at the edge of the lantern light with his hands open and empty, snow melting off his shoulders, and his badge of office — the brass eye-and-flame — already unclipped from his coat and held out in one gloved hand like a man surrendering his teeth.

"Heard your fire," the witch-finder said. "Been following your trail since the Throat. My partners went down into the ice crevasse two days back. I've been walking since." He smiled without any humor in it at all. "Name's Corven. And I'm here to offer you the same deal the Crown offered me."

"Which is?" Bram asked, though he already knew.

"Amnesty. All of it. You especially, deserter." Corven's eyes moved to Tamsin, and stayed there. "Broken Spear Company, Third Regiment. They never struck you from the rolls. Do you know what that means? It means you were never legally a traitor. Walk away from these people, hand over the storm-caller, and tomorrow morning you're a soldier again. Back pay. Pension. Your name cleared in front of the whole army that spat on you." His voice dropped, went almost gentle. "I've seen the rolls, Sergeant Vane. I know what they took from you. I'm offering it back."

The silence in the waystation was absolute. The fire popped. Outside, the wind worried at the rocks like an animal testing a fence.

Bram did not look at Tamsin. He made himself not look at Tamsin, because looking would be asking, and asking would be begging, and he would not beg. But he heard her breathing change. He heard the particular quality of silence that comes from a person standing very still so that no one will see their hands shake.

"You can't buy her," Pip said fiercely, stepping forward, and the ghost rose behind her like a drawn bow. "You can't—"

"Pip." Tamsin's voice was steady. Steadier than Bram could have managed. "Go stand by Wren."

"I won't—"

"*Pip.*"

The child went, glaring, small fists balled, and Corven watched her go with something flickering behind his eyes that Bram recognized too late, because he'd spent years learning to read storms and only months learning to read men, and men lied better.

"Well?" Corven said softly. "Sergeant?"

Tamsin was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had gone strange and far away, the voice of someone reading aloud from her own past. "Third Regiment wintered at Harrow Ford, my last year. We were ordered to clear a village of 'unregistered sensitives.' Forty-one people. The youngest was six." She rolled her shoulders, once, like a soldier settling kit. "I broke my spear over my knee and walked out rather than do it, and I have regretted precisely nothing for eleven years, and you think you can sell me my name back? My name is the one thing they never touched."

Something in Corven's face moved. Not softening — recalculating. And then, so fast it was nearly a blur, his empty hands weren't empty anymore: a thin black wand of finder's bone coming up, aimed not at Tamsin, not at Bram—

—at Pip.

Because the Crown's clerks were thorough, and thorough men read reports, and reports said the child talked to ghosts, and ghosts were evidence, and evidence was to be taken alive.

Tamsin moved the way she must have moved at Harrow Ford, before the breaking. No hesitation, no flourish, no wasted breath. Her knife came off her belt and crossed the room in one long stride and one shorter motion, and Corven folded around it with a sound like a man sitting down hard, and the finder's bone clattered on the stone and spun to a stop against Bram's boot.

For a moment nobody breathed.

Then Tamsin knelt, quickly, businesslike, and closed the man's eyes with two fingers, and said, quietly, in the voice of someone keeping a promise: "That's for the six-year-old. Both of them." She stayed kneeling a moment longer than she needed to. When she stood, her hands were shaking after all, and she let them shake, and did not hide it, and Bram understood that this, more than the killing, was the proof of what she'd chosen.

"Winter's closing," she said hoarsely. "We should move."

Wren was already wrapping the body for the snow, murmuring the fence's last rites — *no name given, no debt recorded, account closed* — and Pip had her face pressed into Sera's coat, and Sera was holding her and watching Bram with wide, level eyes over the child's head.

And Bram picked up the finder's bone.

It was warm. That was the first horror of it — warm, as if recently held by a living hand, though Corven had carried it through days of blizzard. It hummed faintly against his scarred palms, and the hum had a shape, and the shape was words, and the words were in the same hand as the prophecy book. He knew it the way you know your own signature forged.

*Alive,* the bone whispered, in the voice of a patient spider, a patient crown. *Deliver him alive. The door opens from within. The king returns by the road of his own choosing.*

Bram dropped it as if it burned, because it did, because it had.

When he looked up, the others were staring at him, and outside the wind had stopped entirely, all along the pass, everywhere, at once — the whole sky leaning in to listen.

"He's not their prisoner," Bram said. His voice came out rough and strange. "He's their cargo. And so am I. The Crown isn't hunting me to burn me." He looked down at the bone on the floor, and past it, toward the south, toward the drowned country under the map, where a bell had rung hello. "They're escorting me home. Something in that book wrote their orders, and it wants me crowned, and it has been patient for a hundred years, and it doesn't care whether I come willing."

Sera crossed the room to him. She didn't touch him; she stood close enough that he could feel her warmth, which was its own kind of touch. "Then we get there first," she said. "And we open the door on our terms. Whatever's waiting on the other side of it learns what it feels like to be surprised."

Above them, through the smoke-hole, the first true flakes of the long winter began to fall — slow, deliberate, enormous, like pages torn from some vast and patient book.

Bram shouldered his pack with the prophecy inside it, heavier than lead now, heavier than crowns.

"To the glacier," he said. "While the trail's still ours."
