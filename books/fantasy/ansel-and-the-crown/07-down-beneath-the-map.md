## 7. Down Beneath the Map

The herald's memory was failing, and Sera could see it happening the way one watches a candle drown in its own wax.

Pip sat cross-legged on the wet stone of the mine entrance, her small hands cupped as though holding something weightless and dying. "She says the way down is the way the water used to go," the girl reported, her voice pitched strange and formal, borrowed. "She says—she says she can't remember the bells anymore. She can't remember what they *sounded* like. Only that there were bells, and that everyone in Vaelmoor knew what they meant without being taught."

"Ask her how many shafts," Old Wren said, hunched against the cold with his collar up, his fence's eyes already cataloguing the rusted winch, the rotten timber, everything here that was worthless and everything that might not be.

"She doesn't count them." Pip's brow furrowed. "She says you don't count your own ribs."

Bram said nothing. He had barely spoken since the pass sealed behind them three days ago, since the snow had come down like a verdict and Tamsin had come back from the rocks with a dead witch-finder's blood freezing on her sleeve and Pip's hand locked in hers. He stood at the mouth of the drowned mine with the prophecy book strapped to his chest like a wound, and Sera watched him watch the black water below, and she thought: *he is already saying goodbye to something. I don't know what yet, and neither does he.*

"Then we go by my trade," Sera said, and unslung her satchel.

She had not drawn a map since burning her commission on the glacier. It had felt, that night, like cutting out her own tongue. But her hands had not forgotten. They never would. She knelt at the shaft mouth, tested the air with a strip of linen — stale, mineral, but breathing — and began, by lamplight, to sketch what could not be found on any royal chart: the descent into the country beneath the country.

"The water carved this before men did," she said, marking the shaft's angle. "Which means it will lie, the way water lies. It will look like it goes straight down and then it won't. Tamsin, you're heaviest — you take the rear rope. Wren, if the timber groans, you say so *immediately*, do you understand me? Not when you've finished appraising it."

Old Wren gave her a look of profound, wounded dignity. "I have never once finished appraising anything in my life, girl. It's the only honest way to live."

They went down.

* * *

The mine-shafts were drowned in the upper reaches — black water standing in the galleries like held breath — and then, two hundred feet below the frost line, they were drowned in something else. Light. Faint, sourceless, the color of the moment just after dawn when the world is all promise and no detail. It seeped up through cracks in the rock, and where it seeped, things grew that had no business growing a mile under a mountain: pale moss with the texture of velvet, white flowers like dropped coins, a thread of water running silver and warm.

Pip walked with her hands out, touching everything. "She's getting louder down here," the girl said. "She's getting *louder* but she's getting smaller, do you understand? It's like — like a voice coming from further away but through a bigger door."

"I understand," Sera said, and was surprised to find she meant it. She had spent her life translating the world into lines and symbols, and here was a child translating a ghost, and both of them were doing the same work: making the unmappable *held*.

Bram walked in the middle of the line and did not touch anything. Once, his scarred hand brushed the wall as he steadied himself, and the pale moss flared green under his fingers and then went dark, as if startled. He snatched his hand back and looked at it for a long time.

"The book's warm," he said quietly, to no one. "It's been warm since the snow closed the pass."

Sera let him carry that alone. It was the hardest discipline of love she had ever learned: not to reach.

* * *

They came out of the last gallery at what the herald's fading memory insisted — through Pip, in a voice gone thin and reverent — was the *threshold*, and Sera, who had drawn coastlines for the Crown and mountain passes for armies and had believed herself incapable of awe, stopped so suddenly that Tamsin walked into her back.

Below them, at the bottom of a stair of living rock, a country was holding its breath.

Vaelmoor.

It lay in a valley that had no right to exist beneath the mountains, wide as a lake and lit by that same sourceless dawn. Streets of pale stone ran in patterns Sera's cartographer's eye recognized instantly as *deliberate* — not built, *composed*, the way a sentence is composed. Rooftops of green copper gone soft with a century of unmoving weather. Gardens where fountains stood frozen mid-plume, water hanging in the air like glass fruit.

And the people. Or the shapes of them. A woman at a market stall, her hand extended toward a customer who was not there, a smile fixed on her face like a portrait's. Two children mid-game in a square, a hoop rolling toward a wall it would never reach. A man on a balcony, looking up, forever looking up, at a sky that held no sun.

"Nobody move," Bram said, and his voice cracked in the middle of it.

"They're not dead," Pip whispered. Her eyes were enormous. "She says they're not dead, they're *waiting*, she says the bargain put everyone to sleep so the crown couldn't eat them, she says—" Pip stopped. Her face did something terrible and young. "She can't remember her own name. She's my ghost and she can't remember her own name, and she says when the door opens she'll get it back."

Old Wren had removed his hat. Sera had never seen him do that. Tamsin stood with her hand on her knife out of pure habit, and then, slowly, deliberately, took it off her belt and let it hang.

Sera knelt at the top of the stair and did the only thing she knew how to do with unbearable things. She began to draw.

Her pencil moved and her eyes filled and she let them, because no one down here was the Crown, no one would take the map and weaponize it, and because some things demanded to be recorded by a human hand or they had not truly been witnessed. She drew the frozen fountains. She drew the woman at the stall. She drew the light, and knew even as she did it that there was no ink on earth for that light, and wept anyway, and did not stop drawing.

"It's mid-morning," she said at last, her voice wrecked. "Do you see it? Everything is mid-morning. A hundred years ago they stopped the whole country at mid-morning, on a market day, so that it would be somewhere *safe*. Somewhere ordinary. Not at night, not at the end of something — at *mid-morning*." She looked at Bram. "Whoever made this bargain loved these people. Whatever else is true, whatever the crown down there is — someone loved them enough to freeze them at the gentlest hour there is."

Bram had gone the color of the pale stone. His scarred hands hung at his sides and around them, faintly, the air *itched* — Sera felt the hair on her arms rise, felt the pressure drop the way it did before his storms, and saw him clamp down on it, jaw tight, swallowing thunder the way he had swallowed it his whole exiled life.

"It knows I'm here," he said.

* * *

They went down the stair in single file, because the herald's memory, flickering through Pip like a lamp running dry, insisted on protocol even now: *the door is at the heart of the city, the door is under the bell tower, the door has been open for a hundred years, the door is only waiting for the one the book named.* Each sentence cost Pip something; Sera could see it, could see the ghost spending herself like a miser's last coins, and hated the prophecy for it, hated the book strapped to Bram's chest, hated a bargain made a century before any of them were born that had spent a little girl as its messenger.

The streets of Vaelmoor did not echo. That was the worst of it, Sera thought. Their boots made no sound on the pale stone, as if the country were listening too carefully to let anything be repeated. They walked through the market square between the frozen vendors and their frozen wares — bread that had not staled, apples that had not rotted, a fishmonger's stall bright with ice that was not cold — and Sera understood that they were walking through the inside of a held note, and that the note had been held so long it had forgotten it was a note, and had become a kind of silence.

The bell tower rose at the city's heart, slender and white, and beneath it, where the herald's memory said it would be, the door.

Sera had imagined iron. She had imagined the Crown's work — bolts, wards, chains, a lock the size of a cathedral. Instead it was a simple archway of pale stone at the tower's base, standing free, framing nothing but the tower wall behind it. And through the arch she could see the wall — and through the wall, impossibly, the tower's interior, and beyond that, faint as a memory of a memory, something *moving*. Air. Weather. The suggestion of wind.

The door was open. It had always been open. It was a door the way a held breath is a breath.

Bram walked toward it and the air around him began to sing — a low, subterranean hum, the sound Sera had learned to hear in the last weeks the way you learn to hear a lover's footsteps, and the frozen city answered it. In the market square behind them, the woman at the stall turned her head. One degree. The children's hoop rolled an inch and stopped. The man on the balcony, still looking up, *blinked*.

"They're waking up," Pip said. Tears ran freely down her face and she did not wipe them. "She says they're waking up because he's close. She says the door doesn't open, the door *recognizes*."

Bram stopped before the arch. The prophecy book was hot enough now that Sera could smell the leather scorching. His scarred hands rose toward it, and she saw — everyone saw — that they were shaking, and that under the shaking they were *reaching*, the way a man starving reaches for bread, and that he hated himself for it.

"Sera," he said, without turning. His voice was very quiet under the hum of the waking city. "If I go through there, and I come out the other side of this wearing a crown — you'll stop me. Promise me you'll stop me. Whatever it costs."

Sera looked at the open door, and at the man she had chosen over a kingdom, and felt the whole unbearable weight of the morning — the frozen bread, the sleeping children, the ghost with no name — settle onto her shoulders like a mantle she had never asked to wear.

Behind them, far above, through a mile of drowned rock and sealed snow, came a sound none of them could possibly have heard and all of them heard anyway: the crack of witch-finders' boots on ice.

The city heard it too. All at once, everywhere, the frozen people turned their heads — a hundred faces, mid-morning forever, now looking *toward the door*.

Waiting to see what Bram Amberdale would do.
