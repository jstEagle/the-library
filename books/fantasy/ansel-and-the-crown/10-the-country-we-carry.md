## 10. The Country We Carry

The valley came apart the way a held breath comes apart — not all at once, but in long, shuddering releases, like something enormous remembering how to sigh.

Sera walked with her arm through Bram's, though he didn't need the support. That was the strange thing, the thing she kept turning over as they climbed the switchbacks out of Vaelmoor: he didn't need it. A week ago he had been a storm-caller who couldn't cross a frozen lake without the ice groaning under the weight of what he carried. Now his hands were just hands, scarred and ordinary, and when the mountain rumbled beneath them he flinched like any other man.

"Do you miss it?" she asked. She hadn't meant to say it aloud.

Bram considered the question with the seriousness he gave everything, even now, even exhausted, dusted white with the ash of a city that had finally been allowed to end. "It's like asking if I miss a tooth that ached for twenty years," he said. "I keep reaching for it. I keep expecting the hurt." He flexed his fingers, watching them as if they belonged to a stranger. "But no. I don't miss it. I miss knowing what I was."

"You're still that," Pip said from ahead of them, without turning around. The girl walked the ruined road like she'd been born on it, arms out for balance along the fallen wall, the herald's memory flickering around her shoulders in colors only she could see. "You're just less loud about it."

Behind them, the silent city was exhaling. They could hear it even here, three hundred feet up the pass — bells that had hung motionless for a century swinging at last, market awnings snapping loose, fountains unsealing themselves in bright ropes of water. And under all of it, threading through the stone like light through cloth, the magic of Vaelmoor going out into the world. Sera felt it pass through her twice on the climb: once as warmth, once as a pressure behind her eyes that made every contour of the mountains leap into sudden, unbearable clarity, as if the land itself were showing her its true map before letting her go.

Old Wren stopped at the last bend and looked back. The old fence rarely looked back at anything; Sera had watched him walk away from burning warehouses with the same mild expression he gave bad exchange rates. But he stood there a long moment, hat in his hands, watching the towers of the buried capital dissolve into gold dust that rose instead of fell.

"Never thought I'd see a country die happy," he said.

"It's not dying," Pip said. "It's planting."

Tamsin said nothing at all. She had a witch-finder's blood on her sleeve that wouldn't wash out, and she'd been quiet since the vault, since the moment the crown shattered and the witch-finders behind them simply — stopped, their instruments dead in their hands, their warrants suddenly referring to a country that no longer existed to be threatened by. She walked at the rear of the company the way she always did, sword loose, watching the passes. But when the last tower fell, Sera saw her press two fingers to her lips and then to the air, an old soldier's farewell to the dead, and Sera understood that Tamsin was saying goodbye to more than a city. She was saying goodbye to the woman who had taken the Crown's coin, and deciding, publicly, irrevocably, not to be her anymore.

* * *

They camped that night above the snowline, in the lee of a shelf of granite, because Bram insisted — some smuggler's habit too deep to break — on putting a full day between themselves and anything that might follow. Nothing followed. The witch-finders' instruments were junk now; half the warrants in the realm referred to prophecies whose pages had gone blank.

That was the other thing Sera kept turning over. The book. It had come out of the vault in Bram's pack, water-stained and heavy, and somewhere on the second day of the climb it had begun to lighten. By the time they made camp it weighed nothing at all. Bram set it on the fire-flat between them and opened it, and the pages crumbled like autumn leaves, one after another, blank, blank, blank, until there was nothing left but a smell of old smoke and a cover with no title, which Wren fed to the flames without ceremony.

"No more dates that shouldn't exist," Sera said softly.

"No more names in other people's handwriting," Bram agreed. He stared into the fire. "I keep waiting to feel empty."

Pip, curled against Tamsin's side with the ghost's last fading warmth still in her hair, mumbled half-asleep: "You're not empty. You're just not full of somebody else's storm anymore."

From the mouth of a child who talked to the dead, it had the weight of scripture. Bram laughed — really laughed, the first time Sera had heard it since the chapel — and lay back on the cold stone with his hands behind his head, and slept without calling anything down out of the sky.

Sera stayed awake longest. She took out her kit, out of habit, and unrolled the maps she'd drawn on the way in: Thornfell, the passes, the drowned mine-shafts, the sealed gate. Every line she'd inked of Vaelmoor itself had faded to a faint gold ghost, and by morning those would be gone too. There would be no map of the country they'd freed. For a cartographer, it should have been grief.

Instead she found herself thinking of blank space. Of all the margins on all the royal maps where she had once written *here be nothing*, *uninhabited*, *of no account*. The world was full of places the Crown had labeled empty because it hadn't bothered to look. Somewhere out there, seed-magic from a hundred years of held breath was settling into valleys and rivers and root-systems, and children would be born next spring who could do small impossible things, and no book would have predicted a word of it.

She burned her commission papers last, feeding them to the fire page by page, watching the royal seal curl and blacken. She'd already burned them once, on the glacier. These were the copies she'd kept hidden in her boot lining, because some part of her had never quite believed her own courage. She believed it now.

* * *

Winter did what winter does. They went down out of the high country ahead of the deep snows and wintered in a river town whose name Sera never learned to say properly, in a rented loft above a cooper's shop, five people and a stove and a roof that leaked in exactly one place, over Pip's bed, which Pip declared was on purpose so she could hear the ghosts in the rain. Wren set up as a fence again within a week and was respectable within a month, which everyone agreed was the most alarming magic any of them had witnessed all year. Tamsin took work as a caravan guard and came home each evening smelling of horses and woodsmoke, and each evening stood a moment in the doorway as if checking that the room — the family — was still real before she entered it.

Bram chopped wood. It was, he said, the first honest labor of his life, and he was terrible at it, and he loved it. His hands blistered and healed and blistered again, and slowly the old scars faded among new ones, and no storm ever answered him, and he woke some mornings shaking anyway, reaching for the ache of the missing tooth. On those mornings Sera put tea in his hands and didn't ask, and by midmorning he was himself again — or rather, himself, the version no prophecy had gotten around to writing down.

And in the spring, when the river ice broke up with a sound like cannon fire and the roads opened, they stood at the edge of town where the new road met the old one, arguing about where home would be.

"South," Tamsin said. "Coast. Warm. I've earned warm."

"West," said Wren. "There's a mining town past the ridge paying scandalous prices for someone with my particular talents, and I use the word *talents* loosely."

"The coast has ghosts," Pip reported gravely. "Good ones. They want to see the sea. I promised."

They all looked at Bram. He looked uncomfortable, then amused, then — Sera watched it happen — quietly certain.

"I've spent my whole life crossing borders," he said. "Running from one country into another, always someone's exile, always someone's smuggler. And now—" he turned, and gestured down the road, which ran open and unmarked through green country with no gate, no pass, no seal, no warrant waiting at the end of it, "—now there isn't a border anywhere that means what it used to. Vaelmoor's magic went everywhere. Into everything. There are kids two towns over making little lights in their cupped hands, and nobody's sent witch-finders, because the witch-finders' instruments all read *nothing* now, forever." He laughed softly. "We didn't free a country. We freed the whole idea of countries."

"So where's home?" Pip demanded, practical to the bone.

Bram looked at Sera. She realized, with a warmth that had nothing to do with the spring sun, that he wasn't asking her to decide. He was telling her that wherever she pointed, that was the answer.

She unrolled the last map she would ever draw — a fresh sheet, nearly empty, with the river town inked small near the bottom edge and everything north of it left deliberately, lovingly blank. She thought of margins. Of unlabeled spaces. Of a valley that had chosen to stop existing rather than wear a corrupt crown, and a man who had chosen to shatter a kingdom rather than wear one, and a child who carried the dead gently, and a deserter, and a fence, and a cartographer who had burned her orders twice to be sure.

"North," she said. "Past the ridge, past the old gate, into the high country nobody's mapped. We'll be the first names on the map. Ours, not the Crown's."

"First settlers of nowhere," Tamsin said, grinning despite herself.

"Best kind of somewhere," said Wren.

Pip was already walking. The road went north through the greening hills, wide enough for all of them, marked by nothing but their own footprints — and somewhere far off, in a field coming up wildflowers where no wildflowers had been planted, Sera could almost swear she heard bells, ringing freely, for no one's coronation at all.
