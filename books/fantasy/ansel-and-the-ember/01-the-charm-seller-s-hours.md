## 1. The Charm-Seller's Hours

The bell above the door of Sterling & Sons Charms and Curiosities had not rung all morning, and Rafael Galloway was grateful for it.

He had learned to measure his days in the silence between customers. Silence meant safety. Silence meant that no one in the gray city of Vell had decided, on a whim or a rumor, to come looking at the small man behind the counter with the ink-stained fingers he claimed were a stain of trade. Five years of silence had taught him to love it the way other men loved applause.

He was polishing a luck-bracelet when the light through the front window changed — thickened, went the color of weak tea. Rain coming off the harbor again. Vell's autumn did nothing halfway; it drowned you slowly, politely, one gray afternoon at a time. Rafael set down the bracelet, wiped his hands on his apron out of habit, and looked at them anyway: long-fingered, precise, the nails trimmed close, the knuckles mapped with faint scars from compass-points and drafting knives. A shopkeeper's hands, he told people. A tinker's hands. Hands that strung beads and soldered tin.

They were lying hands. They had drawn the Ember Realm entire — every glass forest, every singing stone road, every river that ran warm as breath — and they had drawn, once, in a tower lit by candles made of tallow and fear, the diagram that bound a dying king into a crown of iron and appetite. He had told himself for five years that he hadn't known what it was for. Some mornings he even believed it.

The rain arrived. It came down the street in sheets, drumming on the awning, turning the cobbles of Cooper's Row into a river of dull silver. Across the way, the fishmonger hauled in her awnings with the practiced violence of a woman who had stopped expecting anything from weather or gods. Somewhere up the lane a watchman called the hour — the third hour past noon, the dead hours, when honest folk ate their bread and dishonest ones slept.

Rafael lit the lamp over the counter because the shop went dim so fast this time of year, and the dimness made him think of things he preferred not to think about. The lamp was a charm itself, technically — a wick soaked in a tincture he'd brewed from crushed glowmoss and river salt, harmless, legal, the kind of magic that had been decreed too small to bother outlawing. That was the whole architecture of his survival, distilled: be too small to bother outlawing.

He turned the bracelet over once more and hung it on the display tree by the door, between the traveler's knots (which did nothing but look pretty) and the mothering-stones (which kept tea warm for an extra quarter hour, if you believed the label, which he had written himself). Every item in the shop was exactly as magical as a good dinner and a full night's sleep. He sold comfort shaped like enchantment, and the people of Vell bought it because they needed to believe something in this city still worked the old way, even if only a little, even if only in their imaginations.

It was, he sometimes thought while counting coins at night, the most honest fraud he could have chosen.

* * *

The customer came in at half past four, shaking rain from a wool cloak: a broad woman with flour to her elbows, Mrs. Tam of the bakery two streets over, wanting a charm for her son who was going to sea.

"Something for luck," she said, breathing on her hands against the cold. "And don't tell me it's just string, Galloway. I know it's just string. I want the *saying* of it."

Rafael smiled the shopkeeper's smile — he had practiced it in a cracked mirror until it sat naturally on his face, warm and slightly apologetic, a smile that promised nothing and forgave everything. "Then you'll have the saying," he said, and took down a knot-worked cord dyed harbor-blue. "You tie it on his wrist yourself, before he boards. You say: *the water remembers your name.* And then you don't say anything else, and neither does he, and he goes up the gangway without looking back."

"That's superstition."

"It's the best kind. The kind that costs six coppers." He wrapped the cord in brown paper. "The water remembering your name is just a way of saying someone on shore is thinking of you. Sailors drown less when they believe that. I've read it somewhere."

Mrs. Tam paid him and, being Mrs. Tam, stayed to talk, which was the true price of shopping at Sterling & Sons. She talked about her son, about the price of flour, about the inquisitors who had been seen at the Market Gate that morning — three of them, gray-cloaked, asking questions.

Rafael's hands, wrapping the parcel, did not pause. He had trained them well. But something behind his ribs went very quiet and very still, the way a field goes still before lightning.

"Inquisitors?" he said mildly. "In Vell? What would they want here? We've no magic worth confiscating."

"That's what I said to Bess Corrow. But they were going stall to stall. Asking after travelers. Asking—" Mrs. Tam lowered her voice, though the shop stood empty but for the two of them and the rain. "Asking after children, would you believe. A child, they said. One that talks strange."

"Talks strange how?"

"Oh, I don't know. Foreign, maybe. Or simple." She took her parcel. "You hear everything on this street, Galloway. If you hear anything, you'll tell the watch, won't you? It's the law now."

"The law," Rafael agreed, "is the law."

She left. The bell rang. The rain swallowed her footsteps, and Rafael stood in the lamplight with his heart going like a trapped bird and his face still wearing the shopkeeper's smile, because the muscles had not yet been informed.

A child that talks strange.

There were a hundred thousand children in Vell and its sprawl. Half of them talked strange; the other half didn't talk at all, having learned early what talking cost. There was no reason — no reason at all — for the cold spreading through him like water through a hull. He was a charm-seller. He was small. He had spent five years sanding himself down to a shape no one would trouble to look at twice.

He blew out the lamp, locked the till, and let himself remember, just for a moment, in the safety of the dark, what he had been.

Rafael Galloway, Royal Cartographer to the Court of Embers. Keeper of the Great Survey. The man whose maps had opened a kingdom to a conqueror — not with armies, worse than armies, with *lines*. Roads and passes and the hidden ways beneath the singing stones. He had drawn the realm so beautifully that Malvane's engineers had wept over the vellum, and then he had drawn the binding circle, because a king on the edge of death had asked him to record the rite for posterity, and posterity had turned out to be a parasite wearing iron.

He had run. That was the sum of his nobility. When the Ember Realm burned, he had run west with a satchel of maps and burned them one by one along the road, feeding five years of his life's work to cookfires, until he reached Vell, where magic was illegal and therefore invisible, and had bought a shop with money he pretended he'd inherited.

Sterling & Sons. He wasn't a Sterling. He had no sons. The sign was a joke he'd played on himself, a tombstone with a pun on it.

* * *

That night the dream came, as it came most nights, and as always it began with the smell of hot wax.

In the dream he was in the map room of the Ember court, except the room was wrong — vast, cathedral-high, the shelves rising into darkness where no shelves had been — and the great table before him held a single map, unrolled, weighted at the corners with candlesticks. It was a map of the Ember Realm, but drawn in a hand he almost recognized. His own, but younger. His own, but truer.

He bent over it. The rivers ran. Not shimmered — *ran*, slow and warm, and where the glass forests stood the vellum caught the lamplight and threw it back in colors that had no names in common speech. In the mountains, tiny and perfect, the stones were singing. He could hear them if he put his ear close enough. They sang in the pre-kingdom tongue, the old speech, the language of before crowns, and he understood it in the dream the way he never had awake, and they were singing a warning.

*The map is burning,* the stones sang. *Look. Look. The map is burning.*

And it was. Fire walked in from the western edge, patient as tide, consuming the roads first — the roads he had drawn, the roads that had led the usurper's legions in — and the fire did not blacken the vellum but erased it, left clean blank parchment behind, and Rafael scrabbled at the table trying to save something, a city, a river, a single mountain range, and under his palms the ink came away on his fingers, staining them, and he thought with dream-logic clarity: *this is why my hands are stained, this is the stain, it never comes out, it has never once come out—*

He woke in the narrow bed above the shop with his heart slamming and his hands curled into fists against his chest, and lay listening to the rain and the groan of the building and the distant clang of the harbor bell counting the hour.

Three o'clock. The dead hours.

He got up, because sleep was finished with him, and went down the dark stairs to the shop, and lit the lamp, and sat at the workbench where he strung his harmless charms. From the bottom drawer, beneath the false bottom he'd built himself, he took out the thing he allowed himself to keep: a scrap of vellum no bigger than his palm, a fragment rescued from the burning of the rest. On it, in his own young hand, the corner of the Ember Realm — a stretch of coastline, a few words of annotation in the old speech.

He could no longer read the old speech. That was the cruelty of it. He had transcribed it phonetically, a surveyor's diligence, recording sounds he didn't understand, and the man who might have explained it to him was ash, and the kingdom was a province, and the king was a prisoner inside his own body wearing a crown that ate his memories one by one like a moth eating wool.

Rafael knew that last part the way you know a debt. He had helped build the cage. He had measured the bars.

"I stayed small," he said aloud to the empty shop, to the rain, to the scrap of vellum. His voice sounded foolish in the dark. "I stayed small, and it worked, and no one came."

The lamp hissed. Outside, Cooper's Row lay drowned and silent, and far off, at the edge of hearing, the Market Gate clock struck the quarter hour.

No one had come for five years. He had made himself a man of six-copper charms and practiced smiles, a footnote to his own life, and the war had rolled on eastward without him, and the king had gone on forgetting, and Rafael Galloway had slept badly and woken tired and called it survival, and some mornings he could almost bear the arithmetic of it.

He put the vellum away. He banked the lamp. He climbed back up the stairs, and paused on the landing, because — he told himself it was the wind, the storm rattling loose shutters up the lane — but there had been a sound. Faint. Under the rain.

Like a bell struck once, very far away, humming on the edge of hearing.

He stood in the dark with his hand on the banister and listened until the sound faded, or stopped, or had perhaps never been there at all, and told himself, in the voice he used for customers, that it was nothing. That he was safe. That he was small.

Down the street, at the mouth of Cooper's Row, something small and mud-streaked stood at the edge of the lamplight, looking at the dark windows of Sterling & Sons Charms and Curiosities, and did not move.
