## 2. A Knock in the Old Tongue

The knock came at the hour when Vell's lamps burned low and honest, when even the drunkards had gone home and the only sound on Cobbler's Row was the drip of rain from the eaves into the gutters. Rafael Galloway was counting his till by candlelight—eleven coppers, four silvers, and a button someone had once paid him for a luck-charm that did nothing, which was, he liked to think, the most honest coin in the drawer—when the sound reached him.

Three knocks. Then a pause. Then three more, soft as a child's fist could make them, and then a third set, slower, deliberate, spaced like footsteps.

He sat very still.

It was not the knocking itself that stopped his breath. Children knocked on charm-sellers' doors; they came for love-knots and fever-wards and little glass beads that were supposed to keep nightmares out, and Rafael sold them all with a smile and a blessing and no magic in any of it. It was the rhythm. Three-and-three-and-three, patient, unhurried, the cadence of someone who knew the door would open because doors always opened for those who asked properly.

He had heard that rhythm exactly once before, five years ago, spoken aloud by a dying woman in a language that predated kingdoms, in a room full of burning maps.

*Ember-speech,* the old scholars had called it. The tongue of the realm before it had a crown to bind it, the language the stones themselves were said to dream in. Rafael had spent two years of his life learning it, tracing its grammar across vellum until his hand knew its shapes better than his own name. It was the language of the maps he'd made—the true ones, the ones that had cost him everything—and he had not heard it spoken since the night he fled the Ember Realm with ink still wet on the worst thing he would ever draw.

Nobody spoke Ember-speech anymore. That was rather the point of it. The usurper's first edict had been silence: burn the old books, close the old mouths, let the language die so the past would die with it.

The knock came again. Three-and-three-and-three.

Rafael rose from his stool, took up the iron poker from the cold hearth—it was theater, mostly; his hands remembered other weapons—and crossed the small shop with its shelves of harmless wonders. Through the diamond panes of the door, warped by old glass, he could see a shape standing in the rain. Small. Slight. A child, wrapped in something that might once have been a coat.

He opened the door on the chain, the way a careful man does.

"Shop's closed," he said. "Come back tomorrow if you want a charm. Fever-ward's a copper."

The child looked up at him.

Mud streaked its face in long drying lines, like tears that had turned to earth. Its hair was dark and plastered flat by rain, and its eyes—its eyes were the color of banked coals, a deep amber that seemed to hold light the way embers hold heat long after the fire has gone. Not a strange color, precisely. He had seen amber eyes before. But not like this. Not eyes that looked *through* him, past the chain and the poker and the shopkeeper's smile, as though reading a map drawn on the inside of his skin.

And then the child spoke.

"*Sera-veth an kael. Sera-veth an tor.*"

The words fell into the dark doorway like stones into still water, and every ripple they made was five years deep.

*The road is closed. The road is open.*

It was a greeting. An old one, older than Vell, older than the kingdom the usurper wore like a stolen coat—a traveler's greeting, said to a doorkeeper, meaning: *I come from far away, and I ask leave to come farther.* Rafael had transcribed it a hundred times. He had written it in the margins of maps, in the headers of charts, in letters to colleagues who were now dead or scattered or worse. He had never expected to hear it again in a living voice, least of all a voice this small, pitched high and cracked with exhaustion, shaped by a mouth too young to know what treasures it carried.

His hand went slack on the poker.

"You—" His own voice came out wrong, rusty. He tried again in common tongue. "You can't be here. Where are your parents? Who taught you—"

"*Kael-tor,*" the child said, and held something up.

The shard.

Later, Rafael would try to describe it and fail, the way one fails to describe a bell's tone after the ringing stops. It was perhaps the length of a finger, irregular, faceted like flint but darker, with veins running through it that caught the lamplight and moved—actually *moved*, slow as blood through a wound. It hummed. Not a sound he heard so much as felt, low in his sternum, in his back teeth, in the old scar along his palm: a sustained note like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing, a note he recognized down to his bones because he had heard it once before, muffled through stone walls, on the worst night of his life.

It was a piece of the crown. The true crown. The one the usurper had bound to the dying king, the one Rafael's own maps had helped cage.

Every instinct he had spent five years sharpening screamed at him to shut the door. To bar it, blow out the candle, climb out the back window into the alley, and be gone from Vell by morning under a new name with new hands. He had survived this long by staying small, by selling nothing, by knowing nothing, by being a gray and forgettable man in a gray and forgettable city. The child at his door was a lit beacon in a country of hunters. Everything about this moment was the end of the life he had built.

But the child swayed on its feet. And its knees buckled.

Rafael caught it—one arm under the small shoulders, the poker clattering to the floorboards—and the moment his skin touched the child's, the humming in the shard climbed a register, sharp and clear, and for half a heartbeat he saw—

Glass forests. Trees of green and violet glass bending in a wind that sang. A road of white stone unspooling toward mountains the color of dawn. And over it all, a pressure, vast and hungry, like a hand closing around a candle flame—

Then it was gone. The child sagged against him, breathing fast and shallow, and the shard lay in the crook of its elbow, still humming its one long note.

Rafael stood in his own doorway holding a stranger's child, and understood, with the terrible clarity that comes only when it is already too late, that his life as Rafael the charm-seller had just ended.

* * *

He laid the child on the cot behind the shop, beneath the shelf where the fever-wards hung in their neat rows. The boy—he was a boy, seven or eight years old perhaps, though it was hard to say; hunger and road-dirt made children ageless—did not wake when Rafael peeled off the ruined coat or wiped the mud from his face with a warm cloth. He slept the way soldiers sleep and fugitives sleep: lightly, with his fists curled, ready to run even in dreams.

The shard Rafael did not touch. He had wrapped it in wool without letting his bare skin meet it again, and set it on the table across the room, where it sat like a sleeping animal, humming its low note into the quiet. He kept glancing at it. He couldn't help himself. It was like keeping a wolf in the house and pretending it was a dog.

He made tea he didn't drink. He checked the door chain twice, the back window latch three times. And all the while his mind ran in circles around the same impossible arithmetic:

No one alive spoke Ember-speech. The last speakers had been purged within a year of the usurper's coronation—scholars, priests, midwives who sang the old lullabies, all of them gone into gray cloaks and gray wagons and unmarked graves. Rafael himself had burned his notes, buried his instruments, and trained his mouth never to shape those syllables even in dreams. So how did a mud-streaked child of eight come knocking at his specific door, in a city of forty thousand, speaking a dead tongue, carrying a fragment of the crown that had ruined his life?

Unless the child hadn't found him at all.

Unless the child had been *sent*. Or *drawn*. Or unless the shard itself had chosen its bearer's road, and roads, in the old craft, always ended where they needed to.

Rafael looked at his hands. Ink-stained, even now, even after five years of scrubbing; the stains had sunk into the whorls of his fingerprints and would not come out, as though the ink had decided his body was simply another page. Cartographer's hands. Traitor's hands. Hands that had drawn the binding-lines the usurper's mages had used to cage a king inside his own failing body.

"If you've come for me," he said quietly, to the sleeping boy, to the humming shard, to whatever power had arranged this, "you should know I'm not worth finding."

The boy stirred. His eyes opened—not all the way, just a slit of ember-amber in the dark—and he looked at Rafael with an expression that was not fear, exactly. It was the look of someone checking whether a thing was real. Whether it would still be there in the morning.

"*Sera-veth?*" the boy whispered. *The road?*

"I don't know," Rafael answered, and hated how gently his voice came out. "I don't know yet."

The boy's eyes closed again. But before sleep took him under, his mud-cracked lips moved one more time, shaping words so soft Rafael had to lean close to catch them, and when he did, the floor seemed to tilt beneath him:

"*Ansel. Kael-tor Ansel.*"

A name. The child had given him a name, in the old grammar, the way travelers once gave their names to doorkeepers to show good faith. *Ansel, of the open road.*

Rafael sat back on his stool and pressed both palms against his face and laughed, once, a dry broken sound with no humor in it anywhere. Somewhere out in the rain-washed dark, the inquisitors of King Malvane were surely already asking their questions—*a child of the old speech, have you seen it, a child who speaks what should be dead*—and here he sat, harboring exactly that, in a shop above a cellar full of maps he'd sworn he'd destroyed.

He looked at the shard on the table. It hummed. Patient. Certain. Like a struck bell that knew, somehow, that the second strike was coming.

Outside, far off across the rooftops of Vell, a church bell tolled the third hour, and beneath its bronze voice—if you listened the way Rafael had learned to listen, the way he had spent five years trying desperately to *unlearn*—he could almost swear the shard answered it, note for note, in a language of pure tone that predated bells, predated churches, predated every kingdom that had ever risen and fallen over the singing bones of the world.

Tomorrow, he thought. Tomorrow he would decide whether to run, and which way, and how far, and whether eight years of a child's life was worth more than the fragile peace he'd bought with five years of his own.

The candle guttered. The rain went on falling. And on the cot behind the counter, Ansel of the open road slept on, fists curled, dreaming—in whatever tongue dreams take—of roads that were still open, and a door that had opened when he knocked it properly, three-and-three-and-three.

Rafael did not sleep at all.
