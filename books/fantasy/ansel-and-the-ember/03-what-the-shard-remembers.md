## 3. What the Shard Remembers

The world came to Ansel in pieces.

That was how he understood it, anyway, though *understanding* was a word that sat far away from him, on a high shelf he could not reach. Words in the mouth-tongue—the tongue of the gray city, the tongue of the tall folk who moved too fast and spoke too much—slipped off him like rain off oiled cloth. But the other tongue. The old one. The one that lived in his chest like a second heartbeat, that rose out of him without permission, that made the charm-seller's face go white as bone—that tongue was not made of words at all. It was made of pieces. And pieces were all Ansel had ever had.

He knew warmth before he knew mothers. He knew hunger before he knew names. Somewhere behind him—behind a wall in his mind, smooth and doorless—there had been a fire, and voices singing in the old speech, and hands. Big hands. Whose hands? The wall would not say. The wall never said anything. It only stood there, warm on one side, cold on the other, and Ansel had learned not to press his ear against it because what hummed through it was too big for his small body to hold.

So: pieces.

A piece was the smell of woodsmoke that meant *safe* even when nothing was safe. A piece was the way certain stones felt under his bare feet—*listening*, they felt, like the ground was an ear pressed flat against the sky. A piece was the shard itself, which he had carried so long he could not remember carrying anything else, tucked against his chest inside his shirt where it lay warm as a sleeping bird.

It was warm now. Warmer than usual. It had been warm ever since the door opened and the ink-fingered man looked at him with his face coming apart.

* * *

Ansel sat cross-legged on the floor of the back room while the man—Rafael, Rafael, the name rang against the inside of his skull like a coin dropped in a dry well—moved around him doing small nervous things. Building up the fire. Setting out bread and honey without being asked, which was good, because Ansel's stomach had been shouting at him since morning in the wordless language of the belly, which was also an old tongue, maybe the oldest one. Cutting a wedge of cheese and then stopping, staring at his own hands as if they belonged to someone else.

His hands did not have ink on them. Ansel noticed this the way he noticed most things: sideways, without deciding to. The man kept looking at his own fingers like they had betrayed him. There was stain in the creases, dark blue-black, worked deep, the kind of stain that does not wash out because it has been there for years. Ink-hands. The shard stirred against Ansel's chest when it saw them. Not stirred—*recognized*. The difference was a shiver.

*Ink-hands,* said something deep in the shard's humming, in no language at all, or in the language underneath all languages. *We know those hands.*

Ansel pulled the shard out.

He did not decide to. That was the thing about the old speech, about the pieces—it all happened to him more than he made it happen. His fingers simply went to his collar and drew the thing out into the firelight, and there it was: a splinter of crown, no longer than his thumb, jagged at one end and smooth at the other, gold that was not quite gold, shot through with veins of red like ember-light caught in ice. It should have been beautiful. Mostly it was loud. Other people could not hear it, but to Ansel it had always been singing—a thin, endless note, like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass, like something holding one long scream very politely inside itself.

Rafael went still. Completely still, the way small animals go still, the way the whole forest goes still when something with teeth walks through it.

"Don't," the man said. His voice cracked in the middle of the word. "Child, don't—"

But the shard was already singing louder.

* * *

Here is what Ansel saw. Here is what the shard gave him, the way you give a beggar coins one at a time, pressed into his palm so fast he cannot count them:

*A hall of black stone, pillars going up and up until they became darkness, and every pillar carved with words in the old speech, and the words were singing, low and vast, a song with the weight of mountains. Firelight everywhere but no fires. Light living in the walls themselves, breathing.*

*A king standing in the middle of it. Tall. Gray at the temples. Wearing a crown that was not this crown—or rather, wearing the crown this shard had once been part of, whole and unbroken, and the crown fit him the way a name fits, the way a hand fits a hand. He was laughing at something. Ansel could not hear the laugh but he could feel its shape, warm and round, and he understood suddenly, in the way of dreams, that this was what a king was supposed to be: not power sitting on a head, but a person carrying a country the way you carry a sleeping child, carefully, all night, arms aching, unwilling to wake it.*

*Then the crown beginning to whisper.*

*Ansel felt that too—the first wrongness, the first cold thread drawn through the warm hall. The king's smile faltering. The king pressing two fingers to his temple as if a bee had gotten inside his skull. And the crown—not the crown anymore, something else wearing the shape of a crown—leaning close, leaning *in*, drinking. Drinking memories the way a tick drinks blood. Ansel watched the king forget the name of his own dog. Watched him stand in the garden where his daughter had been born and feel nothing, nothing, a field burned back to bare soil. Watched him try to shout and find that his mouth would not obey, because the crown had learned to speak through it, and what the crown said through the king's mouth was smooth and reasonable and full of orders.*

*And then—*

*Hands. Ink-stained hands, young hands, trembling, holding a quill over a map spread across a table, a map of roads and rivers and a palace drawn in sections, and the hands were copying out a diagram, a pattern of lines and binding-words, and the face above the hands was young too and afraid and telling itself a lie—I am only drawing, I am only drawing what they asked me to draw—and the ink going down onto the vellum like blood going into water, spreading, final, and somewhere far away a king screaming without a mouth—*

The vision tore.

Ansel came back to himself on his hands and knees on the floorboards, shaking, the shard clenched in his fist so hard the jagged edge had cut his palm, though he hadn't felt it happen. Blood ran between his fingers. The fire had burned lower. Rafael was crouched in front of him—not touching him, holding himself back by visible force, his ink-stained hands hovering in the air like birds afraid to land.

"Ansel." His voice was wrecked. "Ansel, look at me. What did it show you?"

Ansel looked at him. And here was the terrible thing, the thing that made his chest crack open like river ice in spring: he recognized the hands. The hands hovering in front of him, stained blue-black to the knuckle, were the hands from the vision. Older now. Stained deeper. But the same.

*You,* he tried to say. *You were there. You drew the lines. You drew the lines that made the cage.*

What came out instead, because the old speech did not ask permission, was a flood of it—low sounds, singing sounds, syllables that bent the firelight and made the shadows lean in to listen. *The hall. The king. The crown that drinks. The map. Your hands. Your hands. Your hands.*

Rafael's face did something complicated and awful. He understood—he understood every word, that was plain, that was written across him in letters darker than ink—and understanding did not make him free him; it made him flinch as if each word were a stone thrown by a small, accurate hand.

"I know," he whispered. "I know what I did."

* * *

Later—how much later Ansel could not say; time in the gray city moved strangely, in fits and starts, like a cart with a bad wheel—they sat on either side of the fire and ate the bread and honey, and Rafael talked, quietly, half to himself, in the mouth-tongue that Ansel mostly couldn't follow, and Ansel let the words wash past him and kept only their shapes. Sad shapes. Guilty shapes. The shape of a man confessing to a child because a child was the only priest available.

Ansel didn't care about the confession. Confessions were mouth-tongue things. What Ansel cared about was this: the shard had gone quiet. For the first time since he'd found it—since it had found him, which was maybe truer—it lay against his chest warm and almost calm, its long held-in scream softened to something like a lullaby. As if showing him the visions had let something out of it. As if recognition was a kind of medicine.

And Ansel understood something else, in his piece-wise, sideways way. He understood why the shard had brought him here. Across the gray city, through the mud and the cold, past all the doors that had stayed shut, to this one door, this ink-handed man. The shard had not been looking for safety. The shard had been looking for *him*. The hands from the vision. The man who drew the lines—who alone, maybe, in all the wide broken world, knew where the lines had been drawn and how they might be drawn backward.

Loneliness was a country Ansel had been born in and never left. He had walked its roads his whole life without meeting another traveler. But tonight, sitting across a small fire from a stranger who flinched at his own hands, Ansel felt the borders of that country shift. Not fall. Just shift. Enough that somewhere far off, faint as a lamp in a window, there was another light burning in the same dark.

He reached across the fire and touched Rafael's ink-stained fingers, once, lightly, the way you touch something you're afraid will vanish.

Rafael made a sound like a man swallowing a stone.

Outside, far off across the rooftops of Vell, a bell began to ring. Not the hour-bell. A different bell—faster, harsher, the kind of bell that means *look out your window*. Rafael's head came up. In the street below, torchlight moved in patterns torchlight shouldn't move in, orderly and searching, and beneath the bell and the torches, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of many boots walking in step.

Rafael snuffed the candle with his fingers and stood in the dark, listening, and Ansel pressed the shard against his heart and felt it begin, very softly, to sing again.
