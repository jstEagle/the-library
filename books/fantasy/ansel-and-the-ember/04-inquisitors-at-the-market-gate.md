## 4. Inquisitors at the Market Gate

The dream came again that night, as it always did on the eve of trouble: a map of the Ember Realm unrolling across a burning table, its rivers running like molten copper, its mountains crumbling into ash even as his pen traced them. In the dream he was still drawing. That was the worst of it—that some part of him could not stop drawing, could not stop naming things, even as everything he named was consumed.

Rafael woke with ink-stained fingers curled against his chest, though he had scrubbed them raw for five years and they were only ever brown now, the stain of cheap walnut dye and candle smoke. He lay in the narrow room above his shop and listened to Vell wake up around him: the fishwives crying their catch down on Tallow Row, the groaning of cart wheels in the mud, the bell of the Chantry of Saint Verene tolling six, off-key as always because no one in this city had ever paid to fix it.

Vell did not believe in magic. That was the whole of its charm. The city had outlawed the craft two hundred years ago with such thoroughness that even the word *mage* had become a curse children were washed out with soap. Here, a man who once mapped the veins of a living realm could sell dried lavender knotted into "luck charms" and copper coins drilled through and strung on leather cords—"against fever," he'd tell the grandmothers, "against bad husbands," he'd tell the wives—and no one would ever look at his hands twice.

He dressed, went down, opened the shutters, and set out his wares in the gray light: rows of knots and knots of nothing, little bags of salt and rosemary, a tray of river stones polished smooth and sold as *worry-eaters*. Ansel was still asleep in the back room, curled on the pallet beneath Rafael's own winter cloak, one hand closed loosely around the shard.

The child had been with him four days now. Four days of soup and silence and the low, bell-like hum of that thing in the boy's fist whenever he dreamed. Four days of Rafael telling himself that no one knew, that the roads between here and the Ember Realm were long and blind, that a mud-streaked mute child was invisible in a city full of mud-streaked children.

He told himself this while his hands shook so badly he dropped the salt spoon twice.

* * *

The market gate of Vell stood at the mouth of Tallow Row like an old man's missing tooth—a gap in the wall where the gatehouse had burned a generation back and never been rebuilt. Through it poured the morning: carts, geese, soldiers home from the coast garrisons, and today, moving against the current of all of it, six figures in gray.

Rafael saw them before he understood what he was seeing. Gray cloaks, undyed wool, hoods back to show clean-shaven heads. They did not push through the crowd; they moved *with* it, unhurried, and the crowd parted for them the way water parts around a keel—without being asked, without quite knowing why. At their belts hung instruments instead of swords: long brass calipers, silver chains strung with small weights, and at the hip of the leader, a case of black lacquer that Rafael's body recognized before his mind did.

A resonance case. For holding shards.

His stomach turned to cold lead. He kept sorting river stones. His fingers, traitorously steady after all these years of practice, arranged three worry-eaters into a neat triangle and did not tremble at all.

The inquisitors stopped at the fishmonger's stall nearest the gate. Rafael could not hear the words over the market noise, but he could read the shape of it—the leader's courteous tilt of the head, the fishwife's shrug, the way she jerked her thumb up the row without interest. Then the next stall. Then the next. A question, asked over and over, patient as dripping water.

They were asking after someone. And they were working toward him stall by stall, the way a man works along a seam, looking for the loose thread.

*Child of the old speech.* The words arrived in his memory whole, though he hadn't heard them spoken yet—he simply knew, with the sick certainty of a man who has read enough interrogations to recognize one from a distance, that this was the question. *Have you seen a child of the old speech?*

Five years. Five years of staying small, of selling nothing, of being a man so dull that even boredom overlooked him. And it had taken four days for the world to find him anyway.

He looked up and made his mistake.

It was only a glance—one heartbeat too long, his eyes finding the gray cloaks and snapping away again—but the third inquisitor, a woman with a face like a folded letter, caught it. She didn't turn her head. She didn't change her pace. But Rafael had spent a war watching people watch each other, and he saw the small adjustment in her walk, the way her path bent, very slightly, toward Tallow Row's middle stretch. Toward the charm-seller's stall.

*Don't run,* he told himself. *Running is a confession.*

He smiled at old Marta Brune when she hobbled up to buy a fever-charm for her grandson, and his voice came out warm and easy and pitched exactly right, and inside he was screaming. He wrapped the knot of lavender, took her coppers, wished health on the boy. Behind Marta's shoulder the gray cloaks advanced one stall, then another. The woman with the folded-letter face had fallen half a step behind her companions now. Drifting. Casual as smoke.

*Rafael Galloway,* he thought, *you are the worst liar in two kingdoms, and today you are going to have to be a good one or die.*

* * *

She reached his stall while he was pretending to count his stock.

"Charm-seller." Her voice was pleasant, educated, the accent of the capital worn thin by years in the provinces. Up close he could see the instruments at her belt properly now, and one of them made his blood stop: not a caliper but a tuning fork of dark metal, its prongs wrapped in waxed thread. A speech-fork. It resonated against the old tongues. Against words spoken in a language older than kingdoms, older than the crown, older than the maps he'd bled to make.

"Morning to you," Rafael said. Shopkeeper's smile. Small man's shoulders. "Luck, love, or protection? I've got charms for all three, though I'll be honest, the luck ones work best."

Something flickered behind her eyes—not amusement, exactly. Assessment. "You've a funny way of talking about your own trade."

"It's dried lavender and string, mistress. If I talked about it like it was real, I'd be a fraud AND a fool." He laughed. It sounded almost natural. Somewhere under his ribs his heart was trying to claw its way out.

"Mm." She picked up a worry-eater, turned it in her fingers. Her nails were trimmed short and clean. Everything about her was clean, scrubbed, deliberate—the opposite of everything Vell was. "We're asking after a child. Come down from the east, we think. Mute, or near enough. Speaks a tongue nobody knows." She set the stone down and looked at him directly, and her eyes were the pale gray of paper left too long in weather. "You keep a shop on this row. You see everyone."

"I see everyone buying luck," Rafael said. "Which is most of Vell, and none of them interesting."

"A child of the old speech," she said, and this time she said it slowly, letting the words sit in the air between them like something laid on a table. "Perhaps eight, nine years. Mud to the knees. Alone."

And here is where Rafael Galloway, royal cartographer, who had lied to generals and kings and once to a god wearing a bishop's face, discovered that five years of hiding had rusted every hinge he had.

"No," he said.

Too fast. Too flat. No curiosity, no follow-up, no *what's a child doing alone on the roads, poor mite*. Just *no*, slammed down like a door, and he watched the woman hear it, watched her file it away behind those paper-gray eyes, watched her smile widen by a single degree.

"No?" she said gently.

"Haven't seen any children this week except the Cooper's twins, and they're loud enough in two languages." He laughed again. It came out wrong, a beat late, and he heard it come out wrong, and knowing it was wrong made the next silence worse.

"What's your name, charm-seller?"

"Tolly," he said. "Tobias Tolly."

It was the name on his license, the false one he'd bought for forty crowns from a clerk in the harbor district, and it had served him five years without a whisper of trouble, and saying it now felt like handing her a rope and asking her kindly to make the noose a proper size.

"Tobias Tolly." She wrote it in a small ledger she produced from her cloak—actually wrote it, unhurried, while he stood there sweating in the morning cold. "And you've been in Vell long?"

"Six years. Seven. Time runs together."

"Mm. And before that?"

"Begging your pardon, mistress, but before that's my business, and my business was boring." He spread his hands, the honest shopkeeper's gesture, palms open, nothing hidden. "Sold cloth in Marrowdale. Cloth doesn't make for stories."

"No," she agreed. "Cloth rarely does." She closed the ledger. For a moment she simply looked at him, and Rafael had the vertiginous sense of being measured, mapped, his dimensions taken down in fine lines for later reference—by someone whose craft was the dark twin of his own. "Thank you for your time, Master Tolly. We'll be in the city a few days. Sweeping the markets, the docks, the temple wards." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "If you remember anything. A face. A sound. Children who speak strangely carry a particular... music about them. You'd know it if you'd heard it."

"I'm sure I haven't," Rafael said.

"Almost certain," she corrected softly, and touched her hood, and walked on.

* * *

He did not see the rest of her day. He didn't need to. He watched from his doorway as the six gray cloaks finished Tallow Row and split at the gate—two toward the docks, two toward the Chantry ward, and the woman with the folded-letter face walking slowly, alone, back up the row past his stall without turning her head, the way a hunter walks a line she has already decided on and is merely confirming.

He closed the shop early. First time in five years. He turned the sign, dropped the bar, and stood in the dim interior listening to his own breathing.

Then he went to the back room and knelt by the pallet and shook the child awake, more roughly than he meant to.

Ansel came up out of sleep already reaching for the shard, eyes wide and dark, and Rafael put a finger to his lips and held up one hand: *wait*. He gathered the boy's few things—the ruined boots, the cloak, the bread he'd saved—and his own things, which amounted to a satchel, a knife, and a oilskin packet he pulled from beneath a floorboard with hands that finally, finally shook.

Inside the packet: a map. Not of the Ember Realm—he'd burned every one of those, or told himself he had. This was of Vell itself, drawn from memory in the first month of his exile, because a man can leave a war but apparently cannot leave the habit of knowing exactly where every door and drain and dead-end lies. Sewers. Cellars. The smugglers' stairs below the tannery that the city guard had never found because they weren't on any official survey.

Because he had been the official survey.

"The sewers," he whispered, mostly to himself. "Odile's people run the flood-tunnel under the tannery. If she still owes me—" He stopped. Debts from the old war were not the kind you collected lightly. "If she still owes me, we go east. If she doesn't, we're drowned men either way, so it hardly matters."

Ansel watched him with that unnerving stillness the child had, and then lifted the shard, and it hummed—softly, but in the silent room it might as well have been a cathedral bell. Rafael flinched.

"Not now," he breathed. "Not ever, if I get my way. That thing is a beacon, do you understand? Every gray cloak between here and the sea can feel it singing." He crouched to the boy's level, and gentleness cost him something, because what he actually felt was terror wearing gentleness like a borrowed coat. "Listen to me. We're leaving tonight. Down, and out, and east. You don't speak. You don't hum. You hold that shard like it's a stone and you are bored of stones. Can you do that?"

Ansel nodded. Then, after a moment, the child did something he had not done in four days: reached out and pressed two small fingers against Rafael's wrist, right over the pulse, the way you'd check whether a wounded bird was still alive.

Rafael swallowed. "I'm fine," he lied, in common tongue, and then—because the boy's eyes didn't move, because lying to a child who couldn't answer felt like lying to a mirror—he said it in the old speech instead. *I am afraid. But I am here.*

The shard hummed once, softly, like agreement. Or like warning.

Outside, distantly, the Chantry bell tolled the hour, and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, came another sound: boots on cobbles, many, in step, moving up Tallow Row. Not six now. More than six. They weren't sweeping the market anymore.

They were coming to the charm-seller's door.

Rafael snuffed the candle, took the child's hand, and led him down through the floor.
