## 5. The Smuggler's Toll

The sewers of Vell had been built by optimists. That was Rafael's first thought, three hundred paces in, ankle-deep in water that moved too slowly to be honest about its contents. The city's founders had drawn their drainage with the tidy confidence of men who believed the sea would always cooperate, and the sea had spent two centuries proving them wrong. Channels that should have flowed toward the harbor instead pooled, doubled back, dead-ended in brick throats furred with black mold. Somewhere above, the inquisitors were knocking on doors, and below them Rafael Galloway was lost in a city he had lived in for five years.

He kept his left hand on the wall and the shard-bearer close.

"Stay where I can reach you," he said, and then, because the child did not answer and never answered, he added uselessly, "Ansel."

The name felt strange in his mouth still, like a coin from a country that no longer existed. The boy had offered it once, in the first terrible hour, pointing at himself with mud-caked fingers and saying it twice—*Ansel, Ansel*—in the common tongue, before slipping back into the old speech that had gone through Rafael's ears and straight into the marrow of him. Now the child walked half a step behind, bare feet making almost no sound on the flooded stone, cradling the wrapped shard against his chest the way other children carried bread they weren't sure would last the week.

The shard hummed. Even through the cloth, even through Rafael's fear, he could feel it—a low, patient tone at the edge of hearing, like a bell struck once in a distant room and never quite allowed to finish ringing. It had hummed all night while they waited in the dark of his shop for the boots in the street to pass. It hummed now, and Rafael could not stop thinking about the inquisitor's word, spoken through the door of the charm-seller's shop in a voice like a key turning: *a child of the old speech.*

They knew about Ansel. Which meant someone else knew about the shard, or suspected it, or wanted what the child carried badly enough to send gray cloaks into a magic-outlawed city to find one small boy. Five years of staying small, of selling love-charms made of nothing but sugar and suggestion, of keeping his hands soft and his memory softer—and the war had found him anyway, standing on his doorstep in the shape of a child with river-colored eyes.

"Left here," Rafael murmured, mostly to himself, and turned them down a channel that smelled of tar and rotting rope. He had memorized Vell's sewer grid years ago—not because he'd expected to need it, but because his mind betrayed him that way. Give him any space and he would map it. He couldn't help it. It was like breathing for a man who had once been drowned.

Except the map was wrong.

He realized it slowly, the way one realizes a conversation has gone badly: the junction ahead should have opened into the great collector basin under Fishmonger's Square, a wide brick chamber he had charted in his third month in the city, bored and frightened and filling notebooks he later burned. Instead the passage narrowed. The ceiling dropped until he had to stoop. The water rose past his ankles to his shins, and the current—which should have been pulling seaward—tugged gently *inland*, as if the whole tunnel were swallowing.

"That's not right," he said quietly.

Ansel tugged his sleeve. The boy pointed forward, then made a gesture Rafael had not seen before: both hands flat, moving apart, then together again, like something folding. In the old speech it would have been a single word, one of those compound syllables the pre-kingdom tongue used for things that changed their own nature. Rafael didn't know the word. But he understood the gesture perfectly.

*It's moving.*

"The tunnels are shifting." His voice came out thin. "Of course they're shifting. Of course."

He had heard rumors, back when he still let himself listen to rumors—that Vell's underworld had grown strange since the ban on magic, that the smugglers who moved contraband beneath the city spoke of passages that rearranged themselves like a hand reshuffling cards. Everyone assumed it was smugglers' talk, designed to discourage pursuit. Standing in water that pulled the wrong direction, listening to a crown-shard sing against a child's chest, Rafael understood that it was true, and that it was not natural, and that whatever unmapped these tunnels did so deliberately, the way a spider deliberately wraps a fly.

The light he carried—a stub of tallow candle shielded inside a lantern of green glass, charmed to burn slow—guttered once, twice. Then, ahead of them, out of the dark, came a sound that stopped his heart for one full beat.

A voice. A woman's, dry and unhurried, with the flat coastal vowels of the Salt Reaches underneath it.

"You're dripping on my floor."

* * *

The lantern-light found her by degrees, and Rafael's heart went from stopping to hammering in the space of a breath, because he knew her. Five years older. A scar she hadn't had before, running white from the corner of her left eye down to the hinge of her jaw. But there was no mistaking Odile Vance, sitting on an overturned crate with her boots propped on a second crate as though the flooded sewer were a parlor and he an inconvenient guest, a long-barreled hand-crossbow resting across her knees with the casualness of a cat that knows exactly how fast it is.

"Odile," Rafael said.

"Rafael Galloway," she said, and the sound of his real name in this place, spoken aloud, made Ansel flinch and press closer to his side. She noticed—of course she noticed; Odile Vance noticed everything short of the weather and most of that too—and her gaze slid from the boy to the bundle in his arms to Rafael's face, and something in her expression sharpened like a blade being turned. "Well," she said softly. "Well, well. Five years, and you come crawling through my cellars carrying exactly the thing half the gray cloaks in Vell are wetting their boots about tonight. Do you know what they're paying for word of you? I do. I've been offered. Twice."

"Odile—" he began.

"Don't." She raised one finger, and even seated, even outnumbered by no one, she managed to make the gesture imperial. "Don't talk. Just stand there and think very hard about the last time we saw each other, and whether I shot at you or for you, and how much arithmetic that's worth tonight."

He thought about it. There had been no time to think about anything else since the knock at his door, so the memory came up whole and burning: the last year of the old war, the Ember Realm bleeding out along its borders, and Rafael—younger, arrogant, certain that maps could save people—standing in Odile Vance's cabin aboard the *Salt Mercy* while she agreed, against every instinct and every invoice, to run a cargo of refugee mages out of a burning harbor. She had lost her ship for it. She had lost her commission with the free traders' guild. She had gained a scar from a usurper's boarding party and a debt that everyone said Rafael owed her, because everyone always decided the man with the famous name was the one who'd been done the favor.

But she had never once collected. Not in five years. Not when his shop had burned in the Charm Riots and he'd needed a roof; he'd slept in it anyway, in the ashes. Not when he'd sent word asking after news of the Ember Realm and received, months later, a single sheet of paper with nothing on it but a tide table. He had told himself that meant forgiveness, or at least forgetfulness. Sitting here in her sewer, watching the crossbow's muzzle drift lazily until it pointed somewhere in the general region of his heart, he understood that it might have meant something more like patience.

"I shot *for* you," Rafael said. "You shot over my shoulder at the boarding captain."

"Mm. And hit him. Through the eye. At night. In a storm." Her mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "I've been chasing that shot for five years, Galloway. Never landed another like it. You know what that does to a woman? Makes her sentimental." She swung her boots off the crate and stood, and the crossbow came up with her, no longer casual at all. "So here's where we find ourselves. The inquisitors have the market gate, the fish gate, and the west stair. They'll have the harbor by dawn. Every road out of Vell will be watched by morning, and every road under it belongs to me, and here you come wading through my water with the one piece of contraband in this world that gets a person not just killed but *unmade*—you know what they do to carriers, don't you? They don't hang them. They ask them questions with instruments that take the answers out through the skin."

"I know," Rafael said quietly.

"And you brought a *child*."

"He brought himself." The words came out harder than he intended, and he saw her eyes flicker—the first honest surprise he'd seen on her face. "I didn't find Ansel, Odile. Ansel found me. There's a difference, and it matters, and I'll tell you everything I know if you ask. But if you put a bolt in anyone tonight, make it me, because the boy is not part of any bargain you strike with me. He never was. He only arrived."

The sewer dripped. Somewhere far above, muffled by forty feet of brick and earth, a bell tolled the second hour after midnight. Ansel stood pressed against Rafael's leg with the shard humming against his chest, and he was looking up at Odile Vance with an expression Rafael was beginning to recognize and dread: not fear, but assessment. The child was deciding something about her. As if the shard had opinions. As if the old speech had words for people that Rafael couldn't hear.

Odile looked at the boy for a long moment. Then at Rafael. Then she did something worse than shooting him: she laughed, one short, bitter bark that echoed down the flooded passage and came back sounding like several women laughing in agreement.

"*Arrived,*" she repeated. "Gods preserve me. You always did have a gift for finding the exact center of trouble and building a house on it." She crossed to the tunnel wall, rapped twice on a brick that looked identical to every other brick, and a section of it—no, a section of the *space* around it, Rafael corrected himself, feeling the old cartographer in him recoil—swung inward on darkness and the smell of woodsmoke. Warmth spilled out. Lamplight. The sound of a kettle, absurdly domestic.

"Here's my price," Odile said, without turning around. "Not coin. Coin I can get anywhere, and you haven't got any worth taking. My price is this: you tell me the truth, all of it, everything you know about what that child carries and why the usurper wants him—and then you keep walking east. All the way. No stopping in Harrowgate to lick your wounds, no hiding in some free-city garret pretending to be a spice merchant. Whatever this is, you finish it, because it started with you, and I am *tired*, Galloway, of cleaning up the leavings of important men who mapped the world and then lost their nerve in it."

The words went into him like a splinter driven deep. He thought of denying it—of telling her she didn't understand—but his throat closed around the lie, because the worst of it was that she wasn't wrong. He had mapped the world. And then, in the end, he had helped bind its king, however unwittingly, however innocently, with ink and vellum and a young man's faith in his own cleverness. He had run. He had stayed small. He had sold sugar charms for five years while the Ember Realm suffocated.

"All right," he said. "Truth, and the road east. I'll pay it."

"I know you will." She stepped aside from the hidden door and gestured them through with mock courtesy, and as Ansel passed her—small, silent, clutching his singing burden—the boy paused. Looked up at her. And did the strangest thing Rafael had seen yet: he reached out and touched the scar on her cheek, one fingertip, feather-light, and made a soft sound in the old speech. Two syllables. Almost tender.

Odile went absolutely still.

"What did he say?" she asked, and her voice had gone very quiet, and Rafael realized with a jolt that she was afraid—not of the boy, but of how much she wanted the answer.

"I don't know," Rafael admitted. "I'm not fluent. But—" He hesitated. "There's a word that recurs in the old tongue. *Vance.* It means something like *the debt that keeps you alive.*"

For a moment Odile Vance, smuggler, exile, owner of exactly one secret door and no ships at all, looked as if she might weep. Then she snorted, shouldered her crossbow, and shoved them both gently through the door into firelight.

"In," she said. "Kettle's on. And Galloway—if either of you drips on my floor again, the price goes up."

* * *

The room beyond the door should not have existed. Rafael understood architecture the way priests understood scripture, and the room offended him on principle: it was dry, warm, and roughly four times larger than the space available for it, which meant either that Odile's cellar extended under foundations it had no business extending under, or that someone had worked spatial magic here, carefully, illegally, and recently. Copper kettles. A camp bed. Charts nailed to the walls—charts of Vell's underworld, redrawn weekly, and Rafael's professional soul cried out at the sight of them because they were *good*, better than good, annotated in a tight left-slanted hand with currents and collapse-risks and little symbols he didn't recognize.

"You remapped the shifting tunnels," he said. "Weekly. How?"

"Toll-takers. I pay beggars and rat-catchers to walk the wet ways and report what's changed." Odile poured three cups of something dark and steaming and pushed one at Ansel, who took it with both hands and drank like a small solemn lord. "And before you ask—no, I don't know why the tunnels move. Nobody does. But they've been moving faster lately. More every month, since spring. Like something under the city is turning over in its sleep." She sat across from him, cup in her scarred hand, and her eyes were flint again. "Now. Truth. Start with what the child is carrying, and don't insult me by starting small."

So Rafael paid the first installment of the toll. He told her about the knock in the old tongue, the shard that hummed like a struck bell, the dreams of the burning map that had plagued him for five years and had, in hindsight, been less dream than summons. He told her what he had pieced together from Lorena Ashe's letters years ago, before the silence: that the usurper Malvane wore a crown that was not a crown but a parasite, that the dying king Aldric had been bound to it in a ritual that required a map of the binding—*his* map, drawn in innocence and used in malice—and that the crown fed on the king's memories the way a tick feeds on blood, growing fatter and hungrier with every year.

"And the shard?" Odile asked, when he paused for breath.

"Is part of the true crown. Or its memory. I don't know which, and I don't know how Ansel came by it, because—" he spread his hands helplessly "—he can't tell me. Not in any tongue I speak. But the inquisitors want him, Malvane wants what he carries, and I know enough of the old rites to know that a shard of the true crown, brought into the presence of the false one..." He trailed off. He had promised truth, not certainty.

"But?"

"But I think it can unbind the king. And I think that whoever carries it there pays for the unbinding out of their own self. Out of their memories. Everything they were." He looked at his hands—at the ink stains that five years of scrubbing had never fully lifted, as if his skin itself refused to forget its craft. "That's the shape of it. That's all I truly know."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Ansel had fallen asleep sitting upright, chin on his chest, the wrapped shard still humming against him like a second heartbeat, and Odile stared at the sleeping child for a long, long time.

"One condition more," she said finally. "Not instead of the toll. On top of it." She set down her cup. "When you go east—and you will go east, because you've already decided, I can see it in your cursed cartographer's face—you go by my roads. My toll-gates. My people. And I come with you as far as Harrowgate, because if this ends the way you say it might, somebody in this room has to be hard enough-hearted to carry the rest of you through it." Her jaw tightened. "Nobody takes a child to be unmade, Galloway. Not on my floors. Not on my roads. We find another way, or we die looking."

"There may not be another way."

"Then we die looking," Odile said, "which is a better use of dying than most of us manage." She stood, stretched until her spine cracked, and jerked her thumb at a low door at the back of the impossible room. "Sleep. Both of you. You leave at low tide, through the drowned stairs, and if you wake to shouting, it's either inquisitors or my crew, and either way stay behind me." At the doorway she paused, without turning around.

"For what it's worth," she said gruffly, "the last man I ran cargo for was fleeing the Ember Realm too. I told myself I'd never do it again—carry people toward that place instead of away from it." A pause. "Get some sleep. Low tide comes early, and the roads east have been eating travelers all season."

She doused the lamp, and in the dark Rafael lay listening to two kinds of breathing—the child's quick and shallow, the smuggler's slow and deliberate—and to the shard, humming on and on beneath its wrappings like a bell rung once, five years ago, that had never been permitted to stop.
