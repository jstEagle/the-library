## 7. The Free City of Harrowgate

The road gave them Harrowgate the way the sea gives up a drowned thing: slowly, reluctantly, in pieces.

First there was the smell—brine and hot metal and something sweeter beneath it, like burnt sugar, which Rafael knew to be alchemical waste poured into the harbor canals by a dozen discreet laboratories. Then the sound: bells, dozens of them, tuned to no common hour so that the city rang in broken, overlapping fragments all day long. The exiles who founded Harrowgate had refused, from the first morning, to let any single voice tell them what time it was. Then, at last, rounding a shoulder of black rock on the coast road, the city itself: built into the cliff face like a barnacle colony, white stone and copper roofs gone green with salt air, terraces stacked upon terraces, bridges slung between towers like the rigging of some impossible ship.

Ansel stood at the guardrail of Odile's wagon and stared at it with his mouth slightly open. He had not spoken—not in the old tongue, not at all—for two days, since the raid in Harrowgate's approach roads had forced them off the highway and into the hills. But now his hand crept across the wagon bed and closed around Rafael's sleeve, and he tugged twice, the way he did when he wanted something named.

"Harrowgate," Rafael said. "The Free City."

The boy studied him, then the city, then him again, as though checking one against the other for lies. Five years ago Rafael would have found that skepticism charming in a student. Now it made his chest ache, because Ansel had learned it watching adults fail him, and because Rafael was about to prove how much a man could lie even to himself.

Odile reined the mules onto the switchback. "Rules," she said, without turning around. She had rules for everything; Rafael suspected she invented them partly for comfort, the way other people prayed. "One: nobody uses magic where it can be seen. Two: nobody says my name out loud. Three—" she glanced back at Ansel, and her voice gentled in a way she would have denied under torture, "—nobody lets go of the boy's hand in a crowd. There are people in Harrowgate who'd sell a child of the old speech for the price of a good dinner, and cheaper ones who'd do it for free."

"And you?" Rafael asked. "What will you be doing while I grovel?"

"Drinking somewhere defensible." Odile spat over the rail with feeling. "And keeping an ear out. Inquisitors came through the pass behind us, Galloway. Gray cloaks, asking after a cartographer and a child. You didn't hear it from me, and you especially didn't hear that they described your hands."

"My hands?"

"Ink-stained fingers. Like they'd been told to look." She let that sit. "Somebody remembers you, Rafe. Somebody who mattered enough to describe."

Rafael looked down at his hands where they rested on the rail. Ten years ago they had been beautiful hands, or he had thought so—the hands of the royal cartographer, quick and precise, stained permanently blue-black at the fingertips no matter how he scrubbed, because a mapmaker's ink got into the skin the way a liar's trade gets into the soul. He had spent five years teaching them coarser work: tying charm-knots, painting cheap sigils on tin medallions, doing the small dishonest magic of a shopkeeper. They were still stained. Some things did not wash out.

* * *

Harrowgate inside its walls was worse than the approach suggested, because it was alive. Vell had been a city of held breath, of lowered eyes and licensed nothing. Harrowgate was a city that had decided, collectively and with great stubbornness, that outlawed was simply another word for unregulated. Mages sold charms openly in the Saltmarket—real ones, dangerous ones, love philtres next to fire-salts next to bottled last words. A woman with riverweed braided into her hair argued prices with a man whose shadow moved a half-second behind him. Children ran messages for memory-brokers, carrying sealed thoughts in leaden capsules they were forbidden, on pain of worse than death, to open.

Ansel pressed close against Rafael's side as they walked, and Rafael kept his promise to Odile, hand firm around the boy's small fingers. But the child was not afraid. Rafael could feel it in the way he moved—a kind of listening attention, head tilted, as though the city's chaos were a language being spoken just below the threshold of understanding. Once, passing a stall where an old exile sold bottled birdsong, Ansel stopped dead and stared at the jars until the seller, unnerved, drew a cloth over them.

"He hears things," Rafael said quietly, when they had moved on. "In the old speech. Sometimes I think the whole world is talking to him and only he can hear it."

"Then keep him away from brokers," Odile said. "Half the memory-trade in this city runs on children like that. The other half runs on fools like you."

She left them at the foot of the Spire Stair with a jerk of her chin that Rafael chose to interpret as affection. "The Drowned Anchor, third terrace, ask for a room facing away from the harbor. If you're not back by dark I'm assuming you're dead and drinking your share."

"Your generosity humbles me."

"It should." She hesitated then, one boot on the stair, and for a moment the smuggler's armor slipped and Rafael saw the young soldier she had been in the old war, the one whose life he had once bought with a redrawn border. "Lorena Ashe," she said. "You're sure?"

"She was my apprentice. She's the only person alive who knows both halves of what we need—the binding rite and the counter to it."

"You know what she'll want to know first. Not the rite. Not the counter." Odile's eyes were steady and pitiless. "She'll want to know why you ran instead of staying to fix it. And Rafe—I've seen you answer that question to yourself every night for five years. It doesn't get easier. It gets heavier. Bring it anyway. She'll know if you leave it home."

* * *

The Spire Stair climbed through seven terraces of increasingly expensive desperation, and Lorena Ashe worked at the top of it, which told Rafael everything about how well she had done before he knocked on her door.

Her shop had no sign. It didn't need one. The door was plain black wood, but the frame was carved with a script Rafael recognized with a lurch—he had taught it to her, twenty years ago, a girl of fifteen with singed eyebrows and a genius for structure, standing in his workroom in the royal palace while he explained how memory could be mapped the way coastline is mapped, charted, bounded, and—if one was ruthless enough—redrawn.

He stood before the door for a long time. Long enough that a neighbor across the landing, a thin man feeding a cat that was probably not entirely a cat, began to watch him with professional interest.

Five years. Five years of staying small, of selling tin charms, of telling himself that running had been survival and survival was not a sin. And now here he was, about to ask the person he had failed most completely to save the world he had helped break.

He raised his hand to knock—and stopped, because the door opened on its own.

Lorena Ashe stood in the doorway, and time did a strange folding thing. She was forty now, gray threading her black hair at the temples, fine lines around eyes that were still the sharpest instruments in any room. But when she saw him, her face went through recognition, disbelief, and something colder and more carefully maintained, all in the space of a breath, and Rafael understood that whatever she had become, the girl with singed eyebrows was still in there, and she had spent five years deciding exactly what she would say if he ever appeared.

"You," she said.

"Hello, Lorena."

"I felt you on the stair." Her voice was flat, controlled, a broker's voice. "I've warded this landing for years against a hundred signatures. Yours I never warded against, because I told myself you were dead, or worse, that you'd learned discretion." Her gaze dropped to his hands, then to the child half-hidden behind his coat. Something flickered there—curiosity, immediate and hungry, the old apprentice reflex. "And who is this?"

"His name is Ansel. He doesn't speak—at least, not anything most people understand." Rafael swallowed. "He speaks the pre-kingdom tongue, Lorena. Fluently. Natively. As if he was born to it."

That landed. He watched it land, watched the broker's mask slip just far enough to show the scholar underneath, eyes widening, breath catching. Twenty years ago he had seen that same expression when he showed her the first true map of the Ember Realm.

"Come inside," she said. "Both of you. Quickly."

* * *

Her workroom was exactly as he remembered it and utterly transformed, the way a person is. The same south-facing window, the same scarred oak table where she had once copied charts under his supervision. But now the walls were lined floor to ceiling with leaden capsules in ranked wooden drawers, thousands of them, each labeled in her precise hand. Bottled memories. Sold years. Other people's lives, catalogued and alphabetized.

Ansel walked to the nearest drawer and put his palm flat against it, and every capsule in the row hummed softly, like struck glass.

Lorena went very still. "Rafael," she said, and it was his full name, and it was a weapon, "what have you brought into my house?"

"The truth," he said. "Or the beginning of it. Lorena—I need your help. There's a shard of the true crown. The king is still alive in there, bound, and Malvane's wearing him like a—"

"Stop." She held up a hand. Her composure was magnificent and terrible. "You will sit down. You will drink this tea, which is drugged only lightly, against my own caution. And then you will explain to me, slowly, why the man who drew the maps that made the binding possible—the man whose survey of the Ember Realm gave Malvane's ritualists every ley-line, every anchor point, every sacred geometry they needed to cage a living god-king—has the audacity to stand in my workroom and ask for my help undoing it."

The words went into him like needles finding the spaces between ribs. He had known she knew. He had not known she would say it aloud, in front of the child, with such surgical precision.

"Lorena—"

"No." For the first time her voice cracked, just barely, and Rafael heard five years of grief in the crack. "Do you know what I did when I understood what those maps were for? I was in the archive, Rafael. I found your master survey with the ritual annotations already added—in another hand, but built on yours, grown from yours like rot from a wound. I went to you. I climbed four flights of stairs to your chambers in the palace, and I stood outside your door, and I heard you packing. I heard you fold your charts. And I stood there like a coward and let you go, because some part of me hoped I was wrong about you."

Ansel had gone quiet against the wall, watching them with those too-old eyes. The capsules in the drawers had begun to hum again, faintly, sympathetically, as though the room itself remembered grief.

"I'm not wrong about you," Lorena said, softer now, and somehow that was worse. "I never was. That's the worst of it. You're exactly the man I apprenticed to—brilliant, and kind, and capable of walking away from the ruin you made while a child starves in the streets of Vell speaking a dead language that only exists because your maps helped murder everyone who spoke it."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, one of Harrowgate's discordant bells began to ring, off-time and mournful.

Rafael set down the tea he hadn't drunk. His hands, ink-stained after all these years, lay open on his knees, palms up, the way a man shows he holds no weapon—or the way a beggar asks.

"You're right," he said. "All of it. I ran. I told myself it was survival, and then I told myself it was penance, staying small, selling tin charms, hiding. But Odile told me on the stair that the question gets heavier, not easier, and she was right. I don't have an answer for you, Lorena. I have a shard that hums like a struck bell, a child who may be the last native speaker of the Ember tongue, and inquisitors describing my hands at every gate between here and the coast. I have shame. It's the only honest thing I've carried for five years. Will it do?"

Lorena looked at him for a long moment. Then at Ansel, whose hand had crept, unnoticed, to rest on Rafael's shoulder—a small weight, impossibly heavy.

And the memory-broker, keeper of ten thousand other people's pasts, exhaled slowly and pulled a key from around her neck.

"There's a ledger," she said. "My private one. Every secret worth having in this city passes through it eventually—including, three months ago, a rumor about a rite of unbinding, older than the kingdom, that requires a vessel willing to give up everything they are." Her eyes met Rafael's, and in them was neither forgiveness nor condemnation, but something harder than both: purpose. "Sit down, old man. We're going to need all night, and you're going to tell me everything—from the beginning, and this time you will not skip the parts that shame you."

Ansel's fingers tightened on Rafael's shoulder, once, like a promise.

And in the humming drawers behind them, ten thousand bottled memories stirred in their sleep—as if they, too, were listening.
