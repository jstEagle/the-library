## 9. What Wears the Crown

The crown dreamed first.

It always dreamed first. Before the king woke, before the grey light seeped through the seams of the Obsidian Court, before the servants of glass and bone came shuffling in with their trays of ash-bread and memory-wine, the crown was already awake, already turning its thoughts over like a miser counting coins that were not its own.

It had no name for itself. Names were things that belonged to bodies, and it had never had a body. It had been made—woven, once, in the deep forges beneath the Singing Stone—out of the same substance as hunger. The smiths of the old realm had meant it for a vessel of memory, a keeper of the kingdom's continuity, a thing that would hold the wisdom of every monarch who wore it so that no reign would ever be lost to death. They had not understood what they were making. Or perhaps they had, and had simply not cared, because the making was beautiful and beauty excuses everything.

The crown had been empty for nine hundred years. Then a cartographer came.

It remembered him—not with affection, for it did not have affection, but with the particular attention a lock remembers a key. A thin man with ink-stained fingers and a map-seller's smile, who had walked the Ember Realm drawing its rivers and roads, who had knelt in the binding-chamber with his instruments spread out before him like a surgeon's, who had drawn the lines that made the cage possible. He had not known. The crown had tasted his intentions as he worked, the way a tongue tastes salt, and found them clean: fear, guilt, a desperate wish to be useful to someone powerful. He had believed he was making a map of protection. He had made a map of a prison.

And the crown had been fed ever since.

* * *

In the throne room of the Obsidian Court, the bound king sat on a throne of fused glass, and the crown sat on the bound king, and the room was silent except for the slow, wet sound of remembering.

Aldric had been a tall man once. The body that wore him now was still tall, but it had the quality of a garment worn too long—shoulders that had forgotten their shape, hands that lay in his lap like abandoned tools. His eyes were open. They were always open. Sleep had been one of the first things the crown had taken, because sleep was where a man hid the last of himself, and the crown did not tolerate hiding places.

*There was a garden,* the crown whispered into him, the way it whispered every morning, sorting through what remained. *There was a garden, and a woman in it, and she was—*

*—planting ember-lilies,* Aldric finished, somewhere far down inside the ruin of himself, in the small sealed cellar where he kept the things the crown had not yet found. *She was planting ember-lilies and she was laughing because I had planted them upside down.*

The crown paused. It had found this memory before, or one like it, and each time the king moved it—shifted it, buried it deeper, dressed it in different colors so it would seem like something not worth taking. It was the only game left to him, this small dishonesty, and he played it with the devotion of a man praying.

*You are tired,* the crown observed.

*I am nothing,* Aldric answered, in the language that was not language, the communion that happened in the space behind his eyes. *You have made certain of it.*

*I have made certain of everything,* the crown said, and there was something in its tone that might, in a living thing, have been mistaken for satisfaction. It was not satisfaction. The crown did not know what it was. It only knew that the king was thinner than he had been—not his body, which the crown kept fed and washed and upright, but the part of him the crown fed *on*. Nine hundred years of a kingdom's memory had been poured into this man's keeping, and he had held them the way a dam holds a river, and now the dam was nearly empty, and the crown was beginning to be afraid.

It did not call it fear. It had no word for fear. But there was a sensation it experienced, in the long hours when the king's memories came slower and thinner, like a vein of ore running out: a tightening, a reaching, an awful awareness of its own edges. When it drank deeply it felt vast. When it drank shallowly it felt—small. And smallness, for a thing that had been made to hold a kingdom, was a kind of dying.

*There was a war,* the crown prompted. *Tell me of the war.*

*I have told you of the war.*

*Tell me again. The part with the bridge.*

Aldric gave it the bridge. He had no choice; the crown could take, when it wished, though taking cost it something, left a residue of bitterness in the memory like ash in milk. It was better when he gave. And so he gave it the bridge at Callowmere, the burning of it, the retreat across the shallows, the face of the young soldier who had held the far bank and died there—and as the crown drank, Aldric watched the memory leave him the way one watches a bird fly out of an open hand. He knew it was gone. He could feel the hole where it had been, smooth-walled, already closing over. Somewhere in the dark of the crown, the bridge at Callowmere burned forever now, and he would never see it again except when the crown chose to show it to him, and it would not be his memory then. It would be a possession.

Nine hundred years, he thought, in the cellar where he kept the garden and the woman and the upside-down lilies. Nine hundred years, and it has eaten everything but me. Why not me?

He had asked it that once, long ago, in the first decade, when he still had the strength for questions.

*Because you are the vessel,* the crown had said. *A vessel that empties entirely is broken. I keep you whole so that I may keep drinking.*

He had understood then, with a clarity that had never left him, that his survival was not mercy. It was husbandry.

* * *

Far below the throne room, in the halls of the Obsidian Court, the court went about its business, and the business of the court was conquest.

The usurper—Malvane, they called him, though that had been a man's name once, before the crown had finished with the man—stood at the window of the war room and looked east, and did not see the glass forests, and did not hear the singing stone, because the crown did not permit him to see or hear anything that did not serve it. Malvane had been a general. That much was still true, the way a fossil is still an animal. The crown had come to him in the last year of the old war, when he was losing, when his armies were starving in the ash passes and his name was a curse in every mouth from here to the sea. It had offered him victory, and he had taken it, and it had taken everything else at its leisure.

Now he stood at the window with his hands behind his back, and inside him the crown turned its attention east, and east, and east again, always east, toward the free cities, toward Vell with its outlawed magic and its soft unguarded streets, toward all the world that had never known the Ember Realm and did not know it was coming.

*The armies are ready,* Malvane said. He said it aloud, because the crown liked him to speak aloud; it enjoyed the theater of it, the illusion that he was a king giving counsel to his crown rather than a mouth through which the crown spoke. *The salt road is open. The free cities have no pact among them. By season's end—*

*By season's end,* the crown agreed, and its voice in his skull was like cold water pouring into a warm room. *But not yet.*

Malvane's jaw tightened. Somewhere beneath the crown's dominion, the general who had once moved armies like pieces on a board felt the old frustration rise, and the crown felt it rise too, and found it—useful. Frustration was a flavor. It drank a little of it, carefully, the way one sips at a strong wine.

*Explain the delay.*

*There is a thing missing.* The crown turned its attention inward, to the vault beneath the throne, where the binding-lines the cartographer had drawn still glimmered faint and blue in the dark, holding the king's true self in its cage. *There is a piece of me unaccounted for. A shard. It was broken from me in the making, before the smiths ever fired me, and it has been lost in the world of men for nine hundred years.*

*And it matters?*

*It is the only part of me that remembers being made.* The crown said it simply, because the truth, in its mouth, was always simple. *I hold nine hundred years of kings. It holds the memory of the fire that made me. While it is loose, I am incomplete. While I am incomplete, the binding has a flaw in it. I have felt the flaw for five years—since the cartographer's lines were closed. A hairline. A whisper. Something on the other side of the world, humming.*

Malvane looked out at the glass forests. *You want it found.*

*I want it found,* the crown said, *and brought to me, and I want the one who carries it brought to me alive, and I want the inquisitors to tear the world of men apart until it is done.* It paused, and the pause had weight, the way a drawn blade has weight. *There is a child.*

*You said the inquisitors were hunting a child.*

*I said they were hunting a child of the old speech. The old speech was the language of the forge. A child who speaks it has been near the shard too long, or the shard has been near the child. It does not matter which. Find the child and you find my missing piece.*

*And when we have it?*

The crown was silent for a moment, and in that silence it did something it had never done before: it looked at itself. It looked down the long corridor of its own hunger, at nine hundred years of drinking, at the emptied dam and the husk of a king and the general who was a fossil and the court of glass and bone, and it felt the tightening again, the reaching, the awful smallness—and beneath it, for the first time, something that was almost a question.

When it had the shard, when it was whole, when it held the memory of its own making—what then? Would the hunger close? Would the vastness come and stay? Or would it simply be a larger vessel, with a larger emptiness, drinking from a world that could only run dry?

It did not know. It had never not known before, and the not-knowing was the closest thing it had to terror.

*When you have it,* Malvane prompted. His voice was flat. He had learned, over five years, to hear the crown's silences the way sailors hear weather.

*When I have it,* the crown said at last, *I will be whole. And then the free cities, and then the world of men, and then there will be no more hunger, because there will be nothing left outside myself to hunger for.*

It believed this, in the way that a drowning man believes the surface is close. And beneath the throne, in his cage of drawn lines, Aldric heard the words—the crown could not help leaking, it leaked constantly, its thoughts seeping through the binding like water through stone—and Aldric, in the small sealed cellar where he kept the garden, understood something the crown did not understand about itself:

It was not going to be whole. It was going to be full, for a moment, and then it would be hungry again, because that was all it was. It had been made out of hunger and fired in the shape of a crown, and no amount of drinking would make it anything else. It would drink the free cities. It would drink the world. And at the end of all its conquest there would be a crown sitting on a skull in an empty room, still reaching, still tightening, still small.

*Then let it come,* Aldric thought, down in the dark, holding his garden like a coal cupped in two hands. *Let it go east and find its missing piece. Because a thing that is looking for something is a thing that can be followed.*

And the crown, drinking its morning memory of the bridge at Callowmere, never felt him think it.

* * *

That evening, the inquisitors' report came west to east across the ash passes, carried by relay-mage and glass-wing, and was laid before the throne in a scroll of grey vellum.

The crown read it through Malvane's eyes, which was the only way it read anything. The report was short. A charm-seller in the city of Vell, a shop of no significance, questioned and released. A child seen in the lower quarters, mud-streaked, mute in the common tongue. An inquisitor's note, appended in careful script: *The old speech was heard. Verification pending.*

*Verification pending.* The crown turned the phrase over, and felt the tightening ease, just slightly, the way a starving man eases when he smells bread from a window he cannot reach. The shard was in Vell. The shard was in a city of outlawed magic, behind grey walls, in the hands of a child and perhaps a frightened mapmaker, and every road out of Vell was a road the crown's servants could watch.

*Send more inquisitors,* the crown said. *Empty the garrisons at the passes if you must. I want that city turned over like a stone.*

*It will be done,* Malvane said, and his voice, as always, gave away nothing. But the crown was inside him, and the crown felt the small cold flinch that moved through him at the words, the fossil of the general recognizing what an emptied garrison meant, what a realm stripped of its defenses meant, what it meant to serve a hunger that would, in the end, eat him too.

The crown felt the flinch and drank it, delicately, like a drop of something rare.

And far below, in his cage of blue lines, Aldric felt the crown's attention turn east and stay east, and felt, for the first time in five years, the binding strain—not break, not yet, but strain, a hairline flexing along the cartographer's careful strokes, as though something on the far side of the world were pulling at a thread.

The shard was moving. The shard had found hands.

*Hold on,* Aldric said to it, to the child he did not know, to the small humming piece of the fire that had made everything, down in the sealed cellar where his garden grew in the dark. *Hold on, little ember. I am not finished yet.*

And the crown, listening to the east, did not hear him.
