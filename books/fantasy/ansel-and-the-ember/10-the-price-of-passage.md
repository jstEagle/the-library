## 10. The Price of Passage

Odile Vance had survived three shipwrecks, two mutinies, and one marriage, and she had learned from all of them the same lesson: the sea never announces itself before it turns. It simply changes its mind beneath you, and you either swim or you don't.

So when she woke in her rented room above the Saltcellar tavern in Harrowgate and heard gulls screaming in a pattern that meant *something is wrong*, she did not waste time wondering what. She pulled on her boots, strapped the knife to her thigh, and went to check on her people.

Her people. That was the trouble with Harrowgate. She'd come here for a week's trading, maybe two, and somehow she'd collected strays the way a hull collects barnacles—slowly, invisibly, until they were part of the structure and pulling them off would tear something open.

Rafael Galloway was first on her list. She found him in the common room of the Saltcellar, already awake, already dressed, sitting by the cold hearth with Ansel asleep against his shoulder. The man looked like he hadn't slept at all. He probably hadn't. He had that particular stillness Odile had learned to read in men who'd been soldiers or prisoners or both—the stillness of someone listening past the walls.

"Tell me," she said.

"Inquisitors came through the east gate at dawn." His voice was low, level, the voice of a man reporting weather. "Gray cloaks. Six of them, then six more behind them. They're asking at every door."

"Asking what?"

"You know what."

She knew. A child of the old speech. A child who hummed songs that made candle flames lean toward him. A child who had walked out of nowhere five months ago carrying a shard of a crown that should not exist, and who had attached himself to a disgraced cartographer like a burr to wool.

Ansel stirred against Rafael's shoulder and opened his eyes. He didn't speak—he almost never spoke; his silence was a country Odile had stopped trying to map—but he looked up at Rafael with an expression of such complete trust that it made something twist behind Odile's ribs. She'd seen sailors look at lighthouses that way. She'd never once seen anyone look at *her* that way, and she'd decided some time ago that this didn't bother her, and she'd also decided that lying to herself about it was none of her own business.

"How long do we have?" she asked.

"Hours. Maybe less. Lorena's sending word through her channels." Rafael's ink-stained fingers drummed once against his knee and went still. "Odile, if they've come to Harrowgate, they know about Lorena. They may already know about you."

"They don't know about me," Odile said. "Nobody knows about me. That's the whole point of me."

But even as she said it, she was doing the arithmetic she always did: tides, distances, guards, gates. Harrowgate sat on a drowned coastline, half its streets built on pilings over black water, its harbor a maze of sunken ruins that only smugglers and fools navigated without a pilot. Her ship, the *Kindly Tide*, lay moored in the outer anchorage beyond the customs chain, because Odile Vance did not pay harbor fees to cities that might sell her to the Ember Realm for a chest of crown-silver.

Getting to the *Tide* meant crossing three bridges and the customs quay. With an inquisitor sweep tightening around the city like a hand closing.

"We go now," she said. "Before the net finishes closing. Get the boy fed, get your pack, meet me at the fish-stairs in twenty minutes. And Rafael—" she caught his eye, and held it, "—if I say run, you run. If I say swim, you swim. No heroics. You're no use to anyone dead, and neither am I."

He nodded. Then, quietly: "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet. Thanking me implies I'm going to succeed."

* * *

The fish-stairs stank of brine and old blood, which Odile considered a point in their favor. Nobody lingered where it smelled like that unless they had business there. She crouched at the top step, watching the water slap the stone below, while Rafael settled Ansel beside her and Lorena Ashe materialized out of the morning fog the way she always did—suddenly, silently, smelling faintly of lamp-oil and old paper.

"The sweep has reached Candlemaker's Row," Lorena said. Her voice was flat as a ledger line. "They took Master Corvin from the glasswrights' hall. In front of his apprentices. They're not asking politely anymore."

"Took him where?"

"Ships. There are gray-cloaked ships in the outer roads. Three of them." Lorena's eyes flicked toward the anchorage, and Odile felt her stomach drop. "They'll have found your vessel by now, Vance. They'll be watching it."

"Then we don't go to my vessel." Odile stood, knees cracking, and stared out at the fog-shrouded harbor. Somewhere out there, the *Kindly Tide* rode at anchor with all her false papers and her hidden compartments and eleven years of her life soaked into her timbers—and somewhere between here and there, inquisitors were doing sums of their own.

"There's another way," Rafael said slowly. "The drowned quarter. The old streets under the waterline. If we could get a boat inside the ruins—"

"If we could get a boat inside the ruins," Odile said, "we could row out past the customs chain under the cliff-shadow, and board the *Tide* from the seaward side, where their watchmen will be looking landward because smugglers come *from* land, not from open water." She grinned, and knew it wasn't a kind grin. "Which is exactly why it'll work. Nobody expects a smuggler to approach her own ship like a boarding action."

"And the boat?" Lorena asked.

"I know a man who owes me a skiff and several apologies."

The man turned out to be a one-eyed eel-fisher named Brack who owed Odile considerably more than a skiff—she'd once carried his daughter out of a plague-quarantine zone in a cargo hold full of salt cod—but he haggled anyway, because pride was the last currency some men had. She let him keep his pride and paid him in silver, and ten minutes later the four of them were sliding through the drowned streets of old Harrowgate in a leaking skiff, past rooftops that rose out of black water like the spines of drowned animals, past bell towers whose bells had been silent for sixty years and whose doors stood open to the tide.

Ansel knelt in the bow, gripping the gunwale, watching the sunken city slide by with wide dark eyes. Once, as they passed beneath the arch of a flooded chapel, the boy raised one hand and touched the water, and Odile would have sworn—would have sworn on her ship and her mother's grave—that the ripples moved away from his fingers in perfect rings, too regular, too deliberate, like something answering.

She decided not to think about it. She was getting very good at deciding not to think about things.

* * *

The seaward approach worked exactly as well as she'd planned it, right up until it didn't.

They came alongside the *Kindly Tide* in the gray half-light, and Odile went up the ratlines first with a knife in her teeth and thirty years of spite in her arms, and found her deck empty, her hatch covers undisturbed, her ship exactly as she'd left her—and that was when she understood it was wrong. Ships were never exactly as you left them. Ships breathed and shifted and settled. A ship that matched memory perfectly was a ship that had been arranged.

"Down," she hissed. "Everyone down the seaward rail, into the water if you have to—"

The trapdoor of the forward hold burst open and gray cloaks boiled out of it like smoke given soldiers' boots. Four of them. Five. Crossbows. Odile got her knife into the first one's thigh and her elbow into the second one's throat and then a crossbow stock caught her across the temple and the world went white and sideways and she was on her knees on her own deck, her own deck, watching blood drip from her brow onto planks she'd caulked herself, and an inquisitor's boot pressed down on her shoulder blade.

"Odile Vance," said a voice above her, dry as dust in a dead man's pocket. "Master smuggler. Associate of the traitor Ashe. Harborer of the child." The boot pressed harder. "You will tell us where the shard is kept."

She laughed. It hurt to laugh; something in her ribs was probably cracked, and laughing with cracked ribs was a specialty of hers. "You've got it backwards, friend. I don't keep anything. Things keep *me*. Ask my creditors."

The boot ground down. Through the red haze of pain she saw Rafael being pinned against the mainmast, two inquisitors wrenching his arms back, and Ansel—

Ansel was standing at the seaward rail, small and still, and the shard was out. He must have pulled it from the wrapping at his throat, and it was glowing—not bright, not dramatic, just a deep ember-red light, steady as a coal that would not die, and the air around the boy had gone strange and thick, the way air goes before lightning.

One of the inquisitors saw it and made a sound Odile had never heard a grown soldier make. Not a scream. A *recoil*, whole-body, the sound of a man whose training had just collided with something older than his training.

"The shard," the dry voice rasped, and now there was hunger in it, ugly and naked. "Take it. Take the child. The Crown requires—"

The light flared.

Odile felt it more than saw it—a pressure, a warmth, a vast slow attention turning toward the deck like a whale turning beneath a boat—and every candle-lantern aboard the *Kindly Tide* lit itself at once, and the ropes groaned, and the sea beneath the hull began to glow faintly, far down, like embers under ash.

And Ansel spoke.

"*Stay.*"

One word. Common tongue, rough and small and cracked with disuse, the first word Odile had ever heard him say in five months of knowing him, and he said it not to the inquisitors, not to the light, but to *her*—to Odile Vance, on her knees on her own bloody deck, with a boot on her spine. His eyes were fixed on hers. His small hands gripped the rail. And the word came out again, louder, ringing off the rigging:

"*STAY.*"

Later, Odile would try to explain what happened next and fail, because there was no explaining it. The inquisitors simply—stopped. All of them. The boot lifted from her back. The men holding Rafael released him as though his arms had become too heavy to hold. The dry-voiced officer staggered backward into the shrouds, clawing at his own gray hood, making sounds like a man trying to remember how to breathe. Whatever the boy's word had done, it had done it not to their bodies but to something underneath their bodies—to whatever it was that wore them and steered them—and for eleven heartbeats, eleven heartbeats Odile counted because counting was the only thing keeping her sane, every gray cloak on her deck stood frozen, empty-eyed, *listening*.

Then the light faded. The lanterns guttered back to ordinary flame. The sea went dark.

And Ansel swayed, and Rafael caught him before he fell, and the boy slumped against the cartographer's chest with his eyes closed and his small fists still clenched, and the inquisitors came back to themselves all at once like men surfacing from deep water—and fled. Actually fled, scrambling down their own grapnel lines to the boat that had brought them, casting off in such panic that one of them fell into the harbor and had to be fished out by his fellows.

Silence, afterward. Just the water slapping the hull, and the gulls, and Odile's own ragged breathing.

"Well," she said, when she could. She levered herself upright, tasted blood, spat it over the rail. "That's going to bring every warship in the outer roads down on us within the hour. So." She looked at Rafael, kneeling on her deck with the unconscious child in his arms, and at Lorena standing pale by the helm, and she felt the arithmetic finish itself in her chest, the sum she'd been avoiding since Vell.

The *Kindly Tide* was burned. Her name was known now, her position marked, her papers worthless. Every port from here to the Salt Road would have her description nailed to a customs post by week's end. Eleven years of careful anonymity, spent in a single morning.

There were worse things to spend it on.

"Lorena," Odile said. "Can you get yourself clear? Back channels, safe houses, all that broker nonsense?"

"I can vanish," Lorena said. "It's what I'm for." Her gaze lingered on Rafael, and on the boy, and something passed across her face that Odile chose not to name. "Vance. The unbinding rite. What we found in the archive—it's real. And it needs what it needs. When you understand what it costs—" She stopped. Shook her head. "Keep them alive long enough to choose. That's all any of us can do."

Then she was gone, down the ratlines and into the fog, and Odile turned to the business of leaving.

She cut the *Kindly Tide* loose from her moorings with her own hands. She set what sails her aching body could manage, with Rafael hauling beside her, saying nothing, because there was nothing to say that either of them wouldn't choke on. The gray-cloaked ships in the outer roads were already turning, sails blooming like wounds against the dawn, but the *Tide* was lighter and meaner and knew these waters the way Odile knew the backs of her own hands, and by full morning the warships had fallen astern, and by noon there was nothing behind them but open gray sea and the smoke of Harrowgate burning on the horizon.

They'd fired the customs quay on their way out. Odile chose to believe that was the wind's doing.

* * *

Ansel woke near sunset, in the cabin that had been Odile's and was now, apparently, everyone's.

She was mending a sail patch by lamplight—her hands needed work the way other people's minds needed rest, cracked ribs notwithstanding—when the boy padded out from the bunk, wrapped in a blanket, hair sticking up like a startled hedgehog. He crossed the cabin and stood in front of her, and stared at her with those dark serious eyes, and said nothing, because saying nothing was his native country.

"What?" Odile said, gruffly, because tenderness embarrassed her worse than bleeding did.

He reached out and touched her bandaged temple, very gently, where the crossbow stock had split her skin. Then he looked at her, and his mouth worked, and she watched him fight for it—watched the word climb up out of wherever his words lived, that deep pre-kingdom place where his language came from—and finally he said, in that same cracked, rusted, hard-won common tongue:

"Stay."

Not the command from the deck. Something else. A question, maybe. Or an offer. *I stayed,* it might have meant. Or *you stayed.* Or simply: *here, now, this—this is ours.*

Odile Vance had survived three shipwrecks, two mutinies, and one marriage, and she had never once in her life been at a loss for words. She was lost now. She set down the sail patch. She put her scarred hand on the boy's head, carefully, the way you touch something you're afraid of breaking and more afraid of wanting.

"Aye," she said, and her voice came out rougher than she intended. "I'm staying. Wherever this road goes—even if it goes where I think it goes, even to that burning country—we stay together. That's the toll. That's the price, and I've paid dearer for worse cargo."

Ansel smiled. It transformed his whole face, that smile, made him suddenly, unbearably young.

From the doorway, Rafael cleared his throat softly. He stood leaning against the frame, ink-stained hands folded, watching them with an expression Odile couldn't read—or could, and wished she couldn't, because it looked like a man memorizing something he expected to lose.

"We need to talk about the route," he said quietly. "East. Toward the Ember Realm. Before the season turns."

Odile looked at the boy, and at the man, and at the chart rolled on the table between them, showing the long salt road running east into territory no honest map covered anymore.

"Get the lamp," she said. "Let's see how much of the world we can still find our way through."
