## 12. Ansel's Choice

The shard sang to him at night.

Not with sound, exactly. Not the way Rafael's kettle sang, or the way Odile's ship—*The Kindly Thief*, though she never called it that when anyone could hear—creaked and groaned in Harrowgate's harbor. The shard sang in the place behind his eyes where words lived, the place where his own voice had gone quiet years ago and never come back. It sang in the old tongue, the one that lived in his chest like a second heartbeat, the one that made Rafael go pale and still whenever Ansel spoke it aloud.

*Ember-kin*, it called him. *Little ember-kin.*

He had learned not to answer at first.

But lately—here in Lorena Ashe's house of shelves and secrets, where the candles burned blue-green and the books whispered to each other when they thought no one was listening—lately he answered.

He sat cross-legged on the narrow bed they'd given him, in the little room under the eaves where the roof sloped so low he could touch the ceiling if he stretched. Rain ticked against the shingles. Downstairs, muffled by floorboards, Rafael and Lorena were arguing again. He couldn't hear the words, but he knew the shape of it: Rafael's voice going low and careful, Lorena's going sharp as a paper edge. They argued about him. He knew that too. Children always know.

Ansel opened his hands.

The shard lay in his palm like a sliver of morning, catching the candlelight and holding it, turning it gold at the edges. It was warm. It was always warm, the way a stone is warm after a long day in the sun, except this stone had been sitting in someone's hand for a hundred years and remembered what hands were for.

*I'm going to carry you*, he told it, in the old tongue, the way he told it every night. *I'm going to be the one.*

The shard pulsed, gentle, like something breathing in its sleep.

That was the trouble. That was the thing he couldn't say to any of them—not to Rafael with his ink-stained fingers and his sad smile, not to Odile who ruffled his hair like he was something small and breakable, not even to Lorena, who looked at him sometimes with her broker's eyes and saw too much. The trouble was that carrying the shard meant forgetting. Lorena had said it downstairs three nights ago, thinking the doors were closed, thinking he was asleep: *the bearer gives up every memory of themselves, Rafael. Every one. There is no coming back from that kind of empty.*

Ansel had lain in the dark and turned the words over, the way he turned over river stones, looking for what was underneath.

And here was what he found, what he had known since the night he knocked on a charm-seller's door with mud on his knees and a king's grief humming in his fist:

He did not have very much to lose.

* * *

Other children had mothers. He had a road.

That was how his memory arranged itself now, when he let it unspool: not rooms and faces and names, but distances. The length of the forest where he'd walked before he could remember walking. The number of days between the burning village—that came to him in flashes, orange light, a smell like hot iron—and the city of Vell, where magic was outlawed and everyone spoke with their mouths instead of their hearts. The particular gray of the sky over the mountains, which had gone on being gray for so long he'd begun to think the sky itself was tired.

He had no name before Rafael gave him one. Or if he had one, it had burned with the village, or drowned somewhere on the road, or simply worn away like a footprint in rain. *Ansel* fit him the way borrowed clothes fit—slightly wrong in the shoulders, but warm, and better than nothing.

So when he counted up what the shard would take from him, the list was short. A road. A sky. A smell like hot iron. A wordless language that lived in his bones whether he wanted it or not.

Against that, he set the other list, and the other list was longer.

Rafael, laughing for real—one sharp surprised bark of a laugh, the day Odile's monkey stole his spectacles and wore them for an hour. Rafael kneeling in the mud of the salt road to show him how to read the stars, drawing lines between them with one finger, saying *there, and there*, as if the sky were just another map and maps were things a person could trust. The taste of honey-bread in Harrowgate's market, the first sweet thing he could remember eating. Odile's rough voice singing something low and filthy while she mended nets, and stopping halfway through when she saw him listening, and then—this was the important part—*not stopping*, because she'd decided he was allowed to hear it. Being allowed. Belonging to a *they*. Having a bed that was his bed, in a house where people knew where he was.

Five years Rafael had hidden, small and safe and slowly disappearing. Ansel had watched him do it, that first week in Vell, watched him fold himself smaller and smaller behind the charm-seller's counter like a letter being folded to fit an envelope. And Ansel had understood, in the wordless way he understood most things, that Rafael was waiting to die without admitting it. That staying small was just a slow way of leaving.

The shard could take his memories. Fine. Let it take them.

But it could not take what he would have *given*: the sight of Rafael standing tall in the Ember Realm with his chin up and his hands steady, doing the brave thing at last instead of the safe thing. Odile free of the debt that dragged at her like an anchor chain. A king waking whole. Even if Ansel wouldn't remember any of it—even if he stood blank-eyed afterward while strangers wept around him—the world would still *have* those things. The world would keep them for him. Somewhere there would be a man who laughed, a woman who sang, a child who had once mattered enough to save a kingdom, and none of them would know why they felt lighter.

That was a trade. He was small, but he wasn't stupid.

* * *

He began to practice.

Not carrying—that part was easy; the shard liked his hands, settled into them like a bird into a nest. No, what he practiced was the other thing. The letting-go.

Lorena's library was full of memories. That was her trade: bottled recollection, vials of somebody's first snowfall, somebody's wedding day, somebody's last look at a burning house. She kept them in a back room behind a door with three locks, and she thought nobody noticed when Ansel slipped in after her, small and quick as a cat, to sit among the shelves of glowing glass.

He didn't steal. He only listened.

Each vial hummed faintly with what it held, and if he pressed his palm flat against the glass and closed his eyes, he could feel the shape of the memory inside—a warmth, a weight, a color. Some were heavy as stones. Some were light as moth wings. One, near the bottom shelf, wrapped in extra cloth, felt like crying.

And one evening, alone in the memory room with the blue-green candles guttering, Ansel picked up an empty vial—just an ordinary empty vial, the kind Lorena used for new stock—and held it in one hand, and the shard in the other, and tried to imagine the feeling of being poured out.

*If I give you everything*, he asked the shard, *will it hurt them?*

The shard flared, startled, and showed him a picture: a hall of black glass, a throne, a figure slumped in it like a coat hung on a chair. The bound king. The crown on the king's head writhing like a nest of silver snakes, drinking, always drinking. Beneath the image ran a current of feeling that needed no translation anywhere in any tongue: *hurry. hurry. hurry.*

"I know," Ansel said aloud, and his voice cracked on it, rusty from disuse, strange to his own ears. Speaking common words still felt like walking in someone else's shoes. "I know you're hungry. I'm coming."

He put the empty vial down. His hands were shaking. Not from fear—from the effort of holding a secret this large inside a body this small. It was like swallowing a sun and having to walk around all day pretending your stomach didn't glow.

Because here was the other thing he'd figured out, the thing that made his chest ache worse than any cold ever had: they would try to stop him. Rafael especially. Rafael, who had already decided—he'd seen it in the set of the man's shoulders, heard it in the careful way he asked Lorena questions about the rite—that *he* would carry the shard. Rafael, who carried guilt the way other men carried coin, counting it constantly, spending it nowhere, hoarding his own punishment. Rafael would stand in front of that throne and offer himself up like a man paying a debt, and he would mean it, and it would work, and then—

Then Ansel would be left alone again. On a road, in a world, with a family that had emptied itself out for him. He would be the boy who was saved, forever, by everyone else. He would spend his whole life being the reason someone else disappeared.

No.

He pressed the shard to his forehead, the way he'd seen it done in its own memories, and let it fill him with the picture of the road ahead: east, past the salt flats, into the ash passes, into the realm of glass forests and singing stone. Into the presence of a king who had forgotten his own name.

*I'll do it quietly*, he promised. *I'll do it fast, before anyone can stop me. And maybe—*

Maybe what? Maybe the forgetting wasn't total? Maybe some crumb of it would survive, some grain of sand at the bottom of the emptied hourglass? He was a child; he believed in maybes the way other children believed in dragons, completely and without evidence.

Maybe he would forget Rafael's face but keep the feeling of being loved. Maybe the shape of it would stay in his body the way the old tongue stayed, deeper than memory, below where the shard could reach. Maybe love wasn't stored in the head at all. Maybe it lived in the hands, in the feet, in the hollow of the throat—and those places belonged to him.

* * *

Footsteps on the stairs. He knew them before he knew anything else about them: the slight drag of the left foot, the pause on the fourth step where the board was loose. Rafael.

Ansel slid the shard into the pouch beneath his shirt—it slept against his sternum now, most nights, a second heart—and pulled a book off the shelf, opening it upside down. By the time the door opened he was the picture of a boy reading, lamplit and innocent, and if his eyes were too bright, well, children's eyes were often too bright.

"Still awake?" Rafael's voice, soft. He crossed the little room and righted the book without comment—"the letters go this way up, *sprout*"—and sat on the edge of the bed, and for a moment neither of them said anything. The rain filled the silence. Below, Odile's laughter rose through the floorboards, and someone's bottle clinked.

"You were in Lorena's room today," Rafael said.

Ansel went still.

"The third lock sticks," Rafael went on, mild as milk. "It always has. She thinks no one notices when it takes her two tries." He looked at Ansel then, and his eyes behind the spectacles were tired and kind and terribly perceptive, the eyes of a man who had spent five years noticing everything and surviving it. "You weren't stealing."

It wasn't a question, so Ansel didn't answer it. He shrugged, elaborately casual, a gesture he'd learned from Odile.

"She keeps memories in there," Rafael said. "Bottled. People sell them when remembering hurts too much." He paused. "Some of them are mine."

Ansel looked up sharply.

"A few," Rafael said, with a small crooked smile that didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "From before. Things I couldn't bear to carry across the sea. She's kept them for me, all these years, in case I ever wanted them back." He rubbed his ink-stained fingers together, the nervous habit Ansel knew meant a hard truth was coming. "I never have. Do you know why?"

Ansel shook his head.

"Because a man is what he remembers," Rafael said. "Every piece, even the bad ones. Especially the bad ones." He reached out and tucked the blanket up under Ansel's arms, the fussy mother-hen gesture that always made Odile snort. "Whatever you were looking for in those bottles, sprout—I hope you find it somewhere better."

He blew out the candle. At the door he paused, silhouetted against the hallway light, and said, quietly, almost to himself: "Whatever it costs. Whatever anyone offers to pay for you—you're not a price, Ansel. You're the point."

Then he was gone, and the dark came in soft as water.

Ansel lay awake a long time, the shard humming against his heart, Rafael's words turning over and over like stones in a stream. *You're the point.* As if Rafael knew. As if some part of the man had already seen the small determined shape of what Ansel intended, and was trying, blindly, to argue with the future.

*You're wrong*, Ansel told him silently, fiercely, into the dark. *I am a price. I'm the cheapest thing in this whole war. Five years of hiding, a smuggler's ship, a memory-broker's secrets, a king's freedom—all of it, bought with one small empty boy. That's not a sacrifice. That's a bargain.*

Below, the house settled into sleep. In the back room behind three locks, a hundred bottled memories glowed faintly in the dark, each one a life someone had chosen to put down.

Ansel put his hand flat against his chest, over the shard, over his own quick heartbeat, and practiced the hardest lesson of all: loving something enough to be ready to leave it.

Outside, far to the east, beyond the rain and the rooftops and the sleeping harbor, the ash passes waited. And in the morning, Odile would come up the stairs with the tide-tables in her hand and a grim set to her mouth, and say the words that would end all their hiding:

The usurper's armies were moving.
