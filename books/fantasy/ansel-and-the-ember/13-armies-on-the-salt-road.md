## 13. Armies on the Salt Road

The refugees came like weather.

Odile Vance had watched storms roll off the Salt Road for thirty years—the dust rising first, a brown haze on the horizon, then the sound, a low grinding murmur like the sea deciding to come ashore. She stood on the deck of the *Kestrel's Regret*, which was not currently regretting anything because it was currently beached in a farmer's fallow field two miles east of Harrowgate, and she watched the road fill with people.

They came on carts and on foot. They came with goats and without, with children slung on hips and bundles on backs, with the particular hollow-eyed shuffle of folk who had walked past the point of exhaustion and into something worse. An old man led a horse so thin its ribs showed through its hide like barrel staves. A woman carried a birdcage with no bird in it, and when Odile asked her why—because Odile asked everyone why, it was how she took the measure of a person—the woman said, "He'll come back. He always comes back," and walked on, and Odile decided not to sleep well that night.

"They're coming from the eastern valleys," Rafael said beside her. He had his hands flat on the rail, and Odile noticed—he was always doing this now—how carefully he kept them still, as if they might start drawing without his permission. "That's the second wave. The first wave was the border villages. This is everyone who waited to see if it would get better."

"And it got worse."

"It got worse."

Below them, on the trampled grass, Ansel sat cross-legged with a circle of refugee children around him. He couldn't speak to them—had spoken to almost no one since Harrowgate burned, since Odile had traded her ship for their lives and stood in the smoke watching the masts of the *Kestrel's Regret* go up like candles—but he didn't need to. He drew in the dirt with a stick, and the children leaned in, and whatever he drew made them laugh, or go quiet, or point east with small grim faces like generals planning campaigns. The shard hung at his throat on its leather cord, and even at this distance Odile could see the faint pulse of it, ember-red, keeping time with something.

With his heartbeat, Lorena said. With the king's, Rafael said. Odile figured it was with both, and that the distinction mattered less every day.

"Season's end," she said. "That's what the carter told me. The usurper's columns will reach the free cities by season's end. He said it the way you'd say *harvest's in* or *winter's coming*. Like a fact about the weather."

"Because to him it is one." Rafael's voice was flat. "Malvane doesn't need to hurry. The crown does the work. Every town it takes, it takes the memories of everyone in it—their fighting spirit, their grief, their reasons to resist—and feeds them into the army. His soldiers aren't men anymore, Odile. They're what's left after the men are eaten."

Odile had seen them. From the ridge above the Ash Passes, before they'd turned back, before the inquisitors and the burning of Harrowgate's lower quarter—she had seen the columns moving in the distance, and they had moved wrong. Not like soldiers. Like a tide. Like something poured rather than marched.

She spat over the rail, an old habit, mostly ceremonial. "So we run. That's the argument, isn't it? That's why you've been orbiting me all morning like a gull after fish guts. You want to know if I'll run."

"I want to know what you'll do," Rafael said. "There's a difference."

"There is. One of them flatters you."

* * *

They argued in the hold of the dead ship that evening, by lamplight, because Odile refused to argue in the open where the refugees could hear. A captain learns early: fear is cargo, and it leaks.

Lorena came with her ledger, because Lorena came with her ledger everywhere now; Odile suspected the woman slept with it. Rafael sat with his hands wrapped around a cup of tea he wasn't drinking. Ansel sat on a coil of rope near the ladder, ostensibly asleep, and Odile let him believe she believed it.

"Here's the arithmetic," Lorena said, tapping the ledger. "The free cities can field perhaps twelve thousand spears if every militia musters. Malvane's host is conservatively forty thousand, and it grows. Every city that falls feeds it. We cannot win a battle. We cannot win a war of attrition. We cannot even win a war of running, because there is nowhere left to run to—the coast, and then water, and the usurper's inquisitors have already been seen in the port towns buying passage."

"So we run faster," Odile said. "There are islands. There's always islands."

"For how long?" Rafael's voice was quiet, which was worse than loud. "A year? Five? I hid in Vell for five years, Odile. I know exactly what staying small buys you. It buys you five years, and then a knock at the door."

The lamp guttered. Outside, the murmur of the refugee camp went on, thousands of small sounds, coughing and settling and a baby crying somewhere with the tireless patience of babies.

"The unbinding rite is the only lever," Lorena said. "You know this. I know this. The drowned archive was clear: return the shard to its bearer, and the crown tears free, and everything the parasite holds—every bound soldier, every stolen memory—comes loose. It's not just freeing a king. It's cutting the puppet strings on forty thousand men at once."

"And the cost," Odile said.

Nobody answered for a moment, because the cost was sitting right there in the lamplight, wearing the shape of a small boy pretending to sleep.

"The bearer of the shard gives up every memory of themselves," Rafael said, as if reciting from a ledger of his own. "Everything. Not death—worse, maybe. A whole self, poured out. That's the price of the rite. That's what the archive said, and Lorena checked it three ways."

"I checked it five," Lorena said. "I was hoping I'd made an error. I don't make errors."

"So we find someone else," Odile said. "Some volunteer. Some dying old monk with nothing left to forget—"

"There's no such thing," Rafael said. "And even if there were, the shard has to be carried into the crown's presence. Into the throne room. Past Malvane, past the inquisitors, through an army of the hollowed. It's not a martyr's errand, it's an impossible one. The only person it's even *possible* for is—" He stopped.

"The child," Odile said. "The child who speaks the old tongue. The child the shard chose. The child who can walk through the singing stone and the glass forests because the land remembers him and he remembers it." She leaned back against the hull and looked up at the dark beams. "That's the arithmetic nobody wants to do, isn't it? The only key that fits the lock is a boy."

Ansel's eyes were open. She saw them in the dark, two points of reflected lamplight, and they weren't afraid. That was the thing that scared her most.

* * *

The argument proper happened three days later, on the road, because the camp couldn't hold. Harrowgate's council was moving the refugees west toward the coast, a slow river of the dispossessed, and Odile's crew—what was left of it, the ones who'd chosen her over the ship, gods keep them—had fallen into the work of shepherding because Odile couldn't stand by and watch old men die of thirst two miles from a well.

They argued on a hill above the road, where the wind took their voices.

"We go west," Odile said. "We get the boy to the coast, we get him on a boat, we take him somewhere Malvane's reach can't follow. The rite needs the shard near the crown—but it needs the *bearer* more, and as long as Ansel lives, there's hope. Dead hope is no hope."

"He'll never be safe," Lorena said. "The inquisitors found us in Harrowgate inside a week. They'll find us on an island. The shard is a beacon, Odile. It hums louder every day—I can feel it in my teeth. You can't outrun a sound."

"Then we bury it! We put it in a lead box at the bottom of the sea—"

"It doesn't work that way." Rafael had his back to them, looking east. The Salt Road unspooled behind them, brown with dust and people, and beyond the haze, very far off, the mountains that marked the Ember Realm's edge. "I've been reading what Lorena recovered. The shard isn't a thing the king *had*. It's a piece of what he *was*. You can't bury a man's name at the bottom of the sea and call him safe. It would just—" his hands moved, involuntary, sketching a shape in the air, a coastline, a border, "—it would just hurt him. Forever. Slowly. While we lived comfortably on an island."

"Comfortably," Odile repeated. "You want to talk to me about comfortable, Galloway? I sold my ship. I watched it burn with inquisitor fire on the decks I built with my own— " She stopped. Breathed. The wind off the eastern hills smelled of dust and, faintly, of ash. "I have paid, this year. I have paid more than most. So don't you stand there and tell me the only decent thing left is to walk a child into the usurper's throne room and let him pay *everything*."

"I'm not telling you that."

"Then what are you telling me?"

Rafael turned. And Odile saw it, and hated that she saw it: the cartographer coming up through the charm-seller's skin, the man who had once mapped the Ember Realm, whose eyes did a thing when they looked at a distance that other people's eyes didn't do. He wasn't seeing the road. He was seeing the shape of it, the way it *had* to go.

"I'm telling you the armies reach the free cities by season's end," he said. "I'm telling you there is no west. There's a delay, and then a drowning, and then the crown's reach crosses water eventually because it grows, Odile, it *grows*, and the world is only so big. I'm telling you that every day we run, the rite gets harder and the boy gets more afraid and the king gets emptier. And I'm telling you—" his voice cracked, just slightly, a hairline fracture in old masonry, "—I'm telling you that I already made this choice once. Five years ago. I chose the safe road, and I mapped a king into a cage. I will not choose it again. Not even to keep him alive. *Especially* not to keep him alive, if keeping him alive means giving Malvane the world."

The wind blew. Down on the road, the refugees flowed past like a river that had forgotten which way the sea was.

"And the boy?" Odile said. "Does he get a choice? Or is he cargo too? Is that what we are now, the three of us—smugglers and brokers and mapmakers, arguing over the freight?"

"He's standing right there," Lorena said quietly.

They all turned. Ansel stood a few paces down the hill, the shard pulsing at his throat, and he had been listening, of course he had been listening, he always listened, the way the Ember land listened, the way stone listened. He looked at each of them in turn. Then he crouched and drew in the dust with his finger, quick and sure, the way he drew everything.

A road. Long, winding, west to east across the flattened dirt, from a little square that might be Harrowgate to a jagged shape that might be mountains, and beyond them a crown, drawn with terrible precision for something scratched by a child's finger. And beside the road, walking it, two figures. One small. One tall, with careful lines for hands.

He looked up at Rafael. He pointed at the tall figure. Then at the road. Then, with a deliberation that made Odile's chest hurt, he pointed at the crown, and drew a line from the small figure's heart to it, and crossed the line out.

*Not me*, the drawing said. *Not yet. But the road goes east.*

Then he stood, and took Rafael's hand, and waited.

* * *

They voted, because Odile insisted on voting, because a captain's last dignity is procedure. Lorena for the east, Rafael for the east, Odile—after a long silence, after looking west down the road full of refugees, after looking at the boy holding the mapmaker's hand—for the east, with a curse attached.

"Season's end," she said, spitting into the wind. "Then we'd best move like the season's short. We'll need horses, and a guide through the Ash Passes, and a miracle, in roughly that order. And Galloway—" she jabbed a finger at him, "—if we do this, we do it *my* way on the road. No heroics. No standing on ridgelines admiring the terrain. You're a cargo escort now, and the cargo is a child and a piece of a king, and I have lost one ship this year and I will not lose the rest."

Rafael almost smiled. It looked strange on him, like a crack of light under a door. "Understood, Captain."

Ansel tugged Odile's sleeve. She looked down. He held up his slate, and on it, in the careful letters Rafael had been teaching him, he had written a single word.

*When?*

"Two days," Odile said. "Two days to provision, and then we ride east, into the teeth of it, and we don't stop until the singing stone."

The boy nodded, solemn as a magistrate, and turned to help load a cart, and Odile Vance stood on the hill above the Salt Road and watched the dust of the armies' coming stain the eastern sky the color of old rust, and thought: *I traded a ship for a war. Let's hope I'm a better smuggler of kings than I was a keeper of them.*

Behind her, faint under the wind, the shard began to hum.
