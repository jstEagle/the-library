## 15. Through the Ash Passes

The pass began where the maps ended.

Rafael had drawn this border once—had sat on a cold ridge with his compass and his ink and rendered the line between the free hills and the Ember Realm as a neat dashed stroke, the way cartographers do when they want a country to behave. He remembered thinking, five years ago, that the line was arbitrary. That the land on either side was the same land.

He had been wrong. The moment they crossed it, the world changed under their feet like a held breath released.

The air warmed. Not the damp warmth of lowlands but something drier, older, carrying a faint mineral sweetness, as if the stones themselves were exhaling after a long sleep. Ahead of them the road climbed through a corridor of black rock veined with threads of dull red light, pulsing slow as a heartbeat. Odile pulled up short at the first vein, her hand going to the knife at her hip out of pure habit.

"That's not lava," she said.

"No," Rafael said. "It's the realm remembering itself."

Ansel walked past them both without stopping. The child's head was tilted, listening to something Rafael could no longer hear, and his small mud-brown boots found the path as though he'd walked it every day of his life—which, in a sense that made Rafael's chest ache, he might have. The shard hung at Ansel's throat now, wrapped in leather and cord, and even through the wrapping Rafael could feel it: a warmth against his own skin, a pressure behind his eyes, like standing too near a struck bell.

They climbed for most of the morning. The switchbacks were old work, cut by hands that had known stone the way sailors know water, and here and there Rafael recognized the marks—surveyor's glyphs, the same system he'd been taught, the same system he'd used to betray this place. He kept his gloved fingers away from the walls. He didn't want to touch them. He didn't deserve to touch them.

* * *

By midday they came down into the first of the glass forests, and Odile swore softly, and Rafael understood why.

The trees were trees no longer—or perhaps they had never been trees at all. They rose thirty, forty feet from a floor of silvered ash, their trunks translucent as blown glass, their branches fanning into panes that caught the pale highland sun and scattered it across the ground in shifting coins of amber and rose. When the wind moved—and the wind did move, though nothing else in the forest stirred—the whole wood rang. A low, sweet chord, sustained, changing. It was not unpleasant. It was the sound a cathedral might make if a cathedral could sing to itself in its sleep.

"It's beautiful," Odile said, and then, because she was Odile: "What killed it?"

"Nothing," Rafael said. "Or everything. The Ember Realm doesn't distinguish between making and unmaking the way we do. When the king was bound—" His voice caught on the word *bound*, and he smoothed it flat. "When the binding happened, the land stopped being able to forget. Glass is what stone becomes when it can't let go of fire."

Ansel had stopped beneath the largest tree. He reached up—not touching, just reaching, fingertips an inch from the trunk—and hummed three notes.

The forest answered.

The chord shifted, deepened, resolved into something almost like speech. Rafael felt the hair rise on his arms. He knew enough of the old tongue to catch the shape of what passed between child and tree—a greeting, a question, an answer too long and too sad to translate—but Ansel turned back to them with tears running silently down his face, and pointed east, and walked on.

"What did it say?" Odile asked.

Rafael hesitated. "It asked him if he'd come home. And he said yes." He looked at the ground. "And then it told him how many years it had been waiting. I couldn't follow the number. It was very large."

Odile said nothing for a long while. Then: "Keep him close, Rafe. Things that wait that long don't always wait gently."

"I know," Rafael said. "I'm counting on it."

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow ringed by singing stone—boulders the size of cottages, hollow, resonant, humming to one another across the dark in voices so low Rafael felt them in his teeth rather than heard them. Odile took first watch without being asked; she had been taking first watch since Harrowgate, since the raid, since Ansel had shouted *Stay* in a voice that cracked and had meant it with his whole body, and none of them had spoken of it since. Some things you didn't speak of. You just kept watch over them.

Rafael sat beside the boy while he slept, or half-slept, turning restlessly against Odile's rolled cloak. In the firelight the shard's glow leaked through the leather wrapping, a thin seam of gold along Ansel's collarbone, rising and falling with his breath.

"You're dreaming about it," Rafael murmured. "Aren't you."

The boy's eyes opened. Not startled—Ansel was never startled anymore, not by Rafael, not by anything in this country. He looked at Rafael the way he looked at everything here: with the patient attention of someone reading a language everyone else had forgotten.

"I dreamed about it too," Rafael said. "For five years. A burning map. Every night, the same one. I thought it was guilt." He smiled, and it hurt. "Maybe it was just the realm calling collect."

Ansel sat up. He studied Rafael's face for a long moment, then reached out and took Rafael's hand—turned it palm up, traced the ink stains that no amount of scrubbing had ever fully lifted, the calluses of a pen-grip worn into the fingers. Then he pressed Rafael's hand flat against the ground, against the warm singing earth, and held it there, and looked up with an expression of such fierce, uncomplicated certainty that Rafael had to look away.

*I know what you did,* the gesture said. *The land knows. It kept your hands anyway.*

"I don't understand you," Rafael whispered. "I've never understood you. Why aren't you afraid of me?"

Ansel tilted his head. Then, slowly, deliberately, he drew in the ash between them: a stick figure with ink-dark fingers, holding a smaller figure's hand. Around them he drew a circle, and inside the circle, a crown with a crack through it.

Then he lay back down and went to sleep, as easily as a child putting down a toy, leaving Rafael alone with the drawing and the humming dark and the terrible suspicion that the boy had just forgiven him for something he hadn't confessed to yet—and that forgiveness, undeserved, was heavier than any accusation could ever be.

* * *

On the third day the passes narrowed, and the singing grew strange.

Rafael noticed it before the others: the chords had developed a discord, a wrongness threaded through the harmony, like a choir member gone hoarse. By noon the glass trees nearest the trail showed cracks running through their panes, spiderwebbing, weeping thin threads of red light that guttered instead of pulsed. By late afternoon the ash underfoot was hot.

"The crown's reach," he said quietly. "It extends this far. Everything the parasite touches starts to... fray. The realm can't hold its own shape near it."

"So we're walking toward the thing that's killing the music," Odile said.

"Yes."

"And you're sure the boy knows the way."

Rafael watched Ansel walk ahead of them, small and straight-backed, following a path that appeared to exist only for him—turning left where the trail split and finding, ten paces on, that a way opened through rock that had looked solid. Twice that day the landscape had simply rearranged itself around the child: a ridge that had blocked their view at dawn was passable by dusk, a ravine that should have cost them hours was bridged by a spine of stone that hummed welcome under their boots. The realm was making room for him. Or he was asking it to, in a tongue it had been starving for.

"He doesn't need to know the way," Rafael said. "He *is* the way. The realm remembers its own."

"And does it remember you?"

The question landed harder than Odile probably intended. Rafael was quiet for several steps.

"When I mapped this place," he said finally, "I did it properly. That's the worst part, Odile. I loved it. Every surveyor's mark, every glyph—I copied them faithfully, beautifully. Malvane's people only needed my map because my map was *true*. If I'd been lazy, if I'd been careless, the binding ritual would have failed. The king would have—" He stopped. Started again. "So yes. I think it remembers me. I think that's exactly what frightens me."

Ahead, Ansel had stopped. He stood at the mouth of a defile where two walls of black glass leaned together like praying hands, and beyond them, far below, Rafael could see it: a valley floor stitched with rivers of ember-light, and rising from its center, impossibly distant and impossibly clear in the crystalline air, a spire. White stone shot through with veins of dying gold. The usurper's seat. The throne where a king sat bound inside his own body, wearing a crown that ate him alive.

Ansel looked at the spire for a long time. Then he looked back at Rafael, and Odile, and last at the shard against his chest, which he touched with both hands the way other children touched amulets of saints.

The discord in the singing stone swelled around them, and beneath it—so faint Rafael might have imagined it—came another sound. Thin, ragged, human. A voice carried on the resonance of the pass itself, traveling through miles of singing rock the way a scream travels down a well.

Rafael froze. He knew that voice. He had transcribed its edicts once, five years ago, in a scriptorium that smelled of ink and shame.

"It's him," he breathed. "The king. He's—he's trying to speak through the stone. He's been trying for years, and the realm carries it, and no one's been close enough to hear—"

Ansel was already moving toward the sound, quick and certain, and the pass opened before him like a door.

Odile caught Rafael's sleeve. "Rafe. Whatever's down there—it knows we're coming now. That song goes both ways."

"I know," Rafael said. He looked at the spire, and at the boy disappearing into the defile, and felt the old burning map rise behind his eyes—not as nightmare now, but as instruction. Every line of it. Every mark of his own faithful, traitorous hand.

"Good," he said. "Let it listen. This time, I'm the one bringing the truth through."

They followed the child down into the valley, and all around them the cracked glass forest sang its broken chord, and somewhere ahead a bound king waited to learn whether anyone had finally come to answer him.
