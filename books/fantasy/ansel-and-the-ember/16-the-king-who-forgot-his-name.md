## 16. The King Who Forgot His Name

He had been a king once. He was almost certain of it.

The certainty came and went like weather. Some hours—were they hours? The crown did not permit him clocks, or mirrors, or any of the small honest instruments by which a man measures his captivity—it sat heavy on his brow and he could not have said his own name if a priest had held a blade to his throat and demanded it. Other times, in the thin gray intervals when the parasite slept or fed elsewhere or simply grew bored of him, something surfaced from the drowned dark of him: *Aldric.* A name like a stone dropped into deep water. He would hold it for as long as he could, turning it over, feeling its weight, before the water closed over it again.

*Aldric.* He practiced it now, the way a starving man practices the shape of bread.

He sat—he thought he was sitting; his body obeyed him so rarely that he had learned to think of it as a house he rented from an unfriendly landlord—in the high seat of the Ember throne room, and he watched the light come through the glass forest beyond the open colonnade. That was one mercy the crown had never thought to take: the windows. Perhaps it did not occur to the thing that a prisoner might find freedom in a view. Or perhaps it knew precisely what it was doing, letting him see the singing stone and the amber woods every day of his long ruin, letting him look at everything he had failed.

The glass trees chimed in the wind. They always had. When he was crowned—the true crowning, the one with fire and oaths and ten thousand voices singing the old speech—he had stood on the balcony with his young queen's hand in his and listened to the whole realm ring like a struck bell, and he had thought: *no man deserves this. No man could ever deserve this.*

He had been right. That was the bitter arithmetic of it. No man deserved the Ember Realm, and he had proven it by losing it.

*You are thinking again,* said the crown.

It did not speak aloud. It had no need. It lived against his skull the way a leech lives against skin, and its voice arrived inside his thoughts already wearing his own intonation, which was the cruelest part. When it spoke, he heard himself agreeing.

*I am always thinking,* Aldric answered. It was one of the few rebellions left to him, this dialogue. The crown permitted it because it amused itself with his resistance the way a cat amuses itself with a wounded bird. *You cannot stop me thinking. You've tried.*

*Thinking is how I feed,* the crown said, almost fondly. *Every memory you claw back from me costs you more than it costs me. You are a larder, little king. Larders do not argue with the cook.*

He let it go. There was no winning these exchanges, only surviving them, and survival—for five years, five years of gray intervals and stolen names—had become its own stubborn discipline. He turned instead to the window, to the glass forest, and did what he did every day when the parasite's attention wandered: he took inventory.

The realm was dying. He could see it even from here, even through the crown's dampening haze. The glass trees nearest the palace still sang, but their song had gone thin, reedy, a choir running out of breath. Farther east the passes had gone silent; the smugglers' rumors that drifted up to him secondhand, through guards who did not know he could hear, spoke of ash where the ember-orchards had been, of rivers running warm and dead. Each memory the crown devoured from him, it seemed to take a tithe from the land as well. The binding had been made that way—crown to king, king to realm, three strands of one rope—and whatever strangled him strangled everything.

*And whose fault is that?* the crown murmured, lazy, needling. *You signed the map yourself. Well. You signed the man who drew it.*

Aldric went very still.

That was new. The crown rarely spoke of the cartographer. In five years it had mentioned him perhaps a handful of times, always with a strange carefulness, the way a thief speaks of the one witness it failed to kill. *Galloway,* Aldric said into his own dark. *Rafael Galloway.*

The name surfaced whole, and he clutched at it. Strange—that one he had never lost. The crown had eaten his mother's face, his wedding day, the sound of his daughter laughing (he believed he had had a daughter; there was a hollow in him shaped exactly like her laughter), but it had left the cartographer untouched, and Aldric had spent years wondering why before he understood: the crown kept Galloway the way a jailer keeps the key. The map was the crown's founding crime. It needed the memory of it intact, needed the guilt of it intact, because guilt was a leash, and the crown had learned early that a king who hated himself did not fight.

*There he is,* the crown said, savoring. *Do you know what I feel, little king? Do you know what I taste, this morning?*

Aldric did not answer. But he felt it too. That was the horror of their intimacy: after five years welded skull to skull, some of the thing's sensations bled backward into him, and what bled through now was—

Hunger. A vast, rising, animal hunger, and beneath it, unbelievably, fear.

The crown was afraid.

In five years he had never known it to be afraid. It had fed on his griefs like a glutton at a wedding feast. It had worn his body to war councils and executions and had never once flinched. And now something moved through its thoughts like a fishhook through water, and the thing that wore him gripped the arms of his own throne with his own hands until the knuckles went white, and Aldric realized he was feeling, filtered through the parasite's panic, a sensation he had nearly forgotten:

Hope. Someone else's hope, arriving like weather from far away.

*What is it,* he asked. Not a question. A demand. His voice—his real voice, unused for so long that it cracked in his chest like river ice—came out ragged. *What are you afraid of?*

*Nothing,* said the crown, too quickly.

*You lie,* Aldric said, and laughed—a terrible sound, rusty, half sob. *You lie! Five years you have told me I am nothing, I am a larder, I am a husk, and now something walks toward us that makes you lie to me.* He surged forward inside himself, against the bindings, against the gray fog the crown kept poured over his mind like a lid over a well. For one blazing moment he broke the surface. He saw the throne room whole and sharp: the banners of the usurper's gray host hanging where the ember-banners should be, the guards at the doors wearing Malvane's colors, and beneath all of it, humming up through the singing stone itself, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall—

*a bell. A shard of something ringing like a bell, coming from the west, coming closer.*

*What is it?* he whispered.

The crown slammed the lid down. The gray flooded back, warm and numbing, and Aldric sank beneath it—but not before the thing answered him, not in words but in a single leaked image, unguarded, torn loose by its fear: a fragment of crystal turning over and over in the dark, singing, and around it a darkness that recoiled.

A shard of the true crown.

His crown. The one Malvane's sorcerers had shattered on the altar-stone the night of the binding, the pieces scattered to the four winds so that no lawful rite could ever be sung again. One piece had survived them. One piece was moving toward him through the world, carried by hands he could not see, and the parasite that had eaten his life was terrified of it.

*No,* the crown said, and for the first time in five years its voice was not lazy, not fond, not amused. It was thin. *No. It is nothing. A rumor. A trinket.*

*It sings,* Aldric said. *I can hear it sing.*

*You hear nothing. You are meat. You are a coat I wear.*

*I am the king,* Aldric said, and the words came out of him with a force that surprised them both, ringing off the inside of his own skull, off the walls of the prison that was himself. *I am the King Under the Glass, and that is my crown, and it is coming home.*

* * *

They fought then, silently, the way they had not fought since the first year—since the early days when he still had strength to rage and the crown still had patience to mock him. It pressed the gray down and he rose through it. It showed him his losses, paraded the hollows where his memories had been, the daughter-shaped silence, the wife-shaped absence, saying *see, see, there is nothing left in you worth saving,* and he wept, because it was largely true, and he rose anyway.

Because here was the thing the crown had never understood about hungering men: take everything from them and you do not make them quiet. You make them patient. You make them into creatures with a single tooth, and all their patience sharpens that one tooth.

*I remember something,* Aldric said at last, when the struggle had burned down to embers on both sides. The crown, exhausted, wary, did not answer, and he went on anyway, feeling his way through the fog like a man walking a familiar road blindfolded. *I remember the night of the binding. You keep that one bright—you make me watch it sometimes, when you want to break me. The altar-stone. The sorcerers. Malvane smiling with my mouth's smile. And the cartographer kneeling in the corner with his maps, not knowing what he'd drawn.*

*Yes,* the crown said slowly. *I remember it perfectly. It is my favorite meal.*

*Then you will remember what the sorcerers said. What the ritual required.* He gathered the words carefully, each one a stone laid across a chasm. *A binding needs two anchors. The crown to hold the king. And the king's consent—not freely given, no, you stole that—but given. Spoken. The rite is a lock with two tumblers, and you are only one of them.*

Silence. Long, dangerous silence. Through it Aldric felt the parasite doing arithmetic of its own, and he pressed on, gently now, almost kindly, the way one speaks to a frightened animal.

*You have fed on me for five years and you still do not understand what you swallowed. A king is not a storehouse, little worm. A king is a vow. And vows outlive the tongue that spoke them.* He let the truth rise in him like dawn. *Something is coming that knows the other tumbler. Something is coming that can turn the lock from the outside. And you brought it here yourself—you wore my body to your wars, you spent my realm like coin, and every step of your conquest has been a step along the road back to this throne room. You built the road, and now it walks toward you, and it is singing.*

*Stop,* the crown said.

*You should have killed the cartographer,* Aldric said. *You should have burned every map. You were greedy. You wanted the man who could draw roads, because roads are useful to thieves. And somewhere behind him, following the ink like a scent, comes—*

*STOP.*

But it was too late, and they both knew it. The fear had passed fully into him now, and fear was a key of its own kind. Beneath the gray, beneath the five lost years, Aldric reached down into the deepest water of himself, past the crown's hooks, past the eaten places, down to the bedrock where the crown could not dig without collapsing the mine it lived in—and he found what he had hidden there on the first night, in the last hour of his freedom, when the sorcerers were coming up the stairs and he had perhaps a minute left of being a man.

He found the name of the unbinding.

Not the words. The words he had never known. But the *place* of them—the door in himself where such knowledge would live if he ever heard it, sealed with his own blood and his own final free act of will, a locked room built in desperation by a drowning king. He had forgotten making it. The crown had never found it, because the crown looked for treasure, and he had buried a door.

*It exists,* Aldric breathed. *Somewhere in the world, someone knows the words. And when they come—and they are coming, I can hear the bell—I will be able to recognize them. To open to them. To help them.*

The crown did not rage. That was the worst of it. It went terribly, intelligently quiet, and when it spoke again its voice had returned to its old fond laziness, which meant it had found a countermove.

*Very well, little king. Ring your bell. Open your door.* A pause, silk over stone. *But consider: whoever carries the shard must give up every memory of themselves to unbind me. Every one. That is the price of the rite—they will stand before you and pour themselves out like water, and the man or child who frees me will not know they freed me, will not know their own name by sundown. So I ask you, King Under the Glass: what will you do with a freedom bought by someone else's entire self? How will you bear a throne built on a stranger's grave of everything they were?*

And that, of all its cruelties across five years, was the cut that went deepest—because Aldric, even bound, even hollowed, even less than a shadow of what he had been, knew himself well enough to know the answer.

He would bear it. He would carry it every day of his restored life, the way he carried the view of the dying glass forest, the way he carried the name *Aldric* through the gray water. Freedom purchased with another's memory would be a debt no throne could weigh, and he would spend the rest of his reign trying to pay it, and he would die owing it, and that was still—*still*—better than this.

*Then we understand each other,* he said quietly. *Come, then, whoever you are. Come quickly. I have been a house too long. I would like, before the end, to be a man.*

Beyond the colonnade, the glass forest stirred though there was no wind, ten thousand trees chiming at once, a sound like the realm clearing its throat after five years of silence. The guards at the doors shifted and muttered and looked at one another, and did not know why the hair was rising on their arms.

Far to the west, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, the shard rang on—and something beside it, something patient and ink-stained, walked the road home.
