## 17. Gates of the Usurper's Court

They took his boots first.

Rafael did not understand why until they marched him across the obsidian causeway toward the usurper's court, barefoot on stone that drank the heat of the sun and gave nothing back. The inquisitors had learned something in five years of hunting mages: that a man's power lived in his hands, yes, but also in his feet. A cartographer read the ground beneath him. A barefoot man on black glass felt only pain, and pain crowded out everything else.

So he let it hurt. He counted the steps instead—four hundred and eleven from the gate to the outer wall—and made himself a map of the palace he was being dragged into, because making maps was the only prayer he had left.

The Ember Realm had been beautiful once. He knew this not from memory but from the shard's dreams, which leaked into his sleep now every night: forests of green glass chiming in the wind, rivers that ran warm as blood and bright as copper, cities grown rather than built, their towers spiraling out of living stone like the horns of sleeping rams. What remained of that beauty surrounded him now in ruins. The glass forests outside the passes had gone gray and brittle, singing only when the wind broke them. The road they had been taken on was paved with fragments of it, and Ansel had wept silently the whole way, each jolt of the prison wagon grinding old songs into silence beneath the wheels.

Ansel. Odile. He did not know where they had been taken. That was the cruelty of it—the inquisitors had separated them at the pass with the efficiency of men who had done it a thousand times, and Rafael had shouted himself hoarse against a gag while a boy of perhaps ten years looked back at him once, calm as still water, and mouthed something he could not read.

He told himself it had not been goodbye.

* * *

The gates of the court were iron, which was itself an obscenity here. Iron did not belong in the Ember Realm; the old tongue had no word for it. The inquisitors had hauled ingots of it across the ash passes for years, Rafael realized, dragging the language of chains into a country that had never needed them. The gates groaned open on hinges that screamed, and beyond them the court of King Malvane opened like a wound.

It had been a courtyard once—he could see the bones of it, the fountain basin cracked and dry, the colonnade scorched to charcoal skeletons. Now it was a drill yard. Rank upon rank of soldiers stood in the gray light, and Rafael's cartographer's eye catalogued them before his fear could stop it: two thousand, maybe three, in black lacquered plate, motionless as the glass trees. Not resting. Not waiting. *Standing*, in perfect formation, in perfect silence, the way objects stand when no one is animating them.

His stomach turned over slowly, the way a boat turns in heavy swell.

They're bound too. All of them. The crown doesn't have an army. It has a body.

"Move," said the inquisitor behind him, and prodded him between the shoulder blades with a rod of cold iron, and Rafael moved.

* * *

The throne room had been the great hall of the summer palace, if the faded murals on the surviving walls could be believed. He caught glimpses of them as they marched him down its length: processions of robed figures, a king with a laurel of copper leaves, children releasing lights into a dark sky like paper lanterns. Someone had defaced them. Every face in every mural had been scraped smooth, so that the whole history of a nation stared at nothing with blank, featureless eggs of stone.

At the far end, on a dais of black basalt, sat the throne.

And on the throne sat King Malvane.

Rafael had seen him once before, five years ago, from the edge of a coronation crowd in Vell's sister city—a broad, ruddy man with a soldier's laugh, a general who had promised to hold the border while the true king went east to treat with the Ember Realm. He remembered thinking: there is a man who will die for his country. He had even drawn the man's portrait in the margin of a survey map, laughing, holding a cup aloft.

The thing on the throne wore that man's body the way a glove wears a hand.

It was thinner. That was the first wrongness. The broad shoulders had collapsed inward, the soldier's flesh melted off the frame until the armor of state—black enameled plate worked with silver filigree—hung on him like a child's costume on a scarecrow. The skin of his face was stretched and waxy, and his eyes were open, and they were the worst part, because they were *aware*. Somewhere behind them, deep down in the drowned cellars of that skull, Rafael was certain, a general who had once laughed over a cup of wine was screaming, or praying, or simply watching, as Rafael had watched, helpless, through five years of dreams.

And above the ruined brow, set into hair that had gone white as ash—

The crown.

It did not look like a crown. That was the second wrongness, and it made Rafael's breath go shallow, because he had helped design the binding and even he had never seen the finished thing. It looked like a root system. Black tendrils, fine as wire, sank into the king's scalp at a dozen points, spreading down his temples, along his jaw, vanishing beneath the collar of his breastplate. They pulsed faintly, rhythmically, and Rafael understood with a lurch that he was looking at a heartbeat that was not the king's own. The crown was drinking. It had been drinking for five years, and the man beneath it was nearly empty.

"Well." The voice came from Malvane's throat, but it did not fit the mouth. It was layered, resonant, a chord struck on an instrument built for single notes. "The cartographer."

Rafael was forced to his knees on the basalt. The impact sang up his shins. He kept his eyes down—not from humility, but because he had learned in Vell that the safest lie is the one closest to the truth, and the truth was that he had come as a supplicant, more or less, and supplicants knelt.

"Your Majesty," he said. His voice came out steadier than he'd expected. Five years of selling charms to suspicious customers had taught him how to sound like a harmless man. "I was summoned."

"You were *captured*." The layered voice seemed amused. "Crossing my ash passes with a smuggler's papers, a stolen wagon, and—" a pause, delicate as a knife laid flat against skin, "—a child who speaks the dead language."

"They say many things about me," Rafael said. "Most of them flatter my importance."

Silence. He risked raising his eyes.

Malvane had risen from the throne. The movement was wrong—all of it was wrong, joints moving a half-beat late, the body steering around its own occupant—but what froze Rafael's blood was the way the crown's tendrils *tightened* as the thing descended the dais, flexing like the fingers of a hand preparing to grip.

"The Ember Realm remembers you, Rafael Galloway." It stopped three paces away, close enough that he could smell it—ozone and old blood and something sweetly rotten, like fruit left too long in a sealed room. "You walked our glass forests. You sat with our loremasters. You learned enough of the old speech to draw our roads." The waxen face bent toward him. "And then you drew one more thing. A circle. A cage. A map of a king's mind, with all its doors marked."

"I was deceived," Rafael said. "I thought I was drawing a ward against invasion. A protection."

"You were drawing a *lock*," the thing said, "and you were paid in gold, and you spent it, and you ran." The chord-voice softened, which was infinitely worse. "Do you know what I have wondered, these five years? Whether you knew. Whether some small part of your clever mind understood what the lock was for. Because a lock is such a specific thing to draw, cartographer. Doors do not need marking unless someone means to keep something *in*."

Rafael said nothing. There was nothing to say that the thing would believe, or that he could bear to offer. He knelt on the hot stone and let the silence be his confession, and somewhere in the ranks of bound soldiers a single helmeted head turned toward him with mechanical slowness, and turned back.

"But none of that matters now," the crown said, almost gently. "Locks are only interesting when there is a key. And you, Galloway—you've been carrying a key across my country for weeks."

The cold that went through Rafael then had nothing to do with fear of death. *It knows.* Of course it knew. The shard hummed like a struck bell; the crown was a bell of the same casting. They had probably felt each other since the ash passes, two halves of a harmony calling across the miles.

"I don't know what you mean," he said.

The thing wearing Malvane crouched. Up close, Rafael could see that the whites of the king's eyes were threaded with fine black filaments, rootlets of the crown growing inward, year by year, toward whatever light still burned at the center of the man. It raised one gauntleted hand—it moved it with visible effort, like a puppeteer hauling a marionette against its strings—and held it an inch from Rafael's cheek. Cold poured off the metal.

"Your pockets were emptied at the gate," it said. "Your wagon searched. Your companions questioned separately, thoroughly." A pause. "And yet when my inquisitors reached into the child's bundle, they found bread, a tin whistle, and a carved wooden horse. No shard. No ember-glass. Nothing that sings." The layered voice dropped to almost a whisper, and Rafael heard, threaded through the resonance, something new—something thin and high and unmistakable.

Fear.

"So either the stories are wrong," the crown said, "and the key was never found. Or you hid it. Or—" and here the thing's composure slipped, just barely, a crack running through the chord, "—or the key has hidden *itself*. Which the old texts said it might. Which the old texts said it *would*, in the hands of—" It stopped.

In the hands of a speaker. In the hands of one born to the old tongue. In the hands of a child the Ember Realm itself had chosen, the way a country chooses a messenger, the way a dying king chooses where to hide his heart.

Rafael lifted his head.

He had spent five years being small. He had spent five years perfecting the shopkeeper's smile, the harmless hands, the art of staying beneath notice. But kneeling on the scorched floor of a murdered kingdom, in front of the thing that had eaten a king and worn his friend's body like a coat, Rafael Galloway felt something kindle in his chest that he had not felt since the burning map, since the night he understood what his beautiful circles had built.

It was not bravery. It was better than bravery. It was *leverage*.

Because the crown was afraid. This thing that had devoured a realm, that stood three thousand soldiers like furniture around it, that had scraped the faces off history itself—this thing was afraid of a wooden horse and a tin whistle and a child's empty bundle. It had torn the country apart looking for a piece of singing glass, and it had not found it, and it did not know why, and not knowing was eating it alive from the inside of its stolen skull.

"You're frightened," Rafael said quietly.

The court went utterly still. Even the pulse of the crown's tendrils seemed to falter.

"I have seen what you actually are," Rafael went on, and his voice grew stronger with each word, the voice of a man reading aloud from a map he alone could see. "You're not a conqueror. Conquerors want things—land, tribute, crowns. You don't want. You *need*. You need memories the way a leech needs blood, and the well is running dry, isn't it? Five years of drinking a king dry, and now you're scraping the bottom, and the armies you animate are costing you more than they earn you. And somewhere out there is one small piece of the true crown, one shard of what you were before you became—*this*—and if it comes back to the bearer, everything you've swallowed comes back up. Everything. Five years. A whole kingdom's memory. Him." He nodded at the ruined face above him. "You'd have to give him back."

The thing wearing Malvane rose to its full height, and for a moment the whole hall trembled, and the pressure coming off it was like standing under a waterfall of cold. The bound soldiers shuddered in their ranks, a thousand bodies twitching on a thousand invisible strings.

"I could unmake you," it said, "in ways your body would take years to finish."

"Yes," Rafael agreed. "But then you'd never learn where the shard is. Or whether the child speaks. Or what I told him. Or what he told *me*." He smiled, and it was the shopkeeper's smile, honed on five years of wary customers, and it was the most dangerous thing he had ever done. "You didn't bring me here to kill me. You brought me here because I'm the only person in the world who has touched both the crown and the key. You brought me here because you're desperate. And I know something about desperation, Majesty—I drew your lock, remember? I know exactly how afraid you were, five years ago, when you paid a tired mapmaker to build your cage. You were afraid of him even then. You've been afraid of him ever since."

For a long moment, the only sound in the hall was the wind worrying the broken glass forests outside, a vast faint chime, like a country trying to remember its own name.

Then the crown laughed. It was a terrible sound, the chord-voice fracturing into its separate notes, some of them high and thin and almost human.

"Ohh," it breathed. "Oh, he sent me a *brave* one. After all these years of cowards." The waxen face tilted. "Very well, cartographer. You will stay. As my guest. You will dine at my table and walk my gardens and tell me, in your own time, everything you know about keys and the children who carry them. And when you have told me everything—" the black filaments in the king's eyes pulsed, hungry, patient, "—I will decide what is left of you worth keeping. Perhaps your hands. Cartographers' hands are such precise instruments. I have a use for precise instruments."

Guards took his arms—living guards, he noted distantly, the only unbound men he'd seen, which meant the crown trusted flesh more than puppets for this—and marched him from the hall, past the faceless murals, past the fountain where children had once released lights into the dark.

And it was as they passed the dais, as he was dragged with his back to the throne, that Rafael saw it.

A door, low in the wall behind the throne, half-hidden by a hanging banner of black silk. And beside the door, on a plain iron hook, hung a small bundle—a child's bundle, roughly searched and tossed aside, worthless to inquisitors.

Except that the flap of it had fallen open, and inside, nestled among the bread and the tin whistle, something glowed. Faintly. Warmly. Like a coal breathing.

Like a shard pretending to be a lump of river stone, humming its silent note, waiting.

Ansel was here. Ansel was *here*, somewhere in this fortress of iron and ash, and the shard had stayed with him, hidden itself in his keeping, exactly as the old texts promised—and the crown did not know. The crown had searched the bundle and seen nothing, because the shard showed itself only to those it chose, and it had chosen a mute child who spoke the language of the dying land.

Rafael kept his eyes forward. He did not stumble. He did not smile. He walked out of the throne room of the usurper with his bare, ink-stained hands open at his sides, and in his chest a map assembled itself, line by line, door by door: the hall, the dais, the banner, the low door, the hook.

Tomorrow they would feed him at the usurper's table. Tomorrow the thing would ask its questions, patient and poisonous, and begin to suspect that the cartographer's courage had a shape, a source, a reason.

But tonight—tonight, somewhere above or below or behind that low door—a child was waiting, holding a piece of a king's heart in a bundle of bread and toys, and neither of them knew yet that help had arrived.

*Ride toward us*, Rafael prayed, to Odile, to Lorena, to anyone the fates still owed a debt. *We only need one moment. One door. One pair of small hands.*

Behind him, faint as a struck bell, the shard sang.
