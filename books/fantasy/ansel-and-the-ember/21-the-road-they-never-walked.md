## 21. The Road They Never Walked

The paper was the best thing in the house.

Ansel knew this the way he knew the weight of rain coming, the way he knew the smell of a storm before the sky darkened—without knowing how he knew. The paper was thick and cream-colored, ragged at one edge where the mill had caught it wrong, and it held the light of the window the way water holds the moon. Rafael had given him three sheets of it that morning, along with a stub of charcoal and a stick of red ochre, and had said, in the careful voice he always used, the voice that was kind the way a bandage is kind: *Draw whatever you like.*

Ansel did not like anything. Liking required a shape, a name, a place in the chest where a feeling could sit down and stay. What he had instead was a restlessness, a humming under his skin like a struck bell that would not finish ringing, and so he sat at the table by the window with the paper in front of him and let his hand move.

His hand knew things.

This was the strangest part of being Ansel, though he had no way of comparing it to anything else, since he could not remember being anyone else. His hands knew things his head did not. They knew how to fold a blanket so the seams vanished. They knew how to hold a cup of tea so the heat spread into palms that were always, always cold. They knew the alphabet Rafael was teaching him, mostly, though sometimes they wrote letters in a different alphabet entirely—curved, urgent, beautiful—and Ansel would look down at the page and find words he could not read staring back at him like strangers at a window.

Rafael never scolded him for it. He would go very still, the way a man goes still when a wild bird lands near him, and then he would smile with his mouth while his eyes did something else entirely, something that looked like grief wearing a smile as a mask.

Ansel had learned to watch people's eyes. It was how he compensated. Words floated past him like leaves on water—*breakfast, letters, careful, Ansel*—and he caught the ones he could, but the eyes never lied, and Rafael's eyes were always, always sad.

He did not know why. He only knew that when Rafael left the room, the cold in Ansel's hands got worse.

* * *

The charcoal moved.

Ansel watched it the way one watches a divining rod, half curious, half afraid of what it might find. It started with a line—a long, confident horizontal stroke—and then another line beneath it, and then the lines began to branch, and Ansel realized, with a feeling like stepping off a stair that isn't there, that he was drawing a road.

Not *a* road. *The* road.

The charcoal knew it. The charcoal flew. It laid down the road the way a river lays down a valley, without hesitation, without correction, and the road had fords and forks and a place where it split around a hill that Ansel's hand drew before his mind could object, a hill with a shrine on top, a small square shrine, and he had never seen a shrine like that in Harrowgate, had never seen one anywhere that he could remember, which was nowhere, which was the whole world, which was nothing.

He kept going.

The paper filled. Roads braided off the main one like roots. A river crossed at a place marked with three quick strokes that his hand insisted meant *the water sings here.* A town appeared—walls, a gate, a crooked tower—and beneath it his hand wrote a word in the strange curved alphabet, and then, wobbling and careful, in the letters Rafael had taught him: *VELL.*

Vell. The word sat on the page and hummed.

Ansel put his palm flat against it. The paper was cool. The paper was only paper. But somewhere behind his eyes, behind the place where memory should have been and was only smooth white fog, something answered—a bell-tone, a single clear note like the one a wineglass sings when a wet finger finds its rim—and for a moment he saw, or almost saw, or remembered without remembering: mud. Rain on mud. A door. His own small fist, knocking.

Knocking what? A rhythm. His hand began to tap the table before he could stop it. *Da-da-DA. Da-da-DA.* Three and two. Three and two. A knock like a sentence, like a question, like *let me in, let me in.*

The door of the house was behind him. He did not turn around. He was afraid that if he turned around, the rhythm would stop, and the rhythm was the only thing in the world that felt like it belonged to him.

* * *

Rafael came in with the midday bread and stopped so suddenly that the door banged the wall.

Ansel looked up. He had learned to read Rafael's face the way he read the weather, and what he read now was a storm arriving all at once—no build, no warning clouds, just the whole sky breaking open. Rafael set the bread down without looking at it. His eyes went to the paper, and Ansel watched them move the way a man's eyes move when reading, left to right, following the road, and with every inch the sadness in them changed into something else, something that hurt to look at, something that was not sad at all but its opposite, its wild and terrified opposite.

Hope. Ansel had seen hope once, on a dog in the market, watching a butcher's hands. He recognized it now.

"You drew this," Rafael said. His voice came out wrong, cracked down the middle.

Ansel nodded. Then, because Rafael had taught him that words were gifts you gave to people, and because the man's face was asking so hard that it hurt, he said, "The road. I knowed it." He frowned. "I knew it. I don't know how I knew it."

Rafael pulled out the other chair. It scraped. He sat down slowly, like an old man, though he was not old, though his hands when he reached across the table were steady as a mason's. Ink-stained hands. Ansel had noticed them the first day and noticed them every day since—the stains lived in the creases and under the nails and would not wash out, blue-black, like the feathers of a crow. Rafael always apologized for them, said *a cartographer's shame, they never come clean,* and Ansel had learned to say *I don't mind* because it was true, though he could not have said why it was true, only that the stains felt like something he had a right to see.

"Tell me the road," Rafael said. "From the beginning. Where does it start?"

Ansel looked down at the map. The beginning was at the bottom left, where the charcoal had started, a small dense knot of streets. "There," he said, and touched Vell. "The mud door." His finger traced. "Then the singing water. Then the hill with the—" he searched for the word, found the curved alphabet on the page instead, and read it without knowing how, the sounds coming up out of him like fish rising to feed, "*—the shrine of the second ford. Then the long dry, where you can't stop. Then the pass.*" His finger reached the top of the page, where the charcoal had drawn, without his permission, a range of mountains rendered in tight hatched strokes, and beyond them a shape that was not a shape, a suggestion, a country drawn in a single unbroken line like a coastline or a wound. "Then there."

"What's there?"

Ansel opened his mouth and what came out was not common tongue. It was the other language, the curved one, the one that lived in his mouth the way his teeth lived in his gums, and it said something long and rolling and sad and true, and he heard it leave him and did not understand a single word of it, and neither, he saw, did Rafael—and yet Rafael's eyes filled up with water so fast that one tear fell and struck the table, and he pressed his knuckles to his mouth and said, in a whisper, in a voice like a man praying in an empty church:

"That's the Ember Realm. That's its name. In its own tongue." He breathed. "You just named it. You named it the way it names itself."

Ansel looked at the man. He looked at the map. He looked at his own hand, which was stained gray with charcoal to the wrist, and he thought: *my hand knows a country my head has never seen.*

"Did I live there?" he asked.

Rafael was quiet for a long time. The bread sat between them, going cold. Somewhere outside, Harrowgate went on being Harrowgate—gulls, gulls, a cart, the bell of the memory-brokers' hall ringing the hour—and the light moved on the paper, and the road lay there, perfect, patient, waiting.

"No," Rafael said at last. And then, because his eyes had never once lied to Ansel, he added, softly, "But you walked it. Every mile. With someone."

"Who?"

Rafael looked at his own ink-stained hands, and folded them, and hid them under the table, and said, "Someone who loved you very much."

* * *

That night Ansel could not sleep.

He lay in the narrow bed under the eaves and listened to the house settle and the wind worry the shutters, and the map lay on the table below, he knew it did, he could feel it the way you feel a tooth that's been pulled—a presence by its absence. The fog behind his eyes had a shape now. That was the terrible thing. Before tonight the fog had been simply fog, and fog is nothing, and nothing cannot hurt you. But now the fog had the shape of a road in it, a road-shaped hole, a hole the exact size and length of the distance from Vell to the Ember Realm, and every time he closed his eyes he walked it. The singing water. The shrine at the second ford. The long dry where you can't stop. And beside him, on the road, always beside him, close enough that their shoulders bumped—

He sat up.

Beside him. There had been someone beside him on the road. He could not see a face. He could not see anything, the fog was total, the fog was merciless—but he could feel a presence the way you feel sun through a wall, warmth without light, and the presence was small, smaller than him, and it was humming. Humming the bell-note. Humming the knock. *Da-da-DA.*

Ansel got up. He went down the stairs in the dark, because his feet knew the stairs, his feet knew everything about this house except why it felt like the inside of a word he'd almost remembered. The moon came through the window and lay on the table, and the map lay in the moonlight, and he stood over it in his nightshirt with his heart going like a bird in a box.

He should go back to bed. Rafael would be angry—or not angry, worse, sad; Rafael was never angry, Rafael was sad in a way that made the whole house colder, and Ansel had learned to dread that cold more than any scolding.

But his hand was already reaching for the charcoal.

He knelt on the chair. He pulled the second sheet of paper toward him—the good cream paper, the paper that held the light—and he did not decide what to draw, because deciding was not how his hand worked. His hand worked the way water works, the way roads work, finding the low places. And what it drew, in the small hours, in the moonlight, while the house slept and something behind the fog of his mind pressed its palms against the inside of his skull like a child pressing against a window:

He drew a road. The same road. Vell to the mountains, mountains to the line that was the Ember Realm.

And on the road, two figures.

One was small. He drew it small and simple, a child-shape, and he knew it was himself the way you know your own name. And beside it, holding its hand—or nearly holding it; the charcoal hesitated there, hovered, ached—he drew a second figure. Tall. Thin. With a coat that flared at the knees, and hair the charcoal made wild, and hands that Ansel shaded and shaded until they were dark, dark, dark, ink-stained to the wrist, the most careful thing on the whole page, the most loved.

He looked at it. The fog did not lift. The fog would not give him a name, would not give him a face, would not give him a single morning or a single meal or a single word spoken on that long road. The fog kept everything. The fog was a thief with a locked room.

But his hand had gotten out.

His hand had carried one thing over the wall, one small smuggled thing, and it was lying on the paper in the moonlight: two figures on a road they had walked together, in a life he could not remember, and the tall one's hands were stained with ink, and the small one was looking up at him.

Ansel put his finger on the tall figure's chest, very lightly, the way you touch something you're afraid will vanish.

"Who are you," he whispered.

The house said nothing. The moon moved. And then, from the fog behind his eyes, so faint he would never be sure in the morning that he had heard it at all, came a sound: a knock. *Da-da-DA.* Three and two. The knock from a mud-streaked door in a city of rain. The knock that meant, in whatever language knocks speak, in the language that lived below words where nothing could be stolen from:

*Let me in. I'm still here. Let me in.*

Ansel sat down on the floor beside the table, pulled the drawing onto his knees, and stayed there until the sky went gray and then gold, holding the paper against his chest like a door he had decided, whatever came, never to lock again.

Down the hall, he heard Rafael's door open. Heard the pause. Heard the footsteps stop—heard the man see him, the child on the floor with the drawing, and heard the breath catch, and heard nothing else, because Rafael was crying too quietly to hear, the way he always did, the way Ansel had somehow always known he would.

Ansel did not look up. He only turned the paper around, slowly, and held it out into the morning light, toward the ink-stained hands, and waited to see what the man's face would do.
