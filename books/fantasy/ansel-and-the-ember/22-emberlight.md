## 22. Emberlight

One year, and the seasons had come around like a wheel finding its old groove.

Rafael felt the anniversary in his body before he remembered it with his mind. He woke on the morning of the first frost with his hands aching—not the old ache of ink cramps or the newer ache of shopkeeping, but something deeper, a memory held in the bones. A year ago tonight, he had stood in a throne room full of dying light and watched a child unmake a god.

He rose, banked the coals, and put water on to boil. These were the small liturgies of his life now: fire, water, bread, the sweeping of a floor that belonged to him. The house sat at the edge of Harrowgate's rebuilt quarter, where the streets still smelled of new-cut timber and lime mortar, where every window he passed on his morning walk held someone hammering or planting or arguing cheerfully about the pitch of a roof. The free cities were learning to be free. It was slow work. It was good work.

Ansel lived three doors down, with Odile.

That arrangement had been Odile's doing, in the way most merciful things were. *You'll smother him,* she'd said, arms crossed in the doorway of the healer's house, the week after they came back from the Ember Realm. *You look at him like he owes you something. He doesn't owe you anything, Rafe. Neither of you does.* And she was right, the way she was always right about people even when she was wrong about ships. So Rafael had taken the small house and Ansel had taken the room above Odile's, and every day for a year Rafael had walked the three doors between them carrying bread, or lessons, or nothing at all—just himself, offered up without weight.

The child did not know him.

The healers had been gentle about it. *Memory is not a room that empties,* the eldest of them had said, folding her long hands. *It is more like a burned forest. Some things will grow back. Some things never will. What grows back may not be what stood there before.* Ansel had woken from the unbinding blank as fresh snow, knowing his own name because Odile told it to him, knowing nothing else. Not Vell. Not the road. Not the glass forests singing under a violet sky. Not the man who had taught him his letters by lamplight and lied to him about being brave and loved him with the whole ruined acreage of his heart.

Not Rafael.

For the first month, Rafael could not enter a room the child was in without feeling like an intruder in his own life. He would stand in doorways with bread going cold in his hands, watching Ansel laugh at some joke of Odile's—a real laugh, unburdened—and think: *I paid for that laugh. I would pay again.* It was a terrible arithmetic and he knew it, and he kept doing it anyway, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

But grief, he had learned, could learn patience. He had written that once, in the margin of a map, in another life: *grief is a country; survey it slowly.* He had not known then that he was writing instructions for himself.

So he taught the child letters. That was all he allowed himself to be: a tutor, a neighbor, a man with ink-stained fingers who came three mornings a week with a slate and a patient voice. Ansel took to letters the way he had once taken to the old speech—as if language were a country he'd been born to and only briefly exiled from. Within two seasons he was reading. Within three, he was correcting Rafael's penmanship, which Rafael bore with theatrical wounded dignity while something in his chest quietly broke and quietly healed, broke and healed, over and over, like a tide.

And sometimes—this was the thing Rafael never said aloud, the thing he carried the way you carry a coal in cupped hands—sometimes the child would pause over a word and frown, and say, *I know this one. I don't know how I know this one.* And once, only once, copying out the alphabet in careful rows, Ansel had drawn instead a small looping shape that was not any letter Rafael had taught him, and looked at it a long time, and said, "This means *road*, doesn't it? In the old way of writing."

Rafael had said yes. His voice had held. He counted that among the great achievements of his life.

* * *

On the morning of the anniversary, Rafael opened his shutters and saw the frost lying on Harrowgate like a first draft of winter, thin and silver and easily erased. The harbor bells rang the hour. Somewhere below, Odile was surely swearing at a bilge pump; her new ship—the second *Vance's Mercy*, the first having burned rather spectacularly at anchor during their escape from the inquisitors—was due to sail on the evening tide, running grain north to the river towns.

He spent the day the way he spent most days now. There was work, real work: the liberated cities needed maps, honest ones, drawn by hands that understood what maps could do when they fell into wicked hands. Rafael had hesitated a long time before taking up the craft again. Lorena had settled the matter in her dry way, sliding a commission across her ledger-table: *You can spend the rest of your life apologizing to paper, or you can make it behave. I recommend the latter.* So he drew. Coastlines, trade roads, the slow reknitting of a world the usurper's war had torn. He drew carefully. He drew as a man makes amends.

In the afternoon he baked. This too was new, and he was bad at it, and he did it anyway—honey-bread, because a year ago tonight there had been no honey anywhere in the world, and now there was, and he intended to mark the difference.

Lorena came by at dusk, ostensibly to return a borrowed atlas, actually to check on him, though neither of them would have admitted it under torture. She stood in his kitchen doorway with snow melting on her shoulders and looked at the honey-bread cooling on the sill and the two cups set out and the third cup he always set out anyway, out of habit, out of hope, out of some stubborn refusal to let the table forget.

"You'll go down to the quay to see Odile off?" she asked.

"After dark," Rafael said. "There's a thing that happens after dark. Or doesn't. I'll know which by then."

Lorena studied him with those broker's eyes that priced everything honestly, including grief. "A year," she said. "You've done well with it."

"I've done adequately with it."

"You've done well." She took a piece of bread, kissed the air near his cheek in the Harrowgate fashion, and left him alone with the fire and the waiting.

Because here was the thing he had told no one, not even Odile, not even Lorena, not even the pages of his private journal where he confessed everything else: for weeks now, Ansel had been drawing.

It started small. A doodle in the margin of a lesson—a winding line, a fork, a little square that might have been a house. Then the doodles grew. Maps, Rafael realized with a cartographer's jolt. The child was drawing maps, unprompted, untaught, maps of places Ansel had never been—or rather, places Ansel had been and did not remember being. A coastline that could only be the Salt Road. A river with three bridges, rendered in confident strokes. And once, spread across the whole slate while Rafael's back was turned: a road running east out of a walled city, through hills marked with tiny careful trees, through a pass drawn as a jagged scar, into a region filled with spindly branching shapes that made Rafael's breath stop in his throat.

Glass forests. The child had drawn the glass forests of the Ember Realm, from memory that was not memory, from a year that had been burned out of him and yet somehow persisted, root and ember, under the snow.

Rafael had said nothing. He had copied the drawing secretly that night, hand shaking, and locked it in the drawer with the shard's old wrappings and the letter he would never send. Because the healers had warned him: *do not press. Do not lead. What grows back must grow back on its own, or it will grow back wrong.* So he waited, and taught letters, and set out the third cup, and let the forest grow.

Tonight was the anniversary. Tonight, a year ago, light had torn a parasite loose from a king, and a child had stood in the aftermath with empty eyes, and Rafael had knelt in the wreckage of his own sacrifice—because it *had* been meant to be his, he had seized for the crown with both hands, he had wanted to be the one erased—and learned that love does not ask permission before it outbids you.

Tonight, he would walk down to the quay, and either the frost would hold its ordinary silence, or—

Or.

* * *

The knock came at moonrise.

Three knocks, then a pause, then two. Rafael's hand stopped on the latch. That rhythm meant nothing; it had never meant anything; it was simply how a child knocked. But his heart performed its old treachery anyway, leaping ahead of his reason the way it had for a year, every time footsteps paused outside his door.

He opened it.

Ansel stood on his step in the blue hour, wrapped in a coat too big for him—one of Odile's, sleeves rolled four times—holding a lantern in one hand and a folded paper in the other. Frost silvered the child's dark hair. Behind him the street lay quiet, roofs smoking gently with chimney-warmth, the rebuilt city breathing around them.

"Master Rafael," Ansel said—*Master Rafael*, because that was what he called him, formal as a little clerk, and Rafael had never managed to correct it because hearing his own name in that voice was a gift he hoarded shamelessly. "I'm sorry it's late. I couldn't sleep, and then I finished it, and then I couldn't wait until morning. Is that all right?"

"It's all right," Rafael said. "Come in out of the cold."

The child stepped past him into the warmth, and the smell of honey-bread met him, and he smiled—that smile, the one that had survived the burning somehow, that had been the first thing to come back and the thing Rafael built every day upon. "You baked. You only bake when it's an important day. Odile says so. What's today?"

Rafael closed the door on the frost. "Just a day," he said, which was a lie, but a kind one, the kind he'd gotten good at. "Sit. Eat. Show me what wouldn't wait until morning."

Ansel sat at the kitchen table, in the chair that was his chair though he didn't know why it was his chair, and unfolded the paper with the exaggerated care of someone handling a treasure. The lantern-light caught it. Rafael leaned over, and forgot, for a moment, how to breathe.

It was a map. Of course it was a map. It began at a small walled city labeled in the child's careful new letters—labeled *VELL*—and ran east along a road drawn with loving precision: the smuggler's toll-gate, the hills where the terrain shifted and Rafael had walked them straight by craft he'd sworn never to use again, the river with its three bridges, Harrowgate itself with its harbor drawn as a curved arm holding the sea. Then the salt road, the ash passes, the jagged scar of the mountains, and beyond them—the glass forests, every tree a slender branching stroke, and past them a great hall, and within the hall, at the center of everything, a small radiant circle.

And beside the road, walking it, two figures.

One was small. One was taller, and the taller figure had been drawn with its hands open at its sides, and the hands were stained dark at the fingertips. Ink-stained. The child had given the tall figure ink-stained hands.

"Where did you learn the Ember Realm's shape?" Rafael asked quietly. "No one has mapped it since—" Since me, he did not say. Since I mapped it for a tyrant.

Ansel shrugged, and frowned at the paper, and the frown was the old frown, the one from the lesson-slates, the *I-know-this-one-and-don't-know-how* frown. "I didn't learn it," he said. "It's just... when I close my eyes, it's there. All of it. The whole road. Like I've walked it." He looked up, and his eyes in the lantern-light were clear and bottomless and young. "Isn't that strange? I've never been east of Harrowgate. Have I?"

"No," Rafael said. "Never."

"But it feels—" The child pressed his palm flat against the map, over the long road, over the two small figures. "It feels like something happened on this road. Something important. I keep dreaming about it. There's a bell sound, a humming, like a struck bell that won't finish ringing. And fire that isn't hot. And someone—" He stopped. His brow furrowed deeper. "Someone is always just about to turn around. In the dream. Someone is walking ahead of me and I'm calling out and they're just about to turn around, and then I wake up." He looked at Rafael, troubled, hopeful, asking without knowing what he was asking. "Do you think that's silly?"

Rafael Galloway, royal cartographer, betrayer, charm-seller, survivor, stood in his own kitchen with his heart breaking open for perhaps the thousandth time that year, and found that this time it did not hurt. This time it felt like spring thaw—water moving under ice, the ground giving back what it had held.

"No," he said. "I don't think it's silly at all."

Ansel slid the map across the table. "Keep it," he said. "You're the map person. It should live with the other maps. Only—" and here the child hesitated, and Rafael saw him gather himself the way children do before a question they've been carrying a long way, "only there's something I want to ask, and I don't know why I want to ask it, and that's the strange part. It's like the question came with the map. Like it was rolled up inside it."

"Ask me anything," Rafael said. "Anything at all."

Ansel looked down at the two figures on the road. He touched the taller one, the one with ink-stained hands, very lightly, the way you touch something you're afraid of waking.

"Did I stay?" he asked.

The kitchen was very quiet. The fire ticked. Out in the street, the frost glittered under a fat white moon, and somewhere below it all, beneath the rebuilt houses and the mended roads and the whole patient, healing world, the old stones of Harrowgate held their ancient warmth.

Rafael thought of every answer he had rehearsed across a sleepless year—*you were brave, you saved a king, you saved me, you paid a price no child should pay*—and understood, finally, that none of them were the answer. The answer was smaller than that. The answer was the only true thing.

"Yes," he said.

Something moved through the child's face—recognition's distant cousin, a fish glimpsed far under clear water. Ansel nodded slowly, as if a knot had loosened somewhere behind his ribs, as if some sentry deep in the burned forest had seen, at last, a familiar shape on the road and lowered its guard.

"Good," the child said softly. "I hoped it was yes. I don't know why. But I hoped it."

Rafael cut two slices of honey-bread and set them on the table, and sat down across from him, and for a long time neither of them spoke, and the silence was not empty. It was the silence of a house with everyone in it.

Then Ansel, mouth full, eyes bright, tapped the map where the road ran east into the singing forests, and said:

"When I'm bigger—I'm going to walk this road. The real one. To see if it matches." He glanced up, suddenly shy, suddenly certain. "Will you come with me?"

Outside, the frost lay silver on a world worth mapping. Inside, a man who had once believed he would spend the rest of his life paying for a road reached across the table and rested his ink-stained hand beside the drawing of it.

"Yes," said Rafael. "All the way to the end."
