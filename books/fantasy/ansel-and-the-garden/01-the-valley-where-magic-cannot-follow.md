## 1. The Valley Where Magic Cannot Follow

The frost came to Hollowmere the way all quiet things came: without announcement, without permission, and without any consequence worth fearing.

Jonah Ravensworth knelt at the edge of her garden beds in the grey first light, her breath pluming, and pressed two fingers to the scorched edge of a feverfew leaf. The damage was old, a week at least—a candle's carelessness, she had told herself when it first appeared. But she knew better. The burn began at the tip and curled inward like a held breath released wrong, and it had appeared on the same morning her fingertips had ached, deep in the bone, for no reason at all.

Nothing happened, she reminded herself. Nothing ever happened.

That was the whole covenant of the valley. No spell crossed the ridge. No hex, no blessing, no whisper of working great or small could survive the crossing from the world beyond into the bowl of Hollowmere. The mages who had drawn up the terms of her exile ten years ago—the ones with their ink-stained fingers and their voices pitched with pity—had explained it to her slowly, as if grief made her stupid. *Magic cannot follow you there, child. You will be safe. And we will be safe from you.* They had said the second part more quietly, but she had heard it all the same.

So nothing happened. Her fingertips ached because she was twenty-four years old and autumn was coming on and old scars did what old scars always did. The feverfew burned because she had been careless with the lamp. That was all. That was everything.

She worked the row anyway, gloved now, stripping dead leaves from their stems with the methodical tenderness she had taught herself over a decade of mornings. The garden was the only thing she had ever built that hadn't broken. Nine beds in a rough spiral, herbs mostly, with the vegetables tucked into the sheltered curve at the center where the stone wall broke the wind. Comfrey and yarrow for poultices. Valerian for sleep. Feverfew, when it wasn't scorching itself, for headaches. She knew every plant the way she had once known the names of winds—not their shapes or seasons but their tempers, their preferences, the particular way each one leaned when it wanted something. Plants didn't ask for storms. They asked for water and patience, and Jonah had both in quantities that would have astonished anyone who knew what else lived coiled under her skin.

Because it did live there. She was not going to pretend otherwise; pretending otherwise was a luxury reserved for people whose hands had never—

She stopped the thought the way she always did, lifting it out by the roots before it could flower, and set it aside to die in the dark.

Under her skin, like scar tissue. That was the truest description she'd ever found for it, and she'd had ten years of long nights to search for words. Not gone. Never gone. A storm-caller's power couldn't be killed, the council had told her, only silenced, and Hollowmere's dead air pressed down on it the way snow pressed down on a buried field. Some mornings, in the first years, she had felt it shifting under the weight—restless, enormous, pressing up against the inside of her ribs like a hand against a window. She had learned to breathe around it. She had learned to think of it not as hers but as an injury, something that had happened *to* her rather than something she *was*. Scar tissue didn't want anything. Scar tissue didn't hunger. It just sat there, dense and dumb and permanent, marking the place where the living thing had been cut away.

Most mornings she almost believed it.

This morning the ache in her fingertips throbbed once, hard, like a second pulse, and Jonah sat back on her heels and stared at the ridge.

Hollowmere sat in its bowl of hills like water in a cupped palm. From her garden she could see the whole sweep of the valley floor—the village huddled gray and brown along the mere's southern shore, the smoke of forty hearths standing straight up in the windless cold, the terraced orchards climbing the near slopes, and then, ringing everything, the ridge itself: a long dark spine of pine and granite that sealed the valley off from the sky beyond the way a lid seals a pot. There was exactly one road through, a stony switchback track on the western face, and Jonah hadn't set foot on it since the day they brought her down it, fourteen years old, wrists bound in lead-thread so the escort wouldn't have to feel whatever it was they felt when they stood too close to her.

She looked at the ridge the way she looked at it most days—one glance, brief as a blink—and went back to the feverfew.

* * *

By midmorning the frost had burned off and the village had come awake, and Jonah loaded her baskets onto the handcart and made her weekly descent to the market square.

She had learned, over ten years, exactly how to be small in Hollowmere. It was a discipline like any other, requiring daily practice. Come down at the same hour. Wear the same gray wool. Buy your salt and lamp oil from the same two shopkeepers, the ones too old to be curious and too kind to be cruel. Sell your herbs to Mother Hessel at the apothecary stall and never haggle, because haggling meant conversation and conversation meant questions. Smile at no one. Meet everyone's eyes exactly once. Be boring, be harmless, be weather that has already passed.

For the most part it worked. Hollowmere had absorbed her the way still water absorbs a stone: completely, silently, without disturbance. The valley had a population that hovered around three hundred souls, and three hundred souls will forgive almost anything given enough decades, but they do not forget. Jonah was the woman who grew things. That was her title here, her entire biography compressed into four syllables, and she had come to treasure its poverty. The woman who grows things. Not the Ravensworth girl. Not the storm-child. Not *her*.

Except.

"Ravensworth witch," said a boy's voice, low and delighted, from behind the fishmonger's barrels. "Ravens-worth curse."

She didn't turn. Ten years ago turning would have meant tears, or fury, or worse—ten years ago she might have felt the thing under her skin lift its head at the sting of it. Now she simply adjusted her grip on the cart handle and kept walking, and let the whisper trail her like a smell.

There were four of them this year, she estimated. The oldest couldn't be more than nine. Children were born in Hollowmere and raised in Hollowmere and rarely left it, which meant the story got handed down like an heirloom, worn smooth by retelling: *the Ravensworth girl called lightning out of a clear sky and buried her whole family under the mountain ash, and they didn't hang her because even hanging won't kill that kind, so they put her in the dead valley where magic can't breathe.* The details mutated with each generation of children. In some tellings she'd summoned a flood. In others she'd struck the church itself. In one memorable version told to her face by a seven-year-old three summers past, she had eaten her brothers, which had at least the virtue of originality.

The truth was simpler and uglier and she carried it everywhere, folded small, the way other people carried knives.

A cart wheel jolted over a rut and the baskets rattled, and Mother Hessel looked up from her scale weights with her clouded eyes and said, "Feverfew's early this year."

"Aye." Jonah laid out the bundles. Her voice came out rusted from disuse; she cleared her throat. "And valerian. Good root this time."

"Hessel doesn't pay extra for good root."

"I know what Hessel pays."

The old woman's mouth folded into something that was almost a smile—the closest thing to warmth the market permitted between them—and she counted out the coins, and for ninety seconds the transaction proceeded in the wordless choreography of women who had done this five hundred times and intended to do it five hundred more. This was the shape of Jonah's life. Not silence exactly—silence implied something missing—but a low continuous hum of ordinary exchange, coin and goods, weather and crops, the small talk of small lives. Enough. It had to be enough. She had made it enough.

It was on the walk home, hauling the empty cart up the lane with the afternoon sun finally warm on her shoulders, that the ache came back.

Not in her fingers this time. Everywhere. A deep subcutaneous itch, the feeling of skin trying to heal over something that refused to stay buried, and Jonah stopped in the middle of the lane and stood very still and listened—with her ears, which heard only wind in the pines, and with some older sense she had spent a decade starving—which heard nothing at all.

That was the strange part. Hollowmere was silent. Hollowmere was always silent; that was its purpose, its architecture, the whole point of the valley. Dead air. Null ground. Whatever pressure she was feeling had to be her body remembering wrongly, a phantom limb flexing, scar tissue aching before rain the way ordinary scars were supposed to.

But there was no rain coming. She checked the sky out of habit, the way a recovering drunk checks a doorway, and the sky was clean and blue and utterly innocent.

"Nothing," she said aloud, to the lane, to the pines, to the thing under her skin. "Nothing happens here."

The thing under her skin said nothing, which was how it had answered for ten years, and she pulled the cart the rest of the way home and tried to believe herself.

* * *

Evening came early this far north in autumn. Jonah lit her lamp, ate bread and hard cheese and the last of the tomatoes from the sheltered bed—she grew tomatoes against all local advice, out of pure stubbornness, and coaxed them along with cloth covers and south-facing stones—and settled into the chair by the window with a book of grafting diagrams she'd copied out herself years ago, in her careful gardener's hand.

She read three lines. Then she looked up, because the windowpanes had frosted.

Only the windowpanes. The glass over the fire-warmed room, in a house heated by a stove she'd fed an hour ago, had bloomed white with cold, delicate ferns of ice spreading inward from every corner of every pane, and the room's temperature hadn't dropped at all, and the lamp flame stood perfectly still as though the air itself had paused to watch.

Jonah sat without moving. Her heartbeat counted out eight full beats. Under her skin, the scar-tissue thing lay flat and silent as it always did, and somehow that was the worst of it—if this was her power stirring, she'd feel it, wouldn't she? She'd know. After ten years she would *know*.

The frost finished its spread, hung glittering in the lamplight for one long moment—

—and slid off the glass all at once, sheeting down like water, gone.

The flame resumed its flicker. The stove ticked. Outside, the wind moved in the pines with its ordinary sound.

Jonah set down the book. She crossed the room on legs that felt borrowed, touched the wet glass, tasted the water on her fingertip. Cold. Clean. Nothing.

She thought about the feverfew, scorched from the inside. She thought about the ache in her fingertips, twice now. She thought about how the council's binding had a name among the mages—she'd overheard it, that last day on the ridge road, two young acolytes whispering behind their hands: *null-sink, they're putting her in a null-sink, gods keep anyone who lives downstream of one*—and she thought about what happened downstream of a dam, in the spring, when the meltwater came high.

"No," she told the window, quietly, in the voice she used on aphids and weeds and her own worse thoughts. "No. I paid. It's done."

The window said nothing. Windows were good like that.

She banked the stove, barred the door out of a caution she couldn't justify, and lay down in her narrow bed with the lamp blown out and her eyes open. Sleep took its time coming, and when it came it brought the usual country with it—the mountain ash, the smell of ozone and torn earth, voices calling her name in a register that no living throat used—and she woke twice in the dark with her hands fisted in the blankets and the taste of lightning at the back of her teeth.

The third time she woke, it was not the dream that woke her.

It was sound. A heavy, uneven sound outside, boots dragging on frost-stiffened grass, and then a thud against her garden gate that she felt through the floorboards of the house.

Jonah lay listening. Wolves didn't come this close to the village. Neighbors didn't come at all, not at this hour, not up her lane. The sound came again—a body striking wood, weakly, the way falling things strike—and beneath her sternum, faint as the first drop before rain, something under her skin turned over in its long sleep.

She rose. She took the iron poker from beside the stove because she owned no knife bigger than a herb blade, and she unbarred the door and stepped out into the freezing dark, breath pluming, the frosted grass loud under her bare feet.

At her gate, tangled in the latch where he'd fallen, lay a man she had never seen before—and Jonah Ravensworth had seen every soul in Hollowmere. He wore riding leathers caked half up the thighs with dried mud, and the mud was wrong, red-clay red, the color of earth from beyond the ridge, from a country Jonah had spent ten years refusing to imagine. One of his hands was pressed to his side and came away black in the starlight. His eyes found her, wild and unfocused and drowning, and his cracked lips moved.

He said—or she thought he said, though the wind tore at the words—"Anyone. Please. Anyone still listening."

Then his head dropped against her gate, and the paper in his fist, sealed with a crest she did not recognize, began—softly, impossibly, against her skin—to hum.
