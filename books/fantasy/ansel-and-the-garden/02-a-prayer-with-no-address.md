## 2. A Prayer With No Address

The letter came on the last warm day of autumn, and it came bleeding.

Jonah found it the way she found most things in Hollowmere: late, and by accident. She had gone down to the gate to close it against the dusk, a habit so worn into her bones that her hands performed it while her mind wandered elsewhere—elsewhere being the row of winter cabbages she'd have to mulch before the first frost, and the bees she'd need to wrap in straw, and the small domestic litany of tasks that made up a life she had chosen the way a prisoner chooses the dimensions of her cell. The gate was only a gate. Split oak, older than the exile, older than the curse the village children whispered about. She had oiled its hinges every spring for ten years so that it would never scream.

It was screaming now. No—the hinges were silent. It was something else, a thin high sound, and it took her three full breaths to understand that it was a man, and that the man was lying across her threshold with his boots in the lane and his blood in her garden.

He had crawled, she realized. There was a drag-mark in the gravel all the way from the turn where the road dipped out of sight of the village, twenty yards of it, and at the end of the mark a man in a courier's grey coat was trying to press his own guts back into his own body with both hands, and failing, and laughing at the failure in a cracked whisper that was worse than screaming would have been.

"Door," he said. "Said there'd be a door."

"There's a door." Jonah was kneeling before she had decided to kneel. Her hands, which knew wounds the way her garden knew frost—she had set bones in this valley, stitched hunters, pulled children back from fevers, because a valley with no magic must make do with hands—were already moving, parting the coat, finding the wound. It was bad. It was the kind of bad she had learned to recognize in the first year, when a reeve had gored himself on a boar spear and she had held his life in her palms like water in cupped fingers and watched it run out anyway. "Who did this to you?"

"Road," the courier said, as if that were an answer, and perhaps it was. "Road between here and the ford. They knew what I carried. They—" A cough took him, and brought red with it. "You're her. The gardener. He said—the king said—find the woman who grows things where nothing grows."

Jonah's hands went still.

She was aware, in the way one is aware of a distant storm-front, of several things at once. That no courier should know her name. That no king's man should cross the ridge into Hollowmere, because the ridge was the whole point of Hollowmere, because ten years ago a council of frightened, merciful people had drawn a line across a map and told her: *here, and no farther, and nothing you are can follow you past this line.* That the valley was magic-dead, had been for longer than the exile, that spells unraveled at the ridge like thread pulled from a hem, and that whatever this man carried, whatever words had been spoken over it in the capital, should have arrived at her gate as nothing. As paper. As ink and ignorance.

The courier's hand came up. It shook. It found her wrist with the terrible accuracy of the dying, and pressed something into her palm, and closed her fingers over it.

The packet was small. Oilcloth, waxed twice, tied with cord that had been knotted in a pattern she didn't know—knots that moved, almost, when she wasn't looking directly at them. And it was warm. Not body-warm. Warm the way a stone is warm at evening after a day of sun it should not still be holding, warm the way the air goes before lightning, and it was humming. Not a sound. A hum she felt in the bones of her hand, in the small sealed chamber behind her sternum where she kept the thing she had sworn never to be again.

Inside the valley it should have been inert. Inside the valley *everything* was inert. She had spent ten years leaning against that silence like a wall, like the one kind thing the world had ever done for her. Spells died at the ridge. Blessings died at the ridge. The little hedge-witches of Hollowmere brewed teas that were only teas and hung charms that were only knots, and Jonah had made her peace with it, had built her whole survival on it: *here, nothing you are can touch you, and nothing you are can get out.*

The packet hummed against her skin like a struck bell that would not finish ringing.

"Open it," the courier whispered. "Please. I was told—only her hands. Anyone still listening. That's what it says. Anyone still listening." He laughed again, that awful cracked laugh. "Anyone. As if the old ones were a household someone forgot to write to."

"Drink this first." She got an arm under him, dragged him inside the gate, propped him against the wall where the morning sun would find him. She fetched water and willow bark and the bitter tincture she kept for pain, and he drank because she told him to, obedient as a child, and she worked over him for an hour by lamplight with the packet sitting on the step like a patient animal, humming its one note, and she did not look at it, and she could not stop feeling it, the way you cannot stop feeling a tooth that aches.

He was dying. She had known it from the first touch and she worked anyway, because that was what hands were for, and because the alternative was to sit with the humming thing and think about what it meant.

*They knew what I carried.* Bandits did not know what couriers carried. Assassins did. And assassins meant the capital, and the capital meant the king, and the king meant a world she had spent a decade not thinking about, and all of it had come down her road and bled out on her gravel and put a humming letter in her hand.

*Find the woman who grows things where nothing grows.* The king had said that. King Aldric. She had seen his face once, from a distance, at fourteen—no. She did not think about that year. She had built her life on not thinking about that year, the way the valley was built on not letting magic in, and both structures had stood for ten years, and both, she understood with a dread that came up through the soles of her feet like ground-frost, had just been cracked by a dying man on her threshold.

* * *

She sat up with him through the night. It was what she did. It was, she sometimes thought, the only prayer she had left: to sit with the dying so they were not alone, since she could not—would not—do anything else for anyone, ever again.

Toward the small hours, when the pain-tincture had carried him somewhere past fear, he talked. Dying men talk; she had learned to listen the way she listened to weather, for what was coming.

"Three months ago the prayers started going somewhere," he said. He was looking at her ceiling beams as if reading something written there. "You'd not have heard, out here. Chaplains wept in the temples. Old shrines that had been cold since the grandmothers of grandmothers—warm again. Candles lighting themselves. And then the king took sick, or they said he took sick, and the edicts started coming down strange, and my order—we carry letters, that's all we are, we're the ones who go where the roads don't—" He turned his head. His eyes were very bright and very clear, the way eyes go at the end. "I carried prayers for a living, woman. Do you understand? Little sealed things, for people who paid a copper, carried up the mountain to the high altar. And three months ago they started to *arrive.* And then the king gave me this one, with his own hand, and said, take it past the ridge, to the valley, to a gardener, and don't let anyone else touch it. And I said, sire, nothing of the art survives in the valley, it's a dead country, and he said—"

He stopped. His breath had gone the rattling way. She knew the sound. She hated the sound.

"He said," the courier whispered, "*that is why she will hear it. Because she is the only one in the world who has made herself deaf.*"

The fire ticked. Outside, the wind moved through the dead garden rows, and Jonah sat with her hands folded in her lap like a goodwife at a wake, and felt the packet humming on the step, and understood that the wall had a hole in it.

"Anyone still listening," the courier murmured. "That's the address. That's the whole address. He made me memorize it. He said if the seal broke, if I couldn't deliver the thing itself, I was to say it aloud to her, and it would be the same. Anyone still listening." His eyes found her. "Are you, gardener? Is anyone? Because I've carried a hundred prayers up that mountain and I never once knew, and now I'm—" the laugh again, thin as thread, "—now I'm the last letter, and I'd like to know if the post is real."

"I don't know," Jonah said, and her voice came out rough, and true. "I don't know what's listening. I made sure I'd never know."

"Then open it," he said. "For me. Let me know before I go whether anyone's ever heard me."

She should have refused. She understood that later, turning the night over and over like a stone with a warm side. She should have buried the packet in the cold corner of the cellar where she kept things that frightened her—there were only two things in it, a nail from her old house and a child's shoe—and let the courier die not knowing, the way the whole world had died not knowing for three hundred years. But his eyes were bright and clear and asking, and she had spent ten years practicing smallness, and smallness, it turned out, could not survive a dying man's request.

She brought the packet in. She untied the moving knots—her fingers seemed to know how, which frightened her more than the humming had—and broke the seal, and unfolded the paper inside.

It was not a letter. It was a prayer. It was written in a formal, antique hand, the kind they taught in temple schools, and it was addressed the way prayers are addressed, upward, outward, to no one, to anyone:

*To the old gods, whoever of you remain: anyone still listening.*

*We have forgotten how to ask. We have forgotten you so long that our forgetting has teeth. Something wears our king. Something answers our prayers too well. We are a city kneeling to a hunger and calling it holy.*

*If any of you remember mercy: send us someone who remembers you.*

*Send us the storm-child.*

Jonah read it twice. The fire had gone down to coals. The courier had stopped breathing somewhere in the middle of the second reading, gently, between one word and the next, and she had not noticed, because the words had her the way the winter has the last green thing.

*Storm-child.*

There was no one else it could mean. There had only ever been one storm-child; the council had used the words at her trial, spat soft and horrified, *she called the storm, the child calls storms,* and then they had drawn the line on the map and sent her here where nothing of the art could follow, and she had spent ten years being a gardener, being small, being deaf on purpose.

And now a prayer had crossed the ridge that should have died at the ridge. It should have been ink on paper, nothing, a dead letter to dead gods. Instead it hummed. Instead her hands, folded in her lap, were answering it—she could feel them, a pressure like the moment before a storm breaks, like the held breath of the whole grey sky, gathering in her palms, in her wrists, in the sealed chamber behind her sternum where the scar tissue of her old life lived.

The valley was waking up. Or something in the valley was. Or something in *her* was, and had only ever been sleeping, leashed, waiting for an address.

"No," Jonah said aloud, to the empty room, to the dead courier, to the paper. "No. You've got the wrong woman. There's no one here. There's no one listening."

The packet hummed. And beneath it, so faint she might have made it out of dread and lamplight and ten years of buried longing, she felt—on the other side of the ridge, far off, patient as weather—something answer.

She wrapped the prayer in oilcloth with hands that were not quite steady, and set it on the table, and did not touch it again. She washed the courier's face and closed his eyes and covered him with the good blanket, the one she saved for guests, because he had been a guest, because he had carried a letter to her door and died of it. Toward dawn she went out and stood at her gate, looking east, where the ridge stood black against the paling sky, the line on the map, the line across her life.

The wind was coming down off the ridge, as it always did at dawn.

For ten years it had smelled of nothing. Stone and pine and cold.

This morning it smelled of rain.
