## 3. What the Ridge Remembers

The courier lasted three days.

Jonah knew it would be three days the way she knew when frost would take the last of the beans—by some wordless arithmetic of the body, a counting she had never asked for and could not put down. He lay in her spare bed beneath the eaves, fevered and muttering, his wounds blooming colors no poultice of hers could argue with. The gash along his ribs had gone the deep violet of a storm bruise, then a black that shone like wet slate, and by the second morning it smelled of iron and something older, something that made her think of cellars under churches.

She had done what she could. Comfrey and yarrow, honey packed into the wound's edges, willow bark steeped until the kitchen smelled bitter as regret. She had stitched him with the small, precise hands her mother had taught her—*a garden is only patience made visible, Jonah-girl*—and the stitches held, but holding was not the same as healing. Whatever had been done to him on the road beyond the ridge, it was not a wound so much as an argument, and his body was losing the debate.

On the third night, he woke clear-eyed and lucid, which Jonah had learned—in another life, in another valley—was often the worst sign of all.

"You're the Ravensworth woman." His voice was a dry leaf dragged over stone. He was younger than she'd first thought, mid-twenties perhaps, with a courier's lean frame going to waste beneath her quilts. His eyes found hers in the lamplight with terrible focus, the way dying men sometimes spent their last clarity like a coin they'd been saving. "They said if I could reach Hollowmere. They said the valley would keep me safe."

"Who said?" Jonah kept her voice level. She sat in the chair beside the bed, hands folded in her lap because they wanted to tremble and she did not permit them to. "Who sent you?"

"Doesn't matter now." A ghost of a smile. "Matters where I got to. Matters what I carried." His hand crept out from under the quilt, and Jonah saw that the fingers were gray at the tips, the nails dark as hoof-horns. "You felt it. Don't lie to me, gardener. I watched you hold that letter like it was singing to you."

The letter lay in the cedar chest downstairs, wrapped in three layers of linen, and even through the floorboards Jonah fancied she could feel it—the faint hum against her skin like a struck tuning fork buried in the garden soil. *To anyone still listening.* She had not opened it again since the first night. She had not needed to. The words had taken up residence behind her sternum and read themselves to her in the dark.

"The king," the courier said. "Aldric. You know the name."

"Everyone knows the name."

"He sent me." The man coughed, wetly, and Jonah leaned forward without deciding to, pressed a cloth to his lips, took it away spotted rust-brown. "Not his council. Not his priests. Him. He wrote it himself, in the hours when he's still himself, and he sealed it with wax from his own candle because—" the laugh that came out of him was terrible, a small broken thing— "because he couldn't trust anyone else's fire. Do you understand? The King of Vael cannot trust fire anymore."

Jonah's mouth had gone dry. Outside, the wind moved through the valley the way it always did here—gently, dumbly, a beast with no thoughts in its head—and she hated it suddenly, hated the softness of it, the way it had never once in ten years carried a whisper of anything true.

"What is hunting him?" she asked.

The courier's eyes went past her, to the low rafters, to something further than rafters. When he spoke again his voice dropped to the register people used in sickrooms and chapels, hushed and certain.

"He's wearing something hungry."

The lamp guttered, though there was no draft.

"It came to him as a gift. That's how they do it—that's how it's always done, my grandmother told stories, old ones, from before the forgetting. It comes as a crown, or a blessing, or a bride, and it waits to be invited, and men are always inviting." His breath rattled. "He wears it and it wears him and every day there's less of him inside the wearing. But he has hours. Sometimes a whole morning. And in those hours he's been writing letters and hiding them in boot heels and prayer book bindings and sending couriers out through the postern gate like a man dropping seeds from a prison window."

"And yours reached me." It wasn't a question. Her voice sounded far away to herself. "Why me?"

His eyes came back to her face, and there was pity in them, which was unbearable. Pity, from a dying stranger, aimed at her like an arrow she'd been dodging for ten years.

"They said find the storm-caller in the dead valley. They said she'll hate us for asking. They said ask anyway." His hand found hers on the quilt—cold fingers, cold as the frost that bloomed unbidden on her windowpanes some mornings, though there was no magic in Hollowmere to make it, only her, only the thing sleeping under her skin dreaming itself into the glass. "The king said—his words—I wrote them down to carry exact—he said, *tell her the valley was never a punishment. Tell her it was a mercy, and mercies end.*"

Jonah did not remember standing. She was at the window with her back to the bed, looking out at her garden silvered under a fat and indifferent moon, and her heart was going like a bird trapped in a shuttered room.

Behind her, softly, the courier said: "I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I don't have more. Only—" and then the rattle took him, and she turned in time to see him go, not dramatically, not with any last grand utterance, just a man exhaling a breath he didn't take back in. His eyes stayed open on the rafters. The lamp flame stood very straight, as if attending.

Jonah sat with him until dawn. She closed his eyes. She washed his face with warm water and combed his hair, because no one should leave the world ungroomed, her mother had said, everyone deserves to arrive tidy. Then she went down to the kitchen and made tea she did not drink and sat watching the light come up gray over the ridge, and the letter hummed in its cedar chest like a wasp in a jar, and she understood that the ten years were over, whatever she decided. The valley had been breached. Something had crossed the silence to reach her, and things that cross once cross again.

*Mercies end.*

She looked at the ridge, black against the paling sky, and felt the braid in her chest pull tight—terror on one strand, longing on the other, wound together so long she could no longer tell which was which.

* * *

She climbed at night, because of course she climbed at night. Daylight would have meant witnesses, and witnesses meant questions, and questions meant the village children would have a new verse for their skipping song by week's end. *Ravensworth, Ravensworth, don't look at the sky.* Besides, some errands are too private for the sun. This one especially.

She told herself she was only walking. She told herself a great many things as she took the shepherd's track above the village with a lantern she never lit, the moon doing the work instead, laying the hillside out in silver and shadow. She told herself the letter could stay in its chest forever. She told herself the king of a country she'd been exiled from was not her grief to carry. She told herself that the dead courier had simply been mistaken, delirious, that kings did not write letters to disgraced gardeners, that hungry things did not wear crowns, that the world beyond the ridge was exactly as ordinary as she had spent a decade pretending it was.

The mountain let her tell herself these things for about an hour. Then the trees thinned, and the track steepened, and Hollowmere began to fall away below her—the village a scatter of embers-light, her own roof just distinguishable, the garden a darker rectangle of dark—and Jonah stopped telling herself anything at all.

Because she could feel it.

Not yet. Not quite yet. But close—a pressure on the air like the weight before rain, like the moment a bowstring reaches full draw. Ten years she had lived inside this silence, this blessed, suffocating nothing, where magic ran off her like water off oiled canvas and the storm under her skin slept the dreamless sleep of the buried. Ten years of numbness she had called peace. And now, twenty paces from the ridgeline, she could feel the other side of the mountain breathing.

It was faint. Anyone else would have missed it entirely—a shimmer in the air, a taste of copper and ozone at the back of the throat, the sense that the world beyond the crest was slightly more awake than the world behind her. But Jonah had been born fluent in this language and had spent ten years refusing to speak it, and fluency does not fade; it only waits. She stood at the tree line with her hands hanging empty at her sides and felt magic lying against the far slope of the ridge like a sea against a seawall, patient, immense, aware.

Aware of her. That was the part that stole her breath. The pressure was not uniform—it gathered toward her, leaned toward her the way plants leaned toward light, and somewhere beyond the crest something vast and old turned its attention through the stone and the soil and the silent air and found the small figure standing at the border of its sleep, and *recognized* her.

Her skin prickled. Every hair on her arms rose. Under her breastbone, in the place where the scar tissue of her power lay folded flat, something stirred—not waking, not yet, but turning over in its sleep the way a sleeper turns toward a familiar voice. Warmth spread through her chest, terrifying and sweet, the feeling of a limb long asleep beginning to prickle back to life, all pins and needles and returning blood.

"No," Jonah whispered, to the mountain, to herself, to whatever listened. "No, I didn't call you. I'm not calling you. Go back to sleep."

The pressure did not recede. If anything it deepened, gentle as a hand laid on her shoulder, and she realized with a lurch that it wasn't reaching for her at all—or not only for her. It was *waiting*. Holding itself back. The way a dog holds itself back at the edge of a yard, quivering, eyes fixed on the door. All that power, all that ancient attention, and it was waiting for her to decide. Waiting for permission. Waiting for her to take one step over the ridge and become, again, the thing she had buried.

The wind came up the slope and touched her face, and it was different here—she could feel that too, the difference, the way a deaf woman feels music through a floor. On the Hollowmere side the wind was empty. Here, three steps from the crest, it had texture, intention, a low note running through it like a finger circling the rim of a glass. Somewhere beyond the ridge a storm was building or had built or remembered how to build, and its edge brushed the mountaintop, and every drop of rain in it knew her name.

Jonah sank to her knees in the wet grass.

This was the thing no one had understood at her trial—not the council in their gray robes, not the neighbors who had once shared bread with her mother, not even, perhaps, the child she had been. They thought exile was a punishment. They thought taking her magic-dead valley was cruelty dressed as mercy. But Jonah had knelt in the ashes of her family's house at fourteen years old, lightning still crawling the sky above her like veins of white fire, and she had known the truth with a certainty that never left her: the valley was the kindest thing anyone had ever done for her. Because the storm was not something she did. It was something she *was*, and you cannot cut out what you are—you can only bury it somewhere the ground itself refuses to echo.

And now the ground was echoing.

She knelt there a long time, shaking, and the terrible braided truth of it worked itself open in her chest like a knot finally pulled loose: she was terrified, and she was homesick, both at once, both for the same impossible country. Terror of the storm that had taken everything. Longing for the storm that had been everything—her mother's delighted laughter when Jonah at six had called a little whirlwind to spin the laundry dry, her father lifting her onto his shoulders to feel the thunder roll through his ribs, *feel that, Jonah-girl, that's your heartbeat the sky is copying*. Ten years she had mourned her family. No one had ever allowed her to mourn the rest of it, the whole sky-wide inheritance, and mourning it now, kneeling in the grass with magic pressing against the mountain like a tide against a door, hurt worse than the guilt ever had.

"I can't," she said aloud. Her voice cracked on it. "Whatever you want. Whoever's listening. I can't be that again. Last time it cost everything."

The pressure held, steady, unhurried. And then—she would swear this later, swearing it to Bram Ostell with frost climbing the windows behind her, and he would believe her because he had seen the panes—it did something she had not asked for and did not expect.

It withdrew. Not vanishing—never vanishing, she could still feel it the way you feel a hand hovering an inch from your shoulder—but easing back, folding itself down over the far side of the ridge like a bird settling its wings. Giving her room. Giving her the night to choose.

And in the space it left behind, one thread of sensation remained, thin as spider silk drawn across the crest of the world: a pulling. Southward. Toward the capital, toward the palace, toward a king wearing something hungry. Faint as a scent on the wind, unmistakable as a scream.

*Come,* said the thread, without words. *Anyone still listening.*

Jonah knelt until the moon set. Then she rose, knees stiff, face wet with more than the mist, and looked south along the dark spine of the mountains, where the thread ran thin and bright as a wire through the dark.

She did not cross the ridge. Not tonight. But she walked home along the high path with her lantern still unlit and her head bare to the wind, and at the gate of her garden she paused, and looked back once at the black line of the crest, and spoke to it quietly, the way one speaks to a horse one is deciding whether to trust.

"Three days," she said. "Give me three days to put the beds in order."

Under her feet, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, the ground hummed its answer—and behind her, in the dark of the house, the letter in its cedar chest began, very softly, to sing.
