## 4. The Priest Who Buried His God

Bram Ostell had been walking for eleven days, and his boots had begun to speak to him.

Not in the way the old texts promised gods spoke—through thunder, through omens, through the sudden weight of silence. No. His boots spoke in the language of blisters and wet leather, a litany of small complaints that rose through his ankles like a psalm nobody had thought to write down. *You are too old for this, they said. You were too old for this ten years ago. You buried your god in a hole no bigger than a prayer book, and now you are chasing a courier through the ass-end of the world because a dead woman's letter told you to.*

He told his boots, as he told himself, that it was not the letter that had moved him. It was the address.

*A prayer with no address*—that was how the courier had sealed it, according to the tavern gossip in Marrow's Ford. A plea written on consecrated vellum and sent out with no temple, no shrine, no name attached. Addressed, the gossip said, to *anyone still listening*. Bram had heard a hundred such things in the years since Veyra's name had been scraped from the rolls of the divine. Most were the fever-dreams of the grieving, the desperate, the sort of people who mistook a headache for a calling. But this one had come out of the north, out of the direction of the crown, and it had been carried by a man who wept when he spoke of it, and Bram had not wept in ten years and thought perhaps it was time someone in his acquaintance did.

So he walked. And his boots complained. And the road narrowed as it climbed toward the ridge that the maps called the Hollowmere Line, drawn in the cartographers' ink of finality—a hard black stroke, like a scar across the country's throat.

Beyond that line, the stories said, magic did not go.

Bram had never tested the claim himself. In the years before Veyra's fall he had been a man of the capital, a man of incense and argument, and the valley to the north had been a rumor the priests used to frighten novices. *There is a place,* they would say, *where the gods cannot hear you. Where a prayer is only breath. Would you like to live there, little brother? Would you like to find out what you are when nothing answers?*

He had found out anyway, without the valley's help. When Veyra's last altar was pulled down and her priests were given a choice between recantation and the road, Bram had discovered precisely what he was when nothing answered: a man with a good memory for liturgy and no one to say it to.

The ridge rose ahead of him now, dark with pine, its shoulders wrapped in cloud that did not seem to move. There was something wrong about that cloud, he thought. Not menacing, exactly. Not alive, exactly. Just—*still*. The way a room is still when someone in it has stopped breathing to listen.

He paused where the road forked. One path climbed toward the pass and the village beyond, the little settlement the maps called Hollowmere Proper, a scatter of rooftops huddled in the valley's throat like sheep afraid of the weather. The other wound down toward a farmstead Bram had been told about in Marrow's Ford by a woman who traded in herbs and gossip with equal dishonesty.

"The Ravensworth place," she'd said, and then had looked at him the way people look at a man who has asked the wrong question. "You want herbs, priest, you can buy them at market like anyone. You want to go poking at that garden, you go alone."

He had asked what she meant. She had not answered. She had sold him cheese instead, which was its own kind of answer.

Bram took the lower path.

* * *

The farmstead announced itself before he saw it: the smell of turned earth and cut stems, green and wet and stubbornly alive. Then the garden itself, spilling down the hillside in terraced beds—rows of silver-green sage, feathery fennel gone to seed, comfrey with its heavy purple bells bowed by the weight of bees. It was, Bram thought, the most defiant thing he had seen in eleven days of walking. Everything else on the road had been gray with the year's turning, gray with the general grayness that had crept over the country since the king's edicts began arriving in stranger and stranger hands. But this garden was green the way a shout is loud. Someone had argued with the soil for a decade and won.

The house at the garden's crown was stone and timber, modest, its thatch recently mended. And in its eastern window—

Bram stopped walking.

Frost.

It was the first cold week of autumn, true. Frost was not impossible. Frost was, in fact, ordinary, and a sensible man would have seen the white ferning across the glasspanes and thought *cold night* and thought nothing further.

But Bram Ostell had spent thirty years studying the small print of the divine, and he knew what frost meant when it grew on one window of a warm house on a morning when the grass in the yard was wet and unfrozen. He knew what it meant when the frost did not melt in the sun but sat there, patient, arranged in patterns too regular for weather—whorls and spirals, the looping signatures that ice made when it was not falling but *arriving*, summoned by something in the room behind the glass that the air itself was trying to answer.

He had seen it once before, twenty years ago, in the capital, on the windows of a girl the Temple had brought in for testing. They had kept her three days. On the third day the frost on her cell window had spelled out a word, and Bram had never learned what the word was, because he had been reassigned that same afternoon and the girl had been reassigned to somewhere no one spoke of, and the whole episode had been folded away into the Temple's long tradition of not knowing things.

*Storm-caller,* the frost on the Ravensworth window said now, in the wordless grammar of cold. *Something that calls the sky is living in this house, and the sky is trying to answer through the glass.*

And the valley was supposed to be magic-dead. The valley was supposed to be the one place in the world where nothing answered.

Bram stood in the road for a long moment, feeling the frost's meaning sink into him like cold water into leather. Then he straightened his collar, brushed the road dust from his coat as well as it could be brushed, and walked up to the gate like a man with nothing on his mind but herbs.

* * *

She came out when he knocked, and Bram's first thought was that the gossip in Marrow's Ford had undersold her.

Not in beauty—though she was not a woman beauty would have overlooked, all sharp angles and watchful gray eyes, hair the color of wet slate tied back with a strip of leather. It was the *stillness* that undersold her. She stood in her doorway the way the cloud stood on the ridge: not moving, but listening. A woman who had arranged her whole life into a shape that would not attract attention, and who was now examining his attention to see what it wanted.

"Priest." Her voice was flat. Her eyes went to the faded sun-disc stitched on his collar—the symbol of the Concord, the great homogenized faith that had eaten the old gods one by one—and her mouth did something small and contemptuous. "We don't have a tithe. We don't have a temple. We don't have souls that need saving, if it comes to that. The valley saw to that."

"I'm not here to save anything." He held up his hands, palms out, the old gesture of a man showing he carried no blade. "I'm looking for a courier. A man called Denner Voss, or so he was calling himself. Thin. Nervous. A limp he tried to hide. He would have come through here in the last two days."

Something moved behind her eyes. It was gone too fast for Bram to name it, but he had spent a lifetime reading faces in confessionals, and he knew the shape of what he'd seen. It was the look of a woman deciding how much of the truth a stranger could be trusted with.

"No one's come through here," she said. "The road past my gate goes nowhere but the village, and the village isn't expecting anyone."

"He was carrying a letter."

"Then he'll have delivered it, or he'll have lost it, or he'll have died in a ditch, and none of those things are my concern." She began to close the door. "Good day, priest."

"Veyra," Bram said.

The door stopped.

It stopped the way a bird stops mid-flight, all its motion arrested at once. For three heartbeats the woman stood with her hand on the wood, not moving, and the frost in the window behind her caught the light and threw it back in small cold stars.

"Say that again," she said quietly.

"Veyra." He said it the old way, the way it was said before the Concord scraped it from the rolls—*VAY-rah*, two syllables like a wave drawing back before it breaks. "The courier carried a plea addressed to the old gods. To anyone still listening. I've spent ten years listening, mistress—ah. Forgive me. I don't know your name."

For a moment he thought she would not give it. He thought she would shut the door and let him stand in her yard until his pride walked him back down the hill, and he would not have blamed her; he had spent ten years perfecting the art of the closed door himself.

"Jonah," she said at last. "Jonah Ravensworth. And you should know that name is a warning, priest, not an introduction."

"Ravensworth." He turned it over. The name meant nothing to him, and he said so, and something in her expression flickered—relief or disappointment, he could not tell which, and suspected she could not either.

"You'd better come in," she said, in the tone of a woman who already regretted it. "Before someone in the village sees you and decides my garden needs a priest's blessing."

* * *

Her kitchen was warm and smelled of rosemary and woodsmoke, and it contained, Bram noted with professional interest, no shrine of any kind. Not even a hidden one. He had learned to spot the hidden ones—a candle stub turned just so, a stone on a mantel polished by anxious thumbs. Jonah Ravensworth's house was a house that had scrubbed itself clean of the divine with the thoroughness of a woman scouring a stain.

She put tea in front of him without asking whether he wanted it, and sat across the table with her own cup untouched, and looked at him with the flat gray patience of a cat watching a mouse make its own decisions.

"Tell me about Veyra," she said.

It was not a request. Bram wrapped his hands around the cup and considered, for the first time in ten years, what it would be like to say it all out loud.

"She was a small god," he began. "That's what the Concord never understood, when they came for us. They thought a god's size was in her following, and so when they'd taken our temples and our tithes and our name from the rolls, they believed they'd taken everything. But Veyra was never large that way. She was the god of small things kept. Seeds in a drawer. Letters in a box. The last ember banked for morning." He smiled, and it hurt his face, because his face had forgotten how. "Farmers' god. Gleaners' god. The god you prayed to when you had nothing left to pray with except the keeping of what you had."

"And you were her priest."

"Last of them. The others recanted, or died, or went into the valley—" He stopped. He looked at her. She looked back at him, unblinking, and he understood suddenly that she was waiting to see if he would say the thing everyone said, the thing about the Ravensworth curse, the thing that had made a herb-woman in Marrow's Ford go quiet and sell him cheese instead of answers.

He did not say it.

"The others went wherever they went," he said instead. "I buried what was left. Her last altar-stone. Her prayer book. A lock of her—well. It doesn't matter. I buried her in a hole in the ground like a woman instead of a god, because that was all anyone would let her be at the end. And then I walked away from the grave and kept walking for ten years, and I told myself I was done." He drank the tea. It was too strong and slightly bitter with something medicinal—valerian, he thought, or feverfew. "And then a letter came through my hands with no address on it, and it was warm. Physically warm, mistress Ravensworth, like a living thing. And I remembered that I had spent my whole life learning to listen, and that a man does not stop being a thing just because the thing is no longer wanted."

Jonah Ravensworth had gone very still. Not the listening-stillness of the doorway—something rawer. Bram had the sense of a woman standing at the edge of a well she had spent a decade pretending was not there.

"The courier," she said. "Denner Voss. He came to my gate two nights ago. He was dying."

Bram set down the cup. The small sound of it against the table was very loud.

"Dying of what?"

"Wounds. Something had—there were marks on him. Like burns, but wrong. Cold burns." Her hands had wrapped around her own cup now, knuckles white. "He gave me the letter. He said it was addressed to anyone still listening, and that it had been warm the whole way from the capital, and that everyone who'd touched it before him had given it up because it frightened them. It didn't frighten me. It should have. It *hums*, priest. It's sitting in my back room right now, and I can feel it through the wall, like a bee trapped in glass."

"May I see it?"

"No."

The word came out fast and hard, and then she closed her eyes and breathed, and when she opened them again there was something in them that Bram recognized with a jolt, because he saw it in his own mirror every morning. It was the look of a person who had decided, long ago, that hope was a debt collector, and who had just heard a knock at the door anyway.

"Tell me why," she said. "Tell me why a dead god's last priest walked eleven days to chase a letter. Tell me what you think is listening."

And Bram Ostell, who had rehearsed a hundred careful answers on the road and trusted none of them, found himself saying the true thing, because her kitchen was warm and her frost was on the window and he was so tired of the closed door inside his own chest.

"I think the king is wearing something hungry," he said. "I think the edicts coming out of the capital are written in a hand that isn't his. I think the old gods are waking up whether the Concord likes it or not, and one of them woke up inside a crown, and no one has been praying to her, and she has been *starving*." He leaned forward. "And I think, mistress Ravensworth, that the letter on the other side of that wall is a prayer that finally found an address. And I think the address might be you."

"That's madness."

"Almost certainly."

"I'm not—I can't be an address. I'm a gardener." Her voice cracked on the word, and she hated it, he could see her hating it. "This valley is dead. That's the whole point of it. Nothing answers here. Nothing has answered for ten years."

"Then explain the frost."

The silence that followed was the longest of the day. Outside, a wind came down off the ridge and moved through the garden, and every plant bowed to it in turn, sage and fennel and comfrey, like a congregation.

Jonah Ravensworth rose without a word and went to the back room, and Bram heard a latch, and then she returned carrying a packet wrapped in oilcloth, and even across the room he could feel it—the warmth of it, the low animal hum of a thing alive with purpose. She set it on the table between them the way one sets down a snake one has decided not to kill.

"Open it," she said. "You're the listener. Let's see what it says to you."

He unwrapped the oilcloth. Inside lay a single sheet of vellum, sealed with wax that bore no sigil at all—just a thumbprint, pressed deep, as if the sender had wanted to leave a piece of themselves with it. The paper was warm as a sleeping bird. And as Bram's fingers closed around it, the frost on the kitchen window behind Jonah bloomed suddenly wider, spirals of white racing across the glass, and the humming in the letter rose to a note that Bram felt in his teeth, in his breastbone, in the buried altar-stone of his own long-buried faith.

He broke the seal and read it aloud, because some words are not meant to be read in silence:

*To anyone still listening. The king is not the king. The crown is not a crown. I sent for help and I no longer know if I am the one who sent. If you can hear this—she is starving, and a starving god will eat the world before she admits her hunger. There is a storm-caller in Hollowmere. Find her. Tell her the sky remembers her. Tell her—*

Bram stopped. His mouth had gone dry.

"Tell her what?" Jonah whispered. She had come around the table without seeming to move, and her hand had found the letter's edge, and where her fingers touched the vellum the frost on the window flared so bright it threw their shadows hard against the far wall.

"The rest is smudged," Bram said. "Water damage, or—" He turned the page toward the light, and stopped again, because it was not water damage. The final line had been *burned* away, scorched from the vellum by something that had wanted it unread, and the scorch-mark did not fade into the paper the way fire leaves paper. It sat on the surface like a bruise, and it was cold to the touch, and it was shaped, if a man squinted, like a handprint.

Jonah was staring at the window. At the frost. At the spirals that were still turning there, patient and regular, like a thing waiting to be answered.

"The sky remembers me," she said, in a voice Bram had last heard in the mouths of the dying. "Priest, what did you say the king was wearing?"

"Something hungry."

She looked at him then, and the wariness between them—the two strangers measuring each other across a kitchen table, each certain the other was the more dangerous—did not break so much as *settle*, the way two animals settle when they realize they are in the same trap.

"Then you'd better tell me everything," Jonah Ravensworth said, "before I decide whether to burn that letter or answer it."

Outside, far to the north, past the ridge and the still gray cloud, thunder rolled—a single, distant, deliberate sound, like a knock at a door.

In a valley where magic could not follow.

Bram looked at Jonah. Jonah looked at the window.

Neither of them said what they were both thinking: that the valley had just answered back.
