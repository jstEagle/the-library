## 5. Terms of Exile

The bread had gone stale overnight, which was fitting, because so had the argument.

Jonah set the loaf on the table between them like a peace offering neither of them wanted, and Bram Ostell looked at it the way he'd been looking at everything since dawn — as though it might be a message from a god who no longer took his calls.

"You should eat," she said.

"You should listen." He didn't touch the bread. He sat across from her with his hands flat on the scarred wood, a big man gone soft at the edges, his priest's collar replaced by a traveler's wool that had seen too many roads and washed too few times. His eyes were the problem. Eyes like that didn't belong in Hollowmere. They were city eyes, council-chamber eyes, eyes that had watched men decide things and then lived with the decisions. "The courier came here for a reason, Jonah. He didn't stumble over the ridge. Men don't stumble over the ridge. The valley doesn't let them."

"The valley doesn't *do* anything. It's a hole in the world where magic forgot to grow."

"That's exactly what I mean." He leaned forward. "You think a dead place stays dead out of accident? Something made it dead. Something holds it dead. And whatever holds it — it held long enough to keep you alive when every other storm-caller in the realm was burned or bound or driven into the sea."

The kettle muttered on the hearth. Jonah watched steam thread up from its spout and thought about how, ten years ago, she would have read an omen in that. She had been taught to read omens the way farm children were taught to read weather — before the weather became the thing everyone blamed her for.

"Bram."

"Yes."

"I buried my family under fourteen feet of wet earth. My mother. My father. My brother Tam, who was nine and afraid of geese." Her voice came out level, which surprised her; she had expected it to crack the way it always cracked when she went near this, the way a floorboard cracks that has never quite been repaired. "The council spared me because killing a child looked worse than exiling one, and because Hollowmere was already there — a grave with a fence around it. They didn't spare me out of mercy. They spared me because I was pre-contained. You don't get to walk into my kitchen and tell me that my prison was actually a fortress, and that I owe it my sword."

"I'm not asking for your sword."

"You're asking for exactly that. You just want to call it something gentler."

* * *

The storm had come in off the western hills on the last night of harvest, ten years and one month ago, and Jonah had called it without meaning to, the way a sleeper cries out without meaning to.

She remembered it in pieces, the way you remember anything unbearable: in fragments sharp enough to cut. The sky going the color of a bruise at sunset. The pressure behind her sternum, that old familiar ache she'd been taught to breathe through, to sit with, to *starve* — the discipline of the Ravensworth line, weather-callers going back nine generations, all of them trained from the cradle to hold the door shut. Her grandmother's voice in her head: *The storm is always at the door, little one. Your whole life is learning not to open it.*

And then Tam, screaming from the orchard, because the branch he'd climbed had cracked, and he was hanging above the drop with his legs kicking, and their parents were down at the far field, and there was no one, no one at all, except a fourteen-year-old girl who knew one thing in the world with perfect certainty: *she could catch him.*

Wind could catch him. Wind could cradle him down like a leaf. She had done it before, small things, stolen things — a hat lifted off a fencepost, a kite pulled back from a tree — little thefts of gravity that felt like the only honest use of what she was.

So she opened the door.

Not all the way. She would tell herself that forever after, and it would even be true. She opened it a crack, a keyhole, a breath — enough to pull one boy out of one tree. But the storm had been waiting at that door for fourteen years, and storms do not understand fractions. What came through her came through like a river through a failed dam, and the sky over the Ravensworth farm turned black at noon-dark, and lightning came down in ropes, and the oak that Tam had climbed — the tree she'd meant to set him gently beneath — took the strike instead of her hands taking him, and the whole row of them went down together: tree, boy, father running toward the light, mother running toward the father.

Three heartbeats. That was all the storm needed. Three heartbeats of being wanted, truly wanted, invited — and it ate a family.

They told her afterward that the funnel had been visible from the county seat. They told her the council had argued until dawn. They told her, in the end, in the gray room with the seven hooded chairs, that the law said burn, and the law also said that Hollowmere existed precisely so that the law could choose not to.

*"The valley will hold her,"* the eldest councilor had said, in a voice like a man signing something he hoped he'd never have to read again. *"Nothing crosses the ridge. Nothing answers her there. Let her live in the silence she made."*

Let her live in the silence she made. As if the silence were her punishment. As if she hadn't been living in it since the day the door first appeared in her chest.

* * *

"You're doing it again," Bram said.

Jonah blinked. The kitchen came back around her: the stale bread, the muttering kettle, the courier's body laid out with what dignity she could manage in her front room, stiffening under a sheet that had once been a curtain.

"Doing what?"

"Going somewhere else. Somewhere with rain in it." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "I know that look. I wore it for twenty years in the temple of Veyra. Every time someone said her name, I went somewhere else. Somewhere with incense in it." He rubbed his face with both hands, and for a moment he looked less like a zealot and more like what he probably was underneath: tired. Old in the way grief makes people old rather than years. "Jonah. I am not asking you to forgive yourself. Gods know I haven't managed it, and my sin was only standing in a temple while my god starved. I'm asking you to look at arithmetic."

"There's no arithmetic."

"There is. Here." He counted on blunt fingers. "One: the prayers are getting through again. All over the realm, at shrines that have been mute for three hundred years, people are hearing *something* answer. Two: the king is dying, or worse than dying, and whatever wears him sent a courier through a magic-dead valley to find a woman who hasn't cast so much as a cantrip in a decade. Three: the courier carried a seal I recognized, because I spent my life studying the old orders, and that seal belonged to the office that bound Veyra into the crown in the first place." He stopped counting and spread his empty hands. "Four: the valley is not going to hold. Whatever silence sits over this place, it's thinning. You've felt it."

"I haven't."

"Liar." He nodded past her, toward the window. "Frost on your panes. In high summer. I saw it when I came up your path yesterday morning, and I've seen it twice more since. Frost takes shape, Jonah. It doesn't take *accident*. Something in you is waking up because something outside the ridge is calling, and you can pretend the two aren't connected right up until the day they arrive together."

Jonah didn't look at the window. She looked at the table, at the grain of the wood her own hands had planed, at the ring her teacup had worn into it over ten patient years. Ten years of rings and planings and plantings. Ten years of a life built small on purpose, the way you build a wall against a floodplain.

"You want to know why I won't help you?" she said quietly. "It isn't fear. Well. It isn't only fear."

"Then what?"

"It's that the last time I believed I was the answer to someone's emergency, four people died. Tam screamed for help and I answered, and answering was the worst thing I ever did. So now a king I've never met sends a dying man to my gate with a letter that hums, and everyone wants Jonah Ravensworth to *answer* again." She finally raised her eyes to his. "Hope is just guilt wearing nicer clothes, Bram. I've met both. They have the same voice."

For a long moment the priest said nothing. The kettle climbed toward a boil behind them, and neither moved to take it off the heat, and the frost on the windowpanes — she allowed herself one glance — had grown a fern-patterned finger longer since breakfast.

"You're wrong about one thing," Bram said at last. "About what happened in your orchard."

"Don't."

"A child called for help. A child with a gift she'd been taught to hate and hide and starve, and no one — no priest, no teacher, no council — ever told her the truth about it. That it wasn't a disease. That it wasn't a curse. That power doesn't ask whether your reasons are pure; it only asks whether you'll use it, and then it uses *you* right back." His voice roughened. "That was our sin, mine and my order's. We caged Veyra because we were afraid of her hunger, and then we abandoned the cage, and then we taught the whole realm that the old powers were monsters to be walled up. You weren't the failure, Jonah. You were the bill coming due on three hundred years of failure. And now the bill has reached the crown itself, and it's eating a king, and it will not stop at the ridge. Hunger like that doesn't stop. It only waits."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then I've wasted a walk in the country." He almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, worn at one corner, like a tool kept past its usefulness. "But I watched a man die on your floor yesterday, and his last word wasn't 'help.' It was 'Hollowmere.' He crawled over the most dangerous ridge in the realm, gut-shot, half-frozen, to reach a gardener. Someone told him you mattered. Someone wearing a crown, in the lucid scraps of time he can still steal from the thing that eats him, chose *you*, of all the names in the realm, and trusted you with his last free act." Bram stood, joints cracking, and gathered his cloak from the back of the chair. "I'm leaving within the hour. There's a shrine two days east where the road to the capital begins, and I mean to be on that road. Come or don't. But before you decide, you should read the rest of what he carried."

Jonah went cold in a way that had nothing to do with frost. "The rest?"

"He had two letters sewn into his coat. One was sealed to the old gods — the one that sang to you, the one addressed to anyone still listening. The other—" Bram drew it from inside his shirt, a slim packet wrapped in oilcloth, and set it on the table between them with the care of a man laying down a card he suspected might win or lose everything. "The other was sealed with the royal signet and addressed by name. I didn't open it. It isn't mine. But I'll tell you this much: whoever wrote it knew things about the Ravensworth family that no living soul should know. Knew about your grandmother's binding-work. Knew about the door."

He walked to the doorway and paused there, a silhouette against the pale summer light, and the frost on the windows crept another inch while neither of them pretended not to notice.

"Read it or burn it," Bram Ostell said. "But decide quickly. Because the silence over this valley is thinning, Jonah, and I don't think it's waiting for your permission — and neither, I suspect, is whatever wrote that letter."

Then he was gone, boots crunching down the gravel path toward the road, and Jonah sat alone in her kitchen with the dead courier in the next room and the king's letter lying on her table like a seed that hadn't yet decided what to grow into.

Outside, far to the west, past the ridge and the ridge's ridge, thunder rolled — soft, distant, patient.

It sounded, she thought, exactly like knocking.
