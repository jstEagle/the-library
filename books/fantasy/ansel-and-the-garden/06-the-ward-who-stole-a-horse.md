## 6. The Ward Who Stole a Horse

The horse was not stolen. Sef had decided this on the first night and held to it like a rosary: the horse was *borrowed*, and she intended to return him, and returning a thing was the opposite of stealing it. The fact that she had taken him at midnight, without leave, through a gate she'd bribed a groom to leave unlatched—those were details for later, when she was safe, when she could afford honesty.

His name was Copperpot, which was an undignified name for a warhorse, which was why nobody would look for him. He belonged to the third string of the royal stables, the horses too old for parade and too proud for ploughs, and he had looked at her over his stall door with one liquid brown eye as if to say, *finally, someone with sense.* She had talked to him the whole way out of the city, whispering into his mane, because her voice might shake if she let it out loud and shaking voices got caught.

Twelve years old, four foot eleven in her boots, disguised in a stable boy's cast-off coat that smelled of liniment and other people's sweat, Sef Marrow rode out of the capital of Vhalen with a dead man's letters sewn into her shirt and did not cry until the city walls were behind her and the dawn fog swallowed them.

Then she cried for exactly two hundred breaths. She counted. It was a trick she'd invented herself, back when she was six and newly arrived at the palace, an orphan of the river fever that had emptied half the low quarters: grief was permitted, but only in measured doses, because unmeasured grief made you stupid and stupid people died. Two hundred breaths of crying, and then she wiped her face on Copperpot's mane—which he tolerated with the weary patience of the old—and sat up straight in the saddle, and became no one.

That was the whole art of it, really. Becoming no one. She had been practicing since before she could write.

* * *

The letters had come to her by accident, which was how all the important things in Sef's life seemed to arrive: sideways, unwelcome, and heavy.

She was a ward of the crown, which meant she ate at the second table, wore cast-down clothes from the queen's memorial wardrobe, and was educated alongside children whose fathers owned provinces. It also meant she was invisible, and invisibility was a currency, and Sef had learned to spend it. She carried messages between servants' stairs. She knew which pantry keys stuck and which guards drank on the night shift and which of the king's secretaries kept a mistress in the tanners' quarter. A ward who made herself useful was tolerated. A ward who made herself useful *and* forgettable was safe.

Three weeks ago, Master Ellery—the old under-secretary, the one who smelled of ink and cough drops and had once smuggled her honey cakes during a fasting week—had pressed a folio into her hands in the linen corridor and said, "Count the words, girl. Just count them," and walked away, and two days later he was dead of a fall down the north stair, though everyone agreed the north stair had never been slippery in forty years.

So she had counted the words.

The king's edicts came off his desk in the king's hand—or they were supposed to. Aldric Vane had ruled for nineteen years, and his handwriting was famous, taught to clerks as a model: strong vertical strokes, a distinctive crossed double-t on words like *letter* and *better*. Sef had copied it a hundred times in her lessons. Every child in the palace school had.

The edicts in the folio were written in something very close to it. Close enough to fool a clerk glancing at a signature. But the double-t's weren't crossed. And there was a hesitation in the letterforms, a certain careful slowness, the way a forger writes when he is copying rather than composing—as if the hand holding the pen knew the shape of the letters but not their weight.

And the edicts themselves. That was the part that had turned her blood to well water. New levies on the shrine districts. Dissolution of the temple courts. An order that prayers to the old gods be reported as sedition. Each one reasonable-sounding alone, each one wrapped in the language of order and prosperity—but strung together, they read like someone dismantling the kingdom joint by joint, and doing it in the king's name while the king himself had not been seen in public for five months except at a distance, veiled, silent, his face pale as candle wax.

Sef had done what any sensible person would do. She had gone looking for an adult to tell.

That was where the plan failed, because every adult she approached either didn't want to hear it or already knew, and the ones who already knew looked at her afterward with a new attention that made her skin crawl. On the ninth day, a maid she'd trusted told her, very quietly, while folding sheets, "You should be sick, little mouse. Sick girls get sent to the country." And Sef understood that the window was closing, and that the next person she spoke to might not warn her first.

So: the horse, the coat, the letters unpicked from their folio and sewn flat against her ribs beneath the binding cloth that flattened what little there was of her. She couldn't read all the courtly script—not yet, she was still learning—but she knew what she was carrying the way you know a hot coal through a glove. Proof that the hand signing the king's will was not the king's will. Proof that somebody, somewhere, needed to see it who wasn't already bought or frightened or both.

There was exactly one name left on her list. Her old tutor, before the palace tutors replaced him—a hedge-priest named Ostell who had taught her letters in exchange for bread and had never once asked her to forget anything she learned. He'd been dismissed years ago for preaching about gods nobody prayed to anymore. Dismissed priests went to the borders. Borders were away from the palace. Away was the direction she needed.

Copperpot carried her east at a steady canter that felt, to her untrained body, like flying and dying in equal measure.

* * *

By the fourth day she had learned that horses eat constantly, that roads fork more often than maps admit, and that fear lives in the body long after the mind has moved on—it sat in her shoulders like a yoke, in her bladder like cold water, in her ears, which kept inventing hoofbeats behind her.

She had also learned that she was good at this, and the knowledge frightened her worse than pursuit did. She stole eggs from a coop and left a copper on the sill and hated herself a little for how easily her fingers did it. She talked her way past a toll-keeper with a lie about delivering medicine to her uncle, and the lie came out smooth and warm and detailed, and afterward she sat under a hedgerow shaking, not because she'd nearly been caught but because she hadn't. Because some part of her had *enjoyed* it.

*Marrow brats survive,* her mother used to say, back before the fever took her and everything else. *We're weeds, us Marrows. You don't pull a weed by being sorry about it.* Sef clung to that. Weeds survived. Weeds grew in cracks. Nobody wrote ballads about weeds, and that was fine; ballads got people killed.

On the fifth day the rain found her, a thin persistent drizzle that soaked through the stable coat and turned the road to grease. By dusk she was lost in a way that mattered: the map in her head said there should be a village, and instead there was a darkening moor and a single thread of woodsmoke rising from somewhere ahead-left, and Copperpot was lame in his near forefoot, favoring it with each step in a way that squeezed her heart.

"There's a shrine," she told him, because talking to the horse had become the only honest conversation available to her. "Smoke means people. People mean shelter. Shelter means I don't die stupid in a ditch." She paused. "Also they might feed us."

The shrine, when she found it, was a disappointment of a holy place: a squat stone building crouched in a fold of the moor, its roof mossed green, its door hanging open on leather hinges worn soft as cloth. No god-mark above the lintel that she recognized. Inside, one room, one hearth, one long-cold altar stone worn smooth in the middle like a loaf kneaded by centuries of hands. Travelers' custom, clearly—there were pegs for wet cloaks, a stack of dry peat, a notice board thick with layered bills and blessings, some decades old.

Sef saw all of this in the time it took to lead Copperpot under the lean-to shelter beside the wall, and what she felt, examining it, was not comfort but calculation. One door. One window, shuttered. Hearth smoke visible for a mile, which meant anyone following her could find it just as easily as she had.

But the alternative was a lame horse and a wet night on open moor, and Sef Marrow was a weed, not a martyr.

She rubbed Copperpot down with handfuls of straw, checked his hoof—stone bruise, nothing worse, thank whatever listened—and carried her saddlebags inside. Then she built a small fire, small being the operative word, barely enough to heat water, and sat with her back to the wall where she could see both door and window, and ate half a heel of bread, and told herself firmly that her hands were not trembling.

They were trembling. She put them under her arms and waited for whoever the smoke would summon.

* * *

It summoned two of them, which was one more than she'd budgeted for.

She heard the horse first—no, horses, two sets of hooves on the wet track—and was on her feet with her knife out before her mind caught up with her body, pressed flat against the wall beside the doorframe, breath shallow, the way she'd watched the palace guards stand when they expected trouble.

Voices outside. A man's, low and tired: "...said there was smoke. Shrines keep. It's the whole point of them."

And another voice, a woman's, answering with a reluctance so profound it was almost funny even through Sef's terror: "Shrines keep for travelers. We are not travelers. We are a mistake walking beside a catastrophe."

The door eased inward. Sef counted—one, two, three—and stepped out of the wall's shadow with her knife up and her voice pitched low, the way she'd practiced in her head for five days.

"Drop your bags and kick them over."

It should have worked. It was a good line. The problem was the man had already seen her, and what he saw was evidently so far from threatening that he simply stopped and stared, rain dripping off his hood, and after a moment said, with genuine scholarly interest, "You're about nine years too young to be robbing shrines."

"I'm twelve," Sef snapped, furious at herself, at her voice cracking upward on the word, "and I'll go nine years younger on you if you take another step."

The woman appeared behind him in the doorway, and Sef revised everything. The man was nondescript, gray, travel-worn, a priest's tonsure grown out ragged—but the woman was wrong in a way that raised every hair on Sef's arms. Not wrong-looking. Wrong-*weathered*. There was a stillness in her, a coiled watchfulness, like a house where every door is locked from the inside. Her eyes went from the knife, to Sef's face, to the fire, to the corners of the room, cataloguing exits, and Sef recognized the habit because she owned it herself.

"We're not going to hurt you," the woman said. Her voice was flat, unhurried, the voice of someone stating weather. "Put the knife down or don't. It makes no difference to me."

"It makes a difference to me," Sef said.

Something flickered across the woman's face—not quite a smile, but the ghost of where a smile might have lived once, before it was evicted. "Good answer."

The man hung his wet cloak on a peg, entirely ignoring the knife, and knelt at the hearth to feed the fire, and Sef realized with a lurch that he was moving around the room like a man who knew it—who set the peat in the pattern the stack suggested, who touched the altar stone in passing with two fingers, briefly, like greeting an old colleague.

"You're a priest," she said.

"I'm a gardener of souls currently between parishes," he said. "Bram Ostell. This is Jonah, who is not a priest, and would like that noted."

"Noted," Sef said, not lowering the knife. "I'm—" and here her prepared lie rose to her lips, smooth as oiled leather, *Tam, stable boy, going to my uncle's* —and then stalled. Because the woman, Jonah, had gone very still, and her eyes had dropped to Sef's chest, to the place where the binding cloth flattened her, and there was nothing leering in the look, nothing at all—just a sharp, tired accuracy, the look of someone reading a page others thought was shut.

"The letters," Jonah said quietly. "Under your shirt. Who are you running from?"

The room went narrow and ringing. Sef's grip shifted on the knife. *Stupid, stupid, the way you sit, the way you guard your ribs, you've been wearing your fear on the outside for five days—*

"They're not mine," she heard herself say, and hated how young it sounded. "I'm carrying them. For someone who died. Master Ellery, he was the under-secretary, he fell down a stair that isn't slippery, and he gave me these and said count the words, and I counted them, and they're—" Her voice did the cracking thing again and she despised it. "The king's edicts are written in a hand that isn't his. Somebody's writing the king. And everybody in the palace either knows and doesn't care, or cares and disappears, and I wasn't going to disappear, I decided that, I decided it myself, nobody decided it for me—"

She ran out of air. The knife point had drifted down without her permission. The rain filled the silence against the roof, patient and gray.

Bram Ostell had gone white. Not shocked-white; *recognized*-white, the color of a man hearing a bell he'd spent years pretending was broken. He looked at Jonah, and Jonah looked back, and something passed between them that Sef couldn't read—some old argument resuming mid-sentence.

"How many days ahead of you is the capital's reach?" Bram asked her.

"I don't know. Four? Five?"

"And you were riding east." He exhaled slowly. "Child, the road you're on doesn't go anywhere but deeper into what you're fleeing. There's a courier we were meant to meet—an old route, a prayer-route, older than the crown—and he's three weeks late, and the valley folk say strangers have been asking after him on the roads. Asking with soldiers' manners."

Sef's stomach dropped through the floor stones. "Soldiers."

"Or something wearing them," Jonah said. She had not moved from the doorway, but now she crossed the room, unhurried, and crouched down—deliberately, Sef realized, putting herself below Sef's eye level, making herself smaller, making it a choice whether to be threatened—and held out one calloused, earth-stained hand, palm up. Empty.

"Show me the letters," Jonah said. "Not because I'm owed them. Because right now, tonight, in this wet rock pile, you're the only person in the world carrying proof, and I'm the only person in the world who believes you before seeing it. That makes us rare. Rarer than we'd like."

Outside, far off across the moor, thunder muttered—low, directionless, a sound with no storm attached to it. Sef saw the woman flinch, a small involuntary tightening, gone almost before it started, and filed it away the way she filed everything: *afraid of storms, the dangerous one is afraid of storms, remember that.*

But her hand was still out. Open. Waiting.

Sef looked at it for a long moment. Then she pulled her knife's edge in, reached under her shirt, and began picking out the stitches—slowly, watching both their faces, keeping the knife within reach the whole time, because she was a weed and weeds survived, but survival, she was beginning to suspect, was not the same thing as doing it alone.

The thunder muttered again. Closer now. And this time, Jonah Coldharbour's eyes went to the shuttered window, and stayed there, and did not move.
