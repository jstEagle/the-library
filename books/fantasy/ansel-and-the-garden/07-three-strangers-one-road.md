## 7. Three Strangers, One Road

*Sef*

The horse was the best decision Sef had ever made, and she'd made it in under a minute, which was how all her best decisions worked.

She'd named him Turnip because a guard who laughed at you was a guard who wasn't watching your hands, and by the time the stable master realized the scrawny "boy" mucking stalls had led the king's own bay gelding out the postern gate, Sef had been three miles gone and learning that horses, unlike people, could not be charmed into doing what you wanted.

Turnip did not care for the road to the capital. He cared for it even less when the woman with the frost-colored eyes insisted they walk him down the steep switchbacks instead of riding, and he cared for it least of all when the priest started singing.

"He's not singing," Sef said, from her place at the horse's head. "He's praying. There's a difference."

"There isn't," Jonah Ravensworth said flatly. She walked with her hands jammed in her coat pockets and her shoulders up around her ears like a hedgehog defending its soft parts. "One of them expects an answer."

Sef snorted. She'd been on the road with these two for four days now and she still couldn't decide which of them was more useless. The priest—Bram—was built like a door and prayed like a man talking to a wall he knew was a wall. The gardener was worse: she looked at thunderclouds the way other people looked at open graves.

But they were going where Sef was going, and two adults drew fewer eyes than one child alone, so here they were. Three strangers pretending to be a family. Sef had played a lot of families in her time—a merchant's daughter, a tinker's niece, once a funeral mourner for a man she'd pickpocketed at the graveside—and she could say with authority that this one would fool nobody.

"Turnip needs water at the next ford," she announced, in the voice she used when she wanted to sound like the sensible one. Nobody questioned a child who sounded sensible. That was the whole trick of it.

"There's a village past the ford," Bram called from the back, breathless from the grade. "Shrine to the River Mother, if it's still kept. We should make offerings."

"To what?" Jonah said.

Bram didn't answer, which was his way of answering. Sef glanced back at him. He had the look priests got when someone asked them the question underneath all their questions—the one about whether anyone was actually home on the other side of the prayer.

* * *

*Bram*

Bram Ostell had buried his god eleven years ago, and he had never quite managed to stop talking to the grave.

It wasn't faith anymore. Faith required belief, and Bram's belief had died in a small stone chapel in the Greymarch with the last of Veyra's congregation, all nine of them, scattered by a king's edict and a fire that had been set, officially, by accident. What remained was habit, and habit was a kind of loyalty, and loyalty was the only thing he had left of her.

So he prayed to nothing, every morning and evening, kneeling on whatever ground would have him, and this morning the ground was wet shale above the ford and the nothing answered with river-sound and wind and the distant complaint of a child arguing with a horse.

"—and if you let him drink too fast he'll colic, and then we're walking, and I am *not* carrying your bag, priest—"

"She has a point," Bram said, coming down the bank.

"She has many points. It's her worst quality." Jonah stood hip-deep in the shallows' edge, sleeves rolled, holding Turnip's bridle while the horse drank in careful pulls. She'd counted the swallows. Bram had watched her do it—counted each one aloud under her breath, patient as liturgy—and something about it had snagged in him for days.

A gardener who counted like that. A valley-woman whose windowpanes frosted over in high summer. Whose hands went still and strange around anything old and holy, the way a tuning fork goes still before it sings.

"You count his breaths," Bram said.

Jonah didn't look up. "Horses bloat."

"That's not why you do it."

The silence stretched. Downstream, Sef was crouched at the ford's edge doing something with her hands that she thought neither of them could see—working a leather purse out of a pilgrim's dropped pack, quick as water finding a crack. Bram decided, in the spirit of their fragile alliance, not to have seen it.

"I counted my sister's breaths once," Jonah said finally, to the horse, to the river, to no one. "When she was sick. I was seven. She lived." She straightened, pulled the bridle free, wrung water from her sleeve. "Afterward I just—kept counting things. Breaths. Swallows. Steps between lightning and thunder." Her jaw tightened. "Five seconds means the storm is a mile away. Everyone knows that. I've known it since I was fourteen."

Bram said nothing. There were prayers for moments like this, in the old rite—not prayers of asking but prayers of witnessing. *We see what was carried. We name it holy.* He'd forgotten most of the words. He suspected they wouldn't help anyway.

"We should reach the shrine by dusk," he said instead. "There may be shelter."

"And maybe the god of rivers will be home," Sef said, appearing between them with the pilgrim's purse already vanished somewhere into her ragged coat. She grinned up at Bram, gap-toothed and shameless. "Maybe she'll give us dinner."

"The River Mother doesn't give dinner."

"All gods give something. You just have to know which pocket they keep it in." She patted the coat sagely and walked on ahead, leading the horse, twelve years old and armored entirely in nerve.

Bram watched her go and felt the old ache shift in his chest—not grief exactly, closer to recognition. Somewhere in Veyra's scripture there had been a line about the faithful being like children gathering crumbs under the table of heaven. He'd always read it as humble.

Watching Sef work the world with her quick fingers, he suddenly understood it differently. Crumbs were food. Children knew that. Only grown men starved themselves out of dignity.

* * *

*Jonah*

The village past the ford was called Marrowbridge, and it had stopped keeping its shrine.

Jonah felt it before they reached the square—that same wrongness she'd been feeling for days now, faint as pressure behind the ears, like the moment before a summer storm when the air forgets how to move. In Hollowmere such a feeling would have been impossible. Magic did not cross the ridge. For ten years the valley had been the quietest place in the world, and Jonah had built her whole life inside that quiet like a ship in a bottle.

Now the quiet was leaking.

"It's not abandoned," Bram said quietly, standing before the little river-shrine. It was a modest thing, a bowl-shaped hollow in a mossy stone where travelers had left offerings for generations. But the offerings were recent—bread gone hard, a copper coin, a child's ribbon tied in a desperate knot—and around the stone, scratched into the flagstones in a dozen different hands, were words.

*Please hear us.*

*We are loyal. We are waiting.*

*Take the whispering away.*

Jonah crouched and touched the stone. Nothing happened—no surge, no song, only cold rock and the faint hum she'd been hearing since the courier died at her gate, the hum of paper addressed to anyone still listening. But the pressure behind her ears deepened, and far off to the west, over the hills toward the capital, clouds were gathering that had no business gathering on a clear afternoon.

Her whole body flinched. She hated that it flinched. Ten years of discipline, ten years of being small and safe and magic-dead, and still her skin remembered what it was for.

"You feel it too," Bram said. Not a question.

"I feel weather." She stood, wiped her hand on her coat. "Weather is honest. It doesn't scratch pleas into stones."

"These aren't pleas to the crown." Bram traced one of the inscriptions with a blunt finger. "Look at the wording. 'Take the whispering away.' The edicts out of the capital have changed tone in the last months—I heard it in the border towns. Less law, more liturgy. The king's proclamations used to be written like a ledger. Now they're written like a hymn." He paused. "Something in that palace is hungry, and it's eating faith, and the countryside is lining up to be eaten."

Sef had gone very still by the horse. Jonah noticed it the way she noticed everything about the girl now—the way you notice weather. "You've seen the edicts," Jonah said.

Sef's chin came up, all ferocious self-reliance, but her eyes flicked sideways first. Twelve years old and already fluent in lying, except with her eyes. "I've seen writing," she said. "I know hands. I copy documents for— I used to copy documents." The correction came half a beat too late. "The king's chancery hand is dry and slants left. The new edicts slant right and press hard, like the writer is digging the pen into the page. Same seal. Different soul." She shrugged, elaborate and careless. "Anyone could see it."

"Anyone," Bram murmured, "who had spent years in the palace scriptorium."

Sef turned away to fuss with Turnip's girth strap, and Jonah let her. There were things a person carried that couldn't be pried loose by questioning, only by patience, and Jonah was learning—slowly, against her will—that these two were teaching her patience again. The garden had taught her everything else. It had not taught her this: how to stand near people without counting the steps to the gate.

They took a room at the inn because Bram insisted and Sef picked the lock of the shuttered stable because no one else could, and by full dark they were fed, exhausted, and arranged around the room's single table like survivors of a shipwreck sharing driftwood.

That was when the birds stopped.

* * *

*Bram*

Bram woke before dawn, as he always did, to pray—and found the silence already waiting for him.

It took him a long moment to understand what was wrong. Silence in a village at dawn was normal enough. But this silence had a texture. It was the silence of held breath. Of an audience.

He rose, crossed to the window, and looked out over Marrowbridge in the grey first light, and understood.

No birds. No sparrows quarreling in the eaves, no wrens in the hedgerows, no crows walking the plowed fields beyond the bridge. He leaned out further, scanning the rooftops, the riverbank, the bare trees along the road—and saw them. Birds everywhere. Hundreds of them. Perched motionless on wires and branches and fence posts, packed shoulder to shoulder in a way birds never gathered, every small head turned west.

Toward the capital. Toward the road they meant to take.

"Bram." Jonah's voice behind him, low and rough with sleep—or with something older than sleep. She'd come awake silently; he hadn't heard her cross the room. She stood beside him at the window, and her breath fogged the glass despite the mild night. Frost feathered outward from where her hand rested on the sill, delicate ferns of ice blooming across the wood, and she stared at it with an expression he recognized from his own mirror, years ago: horror at one's own body remembering what it was.

"They're not sleeping," Jonah whispered. "Birds sleep with their heads turned back, into the wing. These are—" She swallowed. "These are listening."

"To what?"

"Not what." She turned her frost-grey eyes on him, and for the first time since the courier had died at her gate, Jonah Ravensworth looked afraid in a way she couldn't disguise with gardening or sarcasm or counting. "Who. Something knows we're coming, Bram. Something with enough reach to hush every bird on forty miles of road."

Downstairs, a floorboard creaked—Sef, already awake, already moving. Bram heard her stop at the foot of the stairs, heard the long pause of a child taking inventory of a silence, and then her voice floated up, pitched carefully light:

"There's no birdsong. I noticed it last night, actually. Thought it was the weather." A beat. "It's not the weather, is it."

"No," Jonah said.

"Good." Sef appeared in the doorway below, small and wiry in her stolen stable clothes, and her grin had teeth in it now, the grin she wore when fear got converted into forward motion. "Because I stole breakfast for three, and I'd hate for it to be wasted on sitting here waiting to find out what's listening. If something wants us—" she swung the pack onto her shoulder, "—it can explain itself to my face."

Bram looked at Jonah. Jonah looked at the frost on the windowsill, at the road beyond the bridge, at the west where the clouds were massing again, patient and organized, like an army or a congregation.

"We walk fast today," she said at last. "And tonight—" her hand closed over the windowsill, and the frost spread a finger's width further, and she let it, "—tonight we need to talk about what I can do. All of it. Before it decides to do it without me."

They left Marrowbridge as the sun cleared the ridge, three strangers walking a silent road, and behind them, bird by bird, the hedgerows emptied as the watchers lifted westward ahead of them—carrying word, or simply carrying witness, toward the city that prayed too loud.

At the top of the first hill, Sef glanced back once. "For what it's worth," she said, "I'm glad it's you two. Out of everyone in the world it could've been." Then she hurried ahead, mortified by her own sincerity, and did not look back again.

Jonah watched her go, and Bram watched Jonah, and neither of them said the thing that all three of them were thinking, which was that somewhere in the last hundred miles, without anyone consenting to it, *strangers* had quietly stopped being the right word.
