## 8. The God Who Answers

The chapel found them before they found it.

They had been walking three days out of Hollowmere's shadow, following the old pilgrim road that wound down through the hill country toward the capital, and on the fourth morning the fog lifted to reveal it standing in a fold of the land like a broken tooth — a chapel of grey stone, roofless, its bell tower snapped off halfway up so that the remaining arch framed nothing but sky.

Jonah stopped at the edge of the clearing and did not move for a long moment.

She knew this place. Not this chapel specifically, but this *kind* of place — every valley child knew them. The ruins of the old faith dotted the countryside the way scars dot skin: shrines to gods whose names had been scrubbed from liturgy, whose statues had been chiseled faceless by reformers two centuries dead, whose altars were now used by farmers to sharpen scythes and by children to play at kings.

"Veyra's order had chapels like this once," Bram said quietly, coming up beside her. He was studying the ruin with an expression she couldn't read — grief folded over guilt folded over something almost like tenderness. "Before the Concord. Before my predecessors decided it was kinder to let the old gods starve quietly than to feed something they couldn't control."

"Cheerful fellow," Sef muttered. She was walking the perimeter the way she always did in new places, cataloguing exits, counting sightlines, one hand near the knife at her belt even though the only threat in view was a crow picking at moss on the altar steps. Twelve years old and built like a whipcord, all elbows and suspicion, with a stable boy's cropped hair growing out unevenly under her cap. "Ruined chapel, middle of nowhere, no roof. Perfect place to sleep, says I. No roof means no corners for anyone to hide in."

"It also means rain," Jonah said.

"I've slept in rain." Sef said it flatly, without bravado, and Jonah felt the familiar small ache she'd been carrying since the border shrine — the ache of realizing what kind of life a twelve-year-old must have lived to say that sentence without flinching.

"We'll make camp in the nave," Bram decided. "There's shelter enough under the tower stub if it turns. And—" he hesitated, and Jonah saw him weigh the words before speaking them, as he weighed everything, a man who had learned to treat speech as currency he could not earn back, "—and there may be water. Old chapels were sited on springs. Veyra was a goddess of deep places. Wells, roots, the dark beneath the soil where seeds wait." He smiled faintly, without humor. "Ironic, given what I'm told you do."

"Gardening isn't storm-calling," Jonah said.

"No," Bram agreed. "But they come from the same country, I think. Water finding its level."

She didn't answer him. She hadn't answered much of anything since they'd left the ridge behind, since the moment she'd crossed the invisible line where Hollowmere's silence ended and the world's noise began. Ten years in the valley had taught her body to expect nothing — no hum of ley-threads under her feet, no pressure in her ears before weather turned strange, none of the low constant murmur that magic made everywhere else in the world. The silence of Hollowmere had been a mercy. It had been a bandage wrapped around a wound, and for ten years she had almost managed to forget there was anything underneath.

Now the bandage was off, and everything underneath was awake.

She could feel it the way a deaf woman feels a drum — not the sound itself but the vibration of it, in her teeth, in the soles of her boots, in the scar tissue under her skin where the storm lived coiled. The whole countryside murmured. Hedgerows whispered with hedge-spirits gone half-feral from neglect. Streams carried little braided songs of themselves downhill. And overhead, in the grey spring sky, clouds gathered and shifted with a slow deliberation that felt less like weather and more like attention.

*They know you're here,* said a voice in the back of her mind that might have been her own and might not. *They've waited ten years to know.*

Jonah kept her hands busy. That was the rule. Busy hands didn't spark. She helped Sef clear a corner of the nave of fallen masonry, stacking stones with exaggerated care, feeling each one for the faint tremor of old blessing that still clung to some of them like perfume clinging to a coat.

It was while she was working that she noticed the altar.

* * *

It sat at the head of the ruined nave where an altar should sit, though it was hardly an altar anymore — a slab of riverstone cracked clean through, one half toppled into the weeds, the other still upright, worn smooth by centuries of hands and weather. Someone had carved into it long ago, and the carving had nearly eroded away: a spiral, descending inward. A symbol for going down and coming back up. Roots. Winter into spring.

Bram stood before it for a long time without touching it.

"This is hers," he said finally. His voice had gone strange. "This is a Veyran altar. I thought they were all defaced. All of them, after the Concord — the reformers were thorough." He knelt, slowly, the way a man kneels at a grave, and pressed his palm flat against the stone. "Someone hid it. Chipped the edges, buried the spiral under grime, let the ivy grow over. They kept it alive the way you keep a coal alive under ash."

"You talk about your god like you loved her," Sef said, from her perch on a fallen pillar, gnawing an apple she had absolutely stolen from their shared stores.

"I did love her." Bram didn't look up. "That's what made the sin possible. You don't abandon a thing you hate. You abandon things you're afraid to keep loving." He drew a breath. "We were told she was dying anyway. Starved of prayer, fading, a guttering candle — and that binding what remained would be mercy. Containment. My order spent three generations perfecting the rites and then, when it was done, we spent one generation pretending we had never existed. Because we couldn't bear to be the people who had done it."

"And now?" Jonah asked. Her voice came out lower than she intended. "Now that she's waking up mad?"

"Now I am the last priest of a god who has every reason to hate me," Bram said simply, "and I am walking toward her with empty hands. That is the whole of my plan. I have examined it from every angle and it does not improve."

Sef snorted. Jonah almost smiled. Almost.

And then she touched the altar.

* * *

It wasn't deliberate. That was the thing she would return to later, lying awake under the broken tower while the others slept — it wasn't deliberate, wasn't ritual, wasn't anything. She was clearing ivy from the upright half of the slab because leaving it choked seemed wrong to her in a way that predated thought, the way a gardener pulls strangling weed from any stem, anywhere, asleep or awake. Her fingers hooked under the vines. She pulled. The ivy came away in sheets, and beneath it the spiral stood clean and pale in the evening light, and her bare palm came to rest flat on the stone.

The world went white.

No — not white. Not light. It was *sound*, sound beyond sound, a chord struck somewhere beneath the floor of the world, and Jonah was standing on the sounding board. Every ley-thread for fifty miles sang at once. Every neglected well and sleeping seed and half-forgotten name woke and turned toward her like faces turning toward a window when a lamp is lit. And through the stone under her hand, up her arm, into the cage of her ribs, poured a recognition so vast and so glad that it broke something open in her chest —

*You. We know you. Little weather, storm-born, key-cut, ours —*

"No," Jonah said aloud.

The power came up through her like floodwater through a cellar stair.

She had spent ten years believing the valley had taken it from her. That was the story she told herself, the story the council had told her, the comfortable lie that made exile bearable: the storm is gone, leached away by dead earth, and what remains is just a woman with a garden. But it wasn't gone. It had never been gone. Hollowmere hadn't erased it — it had *muzzled* it, pressed it down year after year into a shape small enough to live with, the way a river dammed will still carve its bed in the dark. And now the dam was open, and ten years of held-back water came through her all at once.

Lightning didn't strike the chapel. Lightning rose *out of* it.

She heard Sef scream her name. She heard Bram shouting words in a language that tasted of wells and winter — not commanding her, she understood dimly, not even praying, just *naming*, holding her name against the torrent the way you hold a rope against a current. The air filled with the smell of ozone and crushed green things. Stones rose off the nave floor around her, hovering, trembling, shedding five hundred years of dust. Cracks spidered up the walls. The snapped bell tower groaned above her like a ship in a gale, and the whole ruin rang with the chord, with the vast delighted attention pouring down out of the sky, and somewhere very far away and very close, something enormous turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to wake—

*Little weather,* said a voice like a thousand throats, gentle and starving and ancient, *we felt you cross the ridge. We have missed you so long. Come, let us see what grew in the silence—*

"*NO.*"

Jonah ripped her hand off the altar.

The world slammed shut like a door. Stones dropped. The hovering dust fell as sudden dirty rain. The chord cut off mid-note, and in the ringing silence that followed, the only sounds were Sef's ragged breathing, the creak of settling masonry, and a single piece of the tower stub cracking loose and crashing into the nave, close enough that the impact shivered through the floor into Jonah's knees.

She was on her hands and knees among the weeds. She didn't remember falling.

Her palms were burned — not marked, exactly, but flushed pink, the way skin looks after frostbite begins to thaw. Under her skin, the storm lay coiled again, quivering, *satisfied*, and the satisfaction was the worst part. Ten years she had believed herself emptied out. Ten years of safe, small, chosen smallness — and all along the thing that killed her family had been sitting inside her like a banked fire, waiting, patient, needing only a door.

"Jonah." Bram crouched at the edge of her vision, careful not to touch her. His face was grey. "Look at me. Breathe. In through the nose."

"I nearly—" Her voice came out shredded. "The whole chapel. I felt it start to go. I felt the walls start to *go*—"

"They held." He glanced up at the cracked masonry, and something moved across his face that looked, absurdly, like awe fighting with horror and losing to neither. "Old consecrated stone. It remembered how to stand. Veyra built her houses to survive storms, you understand. Even bound, even starving, some part of her remembers what her houses are *for*."

"That's not comforting," Sef said. She was pressed against the far wall with the apple forgotten in her fist, and her eyes — sharp, too-old eyes — were fixed on Jonah with an expression Jonah recognized and hated: fear wearing the mask of wariness. "What was that? What happened? What was *she* — the voice, the big voice —"

"The old gods," Bram said quietly. "Waking. All of them, or enough of them. The prayers are reaching, and the prayers are opening doors, and doors that have been shut for two centuries open hard." He turned back to Jonah. "And your storm-calling — they recognize it. Do you understand what I'm telling you? The storm-priests weren't a mortal order. They were *keyed*. Made, trained, shaped — whatever word you prefer — to answer the gods when they called. You aren't just a woman with a dangerous gift, Jonah Ravensworth. You are a door that certain beings have been waiting a very long time to walk through."

"Then they can bloody well knock somewhere else," Sef said fiercely, and crossed the nave to plant herself between Jonah and the altar, small and bristling, glaring up at the sky as if it had personally offended her. "She didn't ask. Nobody asked her. Whatever's out there wanting to use her can get in line behind everyone else who's ever wanted to use somebody."

Jonah stared at her.

Ten years ago, no one had stood between her and anything. The council had judged her from behind a table. Her neighbors had watched her family's funeral from behind shuttered windows. And this pickpocket child, this runaway in stolen boots who had known her four days, was standing in front of her shaking like a leaf and daring the heavens to try something.

"Get up," Sef said roughly, holding out a hand. "You're sitting in nettles. I can see them from here."

Jonah took the hand. It was absurd, being hauled upright by a twelve-year-old half her weight, and something in her chest cracked in an entirely different way than it had a minute ago.

* * *

They did not sleep in the chapel after that.

Bram wanted to press on — the road to the capital was long, and whatever window the courier's plea had spoken of was narrowing — but Jonah put her foot down with a firmness that surprised all three of them, herself included. "I need an hour," she said. "An hour where nothing is sacred. Then we walk."

So they made camp in a hollow beyond the chapel's field, under an ordinary oak, beside an ordinary stream, and Jonah sat with her hands flat on ordinary dirt and willed herself to feel nothing but mud and cold and the ache in her knees.

It didn't work entirely. The world still murmured. But the murmur had changed register — she realized, listening, that the hedgerow-spirits and stream-songs had drawn back from the chapel site, leaving a circle of quiet around the ruin like a ring of cleared ground around a lightning strike.

"She's afraid of me," Jonah said.

"She's afraid *for* you," Bram corrected gently, from across the small fire. "Or curious. Or hungry. With gods it's difficult to separate the three." He fed a stick to the flames. "I should tell you what I felt, when it happened. Since you'll ask eventually and better now than on the road."

Jonah looked at him.

"When your power rose, every warding rite my order ever taught me lit up at once. Every prayer I know tried to leave my mouth simultaneously. And underneath all of it—" he paused, choosing honesty over comfort, which she was learning was his way, "—underneath all of it, I heard the binding. Far off. South and east. Where the capital is. It *moved*. Like something rolling over in bed. Something that felt a key turn in a lock three hundred miles away, and opened one eye."

Sef had gone still, apple core forgotten.

"So it knows," Jonah said. Her voice was steady, which surprised her. Perhaps there was nothing left in her steady enough to shake. "The crown-thing. The one wearing the king. It knows I'm coming."

"It knows a storm-caller crossed the ridge," Bram said. "It doesn't yet know why. There may be days before it works out more. There may be hours." He met her eyes across the fire. "I won't pretend the choice is unchanged, Jonah. If you want to turn back to the valley, I'll walk you to it myself and go on alone. I mean that. But I think you should know what I felt when the power left you and the sky went quiet."

"What?"

He looked into the fire for a moment.

"Relief," he said. "Enormous relief. Because for one moment, when you touched that altar, I thought I was looking at a god. And then the moment passed, and you asked if the walls were going to fall, and you were only a terrified woman kneeling in the weeds." He smiled, tired and real. "Gods don't ask that. People do. As long as you're asking that question, I think we're safe from the worst thing in the world — which was never the storm, Jonah. It was someone ceasing to care where the lightning falls."

Jonah said nothing. She lay back against the oak roots and watched the clouds cross the stars, moving south and east, moving with that same slow deliberation, and far off — so far she might have imagined it, so far it might have been only the memory of a chord still ringing in her bones — she heard thunder.

Not her thunder. Not tonight's thunder.

Thunder from the direction of the capital, rolling patiently across a hundred miles of sleeping country, like something very large clearing its throat.

Sef heard it too. Jonah knew by the way the girl's hand crept across the dark ground and closed, small and fierce, around hers.

Nobody said anything at all.
