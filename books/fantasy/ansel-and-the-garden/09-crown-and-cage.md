## 9. Crown and Cage

The king of Vareth woke on the floor of his own bedchamber with his cheek pressed to cold marble and no memory of how he had come to be there.

This was the first mercy of the morning: that he did not remember. The second was the taste of iron in his mouth, which meant only that he had bitten his tongue, and not that he had—*other things*. He lay still and took inventory the way a soldier takes inventory after a battle lost. Fingers: present, aching. Knees: bruised, one split. Voice: he did not test it yet. There were mornings when testing it was the worst thing he could do.

*Aldric.*

It came from everywhere and nowhere, from the crown resting on its velvet stand across the room, from the walls, from beneath the floor where the old stones held their long memories. It was not a voice so much as a weather front, a pressure change, the sense of something vast turning over in its sleep and finding him warm.

*I know you're awake,* it said, almost fondly. *You always wake like a hare. Ears first.*

He said nothing. Ten years of saying nothing had taught him that silence was the one country it could not fully colonize.

*You bit your tongue again,* it observed. *You do that when I take you to the council. You hate what I say to them.*

He hated more than what it said. He hated that when it spoke through his mouth, his ministers leaned forward. He hated that Lord Cassery's eyes went bright and wet with devotion. He hated that the edicts flowed smoother from its lips than they ever had from his own—that the realm, in some secret chamber of itself, *preferred* the thing wearing him.

Aldric pushed himself up off the marble, joints cracking like green wood in a fire, and looked at his hands. They were steady. That was the other thing about the mornings: whatever the crown-spirit did with his body in the dark hours—and he had stopped, years ago, asking—it left him back at dawn, whole, unmarked, returned like a borrowed coat. As though even it understood that a king must be kept presentable.

*There's my boy,* it murmured, as he crossed to the washbasin. The water stared back at him, gray-eyed, gray-bearded, older than forty-two ought to look. *Wash your face. We have petitions today.*

"I have petitions," he said aloud, and his voice came out rusted but his own. "You have nothing. You are a guest."

The laughter moved through the room like wind through wheat. It was not cruel, exactly. That was the horror of it. Cruelty would have been easier to hate.

*Oh, Aldric.* A pause, and then, gentler than any whisper: *We both know what I am. Say it with me sometime. In front of the mirrors.*

He did not answer. He washed his face instead, and dressed without calling his valet—the man had wept once, early on, seeing the king's hands shake, and Aldric could not bear to be seen shaking by anyone who remembered him before—and went down to the day.

* * *

The trick, he had learned, was paper.

The crown-spirit was ancient and vast and hungry in ways that predated hunger, but it was careless with small things. It did not read laundry lists. It did not read the marginalia of tax ledgers. It skimmed the world the way a hawk skims a field, seeing only movement, only the large shapes of things: councils, armies, worship. So Aldric had become small. He had taught himself, over ten slow years, to fold his mind down into the size of a ledger entry, a button, a grain of rice written over a thousand times until the ink wore a groove into the page.

In the third drawer of his writing desk, beneath a false bottom his grandfather had used for love letters, there was a growing archive of himself.

*Day 3,412. Woke in the east gallery. My boots were muddy. There is no rain in the capital. Do not let me sign anything on Thursdays.*

*Day 3,590. Cassery proposed the shrine tithe again. I said yes. I think I said yes. Check the seal impressions against my hand — the R curls wrong when it isn't me.*

*Day 4,001. I dreamed of Mother's garden. When I woke the roses in the conservatory were dead. All of them. Every stem. Tell whoever finds this: I tried.*

He wrote in his own hand, deliberately awkward, letters squared off the way he'd been taught at nine years old before tutors smoothed him into elegance. If the thing ever thought to look, it would find only an old man's failing penmanship. It never thought to look. Contempt, he had learned, was a kind of armor too.

Today, after the petitions—after two hours of watching his own mouth promise the god-starved crowds new shrines, new feast days, new tithes while somewhere behind his eyes he screamed into the dark like a man sealed in a tomb with a lamp going down—he came back to the desk and added a line.

*Day 4,417. The courier reached Hollowmere. Or did not. No word either way is a kind of word. If she comes, tell her I am sorry for the valley. Tell her I chose her because she is the only storm in this kingdom that was never asked to be gentle.*

His hand hovered over the page.

Then, because dignity was a currency he was spending faster than he earned it now, he wrote the truest thing:

*I am afraid today. Not of her. Of hoping.*

He blotted it, slid it into the drawer, and locked it with the little key he kept sewn into the lining of his left cuff, and sat for a moment in the gray afternoon light with his face in his hands.

*You're doing the sad thing again,* the crown-spirit said, from the corner where no one stood. *I can feel it. Like a stone in my shoe.*

"You don't have feet."

*I have yours.*

* * *

He remembered the courier. He made himself remember, every day, because memory was the last territory he held, and he defended it the way a besieged garrison defends its wells.

His name had been Tam Vesset. A quiet man, a good rider, one of the few in the palace service whose devotion had not yet curdled into worship of the thing on the throne. Aldric had chosen him on a Tuesday, in the narrow window between possessions—a window that had once been hours and was now sometimes only minutes—when he could still walk to the map room alone and stand before it and think.

Hollowmere. The magic-dead valley. The place the council sent its embarrassments and its monsters, where no spell could cross the ridge and nothing answered prayer, which was precisely why the crown-spirit never looked there. A blind spot in the god's vast attention. A hole in the world shaped exactly like a door.

And in that valley, tending herbs and pretending the world beyond the ridge did not exist, lived Jonah Ravensworth.

He had been on the council, younger then, the night they sentenced her. Fourteen years old, soaked to the bone, hair full of river silt, standing in the judgment hall while grown men argued whether a child who called a storm that killed six people should hang or merely be buried alive in exile. Aldric had argued for exile. Not out of mercy—or not only. Out of arithmetic. Because he had already begun to suspect what slept in his own crown, and because somewhere in the archives he had found the old records: *storm-caller, bloodline of the Weathered Kings, power that answers the old gods' waking.* A key, sleeping in the one place in the kingdom no lock could be picked.

He had told himself for years that it was strategy. Clean, cold, kingly strategy. Send the courier, give the girl the truth, let her choose.

But sitting now in the dark of his study, listening to the crown-spirit hum through the stones like a hive of bees made of winter, he admitted what the ledger of himself had been trying to tell him for a decade: he had chosen Jonah Ravensworth because she was the only person alive who might understand. Who had killed with power she didn't ask for. Who knew what it was to carry something inside you that the world had every right to fear. If anyone could look at the starving thing in his crown and see past the monster to the wound beneath—

If anyone could forgive him for what he was about to ask—

*You're thinking about her again,* the crown-spirit said. It had a way of arriving without transition, like cold water closing over the head. *The little weather-witch in the dead valley.*

"She is nothing."

*Mm.* It let the silence stretch, savoring. *Then why does your heart do that?*

He pressed his palms flat against the desk. The wood was real. The grain was real. He counted the rings of it, one, two, three, anchoring himself the way Bram Ostell's heretical little pamphlets had once taught him to anchor—though he'd burned those pamphlets afterward, or pretended to.

*She won't come,* the spirit said, and there was something new in it—not mockery. Something thinner. Wanting, maybe, or its shadow. *No one crosses the ridge for a dying king. They didn't cross it for me, either, all those centuries. I waited in the dark under the mountain and I listened and no one, no one, no one—*

It stopped. When it resumed, the voice was smooth as court silk again.

*Petitions tomorrow. Wear the blue. The crowds like the blue.*

* * *

That night he hid another note, and this one he did not put in the drawer.

He had learned that the spirit watched the desk. It watched everything he loved, eventually—had killed his falcon in its first year, had soured every friendship by making him say unforgivable things in lucid-seeming moments, had turned even his griefs into instruments. But it did not watch the garden. Nothing grew where it walked; plants wilted near it the way candles gutter near an open door. The royal conservatory had been abandoned for years, a glass house of brown stems and dead soil, and Aldric went there sometimes in the small hours because it was the one room in the palace where his presence changed nothing.

He knelt by the dried fountain and worked the loose flagstone free with a knife. Beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a bundle of letters—one for each year of his captivity, addressed to no one, delivered to no one, because there was no one left to deliver them to. His father. His mother. The brother who had died believing Aldric strong.

He added the newest letter, and then, on impulse, a second sheet—blank except for three words at the top, written large, in the hand of a nine-year-old boy learning to make his letters square and honest:

*IF FOUND: HELP.*

He replaced the stone. He sat back on his heels among the dead roses and laughed, quietly, horribly, until the laugh became something else, and he let it, because there was no one here to see the king of Vareth weep into his hands among the corpses of flowers.

Behind his eyes, patient as geology, the crown-spirit waited for morning.

*Tomorrow,* it whispered, drowsy, sated on the day's worship, drifting toward the deep places where it kept its hoard of him. *Blue robes. And Aldric—*

*What?*

*Nothing. Sleep. Dream of the valley if you like. I'll be watching.*

He slept. He dreamed of a garden under a ridge of gray stone, and a woman with storm-gray eyes who did not yet know she had been summoned, and in the dream he tried to shout a warning across all that distance—*they will use you, they will spend you, run, child, run*—but dreams carry no words, only weather.

And far away, in a valley where magic could not follow, a sealed letter hummed against a gardener's skin, and the first thunder of the answering storm rolled silent beyond the hills.

In the conservatory, beneath the flagstone, the ink on three small words began, very slowly, to frost.
