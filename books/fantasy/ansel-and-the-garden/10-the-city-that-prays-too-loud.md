## 10. The City That Prays Too Loud

Sef smelled the capital before she saw it.

That was the thing nobody warned you about in the stories—the ones where the city gleamed like a coin at the bottom of a fountain and every beggar was secretly a wizard. The stories never mentioned that Corvale reeked of tallow and horse dung and ten thousand people pressed too close together, and now, on top of all of it, of smoke. Not hearth-smoke. Incense. It rolled out of the gates in a greasy yellow cloud, thick enough to taste, and Sef pulled her cap lower over her ears and thought, *I used to live here. I used to think this was the whole world.*

"Stay close," Bram murmured beside her. He had his hood up and his priest's hands hidden in his sleeves, which fooled no one, least of all Sef. "And whatever you see—"

"I know. Don't gawk." She'd been saying it to him for two days.

Jonah said nothing. Jonah hadn't said much since the ridge road, since the birds went quiet all at once like someone had snuffed them out with wet fingers. She walked with her shoulders hunched and her eyes down, a woman trying to take up less space than a person could, and Sef kept glancing at her sideways because it made her chest hurt in a way she didn't have words for yet. *She's scared,* Sef thought. *The big scary storm-witch is scared of a smell.*

They joined the queue at the Ashgate behind a river of pilgrims—farmers with their hats in their hands, grandmothers clutching candles wrapped in wax paper, a whole family from the wine country walking barefoot on the cobbles, their feet black as tar. Nobody jostled. That was the strange part. In the old days the gate queue shoved and swore and picked your purse while apologizing for it. Now they moved like people wading into cold water, slow and solemn and half-drowned already.

The gate guards didn't even look at papers. One of them held up a hand and called out, hoarse as a crow: "Shrine tax's doubled by order of the Crown. Pay at the first altar or be turned back."

*By order of the Crown.* Sef bit the inside of her cheek. She knew the king's edicts better than most twelve-year-olds, having spent four years watching them get written. The man who signed them now wrote with a heavy leftward slant and pressed so hard the nib tore through vellum. The real Aldric wrote small and quick, letters leaning right, like he was always in a hurry to get somewhere kinder.

She paid the tax with coins lifted off a sleeping carter two miles back, and the guard waved them through without meeting anyone's eyes.

Inside the walls, the city had gone mad in the polite way. That was what unsettled her most—not riots, not screaming, but the *orderliness* of it. Every street corner had sprouted an altar. Some were proper marble things hauled out of temples; others were just crates draped in white cloth, stacked with offerings until the cloth sagged. Bread. Flowers. Dolls. A child's shoe, placed carefully, alone, like a question. People knelt in rows on the cobbles, shoulder to shoulder, praying aloud all at once, hundreds of voices braided into one low drone that followed them down the Street of Lanterns like a tide following a boat.

"It's louder than when I left," Sef said quietly. "Three weeks ago there were shrines, sure, but people still worked. Look at them. Nobody's working."

"They're feeding something," Jonah said. Her voice came out flat, but Sef saw her hands, curled tight at her sides, knuckles pale. "Can't you feel it? All this—" she gestured vaguely at the air, at the drone, "—it's going somewhere."

Bram had stopped walking. He stood very still under his hood, and when he spoke, his voice had the careful flatness of a man reading his own death warrant. "It's going *up*. To the palace. To the spire." He turned his head slowly, taking in the street, the kneeling crowds, the altars drowning in bread. "This isn't devotion. This is a funnel. They're pouring themselves into her and calling it faith."

"Her?" Sef said.

"The crown-spirit." Bram's jaw worked. "Veyra." He said the name like setting down something heavy he'd carried too far.

A bell began tolling somewhere ahead—not the hour, Sef realized, but some new rhythm, three quick strikes and a long one, over and over. The kneeling crowds swayed toward it like wheat toward wind. And then, from the direction of the Great Temple square, came a sound Sef would remember for the rest of her life: a cheer. Thousands of voices cheering, wordless, hungry, the sound a crowd makes when it wants something so badly the wanting has stopped being human.

"Procession," a woman near them whispered, radiant, tears cutting clean lines through the ash on her face. "The King walks among us today. The King *blesses* us." She clutched Sef's sleeve with sudden terrible tenderness. "You should see him, little brother. You should see how he shines."

Sef smiled the smile she'd learned in the royal nursery, the one that got adults to leave you alone, and peeled the woman's fingers off her arm as gently as she could.

*He shines.* She thought of the last time she'd seen Aldric—really seen him, not the puppet at ceremonies but the man himself, six months ago, catching her stealing honey cakes in the pantry corridor. He hadn't shouted. He'd crouched down to her height, and he'd said, *If you're going to thieve, girl, at least do it with better posture,* and then he'd eaten one of the cakes himself and told her which steward counted them. That man's light wasn't the kind crowds cheered at. It was smaller and better than that.

They heard the procession before they saw it, and they saw it before they meant to—a gap opened in the crowd like a wound, and through it came the golden litter, and Sef forgot every rule about keeping her head down.

King Aldric sat upright in the litter, robed in white and gold, and he *did* shine—that was the horror of it. Light moved under his skin like oil under water. His eyes were open and fixed on nothing, and his mouth smiled, and the smile was wrong in a way Sef couldn't name except that it used none of the muscles Aldric used. As the litter passed, the crowd surged forward to touch the litter's edge, to touch each other for having touched it, and the drone of prayer rose into a roar, and Sef felt it happen—felt the air itself *tilt*, all that desperate love streaming upward off ten thousand bowed heads and funnelling, narrowing, pouring into the shining man like water into a drain.

Beside her, Jonah made a small sound. Sef grabbed her hand without thinking. It was freezing.

"He can hear it," Jonah breathed. "Underneath. Look at his hands."

And Sef looked, and wished she hadn't. The king's hands, resting on the litter's arm, were clenched so hard the knuckles had gone bloodless—and one finger was moving. Twitching. Tracing something against the gold, over and over, small quick strokes. Writing. A man locked inside his own shining body, writing the same word into the air again and again where only someone looking would ever see it.

Sef couldn't read it from here. But she knew the shape of urgency when she saw it, and her eyes burned, and she hated herself for it because crying was a luxury and she'd sworn off luxuries the night she climbed out of the laundry window.

Then the moment passed. The litter turned toward the temple square, the crowd closed like water over a stone, and the roar faded into the drone again.

"We need to move," Bram said tightly. "Now. Before that thing finishes feasting and remembers it has enemies."

* * *

Getting into the palace was, embarrassingly, the easy part.

Sef had planned three routes and abandoned two of them by noon, because the city's madness had its uses: the servants' postern below the east terrace stood unguarded, its door swinging loose, because every guard who should have been watching it had been pulled to the procession or the shrines. Who garrisoned a door when the god-king walked abroad? Who imagined anyone would want to sneak *into* the belly of the thing?

"You're certain?" Jonah asked, eyeing the dark slot of the postern.

"No," Sef said honestly. "But I carried linens through it for two years and I know where every stair goes, and that's worth more than certainty." She glanced back at both of them—tall grim Bram, grey-faced Jonah—and felt the old ferocity rise in her, the one that had gotten her out of the capital alive once already. "Rule one: I talk, you stay quiet. Rule two: if anyone stops us, I'm running an errand for the under-steward of the west wing and you're my hired porters. Rule three—"

"There's no rule three," Bram said.

"There's always a rule three. Don't touch anything blessed. Half the door handles in this place will burn you now."

The postern passage swallowed them into cool dark, and Sef felt something unknot in her chest that she refused to examine. She knew these corridors the way other children knew lullabies. Here the wall leaned where the foundations had settled; here the flagstone that clicked; here the turn where you could stand in the niche and let a whole patrol pass. Servants' country. The invisible veins of the palace, built for people whose names the palace never learned, and Sef had been one of them in everything but title. Ward, they called her. *Ward of the Crown.* Fancy word for a foundling the king liked and the court tolerated.

She led them down, and down, past the linen stores gone dusty, past the great kitchen with its fires banked and its ovens cold—*everyone's at the shrines, even the cooks, even the cooks*—until the air changed and grew damp and mineral, and she stopped at an unremarkable stretch of wall and pressed the third stone from the floor.

The panel swung inward on a breath of colder air.

"The undercroft," she said. "Old cellars, older tunnels. The stewards call it the Bones. Nobody comes down here. Especially not now."

"Why especially not now?" Jonah asked.

Sef hesitated. "Because of the singing," she said, and immediately hated how it sounded. "I'm not—it's probably nothing. Rats. Pipes. But the maids used to say the palace hums at night, deep down, and the last month the humming's had *words*."

Bram went very still. "Words in what language?"

"That's the thing." Sef hugged her elbows. "Nobody recognized it. Old Marta said it sounded like being asked something."

They lit no lamps. Bram's fingers glimmered faintly instead—a priest's trick, the last scrap of Veyra's dying craft—and by that dim blue-grey glow Sef watched Jonah change. The gardener who flinched from thunderclouds straightened slowly, spine by spine, her head tilting like a deer testing wind. The frost-fear in her face gave way to something else. Recognition.

"It's stronger down here," Jonah whispered. "Whatever's drinking all that prayer upstairs—it pools. Like water finding the low place." She turned to Sef, and her eyes in the ghost-light were wide and bright and terrified and *awake*. "Sef. Your Bones aren't empty. Something's been listening down here for a long time."

"Listening to what?"

"To whether anyone would come."

The tunnel stretched ahead of them into the dark, and from somewhere deep beneath the palace—deeper than cellars, deeper than foundations—came the sound the maids had tried to describe and failed. Not singing, exactly. Not words. A vibration in the stone, patient and immense, like a heart beating under the world, and threaded through it, so faint Sef might have invented it:

*a voice, asking.*

Bram's guttering light steadied. "The old god's binding was anchored below the throne," he said softly. "I'd forgotten. Or maybe I was made to forget." He looked at the darkness, and then at Jonah, and last at Sef—at her, the runaway stable boy in stolen boots who had just led them straight to the root of everything. "Child. How far does this tunnel go?"

Sef swallowed, and reached for her courage the way she reached for pockets: quickly, and without asking permission.

"All the way," she said. "Under the throne room. There's a door down there that even the stewards don't know about. My friend Pip and I found it when we were nine." She paused. "There's writing on it. We couldn't read it then."

"And now?" Jonah asked.

"Now," Sef said, "I think we'd better."
