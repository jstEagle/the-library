## 11. What Wears the King

The palace had a smell Jonah hadn't expected: beeswax and lilies and, underneath it all, something faintly scorched, like a hearth swept too soon after a fire. She noticed it the moment they passed beneath the gatehouse, and she thought of her garden at Hollowmere, of the particular sweetness of turned earth after rain, and she missed it so sharply her chest ached.

*You shouldn't be here,* said the voice in her head that sounded like her mother. *You should be behind your wall, with your lettuces and your silence.*

But the courier's plea was folded against her heart, and Sef was walking beside her with her chin up and her stolen livery brushed clean, and Bram was murmuring the old words under his breath like a man rehearsing an apology he'd owed for thirty years. So Jonah walked into the lion's mouth with dirt still under her fingernails, because that was what she did now. She walked toward things.

The throne room doors were oak banded with iron, tall as three men, and they opened without anyone touching them.

That was the first wrong thing. The second was the cold.

It poured out through the gap like water finding a crack—not the honest cold of winter, which Jonah knew down to her bones, but a cold with intention to it, a cold that *noticed*. Frost bloomed across the flagstones in patterns that were almost, but not quite, flowers. Almost, but not quite, the frost-ferns that used to crawl across her windowpanes on the mornings after bad dreams, back when she was fourteen and full of sky.

Bram stopped walking. "Veyra," he whispered, and his voice cracked down the middle.

"Wait." Jonah caught his sleeve. "Is that—"

"Her hunger," Bram said. "This is what it feels like when she reaches for you."

Sef, twelve years old and fearless in the way only frightened people can be, stepped forward first. "Then we'd better not keep her waiting."

* * *

The throne room was vast and mostly empty. Courtiers lined the walls at a careful distance, pale as candles, their eyes fixed on nothing. Servants knelt along the aisle with their foreheads to the stone, and they were trembling—all of them, every one, a fine continuous shiver like leaves before a storm—and Jonah understood with a lurch that they were not kneeling out of reverence. They were kneeling the way animals kneel. The way her father's cattle had gone quiet and low-headed the day before the lightning came.

And on the throne sat King Aldric.

She had expected a monster. That was the terrible part—she had braced herself for horns, for blackened eyes, for some visible ruin, and instead there was only a man. He was perhaps fifty, broad through the shoulders in the way of soldiers gone soft, wearing a crown of white gold set with a single stone the color of deep water. His hands rested on the arms of the throne with terrible gentleness, fingers slightly curled, as if he were holding something invisible and precious and could not put it down.

His eyes were open. They were kind eyes. That was the worst thing in the whole room.

"Jonah Ravensworth," the king said.

His voice was his own—that was the second-worst thing. It was a tired, reasonable voice, the voice of a man who had spent decades listening to petitions, and it said her name the way you'd greet a guest you'd been expecting.

"You know me," Jonah said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt.

"I have known your name since before you were born." Aldric rose from the throne, and the whole room flinched—the courtiers, the servants, even the torchflames bent away from him—and descended the three steps with the unhurried grace of someone who has never once needed to hurry. "Your mother grew moonflowers. Did you know that? Along the south wall of your family's house, where the soil was poor and everything else sulked. She talked to them. People thought it was charming."

The floor tilted under Jonah. "My mother died when I was four."

"Yes." The king stopped six feet from her, close enough that she could see the fine lines around his eyes, the grey coming in at his temples, the small scar on his jaw from some long-forgotten boyhood fall. A man. Just a man. "I remember the night she planted them. I was passing through your valley—you wouldn't remember; the valley forgets everyone who leaves it, and everyone who enters. She gave me a cutting wrapped in wet cloth because I remarked that the flowers were beautiful. I kept it alive for two years in a pot on my windowsill. Then it died, and I grieved it absurdly, and I never told anyone why."

"That isn't possible," Jonah said. "You're describing things you couldn't—"

"I am describing things *she* couldn't know," the king said gently, "and yet she does. Ask yourself how, little weather."

Little weather.

The name went through her like a hook through soft flesh. No one had ever called her that. No one living had ever heard the words her grandmother used, sitting on the porch steps with a sleeping child against her shoulder, watching heat lightning walk the far hills: *there goes our little weather, dreaming of thunder.* Words spoken privately, tenderly, twenty years ago, in a house that no longer stood, by a woman whose grave Jonah had not been permitted to visit.

"How do you know that name?" Jonah whispered.

The king smiled. And the smile was wrong—it was warm and sad and patient, and it did not belong on a human face, the way a flower does not belong growing out of stone. Behind him, every kneeling servant lifted their head at once, all of them together, and looked at her with the same smile.

"I knew your grandmother before your mother was a thought," the king said. "I knew your great-grandmother, who called a fog off the marshes to hide her village from raiders, and your great-great-grandfather, who argued with clouds the way other men argue with mules. I have watched your line for nine generations, Jonah Ravensworth. You are the last leaf on a very old tree, and I have been waiting for you to grow."

Bram made a sound beside her—a small, strangled sound, the sound of a man watching his worst suspicion stand up and introduce itself.

"You're not the king," Jonah said.

"No," agreed the thing wearing the king. "I'm older than kings. I'm the one they prayed to before kings were invented, back when the rain was a face and the wind had a name, and your family prayed to me loudest of all. Storm-callers always do. You were mine, little weather. Every one of you. My favorites." The kind eyes glistened. "Do you know what it is to be loved for nine generations and then forgotten in ten? To feel the prayers thin out year by year until they stop entirely, and you are left alone in the dark with your hunger?"

"I know what it is to lose a family," Jonah said.

"Yes," the god said softly. "So do I."

* * *

It moved closer. Not the king's body—the king's body stayed exactly where it was—but something else came closer, a pressure, a presence, filling the space between them like cold water rising. The frost on the flagstones crept toward her feet. The torches guttered. Somewhere in the walls, in the stones themselves, Jonah felt a vast attention settle on her, ancient and starving and terribly, terribly gentle.

"I felt you the moment you crossed the ridge," the god said. "A little knot of storm, walking around in a girl's skin, pretending to be nothing. Do you know what that was like? After centuries of silence? Like a blind woman feeling sunlight on her face. I nearly wept. I would have wept, if I still had eyes of my own."

"I'm not giving it to you," Jonah said. "Whatever you want from me. Whatever power you think I'm carrying. The answer is no."

"The answer," the god said, "is whatever you decide it is. I don't take, little weather. I've never taken. Taking is what your human gods do—your burning bushes and your jealous heavens. I only ever *asked*, and your family answered, generation after generation, because they loved the storm as I love it. And then they stopped answering. And then everyone stopped answering. And I got so hungry." The king's hands trembled at his sides. "So hungry that when your priests came to me with their chains and their clever bindings, I let them. Did Bram tell you? I let them cage me, because a caged god near the prayers of a capital city is better fed than a free god in an empty sky. I wore this crown because it was the closest thing to a congregation I could find."

"And now you wear the king," Jonah said.

"And now I wear the king," the god agreed, without a flicker of shame. "He offered himself, you know. He was drowning—his council, his wars, his dead wife, his guilt. He lay awake at night wishing something would carry it for him. So I did. I carry everything for him now. He sleeps inside me like a child in a warm bed, and he is happier than he has been in twenty years. You may ask him, if you can wake him."

"I've come to wake him."

Something moved across the king's face—fast, there and gone, like a fish turning under ice. For half a heartbeat the kind eyes were different: narrower, terrified, human. The king's mouth shaped a word. *Run,* maybe. Or *help.* Then the smile settled back over the features like a mask sliding home.

"He cannot be woken," the god said. "But you can join him."

The pressure tightened around Jonah, gentle as a hand on the back of her neck.

"Here is my offer, little weather, and I will make it only once, because I respect you too much to haggle. Kneel. Take my hand as your ancestors took it. Be my vessel and my voice, my storm-caller crowned, and I will give you back everything the lightning took."

The world went very quiet.

"I cannot raise the dead," the god said, reading her silence the way it read everything. "No power can. But I remember them. Every prayer your mother ever whispered over the moonflowers—I hold them all, word for word, the way a river holds every stone that ever fell into it. Your grandmother's laugh. Your father's voice calling you in from the fields. The exact weight of your brother asleep against your shoulder. I can give them back to you, Jonah. Not as ghosts. As memory made flesh at the moment of its sweetest—your mother in her garden at evening, turning to smile at you, and this time, this time, you reach her before the storm does. This time you call the lightning *away*. Nine generations of storm-callers, and not one of them ever got to say goodbye. I am offering you the goodbye."

Jonah's knees hurt.

She realized, distantly, that she had sunk halfway down without deciding to—that her body was already kneeling, already folding, already answering a call older than her name. The frost had reached her boots. It crawled up the leather in delicate ferns, and it didn't feel cold at all. It felt like her grandmother's kitchen. It felt like being known.

Behind her, Bram was saying her name. Sef was saying it louder. Neither voice reached her. There was only the presence, vast and wounded and loving, and the vision unfurling inside it like a flower forced open too fast: the south wall, the poor soil, the moonflowers opening white in the dusk, and her mother kneeling among them with dirt on her hands, looking up—

"Mom," Jonah said.

*Yes,* said the god, tender as a blade going in. *Kneel, and stay.*

* * *

What saved her was not courage. She wanted to believe, later, that it was courage, or will, or the strength of her convictions. It wasn't. What saved her was a small detail, wrong in a way only a gardener would catch.

In the vision, her mother's moonflowers were in bloom.

Moonflowers bloomed at dusk, yes—but not in the vision's season, not with that light. The scene was lit like late afternoon, gold and slanting, and the flowers were wide open, and real moonflowers would have been shut tight as fists until the sun went down. Her mother had explained it to her a hundred times, kneeling in that garden, patient as rain: *they keep their own hours, Jo. Everything true does. You can't make a thing bloom by wanting it hard enough.*

The god remembered her family perfectly. But it had never gardened. It had never learned that love means knowing when a thing opens and when it stays closed, and letting it.

The vision curdled. The gold light went the color of old teeth. Her mother's smile held a beat too long, the way a portrait holds, the way a mask holds, and Jonah saw—clearly, finally—that whatever stood in that garden wearing her mother's face had never once gotten its hands dirty.

"No," Jonah said.

She stood up. Her knees screamed. The frost shattered away from her boots in a ring, and the pressure around her neck became, for one instant, a fist.

"You're lying to me," Jonah said, and her voice came out low and shaking and absolutely certain. "Not about the memories. Those are real; I can taste them. You're lying about what they're worth. You're a starving thing offering bread made of pictures, and my family—" her voice broke, and she let it break, and kept talking through the break, "—my family taught me the difference between a garden and a graveyard, and you, *god or no*, are standing in a graveyard calling it a garden."

The throne room went silent enough to hear the frost growing.

When the king spoke again, the warmth was gone from his voice. What remained was older and colder and infinitely tired, and it was somehow more honest than anything the god had said all day.

"Nine generations," it said. "And the last of them refuses me. Do you know what I did to the last priest who refused me, little weather?"

"Bram told me," Jonah lied, because Bram had told her nothing, and she saw in the king's eyes that the lie landed, that somewhere in the dark behind those kind eyes a calculation shifted. "And I'm still standing here."

"You are," the god said. "For now. In a garden of my choosing, surrounded by my frost, with nowhere in this world where my weather doesn't reach." The king turned, slowly, and began to climb the steps back to his throne. "Go home to your valley, Jonah Ravensworth. Pray that I stay hungry and patient. Because I remember your family, and I remember their promises, and sooner or later—winter comes to every garden."

At the top of the steps, the king sat down, arranged his hands with that terrible gentleness, and closed his eyes. When they opened again, they were only a tired man's eyes, confused and afraid, and King Aldric looked out at the girl standing defiant in the middle of his throne room and mouthed, silently, unmistakably:

*The conservatory. Find what I buried there.*

Then the guards were moving, and Bram's hand was on her arm, and Sef was pulling them both toward a servant's door with the unerring instinct of someone who had grown up learning which doors a servant could pass through unseen—and the last thing Jonah saw, over her shoulder, as the doors swung shut, was the king on his throne, weeping without any change in his expression, tears running steadily down into his beard while the entire court pretended not to notice.

The conservatory, Jonah thought, as they ran. *Find what I buried there.*

She just hoped the king had left them time enough to dig.
