## 13. The Garden Beneath the Palace

The conservatory had been sealed for eleven years, and it smelled like it.

Sef pushed the door open with her shoulder, coughing through the dust that came down in a soft grey curtain. Behind her, Jonah carried the lamp low, and its light caught on a wilderness of glass and rot: iron ribs arched overhead like the skeleton of some enormous fish, panes cracked or missing entirely, and beneath them—choked in weeds, in dead vines, in the pale ghost-fungus that eats anything left alone too long—a garden.

Or what had been a garden once.

"Nobody comes here," Sef said. It wasn't a question. She'd checked. Three days of checking, actually, mapping the servant passages with the same obsessive care she'd used to memorize the kitchens at seven years old, because knowing where the walls were thin was the only kind of safety she'd ever trusted. "Not since the old queen died. The new council called it a waste of coal."

"They were right, I suppose." Jonah set the lamp on a workbench whose surface was buried under terracotta shards and seed packets gone soft as wet leaves. "It's a long way to carry water for plants nobody sees."

"That's not why they let it die." Sef climbed onto a bench to peer into the upper glass, where a whole pane had slipped its leading and hung by one corner, swaying when she breathed. "They let it die because the old queen loved it, and the crown-spirit doesn't like things people love. You've seen how it is up there. Everything has to be useful or it gets torn out. Everything has to be *afraid* or it gets ignored."

Jonah was quiet for a moment. In the lamplight her face did the thing it always did when Sef said something too true—the small flinch, quickly folded away, of a woman who had spent ten years learning to be small herself.

"You talk like someone who's watched a garden be killed on purpose," Jonah said.

"I grew up in this palace. I've watched everything be killed on purpose." Sef hopped down, landing in something soft and probably once alive. "Bram says we're hiding until he can get word to what's left of his order. Fine. But I'm not spending three days sitting in the dark counting my own heartbeats. If we're going to hide, we hide *here*."

* * *

It took them two days to make the conservatory breathe again.

Sef knew where the water was—that was easy; palaces are mostly pipes if you know which walls to knock on—and by the end of the first day she'd coaxed a trickle back into the lead spout at the conservatory's far corner, rusty brown at first, then clear. Jonah worked differently, slower, moving through the ruin like a woman reading a letter from someone she'd loved. She turned over pots. She pressed her thumb into soil and held it there as though listening. When she found a rosemary shrub near the south wall—half-dead, woody, but alive, stubbornly, absurdly alive—she made a sound Sef had never heard from her before, something between a laugh and a wound.

"Rosemary," Jonah said. "My mother grew it. She said you could kill everything else in a garden and rosemary would outlive your regrets."

"Cheerful woman."

"The cheeriest." Jonah broke off a sprig, crushed it between her fingers, and breathed it in with her eyes closed. For a moment she looked younger and older at once, the way people do when they're somewhere else entirely. Then she opened her eyes and handed the sprig to Sef. "Here. Keep it in your pocket. When this is over, plant it somewhere it'll get rain."

"What if it isn't over? What if it never—" Sef stopped. She was twelve; she knew better than to finish sentences like that. Finishing sentences like that gave adults permission to pat your head.

Jonah didn't pat her head. Jonah looked at her the way she sometimes did—straight on, no softness added, no softness withheld—and said, "Then plant it anyway. That's the whole trick of gardens, Sef. You plant for a future you can't prove."

* * *

On the third day, Jonah taught her grafting.

She'd found a knife in the potting shed, its blade rusted but sound, and a whetstone, and she sat Sef down on an overturned crate in the grey light coming through the patched glass and showed her how to make a cut that mattered.

"You're not hacking," Jonah said, guiding Sef's hand with two fingers at her wrist, light as a bird landing. "You're introducing. This scion"—a young shoot from the hardy wild plum that had forced its way up through a floor crack—"wants to live more than anything in this room. This rootstock"—the ancient, half-rotted plum against the south wall, still standing, still drinking—"knows how to survive winters neither of them will ever see. You're not making something new. You're letting two survivors help each other."

The cut had to be clean. The cambium layers—the green living lines just under the bark—had to touch, had to match, had to be bound tight with strips of cloth so nothing could come between them while they healed into one thing.

"It'll take?" Sef asked, staring at the little lashed-together impossibility of it.

"If they both want it to." Jonah tied off the last strip. "Grafting fails more often than it takes. But you do it anyway. Because the only alternative is watching both of them die separately."

Sef looked at her hands. There was dirt under her fingernails, real dirt, palace dirt, the first honest dirt she'd touched since she'd run. Her pockets held stolen bread, a stolen knife, a stolen name, and now a sprig of rosemary. She thought about the ward's wing, with its locked doors and its tutors who taught her cipher and etiquette and how to be valuable to a crown that ate kings. She thought about running, which she was very good at, which she had been doing since she was six years old in one way or another—running from rooms, from conversations, from the slow understanding that wards are possessions with better clothes.

"This is different," she said slowly. "This isn't running."

"No?"

"When I ran from the capital, everyone said I was brave. Bram said it. You said it." She turned the grafting knife over in her fingers. "But running isn't surviving. Running is just... surviving pointed the wrong way. Surviving is *this*. Staying somewhere rotten and making one green thing happen in it anyway." She looked up. "You already knew that. Ten years in your valley. You just forgot."

Something moved across Jonah's face then—not the flinch, not the fold-away. Something opening. She reached out and tucked a strand of hair behind Sef's ear, rough and brief, the gesture of a woman who had forgotten how.

"Cocky little thief," she said, and her voice wasn't quite steady.

"Somebody has to be."

* * *

It was Sef who found the records, on the fifth day, because Sef was the one who knew that in any palace the most important room is always disguised as a boring one.

The conservatory had a potting annex—a low brick room off the north wall, full of shelving and dead bulbs and, behind a false-backed shelf that Sef found by knocking (all walls lie; knock enough and they confess), a cache of the old queen's papers. Ledgers, mostly. Planting calendars. Letters from seed merchants in three kingdoms. And at the bottom of a cedar chest that exhaled camphor and time, a folio bound in plain grey leather with no title at all.

Sef almost didn't bring it out. But she was twelve and curious the way other children were hungry, and the folio had been hidden, and hidden things were addressed to her personally as far as she was concerned.

She brought it to Jonah, who was kneeling in the dirt repotting the rosemary's cuttings, her sleeves rolled, a smear of earth across one cheekbone like war paint worn backwards.

"Look what the wall gave me."

Jonah wiped her hands and took the folio. Opened it. Went still.

The pages inside weren't the queen's. The hand was different—sharp, slanted, pressing hard enough to emboss the paper, a hand written fast by someone who expected to be interrupted. Sef read upside down, the way she'd learned to read everything, and the words came to her in fragments first, then all at once:

*I have begun to lose hours. Yesterday I signed an edict doubling the shrine levies and I do not remember writing it. The ink is mine. The hand is mine. The will is not.*

*I have searched every archive for the rite of unbinding. The priests say there is none. The priests are lying or the priests are dead or the priests are hers now. I have written to Veyra's last temple and received no answer. Perhaps there is no one left who remembers how to listen.*

*If anyone finds this: I tried. Whatever I become, remember that I tried. The crown was meant to cage her hunger, and instead it has taught her ours.*

And at the bottom of the last page, smaller, pressed so deep the pen had nearly torn through:

*There is a storm-caller in Hollowmere. Ravensworth's girl. The valley holds her, but valleys do not hold forever. If I ever find one lucid hour, I will send for her. God forgive me for what I may ask of a child.*

The conservatory was very quiet. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the broken glass with a sound like slow breathing.

"He sent for me," Jonah said at last. Her voice came out strange, flattened, like paper after rain. "The courier. The man who died at my gate. Aldric wrote this years ago and he—he remembered, in whatever hours he could steal back from her, and he sent for *me*."

"He chose you," Sef said. "Out of everyone. He chose the person the whole kingdom is most afraid of."

"Why would he do that?"

Sef looked at the folio, at the embossed desperate handwriting, at the line about cages and hungers, and she thought about grafting—about how you don't make something new, you let two survivors help each other—and she said, before she could stop herself, the truest thing she'd ever said aloud:

"Because only something the binding recognizes can undo it. Bram said the crown-spirit is a god wearing a king. Maybe it takes a storm wearing a girl." She swallowed. "He wasn't asking you to be a weapon, Jonah. He was asking you to be the cut. The clean one. The one that lets two things heal into one—or lets one thing go free."

Jonah stared at her. Then down at the folio. Then at her own hands, which had gone white around the knife.

"We have to find Bram," she said. "Now. Tonight. If Aldric can steal lucid hours, if he's been leaving himself notes—there may be others. A whole trail of him, hidden through his own palace, waiting for—"

The bell began.

Not the chapel bell. Not any bell Sef knew. This one came up through the floor itself, through the palace bones, a deep bronze tolling that Sef felt in her teeth and her sternum and the soles of her feet—and she knew it, because every servant child learned it in their first week, the way they learned where the pipes ran and which walls lied.

The alarm of the undercroft. Someone had found the empty cell where Bram had been kept. Someone was hunting.

And beneath the tolling, faint but rising, carried on air that suddenly felt too warm for the season, came a second sound: voices. Hundreds of them. From the direction of the great hall, chanting in unison, in a rhythm Sef had heard only once before—on the night the king last changed, when the whole court knelt and prayed and the prayer had teeth.

Jonah blew out the lamp.

In the sudden dark, her hand found Sef's shoulder and gripped it, steady as a rootstock.

"Sef," she whispered. "How fast can you get us to the throne room?"
