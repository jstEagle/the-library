## 15. Storm Over the Spire

The spire prison rose out of the capital's eastern quarter like a finger raised in accusation, black granite banded with iron, its crown lost in low cloud that had hung there for three days without raining. Jonah had watched it from the conservatory's broken roof that morning, a cup of cold tea forgotten in her hands, and thought: *I know what you are. You are the thing I swore never to be.*

Lightning lived in that cloud. She could feel it the way she once felt weather through her teeth, before Hollowmere, before the ridge, before ten years of teaching her body to be only flesh. The storm above the spire wasn't natural. It was the crown-spirit's leash, coiled and humming, keeping Bram Ostell in the dark the way a spider keeps a fly.

"They'll have moved him to the deep cells by now," Sef said beside her, hunched under a borrowed fishmonger's cloak two sizes too big. "Third landing down. There's a stair behind the cisterns—servants used it to haul water up before they piped it, and nobody's used it since because the piped water smells of rats." She said it the way she said everything now, fast and flat, but her knuckles were white around the strap of her satchel. "Bram held the door for us. He counted to sixty before he ran the other way. I counted with him."

"I know," Jonah said. "I was there."

"You weren't *there* there. You were already running. I watched you run." Sef's voice cracked, and she hated it, Jonah could see her hating it. "He's going to think we left him."

"We didn't."

"Then we'd better not stand here being poetic about it."

* * *

They went in through the cistern stair at dusk, when the guards changed and the cloud above the spire thickened like a bruise. Sef moved ahead, small and certain, counting turns under her breath. Jonah followed with her hands in her pockets, and in her pockets her hands were shaking, and she let them shake because fighting it cost more than it bought.

The last time she had raised a storm she had been fourteen years old and furious, and the sky had answered her like a dog answering a whistle, all teeth and joy, and it had come down on the Ravensworth farm with the indiscriminate love of the old gods and buried her mother, her father, and her brother Tom beneath the collapsed barn. She remembered the silence after. She remembered that her first thought, her shameful, unspeakable first thought, had been *I did that. I can do that.* Power had arrived in her the way a fever arrives, and grief had arrived the way winter arrives, and she had spent ten years in a valley where neither could follow.

The stair smelled of rats, as promised, and of standing water, and of iron. On the third landing Sef pressed her ear to a door, held up four fingers, then folded one down. Three guards.

"I can do two," Sef whispered. "Not three. Not without noise."

"Then make noise somewhere else," Jonah said. "Sef. Listen to me." She crouched to the girl's height, and the words came out of her like something drawn from a well, bucket by bucket. "When I start, everything in this building is going to be about me. Every guard, every eye, every ear. The storm will want their attention and it will *take* it. That's your window. You go down the corridor, you get Bram, you bring him back up this stair, and you don't stop for anything, no matter what you hear. No matter what it sounds like I'm doing. Do you understand?"

Sef stared at her. In the dark her eyes were very wide and very young and absolutely unyielding. "You're going to call it. Here. In the middle of the city."

"I'm going to call it *carefully*."

"There's no such thing. Everyone says so. The Ravensworth curse—"

"The Ravensworth curse was a frightened child with no words for what she was." Jonah heard her own voice steady itself, and was grateful to it. "I have words now. I've had ten years of words. Go."

Sef went. Jonah listened to her boots fade down the corridor, counted to sixty the way Bram would have, and then climbed the last flight alone to the spire's upper gallery, where the cloud pressed against the arrow slits like something leaning in to watch.

* * *

There was no ritual. That was the first thing she had to accept, standing in the cold stone gallery with rain beginning to tick against the slits. There was no liturgy, no safe sequence, no priest's formula that would make this anything other than what it had been when she was fourteen: a girl opening a door and asking something enormous to come through it. The old gods did not do small. The old gods did not do *precise*, not on their own.

But she was not on her own. She had a garden in her head—ten years of it. She had rows and seasons and the patient arithmetic of things that grow. And she had Bram's voice in her memory, from the road, from the shrine, from a hundred fireside arguments: *A storm is not a weapon, Jonah. A storm is a prayer that got an answer. The question is what you were praying for.*

She had been praying for destruction, at fourteen. She knew that now. She had wanted the world that had hurt her to be unmade, and the sky had loved her enough to try.

She put her palm flat against the granite wall, felt the iron bands through the stone, and prayed the way she watered seedlings: evenly. Deliberately. *Come down. Come down gently. Come down where I put you.*

The cloud answered.

It did not come gently, not at first—nothing that large comes gently—but it came *obedient*, and that was the miracle she would never be able to explain to anyone who hadn't felt it. The lightning that had coiled above the spire for three days, the crown-spirit's own weather, turned its face toward her like a sunflower finding the sun. Jonah felt the moment of contact all the way down her spine: a vast, delighted attention, a hunger recognizing a hand that fed it.

*Little weather,* said something in the thunder, in a voice like the crown-spirit's and like her own memory braided together. *Little weather, you came back.*

"Not for you," Jonah said aloud, and pulled.

She pulled the way she had once pulled a stubborn taproot—steadily, hating the violence of it, knowing the ground would break. The storm came off the spire in a single descending column, a rope of rain and white light, and she *aimed* it. That was the word she would use later, the word she would hold onto in the nights afterward: aimed. It struck the iron bands at the spire's crown, and the bands sang, and the singing was the sound of a cage forgetting how to be a cage.

The building screamed. Not the people in it—the building itself, stone groaning, iron shrieking, every bell in the capital's eastern quarter ringing unstruck. Jonah stood in the gallery with her arms out and her eyes open because she refused, *refused*, to do this blind the way she had at fourteen. She watched the lightning go where she sent it and nowhere else. She counted the strikes the way she counted rows. Seven. Eleven. Fourteen. Each one a little harder to hold, each one a little more like the sky trying to take her wrist and lead.

*You could have all of it,* the thunder said, almost tender. *You could have the whole sky. You were only ever leashed by a valley, little weather, and look—the valley isn't here.*

"I know," Jonah said, through her teeth, through the taste of copper and ozone. "That's rather the point."

The fifteenth strike went through the spire's heart, and the deep cells' iron doors burst their locks in a single chord, and every light in the prison died at once.

* * *

Afterward she would not remember descending the stair. She would remember standing in the dark at the bottom, rain running off her chin, listening to footsteps—two sets, one small and quick, one dragging—and then Bram Ostell came around the corner of the corridor leaning on Sef's shoulder, his priest's coat torn at the seam, one eye swollen shut, and the first thing he said, hoarse and incredulous, was:

"You aimed it."

"Come on," Jonah said, because her voice was not steady enough for anything longer.

They went up through the cistern stair while the prison boiled with confusion behind them, guards shouting in the dark, and no one followed, because no one could look away from the sky. Through the arrow slits Jonah saw the capital turning out into the streets, hundreds of faces upturned, watching the storm she had made stand over the spire like a held breath—and then, because she could not hold it a moment longer, she let it go, and it came down all at once as clean, ordinary rain, washing the streets, putting out the shrine-fires, falling on the city like a benediction or an accusation depending on who was standing under it.

In the cistern stair, Sef looked up at her with an expression Jonah had never seen on her before. Not fear. Not awe. Something closer to recognition—the look of a girl who had spent her whole life being told she was nothing, watching someone become unmistakably, undeniably something.

"You made it *obey* you," Sef said. "It just—it did what you said."

"For about ninety seconds," Jonah said. "And then it wanted to do more, and I stopped it, and stopping it was the hardest thing I have ever done in my life. Don't make it sound easy, Sef. Please. I need it to not be easy."

"It's not easy," Bram said quietly. He had lowered himself onto the wet stair, breathing hard, his swollen eye weeping. "It was never supposed to be easy. Veyra's storms were never easy. That was the whole of her liturgy, in the end—*the sky is a hand, and you must ask it to be gentle.*" He looked up at Jonah with his one good eye, and there was something in his face she had not seen there before: not the wariness of a man hiding from his past, but the terrible relief of a man watching his past be redeemed. "Ten years I thought your order's sin was the only sin. That we abandoned her. But there are two ways to abandon a god, Jonah. You can stop praying. Or you can keep praying and never once listen to what she actually wants." He touched the rain-dark stone. "She wants to be asked. Not commanded. Asked. And you just asked."

Jonah sat down on the stair beside him, because her legs had decided the matter without consulting her. The rain drummed on the cistern roof above. Somewhere across the city, a bell was still ringing.

"I felt it know me," she said. "The crown-spirit. When I touched its storm, it—it said my name. Well. Not my name."

"Little weather," Sef said.

"Little weather." Jonah laughed, or something near it, and it came out cracked. "It knew me before I ever crossed the ridge. Bram—when you were captured, did it—did it say anything about—"

"About the Ravensworth farm?" Bram's good eye was gentle and awful. "It talked about your mother's garden. It talked about Tom. It wanted me to know it remembered them." He paused. "It wanted *you* to know, I think. Everything it said to me, it said like a message being carried."

Jonah put her face in her hands. The rain went on falling, steady and ordinary, the first rain she had made in ten years and the first rain she had ever made on purpose, and it was falling on the city where her family's killer wore a crown, and every soul under it had seen the sky bend to her will.

* * *

They came out of the cisterns into the fishmonger's alley an hour past dark, three soaked ghosts, and the city was not the city they had entered. It was awake. It was *lit*—every window, every doorway, people standing in the rain without shelter, faces turned up. And the sound they were making was not fear.

They were praying.

Jonah heard it as they slipped along the alley's edge: hundreds of voices, ragged and overlapping, and not one of them asking for anything. *The storm came down. The storm came down and spared us. The storm chose.* Someone had already given her a name she hadn't asked for—*the Rainmother*, someone was calling her, a woman on a doorstep with her arms spread in the downpour—and someone else had already given her a different name, shouted from a shuttered window with hate in it: *storm-witch. Storm-witch. Curse of Ravensworth.*

"Word travels fast in a city that prays too loud," Sef murmured.

"Faster than we can walk," Jonah said. Her hands had stopped shaking. That was the strange thing. Ten years of hiding, and now that the hiding was over—now that every eye in the capital would know by morning what walked among them—her hands were perfectly, terribly calm. "They'll come for us. The council. Whatever's left of the council that isn't wearing that crown. They'll call me an enemy of the realm before the rain's dried off the streets."

"Then we'd better get somewhere high," Sef said, "and get there first."

Bram pushed himself upright against the wall, swaying, and grinned through his ruined face—a real grin, wild and young, the first Jonah had ever seen on him. "The conservatory," he said. "Your garden under the palace. If they're going to come for a storm-caller, let them come and find her kneeling in the dirt with seed trays."

But Jonah was looking at the sky. The rain was thinning now, her storm spent and scattered, and through the breaking cloud the spire stood black against the stars—and at its crown, where she had shattered the iron, something glowed. Faint. Patient. A light like a held breath, like an eye opening after a long time in the dark.

The crown-spirit had felt every inch of her storm. Had tasted it. Had *recognized* it.

And it was no longer pretending to be only a crown.

"Jonah," Sef said quietly, following her gaze. "What's it doing?"

"Sending for me," Jonah said. "Or sending something to me." She turned from the spire and pulled her hood up, and the rain ran off it in silver threads. "Either way—by tomorrow, the whole realm will know I'm here. So tonight we decide what they know *next*."

Above them, the light at the spire's crown pulsed once, slow, like a heartbeat. And far off across the sleeping city, from the direction of the palace, came the first toll of a bell that Jonah did not recognize—and from the way Bram went rigid, neither did he.

"That's the council bell," he said. "It hasn't rung in forty years. It only rings for one thing."

"What thing?" Sef asked.

Bram looked at Jonah. "A trial."
