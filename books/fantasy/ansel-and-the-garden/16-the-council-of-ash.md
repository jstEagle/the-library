## 16. The Council of Ash

Bram Ostell had stood before councils before. He had stood before them as a young priest with ink-stained fingers and a god's name sweet on his tongue, and he had stood before them later, disgraced, stripped of his vestments, while men who had never prayed a day in their lives explained to him precisely what he had done wrong. He had thought those days had taught him everything there was to know about the architecture of power: the raised dais, the measured distance between the accused and the accusers, the way authority always arranged itself in semicircles so that no single face had to bear the weight of judgment alone.

He had been wrong. Those councils had been made of men.

This one was made of something wearing men like coats.

The Hall of Ash had earned its name honestly—so the stories went—when a fire two centuries past had gutted the original chamber and the masons, in their arrogance or their thrift, had simply polished the charred beams and built around them. Now those blackened rafters arched overhead like ribs of some enormous dead thing, and the morning light came through the high windows gray and thin, catching motes of dust that drifted down through the air like slow snow. Nine seats on the dais. Nine councilors of the realm of Aldric. Bram counted them as they entered, one by one, and his stomach turned over quietly, the way it did before he was about to be sick.

Five of them moved wrong.

It was nothing a stranger would notice. It was nothing most courtiers would notice, because most courtiers had learned long ago not to look too closely at power. But Bram had spent thirty years reading the body language of the divine—the way a god's presence settled into flesh, the small telltales of possession that no amount of practice ever quite erased. The fifth councilor from the left blinked a half-beat too slowly, like a man surfacing from deep water. The woman at the center, Chancellor Vess, who had once been famous for her razor wit, now spoke only when spoken to, and when she did speak her lips moved a fraction before any sound came, as though she were hearing instructions across some distance no one else could measure. And the old lord at the far end—Bram couldn't recall his name, though he knew he should—sat with his hands folded in his lap so perfectly still that stillness itself became an accusation. Living hands fidgeted. Living men shifted their weight, cleared their throats, scratched. These hands might have been carved from the same blackened oak as the rafters above.

*Half-possessed already,* Jonah had said, and she had been generous. Or perhaps she simply hadn't known what to look for. She was standing beside him now, at the foot of the dais, in a borrowed guard's cloak that fit her badly, her hands loose at her sides in the deliberate way of someone who had spent ten years teaching herself not to flinch. Sef was on Jonah's other side, small and sharp-faced in her stable boy's clothes, and if Bram hadn't known what she was about to do he would have found her expression almost comical: the studied boredom of a child forced to attend a sermon.

They had come to the Hall of Ash because there was nowhere else left to go. The informant's betrayal had cost them Bram's freedom for a night and nearly cost them everything else; the rescue from the spire had cost them their anonymity entirely. Every eye in the palace had seen the storm break over the prison tower—had seen lightning walk down out of a cloudless sky and find the one place in the capital where it should not have been able to go. By dawn, guards had been searching the undercrofts. By midmorning, Sef had said the words that stopped them all cold: *If we run, we lose. If we're dragged in, we lose slower. There's a third door.*

The third door was this: walk into the Hall of Ash under your own power, before the summons could be drafted, and force the realm to look at what it was becoming.

"You are either very brave," Bram murmured, "or you have finally lost whatever sense the gods left you."

"Both can be true," Jonah said, without moving her lips. Her eyes stayed fixed on the dais. "Stand straight. They'll be looking for guilt."

"They'll be looking for a great deal more than guilt, I'm afraid."

The herald's voice rang out against the charred beams, thin and official: *"Jonah Ravensworth, called storm-witch of Hollowmere. Bram Ostell, defrocked priest, formerly of the Order of Veyra. And—"*

The herald hesitated. He had reached the third name and found, apparently, that he did not have one. His eyes flicked toward the chancellor, seeking instruction, and Bram watched Chancellor Vess's mouth move again—that terrible fractional delay—and heard her voice come out level and dry as old paper:

"The boy is irrelevant. Proceed to the charges."

And Bram understood, in that instant, exactly how Sef meant to open her third door. Not by denying the charge. By making herself impossible to dismiss.

* * *

The charges were read aloud by a clerk with a beautiful voice and dead eyes, and they were, Bram had to admit, masterfully constructed. Storm-calling within the capital walls—a crime under edicts older than Aldric's reign. Unlawful entry into the spire prison. Conspiracy with enemies of the crown. Each charge was true, or true enough, and each was delivered without heat, without theater, which somehow made it worse. This was not a trial. Trials required someone who wanted to know the truth. This was a formality performed for a room full of witnesses, most of whom were already half-lost, and the sentence had been written before they walked in.

"Jonah Ravensworth." Chancellor Vess rose from her seat, and up close Bram could see what the distance had hidden: the fine tremor in her jaw, the sweat at her hairline despite the hall's chill, the way her eyes had a wet, overbright quality, like a lamp burning oil it didn't have. Something was riding her, and something in her was fighting it, and the fight was going the way such fights always went. "You stand accused of acts of war against the realm. Before this council pronounces judgment, is there anything you wish to say?"

"I'd say a great deal," Jonah answered, "if I thought anyone on that dais was free to hear it."

A ripple went through the gallery. Courtiers exchanged glances. Two of the guards at the doors shifted their grips on their halberds. And five of the nine councilors did not react at all—not with anger, not with offense, not with anything. They sat like furniture while their colleague absorbed the insult on their behalf, and Bram felt the truth of it settle into the room like cold air through a cracked window. Everyone saw. Almost no one understood what they were seeing. The ones who understood were already calculating whether the exits were guarded.

"Your words are noted," Vess said, and her voice caught, just slightly, on the last word—as though the thing inside her had needed to take the sentence away from her and finish it itself. "Judgment will proceed. For storm-calling within the walls of the capital, the penalty is—"

"Wait." Sef's voice. Small, clear, pitched to carry, and utterly without the boredom now. "You forgot to read my name."

Vess paused. The whole hall seemed to pause with her, the dust hanging motionless in the gray light.

"The boy is irrelevant," the chancellor repeated.

"No," Sef said. "I'm really not." And she stepped forward, out of the shelter of Jonah's shadow, into the open floor where every eye could reach her, and Bram's heart clenched because he knew what came next and he could do nothing to stop it and less than nothing to protect her from it. She pulled the cap from her head. She shook out her hair—long, dark, unmistakably a girl's—and looked up at the dais with her chin lifted at the exact angle Bram had seen her use on innkeepers and highwaymen and gods.

"My name is Sefarine of House Merren," she said. "Ward of the crown. Ward of King Aldric himself, since I was six years old, placed in his household by decree and sealed with the royal signet—which I still carry, if anyone would like to see it. And I am asking this council, formally, by right of my wardship, for an audience with the king."

The silence that followed had texture. Bram could feel it pressing against his eardrums.

"You may address your petition to this council," Vess said at last.

"I've seen what happens to petitions addressed to this council." Sef's voice didn't waver, but Bram, standing close enough to see her hands, saw them: fists at her sides, knuckles white, trembling very slightly, the fear held in her body like water held in cupped palms. "I've seen the king's edicts. I carried them. Half of them aren't in his hand. The man who signed the order sending a courier to Hollowmere isn't the man who wrote half the laws this council has passed this year. You want to judge her? Fine. Judge her. But do it in front of the king. Do it in front of *Aldric*, not whatever signs his name these days. Unless—" and here she turned, deliberately, slowly, letting her gaze travel along the row of seated figures, touching each of the still ones, the wrong ones, naming them without naming them— "unless this council is afraid of what the king would say. If he could say it."

Somewhere in the gallery a woman gasped. A guard took one step forward and then stopped, uncertain, looking toward the dais for orders that did not come.

Chancellor Vess descended the dais.

She came down the steps slowly, her robes whispering against the stone, and she crossed the floor until she stood directly before Sef, close enough that Bram could see the chancellor's breath fogging faintly in the cold air of the hall—though the girl's breath did not fog, and his own did, and Jonah's did, and something about that asymmetry made the skin crawl up the back of Bram's neck. Vess looked down at Sef for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was almost gentle.

"Child. Do you know what happens to wards who embarrass the crown?"

"I know what happens to kings who stop being people," Sef said. "I've been watching it happen for a year. From inside the house."

Something moved behind the chancellor's eyes. Bram had a name for it—he had seen it once, decades ago, in the final hours of a dying congregation, the moment when the god's hunger and the host's remaining self struggled over the same mouth. Vess's hand rose, slow and jointless, toward Sef's face—

And stopped.

Stopped as though it had struck glass. Stopped, and trembled, and the chancellor's expression fractured—just for a heartbeat, just for the space of a single drawn breath—into something raw and human and *terrified*, and her voice, when it came, was not quite her own cadence but not quite the other thing either, but a woman drowning, using the last air in her lungs:

"*Take her to the king.*"

Then the stillness closed back over her face like water over a stone, and the hand completed its gesture—turned palm-up, gracious, a courtier's invitation—and the voice that followed was smooth and cold and belonged to no one.

"The council grants the Lady Sefarine's petition. An audience with His Majesty will be granted. Immediately." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "All of you. His Majesty has been *most curious* about the storm-caller."

* * *

They were escorted through corridors Bram did not know, by guards who did not speak, and the whole time he walked he was doing arithmetic he hated.

Sef had gambled, and the wager had paid—but Bram could not stop turning over the manner of its paying. *Take her to the king.* Had that been Vess, breaking free for one desperate instant? Or had it been the other thing, the hungry thing, choosing—with perfect, patient malice—to give them exactly what they asked for? To bring the storm-caller, the unbinding ritual, the last priest of Veyra, all of it, directly into the presence of the crown it wore?

He thought of Veyra. He thought of the texts he had burned, the warnings he had ignored, the order that had starved its own god and called the starvation mercy. Hunger, he had learned, was not stupid. Hunger was the oldest strategist in the world. It waited. It opened doors and stood aside and let you walk through them, because everything you wanted led, eventually, to everything it wanted.

Beside him, Jonah walked with her eyes forward and her breathing even, but he had traveled with her long enough now to read the weather in her. Her right hand kept drifting toward her left wrist, where the old scar tissue lay beneath the sleeve, and pressing there, briefly, like a woman checking a wound.

"It saw us coming," he said quietly. "This is what it wanted."

"Probably," Jonah said.

"And we're walking into it anyway."

"Yes." She glanced at him then, and there was something in her face he had not seen in all their weeks on the road—something that was not courage exactly, because courage implied a choice between fear and action, and what he saw in her had gone past that, had burned the fear down to its clean white ash. "Bram. Whatever happens in that room—whatever it offers me, whatever it shows me—you hold the liturgy. That's your part. Don't let go of it. No matter what I say. *No matter what I beg.*"

"That sounds like a request I'm going to hate honoring."

"It is," she said. "That's why I'm asking now, while I still mean it."

Ahead of them, Sef walked between the guards with her cap crushed in her fist and her spine straight as a lance, twelve years old and betting her life on the memory of a man who might no longer exist. Bram watched the back of her head and thought: *this is what faith looks like when it grows up wild, untended, without a single priest to prune it.* Not certainty. Certainty was cheap; he had surrounded himself with certain men once, and they had buried a god. No—faith was this. Walking toward the thing you feared most on the strength of a maybe. Placing your whole weight on a bridge you could not see, because the alternative was standing forever on the shore.

The corridor ended at doors of pale wood inlaid with silver stars, and beyond them, he knew, waited the throne room, and the throne, and the king.

One of the guards knocked. From within came a voice—Aldric's voice, Bram realized, the actual voice of the man he had met once, years ago, at a consecration neither of them had wanted to attend—warm and tired and terribly, horribly ordinary:

"Send them in. I've been waiting such a long time to meet her."

The doors swung open onto light.

And Bram Ostell, last priest of a forgotten god, gripped the liturgy in his pocket—paper and ink and a dead woman's name—and stepped through after them, praying to nothing, hoping for everything.
