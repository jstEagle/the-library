## 17. Two Kings, One Crown

The lucidity came to Aldric the way dawn came in winter—slowly, grudgingly, and with no promise it would last.

He knew it first by the taste of his own mouth. When the crown-spirit wore him, he tasted of iron and old incense, of blood someone else had spilled. But in the clear hours—and they came less often now, minutes shrinking to heartbeats—he tasted only himself: stale wine, unwashed teeth, the ghost of the lemon drops his mother had pressed into his palm before audiences when he was a boy afraid of his own throne.

He was sitting at the writing desk in his private chambers, and there was a girl asleep on the floor beside the cold hearth, curled like a comma, one hand still wrapped around a stub of candle.

Sef.

His ward. His runaway. The child who had slipped out of the palace disguised as a stable boy and come back through the servant passages with two strangers and a plan that should have gotten them all killed. She had sat with him through the worst of the night's storm—the *other* storm, the one inside his skull—and she had talked to him. Not to the thing wearing him. To *him*. About the conservatory, about grafting apple stock, about the way Jonah Ravensworth hummed when she worked the soil. Small things. Human things. Anchors thrown into drowning water.

And he had surfaced. God help him, he had surfaced.

Aldric looked down at his hands. They were steady. That alone told him how rare this hour was; usually his fingers trembled or clenched without his permission, inkwells shattered, goblets crushed, the signatures on state documents slanting into a hand that was not his own. He flexed them now, watching the tendons move, marveling the way a man might marvel at rain after drought.

On the desk lay the letter he had been writing when the darkness took him last—a letter begun eleven times, finished never. He picked up the pen.

*Jonah Ravensworth,* he wrote, and stopped, because the name itself felt like an accusation.

He remembered the courier. He remembered choosing her.

It had been nearly a year ago, in another lucid hour—one of the long ones, almost a full day, back when he could still buy time by starving himself, by refusing sleep, by any small cruelty to his own body that made him harder to inhabit. He had sat in this same chair and thought: *the crown is bound to me, but I am not bound to be alone in it.* He had needed someone outside the palace walls. Someone beyond the spirit's reach. Someone who owed the old gods nothing and feared them everything.

And there was exactly one person in all the nine provinces who fit that description. A storm-caller buried alive in a valley where magic could not follow. A girl who had killed her family with the sky and been sentenced not to death but to silence.

*She will hate me for it,* he had written in his journal that night. *Good. Hate will keep her alive longer than gratitude would.*

So he had sent the courier with a sealed plea addressed to no god at all—to *anyone still listening*—knowing full well that in Hollowmere the paper would be inert, dead, harmless. Knowing that if anyone could carry it out of that valley alive, it would be the woman who had spent ten years learning how to survive a place designed to bury power.

He had not known she would come. He had hoped. And hope, in a king, is a dangerous kind of prayer.

The pen scratched. Outside the window, the city murmured its endless feverish devotions; even here, three floors up behind stone and oak, he could hear the shrines, the chanting, the wet raw sound of ten thousand throats offering themselves to the thing in his head. It fed on that. It grew fat on that. Every hymn was a hand feeding sugar to a wolf.

But not this hour. This hour was his.

*Aldric?*

The voice came from the doorway, low and careful. Jonah Ravensworth stood there with Bram Ostell behind her, the old priest's face gray with exhaustion, both of them looking at the king as though he were a lit fuse they'd been asked to hold.

"Sit," Aldric said. His voice cracked on the word; he hadn't spoken as himself in days. "Please. We have very little time, and I intend to waste none of it apologizing."

Jonah crossed the room slowly. Up close she looked younger than her reputation and older than her years, sun-browned and wind-chapped, a woman built by soil and patience. Her eyes went to Sef sleeping by the hearth, softened, then returned to him with a wariness he had earned.

"You wrote to me," she said. "A year ago."

"I did."

"Why me?"

"Because you're the only person in my kingdom the crown cannot touch." He gestured at the chair across the desk. "Because you know what it is to be caged by well-meaning people for the crime of what you are. And because—" he exhaled, "—because I am a selfish man, Mistress Ravensworth, and I wanted someone to blame besides myself."

Something flickered across her face—not forgiveness, not yet, but the recognition of honesty when it finally showed itself.

"The ritual," Bram said quietly, from the door. "You said you had it."

"I said I *had* it." Aldric opened the drawer beneath the writing surface and drew out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord gone brittle with age. His hands shook now—not from the spirit, but from something older. Fear, perhaps. Or memory. "My grandfather's grandfather found it in the archives of Veyra's temple, back when the temple still stood and the priests still lied about why their god had gone silent. Unbinding. The severing of a spirit from its vessel." He set it on the desk between them like something that might bite. "I've read it four hundred times. I can recite it. I have never dared begin it, because the failure states are... instructive."

Bram stepped forward and untied the cord with fingers that trembled worse than the king's. As the oilcloth fell open, the old priest made a sound—a small, wounded noise, the sound of a man finding his god's handwriting in a place he'd been told she'd never been.

"This is liturgy," Bram whispered. "This is *hers*. This is Veyra's own rite."

"Then you'll understand it better than I ever could." Aldric turned to Jonah. "Which brings us to the part I have dreaded saying aloud, because saying it makes it real, and because I have watched too many people make promises to my face that the thing in my head later used against them."

Jonah met his eyes. Hers were the color of a sky deciding whether to rain.

"When the unbinding begins," Aldric said, "the crown-spirit will fight. Not like a thief caught in a house—like a starving animal defending the only food it has ever been given. Me. It will pour itself deeper into my bones. It will wear my voice. It will beg, threaten, bargain—it knows things about me that no living soul should know, and it will spend every one of them like coin. And if the rite fails—if the binding holds past the final word—then whatever is left in this body afterward will not be me. Do you understand? There is no coming back from a failed severing. There is only the puppet, wearing my face, walking my kingdom, signing my edicts, forever."

The fire crackled. Somewhere below, a bell tolled the hour, and the chanting swelled to meet it.

"So," Aldric continued, and his voice did not shake, because he had rehearsed this in the dark for a year, "I am asking you for something no subject should ever ask of anyone. If the rite fails—if you feel the binding cinch instead of break—I want you to end me. Before the last syllable fades. Before it can settle back in. Fire, blade, storm, I don't care. I would rather die as Aldric than live as the crown's glove."

Silence. Long enough that he heard Sef shift in her sleep and murmur something about apples.

"No," Jonah said.

Aldric blinked. In a year of horrors, he had not prepared for refusal.

"Your Majesty," Jonah said, and her voice was quiet and absolutely level, the voice of a woman who had argued with the earth itself and won, "I have spent ten years being the instrument of a death I didn't choose. Everyone in my valley looks at me and sees the storm that killed my family. I will not add your corpse to that ledger on a *maybe*. If the rite fails, we try again. If it fails twice, we find a third way. I didn't cross that ridge to be your executioner. I crossed it to cut a god off a man like rot off a tree."

"And if there is no third way?" Aldric leaned forward. "If the choice is my death or my enslavement?"

"Then I choose badly," Jonah said. "That's what freedom is. I don't get to be the knife just because it's cleaner for everyone else."

For a moment the king could not speak. Something in his chest—something the spirit had been gnawing on for years, some soft organ it had assumed long since eaten—turned over, warm and astonished.

He laughed. It came out rusty and broken, half sob, but it was *his* laugh, the first in longer than he could measure.

"Oh, you are exactly who I hoped you were," he said. "Very well. No executioner. But hear my terms, and let them stand as my last royal decree if it comes to that." He drew a breath. "If the binding fails, and I am lost, and you refuse to end me—then you take Sef and Bram and you run. Over the ridge, past the border, anywhere. You do not stay to negotiate with the thing wearing my face. You do not let it use my mouth to talk you into mercy. Promise me that much, at least."

Jonah held his gaze for a long moment. Then, slowly, she nodded. "That much. That I promise."

"That's more than I deserve." He slid the oilcloth bundle across the desk toward Bram. "Priest. Can you hold the liturgy? All of it? The rite requires a voice that isn't mine and isn't hers—someone to speak the words of the god being freed, so the god has somewhere to go that isn't deeper into me."

Bram's throat worked. "I have spent twenty years praying to nothing," he said hoarsely. "I can manage one prayer that answers back."

"Then we do it tomorrow. At dawn, in the throne room, before the whole court." Aldric rose, steadying himself on the desk, and was briefly ashamed of how much the simple act cost him. "Yes, before the court—you think I haven't considered doing this in secret? The spirit feeds on worship. Where better to starve it than in front of every kneeling fool in the capital, when the object of their devotion is torn out of its meat and shown to them for what she is? Let them watch. Let them *see*."

"It could rally them against you," Jonah said. "Against all of us."

"It already has. Half the council wears her fingerprints like rings." He moved to the window. Below, the city sprawled under a bruised pre-dawn dark, shrine-fires dotting it like a rash. "Tomorrow either I am a king again, or I am a corpse, or I am the worst thing this kingdom has ever produced. I find I'm tired of the suspense."

Behind him, a small voice said, "You forgot the fourth option."

They turned. Sef sat up by the hearth, hair wild, candle stub still in her fist, eyes bright and furious and awake—she had been awake, Aldric realized, for all of it, listening the way she listened to everything, cataloguing, surviving.

"Fourth option," she said, standing, chin up, twelve years old and utterly unafraid of kings. "You win. You sit on your throne tomorrow with nothing in your head but your own stubborn self, and you make me officially your daughter, and you owe me about six years of birthday presents. That's the option. Write it down."

Aldric looked at her—at this fierce, thieving, impossible child the court had tried to make into an ornament and who had instead made herself into an anchor—and felt the lucid hour burn brighter, fed by sheer defiant love.

"Noted," he said gravely, "and entered into the record. Six years of presents. Assuming I retain legal ownership of my own soul by week's end."

"Assuming," Sef agreed, and stuck out her hand to shake on it, because she was a stable boy from nowhere and that was how business was done.

He shook it. Her grip was astonishingly strong.

* * *

They left him at grey light. Jonah paused at the door, and for a moment king and storm-caller regarded each other across a room that smelled of ink and cold ash.

"The courier," she said. "He died getting your letter to me. Did you know his name?"

"Teodor Vance," Aldric said quietly. "Seventeen years in my service. I chose him because he was fast, loyal, and had no family to grieve him." He bowed his head. "I will carve it myself. On the memorial. With my own hands, if these hands are still mine."

Jonah studied him a moment longer, weighing him, the way she seemed to weigh everything—soil, weather, men. Then she nodded once and was gone.

Alone, Aldric returned to the desk. The lucidity was thinning; he could feel the other presence stirring in the deep places, tasting the edges of his mind like a tongue testing a cage door. Minutes, perhaps. Less.

He took up the pen and wrote, fast, in his own hand while it was still his own:

*To whoever finds this if I fail: the decision was mine. The request was mine. Whatever stands up from the throne room wearing my face, remember that Aldric Ravensworth's—no. Remember that Aldric the king chose this. Do not mourn the puppet. Mourn the man, briefly, and then burn the script.*

He signed it, sealed it, and set it where Sef would find it, because the girl missed nothing and kept everything.

Then the tide came in. The pressure behind his eyes, the sweet iron taste, the vast patient hunger unfolding itself through his nerves like a hand sliding into a glove. He had learned, over the years, to hide in the small rooms of himself—the memory of lemon drops, the weight of a child's handshake, the sound of his own laugh, rusty and real.

*Little king,* the crown-spirit murmured through his own thoughts, savoring. *I dreamed of storms tonight. Such weather coming. Such wonderful weather.*

Aldric smiled with lips that were no longer entirely his, and held on to the handshake, and waited for dawn.
