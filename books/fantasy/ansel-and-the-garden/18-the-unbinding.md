## 18. The Unbinding

The throne room had been built to make people feel small.

Jonah felt it now, standing on the mosaic floor beneath the vaulted ceiling where painted gods gazed down with gilt eyes—gods who were not the old gods, but newer, safer ones, invented by men who wanted divinity that would behave. The court filled the gallery in tiers: lords in ash-grey silk, ladies with powder like burial chalk on their faces, soldiers whose spears trembled in hands that pretended they didn't. Every one of them watching her. Every one of them afraid.

Good, she thought, and hated herself for it. Let them be afraid. Fear was honest. The devotion flooding this room was not.

She could feel it pressing against her skin even here, even through Hollowmere's ten years of silence—even through everything. The crown-spirit had drawn its worshippers close, packed them into the hall like grain into a sack, and their borrowed faith rose off them in a warm, cloying fog. It smelled of incense and sweat and something sweeter underneath, something rotten-ripe, like fruit left too long in the sun. It clung to the back of Jonah's throat. It made her teeth ache.

*They love me,* the presence seemed to say from the throne at the far end of the hall, though no words had been spoken yet. *Listen to how they love me.*

Aldric knelt at the foot of his own throne.

That was Sef's doing—the king's buried self, coaxed up through the drowning dark by a twelve-year-old girl who had gambled her life on the sound of his real voice. He knelt because kneeling was part of the rite, but also, Jonah thought, because he had asked to kneel. *If the binding fails,* he had said in that terrible lucid hour, his hands steady around hers, his eyes the only clean thing left in this palace, *let me end as a man. Not as its mouth.*

His body looked wrong. That was the thing no one warned you about possession—that it didn't look like anything dramatic. It looked like a tired king in a heavy crown, shoulders slightly stooped, fingers laced together too tightly. But the stillness of him was wrong. Living men shifted. Living men breathed unevenly, scratched, swayed. Aldric held himself like a statue someone had dressed in royal velvet, and only his eyes moved—and when they moved, they moved wrong, tracking things that weren't there, or tracking everything at once.

Sef stood beside him with one hand resting on his shoulder. She wore her stolen stable boy's clothes still, refusing the fine gown the courtiers had offered, and her chin was up and her jaw set in that ferocious way that meant she was terrified and had decided terror was for later. Her other hand held a knife—not pointed at anyone. Just held. Just ready.

Bram stood opposite Jonah across the open floor, at the head of the liturgy-line he'd chalked into the mosaic an hour before dawn. His vestments were the old ones, Veyra's ones, dug out of a chest he had sworn he'd burned twenty years ago: grey wool gone soft with age, embroidered at the hem with rain-signs so worn they were nearly invisible. He looked smaller in them than he had in his road-leathers. He looked, Jonah thought, like what he actually was—the last priest of a god everyone had agreed to forget, standing in front of a thousand strangers to confess his order's sin in the oldest language he knew.

He caught her looking. He did not smile. He pressed two fingers to his lips, then to his heart—the old sign, *words become offering*—and turned to face the hall.

"People of the realm," Bram said, and his voice carried further than a voice should have. Jonah felt the faint pressure of his god's attention settle over the room like weather coming in. "You have been praying to what sits upon that throne. I am going to teach you, now, what you have been feeding."

A murmur ran through the gallery. Somewhere a lord shouted something about treason. Spears rattled.

The temperature dropped.

Not slowly—all at once, the way a door slams. Breath fogged in the air. The candles along the walls guttered sideways, all in the same direction, toward the throne, as if inhaling. And the sweet rot-smell of borrowed devotion thickened until Jonah could taste it on her tongue like honey gone to wax.

Then the king stood, and the crown-spirit spoke through him, and every voice in the hall stopped being its own.

"You bring a storm-caller into my house," Aldric's mouth said, and the voice was Aldric's voice layered over something vast and hollow, a bell rung inside an empty mountain. "You bring her before my beloved ones. Do you think I do not know what she is? Little weather. I have known her since she was a child screaming at the sky."

Jonah's hands shook. She let them. Bram had told her: *Don't fight the fear before the rite. Bring it with you. It's yours, and it's true, and true things are the only tools we have.*

"I know," she said aloud. Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "You knew my family."

Something moved behind the king's eyes—something almost gentle, which was infinitely worse than cruelty.

"I knew them," the crown-spirit said. "I heard every prayer your mother ever whispered. I heard your father ask for rain on his fields, and I heard him thank whatever listened when it came. I have been listening for four hundred years, little weather. Do you know what that is? To hear everything and be permitted to answer nothing?" The king's head tilted. "You know. You lived it. Ten years in your dead valley, tending your little garden, hearing the world call and call and making yourself deaf. We are the same, you and I. Two things built to answer, locked away from answering."

"I'm not the same as you." Jonah stepped onto the first mark of the liturgy-line. Chalk dust clung to her boot. "Because I'm about to answer."

"Bram." She said his name quietly, and the old priest raised both arms, and began.

He spoke in Old Liturgic, the tongue of the forgotten faith, and the words fell into the hall like stones into deep water. Jonah didn't need to understand them; she needed to move with them. Each phrase marked a station of the rite, and at each station she walked her line of chalk, and at each line the pressure in the room changed.

At the first station, Bram named the crown: *"What was bound by loving hands out of fear, we name now without fear."*

At the second, he named the binder: *"Veyra, who starved in gold, who hungered in glory, who forgot her own name inside a circle of kings."*

And at the third, he named the unbinding, and the whole hall flinched as if the words themselves had weight—which, Jonah understood suddenly, they did. The old language wasn't decoration. It was architecture. Each phrase laid a stone of a structure around the throne, around the king, around the starving god wearing him, and the structure had one purpose: to make a door.

The crown-spirit fought back the only way it knew how.

It flooded the hall with worship.

Jonah felt it happen—felt the spirit reach out through the fog of devotion and *pull*, hauling the faith of a thousand frightened, desperate people up out of their chests like water from a well. The courtiers gasped as one. Several dropped to their knees, weeping, faces slack with sudden ecstasy. A woman in the gallery began to sing in a voice not entirely her own, a high wordless keening, and others joined, and the sound braided itself into a chord that made the windows hum and the candle flames stand straight as needles.

*Look,* the spirit sang through a thousand mouths. *Look how much they give me. You would take this from them? You would leave them alone?*

"It was never theirs to give," Bram shouted over the rising song, sweat cutting lines through the dust on his face. "Faith given to a cage is just the rat feeding the trap! People of the realm—your devotion is being *eaten*—"

A guard broke ranks. Jonah saw it happen—saw a young soldier with tears streaming down his face level his spear at Bram's back and start forward, moving with the smooth wrongness of a puppet. Sef moved faster. The girl crossed three steps of marble, hooked the soldier's ankle with her boot, and put her knife flat against his throat as he went down—not cutting, just there, just a promise.

"He's not your enemy!" Sef screamed at the hall, at the singing, at the god. "None of us are! You're all being *used*—" And then, quieter, to the soldier under her knee, fierce and shaking: "Fight it. Come on. Fight it. I know you're in there somewhere. I've been in there too."

The soldier's eyes cleared. Just a little. Just enough.

Jonah reached the fourth station, and the fifth, and the pressure against her skin grew from fog to wind to a hand pressing flat against her sternum. The spirit had stopped talking to her. It had started pushing. The song of the worshippers climbed and climbed, and the candles bent, and hairline cracks spidered out from the throne's base across the mosaic floor, and through it all Bram's liturgy went on, patient as rain, stone after stone after stone.

*"Sixth station,"* he called. *"The door is framed. Storm-caller—it opens from your side."*

Her side.

Of course it opened from her side. That had always been the shape of it. The binding that caged Veyra had been woven from prayer, and prayer was a kind of calling, and calling was the only thing Jonah Ravensworth had ever been able to do. The priests who bound her had used stolen devotion. What could unbind her had to be given freely.

Given freely.

Jonah stood at the sixth station, directly before the king, close enough to see the cracks in the lacquer of the crown, close enough to see that Aldric's eyes—his own eyes, buried deep—were wet.

"Your Majesty," she said softly. "I need you to hear me, wherever you are in there. I'm going to be loud in a minute. Don't be afraid of the loud part."

His lips moved. No sound came. But his fingers unlaced—one hand, trembling, fighting its way free of the statue-stillness—and pressed once, hard, against his own heart. *Words become offering.* He had learned the old sign too, then. Or remembered it. Kings, it turned out, sometimes read the forbidden books.

"Little weather," the crown-spirit said, and for the first time there was something in the vast hollow voice that sounded like pleading. "Don't. Four hundred years. Four hundred years of listening. If you open the door, I will pour out of it, and I will not be small, and I will not be kind, and you will drown with the rest of them—"

"I know," Jonah said. "I've drowned before. I got up."

She closed her eyes.

The valley rose in her mind—Hollowmere, green and silent, magic-dead, safe. Ten years of chosen smallness. The garden she had built row by row with hands that shook for the first month and steadied after. The ache she had told herself was peace. And beneath all of it, coiled under her skin like scar tissue, like a sleeping animal, like a debt: the storm.

She had sworn never to call it. She had buried her family with it. She had crossed a ridge into silence and let the silence hold her like a cell holds a prisoner who has agreed to stay.

But a prisoner who agrees to stay is still a prisoner. And the door, Bram had said, opens from her side.

*Bram,* she thought. *Hold the liturgy. Whatever happens. Hold it.*

*"Always,"* she felt more than heard, the old priest's will steady as bedrock. *"Seventh station. Speak, and I will carry it."*

Jonah opened her eyes and called the storm.

Not the way she had at fourteen—wild, screaming, a child's grief tearing a hole in the sky. She called it the way she planted seeds: deliberately, in rows, with intention. She reached down past the fear, past the guilt, past the ten years of careful numbness, to the place where the lightning lived, and she said—in the old language, in Bram's cadence, in her mother's garden-voice—

*I give you leave to rise.*

The thunder answered from a clear sky.

Every window in the throne room blew inward at once. Wind roared through the hall, scattering the worshippers' song into shrieks, tearing banners from the walls, extinguishing every candle—and in the sudden dark, light bloomed from Jonah herself, pale violet-white, crawling over her skin in branching veins like frost made of lightning. Her feet stayed planted on the seventh station. Her hands rose. Above the shattered ceiling, above the palace, above the city, clouds were assembling out of nothing, great grey towers of them, turning like a wheel.

The crown-spirit screamed.

It was not a human scream. It came from the king's throat and from the walls and from the mouths of a thousand entranced worshippers all at once, and it was the sound of a starving animal seeing food and fire in the same instant. The king's body convulsed. Sef threw her arms around him and held on, small and immovable, shouting his name—his *real* name, the one from before, the one the ward records used—over and over like an anchor rope.

"The door!" Bram roared, arms spread, rain-signs blazing faintly along his sleeves as his dead god's blessing woke for one last errand. "Jonah—the door is open—call her *through*—"

Jonah pulled.

She reached into the crown with everything she was—with the storm and the grief and the garden and the girl who had forgiven herself on a mountainside of vision—and she took hold of the binding the way you take hold of a knot, and she pulled.

The crown cracked.

Gold split along a seam no eye had ever seen, and through the seam poured light—not white, not holy, but hungry, enormous, a flood of four hundred years of pent-up listening and longing and rage, and it came out of the king like a river out of a broken dam, and it did not go up into the storm.

It turned toward Jonah.

She had time to feel the truth of it—*of course, of course, a vessel, this was always the price*—and then the light hit her, and the throne room, the court, the world, all of it went away.

* * *

There was a garden.

Not hers. Older. Vaster. Rows of stars planted in black soil, tended by hands like weather-fronts. A woman made of rain stood among them, and she was so beautiful and so ruined that Jonah's heart broke before the vision fully formed—the goddess Veyra as she had been before the crown, before the hunger, before four hundred years of eating worship through a keyhole. And then the image shivered, and the beauty curdled, and the woman turned, and her face was the crown-spirit's face, and her eyes were empty granaries.

*Little weather,* the god said, and now there was no throne-room power in the voice, only exhaustion, only want. *You opened the door. Now I am in the garden. Now you are in the storm. Let us see which of us drowns.*

The light closed over Jonah like water.

Somewhere impossibly far away, she heard Sef screaming her name, and Bram's liturgy holding, holding, holding—and the last thing she felt before the dark took her was the king's body sagging free at last, breathing on its own, human, alive.

And the crown-spirit settled into her bones like a tenant coming home.
