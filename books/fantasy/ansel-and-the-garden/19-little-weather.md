## 19. Little Weather

The ritual broke in the space between one heartbeat and the next.

Jonah felt it happen the way a gardener feels a frost come early—no warning, only a sudden wrongness in the air, a stillness where there should have been breath. One moment Bram's liturgy was rising through the throne room like smoke, old words in an older tongue, and Sef stood with her hand flat against King Aldric's chest, anchoring him to his own body. The next moment the crown-spirit simply *let go*.

Aldric sagged. Sef caught him, staggering under his weight, her eyes flying wide.

And Jonah understood, with the cold clarity of a woman who has spent ten years learning to read weather, that they had not won anything. The god had not been driven out. It had *moved*.

"Little weather," said the hall, every mouth at once.

Two hundred courtiers spoke together. Two hundred throats, two hundred tongues, one voice—a woman's voice, vast and patient and starving, pouring out of them like water finding every crack in a wall. The sound came from the gallery, from the guards at the doors, from the grey-faced lords on their benches, from a chambermaid near the pillar whose eyes had gone white as hailstone. Jonah's skin prickled. Every hair on her arms lifted toward the ceiling as if the whole room had become the underside of a stormcloud.

*There you are,* the voice said, and it was inside her skull now, warm as sun on stone. *I have been so careful with you. So patient. Did you think I sent for Aldric's little courier by accident? Did you think the valley could keep you forever?*

"You—" Jonah's voice cracked. She was still standing at the center of the sigil Bram had chalked into the ancient floor, her palms bleeding from the ritual cuts, the unbinding half-spoken and dying in the air around her like cut flowers. "You knew about me."

*I have known your name since before you were born.* The possessed courtiers did not move. They only watched, all of those white eyes, and the voice moved through them like wind through wheat. *I knew your grandmother, who called rain for the barley harvests. I knew your mother, who prayed to me once, only once, when you were fevered at three years old—and I heard her, little weather. I heard her and I could not answer. Do you know what that is like? To hear a prayer and have no hands, no rain, nothing to give?*

"I know exactly what it's like," Jonah whispered.

*Yes,* the god said softly. *I believe you do.*

Bram was shouting something—her name, or Veyra's, or both—but his voice came to her from very far away, wrapped in wool. The white-eyed courtiers began to walk forward, unhurried, closing the circle around the sigil without ever stepping inside it. And the pressure on Jonah's mind grew gentle, then insistent, then absolute, like a hand cupping the back of her skull.

*Look,* the god said. *Let me show you what I am offering.*

The throne room went away.

* * *

She was fourteen again, and it was raining.

Not the storm. Not yet. Just rain, the ordinary kind, drumming on the kitchen roof of the house she had not let herself remember in ten years because remembering was a door and doors opened from both sides. Her mother stood at the stove. Her father sat mending a harness by the window, complaining cheerfully about the damp getting into the leather. Her brother Tam was under the table tormenting the cat, and her sister Wren was doing sums by candlelight, tongue between her teeth, because Wren was eleven and solemn and certain that arithmetic would save her from farm life.

Jonah stood in the doorway with wet boots and her heart full of a day's small happinesses, and she thought: *this is the last ordinary hour of my life*, and the knowledge arrived with the memory itself, the way frost arrives with the morning, already too late.

*This is what I can give you,* the crown-spirit murmured, everywhere and nowhere, its voice coming out of the rain now, out of the roof, out of the cat's low growl. *Not a trick. Not a puppet-show. I am a god, little weather. When I take a vessel willing enough, time is clay in my hands.*

"It's not real," Jonah said. Her voice was fourteen years old. That was the worst part—that the vision reached into her chest and used her child's voice to speak her adult refusal.

*It is more real than the throne room. It is more real than anything you will touch again. Stay.*

Outside, the light was going strange. Greenish. The particular green she had learned to dread and had never told anyone about, the color the sky turned when the pressure dropped and the air went electric and something enormous leaned over the horizon looking for a place to set down its weight. Her father glanced up at the window and frowned. Her mother said, "Tam, love, fetch the lantern in off the porch before it blows—"

"No," Jonah said. "No, don't send him out, don't—"

But this was the past, and the past did not bend, and Tam went out laughing into the rising wind, and Jonah followed him because she always followed him, because he was sixteen and golden and hers, and the sky above the ridge was peeling open like a wound.

*I was there,* the god whispered. *Did you know that? I was there that night. Your grandmother's blood called storms the way mine does, and yours called louder than any bloodline in four generations, and when the lightning came down I felt it pass through you like a bell being rung. You were never cursed, little weather. You were never broken. You were a door, and no one taught you how to close yourself.*

The storm came down the mountain.

Jonah had spent ten years refusing to see it whole. In her memory it lived in pieces—the sound first, a tearing like the sky ripping canvas; then the light, white and total; then the smell of ozone and splintered pine; and last, worst, the silence afterward, which had lasted the rest of her life. Now the god held all the pieces together and made her look at the picture entire: the bolt striking the great oak, the oak coming down across the house, the fire following, her father's shout cut short, her mother running into the rain toward the burning door and never reaching it, Tam dragging Wren from under the collapsing beam with his shoulder already smoking—

—and fourteen-year-old Jonah standing in the yard with her arms raised and her hands full of heaven, screaming *stop, stop, stop* at a sky that had finally, catastrophically, listened.

"I didn't mean it," Jonah said. She was on her knees in the mud of a memory, and the rain was warm, and somewhere her family was dying, and it was happening now, it was always happening now. "I was angry at my father. I wanted the storm to scare him. I didn't know I was—I didn't know what I was asking for—"

*I know.*

"They told me I killed them. The council. Everyone. Ten years I've carried it—"

*I know what they told you.* The voice gentled, and gentleness from a thing that vast was more terrifying than rage. *Now hear me tell you the truth, the only creature left who saw it happen from the inside: you were a child holding a sword you were never shown how to carry, and the storm was never yours. It passed through you. It chose you as its road because you were open, because you were strong, because you were *listening* the way your whole line has listened for nine generations. If you had not been standing in that yard, it would have found another way down, and it would have struck the house anyway. Storms are not punishments, little weather. Storms are only storms.*

"But I called it." The words tore out of her. "I felt myself call it. I wanted—"

*You wanted the sky to notice you. Every child wants that. Some children are heard.* A pause, long as a season. *I heard you that night. From inside the crown, from behind the binding, starved and mad and unable to move—I heard you, and I loved you, and I could do nothing, and I have regretted it for ten years the way you have. We are the same, you and I. Two creatures who heard prayers they could not answer.*

The rain kept falling. The house kept burning. And Jonah knelt in it, and something in her chest that had been clenched for a decade—fist-tight around the guilt like a fist around a knife, because letting go meant admitting the cutting had been self-inflicted all along—began, slowly, to open.

She looked up. Through the rain and the firelight she saw herself: a girl of fourteen, soaked and hollow-eyed, arms still raised at a sky that had already gone quiet. The girl looked back at her with the expression Jonah had seen in every reflective surface for ten years. The expression she had crossed a kingdom to escape and had carried anyway, folded small behind her ribs.

"I'm sorry," Jonah said—to the girl, to the burning house, to the dead, to all of it at once. "I was a child. I didn't know. I was a child, and I didn't know, and I'm sorry, and—" her voice broke and rebuilt itself, "—and it wasn't my fault."

The girl in the rain lowered her arms. Smiled—small, disbelieving, like the sun after a long winter—and dissolved into the downpour, and the downpour into dark, and the dark into the god's voice, which was weeping now, or doing whatever a god does instead.

*Yes,* the crown-spirit breathed. *Yes. That is why it must be you. Do you see it now? Take my hand. Take the crown. You were born to hold the storm, and I was born to be answered, and together we would never be hungry again. No more valley. No more smallness. No more kneeling in gardens pretending the sky isn't singing your name. Be what you are, little weather. Be the storm entire.*

* * *

Jonah opened her eyes in the throne room, and the offer was still hanging in the air, glittering, enormous, patient.

The courtiers had stopped moving. They stood in their ring, white-eyed, waiting. Above them, unseen but felt, the crown on Aldric's head burned like a second sun—and Aldric himself lay crumpled in Sef's arms, and Sef was looking at Jonah with an expression she had never seen on the girl's face before. Fear. Not for herself. For her.

"Don't," Sef said. Just that. Just *don't*, in the voice of a twelve-year-old who had lost everything once already and had learned to recognize the shape of losing.

Bram was on his knees at the edge of the sigil, blood on his lip, chanting Veyra's name over and over like a man throwing stones at a tide. He met her eyes. He shook his head, slow and terrible, and mouthed: *it lies.*

*Does it?* the god asked, amused, intimate, everywhere. *Ask him what his order bound me for. Ask him what I was before they caged me. I was hunger, yes—but hunger is only love with nowhere to go. I offered him the same bargain once, little weather. His god refused. His god let herself be buried rather than take a vessel, and her priests called it holiness, and I call it abandonment, and here we all are, ten centuries later, still hungry.*

Jonah stood in the center of the sigil with her bleeding hands at her sides, and she felt the truth of it settle over her like snowfall: the power was still there. All of it. The storm that had killed her family had never left her; it had only been waiting, leashed by exile and guilt, coiled under her skin like scar tissue over a living thing. She could feel it now, answering the god's nearness, rising in her like water in a dry well. One word. One surrender. She could have the sky. She could have hands big enough to hold everything she'd ever lost.

And she thought of the garden.

Ten years of small, deliberate, chosen things: seedlings thinned so the survivors could grow strong. Grafts bound gently until the wounds healed into strength. Water measured, sunlight shared, death pruned away so life could continue—not because she controlled the growing, but because she had learned, season upon season, that the gardener's power was not in forcing anything. It was in tending. In answering what was needed. In staying.

"You're right," Jonah said aloud, and the whole hall flinched at the sound of a single human voice among all that borrowed divinity. "About all of it. About my grandmother. About the storm. About the girl in the rain." She turned slowly, meeting the white eyes one by one. "You heard my family pray and couldn't answer. You've heard ten centuries of prayers and couldn't answer. You're not offering me godhood." Her voice steadied, and something in it made even the possessed courtiers lean back. "You're begging."

Silence. Real silence, the god's attention narrowing to a point, a needle of focus.

"And I'm going to answer," Jonah said. "But not the way you want. Not by taking. By giving."

She stepped out of the sigil.

Bram shouted. Sef screamed her name. The god's voice rose in something that was almost alarm—*what are you doing, little weather, the binding, the ritual, you'll lose the window*—and Jonah walked through the ring of frozen courtiers toward the dais, toward the fallen king, toward the crown that burned above him like a captive star, and she raised her ruined hands, and she let the leash slip.

The storm came up through her like dawn.

Not the green-lit monster of her fourteenth year. Something vaster and quieter, something that had been ripening in a dead valley for ten years, fed on patience and grief and the slow work of growing things. Lightning stood in her veins. Rain filled her lungs. She felt herself become, in that instant, the thing the council had feared and the god had hungered for and the world had waited for without knowing it: a storm-caller with nothing left to prove and everything left to give.

"Veyra," she said, using the god's true name for the first time, Bram's gasp behind her the only sound in the hall. "Old one. Starving one. Sister-in-the-storm. Hear me—you have been praying to *me* for ten years, and I am answering now."

She reached for the crown.

The god rose to meet her—and Jonah did not fight her, did not bind her, did not cage her as the priests of Veyra's order had done. She opened. She opened the way a garden opens to rain, the way a valley opens to morning, and took the starving god into the storm of herself, into the wide and weathered country of a woman who had finally forgiven the child she'd been—and the crown-spirit fell into her like rain falling into earth at last, after a thousand years of drought.

The crown cracked. The light went out of it like a lamp at daybreak.

And far away, beneath everything, Jonah felt the edge of something else—the god's own memory, vast and grieving, turning toward her like a door swinging wide—and understood that taking Veyra in was not an ending at all.

It was a beginning, and it was going to cost her everything she had.
