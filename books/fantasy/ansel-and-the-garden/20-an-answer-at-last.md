## 20. An Answer at Last

The throne room was full of prayers.

Jonah felt them the way she had once felt weather building over the western hills—pressure, weight, a gathering dark. Hundreds of courtiers knelt on the marble, pressed there by guards and terror and something older than both, and their devotion rose off them like heat from summer stone. The crown-spirit drank it in through the crown on Aldric's brow, and the air above the dais shimmered with the feeding, a distortion like the air above a fire.

*Little weather,* said the voice that wore the king's mouth, resonating not through Jonah's ears but through her bones, her blood, the old scar tissue of her power. *You brought your little knife and your little liturgy. Did you think I could be unbound by words?*

Bram's voice lifted behind her—the liturgy of Veyra, ancient and cracked and steady as a drumbeat. Sef stood at Aldric's side with both hands on the king's shoulder, small and fierce as a wren defending a hawk. And Jonah walked forward across the marble, one step, another, into the light that poured off the crown like molten gold poured thin.

She had expected rage. What she felt instead, pressing against her skin, was hunger so vast it had curved back on itself and become something like grief.

*They prayed to me for three hundred years,* the god said, and now the voice was different—still vast, but fraying at the edges, like a scream gone hoarse. *I heard every one. Every harvest prayer. Every dying breath. Every child asking why. Do you know what it is, little weather, to hear ten thousand voices calling you by name and to be unable to answer? To be caged inside your own hunger while they beg you to feed them?*

"I know," Jonah said, and her voice broke on it. "I know what it is."

And she did. Ten years of Hollowmere silence. Ten years of feeling prayers rise off the world beyond the ridge—she had never admitted it even to herself until this moment, but she had felt them, faint as distant thunder, all the people asking the sky for rain and mercy, and she had been unable to answer any of them.

*Then you understand,* the god whispered, sweetening. *Kneel. Let me wear you as I wear him. You were made for storm; he was only made for crowns. I will be gentle with you. I will let you feel everything. You will never again have to listen and say nothing.*

Jonah stopped three steps from the dais. Up close, she could see what the crown-spirit had hidden: Aldric's face beneath the golden light, grey as ash, his lips moving silently—*run, run, girl, run*—and his hands clenched against the arms of the throne as if holding himself to his own body by force.

"No," Jonah said.

*No?*

"Not kneeling." She raised her head. Rain was beginning somewhere far above the palace roof; she could feel it, the whole grey weight of the sky leaning down toward her like an old dog hearing its name. "I'm not here to kneel or to fight. Bram told me what you are. Not a demon. A god who was starved until madness looked like hunger. My family—" Her throat closed. She forced the words through. "My family died because I called a storm I didn't understand, out of fear, out of anger, and the sky answered me before I knew what I was asking. No one ever taught me that power asks a price. So I swore never to call again, and I thought that oath was penance. It wasn't. It was just another kind of cage. I built myself a Hollowmere out of guilt and called it safety."

*Pretty words,* the god snarled, and the light flared, and somewhere in the hall a pillar cracked. *Words do not feed.*

"No," Jonah agreed softly. "But storms don't have to kill. That's the thing nobody told either of us."

She opened her hands.

For ten years the storm had lived under her skin like scar tissue, dormant, leashed, a thing she guarded against the way one guards an old wound. Now she reached into herself—not with force, not with the desperate grasping of a fourteen-year-old girl screaming at the sky—and she *unlocked* it. Gently. Deliberately. The way you open a garden gate for a guest you've invited.

Lightning did not leap from her fingers. Instead the pressure simply left her, rising up through the vaulted ceiling, and the storm that had been circling the city all evening came down to meet her like water finding a channel.

The worshippers screamed. The guards drew steel. Bram's liturgy faltered for half a heartbeat and then resumed, louder, and Sef held onto Aldric's shoulder and did not look away.

"What is she doing?" someone shouted. "What is she—"

Jonah barely heard. She was standing in the center of a column of quiet rain that fell upward, silver in the torchlight, and she was speaking to the god inside the crown without the crown's permission.

"You've been bound for three hundred years," she said. "Bound with chains made of ritual, fed on stolen worship, caged so tightly that every prayer you heard became torture. Your priests bound you because they feared your hunger. And they were right to fear it—you *are* hungry, hungry enough to eat a king, hungry enough to eat me. But listen to me." She stepped closer to the dais, close enough to see Aldric's eyes rolling beneath the golden light, close enough that the rain soaked them both. "There is one kind of offering no binding can hold. Not stolen worship. Not frightened prayer. A gift. Freely given. Because bindings can cage what is taken, but they cannot cage what is *surrendered*. There's nothing left to chain."

*I don't understand,* the god said, and for the first time the vastness in her voice sounded young. Frightened. Like something that had been alone in the dark so long it had forgotten what light was for.

"Nobody taught you," Jonah said gently, "that power asks a price. Same as me. So here is mine, freely given: my storm. All of it. Everything I am, everything I was born to be, the whole inheritance I've spent ten years burying in a dead valley. Take it. Not as food. As an answer."

*An answer.*

"To three hundred years of prayers," Jonah said. "Yes. Someone is listening. I'm listening. And I'm telling you that you can stop being hungry now."

She reached up and took the crown in both hands.

It burned. Of course it burned—it was a cage full of lightning, and she was putting her hands on the bars. But the burn was clean, the burn was honest, the way cold water burns, and she held on anyway, and she pulled.

The crown-spirit rose out of Aldric like smoke drawn up a chimney.

For one endless moment Jonah was nowhere at all. She was in the space between heartbeats, in the hollow place where a god had been kept, and around her stretched three hundred years of unanswered prayer stacked up like sediment—*bless the harvest, save my daughter, why have you forsaken us, please, please, please*—and at the bottom of it all, curled small as a child, the starving thing that had once been Veyra, goddess of storms and green things growing, weeping without eyes.

*Who answers?* the small voice asked. *Nobody answers. Nobody ever—*

"I do," Jonah said, inside the dark where no one else could reach. "Here. This is for you. It was always meant for the sky anyway."

And she gave it.

Every suppressed heartbeat of ten years. Every night she had lain awake in Hollowmere listening to distant thunder and ached. Every seed she had coaxed from dead ground with nothing but patience and water, every graft she had cut and bound and healed, every green thing she had ever loved and could not explain why she loved. She gathered all of it into a single act of surrender and she offered it up, and the storm she had carried since she was fourteen came home to the sky that had made it.

The starving god opened her mouth—

—and instead of devouring, she *drank.* And drinking, remembered what rain was for.

The crown shattered.

Not with thunder—with a sound like ice releasing from a pond in spring, a long crystalline sigh, and then it was falling from Jonah's hands in pieces, and the pieces were falling as rain, and the rain was falling everywhere, warm and silver, coming down through the shattered skylight of the throne room in sheets and shafts and curtains, washing the incense smoke from the air, running in bright rivers between the flagstones, and in the rain, for just a moment, Jonah saw her: a woman made of weather, tall as the storm itself, her hair the grey of clouds before dawn, standing in the ruined light with her face turned up to the sky.

Veyra looked down at Jonah. And smiled.

*Little weather,* she said—but the name had changed shape in her mouth; it was not a predator's endearment now but something older, something a grandmother might have said over a cradle. *Thank you.*

And then she came apart into rain. Not tragically, not violently—simply, the way a cloud comes apart when the wind is done with it, becoming part of the larger water cycle of the world, going wherever rain goes. Jonah felt her pass through the room and out of it, felt her spread over the whole city at once, felt her sink into the dry earth of palace gardens and cracked plazas and the hills beyond the walls, felt ten thousand people below look up out of their windows and doors and hold out their hands.

In the throne room, King Aldric slid from his throne into the rain.

Sef caught him before he hit the marble—twelve years old and half his size, she caught him, went down under him and held him, shouting his name, *Aldric, Aldric, stay, you stay*, and Jonah dropped to her knees beside them in the flooding light and pressed two fingers to the king's throat.

Nothing.

The rain fell. Bram's liturgy had stopped; he was standing at the edge of the dais with his face wet and his hands empty, looking at the place where his god had been, and Jonah heard him whisper, in the tone of a man laying down a burden he had carried for twenty years, "Oh. Oh, you were so tired."

*Aldric,* Sef was saying, her forehead pressed to his chest, her voice cracking wide open, all the ferocious self-reliance of a lifetime stripped away at once. *Please. Please. I stole a horse for you, you can't—you can't die too, everyone dies, please—*

And beneath her ear, faint as a far-off bell, a heartbeat.

Jonah felt it strengthen under her fingers—one beat, two, stumbling like a foal, then steadying. Aldric's chest rose. His eyes opened, and they were only eyes, human and brown and exhausted, and he looked up at the rain falling on his face, and then at the girl pinning him to the floor, and said, in a voice sanded down to almost nothing:

"Sef. You're supposed to be in bed."

Sef made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and hit him weakly on the shoulder, and then wrapped both arms around him and stayed there, shaking, while the king of the realm lay in six inches of rainwater on his own throne-room floor and awkwardly patted her back with a hand that belonged to no spirit but his own.

Jonas sat back on her heels. The storm was passing; she could feel it going out over the city, gentle now, spending itself on rooftops and gardens and the dry hills beyond the walls, and inside her there was—nothing. Not emptiness exactly. Emptiness was what Hollowmere had been. This was something else. A cleared field after harvest. Room.

"Jonah." Bram knelt beside her, rain streaming down his face, and he took her hand in both of his, priest's hands, gardener's hands. "What did you give her? What did it cost?"

"Everything I was keeping," she said. She started to laugh, and found she was crying instead, great ugly gulps of it, ten years of them. "That's the joke, Bram. It cost me nothing. It was never mine to keep. Storms aren't meant to be stored. They're meant to fall."

Around them the court was rising from its knees one by one, staring—at the broken crown, at the rain, at the king laughing weakly on the floor with a twelve-year-old clinging to him, at the bedraggled woman in soaked gardening clothes who had walked into a god's hunger and walked out the other side of it. Somewhere a soldier dropped his sword. Somewhere else, someone began, hesitantly, to sing—an old song, older than the council, older than the crown, a rain-song that Jonah realized she had known as a child without knowing what it was for.

Aldric got one elbow under himself and looked at her across Sef's head. His eyes were clear, and terribly tired, and full of something that might have been gratitude or might have been apology or might have been both.

"Ravensworth," he rasped. "The courier's letter. *Anyone still listening.* I chose you because I thought you'd want vengeance on the crown that exiled you. I was wrong about you."

"Yes," Jonah said, wiping her face with a wet sleeve, and found she was smiling. "Thank the gods."

Outside, over the whole sprawling wounded city, the rain kept falling—soft, unhurried, answering everything it touched—and in the doorway of the throne room, unnoticed by anyone, an old woman in servant's grey stood watching with her head tilted, as though listening to something very far away, and smiling to herself as if she had just heard the punchline of a very long story. Then she turned and walked into the rain, and was gone before anyone thought to ask her name.
