## 21. After the Rain

The rain had not stopped in three days, and no one wanted it to.

Aldric stood at the window of his own bedchamber—a room he had not truly inhabited for years, though his body had slept in its bed every night—and watched the water sheet down the glass. It fell without malice. That was the miracle of it, the thing he could not stop marveling at: water that was only water, falling on a city that was only a city, on a king who was only a man.

His head ached. It would ache for the rest of his life, the physicians said, the way an amputated limb aches—the crown had been pried from him, but the shape of it remained, a hollow where something vast had lived. He caught himself sometimes reaching for thoughts that were not his, prayers that rose unbidden to a presence that was no longer there, and each time he felt the absence like a tooth torn out. He had been possessed for eleven years, and there were mornings when being only himself felt like a kind of grief.

But he was himself. When he spoke, the words came from him. When he signed a decree, the hand was his own—shakier than it had been at twenty-five, but his.

The council chamber waited below. Half its members had been removed already; three had wept when the possession left them, one had tried to flee the city and been brought back soaked and sobbing, and two—Lord Casserly and Dame Ilvane—had shown no signs of the spirit's touch at all, which Aldric found more frightening than the alternative. What did it say about a man, that even a starving god had found nothing in him worth wearing?

He dressed himself. He had not dressed himself in eleven years. His fingers fumbled the clasps of his doublet, and he laughed—a small, cracked sound—at the absurdity of a king who had faced down a god and could not manage a row of buttons. In the end he left the last clasp undone. Let them see it. Let them see a man who was imperfect and present, who occupied his own body and his own office, rather than the polished vessel they had bowed to for a decade.

When he entered the council chamber, the remaining members rose. He waved them back into their seats before they could finish bowing.

"Sit," he said. "We are done with ceremony. Ceremony is how it hid from us." He took his chair at the head of the table, and the wood creaked under his hands, ordinary wood, ordinary hands. "For eleven years, edicts were written in my name by something that wore me like a coat. Some of you knew. Some of you suspected. Some of you—" his gaze found Casserly, held it, "—helped. I am not going to have you all arrested. I don't have the strength for purges, and the realm doesn't have the stomach. But I am dissolving this council."

A murmur went around the table.

"You will reapply," Aldric continued. "Each of you, if you wish, before a new panel that includes voices this room has never heard: a delegate of the shrine-keepers, someone from the river districts that flooded while we prayed for sunshine, and—" he paused, and allowed himself the ghost of a smile, "—a ward of the crown, who has informed me she will attend whether I invite her or not."

Dame Ilvane's mouth thinned. "Your Majesty, the girl is twelve. She cannot—"

"The girl," Aldric said, "walked out of my palace through doors none of you can find, carried proof of my captivity across half a kingdom, and stood in front of a god's voice without flinching. The girl has done more for this realm this season than this council has in eleven years. She will be heard." He rose. "We reconvene in a month. Until then, go home. Look at your families. Remember what your faces look like when they're honest."

He walked out before anyone could answer, and in the corridor he stopped, pressed his palm flat against the cold stone, and breathed until the shaking stopped. A page hovered nearby, uncertain. Aldric straightened.

"Send word to the conservatory," he said. "Tell Lady Sef that the king requests her presence at supper. Tell her—" he hesitated, then smiled properly this time, tired and true, "—tell her her father requests it."

* * *

Bram Ostell had never built anything in his life except sermons, and sermons, he had learned too late, were just prayers with better posture. So when the survivors of the palace court began drifting toward him in the weeks after the rain—not because he asked, but because he had been standing in the throne room when Veyra dissolved into weather, and people trust witnesses—he did the only thing he knew how to do badly: he listened.

They came to the ruined chapel first. Of course they did; it was the chapel where the storm-caller had nearly unmade the walls, where the altar still bore a hairline crack shaped like a lightning fern, and the city had decided the place was holy now, which Bram found darkly funny given that he'd spent thirty years believing holiness required incense and correct vestments and a god who answered on schedule.

They came with offerings. Bread, mostly. Flowers gone soft in the rain. One woman brought a chicken, alive and indignant, and Bram had no idea what to do with it, so it lived behind the chapel and hated him.

"I can't give you what the old faith gave," he told them, the first evening, standing among maybe forty wet and hopeful strangers. "I can't tell you Veyra hears you. I spent my life praying to her and she never once answered me—not because she was cruel, but because she was starving, and a starving thing cannot feed its children." He looked down at his hands, the knuckles scarred from decades of pressing them together in supplication. "My order's sin wasn't false worship. It was abandonment. We bound her, generations ago, because her hunger frightened us, and then we forgot her, and forgetting is the cruelest prayer there is. It's a prayer that says: *we have taken what we needed and you may starve now.*"

Silence, except the rain on the broken roof.

"So here is what I know how to offer," Bram said. "Not asking. Listening. You come here, and you sit, and you tell the truth about your hunger—out loud, or in your heart, it makes no difference—and then you listen for what answers. Sometimes nothing answers. Sometimes the rain answers. Sometimes another person answers, and that is also holy, though we rarely admit it." He paused. "And sometimes—if the stories coming out of the valley are true—someone answers who has been waiting a very long time to be spoken to kindly."

An old man near the front raised a trembling hand. "Father Ostell. Will she come back? The goddess?"

Bram thought about the rain falling over the whole city, gentle and unhurried, three days without stopping, as if something were making up for centuries of drought all at once.

"She's in everything that falls," he said. "That's not doctrine. That's just what I saw with my own eyes." And then, because honesty was the whole discipline now or it was nothing: "I don't know if that counts as coming back. But I know she isn't hungry anymore. Whatever else happens—that much, we fixed."

Afterward, walking home through streets that smelled of wet stone and woodsmoke, Bram passed the palace and saw, high in a tower window, a small figure sitting with her legs dangling over the sill, watching the rain come down. He lifted a hand. After a moment, the figure lifted one back.

He found, to his surprise, that he was humming. An old hymn, one he'd sung ten thousand times without meaning. It sounded different now—slower, less like a demand and more like a lullaby.

* * *

Sef had stolen exactly one horse in her life, and she intended to make it count.

She sat in the royal conservatory—which was hers now, by right of conquest and also because she'd simply refused to leave—in the green light under the great glass roof, repotting cuttings. Jonah had shown her how before leaving: how to score the stem, how to pack the soil firm but not crushing, how to talk to them or not talk to them depending on your temperament. Sef talked to them. She had a lot to say and nowhere else safe to put it.

The conservatory had been sealed for years—since before her time as ward—but the plants inside had kept living anyway, leggy and desperate, growing toward the glass like prisoners toward a window. She and Jonah had spent four evenings cutting them back, and now the place smelled of wet earth and bruised leaves, and Sef had dirt under every fingernail and considered it the finest jewelry she owned.

A servant announced the king. Sef didn't stand. She'd decided early that she was done standing for titles, and Aldric, to his credit, had decided he liked that about her.

"You sent for me," she said, not looking up from the pot.

"I invited you to supper."

"Same thing, in a palace." She tamped the soil down. "I'm busy."

"With what?"

"Grafting." She glanced up, defiant, daring him to mock it. "Jonah showed me. You take a piece of one tree and put it in another tree and they grow together. They don't fight about it. They just—" she searched for the word, "—agree to be both."

Aldric crouched beside her bench, which no king should do and which he did anyway, his joints cracking audibly. He looked older than his years now, thinner, with white coming in at his temples that hadn't been there two months ago. But his eyes were his own, and when he looked at her, he actually saw her.

"Sef," he said. "The council wants to return you to the wards' house. Formal tutelage, a stipend, a match arranged when you're of age. It's generous. It's what the records say should happen."

Her hands went still in the soil.

"And I'm telling you," he continued, "that I looked at those records, and I looked at you, and I've decided the records are wrong. I was wrong for eleven years about nearly everything. I'd like to start correcting the ledger." He took a breath, and Sef realized with a jolt that the King of the Realm was nervous. "You told me once, in the conservatory, that family is whoever shows up. I'd like to show up. Formally. If you'll have me. Not as your sovereign. As—" his voice caught, and he pushed through it, "—as your father. If you want that. If you don't, the wards' house is yours, and I'll bother you never again, and I'll deserve it."

The conservatory was very quiet. Somewhere above, rain ticked against the glass, patient as ever.

Sef looked at him for a long moment. Then she wiped her hands deliberately on her apron—leaving two brown prints, which she noted with satisfaction—and stood, and stuck out her dirty hand the way stable boys shook on deals.

"One condition," she said. "Nobody calls me 'the king's ward' anymore. Wards get sent away when they're inconvenient. I've read enough history."

"What should they call you?"

"Your daughter." Her chin came up, ferocious, terrified, twelve years old and absolutely certain. "Daughters stay."

Aldric took her hand and shook it, solemn as any treaty, and then—breaking every rule of royal decorum that remained to him—pulled her into a hug that smelled of ink and rain and, faintly, of the crown's long absence. Sef let him. She even, after a moment, hugged back.

"There's a second condition," she said into his shoulder.

"Name it."

"When Jonah comes back from Hollowmere—you write to her. Every week. Whether she writes back or not." She pulled away, eyes bright and fierce. "People need to know somebody's thinking of them. I learned that the hard way."

* * *

The supper, when it finally happened, was not a state occasion. It was five people around a scarred oak table in a small dining room, because Bram had refused to eat anywhere with a dais, and Sef had threatened to steal the silverware otherwise.

Jonah had come back over the ridge two days ago—come back with seeds and letters and a satchel of Hollowmere honey, come back the way she now traveled, with magic moving gently around her like weather around a mountain, welcomed instead of feared. She sat across from Sef and watched the girl demolish a plate of roast fowl with the appetite of someone who had learned, young, that food could disappear.

Aldric poured wine for the adults and cider for Sef, and raised his cup. "To the dead," he said quietly. "The courier whose name I never learned. The priests who bound Veyra meaning well. Everyone the crown ate while we called it governance." A pause. "And to the living, who showed up."

They drank. Outside, the rain had softened to a drizzle, and through the window the city glimmered, lamps coming on street by street, ordinary lights for ordinary evenings.

"Grief and gratitude," Bram said, almost to himself. "That's the whole liturgy, isn't it. My order spent centuries trying to separate them. Put gratitude in the temple, grief in the ground. But they belong at the same table. They always did."

"They do," Jonah said. She was turning her cup slowly, watching the wine move. "I spent ten years thinking I had to choose. Grief forever, or forgiveness, never both." She looked up, and her eyes were clear in a way they hadn't been in the valley, in a way they perhaps hadn't been since she was fourteen. "Turns out you set them both a place and let them argue it out."

Sef reached over and stole a honeyed roll off Jonah's plate, entirely without shame. "Is that why you're staying? Really staying? Not just visiting?"

Jonah considered the question seriously, because Sef's questions deserved serious answers. "I'm keeping a garden in both places now," she said. "Seeds travel. Letters travel. And magic—" she smiled, small and wondering, "—magic follows me gently these days. Like it's learned some manners." She broke the roll in half and handed part back to Sef. "Home isn't walls, little thief. I finally worked that out. Home is whoever steals your bread at supper."

"That's the nicest thing anyone's ever said to me," Sef said, mouth full.

Aldric laughed—a real laugh, rusty and surprised at itself—and Bram raised his cup again, and the rain went on falling softly over the city, over the chapel with its cracked altar and its indignant chicken, over the conservatory full of grafted things learning to be both, over the ridge far to the north where a valley that had been silent for ten years was, letter by letter, seed by seed, beginning to speak.

Later, when the plates were cleared and the candles burned low, Sef climbed to her window seat with the week's letters—three of them, one from Bram, one from a shrine-keeper, and one from a girl in Hollowmere she'd never met, sent along with Jonah, addressed simply to *the other kid who ran*. She opened that one first.

Outside, far off over the hills, thunder rolled—distant, unhurried, almost friendly. Sef grinned at it, and settled in to read.
