## 22. Seeds Carried Over the Ridge

The road to Hollowmere had not changed, but Jonah had.

She felt the difference in her own body first—in the way she no longer flinched when wind moved through the high grasses along the ridge path, no longer counted the seconds between lightning's birth and its voice. The storm that had broken over the capital three weeks past still lived in her bones, a low sweet ache like the memory of music. She had called it. She had *held* it. And it had not taken anything from her that she could not spare.

The ridge rose ahead of her now, grey and patient against an autumn sky, wearing the same crown of pines it had worn for ten years. From this side—the world's side—it looked exactly as it always had: a wall. A sentence written in stone. *Here ends the map of what is allowed.*

Jonah stopped at the base of the path and let her pack slide from her shoulder. It was heavier than it needed to be. She had insisted on carrying it herself, though Aldric's men had offered mules and Bram had offered his back and Sef had offered, with great dignity, to hire her a horse with money she absolutely did not have.

Inside the pack: seeds. Dozens of paper envelopes, each labeled in Bram's careful priest's hand—*marrowvine, dusk-rose, king's pepper, weeping silverleaf.* Cuttings wrapped in damp moss. A jar of conservatory soil that Sef had scooped up without asking permission from anyone, because Sef did not believe in asking permission from anyone. Letters, too, bundled in oilcloth: from the king to the Hollowmere council, under his own hand at last, unmistakably his own; from Bram to whatever faithful might be hiding in the valley's silence; and one letter, sealed with plain wax, addressed simply to *the children who whisper about the Ravensworth curse*, which Jonah had written four times before she got the words right.

She stood there a long moment, breathing.

Ten years ago she had climbed this ridge in the other direction, fourteen years old and hollowed out, her hands still smelling of ozone and her family's blood dried somewhere she couldn't see. The councilmen had walked her to the border themselves, all solemnity and fear, and the last thing they'd said was that the valley would hold her. That magic could not cross. That inside Hollowmere she would be safe from herself.

They had never once asked if she would be safe from her grief. Perhaps they hadn't known those were different things.

"Right," Jonah said aloud, to no one, which was how she said most important things. "Home."

She began to climb.

* * *

The crossing itself was nothing. That was the strange part. She had braced for it—for some resistance, some tearing, the valley's old silence closing over her like water. Instead she stepped across the line where the pines thickened and the air changed temperature, and she simply... continued. All of her. Every part.

It was only halfway down the near slope that she noticed the frost.

It laced the grass on either side of the path in delicate ferns, spreading outward from her footfalls, melting behind her in the mild afternoon. Not wild frost. Not the rime of altitude or season. It moved *with* her, blooming where she paused, and when she held out one hand and watched her breath fog though the day was warm, she understood.

Magic was following her down into the valley.

Not surging. Not breaking through the ridge like a flood. Just—seeping. Gentle as groundwater, finding the cracks she made simply by walking. Ten years of dead air, and it was coming back to life around her boots like moss after rain.

The old Jonah would have stopped. Would have stood frozen on the path, heart hammering, waiting for the council's wardens, for chains, for the cold arithmetic of exile renewed. The old Jonah had spent a decade training herself not to reach for the pressure behind her sternum, pretending it was scar tissue, pretending numbness was peace.

Jonah crouched down instead and touched the frost.

It melted against her fingertips, cool and clean, and something in the ground beneath it seemed to sigh—a feeling more than a sound, the valley's long-held breath releasing by degrees. The herbs she'd left wild along the path edges had gone leggy and sparse in the magicless years. But here, in the wake of her passing, a sprig of dusk-rose that should have been months from bloom had put out a single bud, tight and violet-dark, like a secret deciding to be told.

"Well," Jonah said softly. "All right, then."

She let it come. That was all. She didn't call anything, didn't shape anything—she just stopped holding the door shut, and the world's quiet magic drifted through her like light through a window left open on purpose. Her storm-calling stayed coiled and sleeping under her skin, and she was glad of it; storms were for emergencies, and she was done living in emergencies. But the small things—the frost, the quickening green, the way her shadow seemed half a shade deeper than it used to be—those she let settle over her like a shawl.

By the time she reached the valley floor, the air behind her smelled faintly of rain that hadn't fallen yet.

* * *

Hollowmere village looked smaller than she remembered, which was unfair of it, since she was the one who had grown.

Same crooked lanes. Same smoke-stained thatch. Same well in the square with its worn lip where the children dared each other to lean too far. The same children, in fact—or their younger siblings—chasing a hoop between the houses, and when they saw her they stopped as one, the hoop rolling forgotten into a puddle.

She knew what they saw. A tall woman in a traveler's coat, weathered and sun-browned, with a pack full of paper envelopes and eyes that the capital had taught to look directly at things. And underneath that, in the place where village knowledge lived, older and truer than any of them could say why: *Ravensworth. The cursed one. The one whose garden grows wrong.*

Jonah kept walking. Past the baker's, where Maren Tull was setting out loaves and went very still, flour to her elbows, staring. Past the smithy, where old Hedwin dropped his tongs. Past the meeting hall, where someone had already begun to run toward the council house, because that was what Hollowmere did with news: it ran with it, breathless, before it even knew what the news was.

Her own cottage sat at the village's edge, where the gardens began. Ten years of her life were written in its fences and trellises, in the cold frames she'd built stone by stone, in the herb rows she'd kept alive through winters that wanted them dead. The gate hung slightly askew. She'd been meaning to fix it for ten years.

She fixed it first. Before unpacking, before anything—she found the hammer and the spare hinge nail in their places, exactly where she'd left them, and she straightened the gate until it swung true and silent. It seemed important. Some things you put right in order, and small things came first, or the big ones collapsed under their own weight.

Then she opened the door, and the smell of her own house—dried lavender, woodsmoke, earth—rose up and undid her completely.

She sat down on her own floor, in her own dust, and cried the way she hadn't cried at the unbinding, hadn't cried when Veyra dissolved into rain over ten thousand upturned faces, hadn't cried when Aldric gripped her forearm with a freed man's shaking hand and said *I don't know how to thank you* and she'd answered *don't, just live*. She cried for the girl who had crossed this threshold in the other direction with nothing, and for the ten years of quiet she'd mistaken for safety, and for the simple unbearable fact that the door had opened both ways the entire time, and no one—not the council, not the gods, not the valley itself—had ever actually locked it.

When she was done, she washed her face, put water on to boil, and started unpacking seeds.

* * *

They came to her over the following days, the way villagers do: sidling, circling, pretending to pass by.

Maren Tull arrived first, ostensibly to return a borrowed sieve from a decade ago, and stayed two hours asking questions she pretended weren't questions. Then Hedwin, then the widow Corrick with a basket of apples as tribute, then a delegation of three council members who stood in her garden looking deeply uncomfortable, like men summoned to a funeral they suspected was their own.

"We received the king's letter," said Councilor Dree, the eldest, who had been young and frightened when he helped walk a child into exile. His hands wouldn't stay still. "We are given to understand that the... restrictions regarding yourself have been lifted."

"You understand correctly."

"And that you intend to—" He glanced at the seed packets spread across her table, the cuttings in their moss, the strange blooms already opening weeks early in her cold frames. "To remain. And also to travel. Both?"

"Both," Jonah said. "The valley isn't a prison anymore, Councilor. It never really was. It was a wound we agreed to call a wall." She let that sit. "Magic is coming back here. Slowly, gently—I'm not forcing it, it's following me, and I've stopped pretending I can stop it. Your crops will grow better. Your wells will taste sweeter. And someday soon, prayers said in this valley will reach somewhere again." She met each of their eyes in turn. "I came back to tell you that myself, because you deserved to hear it from a person and not a letter. And because I need gardeners."

Dree blinked. "Gardeners?"

"The conservatory in the palace is being restored. There's a girl there—Sef—who runs it like a small kingdom, and she needs hands who know cold frames and grafting. And here—" Jonah spread her arms at the valley, at the thin bright thread of returning life in the air, "—here I want to plant what I brought. Things that haven't grown in Hollowmere in a hundred years. Things that will teach the soil to remember." She smiled, and watched the word *curse* quietly die somewhere behind Dree's eyes. "I'll need help. I pay in vegetables."

Word of the vegetables did more than the king's letter ever could.

* * *

On the first evening of the seventh day, she wrote letters by lamplight while frost ferned delicately up the outside of her windows—not the angry frost of her girlhood, but something ornamental, almost shy, the valley's new magic learning its manners.

*Dear Sef,* she wrote. *The marrowvine is in. If it takes half as well here as it does in your conservatory, we'll be drowning in it by midsummer and you'll have to invent recipes. Enclosed: dusk-rose seed for the south bed, and a pressed flower from the first bloom, which opened the morning I arrived, which I choose to interpret as a good omen rather than showing off. Bram says hello. He won't say more than hello because he is composing a longer letter about listening, which I suspect will take him the rest of his life, and rightly so. The children here have stopped whispering when I pass. Yesterday one of them asked me, very seriously, whether I could make it snow for her birthday. I told her I'd see what I could do. I think I meant it. Come at midwinter, if the king can spare you. Bring the horse you definitely stole fairly. —J.*

*Dear Bram,* she wrote. *The valley hums at night now. Not words—just the sense of an ear opening somewhere, very far off, very patient. I knelt by the old shrine at the crossroads and said nothing at all, and I swear something listened anyway. You were right, of course, about everything, and I hope the smugness keeps you warm. Plant the king's pepper where the morning sun hits. Tell Aldric his letter arrived and Dree read it aloud in the meeting hall and had to stop twice, and the second time the whole room waited for him, which I think is the first mercy this valley has shown him. —J.*

She sealed them, set them aside for the courier who would come with the week's post, and stepped outside into the blue dark.

The garden lay quiet around her, rows and beds ghost-pale under a rising moon. Here and there, faint as breath on glass, frost bloomed and faded among the new-planted beds—her magic moving through the soil like a slow tide, waking things, feeding things. Above the ridge, stars in their thousands. Below, the village lights, and beyond them the dark line of hills where the road went out into the wide world—to the capital, to the conservatory, to Sef and Bram and a king learning to be a man, to a faith being rebuilt around the radical act of paying attention.

Two gardens, she thought. One here, one there. And her standing in the space between, hands in the dirt of both.

She had spent ten years believing home was a place you hid in—that safety was a wall, and love was a liability, and the smaller you made your life, the less the storm could take from you. She had been so careful. And the storm had come anyway, in the end, wearing a crown, starving, mad, asking only to be answered—and what saved the world had not been walls at all. It had been a garden tended with a runaway girl. A liturgy held by a disgraced priest. A king's trust. An answer, freely given.

Home was people. It had always been people. Everything else was just weather.

Jonah knelt in the dark soil of her oldest bed, the one she'd planted her first autumn in exile, and pressed her palms flat against the earth. Beneath her touch, gentle as a heartbeat, she felt the valley answering—slow, green, alive, remembering. Magic rising through the ridgeline like dawn through a window she had finally, finally left open.

Somewhere down the lane, a child laughed. Somewhere far away, rain was falling on a palace conservatory roof, and a twelve-year-old was probably yelling at a gardener about drainage. Somewhere, a god listened, and was no longer hungry.

"All right," Jonah said to the soil, to the stars, to the whole listening world. "Let's grow."

And the valley, softly, began.
