## 2. Ink That Remembers

Sleep did not come for Xanthe Villalobos, which was not unusual. What was unusual was the reason.

They lay in their narrow room above the counting-house, the one assigned to keepers because it was small enough that no one would ever want to visit, and turned the moment over and over like a coin suspected of being counterfeit. *The ninth of Emberfall.* Tonight's date, written in a book bound before Xanthe's grandmother's grandmother had learned to walk. Written in ink that had dried a century ago and could not have known — could not possibly have known — that on this particular night, in this particular year, a keeper with tired eyes and an inventory list would open to that page and feel the floor tilt beneath her certainty.

By the third hour past midnight, Xanthe gave up pretending and dressed. The guildhall never truly slept — somewhere below, the night-traders murmured over their scales, and the vaults breathed their cold mineral breath through the floors — but the corridors at this hour belonged to no one, and no one belonged to them, and that suited Xanthe perfectly.

The prophecy book lived in the Vault of Small Hours, third shelf from the door, between a jar of bottled echoes and a clock that ran backward only when observed. Xanthe had catalogued it years ago under *Miscellaneous — Prophetic, Unverified*, because that was what the old labels said and because questioning old labels was not something keepers did. Keepers kept. That was the whole of the job description, though no one had ever said so aloud; it was simply absorbed, the way one absorbs the smell of a house one grows up in.

Now Xanthe carried the book to the long reading table beneath the vault's single lamp, set it down with the reverence owed to any impossible thing, and opened it to the ninth of Emberfall.

*On this night the gate shall hunger twice,* the entry read, in a spidery hand gone brown with age. *Once as it has always hungered, and once more for what is brought to it willingly. The keeper who reads these words on this night will understand before morning why the ledgers burn.*

Xanthe read it four times. The words did not change. That was somehow worse than if they had.

*Why the ledgers burn.*

There were no burned ledgers. Xanthe knew the archive the way other people knew their own hands — every shelf, every gap where a volume had been withdrawn for restoration, every ledger numbered in the guild's patient, unbroken sequence. Sequence 1 through 412, then 413 through 688, then 689 onward to the present day. Complete. Continuous. A record without a wound in it.

Unless the wound had been stitched closed so neatly that only someone looking for stitches would ever find them.

* * *

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its records the way a miser keeps coins: obsessively, secretly, and with an accounting of every accounting. For every ledger there existed a *provenance folio* — a slim register noting when each volume was created, by whom, amended by whom, and — this was the part that mattered tonight — *redacted* by whom. Redaction was rare. Redaction required keeper's authority, the deep kind, the kind that came with a seal-ring and a seat at the long table upstairs. In eleven years of service, Xanthe had redacted nothing, and had watched exactly two entries pass under another keeper's black wax: a trader's debt struck from the rolls after his death, and a recipe for a door that opened onto yesterday, deemed too dangerous even for the vaults.

Two redactions in eleven years. Xanthe remembered both vividly, because rarity made memory easy.

The provenance folios lived in the Iron Closet off the main archive, and the Iron Closet answered only to a keeper's key. Xanthe's key worked. It had always worked. It had never once occurred to Xanthe, until this precise hour, to wonder *why* a lowly inventory-keeper's key opened a closet full of records about records.

The cold inside the closet was the particular cold of iron that has been cold for a very long time. Xanthe lit the bracket-lamp, found the folio corresponding to the prophecy book — *Folio 7, Ledger of Small Hours, accessioned Year of the Ash 12* — and began, with the methodical patience that was the only gift the guild had ever asked of her, to compare.

The prophecy book was not a book of prophecies, properly speaking. It was a ledger like any other, except that its entries predated their dates. Someone — the first keeper, presumably, though the name signed in the front matter had been scraped away with a knife or a fingernail, leaving only a shallow ghost of letters — had written down nights before they arrived, the way a sensible person writes down appointments. Most entries were mundane to the point of comedy. *Rain. The west stair creaks. A trader lies about his weights.* A hundred years of small hours, recorded in advance by someone who apparently found the future boring.

But some entries were different. Some entries used words like *hunger* and *willingly* and *burn*, and those entries clustered, Xanthe noticed now, around certain dates — and certain dates only.

She copied out seven of them onto a slip of archive-paper in her neat keeper's hand:

*The fourth of Thawmonth.*
*The nineteenth of Highsun.*
*The second of Veilfall.*
*The eleventh of Frostmoon.*
*The twenty-third of Frostmoon.*
*The last night of the Year of Cinders.*
*The ninth of Emberfall.*

Seven dates. Then she took them, one by one, back into the main archive, to the great ledgers themselves — the sequential, unbroken, complete record of the guild's century — and looked up what the guild had officially recorded on each of those nights.

The fourth of Thawmonth, some ninety-one years ago: the ledger recorded a routine inspection of the Hollow Gate. Nothing else. Half a page, in a clerk's tidy script, ending with the phrase *all as expected.*

The nineteenth of Highsun, eighty-three years ago: routine maintenance. *All as expected.*

The second of Veilfall: routine. *All as expected.*

It was the phrasing that snagged, the way a loose thread snags on a ring. *All as expected* — three times, four times, five, six, seven times across seven different decades, in seven different clerks' hands, always ending the entry, always the same final phrase, as though the sentence were not a conclusion but a formula. As though it were a thing one said to make oneself believe it.

And here was the thing that made Xanthe's hands go cold against the vellum: entries that ended in *all as expected* were longer than they should have been. She measured them — actually measured, holding her thumb against the ruled margins, the way she measured everything in the vaults. Each of the seven entries occupied space on the page disproportionate to its content. Half a page of writing that said only *inspection, gate, all as expected*. There was room, in half a page, for a great deal more than three sentences.

Room, or evidence of removal.

Redaction, done properly, did not look like black bars or scraped parchment. Done properly — done with keeper's authority and the guild's own tools — it looked like *nothing*. The ink was drawn out of the page the way poison was drawn out of a wound, and the vellum healed smooth, and the clerk's hand continued on the far side of the absence as though the missing words had never been born. You could not see a proper redaction. You could only weigh the page and find it lighter than it should be.

Xanthe fetched the balance.

It was a vault instrument, brass and agate, sensitive enough to weigh a sigh. Cataloguing it years ago, she had tested it against a blank page and a written one and found the difference measurable, tiny but true: ink had weight, and so did meaning, and the balance knew both. Now she laid the seventh entries upon its pans, one after another, and weighed each against a pristine page of the same dimensions.

Every one came up light.

Not empty-light — the pages still held their three tidy sentences. But light the way a purse is light after a thief has been careful to leave a few coins. Something had been drawn out of all seven entries. Something substantial. And the dates of those somethings matched, precisely, the dates the prophecy book had named a hundred years in advance.

*On this night the gate shall hunger twice...*

Xanthe sat back on the archive stool and let the lamp hum above her. Her heart was doing something complicated — part of it racing with the pure, electric joy of discovery, the joy that had gotten her through eleven years of dust and numbering, the joy of finding the seam in the world's stitching. But underneath the joy, colder and slower, moved something else.

Because there was one more comparison to make, and she made it before she could talk herself out of it. She went back to the provenance folio, Folio 7, and read the amendment history of the seven entries. Every redaction was logged — the guild logged its sins even when it hid them — and every log said the same thing:

*Amended by authority of the Keeper's Seal. Date of amendment: within the week following the entry.*

Within the week. Not decades later, not cleaned up by some embarrassed archivist long after the fact. Within days. Which meant whoever held the Keeper's Seal had stood at this same table, on those same nights, reading whatever the gate had done — whatever the gate had *eaten* — and had chosen, every time, within the week, to draw the truth out of the record like venom.

Seven times in a hundred years. Seven hungers. Seven erasures. All authorized from the top of the guild, all committed by someone who knew exactly what they were hiding, and all of it predicted, dated, and left sitting on a vault shelf for a century in a book labeled *Unverified* — left there, Xanthe thought with a chill that had nothing to do with the iron closet, either because no one had ever bothered to verify it, or because whoever did the verifying had wanted it found.

*The keeper who reads these words on this night will understand before morning why the ledgers burn.*

Morning was perhaps four hours away. Xanthe looked at the slip of paper in her hand, seven dates in her own careful handwriting, and understood that she was holding the most dangerous object in the guildhall — more dangerous than the bottled echoes, more dangerous than the backward clock — because it was the only thing in the building that told the truth about the others.

She folded it small and hid it in the lining of her sleeve, against the skin of her wrist, where keepers hide the things they are not supposed to keep.

Then, because thoroughness was habit and habit was armor, she did one last thing. She checked the provenance folio for the most recent redaction — the newest entry in the amendment log, the last time the Keeper's Seal had been pressed to a page.

The log was chronological. The last line should have been decades old.

It was not. The last line was dated *this week*.

An entry from the current ledger — an entry Xanthe herself had helped shelve not ten days ago — had been amended by authority of the Keeper's Seal four nights ago. She remembered the entry. It was short. It concerned the Hollow Gate. It ended, she was almost certain, in the phrase *all as expected*.

Which meant the hungering was not a hundred-year-old story. Which meant it was *ongoing*. Which meant that somewhere above her, behind the tall doors of the council chamber, Master Corvane — or whichever hand wore the seal — had drawn truth out of a page within living memory, within *recent* memory, while Xanthe had walked the same corridors, drunk the same terrible tea, and suspected nothing.

The lamp guttered. Somewhere in the stacks, the backward clock ticked in reverse, unwinding a moment that had already happened.

Xanthe blew out the flame, locked the iron closet, and stood a long time in the dark of the archive, listening to the guildhall breathe around her — the settling shelves, the murmuring traders below, the vast patient silence of ten thousand records keeping everyone's secrets but hers.

*Before morning,* the prophecy had promised, *you will understand why the ledgers burn.*

She touched the folded paper at her wrist and thought: I have until morning. And I am going to need help — someone the guild never looks at twice, someone who can walk through walls made of attention because no one bothers to build them around her.

Xanthe thought of Wren, and for the first time in eleven years, being invisible felt less like safety and more like a door left unlocked.

She went to find her friend before the guild woke up and the day — the real day, the one the prophecy had scheduled a century ago — began.
