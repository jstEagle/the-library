## 3. The Guild of Impossible Things

Morning in the guildhall arrived the way fog arrives — not all at once, but by degrees, seeping through the high clerestory windows until the great atrium filled with a pale, diluted light that made everything look like a memory of itself.

Xanthe Villalobos climbed the spiral stair from the vaults with the day's inventory ledger tucked under one arm and the prophecy book hidden beneath a loose floorboard three levels down, where no one but a keeper doing honest work would ever think to kneel. They had not slept. Sleep had come to sit beside them around the third bell of the night, consider the matter, and leave again without a word. What remained instead was a strange, humming alertness, the kind that made the ordinary machinery of the guildhall look suddenly theatrical — as if Xanthe were seeing the set from behind, all the ropes and pulleys exposed.

The atrium was already busy. It was always busy. That was the guild's first deception, if you could call it a deception: nothing about the Guild of Impossible Things looked secretive. The hall announced itself with confidence, its vaulted ceiling painted with constellations that did not correspond to any sky above or below, its floors of black marble shot through with veins of silver that older members swore moved when unobserved. Banners hung from the gallery rail — the guild's arms, a door ajar upon a field of stars — and beneath them the morning traffic flowed: archivists carrying ledgers like pallbearers, cartographers arguing over a map of a coastline that had never existed, an apprentice chasing a small brass instrument that scuttled along the wainscoting on six legs, ticking to itself in what might have been laughter.

Xanthe threaded through it all with the ease of long practice, which is to say they made themselves small without appearing to. A keeper's first lesson, learned before ever touching a memory-vial: be furniture. Be the thing people talk over, talk past, forget the moment they turn away. Xanthe had been so good at it for so long that some mornings they had to stand very still in front of a mirror and remind themselves of their own face.

*Inventory,* they reminded themselves now. *Do your rounds. Be furniture.*

But the date sat in them like a swallowed coal. *The ninth night of the Ash Moon.* Tonight's date, written a century ago in a dead woman's careful hand, circled twice as though the writer had wanted to shout it and could only whisper.

"Keeper Villalobos."

Xanthe turned. Master Corvane stood at the foot of the grand staircase, and the crowd parted around him the way water parts around a dark stone — not out of fear exactly, but out of an instinct older than fear. He was a tall man built on the architecture of a much younger one, broad-shouldered beneath robes of deep green gone silver at the cuffs, with a face that seemed to have been handsome so long ago that handsomeness had worn down into something more useful: authority. His eyes were pale gray, the color of paper left too long in the sun, and they rested on Xanthe with an expression that might have been warmth, if warmth could be measured out in exact, non-refundable portions.

"Master Corvane." Xanthe bowed the correct depth. Not deep enough for deference; keepers bowed to no one but the archives. Not shallow enough for insolence; keepers also knew which side of the bread held the butter.

"You were in the vaults late." It was not a question. Corvane never asked questions he did not already own the answers to.

"The Ash Moon inventory," Xanthe said. "The seasonal collections want cataloguing before the damp gets into the western stacks."

"Diligence." He smiled. It reached his mouth and stopped at the border like a traveler lacking papers. "The guild runs on diligence, Villalobos. And on discretion. The two together are rarer than anything we keep downstairs." His pale gaze lingered a half-second past comfort. "You slept well, I hope."

"Well enough," said Xanthe, who had slept not at all.

"Mm." Corvane's attention drifted, deliberately, to the inventory ledger under Xanthe's arm, then back up. "Carry on, then. And Villalobos — the Ash Moon is a delicate season for us. Old things stir. Best to handle old things gently." He turned and ascended the staircase, and the crowd closed behind him like water over the stone.

Xanthe stood a moment in the eddy of his passing, heart tapping a quick, traitorous rhythm against their ribs. *He knows I was down there. Of course he knows. He knows everything that happens in this building except, perhaps, the one thing that matters.*

Or perhaps that too.

They made themselves walk, unhurried, toward the eastern galleries.

* * *

The guildhall had been a fortress once, back when the city outside had walls worth the name. You could still see the bones of it in the architecture — the murder-slits converted to windows, the gatehouse arch now framing a fountain shaped like a weeping woman whose tears ran uphill. But the guild had grown strange growths upon the old stone, wings added by architects who had clearly been paid in something other than money. The Hall of Bargains, for instance, had no right angles anywhere in it, because, the story went, a bargain should have no corners to hide in. Xanthe had never found this reassuring.

It was in the Hall of Bargains that the traders conducted their morning audience, and it was there Xanthe's rounds took them, because the traders required witnesses — guild witnesses, official ones — for anything they wished entered into the record. Keepers served as notaries of the impossible. It was one of the few times anyone looked directly at them, and Xanthe had long ago decided being seen for a purpose was preferable to not being seen at all, which told them something sad about their life that they preferred not to examine.

Madame Ossory held court at the far end of the hall, beneath an arched window of rose-colored glass that poured pink light over her like wine. She was not beautiful; she was something better, which is to say she was *interesting*, silver-haired and sharp-boned, dressed in dove gray with a single garnet brooch at her throat that Xanthe knew — from the inventory, from long nights among the vials — contained a captured sunset. The last sunset of a village that no longer appeared on maps. She wore it the way other women wore pearls.

She was listening to a nervous young man twist his hat in his hands.

"—it's only that my sister's been gone eleven years, madame, and the rivermen say—"

"The rivermen say a great many things," Ossory said pleasantly. "That's why rivermen are rivermen and I am not. Sit, child. Tell me what was taken, precisely, and what you can offer, precisely, and we shall see whether the arithmetic favors us both." Her voice was honey over gravel. Around her, three junior traders took notes in shorthand Xanthe could not read — trader's cipher, forbidden to keepers on pain of expulsion, though every keeper learned it anyway within their first year, because curiosity was the one vice the guild forgave so long as you were quiet about it.

Xanthe took their place at the witness desk, opened the official record, and dipped the pen.

The bargain proceeded in the manner of all bargains, which is to say it resembled a surgical procedure performed by two people pretending to be friends. The young man wanted his sister back — or her memory, or her name, or whatever fragment of her the river had kept. In exchange he offered his childhood: every summer, every storm watched from a window, the smell of his mother's bread. Ossory weighed these things on a small brass scale that did not weigh weight, turning weights that looked like coins but weren't, and in the end the trade was struck, the vials changed hands, and the young man walked away lighter, smiling at a happiness he would never remember having sold.

Xanthe wrote it all down in the record. *One sister, partial. One childhood, whole, less storms.* Their handwriting did not waver. It was part of the job. But something cold had settled behind their breastbone, and it was not the morning chill.

*What do they feed the vials' contents to?* the coal in their chest whispered. *Where does a childhood go after it is bought?*

They had asked that question once, as a first-year apprentice, of their teacher. Keeper Hesk had looked at them for a long moment with an expression Xanthe had spent years trying to decode and finally understood only now, standing in the pink light of the Hall of Bargains with a century-old date burning in their pocket-memory: Hesk had looked *afraid*, and had covered it with a smile, and had said, *Everything in this house goes somewhere, child. Best not to ask where.*

* * *

The midday meal was taken in the Refectory, a long vaulted room where the guild's ranks ate in loose stratification — traders near the fire, archivists near the wine, keepers at the end of the table nearest the door, because keepers were expected to come and go. Xanthe carried their bowl of barley soup to the usual bench and found it, unusually, occupied.

Wren looked up. She was a courier, twenty-something and unmemorable on purpose, with mouse-brown hair cropped short and a face designed by fate to be forgotten five seconds after leaving a room. This was not an insult; it was her professional qualification. Couriers who could not be remembered could carry messages through hostile territory, past checkpoints, across borders, and no guard could later describe them. Wren wore her anonymity like a well-tailored coat.

"This seat taken?" she asked, though she was already sitting in it.

"It's yours," Xanthe said, sitting opposite. "It's always yours. You've stolen my seat four hundred times."

"And you've let me four hundred times, which makes it a tradition, and traditions are sacred." Wren grinned, tore her bread, and pushed half of it across the table without being asked. She always did that. It was one of the small warm mechanics of their friendship, if friendship was the word — neither of them had ever examined it closely enough to name it, in the way that neither of them had ever asked the other certain obvious questions, such as *why does a person with no family come to work for the Guild of Impossible Things*, because the answer was always the same and always unbearable.

Keepers were chosen for having no family to miss them. Everyone knew it. Nobody said it. The recruiters came to orphanages, to debtor's prisons, to war-scarred villages where the census lists had more crosses than names. They looked for children who had already begun to fade — the quiet ones, the unfetched ones, the ones the other children had trouble recalling at year's end. Talent for memory-work, the recruiters claimed, ran in such blood. Xanthe had believed that at eight years old. At twenty-eight they suspected the talent was manufactured by the same process that selected for it: a person no one waited for learned early how to stop waiting too.

"Eat," Wren said, watching them not eat. "You've got the look."

"What look?"

"The vault look. Like someone asked you a question in the dark and you're still deciding whether to answer." Wren chewed, swallowed, studied Xanthe with those quick gray eyes that missed more than people assumed precisely because everyone assumed they missed everything. "Bad inventory?"

"Ash Moon inventory," Xanthe said. "It's always bad. Things migrate. Last year the entire collection of bottled echoes moved two shelves west and nobody heard them do it."

"Hm." Wren let it go, which was another of her talents — knowing which silences were load-bearing. For a while they ate in companionable quiet, the refectory roaring softly around them with the sound of a hundred conversations none of which concerned them. Belonging, Xanthe thought, watching the firelight move on the faces of people who would greet them tomorrow without remembering today. It was a strange word for what they felt. It fit the way a borrowed coat fits: warm enough, close enough, unmistakably not theirs.

"You know what I like about this place?" Wren said suddenly.

"The soup?"

"The soup's a crime against barley. No. What I like—" she leaned forward, dropping her voice into the register couriers used for things that were technically jokes, "—is that everybody here has already lost something. So nobody asks. You walk in missing a sister, a homeland, a decade of your life, and the guild just nods and hands you a livery. First place I ever lived where being broken was the entry fee instead of the scandal."

"That's either very wise or very sad."

"Both. Everything true here is both." She pointed her spoon at Xanthe. "Which is why you've got the vault look. Something down there is both, and you haven't finished deciding which."

Xanthe held her gaze. The temptation to tell her — *a hundred-year-old book with tonight's date in it, Wren, redactions signed in keeper's ink, dates matching vanished records* — rose in their throat like a tide. Wren was the closest thing to a confidant the guild's architecture allowed. But the words snagged on something, some small wire of caution: the way Wren navigated the halls, yes, but also the way she sometimes wasn't where she'd said she'd be, the way guards nodded to her in corridors where couriers had no business walking, the way she had once, drunk on festival wine, said *this building breathes, Xanthe, didn't you know? It breathes in* — and then laughed it off.

"Not finished deciding," Xanthe agreed lightly. "Ask me at month's end."

"Month's end," Wren echoed, and something flickered across her forgettable face — there and gone so fast Xanthe could almost believe they'd imagined it. Almost. "I'll hold you to that."

* * *

The afternoon rounds took Xanthe up through the residential wing, past the novices' dormitory where the newest intake — six children, the eldest nine — were learning their first lesson in the courtyard below. Xanthe paused at the window to watch. A senior keeper, old Maren, stood before the children holding a single lit candle.

"Now," Maren was saying, in the gentle voice teachers use when the content is not gentle, "when I pinch this flame out, each of you will remember it perfectly. Bright, steady, warm. Tomorrow you'll remember it slightly less well. By week's end, you'll remember a candle. By year's end, you'll remember that something happened once. Memory is not a vault, little ones. It is a garden that grows weeds. Our work is to be the gardeners of other people's forgetting."

A small girl in the front row raised her hand. "Who gardens ours?"

The question hung in the courtyard air. Old Maren smiled the smile of a woman who had answered this question a thousand times and had never once found a good answer.

"We do," she said. "That is what the guild is *for*."

Xanthe turned away from the window. Their hands had gone cold. *We do.* The founding promise, recited at every induction, carved over the archive doors in letters gilded twice: *WE KEEP WHAT THE WORLD CANNOT AFFORD TO LOSE.*

And last night, in the vault, in a dead woman's hand, a different sentence had been waiting a century for someone to read it:

*On the ninth night of the Ash Moon, the gate shall hunger, and the guild shall feed it, as it has fed, and shall feed, until the ledger is full.*

Full. Full of what?

Xanthe walked the corridor toward the Master's stair, meaning to pass it, meaning only to look — and stopped. Because there, pinned to the duty board beside the stair, fresh ink gleaming, was the maintenance roster for the evening watch. The Hollow Gate rotation. Every keeper took their turns; it was considered an honor, or was described as one.

Xanthe Villalobos's name was written first on the list.

In Master Corvane's handwriting.
