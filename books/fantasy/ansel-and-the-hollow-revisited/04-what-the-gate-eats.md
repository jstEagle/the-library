## 4. What the Gate Eats

The Hollow Gate lived at the bottom of the guildhall, which was to say it lived at the bottom of everything.

Xanthe had learned this on their first day as a keeper, when Master Corvane walked them down a stair that should not have existed — a stair that began behind the archive's third shelf and descended past the point where Xanthe's counting lost its nerve. Forty steps, then forty more, then a stretch where the walls pressed close and the lamplight went thin and gray as old milk. Corvane had not spoken the whole way down. He had only stopped at the last landing, gestured with one long hand toward the dark ahead, and said: *You will tend it. You will not speak to it. And you will never, under any circumstance, tell it your name.*

Xanthe had been twenty-two then, newly sworn, still carrying the smell of the orphanage laundry in their memory like a stone in a shoe. They had nodded. They had not asked what would happen if they did tell it their name. Some questions you learn to leave in your pockets, unspent, because spending them changes nothing except how much you have left.

Now, six years later, Xanthe made the descent alone, as they did every ninth night, carrying the maintenance satchel over one shoulder and a lantern of guild-cold flame — fire that gave light without warmth, because warmth was a thing the gate could taste.

* * *

The vaults above were full of impossible things, but the gate was not an impossible thing. That was the first lesson, and the strangest. The gate was entirely, terribly possible. It was a door, and doors are the most honest objects in the world: they are made for passing through, and this one had not passed anything through itself in a hundred years, and it *wanted* to. Wanting came off it like heat off a forge.

It stood at the end of the final corridor, set into rock that had no business being beneath a city built on river clay. Black stone, older than the guild's oldest ledger, carved — or grown; Xanthe had never decided — with spirals that turned inward forever without ever reaching a center. The door itself was seamless. No handle. No keyhole. No hinge. Only the surface, and the surface was wrong in a way the eye refused to hold: look at it directly and it seemed flat; look at it from the corner of your vision and it seemed to be breathing.

Around its frame, packed into the grooves of the spiral carvings, glowed the memories.

That was the word the ledgers used, so it was the word Xanthe used, though it felt insufficient every time. The seal of the Hollow Gate was not mortar or iron or spellwork in any sense Xanthe understood. It was *given things*. A century ago, when the war ended and the gate was closed, the founders had fed it — and it had eaten, and what it ate became the shining substance crusted along its edges like amber gone luminous. Each node of light was a memory. Not a copy. The original. Taken whole from someone's living mind, so that somewhere above, walking around in the ordinary world, there were people who had gaps in them shaped like their own childhoods, their own weddings, their own dead.

The keepers' job, on maintenance rounds, was simple: walk the corridor, read the seal, record its condition. *Read*, in the keeper's sense — hold the lantern close, let the cold light play across the nodes, and listen with the part of the mind that the guild trained but never named. Healthy memories hummed. Fading ones whispered. Dead ones were silent, and silent nodes had to be recorded for replacement, because a gate held shut by dying memories was a gate with its latch loosening.

Xanthe set the lantern on its iron stand, unrolled the inspection cloth across the stones, and began.

The first three meters of frame sang low and steady, the way they always did. Old memories, deep-fed, dense as river pearls. Xanthe moved along the wall with the slow patience of long habit, fingertips hovering a hand's width from the glowing crust, reading the vibration through the bones of the hand rather than touching — touching was permitted, technically, but the gate liked touch, and Xanthe had never liked giving it things it liked.

At the fourth meter, the humming changed.

Not faded. Not dead. *Eaten.*

Xanthe stopped. Held the lantern higher. The cold light fell across the carving and showed what the humming had already told them: a patch of the spiral groove, wide as two spread hands, where the amber glow had thinned to a translucent skin. Beneath it, the black stone showed through, bare and dry, like bone through torn flesh. And the edges of the patch — this was the part that made Xanthe's stomach drop slowly, the way a coin drops down a well — the edges were not worn smooth by time. They were ragged. Fresh. As though something had taken a bite only hours ago and the wound had not yet thought about healing.

There should have been a record. Every erosion in a hundred years was logged, dated, cross-referenced against the feeding schedules in the Ledger of Small Hours. Erosion happened — the gate digested even given things, eventually, which was why the feedings continued, why the guild's whole quiet machinery of collection ground on year after year — but erosion happened *slowly*. Centimeter by centimeter across decades. Not a bite out of the middle of a healthy seal in a single night.

Xanthe knelt, spread the inspection cloth, and wrote the entry before their hands finished trembling: *Ninth night. Fourth meter, inner spiral. Loss of approximately—* They paused, measuring with their eyes. *—two hundred square digits. Edges irregular. No prior notation of weakness at this site. Cause unknown.*

The pen scratched. Somewhere far above, the guildhall settled and creaked like an old ship. And the gate—

The gate breathed.

Xanthe had heard it breathe before. Everyone who worked the lower corridors had. It was part of the texture of the place, like dripping water or the tick of cooling stone: a vast, slow expansion and contraction of the air, as if the whole corridor were a lung. You learned to ignore it the way you learned to ignore your own heartbeat.

But tonight the breath carried something in it.

A sound. Thin, at the very edge of hearing. Then again, closer, the way a voice comes closer when it has decided to be heard.

Xanthe went very still, the way one goes still when a wild animal looks up.

"*...raining.*"

One word. Or half of one. It came from everywhere in the corridor and nowhere, from the stone and the air and the inside of Xanthe's own skull, and it had a quality that made the hair rise along their arms: it was *familiar*. Not in the way a stranger's voice is familiar because all voices share features. Familiar in the way your own handwriting is familiar. The cadence of it caught somewhere behind Xanthe's sternum and pulled.

"*...always practicing. You'll wear the letters off the page, little one.*"

The lantern flame stood perfectly straight. The cold light did not flicker, because cold light had no mood to betray. But Xanthe's hands were shaking now, openly, and they gripped the pen like a rail.

They knew that voice.

They knew it the way you know a voice you have not heard since you were seven years old — completely, instantly, and with a grief that arrives before understanding. A woman's voice. Warm, a little rough at the edges, with the particular lilt of someone who laughed often and recently. It said words in the accent of the river districts, the accent Xanthe had spent ten years carefully sanding out of their own speech because the guild preferred its keepers unmarked.

"*Mama?*" The word was out of Xanthe's mouth before they could stop it, small and stupid and seven years old, and the moment it left them they felt the corridor *lean in*.

The pressure changed. Not wind — nothing so honest as wind. It was attention. The whole weight of the gate's wanting swung toward them like a lamp swinging on its hook, and the darkness at the edges of the cold light seemed to gather, to concentrate, to become less like absence and more like a crowd pressing at a door.

"*You used to sit on the step,*" the voice murmured, and now it was coming from directly in front of Xanthe, from the bare black stone where the seal had been eaten away, from the gap in the gate's teeth. "*Summer evenings. Practicing your letters in the dust with a stick because we couldn't waste paper. You wrote your name over and over. You were so proud of the V. You said the V was the hardest letter and you'd beaten it.*"

Xanthe's mother had died when they were eight. Fever, the orphanage matron had said, in the flat tone reserved for facts too large for comfort. Xanthe had no memory of her voice. They had checked, over the years, quietly, the way you check a wound — turning over every recollection of those early years looking for the sound of her, and finding everywhere a silence shaped exactly like her. He'd assumed grief had eaten it. Grief does that. Grief is a kind of gate itself.

But grief doesn't whisper in her exact accent. Grief doesn't know about the V.

"*Where did you get that,*" Xanthe said. Their voice came out level, which surprised them, and cost them more than screaming would have. "*That's mine. That memory is mine.*"

The silence that followed had texture. It had the texture of something considering.

Then, softly, from the eaten place in the seal, in his mother's voice, in his mother's warm rough river-district lilt:

"*Was.*"

* * *

Xanthe did not remember climbing the stairs. That frightened them afterward more than anything else — more than the voice, more than the word *was*, delivered with the mild precision of a clerk correcting an error in a ledger. They had knelt at the fourth meter with a pen in their hand, and then they had been standing at the top of the stair behind the archive's third shelf, lantern in hand, inspection cloth folded and clutched to their chest like a shield, with no passage of time in between. Just the two moments, stitched together with a seam of nothing.

Their heart was going like a courier's feet.

They made themselves walk, normally, past the shelves, past old Keeper Doss snoring at his desk over a ledger he'd been failing to read for thirty years, out into the corridor where the guildhall's night-noise wrapped around them like a blanket that didn't quite cover. Their rooms were three floors up. They walked there. They lit no lamp. They sat on the edge of their narrow bed in the dark and unfolded the inspection cloth and read their own handwriting by the light that leaked under the door.

*Ninth night. Fourth meter, inner spiral. Loss of approximately two hundred square digits. Edges irregular. No prior notation of weakness at this site. Cause unknown.*

Cause unknown. That was a lie, and it was written in their own careful hand, and that was the thing that finally made Xanthe understand the shape of the trouble they were in. Because the cause was not unknown. The cause was that the gate had *eaten* a piece of its own seal — reached into the lock and swallowed part of the key — and the piece it had swallowed was a memory of Xanthe's mother that Xanthe had never known was missing.

Which meant the seal was not being fed memories the guild collected.

Which meant the seal was being fed memories the guild *took*.

And which meant — and here Xanthe's thoughts arrived at the place they had been circling all night, the place they'd been circling since the ledger, since tonight's date written in a book a hundred years old — which meant that somewhere in the guild's machinery, someone had once decided that a fever-orphaned child from the river district should lose the sound of their mother's voice, and had fed that loss to a door, and had written it all down, and had redacted it.

The prophecy ledger. Tonight's date. The matching dates. The redactions signed with a keeper's authority.

Xanthe sat in the dark and held their own name in their mouth like a hot coal. *Xanthe Villalobos.* The name they had practiced in the dust with a stick. The name with the V they'd been so proud of beating. If the gate could reach back six years into their childhood and pull out a thread they hadn't known was gone, what else had it been given? What else had *they* been given — handed over, node by node, across years of maintenance rounds and quiet service, by a guild that chose its keepers for having no family to miss them?

No family to miss them.

Or no family left with enough of them remaining to notice what was missing.

Xanthe looked at the inspection cloth in their lap, at the neat lie in their own handwriting, and made a decision with the cold clarity of someone stepping off a ledge: tomorrow they would pull the records. Every date. Every redaction. Every feeding logged against every erased memory, cross-checked against the prophecy book's calendar of nights. If the guild was feeding the gate, they would find the pattern. And if the pattern led where they feared it led —

A floorboard creaked in the corridor outside. Soft. Deliberate. The particular creak of someone who knew which boards announced visitors and had chosen to announce themselves anyway.

Xanthe folded the cloth quickly and looked up as the door opened a hand's width, spilling warm lamplight across the floor, and a familiar unhurried silhouette leaned into the gap.

"You're up late," Wren said. "Thought I heard you come in twice."
