## 5. A Friend Named Wren

The redactions had a smell.

That was the first thing Xanthe learned, three nights into the search, hunched over the guild's duplicate registry with a tallow candle burning low. Fresh ink, properly cured, smelled of oak gall and iron — a bite in the nose, a faint metallic tang. But ink laid over ink, ink used to bury rather than to write, went flat. It smelled of nothing at all, a small dead patch in the middle of a living page, the way a room smells wrong after someone has stood in it too long without speaking.

Xanthe pressed their nose to the registry's margin and inhaled. Dead patch. Again. Dead patch. Page after page, year after year, dates that had once carried entries now smoothed over with a hand steady enough to be professional and hurried enough to be afraid.

"You're going to set your sleeve on fire."

Xanthe startled hard enough to knock the candle sideways. Wax spattered across the registry's leather corner, and a hand — quick, unbothered, familiar — righted the flame before it could do more than gutter.

Wren crouched on the other side of the reading table, chin propped on her fists, watching Xanthe with the mild expression of someone who had been there for some time. She was a small woman built like a folded letter, all creases and corners, with hair the color of wet sand and a courier's satchel slung across her chest even indoors, even at midnight, because Wren treated the satchel the way other people treated their skeletons.

"How," Xanthe said, pressing a palm to their sternum, "do you keep doing that."

"Practice." She fished a heel of bread from the satchel, tore it in half, and offered the larger piece across the table — a courtesy Xanthe had stopped questioning years ago. "You didn't hear the stair because you weren't listening. You never listen. You read with your whole head, like a heron going after fish."

"I was working."

"You were *smelling a book*." She chewed, watching them with bright, unhurried eyes. "Which is either the strangest thing I've seen this week or the most interesting, and I carried a crate of singing teeth to the Tallow District on Tuesday, so the bar is high."

Xanthe should have closed the registry. That was the thought that arrived, cool and correct, in the part of their mind that kept the guild's rules like coins in a locked drawer. Keepers did not discuss the archives. Keepers did not discuss the vaults. Keepers, in the guild's quiet arithmetic, were chosen precisely because they had no one to tell — no mother expecting letters, no brother splitting an inheritance, no sweetheart waiting under a particular window. Xanthe had been eleven years old when the guild's assessor had looked at their file — orphaned, unclaimed, unremarkable — and smiled the way a mason smiles at a well-shaped stone.

But Wren had been delivering to the archive since before Xanthe held keeper's authority. Wren had taught them which stair creaked and which clerk took bribes in dried figs and which corners of the guildhall the candles never reached. Wren was, if the word could be trusted at all, a friend.

"The registry," Xanthe said slowly, turning the ledger so she could see it, and watched their own voice steady itself on the rails of the decision. "Someone's been redacting entries. Dozens of them. All with keeper's authority."

Wren looked at the page. She looked at it for exactly as long as a person looks at a page, no longer, and then she reached over and turned it back a leaf, and then another, her finger tracing the margins without touching the ink.

"Here," she said. "And here. And — hm. Here." Three dead patches in as many leaves. She sat back. "That's a lot of nothing."

"It's not nothing. It's *specific* nothing. I've been cross-checking against the prophecy ledger in the vault — the old one, the one nobody's catalogued because everyone assumes it's decorative. The redacted dates match. Every date the prophecy names, someone buried the corresponding entry in the registry."

Wren's chewing slowed. Stopped.

"The prophecy ledger," she said. "The big ugly one with the cracked spine? The one that's been sitting on the same shelf since before I had this satchel?"

"Century-old paper. Century-old hand. And last night's date written in it like an appointment."

The candle flame leaned in a draft from the archive's high windows. Wren's face, in that light, did something complicated and brief — a flinch folded inside a blink — and then smoothed back into her usual expression of pleasant, forgettable nothing, the face that made customs officers wave her through and gate-guards forget they'd checked her papers.

"An appointment," she repeated.

"Xanthe Villalobos, expected at the Hollow Gate, such-and-such hour, such-and-such watch." Xanthe heard their own voice go thin. "Written down a hundred years before I was born. Before the guildhall was rebuilt. Before *Corvane* was master."

Wren was quiet for a moment. Then she tore another piece of bread, though Xanthe noticed she didn't eat it, just held it, turning it over in her fingers like a coin she was deciding whether to spend.

"All right," she said. "Say it's true. Say the book knew. What do you want to do about it?"

"I want to know who did the redacting. The hand is the same every time — I checked the stroke weight, the slant, the way they cross their sevens. One person, over decades. A keeper with authority to touch the registry and the nerve to touch the prophecy book. That's a short list. That might be a list of *one*."

"Corvane," Wren said, without inflection.

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't have to. You've been making the face since I sat down. The face you make when you've already decided something and you're waiting for permission." She finally ate the bread, dusting crumbs from her fingers. "Fine. I'll help. But we do it my way."

"Which is?"

"Slowly. Boringly. In the open." She tapped the registry with one finger. "Nobody looks at a courier. That's the whole trick of me. Tomorrow I've got deliveries through the record halls — requisition slips, ink requisitions, nothing. I'll pull the old delivery manifests while I'm at it. Every time a ledger came up from the deep archive, every time one went down, there's a manifest, and every manifest has a signature."

"And if the signature is Corvane's?"

"Then we'll know." She shrugged, and the satchel shifted on her shoulder with a whisper of buckles. "And then you'll have to decide what knowing is worth."

* * *

They worked through the small hours, the way they had not worked together since their first year, when Wren had shown a nervous new keeper how to sleep in the archive's map room without being caught and Xanthe had shown her, in return, which of the guild's locked cabinets could be opened with a hairpin and patience. It was easy, being with Wren. It had always been easy. Conversation with her was like walking a path worn smooth by years of use — no need to watch where one stepped.

"You never told me," Xanthe said at some point past the second candle, "why you stayed. Courier work pays badly. The guild works its runners half to death. You could have taken a contract with any of the trading houses. Ossory's people recruit couriers all the time."

Wren was copying manifest numbers onto a scrap of paper in her quick, ugly hand. "Ossory's people recruit *anyone* all the time. That's not a job, that's a net." She blew on the ink. "I stayed because the guild is where the strange things are. I like being close to the strange things."

"You're a strange thing."

"I'm *unremarkable*. It's different. Unremarkable is a discipline." She said it lightly, but Xanthe caught the edge beneath it, the way you catch a wire under snow. "Do you know how hard it is to be forgotten on purpose? To walk into a room full of suspicious people and have every eye slide off you like rain off slate? It takes work. It takes *years* of work. Anyone can be noticed. Being overlooked — that's a craft."

"You make it sound like a virtue."

"It's a survival." Wren smiled, and for a moment the smile had teeth in it, and then it didn't. "The world doesn't hurt what it doesn't see, Xanthe. I learned that early. You learned it late, or never — you're a keeper, you were *made* to be invisible, but you keep wanting to be seen anyway. It's your worst habit."

Xanthe didn't answer. The candle had burned down to a stub, and in its shrinking light the registry's redacted margins looked like a field of small graves.

"Wren," they said. "The gate. You've done maintenance rounds. Everyone has. What did you — " They hesitated, hearing their own question reshape itself. "Did it ever talk to you?"

The silence that followed had a texture. Xanthe had spent years among objects that remembered — vials of other people's childhoods, mirrors that kept old arguments, doors that sighed when opened — and they knew the difference between a pause and a *held breath*. This was a held breath.

"The gate doesn't talk," Wren said at last, and her voice was perfectly, professionally level. "It's a door. Doors don't talk."

"It whispered to me. My mother's voice. A lullaby I haven't heard since I was six." Xanthe watched Wren's hands, which had gone still around the scrap of paper. "It knew things no record contains. Things that were never written anywhere. So either the gate can reach into a person's head — or someone has been *feeding* it, and what it fed it came from me."

"Or," Wren said, "you were tired, and the vault plays tricks, and you should go to sleep."

"Wren."

"Xanthe." She looked up, and her eyes in the candlelight were very clear and very old, older than a courier's eyes had any right to be. "I've been running deliveries through this guildhall since I was nine years old. I know which floors are hollow and which doors are lies and which of the masters drinks more than he admits. And I know that the people who ask too many questions about the Hollow Gate have a way of getting reassigned. Quietly. To postings very far away. Or no postings at all." She folded the scrap of paper into her satchel, unhurried, precise. "So I'm going to help you, because you're my friend and because you'll do it anyway and because someone should. But I need you to promise me one thing."

"Name it."

"Don't go back to the gate alone. Not until we know what we're carrying." She stood, slinging the satchel across her chest, and for a moment her silhouette against the archive's high dark windows looked less like a courier than like a sentinel — small, unremarkable, and utterly immovable. "Promise me, Xanthe."

"I promise."

"Good." The teeth came back into her smile, briefly, like a knife shown and sheathed. "Then give me until tomorrow's last delivery bell. I'll have the manifests. And Xanthe — "

She paused at the top of the stair, one hand on the rail, and the candlelight caught her face at an angle that made her, for an instant, someone Xanthe almost recognized and could not place.

"When you find the name in the registry — whoever signed the redactions — don't say it out loud. Not here. Not anywhere in this building. Names have weight in this guild. You of all people should know that."

Then she was gone, footsteps fading down the stair with the soft, practiced quiet of a woman who had spent her whole life being unheard, and Xanthe sat alone with the dead patches and the guttering candle and the growing, cold certainty that the friend across the table had known things about the gate long before tonight — and had chosen, for years, to say nothing at all.

* * *

The manifests arrived the next evening, exactly as promised, tucked inside a requisition slip for candle-wax that no one would ever question. Xanthe found them waiting in the archive's reading alcove, weighted under a stone paperweight shaped like a sleeping cat, with a note in Wren's ugly hand: *Third column. Look at the signatures. Look at the dates. And Xanthe — I checked twice. I'm sorry.*

Xanthe pulled the manifests into the candlelight and read.

Delivery manifests, going back forty years. Every transfer of a ledger from the deep archive to the upper halls, and back. The signatures of the requesting keepers varied — a scatter of names, some dead, some retired, one or two Xanthe knew by face. But the *return* signatures, the signatures authorizing each ledger's reburial, were all the same. One hand. One slant. One particular way of crossing a seven.

The same hand that had redacted the registry.

The same hand, Xanthe realized, heart slowing to a cold, deliberate beat, that had signed the transfer order — dated a hundred years ago, in ink that should have crumbled — for the prophecy ledger itself.

Master Corvane's signature had not changed in a century.

Xanthe sat very still. Somewhere above, through the archive's high windows, the last delivery bell rang across the guildhall, and beneath it, faint as a held breath, they heard the stair begin to creak — measured, unhurried, the footsteps of someone who did not need to listen for listeners, because no one dared listen to him.

"Keeper Villalobos." Master Corvane's voice, warm as banked coals, drifted down the stairwell ahead of him. "You work such long hours. Come. There is something I've been meaning to give you."
