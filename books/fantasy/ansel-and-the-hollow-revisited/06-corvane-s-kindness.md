## 6. Corvane's Kindness

The summons came the way frost comes to a window: quietly, and all at once, and with the certain knowledge that warmth was finished for a while.

Xanthe found it waiting on their workbench when they returned from the morning rounds — a slip of grey vellum, unsealed, unsigned, bearing only three words in Master Corvane's narrow hand: *Come at dusk. Alone.*

No reason given. No reason needed. In twenty years within the Guild of Impossible Things, Xanthe had learned that Corvane's summons were like the guild's own inventory — complete in themselves, requiring nothing added, permitting nothing taken away.

They spent the intervening hours doing what keepers did best: pretending the day was ordinary. They catalogued a jar of borrowed thunder (provenance disputed; contents restless). They re-inked the fading label on a drawer of last words. They dusted the shelf where the guild kept its collection of doors that opened onto nowhere, each one patient, each one slightly ajar, as if waiting for someone foolish enough to knock.

But their hands kept returning, without permission, to the thought they had carried for two days now — the prophecy ledger, the date written a century before it came true, the redactions that bloomed through the old records like bruises through skin.

*Someone with keeper's authority had done that.*

There were only four keepers. And Xanthe had not done it.

At dusk, they climbed the spiral stair to the Master's study at the top of the guildhall, where the architecture grew strange the way it always did near Corvane's rooms — steps that shortened as you rose, so that arriving felt like being swallowed gently, floor by floor.

* * *

Corvane received them standing at his window, hands clasped behind his back, watching the city kindle its lamps below. He was an old man built like something that had been old for a very long time — not frail but *settled*, the way ruins are settled, worn smooth by weather no one else remembered falling. His hair was white as ledger-ash. When he turned, his eyes were the pale grey of paper left too long in the sun.

"Villalobos." He said the name slowly, tasting it. "Do you know how many people in this city could tell me your name?"

Xanthe bowed. "I couldn't say, Master."

"No," Corvane agreed. "Neither could I. That is rather the point." He gestured to a chair. "Sit. Drink?"

On the table between two chairs sat a decanter of plum-dark wine and two glasses, already poured, as though he had known to the minute when Xanthe would arrive. That, too, was Corvane. Nothing about him was ever surprised. Xanthe had once privately suspected the man was less a person than a very thorough record of one.

"You've been with us twenty years," Corvane said, settling into his own chair with the care of someone whose joints remembered every year individually. "Twenty years, and in all that time, how many complaints have reached my desk bearing your name?"

"None, Master."

"None." He smiled, and the smile was genuinely warm, which was somehow worse than if it hadn't been. "Do you understand what a miracle that is? The traders below us — Ossory's people, the coin-brokers, the bargain-mongers — they generate complaints the way wet wood generates smoke. Even Wren, who is the most forgettable courier the guild has ever employed, managed to start a small riot over a misdelivered parcel of somebody's childhood. But you—" he lifted his glass toward Xanthe, "—you are silence itself. Twenty years of perfect discretion."

"I try to be worthy of the work, Master."

"The work." Corvane swirled his wine. "And what would you say the work is, Villalobos? Describe it to me. I find I enjoy hearing my own institution described back to me by the people who actually touch it."

It was a test. It was always a test with Corvane, even — especially — when it wore the clothing of courtesy. Xanthe chose the answer carefully, the way one chose which object to touch first in a dark room.

"We keep things that cannot otherwise be kept," they said. "We remember what the world has decided to forget. We stand between the impossible things and the forgetting, so that neither destroys the other."

Something flickered across the old man's face — there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. Pain, perhaps. Or recognition.

"A textbook answer," he said softly. "You've been reading the founding charters."

"The charter is read to every apprentice, Master."

"It is. And most apprentices hear only the poetry of it." He leaned forward. "Tell me, Villalobos. In your rounds of the vaults — and I know you do the deep shelves now, the restricted stacks, you've earned that trust — have you come across anything that troubled you?"

The question dropped into the room like a stone into still water, and Xanthe watched the ripples spread outward through everything Corvane had said and everything he hadn't.

*He knows.* Or he suspects. Or he knows nothing at all and this is simply the shape of an ordinary evening for him, and Xanthe's guilt is painting eyes onto a blind man.

"There is always something troubling in the vaults, Master," Xanthe said. "That is why we keep things there."

Corvane laughed — a dry, genuine sound, like pages turning. "Well parried. You'd have made a fine diplomat. Or a fine liar; the professions overlap more than either admits." He set down his glass. "I ask because I have a gift for you. A recognition gift. Twenty years of service deserves marking, and I am told the anniversary passed last month while we were all distracted by the Ossory negotiations."

He rose, went to a cabinet of black wood that Xanthe had never seen open, and returned with something cupped in both palms like a wounded bird.

A memory-vial.

Not one of the common ones — the small grey phials the guild used for routine erasures, cheap as candle-tallow. This was a vessel of blown glass the color of deep water, its stopper sealed with wax stamped by a sigil Xanthe recognized from exactly one place: the burned ledgers' surviving fragments. The sigil of the first keeper.

"These haven't been made in a hundred years," Corvane said. "The craft died with the craftswoman. There are perhaps nine in existence. This one holds—" he paused, weighing his words with visible pleasure, "—a memory of the Hollow Gate itself. From before the sealing. What it looked like when it was young. What it *wanted*."

Xanthe's breath went carefully shallow. "Master, I don't—"

"Humor me." He pressed it into their hands. The glass was cold, then warm, then cold again, cycling like something breathing. "A keeper should know what they guard against. You've walked your maintenance rounds past that door for twenty years and seen only a door. This will show you what the door sees."

Every instinct Xanthe owned was screaming in a language without words. *This is not a gift. Gifts are given to reward. This is given to measure — to see what your face does when you hold a piece of the thing you've been secretly investigating. To see whether you flinch.*

Or — and here was the colder thought, the one that slid beneath the first like a knife under a door — *this is a warning. He knows exactly what you've read. And he is telling you, in the politest possible way, that he can reach into your head whenever he chooses, and hand you whatever he likes.*

"Thank you, Master," Xanthe heard themselves say. "It's beautiful."

"It's dangerous," Corvane corrected mildly. "Most things worth keeping are. Which reminds me—" he settled back, and his voice took on the texture of idle conversation, the most dangerous texture it owned, "—the prophecy ledgers. The old ones, in the restricted archive. You've been cataloguing that section, yes?"

"Yes, Master."

"Some of those volumes are fragile. Centuries old. The ink in particular — early keepers used an iron-gall compound that eats the page as it writes. Given time, it devours everything." He looked at Xanthe with mild, papery eyes. "If you notice any entries that seem... damaged, or altered, or missing — you'll bring them to me directly. Not to the registrar. Not to the archivists. To me. Some corrections are better made by one steady hand than many."

*There it is.*

Not a warning after all. An instruction. *Whatever you found, bring it to me. Let me decide what survives.*

"I understand, Master," Xanthe said.

"I knew you would." Corvane smiled again, and this time the warmth in it was precise, calibrated, a lamp turned to exactly the brightness required. "You are the best of us, Villalobos. Truly. The guild could not function without keepers like you — people with no one to miss them, no ties to pull them away, nothing in their hearts that the work might bruise." He said it as a compliment. He meant it as a compliment. That was the horror of it.

"Go home. Rest. Open the vial when you're somewhere quiet — the memory is old, and old memories can be... insistent." He turned back to his window, dismissal and blessing in a single motion. "Oh — and Villalobos?"

Xanthe paused at the door.

"The date on tonight's summons," Corvane said, without turning around. "Did it strike you as significant? Dates so often do, to people who work with records. We begin to see patterns everywhere. Providence in the numbering of days." A soft chuckle. "Superstition, of course. But do let me know if anything strikes you as *significant*."

The stair seemed longer going down. Each step had grown back to its full size, as though the guildhall itself had exhaled and released them — or as though Corvane's rooms preferred to keep their swallowed feeling to themselves.

* * *

Xanthe did not go home. Home was a single room above the vaults that contained a bed, a kettle, and no photographs, because keepers were chosen for having no family to miss them and Xanthe had long ago stopped keeping pictures of anyone, including themselves.

Instead they walked to Wren's.

She lived in the courier's wing, in a room so aggressively ordinary that visitors routinely forgot they had entered it — beige walls, a plain chair, a window facing a brick wall. Wren herself answered the door looking unsurprised, which was her native expression.

"You look terrible," she said.

"Corvane summoned me."

"Ah." She stepped aside to let them in. "Tea, then. You've got the face of a man who's been complimented by a spider."

Xanthe sat in the plain chair and put the vial on the table between them, where it cycled cold-warm-cold like a small captive heart. Wren looked at it for a long moment, and Xanthe watched something move behind her eyes — not surprise. Recognition. The same flicker Corvane had shown, and that similarity chilled Xanthe more than either alone.

"Do you know what it is?" Xanthe asked.

"Everyone knows what a memory-vial is."

"This one. Specifically."

Wren poured the tea. She took her time about it, and Xanthe understood she was taking her time deliberately, arranging her face the way couriers arranged parcels — nothing visible, everything secured.

"It's first-keeper work," she said finally. "The seal. Where did he get it?"

"He says there are nine. He gave it to me as an anniversary gift. It holds a memory of the Hollow Gate — from before the sealing. What it wanted."

Wren set down the teapot very carefully, the way one sets down something that might be listening.

"And what did you say?"

"That it was beautiful."

"Mm." She sat across from them, wrapped her hands around her own cup, and studied Xanthe with an expression that was almost fondness and almost pity. "You realize what it is, don't you? The gift."

"A bribe or a warning."

"Both. Neither." Wren blew on her tea. "It's a leash, Xanthe. A kind one. He's handed you a piece of the gate and asked you to carry it. Now you're holding something the guild wants held, and every time you look at it, you'll wonder whether you're studying it or obeying him. That's how Corvane manages people. He doesn't give orders. He gives *objects*. And the objects do the ordering."

Xanthe looked down at the vial. Cold. Warm. Cold. Like breathing. Like something on the other side of the glass, waiting patiently to be let out, or let in.

"He also asked about the prophecy ledgers," Xanthe said quietly. "Told me to report any damaged entries directly to him. Said some corrections are better made by one steady hand."

Wren went very still.

"He knows," she said.

"He suspects. Or he's fishing. I genuinely couldn't tell you which, and I've spent twenty years learning to read that man's silences."

"Then we assume he knows." Wren set her cup down. All the banter had gone out of her voice, leaving something harder underneath, something Xanthe had only glimpsed once before, the night a trader tried to buy a parcel off her route and she had refused with a flatness that had frightened everyone including the trader. "Which means the redactions aren't just history anymore. They're active. Someone is still editing."

"Corvane."

"Corvane, or whoever Corvane answers to. Because here's the thing, Xanthe—" she leaned forward, and lowered her voice although they were alone in the most forgettable room in the guildhall, "—he's old. Older than he looks, older than any record says. I've delivered parcels to his study at hours when the guildhall was empty and heard him talking to someone. No second voice. Just him, and pauses where the answers would go. Pauses shaped exactly like conversation."

Xanthe's skin prickled. "Talking to what?"

"I never stayed to find out. Couriers who stay get remembered, and couriers who get remembered get used." She glanced at the vial. "Speaking of which. Are you going to open it?"

"Not yet. Not here."

"Sensible. Old memories are insistent — his words?"

"His exact words."

Wren nodded slowly. "Then here's my advice, free of charge, courier's discount. Don't open it alone, and don't open it anywhere the walls might be listening. And Xanthe—" she caught their eye, and held it, and for a moment the unremarkable woman across the table was the most present person Xanthe had ever known, "—whatever's in that vial, whatever the gate wanted when it was young — I think you need to see it. I think that's why he gave it to you. Not to leash you. To *show* you. Corvane doesn't waste gifts. Everything he hands out is a message, and I don't think this message is 'stop.'"

"What else could it be?"

Wren looked at the vial for a long moment, and when she spoke again her voice had gone quiet in a new way — quiet the way a house goes quiet when it realizes it has been overheard.

"I think it might be 'hurry.'"

* * *

Xanthe walked back through the sleeping guildhall carrying the vial in their pocket, where it beat its slow cold-warm rhythm against their hip like a second pulse that was not quite their own.

The corridors were empty. The lamps burned low. On the landing above the vault stairs, they paused — just briefly — beside the tall window that faced east, toward the sealed wing, toward the Hollow Gate behind its three doors and its silence.

Far below, in the city, a clock struck midnight. And in their pocket, the vial went suddenly, perfectly warm — warmer than body heat, warmer than it had any right to be — as if something inside the glass had heard the hour, and recognized it, and begun, very softly, to knock.
