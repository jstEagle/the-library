## 7. The Century Gap

The deep archive did not appear on any map of the guildhall, which was fitting, because the deep archive did not entirely exist.

Xanthe had learned its approaches over six years of keeper's work: the stair behind the shelving of extinct instruments, the corridor that grew narrower until a full-grown person had to turn sideways, the door with no handle that opened only for someone carrying nothing they were unwilling to lose. Tonight they carried a lantern, a ring of copied keys, and the prophecy ledger wrapped in a cloth against their chest like an infant. They had told themselves it was routine inventory. They had told themselves that twice on the stairs and three times in the corridor, and by the time the handleless door sighed open, exhaling air that tasted of cold stone and old smoke, they had stopped believing themselves at all.

The archive breathed around them. Shelves rose into darkness overhead — not the tidy oak of the upper vaults but something older, darker, grown rather than built, as though the guild's first keepers had planted their records and let them become forest. Dust lay in drifts like snowfall in a place where no one walked. Xanthe's lantern threw their shadow long across the aisle, and the shadow seemed to walk a half-step ahead, eager.

"Right," they murmured, to no one, to the dust, to the version of themselves who had gone to bed tonight expecting nothing stranger than mislabeled vials. "Let's find out what you're hiding."

The prophecy ledger was organized by century, then decade, then year — the guild's obsessive taxonomy made even its strangest book navigable. The Hollow entries clustered in the earliest folios, written in a hand so fine and upright it looked less like handwriting than architecture. Xanthe had studied that hand for two nights now, tracing the letters of tonight's date, *the ninth of the Ash Moon, when the gate grows hungry and a name must be chosen.* They knew its habits: the way the scribe crossed their sevens, the little hook on every terminal *r*, the habit of pressing so hard the ink bled through to the next page like a bruise showing through skin.

They wanted a name for that hand. Names made things knowable. And the guild's catalogue, if it listed anything, listed everything.

The catalogue of scribes lived in a cabinet near the archive's entrance, chained shut with a lock Xanthe's keys fit — one of the small mercies of keeper's authority, granted freely and never explained. Inside, folios of copyists stretched back in ranked rows. Xanthe ran a finger down the index of the first century of the guild's existence, past names that meant nothing — *Aldous Vane, scribe; Merrit of the Low Quarter, scribe* — until they reached the founding entry, and stopped.

There was only one name for the first fifty years.

*First Keeper, who keeps this record.*

No name. Not redacted — the ink was original, confident, deliberate. Whoever wrote the prophecy ledger had declined, from the very first page, to be anyone at all. *First Keeper, who keeps this record,* repeated in the margin like a signature worn smooth from use. As if the title were enough. As if the person beneath it had already been given away.

Xanthe stood very still in the cold, and felt the vertigo begin — that slow tilting sensation, like standing at the top of a staircase in the dark and realizing there was one more step than your body expected.

The first keeper had written tonight's date a hundred years before tonight. Had written it in a book that predicted, with a keeper's precision, exactly which nights the gate would need feeding. And had signed the work with an absence.

"No," Xanthe said aloud, because saying it made it less true, or at least more bearable. "No, that's not — people don't do that. People don't erase themselves in advance."

Except, said a voice in their head that sounded uncomfortably like their own, except keepers. Except people chosen precisely because no one would miss them. Except people whose whole profession was the careful disposal of what others could not bear to keep.

The lantern flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft. Somewhere far above, through floors of stone and shelving, the guildhall went about its evening — traders laughing over brandy, apprentices hauling crates of confiscated wonders, Master Corvane presiding over all of it with his patient, terrible kindness. And underneath, at the bottom of everything, the gate waited with its mouth open, and the guild carried memories to it on a schedule written before anyone currently alive was born.

Xanthe wrapped the ledger tighter and turned deeper into the archive, toward the shelves where the guild's own institutional memory was kept — the ledgers of record, the day-books, the great run of volumes that should have shown, unbroken, every decision the Guild of Impossible Things had ever made.

They walked for a long time. The shelves changed character as they went — newer wood giving way to older, older giving way to something charred at the edges even now, a hundred years of dust unable to settle on it properly. And then Xanthe came to the gap.

It was not subtle. Whoever had done it had not bothered to disguise it, perhaps because no one was ever meant to come this deep, or perhaps because the destruction itself was the message. A span of shelving — thirty feet, forty — stood empty, and the empty shelves were scorched black, fire-scarred in patterns too regular to be accident. Burned deliberately. Burned thoroughly. And where burning hadn't reached, spines had simply been cut away, sliced off at the boards with a blade, leaving stubs like pulled teeth.

Xanthe lifted the lantern and read the shelf-labels, because the labels had been left. Of course they had been left. The guild catalogued everything, including its own amputations.

The labels ran in sequence, year by year, and every label between two dates — Xanthe counted them twice, then a third time with a finger against each one to be sure — corresponded to a volume that no longer existed. One hundred years of the guild's record. Gone. Burned or cut away, every volume, ending —

Ending on a date Xanthe knew. Every child in the city knew it, the way they knew the shape of the moon. The night the last war ended. The night the sky over the harbor burned green and the armies on both sides laid down their arms at the same hour without any order given, and no one afterward could say why, only that something had ended that was older than the war itself. The historians called it the Reconciliation. The priests called it grace. The old soldiers, the ones who never talked, called it the night the door closed.

The ledgers stopped the night the door closed.

Xanthe stood in the dark with their heart going hard and slow, like a fist knocking on a door no one answered, and understood that they were looking at the guild's founding wound. Everything before the gap belonged to a different institution — or the same institution with a different purpose. Everything after it was the guild as it now existed: quiet, respectable, brokering its forbidden bargains and keeping its impossible things. And the hundred years between — the hundred years that contained the war, and whatever the war had actually been about, and whatever act had sealed the Hollow Gate — had been fed to a fire by someone who wanted the guild's own memory to match the world's.

You cannot redact a century, Xanthe thought. It's too big. Someone would notice.

But no one noticed, because noticing required caring, and caring required proximity, and proximity to the deep archive was reserved for keepers — invisible people, unmissed people, people trained from their first day not to ask what came before.

The vertigo widened. Xanthe gripped a shelf edge and felt the char flake under their fingers, soft as burnt paper, and somewhere below the floor, below the foundations, below anything with a name, the gate stirred in its sleep and turned toward the warmth of a living mind like a flower turning toward a window.

*Come closer,* the dark suggested, in a voice that was almost Xanthe's own.

"Not yet," Xanthe whispered, and stepped back, and held the lantern high, and made themselves look at the whole gap — all hundred years of it — until the shaking stopped. This was the discipline of keepers: to look at the thing directly, once, fully, so that fear spent itself and left knowledge behind.

When it was over, they marked the shelf with a slip of paper disguised as inventory waste, tucked the prophecy ledger back under their arm, and climbed toward the ordinary world, where the lamps were lit and everyone believed the guild guarded the gate.

Everyone believed it because the alternative had been burned.

* * *

Wren had been in the guildhall nine years, and in nine years she had perfected the art of being furniture.

It was not modesty. Modesty was for people who wanted something. What Wren had was a craft, practiced daily with the seriousness other couriers gave to footwork or forgery: she dressed in colors that matched walls, spoke in a register just below notice, laughed at the right moments in other people's jokes and never started her own. Traders who could remember the coat-of-arms on a rival's seal could not remember Wren's face five minutes after she'd delivered to them. She had once stood in a room while Corvane gave orders concerning her own delivery route, and he had looked directly at her twice without seeing her.

Tonight, being unseen was the most valuable thing she owned.

She trailed Xanthe's return from the lower levels at a distance of forty paces, which was farther than it sounds in the guildhall's winding stacks, close enough to see the set of their shoulders and far enough to stay a rumor. They moved wrong, Xanthe did. Too fast for routine inventory, too careful for hurry — the walk of someone carrying a secret the way you carry a full cup across a crowded room. Wren had watched a thousand couriers carry things they shouldn't, and she knew the walk.

She also knew where Xanthe had gone. There were only so many doors in this building that opened for keeper's keys, and the deep archive was the one that changed people. She had seen it happen before. Once. Years ago. To someone else.

That memory arrived, as it always did, with its edges intact and its center missing — she remembered the corridor, the other courier's face, the way he'd come up from below white as paper and started asking questions, and then she remembered nothing at all for some days, and then he was reassigned to a coastal chapterhouse and no one mentioned him again, and neither did she, because she couldn't. The middle of the story wasn't lost the way things get lost. It was *removed*. Cut clean, like the spines off books.

Wren had spent nine years not thinking about that, the way you don't touch a bruise. Now Xanthe was walking the same road, and the bruise ached all the way through her.

She let them go and took the long way to her own narrow room, and sat on her cot with her boots on, and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the night she'd seen Xanthe reading the prophecy ledger by candlelight with that particular hunger.

One courier, years ago, asked questions, erased, gone. One keeper, now, asking questions. And Wren herself, positioned — she could admit it now, alone, in the dark, in words she'd never say aloud — positioned nearby for years, friendly, useful, *unremarkable*, in a way that had always felt slightly too easy to be accident. Who had arranged her rounds? Who had approved her posting to the vault wing? She had assumed luck. She had stopped assuming luck sometime around midnight, lying awake, and the assumption that replaced it sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

If Xanthe vanished the way the other one vanished, Wren would be the last person to have spoken with them. The last person to have seen the questions forming. Which meant either that she was next, or that she was supposed to report.

She got up, lit no lamp, and stood at her window looking down at the courtyard where the night porters stacked crates of confiscated memories for morning transfer. Below the courtyard, below the cellars, below the deep archive, the gate waited. Everyone said *guarded*. Everyone said *warded*, *sealed*, *held*. Nine years of saying it had worn the words smooth as river stones, and Wren had never once asked what was on the other side of the verb.

*Held.* Held how? Held by what? You didn't hold a door with nothing. You held it with weight. With mass. With —

With names, said the part of her that had read the marginalia over Xanthe's shoulder and pretended not to. With people who signed themselves *First Keeper, who keeps this record*, because they had nothing else left to sign with.

Wren pressed her forehead against the cold glass. In the courtyard, a porter dropped a crate and swore, and the sound of breaking glass inside it was thin and high, like a voice.

Tomorrow, she decided. Tomorrow she would find Xanthe, and she would tell them — not everything, not yet, she wasn't brave enough for everything — but enough. Enough to warn them. Enough, maybe, to stand next to them when the questions got dangerous, because someone had failed to stand next to the last one, and she had let those missing days stay missing, and nine years was long enough to carry a thing like that.

Below her window, the last porter banked the courtyard lamps, and the guildhall settled into its sleep, and far beneath it all, in the dark at the bottom of the world, something patient counted the years remaining on a promise written a century ago — and found that the count was nearly done.
