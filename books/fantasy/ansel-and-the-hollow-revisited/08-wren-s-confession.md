## 8. Wren's Confession

Wren had always been good at waiting. It was, in truth, the only talent the guild had ever officially required of her — the talent of being where she was supposed to be, at the hour she was supposed to be there, looking like nothing worth noticing. A courier who could pass through a room without disturbing the dust of it. A woman whose face slid from memory the moment she turned a corner. The traders called it a gift. The masters called it discretion. Wren had long ago stopped pretending to herself that it was anything other than what it was: she had been built, carefully and over many years, into a person the world declined to hold onto.

Which was, she thought now, standing in the shadow of the archive stairwell with Xanthe Villalobos's stolen notes burning a hole in her coat pocket, a very convenient shape for a spy to be.

The lamp at the end of the corridor guttered. Somewhere above, the guildhall settled into its night sounds — the tick of the memory-vials cooling in their racks, the slow respiration of the vaults, the far-off murmur of the Hollow Gate that no one ever admitted hearing. Wren counted to three hundred, the way she had counted a thousand times before, and told herself that this time the waiting was different. This time she was not waiting to report. This time she was waiting to confess.

Xanthe was late. That was unlike them. Xanthe was the sort of keeper who arrived at appointments early enough to have already catalogued the room twice. Wren's stomach performed a slow, cold revolution. *They've been caught. Corvane knows. And when they trace who Xanthe trusted, they will trace it to the courier nobody remembers, and I will have deserved it.*

Then footsteps — quick, soft, Xanthe's particular walk, all purpose and apology at once — and Xanthe slipped out of the dark with a lantern hooded to a coin of light, and the sight of them was such a relief that Wren nearly laughed aloud.

"You're late," she whispered.

"You're early." Xanthe's face was pale, the shadows under their eyes the color of old ink. "I had to make certain I wasn't followed. I've gotten better at telling. There's a — a quality to being watched. Like a draft from a door that isn't there."

"I know the quality," Wren said, and heard how it sounded the moment it left her mouth.

Xanthe went very still. The hooded lantern trembled slightly in their grip, throwing a shaking coin of light across the flagstones between them.

"Wren," they said carefully. "Why would you know the quality of being watched?"

*Here it is, then.* Wren had rehearsed this confession in a dozen drafts, none of which survived contact with Xanthe's actual face. She had imagined doing it in the courier's loft, over tea, with time to soften the edges. Instead it was here, in a cold corridor, with the guild's secrets stacked around them like kindling.

"Because for six years," she said, "I was the door the draft came from."

* * *

She took Xanthe up, not down — up, past the keeper's quarters, past the scriptorium with its sleeping pens, to the bell tower that hadn't held a bell in a hundred years. It was the one place in the guildhall where conversations didn't carry, because the tower's stones had been cut to swallow sound. Wren had never asked why a guild devoted to memory would build a room designed to forget words. She suspected she had never needed to ask.

Xanthe set the lantern on the sill and did not sit. They stood with their arms folded, the way they stood over a ledger entry that didn't balance, and waited. It was the waiting that undid Wren more than any anger could have. Anger she could have answered. Patience she had to simply pour herself into.

"I came to the guild when I was fourteen," she began. "You know that much. War orphan, no family, no name anyone in the city would miss. The same story as every keeper. The same story as you."

"You were recruited as a courier."

"I was recruited as a courier the way a knife is recruited as a letter-opener." Wren's laugh came out wrong. "Master Corvane interviewed me himself. I remember being flattered — *Corvane*, the Master of the Guild, spending an hour on a gutter-child. He asked me questions that seemed like nothing. What I noticed in rooms. Whether I forgot things or remembered them. Whether I'd ever wanted, badly, to be someone else." She wrapped her arms around herself. "I thought he was being kind. It took me years to understand he was taking measurements. He wasn't looking for a courier. He was looking for a shadow — someone who could stand next to a person for years without that person ever feeling the weight."

Xanthe's voice was very quiet. "Next to what person?"

"You know what person." Wren made herself meet their eyes. "You, Xanthe. I was sent to watch you."

The lantern hissed. Somewhere below, a door closed, and the tower swallowed the sound whole.

"Six years ago," Wren went on, because stopping now would have been the coward's version, and she had decided, somewhere on the stairs, that she was finished being the guild's coward. "You'd been a keeper for three years. You were good — better than good, you were the kind of keeper who *noticed*, which is exactly what keepers are not supposed to do. Corvane had a file on you thicker than the ones he kept on the traders. Your habits. Your routes through the vaults. Which ledgers you lingered over. Which nights you worked late." She swallowed. "My reports went to him once a month, in cipher, in the margins of ordinary delivery manifests. *Subject requested the prophecy folio, returned it unopened. Subject questioned the redaction stamps. Subject has begun visiting the Gate on unscheduled rounds.*"

"Six years," Xanthe said. Not a question. Their face had gone through shock and come out the other side into something colder and more careful, and Wren realized with a lurch that she had seen that face before — in the mirror, in the years after she'd understood what she was.

"Every friend you've had in this guild," she said, "has been me. And every friend I've had in this guild has been a lie I was paid to keep."

* * *

The silence that followed was long enough for the moon to move. When Xanthe finally spoke, their voice had a strange, flat steadiness, the voice of someone holding something very heavy at arm's length.

"The redactions. The dates in the prophecy ledger. When did you first know it wasn't routine?"

Wren hesitated — and then understood that hesitation, now, was just another lie, and hated it.

"The night of the Ashfall inventory. Four years ago." She turned away, looking out the empty bell-frame at the city, at the far black line of the Hollow Gate district where no lamps were ever lit. "I was carrying a manifest to the deep archive and I passed the redaction room. The door was open a hand's width. Corvane was standing over a ledger with a keeper's stylus — the authority-stylus, the one that doesn't just cross out a line but *unwrites* it, so the ink forgets what it ever said. And there was a name on the page. Not a date, not a transaction. A name. And beneath it, in Corvane's hand, a single word: *delivered*."

"Delivered," Xanthe repeated. "Delivered where?"

Wren's throat closed. She made herself say it anyway, because the whole point of climbing this tower was to stop carrying it alone.

"To the Gate, Xanthe. The guild doesn't guard the Hollow Gate. That's the founding story they tell every orphan who walks through the door — *we are the lock-wardens, the watchmen, the ones who keep the impossible things contained*. It's a story. It's the story. But it's a lie." She turned back to face them. "The guild feeds it. It has always fed it. Every redaction, every erased keeper, every *delivered* name — they aren't cleaning the records. They're setting a table. The Gate doesn't eat memories the way a fire eats wood. It eats *forgotten truths*. It eats the things people knew and had taken from them. And a memory that's merely lost will feed it for a season. But a person — a whole person, willingly enrolled, erased root and branch, every record unwritten, every name struck from every ledger — a person feeds it for a decade. Sometimes more."

Xanthe had gone white. "The keepers who vanish. The ones they tell us *resigned*. The ones whose bunks are empty by morning and whose names are already gone from the duty rosters—"

"Delivered," Wren said. "One at a time, on the dates the prophecy ledger marks. Corvane's whole office, the whole machinery of the guild, is a feeding schedule. A hundred years of it. Maybe longer." Her voice cracked and she let it. "I've been carrying manifests for that machine for six years. I've carried names, Xanthe. I just didn't know they were names."

"You didn't know." Xanthe said it without accusation, which was somehow worse. "Or you chose not to?"

"I chose not to." Wren's eyes stung. "There's a difference and there isn't. I told myself a courier's job is to carry, not to open. I told myself the guild must have reasons, that Corvane is hard but fair, that a girl with no family and no name should be grateful for a warm bunk and a purpose. I was *grateful*, Xanthe. That's the filthiest part. They made a spy out of me and I thanked them for the work."

The wind moved through the empty bell-frame, and for a moment Wren thought she heard, beneath it, the far murmur of the Gate — that low, patient sound like a tide deciding.

Then Xanthe crossed the tower and, to Wren's complete astonishment, took her hands.

"Six years of reports," they said, "and in all that time, how many times did you lie in them?"

Wren blinked. "What?"

"You heard me. In six years of ciphered margins — how many times did you write something that wasn't true?"

The question turned over in her chest like a key in a lock she hadn't known was there. She thought of the manifests. The little omissions. The nights she had written *subject retired early* when Xanthe had worked till dawn; the times she had written *no irregularities* when there had been nothing but irregularities; the month she had reported that Xanthe had returned the prophecy folio *unopened*, when she herself had seen the crease of a turned page.

"More times than I reported," she said slowly. "I stopped counting after the second year. I told myself it was sloppiness. That I was a bad spy." She laughed, wet and unsteady. "I was a bad spy on purpose. I just never let myself know it."

"Then here is what I know," Xanthe said. Their grip on her hands was firm, and their eyes — tired, frightened, and absolutely awake — held hers without mercy or blame. "You could have let me walk into Corvane's office tonight blind. Instead you brought me here and handed me the truth at the cost of your own neck. Whatever you were built to be, Wren, you've spent six years quietly refusing to be it. That's not a lie I can't forgive. That's the truest thing anyone in this guild has ever done for me."

Wren hadn't known she was crying until the tower's sound-swallowing stones made her sobs feel strangely distant, like someone else's grief heard through a wall. She let them come anyway. Six years of careful, unremarkable, invisible composure, and it broke all at once, and Xanthe stood there and held her hands through all of it, and did not let go.

* * *

Afterward, they sat on the tower floor with their backs against the cold stone, sharing the lantern's coin of light, and it was Xanthe who finally said what both of them had been circling.

"The dates in the prophecy ledger. The ones that match the redactions. They stop at the end of this month." A pause. "There's one more entry. And it's dated for three nights from now, and the margin word isn't *delivered*." Xanthe's voice dropped. "It's *final*."

Wren felt the words land somewhere beneath her ribs. "Final how?"

"I don't know yet. But I found something else tonight, before I came to you. In the deep archive, where the oldest ledgers are kept — there's a gap. A century of records, burned or missing, ending the night the last war ended. And the prophecy book was written by the first keeper, Wren. The *first* one. Which means whatever the guild has been feeding the Gate — whatever *final* means — it was all decided before any of us were born, by someone who knew how the war actually started." Xanthe pulled a folded page from their coat — the stolen notes, the ones that had been burning in Wren's pocket all evening — and smoothed it on the floor between them. "There's more. The redactions have a pattern. Every erased name, every *delivered* keeper — I think they're all hidden somewhere. I think the first keeper hid them. In the prophecy text itself, in the margins, in the ink. And I think if we can find them—"

The lantern flame stood up straight, all at once, as though something in the room had drawn a sharp breath.

Neither of them moved. The tower's stones, which swallowed every sound, swallowed nothing at all now — because the sound that came next came from *outside* them, rising through the guildhall's foundations, through the bedrock, through a hundred years of patient hunger:

The Gate, three nights early, had begun to stir.

And in the shaking coin of lantern light, on the page between them, the ink of Xanthe's own handwriting began, letter by letter, to fade.
