## 9. Names in the Margin

The prophecy book had followed Xanthe home.

Not literally — it sat where they had left it, on the reading table in the keeper's alcove beneath the archive stairs, its leather cover breathing that faint smell of cold iron and old smoke. But it had followed them in every way that mattered. All through the long walk back from the deep archive, all through the supper they'd forgotten to taste, all through the hour they'd spent staring at the ceiling of their narrow cell while the guildhall settled around them like an old animal folding itself to sleep — the book had been there, patient as a held breath.

*The Century Gap.* That was what Wren had called it, and the name fit. A hundred years of ledgers burned or vanished, ending on the very night the last war ended, and a prophecy book written by the first keeper sitting at the center of the hole like a stone dropped down a well. Xanthe had spent three days confirming what they already knew: the dates matched. Every date inscribed in the book's margins corresponded to a night when someone in the guild's records had been quietly, thoroughly unmade. Redacted by a hand with keeper's authority. Erased one person at a time, like coins fed into a slot.

Tonight was written there too. *Tomorrow,* strictly speaking — the ledger marked it for the turn of the moon, two nights hence — but close enough. Close enough that Xanthe's hands had not been steady since they found it.

Now, past midnight, with a single lamp burning and the archive's silence pressing against the walls like deep water, Xanthe opened the book again.

They had read the prophecy text a dozen times by now. It was strange stuff — not the thundering verse of street prophets or the careful riddling of court oracles, but something plainer and more unsettling. Sentences that seemed almost conversational, as though the first keeper had been writing letters to someone she expected to arrive long after her death.

*When the gate grows thin, feed it what is freely forgotten.*

*A name given is worth a hundred taken.*

*The keepers will know their own, for I have written them where only the patient will look.*

Xanthe had always taken that last line for ornament. Prophecy loved to promise hidden knowledge; it was how prophecies survived, by flattering every reader into feeling chosen. But tonight, with nothing left to lose but sleep, Xanthe decided to be patient.

They began with the ink.

Guild ink was a craft in itself. Keeper's black was made from oak galls and vitriol; scribe's brown from walnut husks; the red used for corrections came from crushed scale-insects and faded toward rust over decades. The first keeper's text was black, uniform, confident. But under the lamp, tilted to catch the light at a low angle, Xanthe saw what they had somehow never seen before: texture. The black was not entirely black. Along certain strokes, in certain words, the ink sat differently on the page — a hair thicker, a shade deeper, as if those particular letters had been written twice.

Or as if something else had been written *underneath* them.

Xanthe fetched the keeper's lens from its drawer — a disc of polished crystal in a silver frame, older than the guildhall itself, used for authenticating seals. Held above the page, it did something no honest lens should do. It separated. The topmost layer of ink stayed sharp and dark, and beneath it, faint as breath on glass, a second layer of writing emerged.

Not a second text. The same text, shifted. The first keeper had written her prophecy twice, once in plain characters and once in a cipher laid beneath them, each letter of the hidden version tucked inside the body of a visible one. A palimpsest in miniature, folded into the very bones of the words.

Xanthe's mouth had gone dry. They reached for paper, for a pencil, and began the slow, trembling work of lifting the hidden letters out one by one.

The cipher yielded faster than they expected. It was old — older than the guild's current notation — but the Guild of Impossible Things kept a museum of dead scripts in its lower stacks, and Xanthe had catalogued most of them during their first lonely year, when inventory duty meant handling objects no one else would touch. This was a merchant's hand-cipher from before the last war, the kind traders once used for prices they didn't want customers to read. Which meant the first keeper had learned it from the people she would later help imprison behind the Hollow Gate. Or perhaps she had been one of them.

The first decoded line read:

*ELIRA VOSSE, KEEPER THE THIRD, GIVEN WILLINGLY, YEAR OF ASHES*

Xanthe stared at it for a long time.

Then they turned the page, and the lamp seemed to dim, and they understood what they were holding.

Every page carried a name.

*MAURETH KELL, KEEPER THE FIFTH, TAKEN UNWILLINGLY, HIS NAME STILL BLEEDS*

*ISEULT FARROW, WHO CHOSE THE GATE OVER THE WAR*

*TAM HOLLOWELL, WHO WAS NOT ASKED*

Page after page after page. Some entries were a single line; some ran to five or six, with small annotations in the same buried cipher — notes of grief, of guilt, of instruction. *She sang as she went in. Remember that she sang.* *He fought us. We should have let him fight.* *Do not tell the family. There is no family. That is why we chose her.* The marginalia was a graveyard with the dirt brushed away, and Xanthe knelt in it, decoding name after name, their pencil scratching in the silence like a small animal trying to dig out.

There were forty-one names. Xanthe counted twice, then a third time, because the number mattered — because somewhere in the guild's founding records there ought to be a roster of keepers, and Xanthe knew, now, why they had never found one. The roster *was* this book. The guild had never recorded its erased keepers anywhere else. Why would it? The whole architecture of the thing depended on forgetting. A name written elsewhere was a name the gate might still hunger for; a name written here, folded inside a prophecy no one read, was a name safely digested.

Except it wasn't safe. That was the horror of it, the beautiful terrible joke of it. The gate ate memories — took them from living minds, from records, from the world's collective attention — but it could not eat *this*. Ink pressed into ink, meaning folded inside meaning, waiting a century for one patient reader. The first keeper had built a vault inside a vault. She had known exactly what the guild would become, and she had left the receipts anyway.

*So that someday,* Xanthe thought, *someone would count.*

They worked until the candle guttered, then lit another, then another. Their eyes ached. Their back had locked into a curve they would feel for days. None of it mattered. Each name lifted out of the page felt like raising a drowned body from deep water — heavy, cold, and owed something. *Tam Hollowell, who was not asked.* Xanthe wrote his name on their own paper in full view, deliberately, defiantly, as if visibility were a kind of burial rite. Then the next. Then the next.

Somewhere past the thirtieth name, the pattern changed.

The later entries grew shorter. Colder. Where the early keepers had been mourned — *remembered*, even in cipher, even in hiding — the recent ones were logged like deliveries. *Given. Sealed. Effective.* And the handwriting underneath had changed too; Xanthe could see it now that they knew what to look for. The first keeper's buried script was fluid, anguished, alive. The later entries were neat, controlled, unhurried. Written by someone who had done this many times and no longer wept over the page.

Written, Xanthe realized with a chill that started in the spine and spread outward, by someone still alive. Still working. Still feeding the gate on schedule, century after century, keeping the accounts balanced.

Master Corvane had said, in his warm study with its fire and its rare memory-vial, that he valued discretion. He had asked, so gently, so kindly, what Xanthe had been reading.

*He knew,* Xanthe thought. *He has always known. He wrote half of these names himself.*

* * *

Dawn came grey through the archive's high slit-windows, and Xanthe did not notice it until the lamp's flame looked suddenly weak and yellow against the growing light. They sat back. Their neck cracked when they moved. Before them lay eleven sheets of paper covered in recovered names, dates, and fragments of the first keeper's buried grief — a complete accounting, or nearly one.

Nearly. Because the book was not finished.

Xanthe had noticed it hours ago and refused, with a cowardice they were not proud of, to think about it. The cipher continued past the last historical entry. The final pages of the prophecy — the ones describing the gate's end, the choice of keepers, the sealing — carried buried writing too. Fresh-looking writing, though "fresh" was a strange word for ink a century old. As if the first keeper had written not only the past but the shape of things to come, leaving blank lines in the cipher for names yet to be entered.

And someone had filled them in.

Xanthe turned to the last leaf. The visible text read, in the first keeper's plain voice:

*When the hollow door stands at its thinnest, one shall stand before it who knows the whole truth. To that one the choice: the lie exposed, and the war reborn — or the name surrendered, and the door shut forever.*

Beneath it, in the buried cipher, in that later, neater hand:

*THE CHOSEN IS CHOSEN ALREADY. WRITTEN BEFORE HER BIRTH. THE LEDGER DOES NOT PREDICT. IT APPOINTS.*

And below that, in smaller characters, cramped against the bottom edge of the page as if the writer had hesitated to give it room:

*XANTHE VILLALOBOS*

The world went very quiet.

Xanthe heard their own heartbeat, absurdly loud, counting seconds in the silence. They heard the archive breathing — that vast settling exhalation of old paper and older stone. They heard, or imagined they heard, far below and far away, the Hollow Gate turning over in its sleep.

Their own name. In a book written a hundred years before their birth. In a cipher no one was meant to read, entered by a hand that had been feeding people to a door since before the guildhall's foundations were poured.

*It doesn't predict,* the buried line said. *It appoints.* And Xanthe understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, what that meant. Not fate. Bookkeeping. Corvane — or whatever Corvane was, whatever the gate had made of him — had chosen them years ago. Had recruited them, trained them, praised their discretion, gifted them a memory-vial worth a season's wages. Had cultivated them the way one cultivates anything meant for harvest. Every kindness had been an entry in a ledger. Every lonely year in the vaults, every reason they'd been chosen — *no family to miss them*, the recruiters always said, smiling — had been preparation for this page.

Terror arrived first. It came up through the floor, through the chair, through the bones of their hands, and Xanthe let it come, because fighting it would only make it worse. They let it shake them. They let their vision blur. They sat in the grey light and trembled like a struck bell until the ringing faded.

And then, in the space the terror left behind, something else settled in. Something quieter and harder.

Anger.

Not the hot kind. The cold kind — the kind that takes inventory. Xanthe looked down at their eleven sheets of paper, at forty-one names raised from the dark, at the neat monstrous handwriting of the thing that had appointed them, and felt the anger organize itself into a plan the way water organizes itself into a channel.

They would not run. Running was what the erased were for; running confirmed the premise. They would not burn the book, though their hands ached to — because the book was the only proof, the only grave-marker, the only voice those forty-one had left, and burning it would finish what the gate had started. And they would not, whatever else happened, surrender quietly on schedule. If the ledger had appointed them, then the ledger could be *edited*. The first keeper had proven that much: ink could hide inside ink, truth could survive inside the machinery built to destroy it.

But they could not do it alone. The thought arrived without drama, plain as arithmetic. One keeper with a stack of stolen transcriptions was a problem to be tidied away — a week of memories shaved off, a quiet vacancy in the vaults, another neat entry in another neat hand. They needed someone the guild overlooked. Someone whose talent was being unremarkable. Someone who could walk through any door in the guildhall carrying anything at all, and be seen as furniture.

Someone named Wren.

Who had helped them trace the redactions without being asked. Who had known, that night in the courier's loft, exactly which shelf the missing ledgers had *not* fallen from. Who had gone pale — Xanthe remembered it now, replaying it with new eyes — pale and watchful when Xanthe had joked about the gate whispering. Who knew more than she admitted, and had chosen, for reasons of her own, to keep helping anyway.

Trust was a currency the guild had never taught Xanthe to spend. They spent it now, all of it, in a single decision.

They gathered the papers. They wrapped the prophecy book in a record-cloth and slid it into the false-bottomed document case they used for transporting fragile items — a keeper's habit that had finally, after years of tedium, become useful. They pinched out the lamp. And they climbed the archive stairs into the waking guildhall, past clerks unlocking their desks, past the smell of bread from the refectory, carrying a hundred years of murdered names against their chest like a coal that would not cool.

They found Wren where they knew they would find her: in the courier's hall, sorting the morning rounds, utterly unremarkable among two dozen equally forgettable grey uniforms. She glanced up, saw Xanthe's face, and set down the letters she was holding.

"That bad?" she said quietly.

"Worse." Xanthe kept their voice level, their expression fixed in the mild blankness keepers wore like a second livery. Around them the hall went on clattering and murmuring, oblivious. "There are names in the margin. Forty-one of them. Keepers the gate ate, written in cipher where no one was supposed to look." They paused. Drew breath. Said the rest fast, before courage failed. "And mine. On the last page. Written a hundred years ago."

Wren had gone very still. Not shocked — Xanthe noted that, filed it away — *not shocked*. Still, the way a person goes still when a long-expected blow finally lands.

"Meet me tonight," Wren said. "The old stairwell, after second bell. There are things you need to hear, and—" Her eyes flicked, quick and practiced, to a figure crossing the far end of the hall: a tall shape in trading silks, elegant as a drawn blade, moving through the crowd of couriers without touching any of them. Madame Ossory's chief factor. Xanthe had seen him twice before and both times had felt watched. "—and not here," Wren finished. "Xanthe. Whatever you do today, don't let them see the book."

"Them?"

Wren's jaw tightened. "The traders aren't the only ones who've read the prophecy," she said. "They're just the only ones who liked what it promised."

Across the hall, the factor in silk turned his head — slowly, deliberately — and looked directly at Xanthe. He smiled the way a ledger balances.

And in Xanthe's pocket, against their ribs, the prophecy book seemed, for one impossible moment, to grow warm.
