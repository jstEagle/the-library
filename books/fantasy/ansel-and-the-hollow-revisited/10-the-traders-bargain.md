## 10. The Traders' Bargain

The invitation came the way dangerous things always came to Xanthe Villalobos: quietly, folded inside something ordinary.

It was tucked into the return slot of the vault's lending ledger — a slip of grey paper, heavy as moth wings, bearing a single line in ink that shifted color when Xanthe tilted it toward the lamp. *The Amber Room, ninth bell. Madame Ossory requests the pleasure of your company.* No seal. No signature beyond the name itself, written in a hand so confident it seemed to have signed things for centuries and expected to sign them for centuries more.

Xanthe stood in the cold breath of the vaults for a long moment, the slip pinched between two fingers like something venomous.

They knew what the Amber Room was. Everyone at the guild knew, in the way everyone knows about rooms they are never meant to enter — that it sat on the third floor of the east wing behind a door of blackened oak, that traders met there to broker bargains the guild officially did not permit and unofficially could not survive without, that no keeper had ever been invited inside. Keepers were furniture. Keepers were the hands that shelved the impossible and asked nothing about where it came from or who had paid for it.

And yet here was an invitation, addressed to *them*, in ink that remembered how to be gold.

*You've been reading,* the invitation might as well have said. *We've noticed.*

Xanthe thought of Wren's face when she'd confessed — the guilt worn thin over something fiercer, the way her hands had shaken around the cup of tea she never drank. *The guild doesn't guard the gate,* she'd said. *It feeds it.* And Xanthe had spent three nights since then with the prophecy book open on their knees, tracing marginalia by candle stub, finding names stitched into acrostics and reversed lines, dozens of them, generations of erased keepers hidden in plain text like bones buried under a garden.

They should have burned the invitation. They should have taken it to Corvane, laid it on his desk of dark walnut, and watched his ancient face for whatever flickered there.

Instead, at the ninth bell, Xanthe climbed the east stairs.

* * *

The door of blackened oak opened before they knocked.

Beyond it, the Amber Room earned its name. Every surface held light and gave it back changed — walls paneled in honey-colored wood that seemed lit from within, lamps shaded in amber glass that turned even the rain streaking the windows into falling gold. The air smelled of beeswax and something older underneath, something like the inside of a sealed jar.

Madame Ossory sat in a chair by the window, and Xanthe understood immediately why people spoke of her the way they spoke of weather — with respect, with dread, and with the assumption that she would arrive regardless of anyone's preferences.

She was not beautiful, exactly. She was *finished*. Silver hair pinned in a coil so precise it looked architectural, a gown of deep green that made the amber light do flattering work, hands folded in her lap like two sleeping birds. Her face had the smoothness of someone who had decided, long ago, exactly which expressions she would permit herself, and had practiced them until they were flawless.

"Keeper Villalobos." She did not rise. Rising would have been deference, and Ossory did not deal in deference; she dealt in hospitality, which was a different currency entirely. "Thank you for coming. I confess I wasn't certain you would. Curiosity is rarer than courage, in my experience, and considerably more useful."

"You could simply have summoned me," Xanthe said. "Corvane does."

A smile touched her mouth — permitted, practiced, warm as a hearth seen through a window from a snowy street. "Master Corvane summons servants. I invite colleagues." She gestured to the chair across from her. A second chair, Xanthe noticed, set at a conversational distance, as though this meeting had been arranged before Xanthe had ever found the date in the prophecy book. Perhaps before Xanthe had ever been born. "Please. Sit. May I offer you anything? The guild's cellars hold wines older than its charter."

"No, thank you."

"Wise. Never drink at a bargain." Her eyes — pale, the color of weak tea held up to lamplight — moved over Xanthe's face with the unhurried attention of an appraiser. "You've been in the deep archive."

It was not a question, so Xanthe did not answer it.

"The century gap," Ossory continued pleasantly. "The burned ledgers. The first keeper's book, with all those clever little names hiding in its margins." She unfolded her hands and laced her fingers, tilting her head. "I'll admit, when word reached me that a keeper was asking questions, I assumed Corvane would snuff you out within the week. He's efficient that way. But here you are, unredacted, invited to my room instead. Which tells me either he's grown sentimental—" her tone made clear what she thought of that possibility, "—or he hasn't yet realized how much you know. Either way, it gives us a window. I do love a window."

"Us," Xanthe repeated.

"I'm getting ahead of myself. Forgive me. When one has waited a very long time for something, one tends to sprint once the door finally opens." She rose then — fluidly, without hurry — and crossed to a cabinet of amber glass. From it she took a flat object wrapped in grey silk, and Xanthe's chest tightened before she even unwrapped it, because keepers learn to recognize the shape of a book through any covering, the way mothers learn footsteps.

She set it on the table between them. Did not open it. Simply rested her fingertips on the silk.

"Do you know what the Hollow Gate actually is, Keeper? Not the story Corvane tells his novices — the great seal, the necessary wound, the price of peace. I mean the truth. You've seen the threshold-dream, haven't you?" Something flickered across her permitted expressions, quick and genuine: sympathy. It was worse than any threat. "Yes. I can see it in your face. You've met them. The ones inside."

"They were people," Xanthe said quietly. "The impossible things. They were people, and the guild traded them, transformed them, and locked away what was left when they grieved."

"And the gate is their grief," Ossory finished softly. "Their grief, given walls. Given a hunger. Fed, year after year, century after century, on stolen memories and erased keepers, so that a handful of comfortable men can go on calling a prison a peace treaty." She unwrapped the silk.

The book beneath was old — older than the prophecy ledger, older than the guildhall's foundations, bound in leather gone the color of dried blood. But it was not the book's age that made Xanthe's breath catch. It was the fact that Xanthe recognized it. Had catalogued it, years ago, in the vault's outer stacks. *Ledger, blank, provenance unknown, refuse all requests.*

"This is a blank ledger," Xanthe said. "One of the gate-books."

"It was blank." Ossory opened it, and pages whispered past — filled pages, dense with writing in a hundred different hands, some careful, some frantic, some barely legible with age. "Everything that goes into a gate-book becomes true, Keeper. That's their purpose. That's why the guild hoards them and lets none be used. A record made in these pages isn't a memory of a thing. It's the thing itself, pinned like a moth." She turned the book toward Xanthe. "This one has been written in by every soul the gate has swallowed since the war ended. Their testimonies. Their names. Their *truths* — the forgotten truths the gate feeds on, gathered and preserved by the people who loved them enough to write them down."

Xanthe looked at the crowded pages and felt the vertigo of scale again, the same sickness that had come over them in the archive among the burned ledgers. "Why show me this?"

"Because you're going to help me finish it."

* * *

Ossory returned to her chair and sat, folding her hands again, and when she spoke it was in the reasonable voice of someone laying out terms — not threatening, not pleading, merely *clear*.

"The gate is failing. You know this better than anyone now — you've walked the maintenance rounds, you've seen the fresh erosion where a century of memories should be holding firm. Corvane's answer is to feed it faster. Another keeper. Another name. Another willing fool, when the time comes, to walk up to that door and let it drink them dry." Her tea-pale eyes held Xanthe's. "He will ask it of you, you know. If he hasn't begun asking already. You've been chosen — perhaps since before you were recruited, perhaps since the day you arrived with no family to miss you and no history worth preserving. That's what keepers are, Villalobos. Not guardians. *Provisions.*"

The words landed where they were aimed. Xanthe thought of the recruitment notice they'd answered at nineteen, the interview where the guildmaster had asked almost nothing about their skills and everything about their attachments. They had thought, all these years, that they had been chosen for discretion. For care. For the particular tenderness they brought to impossible objects.

They had been chosen the way one chooses a cut of meat.

"I'm not offering you flattery," Ossory went on. "I'm offering you arithmetic. Corvane's way buys another hundred years of silence and costs a person — a whole person, their name, their loves, every trace of them in every mind that holds them. And in a hundred years, another keeper. And another. Forever, if forever can be purchased in installments. My way ends the account."

"Your way opens the gate."

"My way opens the gate," she agreed, without flinching. "And yes — the old powers wake. The transformed ones, the traded ones, the ones your guild prefers to call *things*. Some of them will be angry. Some of them will be terrible. I won't pretend otherwise; pretense is Corvane's trade, not mine." She leaned forward, and for the first time the perfection of her composure cracked along a hairline, and beneath it Xanthe saw something raw and ancient and unbearably tired. "But some of them, Keeper, have been screaming behind that door for four hundred years. Some of them were children when your founders traded them. Some of them don't remember being children anymore, because the guild fed even that to the gate. Opening it is not mercy the way a gift is mercy. It's mercy the way pulling a splinter out is mercy. It hurts. It bleeds. And then, at last, it heals."

Silence filled the Amber Room, thick as honey.

Xanthe looked down at the gate-book, at the crowded testimony of the swallowed, and felt the pull of it — genuinely felt it, and hated themselves for feeling it. Because Ossory was right about the horror. Because Ossory was right about Corvane. Because every argument Xanthe had assembled against her in the stairwell crumbled on contact with her sincerity, and what remained was only the question Xanthe had been circling for days:

Was a stalemate built on sacrificed people worth defending?

"You want the prophecy book," Xanthe said at last. "That's the exchange. Protection — from Corvane, from redaction — in exchange for the first keeper's ledger."

"Protection, position, and purpose." Ossory counted them off gently. "Protection: my people watch you; no one touches your mind again. Position: when the gate opens, the new order will need archivists who understand what was done and why — people who can tell the truth of it. Purpose:" she tapped the gate-book, "you finish this record. Every name in the margins of the prophecy book, restored. Every erased keeper, remembered. You of all people must feel the wrongness of it, Villalobos — you spend your life keeping things that no one else remembers exist. Imagine giving that back to them. Imagine being the one who wrote the swallowed world back into existence."

And oh, it was temptation. It was temptation wearing a friendly face, pouring tea it didn't offer, speaking in Xanthe's own private language of preservation and care. For one suspended moment Xanthe saw it whole: the gate opening, the grief-thing dissolving into ten thousand freed souls, the ledgers rewritten, the guild's founding lie dragged into daylight and burned —

— and beneath the vision, quieter, the other thing. The thing Ossory's permitted expressions did not cover.

"Who wrote the early entries?" Xanthe asked. "In the gate-book. Before the war ended."

Ossory's pause was small. A fraction of a second. But Xanthe had spent years reading the silences between entries in thousand-year-old ledgers, and a fraction of a second from Madame Ossory was a shout.

"The first keeper," Ossory said. "Among others."

"The first keeper who wrote the prophecy. Who designed the feeding schedule. Whose apprentice is still alive and still running this guild." Xanthe kept their voice level, though their pulse was loud in their ears. "The same hand, Madame?"

Another pause. Longer.

"You've read the marginalia," Ossory said. "Then you already know the first keeper's name appears in the gate-book too. As an author." Her smile returned, seamless, but it had changed temperature. "History is written by survivors, Keeper. Even ours."

*Even ours.* As if Ossory had been there. As if—

The thought arrived whole and cold: *How old is she?*

Xanthe stood. "I need to think."

"Of course." Ossory rose too, graciousness restored, and slid the gate-book across the table. "Take it."

Xanthe stared at it. "You're giving me the one thing you demanded in exchange?"

"I'm giving you a loan," Ossory corrected softly. "Read it. All of it. Read what the swallowed wrote about the door, about the hunger, about what waits on the other side of it. Then bring it back to me, and tell me whether Corvane's forever is truly kinder than my morning." She extended her hand — permitted expression number four, the handshake of equals — and after a moment Xanthe took it. Her grip was cool and dry and strong as a lock.

"One condition," Xanthe said.

"Name it."

"If I decide against you — if I decide the gate stays shut — you let me walk away. No traders in the stacks. No knives in the dark."

Something moved behind the tea-pale eyes, brief and unreadable as a fish under ice. "Keeper," Ossory said warmly, "if you decide against me, I suspect you'll already have found a third option none of us has imagined. People who read as carefully as you do usually do." She released Xanthe's hand and stepped back, the amber light pooling around her like something poured. "Ninth bell, three nights hence. Bring the book. Or don't, and bring me your refusal, and we shall part as friends regardless."

The door of blackened oak closed behind Xanthe with a sound like a held breath released.

* * *

The east wing corridor was dark, and Xanthe stood in it longer than was safe, the gate-book heavy against their ribs, wrapped again in its grey silk.

Three nights. Three nights to read the testimonies of the swallowed. Three nights to weigh Ossory's arithmetic against Corvane's forever. Three nights to find the flaw in an argument that had no visible flaws — because that was the thing about temptation wearing a friendly face; it didn't lie to you. It told you the truth, all of it, the parts that hurt most, and then it handed you the knife and called it a choice.

Xanthe turned toward the vaults — and stopped.

At the far end of the corridor, where the lamplight failed, a figure detached itself from the shadow of a pillar. Small. Unremarkable. Moving with the practiced ease of someone whose entire talent was going unnoticed.

Wren.

She didn't speak. She only looked at Xanthe, then pointedly at the bundle under their arm, then raised one eyebrow with the eloquence of a full paragraph — and jerked her chin toward the stairs leading down, toward the vaults, toward wherever one went when one had just made a bargain with Madame Ossory and needed, very badly, to tell someone about it before the telling became impossible.

Xanthe exhaled, and followed her into the dark.
