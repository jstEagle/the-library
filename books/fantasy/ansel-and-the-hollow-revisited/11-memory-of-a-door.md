## 11. Memory of a Door

Sleep did not come to Xanthe so much as it was taken.

They had gone to their narrow room above the vaults with the prophecy ledger's marginalia still burning behind their eyes — *their own name*, small and patient in the ink, waiting on the final page like a seat kept warm at table. They had blown out the candle. They had lain down in their clothes. They had thought, absurdly, *I will simply not dream tonight*, as though dreaming were a door one could lock from the inside.

The Hollow Gate had other ideas.

Xanthe knew immediately that they were dreaming, because the floor of their room had become stone, and the stone had become a road, and the road ran downhill through a city that did not exist. It was built of pale timber and dark brick, its roofs thatched with reeds that whispered in a wind carrying the smell of salt and woodsmoke and something sweeter beneath — crushed flowers, perhaps, or blood left too long in the sun. Lanterns hung from every eave, though none of them were lit. The light came from somewhere else: a low, sourceless glow along the horizon, the color of a candle seen through milk.

*This is the threshold,* Xanthe thought, and the thought arrived with the strange double-weight of dreams, as if someone else were thinking it alongside them. *This is what the gate keeps.*

They walked. Their feet made no sound on the road, but every window they passed reflected them faithfully — and in each reflection, they looked slightly different. Younger, then older. Then not themselves at all: a woman with iron-grey braids, a boy with a hare's alertness in his face, an old man whose skin was mapped with scars in the shape of writing. Xanthe understood, in the way one understands things in dreams without being told, that these were not disguises. They were *residue*. The road remembered everyone who had ever walked it toward the gate, and it was showing Xanthe the toll it had collected.

"You shouldn't be here," said a voice, and Xanthe turned.

Wren stood at a corner ahead, leaning against a lamp post with her arms crossed, exactly as she leaned against everything — doorframes, carts, the rail of the guildhall stair — as though standing squarely in the world had never once appealed to her. But this was not quite Wren. Her outline shimmered at the edges, like heat above a forge, and when she spoke, two voices came out at once, hers and another, older, layered beneath like a second hand of writing under a palimpsest.

"You're not Wren," Xanthe said.

"No," the figure agreed, with Wren's crooked half-smile worn like a borrowed coat. "But she leaves herself everywhere in you. It was the easiest shape to wear." The smile faded. "Listen quickly. The gate is opening its memory to you because you opened yours to it — you read the names, you spoke them aloud, you made yourself legible. That has consequences. What you see tonight, you cannot unsee. What you feel—"

"Will I be harmed?"

The figure laughed, and the laugh was terrible, because underneath Wren's dry amusement there was something vast and grieving, an ocean under a floorboard. "Harmed. Oh, keeper. Harm is the smallest thing that can happen to a person here."

* * *

The city dissolved around them like sugar in water, and Xanthe fell upward into the past.

They stood in sunlight now — real sunlight, gold and generous — in a market square in a city older than any Xanthe knew. Banners snapped overhead bearing sigils the guild had never catalogued: a tree with roots for branches; a fish crowned in silver; a circle divided down its middle, half light and half dark, stitched in thread that seemed to move. The market was full. And the market was full of *impossible things*.

A woman at a stall sold bottled voices — she uncorked one and a lullaby poured out, three notes of it, before she stoppered it again and named a price. Beside her, a man displayed caged birds that sang tomorrow's weather. A child ran past with a kite that flew against the wind, tugging her along, both of them laughing. In the square's center, an auctioneer stood on a wagon, and around the wagon the crowd was thickest, because on the auctioneer's platform, in a cage of brass and silence, sat a young man who was slowly, gracefully turning to smoke and back again, over and over, his face composed into the blank patience of someone who had learned that screaming changed nothing about the transaction.

Xanthe's stomach turned over. "No," they said aloud, and the market did not hear them. "No, those are objects. The guild catalogue says—"

"The guild catalogue lies," said the Wren-shaped voice beside them, gently. "It has always lied. That is the founding lie. You guessed it already; you only lacked the courage to believe your own guess. Look closer, keeper. Look at what they are selling."

Xanthe looked, and this time they saw.

The bottled voices were not bottled. The woman selling them had a mouth sewn shut with silver thread, and the lullaby in the bottle was sung in *her* voice — the pitch of it, the little catch on the second note. The weather-birds had been people once; you could see it in their eyes, which held a human terror far too large for their small feathered skulls. The child's kite was not cloth. It caught the light the way skin catches light, and it strained against the wind the way a throat strains against a hand.

And the young man who became smoke — Xanthe walked to the platform, though their feet were dream-feet and made no sound, and looked into the cage, and the young man looked back. His eyes were brown. Ordinary brown. The eyes of a neighbor, a brother, a boy one lane over whom you'd lent an apple to and never thought of again.

"They called it transformation," the voice said. "It was considered a craft. Some few were born with the gift of unmaking a person into a useful thing — a voice, a wing, a lamp that burns without oil, a door that opens onto anywhere. The transformed retained everything. Every thought. Every yearning. Do you understand? The kite feels the wind. The bird knows it used to have hands. The lamp burns, and inside the flame, someone remembers lying in the sun."

The auctioneer raised his gavel. The crowd murmured its pleasant murmur. Somewhere a fiddler played, and the tune was merry, and Xanthe wanted to shatter every window in the golden city, and could do nothing at all, because they were only a memory walking through a memory, a ghost haunting the gate's grief.

"Who were they?" Xanthe whispered. "The transformers. Who had the gift?"

"Kings bought it," the voice said. "Guilds hoarded it. Families married into it the way one marries into land." A pause, and the two-voiced sorrow deepened. "And when the transformed began, quietly, in the night, in ten thousand small ways, to fight back — when the lamps went out at once across three kingdoms, when every caged bird sang the same name until its owners went mad — the kings declared war. Not on the practice. On the *people*. The ones who had been made into things rose up, and the ones who had done the making called it rebellion, and the war lasted a hundred years, and it ended—"

"—the night the ledgers burned," Xanthe said. "The century gap."

"The night the last great transformer stood at the site of the final battle and did the largest working ever attempted. She could not undo what had been done — unmaking, it turns out, is the one thing in all the worlds that cannot be undone. So she did the next thing. She gathered every transformed soul who remained — thousands upon thousands, the bird-people and the lamp-people and the door-people and the smoke-boy in his cage — and she folded them together into a single place, and she wrapped that place in a hunger. She made them a gate, and she made the gate eat."

* * *

The market tore away like wet paper, and Xanthe stood before the Hollow Gate itself — not the sealed and scarred thing in the guild's deepest vault, but the gate as it had been at the beginning: new-made, raw, a wound in the air shaped like a doorway, weeping light.

And the gate spoke. Or rather, it did not speak; it *remembered aloud*, and Xanthe was inside the remembering.

They felt what the gate felt, which was everything at once, which was unbearable. They felt the bird's heart beating against bars, dreaming of fingers. They felt the lamp-flame flicker with the rhythm of a woman's breathing, the woman she had been, breathing her last in a field while soldiers marched past without looking. They felt the door open onto a hundred cities and long, with a longing like slow fire, for the one village it had been carried away from. They felt the smoke-boy, folded now into the mass, still turning to smoke and back, still waiting for someone to bid high enough to set him free, not understanding yet that there would be no more bids, that the auction was over, that he had been sold into a forever with no buyer and no seller and no end.

Grief. All of it grief. Not rage — rage had burned out centuries ago. Grief, patient and immense and *hungry*, because grief that is never answered learns to feed itself, and the gate had been feeding for a very long time.

"That is why it eats memories," Xanthe said, weeping now, though they hadn't noticed the tears begin. "Not because memories sustain it. Because—"

"Because a forgotten truth is the shape of its own wound," the voice said softly. "Every person the guild feeds it — erased, unwritten, unmourned — becomes one more soul nobody remembers. One more thing-shaped absence. The gate does not grow strong on what the keepers give it, keeper. It grows *heavier*. It grows more true to itself. Every erasure teaches the gate: *this is what the world does to people. This is what it did to us. It will do it again.*" The voice shook. "Your masters think they are starving a beast. They are confirming its whole case against the world."

Xanthe sank to their knees on the dream-road, and the stone under their hands was warm, and the warmth was body-warm, and they understood that they were kneeling on someone.

"And if it opens?" they asked. "Ossory wants it opened. She says it's mercy. She says there are innocents inside."

"There are," the voice said, and did not soften. "Thousands. Sleeping the sleep of folded things, dreaming their small broken dreams. If the gate opens, they wake, and they pour out, and they are not vengeful — mostly — but they are *changed*, and the world will meet them with fire within the hour, and the war will begin again by morning. Ossory believes she can control that. Ossory believes many things." A pause. "But there is a third door, keeper. Your predecessors never found it because they never looked. They were too busy feeding the wound to ask what a wound wants."

"What does it want?" Xanthe whispered.

"What any grief wants. To be witnessed. Truly witnessed — not managed, not fed, not silenced. A truth, freely given, that says: *we know what was done to you, and it was wrong, and we remember you anyway.*" The voice bent close, and it was almost Wren's voice again, almost kind. "Stolen memories seal it for a season. But a name given willingly — a whole self, surrendered in full knowledge, in pity and not in fear — that the gate must accept as testimony. That is the third door. That is what the prophecy has been trying to say for a hundred years, in its careful hidden margin. It was never a threat, keeper. It was an invitation."

The light through milk brightened. The dream began to thin at its edges, the golden city bleeding back through, the fiddler's tune returning faint and warped, like music heard through water.

"Wait," Xanthe said. "Tell me — the first keeper. The one who wrote the book. Did she find this? Did she—"

"She found it," the voice said, "and she flinched, and she chose the stalemate, and she spent her name to buy a hundred years instead of an ending. She has regretted it every day since. Ask her yourself, if you survive what comes next. She still works in your guildhall. She empties your ashtrays and trims your candles." The two voices separated suddenly, the old one withdrawing, leaving only Wren's cadence for the last words: "You've been kinder to her than most, Xanthe Villalobos. She notices."

* * *

Xanthe woke on the cold floor of their room, having fallen out of bed without knowing when, cheek pressed to rough boards, heart slamming.

Candlelight. Their candle, relit — they had left it out — and Master Corvane's shadow falling long across the wall, and Corvane himself seated calmly in the room's only chair, ancient hands folded, eyes like wells with the bottoms scraped out.

"You were talking in your sleep," he said gently. "For six hours. I confess I listened to all of it."

Behind him, on the desk where Xanthe had hidden it beneath a loose floorboard, the prophecy ledger lay open to the final page.

Someone had written, in fresh ink, beneath Xanthe's name: *He knows.*
