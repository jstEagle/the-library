## 12. Redaction

Xanthe woke to sunlight they did not trust.

It came through the narrow window of their cell in the keepers' wing, warm and gold and entirely ordinary, and that was the problem. The last thing Xanthe remembered with any certainty was rain. Rain hammering the archive skylights, rain silvering the gutters of the guildhall, rain the night they had knelt in the deep stacks with Wren's lamp guttering beside them and understood, finally and completely, what lay at the bottom of the century gap.

Now it was morning. Now the light was dry.

They sat up slowly, the way one sits up in a stranger's house, and took inventory of themselves the way they took inventory of the vault: hands, present; body, intact; a dull ache behind the eyes like a bruise pressed into the mind's soft tissue. Their mouth tasted faintly of copper and chamomile — the guild's restorative draught, they knew that taste, had brewed it often enough for keepers who came back from maintenance rounds at the Hollow Gate pale and shaking and unable to say why.

*Why would I have been given the draught?*

They reached for yesterday and found a smooth place. Not emptiness — emptiness would have edges, would announce itself as a hole. This was worse. This was a wall painted to look like a door. When they thought *rain*, the memory was there, whole and vivid: the sound on the skylight, the cold of the stone floors. But when they tried to remember walking back to their cell from the archive, or eating, or speaking to anyone, the rain simply continued, endless, until morning arrived without transition, like a page torn from a book and replaced with a page that looked almost identical.

Xanthe had erased things before. It was, in the most literal sense, their job. They had held vials of other people's Tuesdays, other people's first kisses and funeral days, and poured them into the gate's keeping with steady hands. They knew exactly how removal felt from the outside: clean, surgical, merciful when done well.

From the inside, it was not clean at all. From the inside it was this — sunlight they could not trust, and a week of their life gone missing, and no way to mourn it because they could not even remember what they were mourning.

Someone had taken seven days from them. And there was only one person in the guild with the authority to do it.

"Master Corvane," Xanthe said aloud, to the empty cell, just to hear something certain.

Their voice sounded wrong to them. Smaller. As if the room had grown slightly larger than it used to be.

* * *

The walk to the refectory taught them the shape of what had been done.

Keepers nodded to Xanthe in the corridors, and the nods held nothing unusual — which meant, presumably, that no one remembered anything unusual either, that Corvane had trimmed the week from every record and recollection alike with the tidy thoroughness of a man weeding a garden. A junior keeper named Pell asked whether Xanthe had recovered from "the fall." Xanthe said yes before understanding the word. *The fall.* There it was: the official story, already installed in the guild's collective memory like a splinter so small the flesh had closed over it. Xanthe had fallen on the stairs. Concussion. A few foggy days. Nothing to see.

The lie was elegant. The lie was even kind, in its way. That was the most frightening part.

Xanthe carried their breakfast tray to the corner table they always occupied — muscle memory served where memory itself had failed, their body knowing the chair, the view of the courtyard linden, the habit of sitting with their back to the wall — and stared at their porridge and understood that they were now living inside the kind of erasure they had administered a hundred times. Somewhere in the vaults, in a labeled vial, sat seven days of Xanthe Villalobos. The night in the archive. The conversation with Wren. Whatever conclusion they had reached at the bottom of the century gap, whatever truth they had chased close enough to touch.

Corvane hadn't killed it. He never killed anything. He was a keeper, after all; he believed, with the devotion of the truly frightened, that everything belonged in the gate's ledger eventually. He had merely *filed* Xanthe's week away, the way one files a letter too dangerous to answer.

And he had done it gently, with restorative draughts and a cover story and, Xanthe would have bet anything, a personal visit to their sickbed full of warmth and concern. Corvane's kindness. The phrase had begun, some time in the lost week — Xanthe was sure of it, sure the way you are sure of a scar's itch — to feel like a threat.

Their hand shook. Porridge slopped against the bowl's rim.

*Think,* they told themselves. *You are a keeper. Inventory is what you do.*

So they inventoried. What remained: everything before the rain-night, whole and bright. Everything after: this morning. Between them, the painted door. And one more thing, which they discovered when they reached automatically for the inner pocket of their keeper's coat and found paper.

A fold of cheap vault-stock, creased into quarters, soft at the edges from handling. Their own handwriting covered both sides in a cramped, hurried script — the script they used when writing by lamplight, leaning close, trying not to be seen.

At the top, underlined twice:

**IF YOU ARE READING THIS AND YOU DO NOT REMEMBER WRITING IT, THEN HE FOUND YOU. DO NOT PANIC. YOU PLANNED FOR THIS. READ EVERYTHING TWICE.**

Xanthe did not open the note in the refectory. They folded it away, ate three bites of porridge to keep their hands busy, and walked back to their cell at the pace of a person with nothing on their mind but a mild headache.

Then they barred the door, sat on the bed with their back against the stone, and read themselves back into existence.

* * *

The first note was a map of the others.

*There are five notes total,* their past self had written. *This one, two in your mattress, one sewn into the lining of your winter coat (you will need scissors), and one written in the margin of the inventory ledger for shelf 44-C, coded as stock numbers. I spread them out because if he takes any single hiding place, the rest survive. He is thorough, but he is old, and old men search where they have searched before.*

*What follows is what you know. Trust it. You verified everything twice.*

*One: the Hollow Gate does not feed on memories. It feeds on forgotten truths. Memories are the vessel; the truth inside them is the meal. That is why the guild erases people instead of merely storing their pasts. A stored memory still belongs to someone. An erased person becomes a truth no one living can recall — perfect food, perfectly digestible.*

*Two: tonight's date — the date you found in the prophecy ledger — is the date the gate was first fed a willing name. The first keeper wrote it down a century ago. Every redacted entry in the guild records matches a feeding. Corvane signs the redactions. Check the pressure of the pen strokes; he bears down on his descenders like a man trying to hold something down.*

*Three: the century gap in the deep archive is not decay and is not accident. Someone burned those ledgers in the year the last war ended, and the burning was ordered by the guild, and the reason is in the prophecy book's marginalia. You have not fully deciphered it yet. That is what the lost week was for. You were close.*

*Four: Wren knows more than she has told you. You decided — before the rain, before you understood everything — that this does not make her an enemy. Re-examine why you trusted her. You trusted her because she once returned a package she could easily have kept, and because she looks at the gate the way people look at graves they dug themselves.*

*Five:* and here their own handwriting changed, grew slower, the letters pressed deeper into the paper as though the writer had needed the physical act of pressure to go on. *Five: he will be kind to you afterward. He will visit. He will bring something. Do not drink anything he pours. Do not accept gifts. His kindness is how he forgives himself, and it is also how he finds out what you still know. Tell him nothing true. Grieve later.*

Xanthe read it twice, as instructed, and then a third time, and by the third reading their eyes had blurred and the cramped letters swam together and they had to press the heel of their hand against their face and sit very still for a long while.

Grief later. Fine. But grief had come anyway, ahead of schedule, rude as death.

Because here was the thing they had not anticipated, the thing no keeper ever learned until it happened to them: they were mourning a person they could not remember being. The Xanthe who wrote these notes had known things, had made decisions, had sat in this same cell and worked up the courage to hide paper in a mattress lining while rain fell on the archive roof — and that Xanthe was gone. Not dead, exactly. Dead would leave a body, a grave, a shape. This Xanthe had been *decanted*, poured into a vial and shelved, and the person left behind was a copy made from all the older pages, holding the new pages' words in their hands without any of the new pages' weight.

They read the sentence about Wren again. *She looks at the gate the way people look at graves they dug themselves.*

Who had written that? Xanthe searched their feelings and found nothing — no recollection of Wren at the gate, no image to attach to the observation. Just the observation itself, orphaned, precise, and clearly the product of hours of watching that had been taken along with everything else.

That was erasure's true cost, they realized. Not the losing. Anyone could lose things; loss was common as weather. The cost was that you couldn't even keep the conclusions without the experiences that earned them. You inherited your own wisdom like furniture from a dead relative's house: useful, familiar in style, and utterly without provenance. You could sit in the chairs but you could not remember the rooms they'd come from.

Xanthe folded the note, and then — because their past self had known them well enough to predict even this — read the final line one more time.

*You are still you. The part of you that checks things twice, that sits with its back to the wall, that cannot pass a mislabeled shelf without fixing it — he cannot erase that, because it isn't stored anywhere. It's just what you are. Start checking.*

So Xanthe started checking.

* * *

The notes in the mattress were wrapped in oilcloth and confirmed everything the first contained, adding detail: dates, ledger numbers, the location of the prophecy book (moved, since the rain-night, from the open stacks to Corvane's private cabinet — *of course*, Xanthe thought bitterly, *he noticed you noticing*). The note sewn into the winter coat required them to spend twenty minutes with embroidery scissors, heart pounding at every footstep in the corridor, and yielded the most valuable page of all: a partial transcription of the marginalia.

The first keeper's hand, tiny and fierce even a century old. Xanthe read the transcription three times and then sat with it in their lap while the afternoon light crawled across the floor.

*We did not build the gate to imprison them,* the first keeper had written. *We built it because they begged us to. They could not bear to be what we made them, and they could not unmake themselves, and so they asked for a door between what they are and what we did. The guild tells itself we guard the world from the impossible things. The truth is we guard the impossible things from remembering that they were people, because if they remember, the grief comes through, and the grief is the war.*

*Every name we feed it buys another quiet year. I am writing this so that someone, eventually, has the choice we never gave ourselves.*

*The gate requires a willing name on the hundredth year, or it opens. I have written my own into the margin. Whoever reads this: you may inherit my choice. I am sorry. I am also, somehow, grateful.*

Below that, their past self had added one line, in the present tense of the rain-night:

*I don't know yet if it has to be me. But I've checked every entry, every margin, every feeding for a hundred years, and they are all keepers, and mine is the last name on the last page. He knows I've read it. He'll erase the week and watch what I do next. If I'm reading this, I have maybe four days before the date arrives.*

*Wren. Tell Wren. She is the only one who will carry it.*

Xanthe put everything back — oilcloth in the mattress seam, thread re-stitched clumsily in the coat, the first note returned to the pocket where their body apparently expected it — and then sat down at their little desk, took out a fresh sheet of vault-stock, and began, deliberately, to rebuild.

Not the memories. Those were gone, shelved somewhere in Corvane's cabinet, waiting for the gate. But a keeper's job had always been to make records out of losses, and Xanthe was, whatever else had been taken, still a keeper. They wrote down everything the notes contained, in their own hand, dated this morning. They drew the pressure-marks of Corvane's descenders from memory. They sketched the layout of the deep archive from a knowledge so deep in their muscles that even erasure hadn't reached it, marking the shelf where the century gap began and the locked case where the prophecy book had lived before it moved upstairs.

By the time the evening bell rang, they had four pages of dense, verifiable reconstruction and a plan assembled from parts their past self had left lying around like tools in an attic.

Four days. Possibly fewer.

They went down to supper with their back to the wall and their face arranged into mild concussion fatigue, and they watched the refectory the way they watched the vaults: cataloguing, cross-referencing. Pell laughed at something. Traders from Ossory's circle dined at the high table, elegant as knives. And near the couriers' entrance, half-unnoticed as always — Wren, with her talent for being unremarkable, eating alone, glancing up once.

Once. Directly at Xanthe. With an expression that was not casual at all.

And Xanthe, holding no memory of her but a stranger's set of borrowed observations, understood two things simultaneously. First: Wren had noticed the fall, the cover story, the foggy days — and Wren, who knew more about the gate than she admitted, knew exactly what a foggy day meant in the keepers' wing.

Second: Xanthe's past self had written *tell Wren* — and had died before doing it. Which meant that the telling was now Xanthe's job, and the person they had to tell might be the only person in the world who remembered the week better than they did, because she had lived it alongside them and no one had bothered to trim it from *her*.

Wren looked away first, unremarkable again, ordinary as bread.

But she left her tray a moment later, and on her way out she passed Xanthe's table without stopping, without looking, and set down beside their cup — lightly, the way one leaves a tip — a small glass vial, stoppered with wax, empty except for a slip of paper rolled tight inside.

Xanthe waited until she was gone. Then they palmed the vial, and under the table, with thumbs that only slightly shook, they cracked the wax and unrolled the paper.

Three words, in Wren's blunt courier's hand:

*Storeroom. Midnight. Bring the coat.*
