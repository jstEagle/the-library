## 13. The Blank Ledger

Wren had spent eleven years learning how to be forgotten on purpose, and it turned out all that practice was worth nothing when what she wanted was to be remembered by exactly one person.

She stood in the shadow of the vault's outer door with her hood up and her heart going like a courier's boots on cobbles, watching Xanthe pick the lock of the Guild of Impossible Things with a hairpin and a look of profound apology.

"You're doing it wrong," Wren whispered.

"I'm doing it *quietly*," Xanthe whispered back. "There's a difference."

"There is. One of them works."

The pin slipped. Xanthe exhaled through their nose — the particular exhale they used instead of swearing, because keepers were trained out of swearing the way other people were trained out of crying — and tried again. Somewhere above them, in the guildhall proper, the night bells rang the third hour, and the sound went through Wren like cold water finding a seam.

Third hour. The traders would be awake now. Madame Ossory's people kept merchant hours and thief hours both, and after last night — after Ossory had smiled at Xanthe across a table of stolen candlelight and offered them protection like a gift wrapped in a threat — Wren had no illusions about who else might be moving through the stacks tonight.

The lock clicked.

"Ha," said Xanthe, with the small fierce triumph of a person who had spent their whole life being invisible and had just made a door open anyway.

* * *

Inside, the vault breathed.

Everyone said the vault was silent, but Wren knew better. It wasn't silence; it was *held breath*. A thousand impossible things, shelved and catalogued and pretending very hard to be objects: clocks that ran on regret, mirrors that showed you the room as your mother would have described it, keys to doors that had been burned down so long ago the ash had blown into the sea. The air tasted of dust and tallow and something older underneath, something like the inside of a shell.

Xanthe moved through it the way water moves through a familiar channel — without looking, without hesitating, their lantern shaded to a coin of amber light. Wren followed a half-step behind, which was where she had always followed, back when following was her job and her orders came from Corvane's office in sealed grey envelopes.

*Watch the keeper,* the envelopes had said, for years. *Note what they read. Note what they touch.*

She had noted everything. She had reported almost nothing. And now here they were, the watcher and the watched, committing burglary together at three in the morning, and Wren found — absurdly, inadvisably — that she was almost happy.

"It should be in the Annals case," Xanthe murmured, stopping before a cabinet of black oak whose locks were not locks at all but knots, tied in wood grown around itself. "Corvane keeps the blank ledgers there. He thinks nobody knows."

"Does he know *you* know?"

"He knows I read." Xanthe's fingers found the first knot. "He doesn't know what I understand. There's a gap between those two things, and I've been living in it for a month."

The knots gave one by one under Xanthe's hands, each with a soft sigh, like an old man being persuaded rather than opened. Wren kept her eyes on the aisle behind them. In the lantern's spill the shelves rose up out of sight, and between them the dark pooled in shapes that were almost figures. She told herself it was the light. She told herself that even if it wasn't, nothing in the vault would hurt a keeper mid-task.

She did not tell herself anything about the traders.

"Left side," Xanthe said. "Second shelf. Do you see—"

Wren saw.

The blank ledger sat where ledgers go to be forgotten: plain black boards, unmarked spine, no clasp, no sigil. Every other book in the Annals case glittered with guild-stamping, gilt corners, the seal of the Impossible Things pressed deep into leather. This one had nothing. It looked like a hole in the shape of a book.

"That's it?" Wren whispered. "It looks like — nothing."

"That's the point." Xanthe lifted it out with both hands, careful as a priest lifting a relic or a thief lifting a heart. "A keeper's ledger isn't written *on*, Wren. It's written *into*. The pages don't hold ink. They hold truth. Whatever goes into a blank ledger becomes real in a way the gate can't digest — can't eat, can't erase, can't pretend was never said." Their thumb moved along the cover, slow. "There are only ever three in existence. One is Corvane's. One is—" they stopped. Swallowed. "One is missing. Has been for a hundred years."

"And this is the third."

"This is the third."

Wren looked at the little black book and felt the weight of it settle somewhere behind her ribs. A hundred years ago, the first keeper had written a prophecy in a ledger like this one — had hidden every erased name in its margins, including, on the final page, Xanthe Villalobos. If the blank ledger could hold truth the gate couldn't swallow, then it could hold *them*. All of them. Every name Corvane had traded away across centuries of stalemate.

It could hold Xanthe.

She didn't say that part. She was fairly sure her face didn't say it either, because eleven years of being unremarkable meant her face could hold a siege.

"Take it," she said instead. "And let's—"

Lantern-light. Faint, gold, wrong — swinging gently at the far end of the aisle behind them, where the dark had been pooling in almost-figures.

Wren put her hand flat against Xanthe's chest and pushed them both down behind the Annals case, into the smell of old oak and older secrets, and killed their own lantern with two fingers.

The light came closer. It moved the way Ossory's people moved — unhurried, elegant, the walk of someone who has never once needed to run. Beneath it Wren caught the whisper of silk and the faint sweet reek of ossuary-incense that the traders wore like perfume, and then a voice, low and amused:

"She's here. I told you. The courier never sleeps, and the keeper never locks anything properly."

A second voice, younger, nervous: "Madame said take the book, not the people."

"Madame says many things. Take the book."

* * *

They ran.

Not at first — first they *waited*, pressed together in the dark while the lantern swung nearer, near enough that Wren could see the trader's rings glinting, four on one hand, each set with a different impossible stone. Then Xanthe did something with their sleeve that Wren would think about later with grudging admiration: they tipped the nearest shelf-clock, the one that ran on regret, and let it begin to chime.

It chimed the way regret chimes — all at once, everywhere, from every direction, each strike sounding from a different corner of the vault. The traders spun toward the noise. Wren and Xanthe went the other way, low and fast, the black ledger hugged to Xanthe's chest like a child.

The stacks blurred past. Wren counted turns out of habit — left, right, left again, past the mirror-case where her own reflection lagged half a step behind her, past the drawer of keys to burned doors — and realized halfway through that Xanthe was following *her* lead, trusting her sense of the vault's geography over their own.

"When did you learn the stacks?" Xanthe breathed.

"When I was watching you," Wren said. "You always took the long way round the west cases. I wanted to know why."

"Why?"

"Because the short way passed the case with the singing knives, and you hated hearing them." She ducked a low beam. "You hummed to drown them out. Badly."

"I do not hum badly."

"You hum like a door that needs oil."

Despite everything — despite the lantern-light blooming behind them, despite the second set of footsteps that had joined the first, despite the fact that in six days the Hollow Gate would stir and the city would wake full of returned ghosts — Xanthe laughed. A short, startled, real laugh, swallowed immediately by running, but Wren caught it and held onto it the way you hold a coal in cupped hands.

That was the thing about Xanthe that the guild had never understood, Wren thought, taking the stairs up from the sub-vaults two at a time. They'd chosen keepers with no family to miss them, no friends to ask questions, no threads connecting them to the world — and then they'd given Xanthe a friend anyway, by accident, by sending *her*. Corvane had built a person out of loneliness and then handed them a companion, and he still didn't know what he'd done.

The traders were faster than they should have been. Silk didn't make that kind of sound. Wren risked a glance back and saw the lantern gaining, and behind it another, and behind *that* — at the mouth of the stair they'd just climbed — a tall figure in dove-grey who did not carry a lantern at all, because Madame Ossory had never needed light to find what she wanted.

"The east door," Wren gasped. "It's bolted from the outside but the bolt's rotten, I checked it months ago—"

"Why did you check it?"

"Because checking exits is what I *do*."

The east door gave on the second shoulder-hit, shedding rust like dried blood, and then they were out — out into the night air of the guildhall's inner court, out under a sky scoured clean by wind, and the sudden openness of it hit Wren like surfacing from deep water. Stars. Actual stars. She'd forgotten the sky had a ceiling higher than shelving.

Behind them, unhurried, the traders emerged into the court and stopped. All of them. As if on a cue none of them had been given.

Ossory stood at the head of the stair, dove-grey and silver-haired, and she did not pursue. She folded her hands. She smiled the smile Wren had learned to dread — the one that contained no threat precisely because it didn't need any.

"Keeper," Ossory called softly, across the empty stones. "Courier. You may keep the book tonight. Consider it a gift." Her voice carried without effort, the way certain voices do, as though the air itself were listening. "But books want readers, and readers want reasons. When you find yours — when you finally decide what the truth is *for* — come and see me. Either of you. My door opens more easily than the guild's."

Then she turned and was gone, and her people with her, folding back into the guildhall like ink back into water, leaving the court empty and the stars enormous and Wren's hands shaking only now, only after, only once it was safe to shake.

* * *

They didn't speak until they reached Wren's room above the stables — the smallest room in the guildhall, chosen years ago because nobody visited the smallest room, and therefore nobody would notice two voices in it.

Xanthe set the ledger on the narrow bed between them. The candle flame leaned toward it, Wren noticed. Actually leaned, like a plant toward a window.

"It knows what it is," Xanthe said quietly.

"Do you?" Wren sat down across from it, knees nearly touching the book, knees nearly touching Xanthe. "What it's for? What you're going to write in it?"

For a long moment Xanthe didn't answer. The candle hissed. Down in the stable, one of the courier-mules shifted and sighed, and the ordinary sound of it made the room feel more fragile, somehow, not less.

"I'm going to write the truth," Xanthe said at last. "All of it. The war. The transformed ones. Corvane. The redactions. Every name in the margins, spelled out plainly, in order, so that no one can ever call it prophecy again. And my own name last, so it sits at the bottom of the page holding all the others up." They looked up, and their eyes in the candlelight were steady in a way that frightened Wren more than Ossory had. "Because whatever happens in six days, Wren — whether I feed the gate or it breaks — someone has to be able to prove we existed. That *I* existed. Not in prophecy. Not in riddles. In plain words, in a book the gate can't eat."

"And you're giving it to me to keep."

"I'm giving it to you to *read*." Xanthe's voice cracked, just slightly, on the word. "Reading it is what binds you to it. Once you've read a blank ledger, its truth lives in you too. That's why Corvane never lets anyone read his. That's why Ossory wants this one untouched." A pause. "I need one person in the world who'll remember me without the guild's permission. Without the gate's permission. Just — because she chose to."

Wren looked at the little black book. At the hole in the shape of a story, waiting to be filled.

"You know I'll say yes," she said. "You knew before you asked."

"I know." And Xanthe smiled — tired, terrified, grateful — and slid the ledger across the blanket into Wren's hands. "But I wanted to hear it."

Wren closed her fingers around the cover. It was warm. Books weren't supposed to be warm.

Outside, far off toward the river, a bell began to ring that no bell in the city had rung in a hundred years — deep, cracked, patient — and both of them went still, listening, as the sound rolled over the rooftops and faded and left the night changed.

Xanthe stood slowly. "That's the gate," they said. "It's early."
