## 14. Ossory's Truth

The note appeared inside Xanthe's glove.

They discovered it the next morning, when they pulled the left one on before descending into the vaults — a slip of grey paper folded into a shape too precise to be accidental, resting in the palm like a sleeping moth. No wax seal. No signature. Only an address in the Tallow District and a single line in handwriting so elegant it seemed embarrassed by its own legibility:

*You have questions that the guild will not let you ask aloud.*

Xanthe should have burned it. They knew that with the flat certainty of someone who had spent years cataloguing objects that had ruined their finders. The vault held three hundred years of cautionary artifacts: rings that whispered, mirrors that kept score, letters whose ink crawled off the page and into the reader's dreams. Every one of them had begun as something small and reasonable. A note. An invitation. A kindness.

But Xanthe had also spent those same years learning what the guild did to people who asked questions quietly instead of loudly. It erased them. And Madame Ossory — because who else wrote in hand like that, who else moved through the guildhall trailing perfume and impunity — was offering something no one else had offered since Wren: a conversation in which Xanthe was not prey.

They went anyway. They told themselves it was reconnaissance. They told themselves a great many things that morning, and the glove kept all of them close against the skin.

* * *

The Tallow District earned its name from the chandlers who had worked there for centuries, and even now, long after the last of them had gone, the streets gave off a faint sweet reek of rendered fat and old smoke, as if the buildings themselves remembered burning. The address was a townhouse squeezed between a shuttered apothecary and a shop that sold nothing but keys — hundreds of them, brass and iron and bone, hung in glittering curtains across the window. Xanthe paused at that window longer than they meant to. There was something obscene about a door with no lock, and something worse about a lock with no door.

The townhouse door opened before they knocked.

"Keeper Villalobos." The servant — or what wore the shape of one — was tall and grey-eyed and utterly forgettable, a courier's trick Xanthe recognized with a lurch, because it was Wren's trick too. "Madame is expecting you. She asks that you forgive the hour; she knows keepers keep strange ones."

Inside, the house was warm in a way that felt engineered. Firelight the color of honey. Carpets deep enough to swallow footsteps. And everywhere, everywhere, small impossible things arranged with the casual confidence of wealth: a birdcage containing weather; a clock whose hands pointed at feelings rather than hours; a jar of light labeled, in careful script, *June 3rd, late afternoon*.

Xanthe's professional eye catalogued them helplessly. Their professional stomach turned. These were not acquisitions. These were *rescues*, or thefts, or both.

Ossory received them in a back room lined floor to ceiling with ledgers.

Not guild ledgers. Nothing so disciplined. These were private books, hundreds of them, spines cracked and titles handwritten, and Xanthe understood with a cold drop through their middle that they were looking at a rival archive — a shadow history compiled over decades by someone who had been asking the guild's questions far longer, and without permission.

"You came." Ossory rose from her chair by the fire. She was smaller than she seemed in the guildhall, and older — silver threading her dark hair, fine lines around eyes the color of strong tea. Up close she looked less like a spider and more like a grandmother who had buried everyone she loved and found the practice instructive. "I confess I gave you until midnight. Most people take longer to decide."

"I decided quickly because I'm bad at being patient," Xanthe said. "And because you already know why I'm here, which saves us the part where I pretend otherwise."

Something flickered across Ossory's face — approval, or amusement, or grief wearing either as a coat. "Sit, please. Tea? It's only tea. I know how the stories go, and I promise you the cup holds nothing but chamomile and my own considerable need for company that argues back."

Xanthe sat. Did not take the tea. Ossory poured two cups anyway and drank from hers, and the gesture was so transparently rehearsed that Xanthe almost smiled.

"You've read the prophecy book," Ossore said. Not a question.

"Parts of it."

"The parts that weren't redacted." She set down her cup. "Then you know the date. You know the gate stirs on schedule, like a tide. And I imagine — I *hope* — you've begun to wonder what exactly your masters have been feeding it all these years, and where the food comes from."

"The guild feeds it memories," Xanthe said carefully. "Erased ones. People the guild removes from record."

"Yes." Ossory leaned forward, and the firelight moved in her eyes. "And here is the question underneath every other question, Keeper, the one Corvane prays you never think to ask: *what happens to the people?*"

The room was very quiet. Somewhere upstairs, the caged weather shifted with a sound like distant thunder deciding against itself.

"They're gone," Xanthe said. "That's what erasure means."

"That's what erasure *looks* like from outside." Ossory's voice stayed gentle, which somehow made it worse. "From inside — well. I've spent thirty years gathering testimony, Keeper. Letters written before the erasure took hold and forgotten afterward, hidden in walls, sewn into coats. Dreams that leak through the gate's seams. Do you know what the erased are? They are not dead. Death would be a mercy the guild does not permit. They are *held*. Held behind the Hollow Gate, aware, unchanged, unaging — imprisoned in the dark by an institution that calls itself the Guild of Impossible Things, when the only truly impossible thing it has ever done is convince a city that imprisonment is safety."

Xanthe thought of the ledger margins. Of names hidden in plain sight, generations of them, tucked into the prophecy text like seeds in winter soil. They had assumed the first keeper had hidden the names as a warning. Now another possibility opened under their feet like a trapdoor.

"You think they can come back."

"I think they never properly left." Ossory stood and crossed to the shelves, running one finger along the spines without reading them; she knew them by heart. "The gate doesn't digest memory, whatever your masters believe. It *keeps* it. Keeps it whole. The guild has spent a century feeding it people and calling the hunger a lock. But locks don't whisper, Keeper. Locks don't remember your childhood back at you." She turned. "You've heard it. Don't lie to me — I can see it in your face. You've stood at the gate and heard something that belonged to you."

The violation of it rose in Xanthe's throat again, fresh as the day it happened: the gate's voice folding around a fragment of their own past, a summer afternoon, a door, a voice calling their name in a tone they hadn't heard since — since —

Since the guild took them in. Since the guild chose them *because no one would miss them*.

"Yes," Xanthe said. The word cost more than they expected.

"Then you already know the truth I'm offering you." Ossory returned to her chair and sat, hands folded, a woman delivering a verdict she had reached long ago and mourned ever since. "Opening the gate is not a catastrophe. Opening the gate is a *rescue*. Yes, there will be chaos — a hundred years of stolen people returning to a world that forgot them, grief and rage and reckoning. The old powers the guild fears may wake. But they were never monsters, Keeper. They were *neighbors*. They were the impossible things themselves — the transformed, the traded, the bargained-away — locked out of their own lives and called dangerous for wanting them back. Your guild didn't end a war. It froze one mid-scream and billed the victims for the ice."

"And the war?" Xanthe's voice came out flatter than they intended. "The ledger says—"

"The ledger was written by the victors' apprentice." Ossory's mouth curved, not quite a smile. "Ask yourself why a hundred years of records burned the very night the fighting stopped. Ask yourself what needed forgetting so badly that memory itself had to be conscripted as a weapon. Your guild tells you the gate keeps the world safe from what's behind it. I am telling you the gate keeps what's behind it safe from the world — and that the world failed them so thoroughly the first time that they might be forgiven for coming back angry."

She let the silence do its work. It was good silence. Practiced. Xanthe hated how well it worked.

"You want the book," Xanthe said finally.

"I want the *truth*," Ossory said. "The book is merely where your guild stores it. Protection, a place in what comes after, access to this archive — everything I have is yours, Keeper. All I ask is that when the date arrives, you stand aside. Or better: stand with us. Let the gate open. Let them come home."

* * *

Xanthe walked the long way back to the guildhall, through districts still shaking off sleep, and tried to locate, with a keeper's precision, exactly where Ossory's argument had gotten under their skin.

It wasn't the offer. Offers were currency; they'd seen enough traders to spend them without bleeding. It wasn't even the threat implied beneath the warmth, though it was certainly there, patient as a knife in a drawer.

It was that Ossory had *believed herself*.

Xanthe had spent their life among liars of every grade — the petty kind, the institutional kind, the kind who lied so long they grew honest around the edges of the lie. They knew the texture of performance. And Ossory's sincerity had none of it. When she spoke of the imprisoned, her grief had been specific, inventory-deep, the grief of someone who had read ten thousand testimonies and kept every one. When she said *let them come home*, her voice had cracked on *home*, just slightly, just once — and cracks like that could be faked, yes, but rarely by women who owned houses full of caged weather and still drank their own chamomile.

Which meant the horror was real. Which meant the imprisoned might be real. Which meant the guild — Corvane, the ledgers, the quiet machinery of erasure Xanthe had served without question — had built its peace on a foundation of living people.

And which meant, equally, that Ossory might be right about the rescue and wrong about everything else. Sincerity was not accuracy. Grief made a poor historian. The gate had whispered Xanthe's own childhood back at them — but it had also, according to the prophecy's marginalia, eaten a century of keepers, and hunger that articulate wanted something, and things that wanted something told you stories.

*Both things can be true,* Xanthe thought, climbing the guildhall steps in the grey pre-dawn. *The prisoners are innocent, and opening the door is a massacre. The guild are jailers, and the jail might be the only wall between the city and the war's second half.* The thought settled into them with the weight of a ledger volume: moral complexity was not a puzzle with a hidden solution. It was a room with two doors, and both doors were load-bearing.

Wren was waiting in the vault corridor, pretending to sort delivery slips, which fooled no one, least of all Xanthe.

"Well?" Wren asked.

"She believes it," Xanthe said. "Every word. That's what frightens me. I can fight a liar. I don't know how to fight someone who's telling the truth badly."

Wren studied them for a long moment. "And the prisoners? Do you believe *her* about them?"

Xanthe opened their mouth — and the guild bell rang.

Not the morning bell. The *deep* bell, the one in the subvault that rang only for gate events, and its voice rolled up through the stone under their feet like something turning over in its sleep. Dust sifted from the ceiling. Down the corridor, a lamp guttered blue.

Wren grabbed Xanthe's arm. "That's early," she breathed. "That's *days* early."

And from somewhere far below — below the vaults, below the archives, below the burned century — came a sound neither of them had heard before and both of them understood instantly: the Hollow Gate, testing its hinges.

In Xanthe's pocket, unnoticed, the grey paper from Ossory's house had begun, very softly, to glow.
