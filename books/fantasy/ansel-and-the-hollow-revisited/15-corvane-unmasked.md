## 15. Corvane Unmasked

Master Corvane had been alive long enough to know the sound of footsteps that meant trouble.

There was a taxonomy to them, learned across centuries of listening in these halls. The traders came up the stairs with a shuffle and a jingle — coin-weighted pockets, careless heels. The couriers moved quick and light, always half-running toward some errand they'd invented on the way. The keepers walked like ghosts who hadn't yet accepted their condition: soft, apologetic, barely disturbing the dust.

Xanthe Villalobos climbed his stairs like someone who had decided, somewhere between the ground floor and this one, to stop apologizing.

Corvane set down his pen. He did not close the ledger in front of him — there was no point; Xanthe would have already seen what it was — but he laid both hands flat upon its cover, the way a man lays hands on the shoulders of an old dog he may soon have to put down.

"Come in," he said, before the knock could land.

The door opened. Lamplight from the corridor spilled across the study floor, and Xanthe stood in it with a bundle of papers held against their chest like a shield, or a wound. Their face was pale in the way of people who have spent too many nights underground among things that whisper. But their eyes were steady. That was the part Corvane had not prepared for. In all his rehearsals of this moment — and he had rehearsed it for longer than Xanthe had been alive, longer than Xanthe's grandparents had been alive — he had imagined fear, or fury, or the blank confusion of a mind he'd already begun to unpick. He had not imagined this quiet, level look, as if Xanthe had come not to accuse him but to *confirm* something they already knew.

"You've been reading," Corvane said.

"I've been reading." Xanthe stepped inside and closed the door behind them. The latch clicked with terrible finality, a small domestic sound in a room full of centuries. "The redactions. All of them. Every entry the guild ever struck from its own record, going back past the founding charter. I pulled the ink-ghosts from the margins. I matched dates. I found the pattern."

"There is no pattern."

"There's a *calendar*." Xanthe crossed the room and laid the papers down on his desk, one by one, each sheet landing with the soft authority of a card turned face-up. "Every redaction falls within three days of a date written in the prophecy ledger. Every one. A hundred years of entries, Master Corvane, and every single night the gate needed feeding, someone with keeper's authority reached into our records and made a person disappear twice — once from the world, and once from the memory of the world."

Corvane looked at the papers without touching them. He had expected this conversation for so long that now it had arrived, it felt almost like relief. Like setting down a load carried so far that the shoulders had forgotten their own shape.

"And you think I don't know," he said. "You think I sit here in my tower, signing requisitions for lamp oil and vellum, unaware of what happens beneath this house."

"I think you *order* what happens beneath this house."

"Yes." He said it simply, because the time for elaborate lies had passed, and because some small, starved part of him — a part he had kept fed on silence for four hundred years — wanted desperately to say it aloud. "Yes, I do."

* * *

The study at the top of the guildhall was the only room in the building with a window that opened. Everyone else worked behind glass that had been spelled shut generations ago, against moths, against drafts, against the possibility that a keeper might lean out and shout the truth into the street. Corvane's window opened onto the rooftops of the city, and beyond them the dark shoulder of the hill where the Hollow Gate slept in its wound of stone.

He rose and went to it now, because he could not have this conversation sitting down. Standing, at least, he could pretend he was still the kind of man who might walk out of a room.

"Do you know how old I am, Xanthe?"

"The records say fifty-one years as Master."

"The records say a great many things." He watched his breath fog the cold glass. "I was apprenticed to the first keeper when I was nineteen. Her name was Ilseva Marchetti, though you won't find it anywhere now — I saw to that myself, near the end, when even her name became dangerous. She was brilliant and she was kind and she believed, absolutely, that we were doing the right thing. Guarding the gate. Keeping the seal strong. She taught me the inventory rites, the maintenance rounds, the whole liturgy of vigilance. And she taught me the first rule of the guild, which is also the last rule, which is the only rule that has never changed."

"*Nothing forgotten can be missed,*" Xanthe recited. It was carved over the guildhall doors.

"It sounds noble, doesn't it. It sounded noble then. What she meant — what the rule actually *means*, if you follow it down past the marble and the ceremony — is that the gate cannot be allowed to hunger for anything anyone remembers. A gate fed on stolen memories grows restless. Stolen memories have owners, and owners grieve, and grief makes noise, and noise travels downhill to the Hollow. So we don't steal memories, Xanthe. We erase *people*. Whole people. People no one will miss, because we make certain no one remains to miss them." He turned from the window. "Keepers are chosen for having no family. You knew that. You were chosen for it yourself."

Something flickered across Xanthe's face — not surprise. Confirmation. "And the ones we erase. The names in your redactions."

"Are keepers." Corvane said it flatly, letting the horror land without cushioning. "Mostly. Not always — sometimes a trader who knew too much, sometimes a courier, once, God forgive me, a cook who found a ledger she should never have seen. But mostly keepers. The gate prefers us. We spend our lives among impossible things; we're already half-made of forgetting. We go down easier."

"How many?" Xanthe's voice was very controlled. "In your time. How many names?"

Corvane considered lying, out of habit more than hope. Then he crossed to his desk, unlocked the lowest drawer — the one that had held the same contents since before the current guildhall was built — and drew out a slim book bound in grey leather. He set it beside Xanthe's evidence.

"Count them yourself. I stopped counting around the second century. It seemed obscene to keep a tally, and then it seemed more obscene not to, and then I simply couldn't decide, so the book does it for me. One line per name. Date, method, duration of seal-strengthening. Four hundred and eleven years of bookkeeping." He laughed, and hated the sound of it — dry as ash, old as the drawer. "That's what I am, in the end. Not a master. Not a guardian. A bookkeeper. The longest-serving clerk of the worst account in the world."

Xanthe didn't touch the grey book. "You're telling me all of this. Just like that. Why? Why not do to me what you did to the others? You erased a week of my memories not ten days ago. One more erasure and I'd never have climbed these stairs."

"Because it doesn't work anymore." The admission came out of him like a tooth coming loose — painful, inevitable, leaving a strange hollow behind. "Do you understand what I'm confessing, Xanthe? Not just the feeding. The *failure*. Each name buys less than the last. When Ilseva sealed the gate after the war, a single willing sacrifice held it for sixty years. By the time I took mastership, we needed two a decade. Now the ledgers show erosion appearing within weeks of a feeding. Fresh cracks where old memories should hold. You've seen it on your rounds — I know you have, don't look surprised, I read the maintenance reports, I wrote half of them under other names. The gate is starving. We are pouring people into it by the fistful and it is *still hungry*, and I no longer believe there is enough of anything left to fill it."

"Then why keep feeding it?"

"Because the alternative is the war." He said it quietly, but the word filled the room anyway. "You found the century gap in the archive. Burned ledgers, missing volumes, everything between the founding and the armistice gone. Do you know why it's gone? Because the war wasn't fought with armies, child. It was fought with *people*. The impossible things we house in this guild — the singing chair, the door that opens onto yesterday, the coat with nine owners' ghosts still arguing in its lining — those were not crafted. They were *made from people*. Transformed. Traded. The old powers didn't build wonders; they built wounds, and sold them, and called the wounds gifts. And when the war ended, the gate was raised over the worst of it — over the place where the transformed were unmade, where their grief pooled so deep it gained weight and appetite. Open that gate, and everything pooled behind it comes through. Everything. Every grudge, every ghost, every power that spent a hundred years learning nothing except how to hate the shape of the world above."

"So you feed it innocents to keep monsters caged."

"I feed it innocents to keep *everyone* caged," Corvane said. "Including the monsters. Including me." He held out his hands, palms up, and let Xanthe see them properly for perhaps the first time — the fine tremor in the fingers, the skin too smooth for the age of the eyes above them. "You want to know the last of it? The part that will make you despise me properly? The gate keeps me alive. Ilseva's final act, her great sin, was to bind her apprentice to the seal — so that someone who remembered the founding would always survive to run the guild. I have died eleven times, Xanthe. Fever, blade, a fall from the north tower in the year the bridge burned. And each time, the gate has poured me back into my body like water into a cracked cup, because a dead master is a loose thread, and loose threads unravel seals. I cannot leave. I cannot die. I can only continue. Four hundred years of continuing, trading names for stalemate, telling myself each erasure was the last necessary one, knowing it never was." His voice frayed. "I stopped believing in this a hundred and fifty years ago. I kept doing it anyway. That is what cowardice looks like when it's given an office and a title. It looks exactly like me."

* * *

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The lamp guttered and steadied. Somewhere below, the guildhall settled against the night with the small groans of any old building, and Corvane thought: how many nights have I listened to this same sound, waiting for it to be the sound of the wall giving way?

"Why tell me now?" Xanthe asked at last. "Why not let Wren's reports stand? Why not erase me again, deeper this time, and buy another few months of pretending?"

"Because of the prophecy ledger." Corvane returned to his desk and rested his weight on his knuckles, leaning over the evidence Xanthe had brought him like a man leaning over a well. "Tonight's date. Written a hundred years ago by a woman who could not have known it. You think I haven't read that page, Xanthe? I have read it every night for a century. Ilseva wrote it in the last month of her life, in a hand going shaky, and she told me only one thing about it: *when the date comes, the choice arrives, and the choice must be made by someone the gate has never tasted.*" He looked up. "Not me. The gate knows me to the bottom of my soul; I'm more its creature than its prisoner by now. Not Ossory — she'd open it wide and call it liberation. It had to be a keeper. Young. Unrecorded in the deep archives. Someone whose name appears nowhere until the final page." He paused. "You've seen the final page."

It was not quite a question. Xanthe's jaw tightened. "My name is in the margin."

"In every margin, if you read the cipher correctly. Ilseva hid the names of every keeper the gate would ever take inside the prophecy text itself — a record even the gate couldn't digest, because it was never written *down*, only woven in. Yours is the last. The date is tonight." He straightened. "So yes. I am telling you everything, now, tonight, with the hour running out — because I am a coward, Xanthe, but I am a coward who has finally run out of hallway to retreat down. There is no next erasure. There is no next decade. There is tonight, and the choice, and whatever you decide to do with the truth I have just handed you like a live coal."

"And if I choose wrong?"

"Then either the gate breaks and the old world pours through, or the gate closes forever on a lie, and the lie calcifies into scripture, and in another four hundred years some new apprentice stands in this room listening to some new master explain why the feeding must continue." He smiled, and it was the saddest expression he owned, worn smooth by use. "I no longer know which wrong is wronger. That is the honest sum of my wisdom. Four hundred years, and I no longer know."

Xanthe gathered the papers slowly, squaring their edges against the desk. When they spoke, their voice had changed — quieter, but with something underneath it that Corvane recognized from a mirror he'd avoided for centuries. Resolve. The terrible, clean kind.

"The transformed," Xanthe said. "The people the gate was built over. Are they still in there? Behind the seal. Are they still *them*?"

Corvane closed his eyes. He had hoped — coward to the last — that this question would not come. "I entered the threshold-dream once. Ninety years ago. I saw the origin. I saw what the impossible things were before they were things." He opened his eyes. "They are still them. Grieving. Waiting. The gate isn't a prison, Xanthe. It's a mass grave that learned to be hungry because no one ever mourned it properly."

"Then the prophecy isn't asking for a sacrifice." Xanthe lifted their chin, and lamplight caught their eyes, and Corvane felt, for the first time in four hundred years, something adjacent to hope. "It's asking for a mourner."

Below them, faint through the floor, a bell began to ring — not the guild's measured hours, but the alarm from the lower vaults, urgent and arrhythmic. Voices rose in the stairwell. Doors slammed. Somewhere in the stacks, Madame Ossory's people had begun to move.

Corvane looked at the window, at the hill, at the dark seam in the hillside that had eaten four hundred and eleven names and was stirring now like something waking.

"It's starting," he said. "Whatever you're going to do — do it fast."
