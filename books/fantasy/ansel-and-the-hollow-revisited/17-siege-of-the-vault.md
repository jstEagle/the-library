## 17. Siege of the Vault

Wren had always believed her talent for being unremarkable would keep her safe. It was a reasonable belief. For eleven years it had kept her off ledgers, out of arguments, past checkpoints where guards looked through her as though she were a draft of cold air given a courier's satchel. She had built a life in the gaps of other people's attention, and she had liked it there.

Tonight the gaps were full of torchlight and boots.

She stood at the top of the great stair of the guildhall with a candlestick in one hand and no better weapon anywhere on her person, listening to the sound of Madame Ossory's faction coming up from the undercroft like water finding its level. Below her, forty voices she had delivered letters to, carried parcels for, nodded at in corridors — traders all, and half the guild's hired muscle besides — moving with the unhurried confidence of people who had already won.

The stair was long. That was the whole of her plan: the stair was long, and Xanthe needed time, and Wren had spent a lifetime learning how to be overlooked. She meant to spend that talent now like a purse of coins, one small miracle at a time, until there was nothing left in it.

*Hold the stair,* Xanthe had said, gripping her shoulders hard enough to bruise, backlit by the vault's green lamp-glow. *Just hold it. I'll be fast.*

*I'm always fast,* Wren had said, because if she said anything truer she would have started crying, and there wasn't time.

The first of them came around the bend of the stair three minutes later — a broad man called Ferren whose face Wren knew from a hundred deliveries, who had once tipped her a copper for carrying nothing heavier than a compliment. He saw her and slowed, puzzled rather than hostile, which was exactly what she'd counted on.

"You're not supposed to be up here," he said.

"No," Wren agreed. "Neither are you."

He blinked. She could feel the moment his certainty wobbled — not because she was frightening, but because she was *familiar*, and familiarity is a kind of authority when you have nothing else. "Madame Ossory's orders. The vault's to be opened. There's a ledger—"

"There's a ledger," Wren said, "that Master Corvane has sealed under keeper's authority. You know what happens to anyone who breaks a keeper's seal without sanction." She didn't know herself, actually. She was inventing it, and inventing it loudly, the way you shout at a dog you're afraid of. "It goes in your file, Ferren. Your name goes in the book. And you've seen what happens to names in this guild's books."

That landed. She watched it land — watched him think of the redactions, the erased keepers, the empty chairs in the refectory that nobody could explain why they missed. Fear of Ossory was abstract; fear of the ledger was local. Ferren's hand came off the rail.

"Corvane's finished," someone said behind him, pushing up — a thin woman with silver rings on every finger, one of Ossory's inner circle, whose name Wren had never bothered to learn because learning names was how you became memorable. "Step aside, courier."

"Courier," Wren repeated pleasantly. "Yes. That's me. Nobody important. Which means nobody told me to step aside, so I suppose I'll just stand here."

The woman's rings clicked against the stone rail as she gripped it. "There are forty of us."

"I can count," Wren said. "I count everything. It's my other talent. Forty-one, actually — you left one in the undercroft to watch the door, and he's already gone, by the way. Ran. I saw him go." This was a lie. She hoped desperately it was a lie that would become true.

The woman smiled, thin and patient, and gestured, and four men came up the stairs past Ferren, who had discovered a sudden fascination with the wall.

Wren tightened her grip on the candlestick and thought: *eleven years of being invisible, and this is what it was for.* Not safety. Ammunition.

* * *

They did not, in the end, simply walk over her, and the reason was the thing she would turn over in her mind for the rest of her life: they hesitated. Not out of mercy. Out of *bureaucracy*. Ossory's orders, shouted up the stairwell a minute later, were explicit — the courier was not to be killed, not to be marked, not to be remembered doing anything worth reporting. A blank ledger in the hands of a witness was dangerous; a dead courier was a martyr with a story. They wanted her moved, not harmed, and moving an unremarkable woman who did not wish to be moved turned out to be like trying to arrest a draught.

She ducked the first grab. She was small and quick and she knew every creaking board on the landing, and when the second man lunged she rapped the candlestick across his knuckles — hard enough to sting, soft enough to obey orders — and stepped backward into the shadow of the archive arch, where the lamplight failed and her whole life had been lived anyway.

"Hold the landing!" the ringed woman snapped. "She can't stay in the dark forever."

But Wren could. Wren had been living in the dark between attentions since she was nine years old, when the guild took her in because no family came looking and no record preceded her. She slipped along the wall, breath shallow, and bought another minute, and then another, hearing below her — muffled through floors of stone — the sounds of the vault door being forced, and worse, the sounds of it *not* being forced quickly, which meant Corvane was still holding it, which meant Xanthe still had time.

Then the lights below went out.

All of them. Every lamp on the lower levels, snuffed in a single rolling wave of darkness that climbed the stairwell toward her like a tide, and with the darkness came a smell Wren recognized from the gate chamber — cold stone and old rain and something underneath like a struck match — and she understood, with a lurch, what Corvane was doing.

He was spending himself.

* * *

She found him at the vault door, or rather she found the place where he stood, because by then he was difficult to see. Master Corvane, first keeper's apprentice, four hundred years old and tired down to the marrow of that, had planted himself in the doorway with his palms flat against the stone frame, and the gate's hunger was pouring *through* him instead of around him.

Ossory's people had fallen back from him in a wide, superstitious arc. Even the ringed woman hung at the edge of the torchlight, and the torches themselves burned low and blue, starved.

"Master Corvane," Ossory said, and her voice was gentle, which was the most frightening thing Wren had heard all night. "You don't have to do this. Step aside. We'll let you retire. A cottage somewhere. Quiet. You've earned quiet."

"I had quiet," Corvane said. His voice sounded strange — thinner, as though each word cost him something material. "Four centuries of it. Do you know what I remember of my master, girl? Her face. Nothing else. The gate took the rest the night she fed it her name, and I let it, because I was a coward then too, and it kept me alive as payment. I have been its coward ever since." He laughed, a dry rustle. "I am owed a great deal of courage. I am spending it all tonight."

"What is he doing?" one of the traders whispered.

"He's giving it memories," Wren said, before she could stop herself. Everyone turned. She stepped out of the archway into the failing light, because some things deserved a witness. "His own. That's what the gate eats — didn't any of you ever wonder why the guild erases people? Why the keepers are always orphans, always alone? It doesn't want *memories*, it wants forgotten truths, and a man feeding it four hundred years of them—" she swallowed "—goes out like a lamp."

Corvane's head turned toward her. In the blue gloom his eyes had gone pale as frost. "Courier," he said. "Wren. Yes. I remember your file. Remarkably empty file." A ghost of his old dry humor. "Go down. The gate stair. Xanthe will need—"

"I'm not leaving you."

"You are absolutely leaving me." He said it kindly. "This is not a sacrifice, child, whatever you're imagining. This is an *account settling*. I traded other people's names to keep my own breathing. Consider this the interest." He turned back to the darkness pooling in the vault behind him, and Wren saw — she would swear forever after that she saw — shapes moving in it. Faces. All the faces he had fed away, coming home to collect.

Ossory made her decision then, and it was the decision of a practical woman: she ordered her people up the alternate stair, the traders' passage, toward the gate chamber, around the old man burning himself down in the doorway. Half of them went. The other half — Ferren among them, Wren noted, and filed it away in whatever corner of her heart keeps such things — did not move at all.

Corvane laughed again, softer. "Too late, Madame. Most of me is already gone. Ask me my mother's name." Silence. "Ask me my *own*." More silence, and now even Ossory's composure flickered, because everyone in that hall understood what a man without his own name was standing in front of them: a warning wearing robes.

"Go," Corvane said to Wren, and his voice was barely a thread now, a boy's voice, the apprentice he had been before all of this. "Tell my master's successor — tell Xanthe — the willing gift seals. Stolen memory only feeds. Tell them the old woman got it right, and I got it wrong for four hundred years, and that is the whole of my confession."

And then he opened his arms to the dark, and the dark came into him like a tide into a broken seawall, and the lamps came back up all at once, and Master Corvane was kneeling on the stones of the vault doorway, alive, breathing, blinking at his own hands with the mild polite puzzlement of a stranger — and there was nothing left inside him to take.

Wren ran.

* * *

The gate stair was narrow and cold and it went down farther than the guildhall had foundations, down past the level where the archives ended and the older architecture began, walls of stone that predated the guild, that predated the city, sweating moisture that was not quite water. Wren took the steps two at a time, satchel bouncing against her hip, the blank ledger's weight inside it more felt than real — a plain book, unremarkable, the most remarkable thing in the world.

Behind her, above her, she heard Ossory's faction finding the gate chamber and finding it barred, heard shouting become something uglier, and she did not stop, because stopping was a luxury belonging to people whose friends were not currently dissolving their identities into an ancient door.

At the last turning she nearly ran into Xanthe, who had come up short at the threshold, one hand pressed flat against the gate's surface, and who looked at her with an expression Wren had never seen on that careful face before: terror and certainty in equal measure, fully resolved.

"You held the stair," Xanthe said.

"Forty-one of them," Wren said, breathless. "Minus one who ran. I made him run. Mostly."

"Wren—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't say whatever you were about to say, because I read it. Last night. While you slept. I read every page you wrote in the blank ledger, every truth you smuggled, including the part at the end about who carries it after." Her voice cracked and she let it, because ceremony was for other people and this was just the two of them on a cold stair at the end of things. "So don't say goodbye. Say *hurry*. Say *hold the door*. Say something I can do."

Xanthe looked at her for a long moment. Behind them both, far above, the guildhall shuddered as something gave way — wood, or stone, or the last of the old order.

"Hold the door," Xanthe said. "And Wren — when it's done, when the world forgets — don't try to make them remember me. Just remember. That's enough. That's the whole prophecy, really. Someone remembering is enough."

The gate stirred beneath Xanthe's palm, and the whisper that came out of it was almost tender, and Wren — loyal, unremarkable, unforgettable-against-all-instruction Wren — put her shoulder against the stone beside her friend and held the door.

Beyond the threshold, the light began to change.
