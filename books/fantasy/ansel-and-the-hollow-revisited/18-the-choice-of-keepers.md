## 18. The Choice of Keepers

The stair behind Xanthe had gone quiet.

That was the worst part — not the shouting that had echoed up through the vaults an hour ago, not the crash of Ossory's traders breaking against the guildhall like a wave against a seawall, but the silence that followed it. Silence meant the fighting was over, one way or another. Silence meant the only thing left between Xanthe and the Hollow Gate was whatever waited inside it.

They climbed the last steps with the blank ledger pressed against their chest, its covers cold as river stones through their coat. The keeper's ledger. The one object in all the guild's impossible collection that could hold a truth the gate could not digest — because it held nothing yet, and nothing was the one language the gate had never learned to eat.

The door at the top of the stair was old iron banded with bronze, and it stood open.

Of course it did. Gates that fed on forgotten things had no use for locks. A lock was a memory of a boundary, and the Hollow Gate had forgotten every boundary it had ever been given.

Xanthe stepped through.

* * *

The chamber beyond was round and vast and wrong in the way that dreams are wrong — the ceiling so far above that the lamplight from Xanthe's hand-lantern couldn't find it, the floor worn smooth as a frozen lake. And there, filling the far half of the room like a wound in the world, stood the gate itself.

It was not what the guild's diagrams showed. The diagrams showed an archway, dignified and architectural, something you could point to on a map. What Xanthe saw was a tear in the air shaped like a doorway, its edges ragged and slowly moving, fraying like cloth worried by moths. Through it there was no light and no dark, only a depth that made Xanthe's eyes water to look at, a depth that seemed to be looking back.

And it was *thin*. That was the thing no one had told them, the thing they understood only now, standing close enough to feel the cold pouring off it like breath off winter glass. All the ledgers, all the redactions, all the centuries of erased keepers — and still the gate was thin. Fraying at the edges. Hungry.

"You've been starving," Xanthe said aloud, and their voice sounded small and absurd in the vastness. "All this time. They've been feeding you scraps."

The gate rippled.

Not an answer. Not quite. But the frayed edges stilled for a moment, the way a dog stills when it hears its name in a stranger's mouth, and Xanthe felt the attention of the thing settle over them like a physical weight — vast, patient, and terribly sad.

Behind them, faintly, up through the open door, came the sound of boots on stone. Many boots. Coming this way.

Xanthe turned, set the ledger down on the smooth floor, and pulled out the notes they had written to themselves after Corvane took a week of their life.

*If you are reading this, you already know what erasure costs,* said the first line, in handwriting that was theirs and somehow not theirs, tighter than they remembered writing. *You woke up missing pieces and grieved people you couldn't name. Do not let them do to the world what they did to you.*

Below it, in fresher ink: *The prophecy demands a name willingly given. Read chapter nine again. Read the marginalia. The first keeper underlined the word WILLING three times. Corvane never understood why. I think I do.*

Xanthe read it twice. Then a third time, because fear kept sliding the meaning sideways, the way grief slides a face out of recognition.

* * *

Here is what they knew. Here is what they had paid a week of themselves to know.

The Hollow Gate had not been built. It had been *made* — made of people, in the last years of the old war, when the guild's founders discovered that certain impossible things could be transformed and traded, and that the transformed did not die but *grieved*, collectively, endlessly, and that their grief had weight enough to hold shut the door between the world and the powers that had been loosed through it. Every impossible thing in the vaults below was a fragment of someone. Every bargain the traders brokered was a piece of a person changing hands. And the gate — the gate was the sum of all that sorrow, pressed into the shape of a door, holding back the flood by the simple fact that it hurt too much to open.

Corvane had kept it fed for four hundred years. Not with memories — that was the lie the guild told its own keepers, the comfortable fiction that forgetting was fuel. Memories were only the wrapping. What the gate ate, what it needed, what it starved without, was *truth*. Specific truth. The truth of a person who had been taken apart and told it was necessary. Feed it stolen memories and it stayed shut the way a man stays upright on a rope bridge — barely, briefly, swaying. Feed it a whole truth, freely surrendered, and—

And the first keeper had believed it would stay shut forever.

Xanthe knelt and opened the blank ledger. The pages were white as snowfall, white as the space where a week of their life used to be. Their hands were shaking. They noticed the shaking the way you notice rain on a window — present, irrelevant.

"Corvane gave me a vial once," they said to the gate, because talking kept the fear at a manageable distance, and because some stubborn instinct insisted the gate deserved honesty even now, especially now. "A rare memory-vial. He said it was a gift. It was my grandmother's voice, singing a lullaby I'd forgotten I knew. I sat in my room and listened to it eleven times before I understood he wasn't being kind. He was showing me what I stood to lose."

The edges of the gate stirred, fraying faster. Below, the boots were louder. Somewhere on the stairs, Wren's voice rose — sharp, bright, buying seconds the way she bought everything, cheaply and brilliantly — and then cut off, and Xanthe's heart clenched like a fist around a hot coal, and they forced themselves to keep speaking.

"I know what you are," Xanthe said. "You're not a door. You're the people who were made into a door. You're their grief, and they never agreed to any of it, and that's why you're always hungry. Stolen things don't fill you. They can't. You can tell the difference between a memory that was taken and a truth that was given. You've been able to tell the difference for four hundred years, and everyone who ever fed you lied about which one they were offering."

The gate went very still.

In that stillness, Xanthe heard it — not words, not quite, but something older than words, rising through the floor and up through the soles of their feet and into the bones of their chest. It was the sound the chamber had been making all along, the sound they had mistaken for silence: a low, continuous note of mourning, like wind through a canyon, like ten thousand voices breathing the same ache. And threaded through it, unmistakable now that they listened, a fragment of their own childhood — the smell of their mother's kitchen, the specific creak of the third stair, the afternoon they had lost a tooth biting into a green apple and cried more from surprise than pain.

Their memories. Returned. Offered back like bait, like a beggar's cup held out to a passerby.

"That's mine," Xanthe whispered. "You're trying to give it back so I'll—"

So they would run. So they would clutch their childhood to their chest and flee down the stairs and leave the gate to break, and the city above would drown in everything forgotten, and the war would begin again, and it would be no one's choice at all. The gate was not tempting them. The gate was *begging them not to do this* — because the gate remembered what it was to be made of people who hadn't chosen, and it would not, even now, even starving, take another soul by force.

Xanthe sat down on the cold floor, cross-legged, the way they had sat a thousand nights in the vaults among the impossible things, and wept — briefly, fiercely, without shame — for the door full of grieving strangers that had more mercy in its hunger than the guild had shown in four centuries of stewardship.

Then they wiped their face, picked up the pen, and began to write.

* * *

The ledger took the truth the way dry ground takes rain.

*Xanthe Villalobos, memory-keeper, third rank, entered the Guild of Impossible Things at age nine because they had no family to miss them.* The pen moved and the words appeared and did not fade, and Xanthe felt each sentence leave them the way heat leaves a cooling stone — not painfully, exactly, but irreversibly. *They found tonight's date in the prophecy ledger on the night of the inventory. They learned the guild feeds the gate. They learned the gate eats forgotten truths. They learned the impossible things were people.* With every line, something loosened in their chest; with every line, the gate's frayed edges drew inward, smoothing, strengthening, the tear in the world knitting itself taut as a sail in fair wind.

Willing truth. Given truth. It was working. It was working better than four hundred years of redactions, and the proof was written across the gate in closing light.

But Xanthe could feel the difference between what they were writing and what the prophecy demanded. These were truths *about* them. The gate needed the truth *of* them — the whole of it, the name and the self and every memory anyone alive held of the person called Xanthe Villalobos, surrendered entire. Not a piece. Not a vial. Everything.

The boots on the stairs were very close now. And beneath them, fainter, another sound: Wren, still fighting, still cursing with inventive fluency, still refusing with her whole unremarkable body to let anyone past the landing. Alive. For now.

Xanthe looked down at the ledger, at the growing record of their own existence, and understood — with the terrible clarity that comes after fear has burned itself down to ash — why the first keeper had underlined *willing* three times. Why the marginalia hid the names of every erased keeper in the prophecy's text. Why the final page bore their own name in ink a century old.

The gate could not be sealed by force. Force was how it had been made, and force was the crack running through it. It could only be sealed by the one thing its makers had never been given: consent. A name, freely offered, by someone who knew exactly what they were losing. A keeper who chose to become the lock instead of the key.

Not stolen memory. *Given* self.

"I'm going to write the rest now," Xanthe said to the gate, and their voice did not shake, which surprised them, and then did not surprise them at all, because fear had done its work and finished, and what remained underneath was something steadier — purpose, plain as a lit candle. "Everything. My name, my years, my grandmother's lullaby, the third stair, the green apple. Every memory anyone holds of me, including hers." They thought of Wren and their throat closed, and they pushed through it. "*Especially* hers. She'll hate that. She'll hate it and she'll do it anyway, because she reads everything, and I'm going to make sure the last page tells her to."

The gate shuddered. The note of mourning changed key — deepened, steadied, became almost a chord.

"And in exchange," Xanthe said, "you hold. Forever. Not because you're forced to. Because someone finally gave you what you were owed, and you'll keep faith with it. That's the bargain. That's the whole prophecy. It was never a warning. It was an *offer*, waiting a hundred years for someone desperate enough and stupid enough and willing enough to accept it."

The door behind them boomed — iron meeting iron, once, twice. Voices. Ossory's silk-smooth command, cracked now with something close to panic, because she had seen the gate mending and knew what it meant for everything she'd promised her traders.

Xanthe bent over the ledger and wrote the last lines quickly, before the giving began in earnest and their hands forgot how to hold a pen:

*My name is Xanthe Villalobos. I am choosing this. No one sent me. No one made me. If you are reading this, the gate is sealed by my consent, and it will hold as long as someone remembers that consent was given. Wren — if it's you, and it will be — the ledger is yours now. Keep it where they can see it. Truth hidden is just another kind of forgetting.*

*Tell them my name anyway. Even if they can't hear it. Especially then.*

They signed it. The signature began to fade before the ink dried — not erased, but *translated*, drawn up off the page and into the gate like water into roots, and with it went the knowledge of having signed, the shape of their own hand, the letters of their own name dissolving one by one like sugar in warm water.

X-X-A-N—

The gate opened its depth to them, gentle as dusk.

Xanthe stood, left the lantern burning on the floor beside the ledger — someone should see, someone should *read* — and walked toward the tear in the world with their arms slightly out, the way you approach something wounded.

Behind them the door burst open, and Madame Ossory's voice rang out, furious and afraid: *"Stop them—"*

Xanthe smiled, took one step, and felt the first true memory lift away — the green apple, the surprise, the crying — leaving behind not emptiness but *room*, clean and bright as a blank page.

Somewhere behind them, a courier's voice screamed their name.

And Xanthe, already forgetting whose name it was, stepped through.
