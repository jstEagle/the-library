## 19. Xanthe Villalobos, Erased

The Hollow Gate did not look like a door anymore.

It had never truly looked like one — that had been the guild's first kindness and its first lie, dressing the wound in the world with architecture so that no one would have to call it what it was. But tonight, with the city behind Xanthe screaming in small forgotten voices, the gate had shed even the pretense of stone. It was a hollow shaped like absence, a threshold of nothing ringed by eroded memory-stone, and it breathed.

Xanthe stood before it with the blank ledger under their arm and their heart going like a courier's boots on marble stairs.

Behind them, far below and far away, Wren held the stair. Xanthe could not hear the fighting from here — the gate ate sound the way it ate everything else — but they could feel it, the way one feels weather through a wall: the tremor of Ossory's traders climbing, the stubborn rhythm of one unremarkable woman refusing to be moved. Corvane was down there too, somewhere, spending himself coin by coin. Xanthe had watched him go. The old keeper had pressed his hands flat against the vault floor and let the gate drink a century out of him, and when he looked up at Xanthere had been nothing left in his face but relief.

*Go,* he had said. *I was always going to end as an entry. Let me at least be a useful one.*

Xanthe had gone.

Now there was only the gate, and the ledger, and the last hour of being Xanthe Villalobos.

They sat down on the cold stone at the threshold's edge — close enough to feel its pull, far enough to keep their hands steady — and opened the blank ledger across their knees. The pages were white as undelivered letters. This was the thing about blank keeper's ledgers that no one had understood for a hundred years, the truth buried in the first keeper's marginalia: they were not made to record what happened. They were made to record what was *true*. The gate could digest any memory, any name, any fact scrawled in ordinary ink on ordinary paper. But a keeper's ledger written in willing sacrifice — that, the gate could not swallow without swallowing itself.

Which was, of course, exactly the point.

Xanthe uncapped the inkhorn with fingers that were already beginning to feel like someone else's fingers. That was the strange part, the part no prophecy had prepared them for: it had started already. Somewhere in the city, in the guildhall, in the minds of everyone who had ever known them, the forgetting had begun to move through the world like frost through a windowpane. They could feel the edges of themselves going soft. Their mother's kitchen — was it blue? Had there been a mother? There had been, there must have been, someone had taught them to hold a pen —

*Hold on to it,* they told themselves. *You're the last one holding it. Write it down before you let go.*

And they wrote.

*My name is Xanthe Villalobos.* The words went down dark and certain. *I was born in the Saltmarsh Quarter in the year the plague ships burned, though no record of that fire survives because the guild bought it and fed it to this gate. I had a mother who sang off-key and a father who mended nets and both of them forgot me when I was nine years old, because the Guild of Impossible Things chooses its keepers from children no one will come looking for.*

The ink seemed to sink into the page eagerly, as if the ledger had been waiting a century for someone to tell it the truth.

*I found tonight's date in the prophecy ledger when I was twenty-three years old, and I thought it was a curiosity. It was a summons. Every date in that book is a name, and every name is a person the guild erased to keep this gate shut, and the gate does not eat memories — it eats forgotten truths, truths people agreed to stop knowing, and there is no hunger in the world so patient or so vast.*

Their hand shook. They steadied it against their knee and kept writing, faster now, the way you speak when you know the bell will ring soon.

*Master Corvane was the first keeper's apprentice. He has traded centuries of names for a stalemate he stopped believing in before my grandmother was born, and he is not a villain, he is a coward with excellent manners, and I forgive him, which I did not expect to do.*

*Madame Ossory believes she is merciful. Behind this gate are the impossible things — and the impossible things are people. They were people once, transformed and traded in the war the ledgers burned to hide, and their grief pooled here until it became a door, and everything the guild has done for a hundred years has been done to keep that grief from spilling out into the world. Ossory wants to open the gate and call it justice. Some of what is behind it deserves justice. But grief does not distinguish, when it finally gets out, between the guilty and the innocent, and I have seen enough of erasure to know it never stops where you intend it to.*

*Wren knows. Wren stayed. Whatever the world forgets, someone has to remember, and I choose her, and if you are reading this and you are not her, then find her, because she is the only proof I ever existed and she is worth ten thousand ledgers.*

The gate stirred.

It was not a sound. It was a pressure, a leaning-in, the attention of something vast turning its whole hollow face toward the small figure writing at its feet. Xanthe felt it like a hand hovering over a candle flame — not touching, not yet, but feeling the warmth and wanting.

*I know what you are,* Xanthe said aloud, and their voice came out steady, which surprised them. *You're not a monster. You're a wound. And wounds don't heal because we feed them other people's lives.*

The hollow breathed again, and this time it spoke — not in words, but in the shape of remembering. A fragment rose up in Xanthe like water filling a cup: a kitchen, blue after all, and a woman singing badly, and small hands being guided around the letters of a name. *Xan-the. Two syllables. Say it back.*

"Xanthe," they said. "Villalobos."

*Yes,* said the gate, in the voice of every keeper it had ever swallowed. *That one. We kept that one. We kept all of them. Every name you gave us, we kept, the way a throat keeps a stone.*

Something in Xanthe cracked open — not fear, though fear was there, fear was a live wire running through them — but pity, vast and terrible. The gate had been keeping them. All this time, the guild had believed it was feeding a beast, starving it into stillness, and the beast had been hoarding every stolen name like a miser hoards coins it can never spend, holding the dead keepers' identities safe inside its hollow because no one else would.

"You kept them," Xanthe whispered. "All this time."

*We keep what we are given. It is the only mercy we were left.*

Xanthe looked down at the ledger. The last lines waited, unwritten. Their own handwriting was beginning to look faintly foreign to them, the loops slightly wrong, as though the hand belonged to a stranger they'd once shared a room with. The frost was spreading inward now. They could not remember the color of Wren's eyes — brown, brown, they had been brown, *hold on* — and somewhere below, faintly, impossibly, they heard a stairwell door bang and a voice shouting a name that was already half-gone from the world.

*Xanthe!* Wren's voice, ragged, furious, alive. *XANTHE!*

"I'm here!" they called back, and their voice carried strangely, both everywhere and nowhere. "Wren — listen to me — the ledger — read it, all of it, before the ink goes quiet —"

Below, silence, and then footsteps, many, and a crash, and Ossory's voice rising like silk over broken glass: *"Then we go up. The gate opens tonight, and your little friend can watch from wherever the guild buries its mistakes—"*

No more time. Xanthe bent over the page.

*Here is the truth, the whole of it, the truth the gate cannot digest because I give it freely:* they wrote, and the pen tore slightly at the paper, and they did not care. *A stolen memory feeds the gate forever, because theft is a debt and debts accrue interest. But a name given willingly — a self surrendered by choice, with love, with eyes open — that ends the hunger. That is what the prophecy meant. Not another keeper. Not another century of erasures. One keeper who walks in on purpose, carrying everything, owing nothing.*

*I am Xanthe Villalobos. I am twenty-six years old. I liked rain on slate roofs and the smell of old paper and Wren's terrible jokes. I was afraid my whole life of being forgettable, and it turns out I was right to be afraid, and it turns out I am choosing it anyway, and it turns out those two things can live in the same heart without breaking it.*

*I give the gate my name. I give it every memory anyone holds of me — my mother's kitchen, my father's nets, Corvane's grudging almost-smile, Wren laughing in the archive stacks with dust in her hair. Take all of it. Take it gladly. And seal.*

They signed it. The signature wavered, then firmed, then blazed on the page like a coal.

And Xanthe stood up, closed the ledger, and set it carefully on the highest dry stone at the threshold's edge — placed, deliberately, where Wren would see it first — and turned to face the hollow.

The gate rose up before them, and for the first time in a hundred years, it opened *outward*, like arms.

Xanthe stepped forward.

The taking was not pain. That was the last cruelty and the last kindness of it — it was dissolution, warm as bathwater, thorough as sleep. They felt their name leave them first, peeling away like a label from a jar, and then the memories, each one lifted with a strange tenderness: the blue kitchen, gone; the burning plague ships, gone; the smell of old paper, gone — and with every loss the gate shuddered, because these were not stolen things, these were *gifts*, and gifts do not feed a hunger, gifts *end* it. Xanthe felt the vast hollow convulse around them, felt a hundred years of hoarded names shift and settle like sediment in a settling sea, felt the gate begin — slowly, groaning, disbelieving — to close.

Through the thinning of themselves, Xanthe saw the stairwell door burst open.

Ossory came through first, elegant even in ruin, her traders fanning out behind her with drawn steel and hungry faces, and she raised her arms toward the closing hollow and began to speak — some invocation, some claim, words of old power dredged up from the burned century, words that were supposed to catch the gate like a hook catches a fish —

— and the words simply *stopped working*.

Xanthe, dissolving, watched it happen. Ossory's voice rang out and fell flat, rang out and fell flatter, the ancient syllables finding nothing left to grip, because the gate was no longer a hunger to be aimed, no longer a weapon, no longer anything but a sealed wound with a name written willingly on the inside of it. Her power collapsed mid-claim like a wave meeting glass. She stared at the sealing hollow, and her face did something complicated — fury and grief and, beneath them, unmistakably, relief — before her own traders, seeing the magic die, saw the cause gone, began to back away from her into the dark.

*Good,* Xanthe thought, or tried to think, though thinking was becoming a rumor. *Let her carry that. Mercy she couldn't spend.*

And then Wren was there.

She came through the traders' scattering like weather, unremarkable and unstoppable, and she snatched the ledger off the stone and clutched it to her chest, and she turned to the place where Xanthe had been — where Xanthe mostly wasn't, where Xanthe was a warmth and a fading outline and a pair of eyes doing their very best to stay open —

"Don't," Wren said. "Don't you dare — I just got up the stairs, you absolute — don't —"

"Wren." It barely made a sound. It didn't need to. "Read it. Remember me wrong if you have to. Just — remember."

"I'll remember you *right*," Wren said fiercely, and tears cut clean tracks through the dust on her face, "and I'll write it down, and I'll make them all learn it someday, every last one of them, I swear it on the ledger, I swear it on the guild, I swear it on — on your stupid beautiful name —"

*Xanthe Villalobos,* the gate said, from the inside, in the voice of a keeper coming home. *Kept.*

The last of Xanthe smiled.

The hollow closed like a book.

* * *

Silence, afterward. The particular silence of a thing that has finished breathing.

Wren stood alone in the vault of the Hollow Gate with a ledger in her arms and no memory in the world of the person who had written it — except the words themselves, which she had read twice through by candlelight while the ink was still wet, and which she now carried in the one place no gate could reach.

On the stone where the ledger had rested, the erosion had stopped. The memory-stone was smooth and whole, and faintly, if she tilted her head, warm.

Far above, through the shafts of the guildhall, dawn was coming up gray over a city full of returned ghosts and lost voices and people waking with gaps in their hearts they could not name. Somewhere in that city, Madame Ossory sat in the wreckage of her ambition, and Master Corvane lay empty as a shed skin, and a hundred years of lies had ended in a single night.

And Wren — unremarkable, unforgettable Wren — wiped her face, tucked the ledger inside her coat, and began the long climb back into a world that had never heard the name Xanthe Villalobos.

Someone had to change that.

She knew exactly where to start.
