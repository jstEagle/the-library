## 21. A New Keeper

The guildhall had been scrubbed, but it had not been cleaned.

Wren noticed the difference the first morning she walked its corridors after the siege. The scorch marks on the stair where she'd held her ground were gone — planed away, sanded smooth, stained to match — but the wood remembered differently than the carpenters did. When she pressed her palm flat against the banister she could feel a faint wrongness in the grain, like a word crossed out in a letter someone else had rewritten. The guild had rebuilt itself the way it always had: by making the damage look as though it had never happened.

She let them. That was the arrangement now, the reluctant reform Master Ellery Voss kept calling *a new covenant* in his careful, committee-polished speeches. The traders' wing stood empty, its doors sealed with wax and ribbon. The memory-vials were catalogued publicly for the first time in three centuries, their contents inventoried and, where possible, returned. There was even talk — scandalous, whispered talk — of letting the city know what the Guild of Impossible Things actually did down there beneath the vaults.

But the gate still needed keeping. And so, when Voss asked for volunteers, Wren raised her hand before anyone else could speak.

"You're a courier," he'd said, frowning at her over the long table. "You have no training in—"

"I carried things for this guild for eleven years," she said. "I know every corridor, every shelf, every lock that sticks in damp weather. And I'm unremarkable." She smiled the smile that had gotten her through a hundred checkpoints and a thousand closed doors. "You've read my file, Master Voss. You know how useful that is."

He hadn't argued. Nobody ever argued with Wren for long; being forgettable had its uses even in victory.

* * *

The keeper's quarters were exactly as Xanthe had left them, which was to say they were nothing at all.

That was the cruelty of it, Wren thought, standing in the small round room above the gate stairs with her single trunk of belongings. Erasure didn't leave wreckage. It left tidiness. The bed was made with corners sharp enough to cut. The desk held an inkwell, a lamp, a cup of pens — all of it arranged with the particular neatness of a person who had expected to come back. On the wall hung a calendar page turned to a date two weeks past the sealing, because whoever had tidied this room hadn't known what day mattered.

Wren set her trunk down and did not unpack it. Instead she sat on the bed's edge and looked at the desk for a long time, the way you look at a grave that has been mowed too neatly.

Then she went downstairs and began the work.

The keeper's library occupied three walls of the antechamber: ledgers going back through the reform, back through the interregnum, back — or so the shelves claimed — into the deep centuries. But Wren knew what the shelves claimed and what they held were different things. A hundred years of ledgers had burned or vanished, ending the night the last war ended. And one book more had gone missing from these racks recently, quietly, between one inventory and the next.

It sat now on the fourth shelf from the floor, third position from the left, spine out among forty identical spines, wearing the plain grey binding of a standard maintenance log.

*A blank keeper's ledger*, stolen from the vault on the worst night of Wren's life, filled in a shaking hand at a desk by lamplight while the world forgot its author — and then hidden here, in plain sight, among the very records no one would think to search. Because who looks for a secret book in the archive of secrets? Who reads a maintenance log? The guild's own genius for tedium would guard it better than any lock.

Every evening, before she slept, Wren took it down and read a little.

Not all of it. She'd learned quickly that reading the whole thing in one sitting was like drinking the sea. Xanthe's handwriting marched across the pages in tight, urgent lines — the prophecy decoded, the redactions mapped, Corvane's confession transcribed verbatim, Ossory's arguments recorded fairly, *fairly*, because Xanthe had been fair even to the woman who wanted the gate broken open. And then the last pages, written in ink that had gone strange and slow, the letters widening as though the hand holding the pen were already thinning:

*If you are reading this, then it worked, and I am sorry, and thank you. You will be the only one. Don't carry it like a weight. Carry it like a door.*

Wren always stopped there. She had never once managed to read past that line without having to sit down.

* * *

Corvane came on the first of the month.

He arrived without ceremony, an old man in a plain coat, carrying his own bag, and the sight of him stopped Wren's breath in her throat for a moment longer than she intended. He looked smaller than she remembered. Of course, she remembered him at the height of his power — centuries old, gate-fed, terrible in his weary way, trading names for a stalemate he no longer believed in. Then he had spent himself buying her and Xanthe time on the vault stairs, pouring his hoarded memories out like a man emptying a purse he'd hated carrying.

What remained was a tall, stooped gentleman with white hair and courteous manners and eyes that were politely, profoundly blank.

"Master Corvane," Wren said. "You came."

"The council thought it proper," he said. His voice was the same voice; that was the worst of it. "I'm told I was keeper here for a very long time. They've asked me to assist with your training, such as my recollection allows." He smiled apologetically. "My recollection allows very little, I'm afraid. There are gaps. Large ones. Like rooms in a house where the furniture has been removed and the wallpaper faded unevenly around where things used to hang."

"Do you mind?" Wren asked. "The gaps?"

He considered this with genuine care, turning it over like a craftsman examining a joint. "I mind less than I probably should," he said at last. "Grief requires particulars, and I have none. I know that I lost something. I don't know what. It's rather like mourning a country I've never visited." He set down his bag. "Now. Show me the gate, Keeper Villalobos—"

"Wren," she said quickly. "Just Wren."

"—Keeper Wren," he amended smoothly, "and we shall see what the old house remembers that I do not."

* * *

The Hollow Gate waited at the bottom of nine flights of worn stone, behind a door that no longer needed locking.

It had changed since the sealing. Before, it had been a wound in the world — a hollow shaped like a doorway, hungry, whispering, edged with erosion where the guild's stolen memories had failed to hold it shut. Now it was quiet. The hollow remained, black and door-shaped and utterly lightless, but around its edges the stone had grown something like lichen, pale and faintly silvered, creeping inward year by year. Wren had watched it in the months since. It grew slowly. Patiently. Like frost forming on a window from the inside.

"It's healing," Corvane said, standing beside her at the threshold line — the painted boundary beyond which keepers did not step. He tilted his head, listening to something. "Or scar tissue is forming. With wounds this old, I imagine those are the same thing."

"Do you remember it? Before?"

"No." He said it simply, without flinching. "But my hands remember the rounds. Watch." And he walked the perimeter of the chamber, touching four stones at the compass points, murmuring words Wren didn't recognize — and realized, with a chill that started at her scalp, that neither did he. The words came out of him whole and fluent, a language his mouth knew and his mind had never met. "You see?" he said, mildly embarrassed. "The body keeps its own ledger."

"What does it say? The words?"

"I haven't the faintest idea. Something old. Perhaps you'll learn it, and perhaps one day you'll tell me what I've been saying all these centuries." He smiled at her, warm and unguarded, a man with nothing left to hide because there was nothing left to hide it with. "You're staring, Keeper Wren."

"Sorry." She looked away, at the gate. "You remind me of someone."

"Someone good, I hope."

"Yes," Wren said, and her throat tightened around it. "Someone very good."

* * *

He trained her the way, presumably, he had once been trained — or the way his hands remembered being trained. Each morning they walked the rounds. Each noon they studied the maintenance logs, and here Wren had to be careful, steering him gently past the fourth shelf, third position from the left, whenever his fingers drifted near it. Each afternoon they practiced the threshold-dream discipline: how to stand at the gate's edge and listen without leaning in.

Because the gate still whispered. Quieter now, softer, like wind through a keyhole rather than a voice through a wall. But some nights, when Wren made her solitary rounds with the lamp turned low, she heard it.

And some nights, it wasn't whispers at all.

It was laughter — a specific laugh, quick and startled, the kind a person makes when they've just surprised themselves. It came from behind the black and dissolved before she could hold it. Once, unmistakably, she heard her own name spoken in a tone of exasperated affection, the way only one person in all the world had ever said it.

Those nights she stood at the threshold line until the lamp burned down, and she did not cry, because she had decided somewhere in the months since that crying at the gate might be heard as an invitation. Grief requires particulars, Corvane had said. She had all hers. She kept them folded up small and carried them like a door.

* * *

"You're fond of me," Corvane observed one afternoon, six weeks into her training.

They were in the antechamber, cataloguing vials — the new honest work, logging each one's contents for the public record. Wren nearly dropped the one she was holding.

"I beg your pardon?"

"You smile at me." He said it without accusation, sorting amber vials from green with deft old fingers. "Not the polite smile. The other kind. The kind that has a history in it. I've caught you doing it perhaps a dozen times — when I correct your stance on the rounds, when I mispronounce a word, when I complain about the tea." He set a vial down and looked at her with those blank, courteous eyes. "I don't have the memories to account for it. So I thought I should ask, rather than pretend not to notice. Pretense seems a poor foundation for a working relationship between keepers."

Wren put down the vial. She looked at him for a long moment — this man who had traded centuries of names out of cowardice and then, at the end, out of something better, had given away everything remaining to buy two strangers an hour. He didn't know that. He would never know that. The ledger knew it; the ledger remembered everything; and the ledger sat ten feet away disguised as drudgery.

"You were different, before," she said carefully. "Before whatever took your memories. Harder. You did things you'd have been ashamed of, if you could remember them."

"And now?"

"Now you're just an old man who likes order and complains about the tea." She picked the vial back up. "I find I prefer you this way. Is that a terrible thing to say?"

Corvane was quiet for a moment. "No," he said finally. "I think it may be the kindest thing anyone has told me since I woke up missing. Though—" and here something moved behind his eyes, some sediment of the man he'd been stirring, "—I hope the person I was earned the person you see. Even if I can't recall the earning."

"He did," Wren said. "At the very end. When it cost him everything."

"Good," said Corvane, satisfied, and returned to his vials, and Wren smiled at him — the other kind, the kind with history in it — and he pretended, courteously, not to notice.

* * *

Autumn came to the city above, and the guild settled into its reformed rhythms. Traders came again — fewer, humbler, dealing now in registered wonders instead of stolen souls, and Madame Ossory's name appeared nowhere at all, which troubled Wren more than if it had appeared everywhere. A woman like that did not simply stop. A woman like that waited, elegant and patient, for the reform to grow complacent.

Wren reported her suspicions to Voss. Voss thanked her gravely and filed them, and Wren understood that the new covenant was already learning the old habit of filing things where no one would read them.

So she added a page to the maintenance log on the fourth shelf — the real one, the true one, the one whose ink had gone silent and permanent the night its author dissolved. She wrote in her courier's hand, small and neat:

*Ossory is not finished. Neither am I.*

And below it, because some evenings the silence in the keeper's quarters was too complete, she wrote:

*I taught Corvane to pronounce your name today. He asked who it belonged to. I said a colleague. He said it sounded like a name worth remembering. He's right. It is. I'm remembering it for both of us.*

* * *

On the night of the first frost, Wren made her rounds alone, as she did every night, and paused at the threshold line with her lamp.

The gate was quieter than ever. The silvered lichen had crept another finger's width across the black. And yet — she leaned forward, listening — beneath the silence there was something new. Not hunger. Not whispering. Not even the startled laughter that broke her heart on bad nights.

Attention. The sense of something vast turned toward her, listening the way she listened: patiently, faithfully, waiting to be spoken to.

Wren knelt at the line, the way Xanthe must have knelt a hundred times, and spoke softly into the dark.

"Good evening," she said. "Nothing to report. The guild is behaving. Corvane complains about the tea. Ossory is moving, but I'm watching. And—" her voice caught, steadied, "—you're remembered. Every day. By one person, reliably, without fail. That's not nothing. That's a door held open."

The dark held still. Then, from somewhere impossibly far behind the black — farther than the chamber, farther than the world — came a sound so faint she might have imagined it.

Her name. Spoken kindly. In a voice she would know anywhere, saying it the way only one person ever had.

Wren rose, lifted her lamp, and turned toward the stairs — and froze, because at the top of the steps, lantern in hand, stood Corvane, looking down at her with an expression she had never seen on his blank face before. Not confusion. Not courtesy.

Recognition, arriving like weather.

"Keeper Wren," he said slowly, and his voice had changed, deepened, gathered some sediment of centuries. "Who taught you that greeting? Because I have just remembered — I don't know how, I don't know from where — that someone used to say those exact words to the gate. Every night. For years." His eyes, politely blank for so long, had gone bright and searching. "And I believe I taught them to say it."
