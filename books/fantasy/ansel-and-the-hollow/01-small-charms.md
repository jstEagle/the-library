## 1. Small Charms

The trouble with selling luck in a city that had outlawed it was that everyone wanted some, and nobody would admit to wanting any.

Edmund had learned this the hard way her first winter in Vell, when she'd stood at the corner of Tanner's Row with a tray of knotted red thread and watched respectable people walk wide arcs around her as though she were plague itself. She'd adjusted. Now she sold *cordage*. Decorative cordage. Festival cordage. Cordage for tying back shutters and bundling kindling and — if a customer happened to mention, in the low voice people used for things they were ashamed of, that their roof leaked or their cow was off her milk or their mother hadn't slept a whole night since the fever came — well. Edmund could suggest a particular knot. For structural reasons.

"Knots hold things together," she liked to say. It was true, harmless, and technically not magic at all, which in Vell made it the safest sentence in the city.

She worked under the name Nell Ferrow, which she had chosen because it sounded like someone who mended pots and owed nobody anything. Three years she had been Nell Ferrow. Three years since she'd slipped out of Mother Ilex's cottage in the dark with one bag, two silver pieces sewn into her hem, and the specific, sour terror of a girl who has seen what happens to hedge-witches when the wrong person knocks.

Vell hated magic the way some cities hated rats: officially, loudly, and without ever quite managing to stamp it out. The Wardens burned charms in the market square on the first of every month, a cheerful little bonfire of confiscated amulets and dried herbs and the occasional unlucky apothecary's stock, and the crowd always came, and the crowd always bought more afterward. That was the joke Edmund never said out loud: you cannot burn luck out of people. They carried it in their pockets like lint.

Her stall was a plank across two barrels near the fishmongers, which meant everything she owned smelled faintly of herring by noon, and by evening she'd stopped noticing. Her stock hung from pegs: braided cords in seven colors, little bags of salt-and-iron filings labeled *for damp*, wooden buttons carved with patterns she called "traditional" and which were, in fact, sigils with the dangerous curves filed off. She'd gotten very good at filing the danger off things. It was, she sometimes thought, the only real skill her apprenticeship had left her.

That morning the fog off the canal was thick enough to chew, and the fishmongers were arguing about the price of eels, and Edmund was re-knotting a display cord for the fourth time because her hands wouldn't stay still. They'd been restless for a week. That was the other thing about hiding from your own power: it didn't sleep. It sat in you like a cat that has decided the house is its house now, and it waited.

"Nell! Nell Ferrow!"

Marta Cade came down the row at a pace that made the fishmongers stop arguing to watch. Marta was a washerwoman with forearms like mooring rope and a laugh that could be heard from the temple steps, except that today there was no laugh anywhere on her. She reached the stall and gripped the edge of it with both hands, and Edmund saw that her knuckles were white and her eyes were red-rimmed and terrible.

"It's Tam," Marta said. "My Tam. The fever's gone into his chest. The physician's been twice and he says—" Her voice cracked and she pressed her wrist against her mouth. "He says make ready. He says tonight, maybe."

Edmund's stomach dropped through the plank, through the barrel, through the cobblestones, all the way down to whatever lay under Vell, which was mostly older Vell.

"Marta," she said carefully, "I sell cord."

"I know." Marta leaned across the stall, close enough that Edmund could smell lye and grief. "I know what you are. I've known since the winter my hands cracked open and you gave me that salve that worked better than it should have. I never said. I'll never say." Her grip tightened. "But he's my boy, Nell. He's eight years old and he laughs at everything, even the things that aren't funny, especially those, and I'm asking you to do the thing you're not allowed to do. And I'm asking you to lie to me after, so we both stay safe."

There it was. Three years of careful, gray, unremarkable life, and here was a woman with soap-burned hands asking her to throw it all into the canal.

"I can't," Edmund began.

"You can," Marta said. "I watched you not-do it for three years. A person who can't do a thing doesn't have to work this hard at not doing it."

Edmund looked down at her tray. There was a cord there, plain red wool, braided in the pattern her own grandmother had used for colic — nothing in it, no charm spoken over it, no intent set into it, because setting intent was the crime and Edmund had spent three years being scrupulous about crimes. She picked it up because her hands picked it up before she did.

"For holding on," she said. "Tie it around his wrist. It's just cord, Marta. It holds nothing but itself."

"Just cord," Marta repeated, and took it like it was a relic, folding it into her fist, and went up the row at a run.

* * *

Edmund closed the stall early, which she never did, and walked home the long way along the canal because the long way took longer and she needed the time to be afraid in.

Here is what she had actually done, and she counted it on her fingers as she walked: she had given a woman an unenchanted cord. She had not spoken over it. She had not warmed it in her palms. She had not thought — well. That was the problem, wasn't it. What had she thought? When Marta gripped the stall and said *my boy is dying*, something in Edmund had answered before her mind caught up, some old reflex, some deep-down reaching, the way a hand flinches toward a falling cup. Mother Ilex had called it the listening. *Magic isn't a thing you do,* she used to say, stirring something foul-smelling over the fire while young Edmund pretended not to watch. *It's a thing that notices you. Your whole job is deciding whether to notice it back.*

Edmund had spent three years declining to notice it back. And today, standing behind a plank of fish-smelling wood, she had noticed. She was almost sure of it. Which meant the cord had gone out of her hands carrying something, whether she'd willed it or no, and somewhere across the city an eight-year-old boy was wearing it around his wrist, and either it was string or it wasn't, and by tomorrow morning she would know which.

She climbed the stairs to her room above the chandler's and sat on the bed and did not light the lamp, because lamplight invited looking-through-curtains, and she was in no state to be looked through. In the dark she could hear the chandler below, wrapping candles for the morning, and the canal slapping at its stones, and her own heart, going too fast, going like a knock at a door.

*Answered,* said the thing in her chest, in a voice that was hers and also not hers, the way a reflection is yours and also not. *You listened. I'm here.*

"No," Edmund said out loud, to an empty room, like a madwoman. "No, we agreed. We don't do that anymore."

But the room didn't answer, and rooms never do, and that was supposed to be the comfort of them.

* * *

Near dawn, boots on the stairs.

Not Warden boots — Warden boots came in fours and announced themselves with a battering ram and a speech about the safety of the faithful. These were one pair, hurrying, and then Marta Cade's fist on the door, and Edmund opened it with the chain still on because she was frightened, and Marta stood in the hall with candle-grease light on her face and an expression Edmund couldn't read at all.

"He's asleep," Marta whispered. "The fever broke an hour past. The physician came at midnight expecting a corpse and found him asking for bread and honey. He says he's never seen a turn like it. He says it in the tone men use when they don't want to say the word miracle, because in this city that word gets you examined." She held out her hand. In it lay the red cord, and Edmund's blood went cold, because she thought — returned, refused, failed — but Marta closed Edmund's fingers over it. "Keep it. I won't have it in the house. Not because it did harm. Because of how it looks, if anyone ever asks what healed my son, and the answer is a washerwoman and a piece of string."

"But it's just—"

"Nell." Marta's voice was gentle and absolutely certain. "I tied it on him at sundown. Before that, he was drowning on dry land. After, he slept, and sweated, and woke up hungry. You tell me what that was, if you can. But don't tell me it was nothing, because I held him while he was dying and I know what nothing sounds like."

Edmund stood in the doorway holding a length of red wool that weighed less than a sparrow and felt, in that moment, heavy as a door.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing she turned over and over while Marta waited, patient as stone: Edmund had not enchanted it. She knew exactly what she had done and not done; scrupulousness was her religion, and she kept its ledger honestly. No words. No warming. No intent — she had *tried* for none, though perhaps trying for none was its own kind of something. The knot was a grandmother's pattern, nothing more. And yet the fever had broken within hours of the cord touching his skin, cleanly, completely, in a way fevers did not break in Vell or anywhere else.

Which left only one explanation, and it was worse than all the others. It wasn't that Edmund had worked a charm. It was that something had worked one *through* her — some power that had heard a mother's need and a girl's flinching reach and had moved between them like water finding a crack, without permission, without ritual, without even her consent. Power that answered prayers. And Edmund had not prayed. She had only *listened*.

Mother Ilex's voice, three years dead in her memory, saying: *the gods went quiet when Ansel fell, child, and quiet is not the same as gone.*

The cord sat in her palm. Ordinary. Red. Smelling faintly of herring, like everything she owned.

And beneath her feet, beneath the chandler's shop, beneath the cobbles and the older cobbles, Edmund felt — distinctly, unmistakably, the way you feel a coach pass in the street below — something vast turn over in its sleep, and settle, and begin, very slowly, to pay attention.

Down in the market square, where the Wardens' bonfire would be lit in four days' time, a beggar was kneeling at the shrine-step of a ruined chapel, whispering into the dark. Edmund didn't know that yet. But she would.
