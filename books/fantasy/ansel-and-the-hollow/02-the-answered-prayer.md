## 2. The Answered Prayer

Edmund did not sleep.

This was not unusual — hiding from Mother Ilex had taught her to sleep the way cats do, one ear open, one hand near the door — but this time it wasn't fear of her mistress that kept her staring at the ceiling beam. It was the charm. The fever-charm she hadn't enchanted. She had tied the knot, muttered the nonsense words she always muttered for the benefit of customers who wanted their money's worth of theater, and handed over an ordinary red cord smelling of herring.

And a child had lived.

She lay in her narrow room above the chandler's shop and went through it again, knot by knot, like a woman counting coins to find the one that had been clipped. The first loop — theater. The second — theater. The third, the one where her fingers had cramped and she'd said a word she hadn't meant to say, a word in no language she'd been taught to speak aloud, a word that had felt less like speaking and more like *opening a window* —

There it was. Again. That sensation, faint but unmistakable, of something on the other side leaning in.

"Absolutely not," Edmund told the ceiling.

The ceiling, being Vellish, said nothing. In Vell, ceilings knew better than to talk.

She got up before dawn, because if she was going to be awake she might as well be paid for it, and because the chandler's wife paid extra for early deliveries of the luck-knots to the fish-stalls. She wrapped six knots in waxed paper, told herself very firmly that they were all ordinary, and went down into the grey streets where the gulls were already arguing over the fish-criers' leavings.

The city was quiet the way Vell was always quiet in the hour before Wardens began their rounds — a held-breath quiet, a city of shutters and lowered eyes. Edmund moved through it the way she'd learned to move through everything: quickly, unremarkably, a thin young woman in a patched coat with the face of someone who had never once been interesting. It was a good face. She'd practiced it for three years.

The shrine-step of the ruined chapel was on her way to the fish-stalls. She'd passed it a thousand times without looking, the way everyone in Vell passed everything old without looking, because looking at old things invited questions, and questions invited Wardens, and Wardens invited bonfires.

She looked.

The beggar was still there.

He was an old man, or old-ish — it was hard to tell with street people, whose faces weathered early — wrapped in a coat that had been fine once, before someone else had needed it more. He knelt on the shrine-step with his hands folded and his eyes closed, and his lips moved, and Edmund, who should have kept walking, stopped.

Because the air around him was *wrong*.

Not dangerous-wrong. Not Warden-wrong. It was wrong the way a room is wrong when someone very large has just left it and the warmth of them is still there. The air had a texture. It had *attention*. And the beggar knelt in the middle of it, whispering, and the whisper was not a performance — Edmund knew performances; she sold them by the knot — it was the real thing, a prayer from the bottom of a person, the kind of prayer that costs something.

*Let her be warm tonight. That's all. Just warm. I'll ask nothing else, I know better than to ask anything else.*

Edmund hadn't heard the words. She'd *felt* them, the way you feel a bell through a floor.

She turned to go. That was the sensible thing. That was three years of survival talking, and survival had an excellent record.

She turned to go, and the world *answered*.

There was no thunder. There was no light. That was the terrible thing, Edmund would think later — how quiet it was. It was like watching a door open in a house where no door had been. The air folded. Something came through the fold — not a shape, not a presence, nothing so manageable — but a *vastness*, a pressure of attention so old and so large that Edmund's mind refused to hold it and offered her instead a series of useless images: a winter sea. A hall of pillars. A woman's voice saying a name she couldn't keep.

The beggar's hands, folded on his knees, steamed.

That was all. The cold morning air around his hands rose in a soft white breath, as though he'd been holding coals. And then the fold closed, the vastness withdrew — or turned away, or *sank*, the way the thing beneath the chandler's shop had sunk — and the beggar opened his eyes and looked at his own hands and began, very quietly, to weep.

Edmund ran.

Not far. Three streets' worth of running brought her to the mouth of Tanner's Alley, where she put her back against the wall and breathed like a landed fish and told herself, in the voice she used for frightened apprentices, that she had imagined it. That it was dawn-light and hunger and the fact that she hadn't slept. That the old gods had been dead for two hundred years, that Ansel had fallen, that the Wardens burned anyone who said otherwise, and that the entire architecture of her survival depended on the dead staying dead.

Her hands were shaking.

She looked at them. Ordinary hands, chapped at the knuckles, smelling faintly of herring. And beneath her feet — faint, distant, patient — she felt the vastness settling again, somewhere deep, like a great body turning over in a bed.

"Go back to sleep," she whispered.

It did not answer. That was, in its way, worse.

* * *

She told herself she wouldn't go back. She went back.

By midmorning the fish-stalls were sold out of luck-knots — the chandler's wife reported, with the satisfaction of a woman whose superstitions had just been vindicated, that the herring catch had been the best in a season — and Edmund had a pocketful of coppers and a problem, and the problem had a shrine-step.

The beggar was gone. The step was not empty.

Edmund stood at the mouth of the lane and stared, and the longer she stared the less she could make herself believe it, because the shrine-step was *missing*. Not broken, not burned, not cracked. Missing. There was a shape in the world where the step had been, and the shape was absence — not shadow, not darkness, but a place her eyes slid off of, a patch of morning perhaps two feet across that simply declined to be looked at. The cobbles around it were intact. The chapel wall behind it was intact. But where the kneeling man had prayed, and where the vastness had leaned through to listen, there was now a hole in the morning, neat as a coin cut from cloth.

She approached it the way you approach a sleeping dog. Sideways, slowly, apologizing in advance.

Up close it was worse. The absence had an edge — a fine, clean edge, like the rim of a cup — and when she held her hand near it, not touching, never touching, she felt a cold that was not temperature. It was the cold of a place where something had been *spent*. Where something had been taken out of the world to pay for a beggar's daughter to be warm for one night.

*Every charm costs,* Mother Ilex had said, in the kitchen, in another life, with her hands flat on the table. *That's the first law and the last. Nothing comes through for free. The question is only who pays.*

Edmund had been fifteen and clever and had said, *And who pays for hedge-charms, then?*

*You do,* Ilex had said. *A little. Every time. That's why I teach you to make them small.*

Edmund looked at the hole in the world, and thought about the size of the thing that had come through to answer a prayer about warmth, and thought about what a thing like that might cost, and to whom, and where the bill was being delivered.

And then she saw the sigil.

It was on the chapel wall, just above the absence, faint as breath on glass — she'd have missed it entirely if the morning light hadn't caught it at an angle. A mark like a knot, or a door seen from above, or a keyhole drawn by someone who had never seen a key. She knew it. Her stomach knew it before her mind did, dropping the way it drops on a stair that isn't there.

She had seen it inked in Mother Ilex's forbidden book, the one kept wrapped in oilcloth beneath the loose hearthstone, the one Edmund had been shown exactly once, with the shutters closed and the wards lit, while Ilex said in a voice Edmund had never heard her use anywhere else: *This is Anselic. If anyone ever asks you where you learned it, you learned it nowhere. If anyone ever asks you what it means, it means nothing. Swear it.*

Edmund had sworn. Edmund had spent three years not thinking about it.

Now she stood in a Vellish street with Wardens' boots due on the cobbles within the hour, staring at an Anselic rite scorched into a wall above a hole where a piece of the world used to be, and she understood — with the slow, sick clarity of a person reading their own name on a list — that this was not a haunting. Not a remnant. Not some old power grumbling in its sleep and leaking a little at the seams.

The old gods were *answering prayers*. Deliberately. Recently. Here, in Vell, in the city that had burned them out of memory.

And every answer left a wound.

"Morning, miss."

Edmund's heart attempted to leave through her ears. A fishwife stood at the stall across the lane, wringing out her apron, nodding at the shrine-step with the easy familiarity of someone commenting on weather.

"Queer thing, that," the fishwife said. "Old Tam's been begging on that step for years, poor soul, and this morning he's gone — off to the tannery district, they say, walking like a man with somewhere to be. And the step's—" she waved a hand, groping for a word, and settled on, "—*off*. Like it's been borrowed." She shrugged. "Well. Wardens will have it patched by evening, or they'll burn someone for it. Either way." She gathered her baskets. "Best not to look at it, miss. Looking's how they catch you."

She bustled off. Edmund stayed.

The absence sat in the wall's shadow, patient as a debt. And beneath her feet, beneath the cobbles, beneath everything — faint, vast, and unmistakably *awake now* — Edmund felt the old thing settle its attention on her like a hand on the back of her neck.

Not menacing. That was the horror of it. It felt like being *recognized*.

She thought of the fever-child, healed by a charm she hadn't enchanted. She thought of the word she'd said without meaning to, the window she'd opened without knowing it was a window. She thought of Mother Ilex, three years gone, who had taught her one Anselic sigil and made her swear it meant nothing.

*Nothing*, Edmund thought, staring at the mark, *does not leave holes in the world.*

The first Warden's boots rang out at the end of the lane. Edmund pulled her collar up, fixed her uninteresting face in place, and walked — quickly, unremarkably — toward home, while behind her the absence waited on the shrine-step like an unpaid bill, and somewhere far beneath the city, something vast and old and patient began, very slowly, to remember her name.
