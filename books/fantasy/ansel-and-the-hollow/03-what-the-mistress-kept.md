## 3. What the Mistress Kept

The courier arrived on a Tuesday, which Edmund would later think was typical of Mother Ilex even from beyond the grave — never dramatic timing, never a storm-lashed midnight, just a gray Tuesday with the smell of cabbage on the wind and a man at the door of her rented room holding a package wrapped in oilcloth and tied with string that had been knotted in a way that made her stomach drop.

She knew that knotting. It was Ilex's luck-knot, the double-looped one she'd taught Edmund to tie before she'd taught her letters. *If you can tie this,* the old woman had said, *you can hold anything together for one more day.*

"You're Marchetti?" the courier asked.

"No," said Edmund, who was not, and had not been for three years.

He looked at the knot, then at her face, then at the knot again, and held out the package anyway. "Sign as whoever you like," he said wearily. "I've delivered six of these today. All the same hand. All the same instructions: *deliver to the apprentice, wherever she's hiding.*" He pronounced *hiding* the way one pronounces *the plague*. "Whoever your mistress was, madam, she knew what she was doing."

Edmund signed as *Mrs. Pettibone*, which was the name on her lease, and closed the door, and sat down on her narrow bed with the package in her lap like something that might bite.

She did not open it right away. That was another thing Ilex had taught her: *fear is information, girl. Read it before you act on it.* So she read it. Her fear said: *she's dead.* Her fear had been saying it since midwinter, when the letters stopped coming — those brief, sharp, unsigned notes that arrived every month or so, always the same message in different words: *Stay small. Stay dull. Don't heal anyone you like.*

And underneath the fear, quieter, worse: *she sent this knowing.*

* * *

The first letter was dated eleven years ago, the year Edmund came to her.

*Girl —*

*You were asleep when I decided, so I'll tell you now while you can't argue. You have a look about you. Not talent — don't preen — anyone can be taught talent, and most people should refuse the lesson. A look. Like a door that doesn't quite sit flush in its frame. I have seen that look once before, in a country that no longer exists, and I swore I would never teach its owner anything.*

*I am going to teach you everything anyway. Pray I'm wrong about why.*

*— Ilex*

Edmund read it twice. Eleven years. Eleven years of being told she was mediocre — *adequate hands, no more* — of scrubbing cauldrons and memorizing herb-lore while Ilex gave the real lessons to nobody at all, of being praised so rarely that each crumb felt like a feast. And all along, apparently, there had been a *look*.

She unwrapped the second letter. The oilcloth crackled; inside, beneath the papers, was something small and hard that she didn't yet look at, because looking at it would make all of this real, and she wanted four more minutes of unreality, thank you very much.

*Girl —*

*You asked me last night why I never let you charm anything bigger than a wart. Here is the true answer, since you'll only sulk otherwise. Magic in Vell is outlawed because Vell remembers Ansel. Ansel prayed, and Ansel was answered, and the answers ate holes in the world big enough to march an army through. The usurper's grandfather watched a village walk into one of those holes singing. He built his throne on making sure it never happened again.*

*You are going to ask how I know all this. I know because I was there. Because my teacher was the keeper of the Hollow, and I ran from her the way you will someday want to run from me, and I will deserve it less than she did.*

*Burn this.*

*I know you won't. You keep everything. It's one of the three things I love about you. Don't ask me the other two; I'm saving them.*

*— Ilex*

Edmund laughed, which startled her, and then the laugh turned into something else halfway through, the way laughter does when grief has been waiting politely by the door. She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until the room stopped swimming.

*Don't ask me the other two; I'm saving them.*

She had saved them up for years, those two unspoken things, like coins in a jar. Now the jar was sealed forever and she'd never get to count them.

* * *

The third letter was shorter, and newer, and the handwriting had gone thin and spidery, the hand of a woman writing against time.

*Girl —*

*They are asking after my apprentices. Inquisitors, grey coats, very polite, which is how you know they mean it. I have given them a list of names, none of them yours, and a description of you that would embarrass a turnip. By the time you read this I will have arranged matters so that they stop asking after anyone at all. Don't be proud of me. It wasn't brave. I was old and tired and I have always been better at endings than beginnings.*

*Now the part I have dreaded writing for eleven years, so I'll write it plainly, because plainly is the only kindness left to me.*

*I did not choose you for your talent. Your talent is real but ordinary, and I am sorry for every time I let you believe otherwise, and sorrier for every time I let you believe nothing at all. I chose you for your blood. You are the last of the Anselic line — yes, THAT line, stop making that face, you can't hear me anyway. The Hollow answers to Ansel's blood and to nothing else in this world. When the kingdom fell, the blood scattered like seed, and I have spent forty years following the scatter, and it ends with you, girl. A hedge-witch's foundling with ink-stained fingers and a temper like wet gunpowder.*

*I could have told you and kept you close. But a secret known is a secret hunted, and I wanted you to have ten good years of being nobody. Nobody is the safest country there is. I lived there myself for a long while. The rent is terrible but the neighbors are quiet.*

*They will come for you now. Run if you must — running has kept you alive this long and I taught you that too. But hear me: the prayers are being answered again. I feel it in my bones the way one feels weather. Something old has woken hungry, and it will find the door, and the door is you whether you open it or not.*

*Be angry with me. Anger keeps a body warm. Then be careful. Then, when you're ready — and only then — go west, to Ostra, and find the cartographers. They'll know why.*

*The other two things I love about you: you mend what you break, and you never once asked me to be your mother, though God knows you needed one and I was half one already.*

*There. Counted.*

*— Ilex*

* * *

Edmund sat for a long time with the letters fanned out on her blanket like a losing hand of cards.

Betrayal came first, because betrayal is loud. Eleven years of deliberate smallness. Every scolding, every withheld lesson, every *you're adequate, girl, adequate* — designed. Her exile hadn't been flight at all; it had been an inheritance held in trust, doled out monthly in unsigned notes, and she had swallowed it whole and called it survival. Even her hiding place had been chosen for her. She had thought herself clever, slipping out of Vell under a false name, and the whole time she'd been a parcel moving along a route some stubborn old woman had mapped decades ago.

Then grief arrived, quiet, and sat down beside betrayal like an old married couple who no longer need to speak.

Because here was the thing she couldn't argue with: Ilex had loved her. Badly, obliquely, in the language of knots and burnt letters and two saved-up confessions released only posthumously — but truly. She had spent her last years forging documents and lying to inquisitors and dying, probably uncomfortably, so that a girl who wasn't even talented could have a decade of being nobody. And Edmund had run from her. Had spent three years flinching at her own shadow, certain the old woman meant to drag her back into something dreadful, when all Ilex had ever done was stand between her and the dreadful thing with her arms out.

"I'm angry with you," Edmund said aloud to the empty room. Her voice cracked on the second word. "I hope you know that. Wherever you are. I'm furious."

The room, being a room, said nothing. But the candle flame leaned toward the window, toward the west, the way flames do when there's no draft at all, and Edmund decided firmly not to notice.

Finally she reached into the oilcloth and took out the thing she'd been avoiding. It was a ring — plain iron, worn smooth, sized for a finger smaller than hers but not much smaller. Inside the band, scratched in a script she recognized from Ilex's forbidden lessons, from the one page of the Anselic rite she'd ever been allowed to glimpse: a single word.

*Keeper.*

Her hand shook. She put the ring on the table, then picked it up again, then set it down across the room on the windowsill, as if distance would help, as if it were a spider.

It was full dark when the knocking started downstairs — not her door, the landlady's, and voices, polite voices, grey-coat voices, asking Mrs. Pettibone in tones of great courtesy whether she had received any correspondence lately, anything unusual, anything *knotted*.

Edmund blew out the candle. She packed in the dark the way Ilex had taught her — everything essential, nothing heavy, the letters against her heart where they'd do the most good and the least harm. At the window she paused, one leg over the sill, and looked back at the little room that had almost been a life.

"Nobody was a nice country," she told it. "But the rent was a lie."

Then she went over the sill and down into the alley, west, toward Ostra, carrying a dead woman's love and a living kingdom's blood and absolutely no plan whatsoever — which, she suspected, was exactly as intended.
