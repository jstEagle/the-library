## 4. The Free Cities Confer

The road into Ostra was lined with prayer-flags, which would have been charming if they hadn't been nailed up by a city government that had outlawed prayer twice in the last century and un-outlawed it once, in a panic, three weeks ago.

Edmund walked beneath them with her hood up and her luck-knots hidden in the false bottom of her flour sack, because some habits were older than sense. Three years of Vell had taught her that magic was like a limp: you learned to walk around it, and eventually you forgot it was there until someone made you run.

Ostra did not let anyone forget anything. The city announced itself first by smell — salt, tar, frying onions, and under all of it the green reek of the harbor weed they burned to keep the plague-ships honest — and then by noise. Vell whispered. Ostra *argued*. Even the gulls sounded like they were litigating something.

She had come for two reasons, and she was only proud of one of them.

The good reason: Mother Ilex's letter had named Ostra as the place to go if everything went wrong, and everything had gone wrong with an enthusiasm Ilex would have admired. The bad reason: the letter had also said *they will ask after my apprentices*, and Edmund had decided, in a fit of courage she already suspected she'd regret, that it was better to be asked than found.

So she presented herself at the Harbor Registry, gave her true name for the first time in three years — it came out of her mouth rusty, like a coin from a drain — and said, "I'm told you're collecting people who know things."

The registry clerk, a woman with ink to her elbows and the expression of someone who had stopped being surprised by the world sometime during a previous war, looked her over.

"Know things how?"

"Charms. Knots. Hedge-work." Edmund hesitated. "And I can read Anselic."

The clerk's pen stopped moving.

"Can you," she said, in the tone of a woman writing down *can read Anselic* in very large letters. "Stay here. Don't leave. Actually — no, do leave, we haven't room, but leave a way to find you and don't make me regret the courtesy."

*They will ask after my apprentices.* Edmund left her name and the address of a lodging house above a chandlery, and spent the walk there telling herself that presenting yourself voluntarily to authorities was not the same as surrendering. It was, she decided, more like opening a door before someone kicked it in. You got to choose which way it swung.

The lodging house was full. Of course it was. Half the free cities seemed to be walking west these days, carrying what they could, and what they could carry was mostly stories.

* * *

She heard the first story that evening, in the chandlery's common room, from a drover with burned sleeves and nothing else burned, which was itself the strange part.

"No ash," he kept saying, to anyone who'd listen. "That's what I'm telling you. No ash. The village was just — gone. Not burnt. *Gone.* Like a page torn out. My cousin's farm sat next to it and his fence still stands, right up to where the edge used to be, and past the fence there's just—" he waved a hand vaguely, "—light. Bright, like noon, but soft. You could look straight into it. Halvard's people say a man prayed there. Begged, loud, for days. For rain, or for his wife's fever to break, or both. And something answered him all at once."

"And the village?" someone asked.

"Took the answer wrong," the drover said. "Or took too much of it. I don't know. But the hole where it stood is round, and perfect, and there's grass growing on our side of it that's greener than any grass I've ever seen, and I'll tell you what, I didn't stay to count blades."

The common room had gone quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when everyone is privately checking their own prayers against the story. Edmund sat with her hands around a cup of barley tea and felt the charm-scar on her palm — the one from the child in Vell, the healing that had moved through her without her leave — itch like weather coming.

Something vast was answering. And answers, she was beginning to understand, were heavier than the world knew how to hold.

* * *

The council convened in the Salt Hall the next morning, and by some bureaucratic miracle — or perhaps because a girl who could read Anselic was now officially a resource — Edmund was given a seat on the visitors' bench, behind a rope, among clerks and couriers and one extremely old woman who was either asleep or dead and whom nobody checked on, which told Edmund a great deal about council meetings everywhere.

The Salt Hall was built like an upturned boat, all pale curved beams, and its walls were hung with maps going back four hundred years. Edmund counted six different renderings of the kingdom of Ansel, each drawn after its fall, each a little wronger than the last. Borders guessed at. Rivers invented. One map, the oldest, had simply written across the whole territory in faded brown ink: *HERE THE KINGDOM WAS.*

"They argue about whether to fight," murmured the woman beside her, who turned out to be neither asleep nor dead but a courier named Pell, "or whether to hand over whoever the usurper wants and hope he goes away. It's been three days. They've voted on lunch more decisively."

On the floor below, a broad man with a general's sash was saying, "—cannot fight what we cannot see. Corvane's columns are forty thousand strong and every one of them believes he is a physician. You do not out-argue a physician. You show him the patient is healthy."

"And how," asked a thin woman with a merchant's chain, "do you propose to prove health? By pretending the gods aren't back? Halvar's Ford is *gone*, General. Ashen Vale is gone. Two hundred souls, unburned, unmourned, unrecoverable. That is not health. That is a symptom."

"It is a symptom of *prayer*," the general shot back, "which this chamber outlawed for good reason thirty years ago, and then un-outlawed in a panic, and I note the panic has not aged well—"

"The gods are not back," said a new voice, dry as old paper. "That is precisely the problem."

Edmund leaned forward despite herself. The speaker was small and gray and seated apart from the factions, with a great roll of paper across her knees like a sleeping cat.

"Explain, Cartographer," said the merchant woman, with the weariness of someone who had heard the explanation before.

Sefa — for so a clerk addressed her, Sefa of the Survey — unrolled a portion of the paper and held it up. It was a map, but unlike any Edmund had seen: instead of borders it showed absences. Places marked not with towns or rivers but with clean white circles, each ringed in fine black line, each labeled in a careful hand. *Halvar's Ford. Ashen Vale. Little Marrow.* And at the center of the sheet, larger than all the rest, a circle so big it bent the map's edges around it, labeled with a single word:

*Ansel.*

"The old gods have not returned," Sefa said. "Returned implies they went somewhere and came back. What my maps suggest—" she tapped the great circle, "—is that when Ansel fell, something was done to shut them *out*. A door, sealed. And doors, honored colleagues, do not care how long they are shut. Only how well. The seal is failing. Every answered prayer is not a god arriving. It is a god *leaking through a crack* — and the crack widens with each answer, and what leaks through does not distinguish between a beggar's hunger and a village standing in the way of it."

Murmurs. The general said, "Then we re-seal it. Find the door and nail it shut."

"With respect, General, we do not know where it is. We do not know what it is. My professional opinion—" and here Sefa's voice did something complicated, grief wearing the costume of precision, "—is that the door is in Ansel. Which is to say, in the place where Ansel was. Which is to say, nowhere, because Ansel is not anywhere anymore. Except—" she paused, and Edmund saw her choose her next words like steps across ice, "—except that lately, my instruments disagree. Lately the surveys suggest that parts of Ansel are somewhere again. That the ghost country is becoming *country*. Roads that appear on no chart and vanish when followed. Travelers reporting fog that smells of woodsmoke and bread. If the door is opening, it is opening *from both sides*."

Silence, of the heavy kind.

And Edmund, on the visitors' bench, with her scarred palm pressed flat against her knee, thought: *I know why it opens from both sides. I am the both sides.*

She said nothing. She was, after all, still a runaway; three years of hiding do not evaporate in an afternoon. But her heart had begun doing something traitorous and new — it had started *leaning toward* the argument instead of away from it.

* * *

She found Sefa that evening, because of course she did; Edmund had never in her life successfully walked away from a locked door, and Sefa was a woman made entirely of locked doors.

The cartographer worked in a tower room at the Survey House, surrounded by instruments Edmund couldn't name and maps she could, including — she caught her breath — a fresh survey of the ghost country, done in silver ink on dark paper, roads glowing like veins.

"You're the Anselic reader," Sefa said, without turning. "Pell mentioned you. Sit. Tell me if this word is right." She pointed to a label on the silver map. It was written in old Anselic script, the letters half-familiar from Mother Ilex's forbidden lessons, lessons learned by candle stub with the shutters barred.

Edmund leaned close. "*Vael-harrow,*" she translated slowly. "No — not harrow. *Threshold.* 'The Weeping Threshold.' Or... 'the Threshold That Grieves.'"

Sefa was quiet a moment. "I've been calling it the Hollow," she said. "A guess. It's good to be less wrong than yesterday." She turned then, and studied Edmund with eyes the color of harbor water — quick, kind, and missing absolutely nothing. "You read it like someone who was taught properly. Who taught you?"

"A hedge-witch," Edmund said. "In Vell. She's dead now."

"Mm," said Sefa, in the tone of a woman adding a pin to a private map. "And did your hedge-witch teach you why the ghost country smells of woodsmoke and bread? Because mine says memory. But memory doesn't bake."

"I wouldn't know," said Edmund, who knew exactly, whose dreams for three nights running had smelled of Ilex's kitchen, of rosemary and woodsmoke, of a house that had never existed in any kingdom she'd lived in.

"No," Sefa agreed mildly. "You wouldn't." She rolled up the silver map with unexpected gentleness, the way one handles a sleeping thing. Then she looked at Edmund directly, and something in her face opened — an offer, shy and enormous.

"There's a table in this room," Sefa said, "that has been short one chair for eleven years, since my partner walked into the fog east of here following a road that wasn't there, and didn't come back. I have work no one else can do and no one to drink tea with while I complain about it. You read Anselic. You flinch at the word *Hollow* like it owes you money. Whatever you're carrying, girl, you don't have to carry it standing in a doorway. There's a chair. It's a good chair. I carved it myself, which is why it's ugly."

Edmund opened her mouth to say something deflecting, something practiced, something with an exit built into it.

And that was when the bells began.

Not the harbor bells, not the alarm bells — the *council* bells, the ones that meant the Salt Hall had finally finished arguing, and from the direction of the tower window Edmund could see torches converging on the hall from every quarter, and runners spilling out of its doors with messages, and over the rooftops came the smell of smoke that was not hearth-smoke.

Pell burst in without knocking, breathless, ink-stained, terrified.

"Sefa — the scouts — Corvane's army isn't forty miles out, he's *twelve*, and—" she gulped air, "—and there's a rider at the west gate under a parley flag. He's asking for someone by name. Not the council. Not the generals." She swallowed. "He's asking for Mother Ilex's apprentice."

The ugly chair sat between them, empty, offered, belonging.

Edmund looked at it — at the belonging she'd wanted her whole life, sitting right there within reach — and heard herself say, "That's me."
