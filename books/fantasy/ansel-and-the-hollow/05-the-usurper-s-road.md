## 5. The Usurper's Road

Corvane had learned long ago that armies moved at the speed of their slowest argument, and his army was full of arguments.

"The mules again," said Sergeant Vess, falling into step beside him on the third morning out of Carrow Ford. She was a broad woman with a face like a closed door and the only officer he trusted to tell him unpleasant things before breakfast. "Farrier says it's the hooves. I say it's the mules' opinions."

"Opinions?"

"They've decided they don't like the road, sir. You can see it in their faces."

Corvane looked down the column. Two hundred wagons, four thousand men, and somewhere near the front, forty mules whose expressions did indeed suggest a settled and theological objection to the journey west. "Mules are entitled to their convictions," he said. "So are we all. Have the farrier do what he can."

"And the men, sir? There's talk."

"There's always talk."

"Not like this." Vess lowered her voice, which for her was roughly the volume of ordinary speech. "They're saying the villages ahead of us — the ones that aren't there anymore — that prayers got answered there. That a man prayed for rain and got rain enough to drown his own fields. That a woman prayed her sick boy well and the fever left him so fast it took the color out of everything around him. Left him healthy as a horse in a house gone grey as ash."

"You don't believe that."

"I believe the houses are grey," Vess said. "I've seen the reports. Grey houses, grey grass, a well full of water nobody will drink because it tastes, one scout said, *like being remembered*. Whatever's happening out here, sir, it isn't weather."

Corvane said nothing for a while. The road unspooled ahead of them through country that grew stranger by the mile — not damaged, exactly. *Thinned.* As if someone had been standing at a great loom and pulling threads out to see what would happen.

"It isn't mercy either," he said at last.

"No, sir?"

"Whatever is answering those prayers, Sergeant, it does not love the people it answers. A loving thing checks its work. This—" he gestured at the pale fields, "—this is something old waking up with no hands left, trying to touch the world through whatever hole it can find. And every touch burns."

Vess chewed on that. "You make it sound almost sad."

"It is sad. Sad things kill you just as dead as angry ones. Often more thoroughly, because they don't notice they're doing it." He adjusted the strap of his satchel — the heavy one, the one with the instruments and the sealed orders and the small worn book he never let anyone see. "Have the priests double the wards tonight. And Vess — if any man in this column prays, quietly, where he thinks no one sees — I want to know about it. Not to punish. To count."

"Count, sir?"

"How many of my own soldiers are asking the enemy for help."

* * *

The enemy. He used the word deliberately, even in his own head, because the alternative words were worse. The old gods were not supposed to be an enemy. They were supposed to be nothing at all — a story grandmothers told, a shape in old stonework, the reason Ansel had been great once and then, when the gods went silent, merely a kingdom like any other until the usurper's grandfather had taken it apart.

Corvane had made a career of the space between *nothing* and *enemy*, and he was very good at it. Twenty years hunting hedge-covens, relic-mongers, whispering priests, the whole soft underground of belief that always regrew like moss after a fire. He was not cruel about it. That was what his enemies — the human ones — never understood. Cruelty was inefficient. Fear made believers lie low; kindness made them come to you. He had sat in a hundred kitchens drinking tea with women who charmed fevers into knots, and most of them had simply wanted to help their neighbors, and he had mostly let them, and the ones he hadn't let go had earned it.

But this — holes burned in the world by answered prayer — this was different in kind, not degree. This was the disease itself walking around in daylight.

He stopped at noon in a village called Merrow's End, or rather, in the place where Merrow's End had been instructed to stop existing.

It wasn't destroyed. That was the thing his reports kept failing to convey and that he needed to see with his own eyes. Destroyed meant rubble, ash, bodies, grief. This was *absence wearing the shape of a village*. The houses stood. The lane ran between them. A cart sat by a fence with its load of turnips still neatly stacked. But everything was the grey of a thing remembered rather than seen, and when Corvane walked down the lane his boots made no sound, and neither did anything else, and the silence had a texture to it, patient and enormous, like the inside of a held breath.

At the center of the village square stood a shrine. Old work — pre-Usurpation, maybe older. The stone was worn smooth by centuries of hands, and carved into it was a sigil: a circle broken open at the top, with a line descending through the gap like light through a door.

Anselic. He knew it from a hundred confiscated books. The sign of the Hollow — the door the old kings had supposedly tended, the wound in the world they'd called holy.

In the dust before the shrine, someone had knelt. The knee-prints were still there, sharp-edged, recent. And radiating out from them, burned into the ground in a perfect ring some thirty feet across, was a pattern like frost — except the frost was made of *gone*, of grass and stone and soil simply subtracted, leaving a ring of clean nothing that hummed faintly against his teeth.

His lieutenant Pell crouched at the ring's edge, careful as a man at a cliff. "Scout says a beggar came through two days back. Half-starved. Knelt here and asked — the scout heard him — asked to be *somewhere warm*, just for a night, just once."

"And?"

"He woke up in the next town over, sir. Forty miles east. Alive, fed, confused. And this place—" Pell gestured at the grey village, "—wasn't here anymore. Not ruined. Just... filed away. Like the answer cost more than the question was worth."

Corvane stood a long time looking at the knee-prints in the dust.

A starving man had prayed, and something vast had rearranged the world to oblige him, and the price had been a village — its people, presumably, spilled out somewhere, alive but unmoored, or perhaps not. Perhaps worse. The god hadn't checked its work. The god hadn't *cared*. It had heard a voice in the dark and reached through the nearest hole with fingers too big to feel what they crushed.

"Yes," he said quietly. "This is what I thought."

"Sir?"

"This is a disease, Pell. And diseases spread toward warmth. Toward cities. Toward crowds." He turned back up the lane, boots silent on the grey stones, and hated how silent. "West of here lies Ostra. Free city, sixty thousand souls, a council that thinks surrender is a strategy. If this reaches them—"

"Then we'd best reach it first."

"We intend to." He paused at the edge of the village, where sound resumed like water closing over his head, and allowed himself one breath of ordinary noise before giving the order to march.

* * *

That night, in his tent, with the camp settling around him in its familiar symphony of coughing and bad singing and Vess shouting at someone about stakes, Corvane took the small worn book from his satchel.

It was a psalter. Cheap printing, cheap binding, thirty years old. On the flyleaf, in a young man's careful hand, was written: *For Lira, who taught me that being listened to is the whole of love.*

Lira had been his wife for six years. Their daughter, Maren, had lived eleven days.

He had prayed then. He remembered it with a clarity that never dulled: kneeling on the stone floor of a roadside chapel while the midwife's footsteps faded, praying with the whole desperate animal force of him, promising anything, offering anything — his career, his certainty, his soul, all of it stacked on the altar like coins. *Take me instead. Take anything. Only listen.*

The gods had been silent for a century by then. He had known that. Everyone knew that. And still he had prayed, because grief makes theologians of us all, and because some part of him — the part he had spent twenty years cutting out of other people — had believed that somewhere behind the silence, something was listening, and that listening was the whole of love.

Nothing had answered. Maren had stayed dead. And Corvane had walked out of that chapel into a life's work, tidy and purposeful and cold, built on a single article of faith: *better a world with no gods than a world where they sleep while children die.*

Now the gods were waking. Now prayers were being answered, extravagantly, terribly, at the cost of villages and fields and the fabric of the world itself. And Corvane found, examining himself honestly by lamplight, that he felt no vindication at all. What he felt was closer to nausea. Because he knew — he knew with the intimacy of a man who had once knelt in that same posture — that if the thing in the grey village had answered *him*, thirty years ago, he would have taken the miracle. He would have taken his daughter back and let it burn whatever it burned, and he would have called it love right up until the frost reached his own door.

That was why it had to be cauterized. Not because the gods were evil. Because they were *indifferent at scale*, and indifference at scale was the one cruelty no army could fight, no law could govern, no grieving man could survive intact.

He closed the psalter. Outside, far off to the west, the sky above the horizon held a faint discoloration, like a bruise coming up under the skin of the world. His outriders had reported it three days running. Something out there was opening.

"Sir?" Pell at the tent flap. "Courier from the capital. Inquisitor's seal."

Corvane took the letter. The wax was black, the hand precise. He read it twice.

*The last keeper of the Anselic line is dead. Her apprentices have scattered. One is unaccounted for — a girl, name unknown, last seen in the city of Vell. The bloodline survives. Find her. The door opens to her blood alone, and while she lives free, the disease has a heart.*

He read it a third time, and something cold and quiet settled over him, the old familiar calm that had carried him through twenty years of kitchens and confessions and fires.

A girl. Running. Carrying in her veins the only key to the wound in the world.

"Break camp an hour before dawn," he said. "We march for Ostra."

Pell hesitated. "And the girl, sir? When we find her?"

Corvane looked at the letter, and past it, at the bruised western sky, and thought of a starving beggar kneeling in the dust of a village that no longer quite existed, asking only to be warm.

"When we find her," he said, "we'll see what she chooses. They always choose. It's the one prayer everyone answers for themselves."

He put the psalter away, and gave the order to march.
