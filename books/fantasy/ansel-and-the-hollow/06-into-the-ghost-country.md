## 6. Into the Ghost Country

The map Sefa gave her was drawn on paper that didn't exist yet.

Edmund had checked it a dozen times on the road north, holding it up to the light, sniffing the ink, running her thumb along the edges looking for the seam where two sheets might have been pasted together. There was no seam. The paper was one piece, and the roads on it moved — slowly, the way clouds move, so that you only noticed if you looked away and looked back. A bridge that sat over the River Vess on Monday had wandered three miles downstream by Thursday, dragging its river with it.

"That's not how maps work," Edmund had told Sefa.

"No," Sefa had agreed cheerfully. She was a small woman with ink-stained fingers and the unbothered air of someone who had made peace with being laughed at. "That's how *Ansel* works. The country's dead, but nobody told it. So it drifts. My job is to keep up." She'd tapped the western edge of the map, where the lines faded into a wash of grey that wasn't quite color and wasn't quite nothing. "You'll want to enter where the road remembers being paved. The fog respects good roadwork. Ansel was very proud of its roads."

"Is that a joke?"

"It's a *mapping note*," Sefa said with dignity. "Everything I say is a mapping note."

Now, standing at the edge of the ghost country with her pack on her shoulders and her luck-knots — real ones now, though she still flinched at the word *charm* — tucked against her ribs, Edmund understood what Sefa had been too kind to say outright: the map was less a guide than an apology. *I'm sorry the world is like this,* it said, in careful cartographer's hand. *Here is where it stops pretending.*

The fog did not roll in. It simply stopped being elsewhere. One moment Edmund was walking a perfectly ordinary hillside in the free territories, sheep-cropped grass under her boots, wind smelling of rain and distance; the next, the grass was longer, older, seeded with flowers she half-remembered from Mother Ilex's garden, and the light had gone the color of weak tea poured over everything. Behind her, when she turned, the hillside was still there — but wrong, softened, as if seen through a curtain. Ahead of her, a road ran into the mist: cobbled, crowned for drainage, immaculate.

Nobody had maintained a road here in sixty years. It was the best-kept road she had ever walked.

"Well," she said aloud, because silence in a place like this felt like an invitation. "All right."

Her voice came back to her a half-beat late, and slightly warmer than she'd said it, as though the country were agreeing with her. That should have frightened her. Instead she felt something loosen in her chest that she refused to name, because naming it would be dangerous, and Edmund Marchetti had survived three years in Vell by never naming dangerous things.

She walked.

The fog kept its own weather. Sometimes it thinned enough to show her fields — wheat standing in rows no farmer had planted, heavy-headed and golden, waiting for a harvest that would not come. Once she passed a village: doors open, tables set, bread on the boards gone to stone but the hearths still faintly warm, as though the whole place had stepped out to watch a parade and gotten lost. A child's chalk drawing on a doorstep showed a sun with a smiley face. The chalk hadn't faded. Edmund stood over it for a long moment, then knelt and, with the stub of charcoal from her kit, added a small crooked dog beside the sun. It seemed rude not to. The house seemed pleased. She decided not to think about the fact that she could tell the house seemed pleased.

Hours passed, or something adjacent to hours. The tea-colored light didn't change, and her shadow grew confused about which direction to fall. Twice the road forked, and both times the fork resolved itself before she reached it, folding away the path she hadn't chosen like a servant clearing dishes. The map in her pocket grew warm. When she took it out, a new line had appeared on it, fine and fresh, marking a route she hadn't walked yet — leading toward a shape in the fog ahead that might have been a shrine, or a tower, or a very ambitious haystack.

"You're showing off," she told the map.

It was. She followed it anyway.

* * *

The keeper was waiting for her at the top of a hill that had not been there a moment before, in front of a gate that led nowhere — a grand double arch of pale stone, standing alone in the mist, framing nothing but more fog. Beyond it, where a garden or courtyard should have been, there was only a suggestion of green, the way you can see a room through frosted glass.

The woman leaning against the gatepost was old in the way mountains are old: not frail, just finished. Silver hair braided in the Anselic style, coiled at the nape. Her robes were the deep green of Mother Ilex's working clothes, and Edmund's stomach dropped through the road beneath her, because she knew those robes, knew the way they hung, knew the mended hem — except this woman wore them sixty years younger, or sixty years truer, and her face was a face Edmund had seen once, in a locket Ilex kept hidden and thought no one knew about.

"You're her teacher," Edmund said. "The last keeper."

"I'm the last keeper's last keeper," the woman said. "Which makes me either the second-to-last or the third-to-last, depending entirely on you." She looked Edmund up and down with frank, unhurried appraisal, the way Ilex used to look at a doubtful herb. "Edmund. Heir of Ansel. Runaway apprentice. Seller of luck-knots under the name Marla Fen, which was clever of you — my own mother's name, as it happens. Did Ilex tell you that, or did you stumble into it?"

"I picked it out of a hat," Edmund said. "Literally. There was a hat."

Something moved across the old woman's face that might have been delight. "Oh, you'll do. Come in, then."

"There's no *in*. Your gate opens onto fog."

"It opens onto whatever you bring through it," the keeper said. "So let's find out what you've got." And she pushed off the gatepost, walked through the empty arch, and vanished — not dramatically, just the way a person vanishes when the place they're walking into has decided they belong there.

Edmund stood at the threshold for a long time. The fog beyond the arch smelled, faintly, impossibly, of Ilex's kitchen: rosemary, woodsmoke, the scorched-honey smell of sugar left a moment too long. Three years she had run from that smell. It had found her anyway, here at the end of the world, wearing a gate.

She went through.

* * *

Inside was a garden that remembered being a courtyard, and a table that remembered being set for two, and a bench worn smooth by generations of students who had sat where Edmund now sat while their teacher asked them questions.

"What do you want?" the keeper asked. She sat opposite, hands folded, patient as stone.

"To survive," Edmund said. It came out fast and honest, the way truths do when you're tired. "That's all. That's always been all. I want a door at my back and food in my belly and nobody asking me who I am."

"Mm." The keeper nodded, unsurprised. "And if I told you this place could give you that? Not the fog outside — *this*. The true Ansel, held together at the center, where the Hollow keeps its heart. You carry the blood. The blood carries the key. Stay, and you need never run again. No usurper reaches here. No inquisitor. No hunger, no cold, no Vell. Every answered prayer in the living world is a hole burned in it — but in here, prayers are just conversation. You could ask for anything, and the asking wouldn't cost the world a thing." She leaned forward. "Stay, Edmund. Belong. Forever."

The word landed in Edmund's chest like a warm coal.

Forever. No more false names. No more checking the street twice, sleeping in shifts, burning her letters. A garden that wanted her. A house that seemed pleased. A country that agreed with her voice when she spoke. She thought of Vell, of the luck-knots, of the child healed by power she hadn't meant to use, of the scorched absence where a beggar's prayer had been answered. She thought of Sefa's moving map, and the way Sefa had pressed it into her hands like it mattered, like *she* mattered.

And underneath all of it, quiet and terrible, she thought of Mother Ilex — who had chosen her not for talent but for blood, who had built Edmund's whole exile like a wall around a door, and who was dead now, and had still, somehow, arranged for her teacher to be waiting at the gate with dinner implied.

"What does it cost," Edmund said. Not a question. She'd learned in Vell that everything was a price list; you just had to get the seller to show it.

The keeper's expression gentled, and that was worse than any trick. "Ah. There it is. Ilex said you'd ask. She said you were the only apprentice she ever taught who haggled with fate." She rose, and the garden dimmed around her, attentive. "Come and see, then. See what opening the Hollow asks of its keeper — and then decide whether forever is a gift or a bill."

She held out her hand. Beyond the garden walls, far off in the fog, something vast turned over in its sleep, and every flower in the courtyard opened at once, all facing Edmund, all waiting.

Edmund looked at the offered hand, and at the garden, and at the warm coal sitting where her fear used to live.

Then she took it — and the ground beneath them folded like a page turning, and the keeper began, softly, to tell her the price.
