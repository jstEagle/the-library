## 7. The Price Named

The ghost-country kept its own hours. Edmund had learned that much in the three days since she crossed the fog-line: dawn here was a suggestion, not a command, and the sun rose when it felt like being remembered.

She sat on a half-real wall above an orchard that smelled gloriously of apples and could not be eaten, watching the keeper's house below. It was a low stone cottage with smoke rising from a chimney that had no fire in it — the smoke was memory-smoke, Ilex had called it once, in one of her forbidden lessons, though Edmund hadn't understood until now. Smoke that remembered being warm.

The ghost of Mother Ilex's teacher came out to fetch water from a well that had been dry for two hundred years, and carried it inside anyway, because that was what the house remembered.

"You're still counting your exits," the keeper said, without turning around. Her voice was dry as seed husks. She was a small woman, this ghost — smaller than Ilex, with hands like knotted roots and eyes the color of the fog itself. "I can hear you doing it. Little click-click-click behind your eyes, like a lockmaker at work."

"I'm not," Edmund said, which was true only because she'd stopped bothering to count. There were no exits here. That was rather the point of the place.

"Good." The keeper set down her impossible bucket. "Then we can talk about the door."

* * *

They walked, because the keeper liked walking and because in the ghost-country walking was how you thought. The roads here were half-real — cobbles that firmed under your feet if you believed in them and went soft as peat if you didn't. Edmund had fallen twice on the first day before learning the trick of it. Now she walked the way she did everything else: carefully, weight balanced for running.

"The Hollow is not a weapon," the keeper said. "I want you to understand that first, because everyone who comes looking for it wants it to be a weapon. Your usurper wants to close it. Your councilmen want to open it and pour soldiers through. Fools, both. A door is not a sword. A door is a promise."

"A promise of what?"

"That there is somewhere else." The keeper gestured, and the fog parted like a curtain drawn by a considerate servant. Beyond it lay the echo of Ansel: terraced hills, a river the color of pewter, a city of white walls rendered soft and out-of-focus, as if seen through tears. "When the kingdom fell, everything that couldn't die and wouldn't leave came here. The dead who weren't done. The roads that loved their travelers. The gods themselves, when they grew too old for the world. We folded it all into the space between, and we sealed it, and we put a lock on it shaped like a bloodline."

Edmund's stomach did its familiar cold drop. "My bloodline."

"Your bloodline. Which your Ilex carried, and carried badly, poor woman — she was a hedge-witch by vocation and a keeper by accident, and she hated every minute of the duty. So she found a girl with the same blood and taught her small charms and gave her a false name and told her to run." The keeper glanced sideways, and there was something almost gentle in the fog-colored eyes. "You've been angry at her for three years. I know. The dead gossip terribly."

"She let me believe I was fleeing my own life," Edmund said. Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "She let me think she'd marked me somehow, that the inquisitors were hunting me for what I *did*. All that time I thought if I just stayed small enough, quiet enough, charmless enough—"

"You'd survive." The keeper nodded. "And you have. You're very good at surviving, Edmund Marchetti. It's nearly a piety with you. Survival, said over breakfast like grace."

Edmund flushed. "There are worse religions."

"There are none lonelier." The keeper stopped at the crest of a hill, where the fog gathered thick, and beneath them — Edmund felt it before she saw it, a hum in her teeth, in the marrow of her — lay the Hollow.

It did not look like much. That was the horror of it, later, when she tried to describe it to herself. It looked like a shrine ruin: broken columns, a cracked altar, wildflowers growing up through the seams. But the air above it bent, the way air bends over a fire, and through the bend she could see — *through*, not into — the white city of Ansel, sharp now, real now, closer than the flowers at her feet.

"It opens once," the keeper said quietly. "That is the first term. Once, for everyone, all at once. Not a gate you prop open and stroll through on market days. One opening. Everything that has been waiting — the dead who aren't done, the displaced, the exiled, the half-real — pours through, back into the world, or onward past it, whichever they choose. And the old gods go with them. Their leaking prayers end. The scorching stops. The holes in the world close."

"And the second term?" Edmund asked, though some animal part of her already knew, the way you know a fever coming on.

The keeper turned to face her fully. "A door must be held from one side. The Hollow opens outward, but something must anchor it from within, or it snaps shut mid-passage and takes everything with it. The anchor cannot be stone. Stone forgets. It must be blood. Anselic blood. Willing blood." She paused. "You would go in last, Edmund. And stay."

The word hung in the fog between them.

"Stay," Edmund repeated. "Stay *how long*?"

"I don't know," the keeper said, and Edmund realized with a lurch that it was the first honest uncertainty the ghost had shown her. "I was never the anchor. My teacher wasn't either. The Hollow has never been opened. We built the lock; we never tested the key. Perhaps the anchor holds it a year. Perhaps a century. Perhaps—" she smiled, thin and dry, "—perhaps forever, and you become what I am. A memory with opinions."

The orchard smell drifted up the hill, apples that couldn't be eaten. The smoke rose from the chimneyless chimney. Somewhere below, in the white city, someone was laughing — a real laugh, warm and human, from a dead person who hadn't finished being alive.

And Edmund understood, with terrible clarity, what the temptation was. Not glory. Not power. *Belonging.* Here was a country made entirely of things that had refused to leave — stubborn, tender, unfinished things — and they were waiting for her. They had been waiting for two hundred years. No one in the ghost-country would ever ask her name twice, or check it against a list, or wonder why a luck-knot seller flinched at the sound of boots.

Here she could stop running. Here, running meant nothing. There was nowhere *to* run.

"No," she said.

The keeper said nothing.

"I mean — not yet. I mean—" Edmund's hands were shaking. She shoved them in her pockets like a child. "You're asking me to trade one exile for another. Three years of running and hiding and sleeping in ditches, and then — what, eternity? In a place where the apples don't feed anyone? You dress it up as belonging but it's a cage with better light!"

"Yes," the keeper agreed pleasantly. "It is exactly that. I'm glad you see it clearly. Most people need longer."

"*Most people?*" Edmund stared at her. "How many people have you asked?"

"Oh, hundreds." The keeper began walking again, unhurried, back down toward the impossible house. "Every keeper-blooded soul who wandered in over the centuries. I show them the door, I name the price, and I watch what they do. It's very instructive. The proud ones say yes immediately, and I turn them away, because pride makes a brittle anchor. The desperate ones say yes too, and I turn them away as well. Despair crumbles." She looked back over her shoulder. "Only one kind of person do I accept."

"Which kind?"

"The kind who says no first." The keeper's smile deepened. "Because the door must be opened by someone who *chooses* it, Edmund — not someone who's merely choosing against the world. Go home. Think. Eat something real; you've been living on ghost-apples and they sit poorly. If the answer is still no when the usurper's army reaches the shrine, then the answer is no, and the Hollow stays shut, and the free cities make their peace with Corvane however they can."

"And if the answer is yes?"

"Then come back," the keeper said, "and bring your whole heart, because we'll need all of it."

* * *

Edmund ran.

Not immediately — she was twenty-three years old and had spent three years perfecting the art of leaving slowly, packing light, saying nothing memorable. She went back to the keeper's house and ate bread the house remembered into existence, which tasted like Sunday mornings she'd never had, and she slept in a bed that remembered being slept in, and in the morning she thanked the ghost politely, the way you thank a host whose hospitality has begun to frighten you.

Then she walked to the fog-line, and stood with one foot in Ansel's grey morning and one foot in the world's ordinary grey morning, and she made herself look back.

The white city glimmered. The laughter drifted down from somewhere. The smoke rose from the chimney that had no fire, remembering warmth.

*You could stay,* said the part of her that had kept her alive for three years, the part that counted exits and memorized faces and never, ever slept deeply. *You could stay and no one would ever hunt you again.*

*Yes,* she answered it. *And no one would ever find me again, either. There's a difference.*

She stepped through the fog.

* * *

The world, when she came out of it, smelled of woodsmoke — the wrong kind, the kind that eats houses.

She was two days' walk from Ostra, on the pilgrim road Sefa had mapped for her, and she knew something was wrong before she crested the first hill, because the road was full of people going the other direction. Carters with their carts half-loaded. A family carrying a grandmother in a chair with poles lashed to it, the old woman clutching a cat that seemed resigned to the whole enterprise. A boy driving geese that refused to be hurried, honking their objections at the entire apocalypse.

"What's happened?" Edmund caught the grandmother's chair as it wobbled past. "Please — the city—"

"Burning at the edges," the grandmother said. Her voice was remarkably calm; Edmund supposed you earned that voice by surviving enough calamaties to sort them by size. "Not the army, girl. The *prayers*. There's a shrine quarter on the south side, and folk got desperate when the rumors came, and they started praying — properly praying, on their knees, the old words and everything. And the prayers were *answered*." She shuddered. "Whole streets gone. Not burned — *gone*. Scorched into absence, like a page torn out. My son-in-law swears he saw a god move through the tannery district. Says it looked sorry. Says that's the worst part."

"Where's the army?"

"West road. Coming slow. Their man — the god-hunter, Corvane — he sent heralds ahead. Terms." The grandmother spat, with dignity. "Hand over the Anselic heir, and the cities keep their walls. Nobody knows any Anselic heir, mind. But nobody believes nobody knows, either. That's the trouble with terms. They make everyone look at each other sideways."

Edmund stood in the middle of the road while refugees flowed around her like water around a stone, and the geese complained, and somewhere ahead Ostra burned at its edges with prayers that should never have been answered.

*Sefa,* she thought. *Sefa is in the city. Sefa, who maps places that don't exist, who drew me a road through the fog with her own hand and said, 'Come back and tell me what's there.'*

Her feet, faithful old cowards, were already pointed east. Away. Toward the hills, the fog-line, the country where the apples smelled sweet and no one could follow. Three days' hard walking and she'd be safe. Safe, and small, and alone, and alive — survival's holy trinity, the creed she'd said over breakfast every morning for three years like grace.

It had never failed her before.

Standing in the road, with the smoke rising black against the afternoon, Edmund discovered that it failed her now — that survival, examined closely in good light, was just another word for *letting everything you love burn while you check your exits.* The keeper had been right. It was a lonely religion, and she was tired of being its only congregation.

The boy with the geese paused beside her. "You coming or going, miss?"

Edmund looked east, at the hills, at freedom.

Then she turned west, toward the burning, toward the terms, toward the question the whole world was about to ask her.

"Going," she said, and started to run.
