## 8. Surrender Terms

The herald arrived at Ostra's gates at dawn, which Edmund thought was a very theatrical hour for the end of the world.

She watched from the wall above the Salt Gate, wedged between two militiamen who smelled of onions and fear, as a single rider in grey and gold picked his way across the frost-browned fields. He carried no weapon. He carried instead a banner folded over his saddlebow like laundry, and when he reached the gate he did not shout or demand. He simply held up a scroll sealed in black wax and said, in a voice pitched to carry to every ear on the wall:

"His Radiance Corvane offers terms. The free cities will be spared entire — walls, granaries, temples, persons — upon surrender of one person only: the heir of Ansel."

A murmur ran along the battlements like wind through wheat.

"The heir is asked to present herself at the ruined shrine of Saint Vess, three days hence, alone. If she does not come, His Radiance regrets that the cities must be searched. His Radiance regrets many things." The herald paused, and something almost human crossed his face. "He asked me to say that exactly. *Regrets.*"

Then he turned his horse and rode back the way he had come, leaving the silence behind him heavier than any army.

Edmund stood very still. Beside her, the onion-scented militiaman — a boy of maybe seventeen named Pell — whispered, "Do you think they'll do it? Hand someone over?"

"They'll debate it," Edmund said. "That's what councils do. They'll debate it until the debating itself becomes the decision."

And she was right, which was somehow worse.

* * *

The council chamber of Ostra had once been a salt warehouse, and it still smelled faintly of the sea, which lent the proceedings an air of pickled dignity. Edmund attended because Sefa dragged her, and Sefa attended because cartographers, she explained, were considered neutral parties — "we make no claims, we only record them" — though Edmund suspected the real reason was that Sefa could not bear to let Edmund out of her sight now that everyone was hunting an heir.

The council was forty-three souls: merchants, guildmasters, a retired admiral who kept falling asleep, and Magistrate Corvel-Ostra, a broad woman with a voice like a landslide, who read the usurper's terms aloud twice so that no one could claim they hadn't heard.

"One person," said a wool-factor named Dossin, "against four hundred thousand. I don't love arithmetic, but even I can do this sum."

"It isn't a sum," said the admiral, waking briefly. "It's a soul."

"It's a *stranger,*" Dossin shot back. "None of us has ever seen this heir. For all we know she's a story Mother Ilex invented to keep apprentices obedient. We're being asked to burn for a rumor."

Edmund sat very quietly with her hands in her lap and felt each word land like a small, polite stone.

Magistrate Corvel-Ostra rubbed her eyes. "We are also being asked to believe that a man who has burned eleven towns will honor terms of surrender. Gentles, I have negotiated with butchers before. The knife always wants sharpening after."

"Then we fight," said Dossin, "and lose, and die, having preserved our *dignity.* Wonderful. Write that on my grave. *He kept his dignity; the worms were impressed.*"

There was laughter — thin, frightened laughter, but laughter — and Edmund found, absurdly, that she liked them. All of them. Even Dossin, especially Dossin, haggling with the end of the world as if it were a bolt of cloth. These were her people. Three years she had hidden among people like these, selling them luck-knots, mending their fevers, learning their jokes. She had told herself she was passing through. She had never once believed it.

The council voted to send a delegation to parley. It voted against surrender, for now. It voted to double the watch and pray to whatever gods were still listening, then caught itself, because praying was rather the problem.

Afterward, in the corridor, Sefa fell into step beside her.

"You heard Dossin," Sefa said.

"I heard him."

"He's not wrong, Ed. One person. You could be through the eastern passes in two days. I've mapped them. There's a shepherd's road nobody uses because the maps are wrong — except mine." Sefa's voice was gentle in the way Edmund had learned to dread, gentle the way a hand reaching for a bird is gentle. "I'd come partway. Draw you a route no army could follow. You could stop running *eventually.* Somewhere far. Somewhere—"

"Sefa."

"I mean it kindly," Sefa said. "For once in my life I mean something kindly, and it's this: run. Please. Let me be useful for once and not just accurate."

Edmund stopped walking. The corridor smelled of salt and tallow, and somewhere below, the city was going about its evening — bread baking, arguments starting, a fiddle badly played — all of it ordinary, all of it mortal, all of it hers in a way she had never let anything be hers.

"When I ran from Mother Ilex," Edmund said slowly, "I told myself it was survival. And it was. But it was also easier. Running means no one depends on you. Running means whatever happens next happens to somebody else." She looked at her hands — charm-maker's hands, healer's hands, hands that had healed a child without meaning to because something old had decided to move through them. "Every place I've fled to, someone paid for my hiding. A landlord lied for me. A customer took a beating rather than name me. I've been spending other people's courage for three years and calling it prudence."

Sefa said nothing. Cartographers, Edmund thought, knew better than anyone that you cannot redraw a line by wishing.

"If I run now," Edmund went on, "Corvane burns Ostra anyway. Not because he needs to. Because he *wants* to, and my absence gives him permission. But if I stand up in front of that delegation tomorrow and say *here I am* — then it stops being his choice. Then it's ours. The cities get to decide whether I'm worth fighting for, instead of deciding whether a rumor is worth dying for. That's different. That's the whole difference."

"And if they decide you're not?"

"Then at least it'll be honest arithmetic," Edmund said, and managed a smile. "Dossin would approve."

Sefa looked at her for a long moment. Then she pulled out her satchel, took out a map — of course she did — and spread it against the wall. "If you're going to do this," she said, "you're going to do it with good cartography. Shrine of Saint Vess. Here. Note the ravine to the north — Corvane's cavalry can't cross it. Note the old pilgrim road under the bracken — I've walked it, it holds. If you're going to offer yourself up, you'll do it standing on ground I've chosen." Her voice cracked slightly on the last words, and she covered it by jabbing a finger at the ravine. "Also I'm coming with you as far as the ridge, and you can't stop me, because technically I'm neutral."

"Sefa—"

"*Neutral,*" Sefa repeated firmly, rolling up the map. "Now go write your letter. I hear tyrants hate receiving mail."

* * *

Corvane read the letter twice.

It had been delivered by a girl of perhaps twelve, on a borrowed pony, who had marched into his camp past four layers of sentries by the simple expedient of announcing "I've got post for the Radiance, mind your boots" in a tone that brooked no argument. Corvane privately approved of the child. He gave her supper and a coin and sent her home before anyone could think better of either mercy.

The letter was short. The handwriting was small and stubborn, like its author, presumably.

*To Corvane, called His Radiance,*

*I am the heir you are hunting. I will come to the shrine of Saint Vess, alone, as asked. But I ask you to know what you are buying. I did not steal anything from you. I did not ask for the blood in me or the door it opens. Your quarrel is with gods who left, not with a hedge-witch who stayed.*

*You prayed once. I know about the prayer. I know what it cost you to be refused. So I will tell you what I have learned since the gods came back: they are not refusing you now out of cruelty. They are leaking out of a wound, and everything they touch comes apart. What I carry can close that wound. That is why I am coming — not to surrender, but to end the miracles you hate.*

*Come to the shrine and see. Bring your army if it makes you feel safe. I have found that most armor is worn on the inside anyway.*

*— E. Marchetti, apprentice, runaway, and — apparently — heir of Ansel*

Corvane set the letter down on his campaign table, beside his maps and his cold tea, and sat looking at it for a long time.

*Apprentice, runaway, and heir.* She had signed herself honestly, all three names, none of them flattering. There was no grandeur in it. No claim of destiny. Just a person listing what she had been, in ascending order of inconvenience.

"She knows about the prayer," said Captain Vere from the tent flap, carefully.

"She knows a great deal, apparently." Corvane rose and went to the flap, looking west toward the darkening hills and the shrine he could not yet see. Eleven years ago he had knelt in a sickroom and begged the silent sky for his daughter's life, and the sky had said nothing, and he had sworn that nothing that refused to answer would ever hold power over him again. Now the sky answered everything, indiscriminately, terribly, and he had built an army to stop it — and here was a runaway hedge-witch telling him, politely, in small stubborn handwriting, that she agreed with him. That she was coming to end the miracles too.

It should have comforted him. Instead it felt like being seen, which was infinitely more dangerous than being opposed.

"Captain," he said. "At the shrine, no one fires unless I give the word. No one. If she truly means to close the wound—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "If there is a chance she speaks true, I would hear it from her own mouth before I let a single soldier touch her."

Vere hesitated. "And if she doesn't speak true?"

"Then I will have been kind for one afternoon," Corvane said, "and I will live with it, as I live with everything else."

He looked down at the letter once more, at the last line — *most armor is worn on the inside anyway* — and found, to his considerable irritation, that he could not decide whether it was wisdom or impertinence.

Three days, then. The shrine of Saint Vess. A runaway, a door, and the end of everything either of them believed about mercy.

Corvane blew out his lamp and stood a long while in the dark, listening to ten thousand men breathe.
