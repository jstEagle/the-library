## 9. The Hollow Opens

The shrine where Ansel died was not grand. That surprised Edmund, though she couldn't have said why. She had imagined — from Ilex's letters, from the ghost-keeper's murmured memories — a cathedral of broken stone, something to match the size of what had been lost.

Instead it was a low ring of mossy pillars in a valley of tall grass, with a well at its center that had gone dry three hundred years ago and never noticed. Sheep had grazed here once. Someone's grandmother had hung ribbons on the eastern pillar for luck, and the ribbons had rotted there, and no one had come to replace them.

*This is where a kingdom ended,* Edmund thought, standing in the grass with her boots wet with dew. *Not with fire. With forgetting.*

Behind her, half a mile down the valley road, Ostra's militia was forming its thin, desperate line. Ahead of her, cresting the ridge like a tide of iron, came Corvane's army.

She had sent him her name two days ago. *I am the heir you're hunting,* she'd written, in her best merchant's hand, because even at the end of the world she wasn't going to spell anything wrong. *Come to the shrine of Ansel. Come alone to the ring, or don't come at all.*

He had come alone to the ring. Of course he had. A man who believed gods were a disease would want to look the last symptom in the eye without witnesses.

He rode up through the grass as the sun cleared the ridge, and Edmund saw that he looked tired. Not weak — never weak — but tired the way stone is tired, worn by weather rather than battle. He dismounted at the edge of the pillar ring and stood there with his helmet under his arm, a gray man with a gray grief living behind his eyes.

"Edmund Marchetti," he said. "Or whatever name you're wearing this decade."

"Quintana," she said. "It was Quintana when your inquisitors burned my first charm-book. You can check."

"I know." Something moved across his face that might have been respect. "You've cost me eleven years, girl. Do you know how many men I've buried chasing the rumor of you? How many villages I've had to burn prayers out of?"

"Four hundred and six," Edmund said quietly. "Sefa counted. She maps them."

Something flickered in his eyes — pain, quickly mastered. "Then she knows more than my own clerks."

"She's better at it than they are." Edmund folded her hands, the way Mother Ilex used to fold hers before saying something true. "Corvane. I didn't call you here to fight."

"No?"

"No. I called you here because you deserve to be told what happens next. You've spent your whole life hating miracles, and in about an hour there won't be any left to hate. It seemed rude not to invite you."

The wind moved through the tall grass, and it did not sound like wind. It sounded like a crowd very far away, holding its breath.

* * *

She had learned the opening in the ghost country, kneeling in fog while the last keeper of Ansel taught her the rite with hands made of memory. It was not complicated. That was the horror of it. The Hollow was a door, and doors only need a hinge and a hand.

But the hinge was her.

*Ilex could have told you,* the ghost-keeper had said. *She chose not to. She let you run for three years hoping you'd find another way, because she loved you and hoped you'd be too clever to come back.* A pause, like fog settling. *You weren't too clever. None of us ever are.*

Edmund had fled the ghost country then. She had stood at Ostra's burning edge and thought about survival, her oldest religion, and found it suddenly, embarrassingly hollow — a god that answered every prayer with *run*, and gave nothing back but tomorrow.

So here she was. In a sheep field. About to die, or worse than die, which was to stay.

"You should know what I'm doing," she told Corvane, "so you can't tell yourself later that you weren't warned. The old gods are leaking into the world because the door between is broken. Every prayer they answer burns a hole somewhere. Your villages. Sefa's blank places on the map. All of it."

"The gods are a sickness," Corvane said. "I've said so for thirty years."

"You're right," Edmund said, and watched him blink. "They are. And I'm going to cure it. But the cure needs an anchor. Someone has to stand inside the door and hold it open while everything that got lost comes home — the dead of Ansel, the people your armies erased, the whole ghost country — and then the gods pass beyond, and the door closes, and the world stops bleeding."

"And the anchor?" His voice was flat. He already knew. Men who had prayed once and been abandoned always knew the shape of a price.

"Stays inside," Edmund said cheerfully, because if she didn't say it cheerfully she would not say it at all. "Forever. Which is why I'm telling *you*. Because after today, there will be nothing left to hunt. No heir, no bloodline, no miracles. Just a world where nobody's prayer gets answered by anything except other people. I think—" and here her voice did waver, just slightly, "—I think you might actually like it."

Corvane stared at her for a long moment. Then he laughed — one short, rusty bark, like a gate that hadn't opened in years.

"You're offering me peace," he said, "as a consolation prize."

"I'm offering you a world with no gods in it. Isn't that what you marched ten thousand men to build? I'm just building it faster, and with less burning." She shrugged. "Also there'll be tea afterward, probably. Ostra does a decent bitter red. You seem like a bitter-red man."

For one strange moment his face almost softened. Almost. Then the ridge behind him filled with sunlight off armor, and the moment passed.

"It won't work," he said. "Whatever you do, it won't—"

The well began to sing.

* * *

It was not a loud singing. It was the sound a dry stone makes when water finds it again after three hundred years — a deep, patient hum, rising up through the soles of Edmund's boots and into her teeth and her bones and the place behind her ribs where Mother Ilex had planted a kingdom's worth of blood without ever telling her.

The grass bowed outward from the well in a perfect circle. The rotten luck-ribbons on the eastern pillar lifted, all at once, bright as new.

"Oh," Edmund said softly. "Hello."

And the Hollow opened.

It did not explode. Doors don't explode; they *open*, and this one opened the way morning opens — gradually, inevitably, light pouring up out of the ground instead of down from the sky. The ruined well became a threshold, and the threshold became a road, and the road ran backward through time into a country made of fog and memory and green hills that had been dead for three centuries and had never stopped being beautiful.

They came through.

First the dead of Ansel — not as ghosts, not anymore, but as people, blinking in the sun, weeping, catching each other, some of them falling to their knees in the real grass of the real world and pressing their palms against it like men confirming a dream. Then the displaced living, the ones whose villages had been scorched into absence by answered prayers, stepping out of the fog with their children and their goats and their bundles of salvaged belongings, staring around at a world they'd been told no longer existed.

And among them, walking slowly, leaning on a staff of blackthorn, came a woman Edmund knew better than she knew her own heartbeat.

Mother Ilex looked exactly as she had the day Edmund ran: sharp-eyed, gray-braided, unimpressed by nearly everything. She crossed the threshold, took one long look at the shining door, at the army frozen on the ridge, at Edmund standing in the center of it all with her arms spread wide like a scarecrow made of courage —

"Well," said Mother Ilex. "Took you long enough."

"You *let* me run," Edmund said, laughing and crying in a way that felt suspiciously like healing. "You let me run for three years!"

"I let you *choose*," Ilex said. "There's a difference. Come here, you dreadful girl."

Edmund did not come there. She couldn't. Her feet were rooted now, part of the hinge, part of the door. But Ilex came to her, and held her face in both hands, and kissed her forehead the way she had on the first day, when a terrified child with dangerous blood had arrived at her cottage pretending to be nobody.

"Proud of you," Ilex whispered. "Now hold the door, keeper. They're still coming."

They were. For hours they poured through — the lost and the erased and the exiled, a nation's worth of grief given back its shape. And through it all Edmund stood, and held, and hurt, and did not stop. The old gods came last, vast and slow and dimming, passing beyond the world like ships going over the horizon, and as each one went, the pressure behind the air eased, and somewhere far away a scorched absence quietly became merely empty land instead of a wound.

When it was done, the door remained — a soft arch of light above the old well, steady as a hearthfire. Edmund stood within it, on the threshold, on *her* side of it now, and looked out at the world she was giving up.

Corvane still stood at the edge of the pillar ring. He had watched all of it. His army had watched all of it. Ten thousand men had seen the dead come home, and there was not one of them, Edmund suspected, who would ever again burn a charm-book without feeling foolish about it.

She held out her hand through the arch.

"There's room," she said. "On the other side. There's a country in there built out of memory and mercy, and honestly it could use a man who knows how to organize things. Or—" she nodded back toward the world, toward the free cities, toward a future with no miracles left to fear or hate, "—you can stay. Build your godless world. It's yours now. We're leaving it to you."

Corvane looked at her hand for a long time.

Then he looked past her, into the Hollow, at the green hills and the lamplight of a town being raised from memory, at people laughing — actually laughing — three hundred years too late and right on time.

"No," he said quietly. And then, with something that was almost a bow: "Thank you. For asking."

He turned and walked down through the grass toward his army, a gray man in a world with no more miracles to hate, and Edmund watched him go until the fog of the Hollow curled gently across her view, and Sefa's voice called her name from the doorway, and the smell of bitter red tea drifted through from somewhere that existed only because she had chosen it.

"Keeper," Sefa said, grinning through the arch, map tube under her arm. "You are *not* going to believe what the pilgrims brought you."

Edmund Marchetti — runaway, apprentice, heir, anchor — turned toward the sound of home, and went in.
