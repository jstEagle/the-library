## 10. A Door, Ajar

The trouble with building a country out of memory, Edmund had learned in her first year as keeper, was that memory was a terrible carpenter.

It insisted on rebuilding the bakery on Ansel's Copper Lane with its door that stuck — because everyone remembered the door sticking. It put the river two streets east of where it belonged and then flooded the tannery every spring, because enough exiled dead remembered it flooding and missed it. And when Edmund finally persuaded the ghost of old Master Corvel to let her move his schoolhouse somewhere with better light, the schoolhouse sulked for a month, growing ivy over its windows out of spite.

"You could just tell them no," said Wren, who had been a shrine-keeper's daughter in life and was now, apparently, Edmund's unofficial deputy, though nobody had voted on it. She sat on the counter of Edmund's workshop, swinging her feet, watching Edmund tie off another luck-knot. "You're the keeper. You could say, *no, Master Corvel, your schoolhouse goes here and you'll like it.*"

"I could," Edmund agreed, biting the thread. "And then he'd haunt me. He's already dead, Wren. There's very little I can threaten him with."

"You could stop fixing his roof."

"Then he'd complain louder." She held the knot up to the light — the workshop light, which she'd made herself, from a jar of bottled dawn she'd coaxed into staying lit. It wasn't magic the way Mother Ilex had taught it, all sigils and bloodline and terrible weight. It was smaller than that. A charm to make a knot hold. A lamp that remembered morning. The kind of hedge-craft she'd sold under a false name in Vell, back when selling it was a crime and making it was a secret.

Here, it was just work. Here, it was just hers.

The luck-knots were for the pilgrims, mostly. Word had gotten out — Sefa saw to that, with her maps and her stubbornness and her complete inability to keep a good thing quiet — that the door at the ruined shrine opened twice a season, and that on those days, anyone carrying grief or exile or simply curiosity could walk through into a country that shouldn't exist and find, more often than not, someone they'd lost.

Not always. That was the part Edmund had to be honest about, standing at the threshold each opening day, greeting the lines of pilgrims with ink-stained fingers and an apron full of knots. The Hollow gave what it had. It had been built from what the dead of Ansel remembered, and what the living carried through the door, and what Edmund chose to plant in the empty places. If your grandmother hadn't died inside the old kingdom, she wouldn't be waiting by the well. But if she'd *told* you about the well — if you'd grown up on stories of it, the water cold and sweet, the willow leaning wrong — then sometimes, sometimes, there'd be a well like that, and a bench beside it, and a feeling like being expected.

"We got a letter from Ostra," Wren said, producing it from somewhere in her endless pockets. "Sefa sent it with the last cart of pilgrims. Also there's a complaint about the river again."

"The river can file it in writing."

"It did. That's the complaint."

Edmund laughed — really laughed, the way she'd almost forgotten how to do in Vell, where laughing too loudly near the wrong window could get a hedge-witch reported — and took the letter. Sefa's handwriting marched across the page like an army that had somewhere important to be: new settlements along the coast where the erased villages used to be, rebuilt not from prayers but from lumber and stubbornness; the free cities' council finally agreeing on something (a border charter, three years in the arguing); and at the bottom, in smaller script:

*Corvane is farming. Actual farming. Barley. People say he talks to the field. I choose to believe the field is winning.*

Edmund read that line twice. She thought about the god-hunter standing at the threshold of the Hollow on the last day, offered passage, refusing it — refusing *her*, refusing mercy, choosing to stay in a world whose miracles had finally gone quiet. She'd hated him, then feared him, then understood him, and now, apparently, she thought about him planting barley, and felt something that wasn't quite forgiveness but was at least a neighborly sort of peace.

Good, she thought. Grow something. It's harder to burn things when your hands are full of dirt.

* * *

Opening day came with the smell of rain that hadn't fallen yet — weather in the Hollow arrived when enough people remembered wanting it, which made forecasting less a science and more a negotiation.

Edmund stood at the door.

That was the formal name for it now, capitalized and everything: *the Door*, set into the ruined shrine where Ansel had died, a doorway of pale stone that opened onto fog and then, past the fog, onto Copper Lane and the bakery with the sticking door and the whole impossible country beyond. From the outside — from the world — it looked like a ruin with a doorway that led nowhere. From the inside, it was the only way home some people would ever get.

They came in twos and threes. An old man from Ostra looking for the regimental banner of a son who'd marched west and never come back — and yes, Edmund knew where that banner was; it hung in the great hall, because the son had died at the fall of Ansel, and the Hollow kept what its dead kept. A young woman clutching a fever-charm, one of Edmund's own, sold in Vell years ago under the false name — she didn't recognize the keeper in the apron, and Edmund didn't tell her. Some debts you collect by letting them rest. Two children, refugees from the usurper's burned corridor, who walked through holding hands and stopped dead at the sight of grass, actual grass, having forgotten it grew so green.

"Is it real?" the smaller one asked Edmund, tugging her sleeve.

"It's as real as anything," Edmund said. "Which is to say: somebody made it, and somebody keeps it, and it holds."

That seemed to satisfy them. Children, she'd found, accepted the metaphysics of the place faster than the councilmen ever had.

Near midday, Sefa came through.

She did this every opening now, and every time she arrived the same way: striding through the Door like it owed her money, map case over one shoulder, surveyor's chain clinking, hair escaping its braid in the manner of a woman who had argued with weather and won. She stopped in front of Edmund, looked her up and down, and frowned.

"You've got flour on your face."

"That's chalk. I was re-marking the flood line."

"You've got flour on your face and you've been crying."

"I have *not*—"

"The children told me. They said the keeper cried when the little one asked if her mother might come through next time." Sefa's voice gentled, which for Sefa was roughly equivalent to other people weeping openly. "Will she?"

"Maybe." Edmund wiped her face with her wrist, dignity abandoned. "Her mother died east of the mountains. Outside the old kingdom. But she prayed, before the end — Ilex told me once that the gods heard everything, even when they couldn't answer. And the gods passed through here on their way out. Some of what they heard stayed in the stones." She shrugged, helpless and hopeful at once. "I'm going to go stand by the eastern gate tonight and listen. Sometimes if you listen long enough, the country remembers someone new."

Sefa nodded slowly. Then she unrolled a fresh sheet of vellum on the shrine's broken altar, weighted the corners with stones, and began, with terrifying cheerfulness, to draw.

"What are you doing?"

"Mapping your country properly. You've lived here three years and you still don't know what's past the western hills."

"Nothing's past the western hills. The country ends there."

"*Currently,*" Sefa said, in the tone of a woman correcting a child who has claimed the sky is blue when it is obviously whatever color she says it is. "Countries grow, Edmund. Especially ones with a keeper who plants things. What's that you've got growing past the mill?"

"Lavender. For the charms."

"And the orchard by the south road?"

"That was the dead of Ansel. They missed having one."

"And the little house past *that?*" Sefa's pen paused. She looked up, and her smile went soft at the edges. "The one with smoke in the chimney. Nobody lives there, Edmund. I checked with the ghosts. It's just... there. With smoke. Like it's waiting."

Edmund felt her ears go warm. "I don't know what you mean."

"You built yourself a house."

"I built a *guesthouse.*"

"With one bed."

"For guests. Singular. Occasionally."

"Mm." Sefa bent back over her map, insufferably serene. "Well. When you're ready to admit it's yours, I'll mark it on the chart. Every country needs a capital, and frankly the shrine smells like damp stone."

* * *

Evening came the way it always did in the Hollow — slowly, and from every direction at once, because the sun here couldn't decide on a single opinion about setting. Edmund walked out past the mill, past the lavender, past the orchard where the dead of Ansel sat in the branches after dark, gossiping, to the eastern gate.

The gate was only partly hers. It had begun as a memory — the old gate of Ansel, which half the kingdom recalled as white and half as grey, so it had settled on both, shifting depending on who looked. Now it was also a listening place. She'd learned that in her second year: that a keeper's job wasn't commanding the country but *attending* to it, the way you attend a sick friend, or a fire, or a promise.

She stood at the gate and listened.

At first: wind. Lavender. The distant argument of Master Corvel's schoolhouse with the evening.

Then, fainter, from far beyond the edge of everything — from the world, where the gods had gone and taken their leaking prayers with them — something like a voice. Not a god. Just a woman's voice, worn thin with dying, saying a daughter's name.

Not *Edmund*. Edmund had buried that name in Vell, under the false one, and let it rest.

Her *other* name. The one Mother Ilex had given her quietly, in the last year, the inheritance beneath the inheritance: the name that had been handed down keeper to keeper since Ansel fell, and that Edmund had never spoken aloud because speaking it felt like putting on a crown.

The voice said it once, hopefully, the way the dead call when they aren't sure anyone's listening.

And the country — her country, the one she'd built from memory and mercy and chalk dust and stubbornness — stirred around her like a sleeper turning toward a familiar sound. The lavender leaned east. The gate's stones warmed under her hand. Somewhere behind her, the little house with the one bed put another log on its own fire, unasked.

Edmund closed her eyes. *I hear you,* she thought at the voice, at the dark beyond the gate, at the whole wide world she'd spent her life running from. *Come the next opening. There's room.*

There was room. That was the whole point. That had always been the whole point, though it had taken her a runaway childhood, a false name, a dead mistress, a war, and a door to understand it: belonging was never a place you found. It was a place you made, and then kept, and then left the lamp burning in the window of.

She turned back down the lane toward the light, and the country walked with her, and the door stood open behind them both — ajar, the way she liked it.

Just wide enough for whoever came next.
