## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Years

The dying are the best customers, if you can stomach them.

They don't haggle. They don't ask where the memory goes after it leaves them, or who might wear it someday, or whether a happy hour spent in someone else's skull is worth the coin I press into their palm. They only want to be rid of the weight before the rest of them goes, and I only want to be paid, and between those two hungers we arrive at an arrangement that would scandalize every priest in Thornfell — which, these days, is not a long walk.

Maren Ostreicher was dying the way old women in hill villages die: slowly, loudly, and with a great deal of commentary on how everyone else was doing it wrong. She'd sent her grandson down to Saltcross three days ago with a message that named me, spelled my fee correctly, and included the phrase *before I lose my nerve or my bladder, whichever comes first*. I liked her already.

I climbed to her house on the last honest evening of autumn, when the light came slantwise through the pines and turned everything gold the way light does right before it stops bothering. The house leaned against the hillside like a drunk against a wall, timber and turf, smoke curling from a chimney that had been patched with what looked like an old shield boss. Inside smelled of rosemary, tallow, and the particular mineral tang of a body that has decided, quietly and without drama, to begin leaving.

"You're younger than I expected," she said from the bed by the window. Her voice was gravel wrapped in wool. "The last thief who came through here had a face like a closed fist."

"The last thief who came through here probably charged you for the fist."

She laughed, then coughed, then laughed again at the coughing, which told me she'd made peace with the whole business. "Sit. There's tea. It's terrible. Drink it anyway, because I made it and I'm dying and that makes me immune to criticism."

I sat. I drank the terrible tea. Outside the window, the valley did its patient work of being beautiful — terraced fields gone brown and sleeping, a stream stitching silver down toward the lowlands, the shrine on the far hill with its little stone god worn faceless by weather. Somewhere below, a bell rang vespers at the village chapel, and Maren's mouth went thin.

"Don't," she said, though I hadn't said anything. "Don't look at me like the priest does. He wants my confession. I want to sell him my best hour instead, and let him choke on the difference."

"That's generally the service I provide."

"Then provide it." She held out her hand. It was a good hand, still — broad-knuckled, scarred across the back from sixty years of work I'd never know about. "My happiest memory. The one with Tomas. You'll know it when you're in."

I set my kit on the blanket: the thin silver needle, the coil of thread fine as spider silk, the small glass vial that caught the last of the light and held it like a prisoner. Memory-work is mostly theater, if I'm honest. The needle doesn't pierce anything but attention. What it actually does is give the mind a door marked EXIT and the permission to use it. The rest — the extraction, the bottling, the selling — that's craft, and craft is just theft that studied hard.

"What do I get?" she asked, as her grandson had already paid half. "Besides empty."

"A full purse and one less thing to carry." I threaded the needle. "Some people find that's a mercy."

"And what do you find?"

I looked up. Old women ask questions like that because they've stopped needing anything from anyone and have discovered this makes them dangerous. "I find it's work," I said. "Good pay, bad hours."

"Liar," she said comfortably, and lay back against her pillows. "Do it, then. Before I remember why I should be afraid."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about stealing memories: you feel them leave.

Not the way you'd think — not like a tooth pulled, not like blood drawn. More like watching someone else's dream from inside their sleep. You stand in the doorway of a life that isn't yours and you hold the door open while the best part of it walks past you into the dark, and you try very hard not to touch anything on the way out, because touching is how thieves like me end up carrying more than we sold.

Maren's happiest memory began with rain.

Not storm-rain. The soft kind, the kind that means the world has decided to be gentle for an afternoon. A kitchen with a low ceiling and a fire going, and a man — Tomas, broad-shouldered, dark-haired, laughing at something before the memory even started, as if he'd been laughing his whole life and simply continued — standing at the table with flour on his hands up to the wrists. A girl of maybe six sitting on the counter, swinging her legs, wearing a crown woven out of the green onion tops because Tomas had put it there and declared her Queen of Everything Edible.

And Maren, young Maren, thirty years old and strong as a plow team, standing in the doorway with wet hair and dry clothes folded over her arm, watching the two of them and thinking — I felt the thought go by like a fish under ice — *this. Whatever else happens, whatever comes, I will have had this.*

That was the shape of it. That was the thing I put the needle to.

Except.

Except that as I drew the memory loose — carefully, the way you draw a splinter, following the grain — it didn't come away clean. Memories don't usually fight. They're tired things, most of them, glad to be lifted. But this one caught, snagged on something deep in her, and for a moment I was standing in that warm kitchen holding a handful of rain and flour-light and love, and the memory *looked at me.*

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left of my credibility: memories do not have eyes. This one did. Or had the equivalent — a pressure, a regard, a sense of being measured by something old that had been waiting inside a happy afternoon the way a hook waits inside bait.

And it spoke.

Not aloud. Not in words, exactly. In the way a prayer speaks — which is to say, it addressed itself to someone who wasn't in the room, and expected an answer.

*Keep her,* it whispered, in a voice like wind moving through a stone corridor. *Keep her. Keep her. Keep—*

"I'm not keeping anything," I said, out loud, in Maren's bedroom, with the old woman's eyes open and fixed on the ceiling and her breath coming slow and shallow. "I'm a courier, not a keeper. Things pass through me. That's the whole trade."

The memory shivered in my grip like a live fish. And then — and this is the part I will be turning over for the rest of my life, however long the rest of it runs — it answered itself.

*Kept,* it said, in a different voice. Deeper. Older. The voice of something that had been listening to prayers for so long it had learned to say them back, the way a parrot learns, the way an echo learns, the way — I understood suddenly, horribly — the way a god learns, when the people who fed it are gone and it is hungry enough to eat its own worship.

The kitchen dissolved. The rain, the flour, the queen of everything edible — all of it packed itself down small and bright and furious, and dropped into my vial like a coal into water, hissing.

I stood there shaking. Maren blinked, slow, and turned her head on the pillow.

"Well?" she said. "Is it out?"

"It's out."

"Do I still—" She frowned, groping. "There was a man. There was flour. There was—" Her face worked through grief, confusion, and finally settled somewhere in the middle, which was worse than either. "There was something. Wasn't there? Something good."

"There was something good," I said. "It's safe now. Nothing can take it from you again."

Which was true, in the way that all my best lies are true: technically, verifiably, and monstrous in every way that matters.

She nodded and closed her eyes. "The boy will show you out. Tell him to feed the hens. I keep forgetting the hens."

* * *

I walked down through the pines in full dark with the vial in my pocket, and I will tell you plainly that I considered throwing it into the stream.

Considered it seriously. Stood at the footbridge for a long time with my arm cocked back, feeling the little weight of it — lighter than an egg, heavier than a mountain — and thinking about the sound it would make, one small splash in all that running water, and how no one in the world would ever know except me.

But the broker in Saltcross pays forty crowns for a clean happiness, and forty crowns is four months of rooms and food and needle-thread and silence, and loneliness makes a poor accountant. So I did what I always do, which is the thing that keeps me alive and costs me everything else.

I pocketed it and walked on.

The road down to Saltcross is three hours if you're healthy and I made it in two, because fear walks fast. All the way, the vial murmured against my hip. Not words anymore — the words had been used up, spent on that terrible self-answering prayer — but warmth. Warmth like a banked fire. Warmth like something in there had noticed me noticing it, and had decided, with the patience of mountains, to wait.

At the crossroads below the village I passed the wayshrine — every road in Thornfell has one, little stone alcoves where travelers leave buttons and bread crusts and whispered requests for safe passage. Mine is a habit older than my trade; I flicked a copper into the alcove without breaking stride, and the old reflex mouthed the old words, *keep me, keep me, keep me*—

—and the vial in my pocket went warm as a struck match.

I stopped. The night held very still around me. Far off, on the faceless shrine hill above Maren's house, I could have sworn — could have *sworn* — that the stone god turned its worn blank face to follow me down the road.

Somewhere south, beyond the hills, beyond the borders I'd never crossed, something vast exhaled. And in my pocket, wrapped in flour-light and rain and a dead woman's love, a stolen memory settled deeper against my side like a cat that has chosen its person, and waited with me for morning.

Forty crowns, I told myself. Just forty crowns, and then it's someone else's problem.

The vial pulsed once, gently, like a heartbeat learning my rhythm.

By dawn, I was already lying to myself about how much trouble one small bottle could possibly be.
