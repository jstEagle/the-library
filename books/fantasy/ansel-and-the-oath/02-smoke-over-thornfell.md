## 2. Smoke Over Thornfell

The morning the Crown erased my country, I was haggling over eggs.

Not metaphorical eggs. Actual eggs, three of them, brown and speckled and priced like they'd been laid by a saint. Marta at the corner stall in Lowmarket had developed a sudden reverence for her poultry since the roads went strange, and she was holding firm at four coppers when any fool could see the eggs were two days from turning.

"Three," I said.

"They're fresh."

"Marta. I can smell them from here. They're not fresh, they're *ambitious*."

She laughed despite herself — Marta always did, which was why I liked her, and why I'd never stolen from her, which in my line of work counted as a kind of love — and while she was laughing I slid the coppers across and took the eggs, and that was the whole transaction, honest as anything I did.

That was when the crier came through the square.

Thornfell didn't have criers anymore, technically. We hadn't had much of anything officially for a while — no assizes, no tax riders, no Crown men, because nobody important could be bothered to ride up into the mountains where the passes were bad and the people were worse. But old habits die hard in market squares, and there was a man who'd taken it upon himself to read proclamations aloud on Sundays, mostly for the entertainment value. He was there now, standing on his crate with a paper in his fist, and he wasn't reading it for entertainment.

He was reading it twice.

"—by order of the Lord Chancellor's Registry, the province formerly designated Thornfell is struck from all rolls, ledgers, and maps of the realm. No writ shall run therein. No law shall apply therein. No protection of the Crown shall extend therein. Persons residing within its bounds are declared—"

He stopped. Swallowed. Started again from the top, like maybe the paper would behave better the second time.

I stood there with three ambitious eggs going warm in my hand and listened to my home be deleted.

*Persons residing within its bounds.* He got through it eventually. Declared outside the law's care. Not outlaws — outlaws were still people, still worth hunting, still worth the ink it took to name them. This was something else. This was the realm shrugging and saying *we were never holding that*, the way you'd disclaim a debt or deny knowing a man who owed you money. Thornfell wasn't being punished. It was being *returned to sender*.

The square was quiet in the particular way squares get quiet — everyone listening without looking like they're listening, everyone doing arithmetic behind their eyes. I watched a tanner count his children. I watched an old soldier touch the stump of two fingers and say nothing. I watched Marta look at her eggs, then at me, and I saw her decide not to ask for the coppers back, and I understood that we had just stopped being neighbors and started being the last two people on a sinking deck, and even that kindness was a kind of goodbye.

"Well," I said, because someone had to. "That'll do wonders for property values."

Nobody laughed. Tough crowd. Dying economy.

I bought the rest of Marta's eggs — all nine, at full price, because if the world was ending I was going to be the sort of person who overpaid for eggs on his way out — and I walked home through streets that felt different under my boots, though nothing about them had changed. That was the trick of it. Nothing had changed. The stones were the same stones. The smoke off the chimneys rose the same way it always had, straight up until the wind remembered itself. But a place is mostly paperwork, when you get down to it. A place is the agreement that it exists. And somewhere south of us, in a warm office smelling of sealing wax, a clerk had dipped his pen and unmade the mountains.

* * *

My lodgings were a room above a cooper's shop in the Tannery Quarter, which sounds grim and was, but the rent was paid in memories instead of coin — the cooper liked reliving his wedding day, and I liked sleeping indoors, so the arrangement suited us both. I climbed the stairs, bolted the door out of habit, and sat on my bed with the little lead-lined box open on my knees.

Inside, wrapped in wool: the memory I'd lifted off the dying woman three nights ago. Her happiest one, she'd said. The one she wanted gone before the end, because her daughter couldn't afford the grief-tithe and the church taxed joy left unclaimed. Standard work. Sad, ordinary work. I'd done it a hundred times.

Except this one had been whispering to me since I took it.

Not words, exactly. More like the feeling of words, the way you sometimes wake certain a voice was saying your name a half-second before sound arrived. It sat in its box like a coal that wouldn't cool, and when I put my palm flat on the lid I could feel it — faint, patient, *attentive*. Memories aren't supposed to be attentive. Memories are meat that remembers being a person. They don't watch you back.

"You're a problem," I told it. "You know that?"

It whispered something that might have been a prayer. And here was the part I hadn't told anyone, the part I kept turning over at night like a stone looking for what lived under it: prayers are supposed to go up. Toward gods, toward whatever listens up there. This one came *down*. Or sideways. Or from inside the box, which amounts to the same thing, directionally speaking.

I closed the box. I am a professional. Professionals do not have conversations with inventory.

Then I did what any sensible person does when their country ceases to exist: I made a list.

* * *

The list looked like this:

One. Coin. I had some, not enough. The southern passes cost money — bribes at the tolls, passage on a caravan, food that wasn't ambitious. Two. Route. South through the Salt Bridge if it was still standing, down into Vetch territory, then wherever the world still admitted I existed. Three. Weight. Everything I owned fit in one pack already; the only heavy thing I carried was the box, and I'd be damned before I sold that particular piece of stock now, when every buyer in Thornfell would be desperate and desperate buyers talk, and talking gets thieves hanged.

Four. Company.

I wrote the word and then crossed it out. There wasn't going to be company. Company was a liability with a face. Company meant slowing down for someone's blistered feet, splitting rations, standing still when every instinct said run. I had spent eleven years learning exactly one lesson on these roads, and the lesson was: *everyone leaves, so leave first.* My mother taught it by example. The smugglers refined it. The one time I'd broken it — and I don't talk about Ferren, we're not doing that today — the road had charged me everything I had and given me nothing back but the habit of counting exits.

So: alone. Alone was clean. Alone was fast. Alone was the whole reason I was still alive in a trade where the life expectancy made mayflies look established.

I was still telling myself this, with real conviction, when the first shrine started burning.

* * *

You could see it from the cooper's roof, which is where I went, because roofs are where information lives and I've always been more comfortable above street level. Smoke off the Pilgrim's Way, thin and black and wrong — woodsmoke goes gray, but lacquered god-wood burns black, and every shrine in Thornfell was built of god-wood because that's what you build shrines out of when your gods have been asleep for three hundred years and nobody's around to complain about the lumber.

Riders on the north road. Six of them, moving in formation, which told me everything. Smugglers don't ride in formation. Bandits don't bother. Only one organization in the world still drilled its people to move like chess pieces, and I'd thought the Inquisition had died with the old king, dissolved and disgraced and ground into history like everything else from that era.

Apparently history had opinions about staying buried.

They burned the wayshrine at Miller's Cross while I watched. Two riders dismounted, stacked the god-wood, set it alight, and stood at attention while it burned — not looting, not hurrying, just *witnessing*, like the fire was a formality being properly observed. Then they mounted up and moved on to the next one, unhurried as tax collectors, and behind them the smoke stood up over the hills in a row, one column per shrine, like the country was raising its hand to ask a question nobody would answer.

And here's the thing I want to be honest about, because honesty is rare enough in my profession that I like to practice it in private where no one can hold it against me: my first feeling wasn't fear. It was offense.

Those shrines were *mine*. Not spiritually — I hadn't prayed at one since I was small enough to believe praying worked. Mine the way a thief owns a city: I knew every crack in them, every loose tile, every hiding place behind the altar where runners stashed messages and idiots stashed savings. I'd picked the lock of the Miller's Cross shrine at fourteen. I'd hidden in the Hollowbrook shrine during a whiteout, once, and slept between the feet of a god whose name nobody remembered, and woken up feeling — I don't know. Held. I'd never told anyone that either.

Now men in gray coats were burning my architecture, and the Crown had ruled that no crime committed against me counted as a crime at all, and somewhere in my chest a cold little voice said: *good. Now nobody will come after you when you take what isn't nailed down. Best weather for thieving in years.*

The cold voice is usually right. That's the trouble with it.

Because underneath the offense, underneath the professional delight at a lawless market, there was the other thing — the thing I'd been stepping around all morning the way you step around a drunk in a doorway. The whispering box on my bed. The black smoke in a neat row across the hills. The dying woman's happiness murmuring prayers that answered themselves, and the question I'd been refusing to ask, which was: *answered by whom?*

The old gods were asleep. Everyone knew it. Three hundred years of silence, of empty altars and dead liturgies, of Sera-whatever priestesses keeping candles lit for listeners who'd checked out before the invention of the stirrup. The silence was the one fixed point of Thornfell theology. You could doubt the gods existed. You could not doubt they were quiet.

But I had a memory in a box that prayed, and I had sold — gods, how many? Dozens? Hundreds? — memories up the chain to buyers I'd never met, and the smoke was rising in a row like the hills themselves were waking up and stretching, and I am many things but I am not stupid. Slow, sometimes. Selfish, reliably. But not stupid.

Something was eating. Something was being fed. And I knew exactly what stolen memories tasted like, because I'd been selling them by the pound.

* * *

I packed by lamplight, which didn't take long. Clothes, tools, the good knife and the deniable knife, dried fish, the nine ambitious eggs wrapped in cloth because waste is a sin in my religion and my religion has exactly one commandment. The lead box went in last, wrapped double, pushed deep against the pack's spine where it couldn't lean against my back and whisper directly into my shoulder blades. I'm not superstitious. I'm *experienced*. There's a difference and it's measured in scars.

The cooper knocked around midnight — I heard him hesitate on the stairs, that specific pause of a man deciding whether to knock — and when he did, I opened the door on the chain.

"Heard you're leaving," he said. He's sixty, hands like tree roots, face like a man who's been disappointed by wood his whole career. "South?"

"South."

"Take the long way past Hollowbrook. Riders burned the short bridge yesterday." He handed me a bundle I hadn't asked for: bread, cheese, a stoppered flask. "For the road."

"Brammel. You don't have to—"

"I know what I don't have to." He shrugged, enormous shoulders rising like a drawbridge. "Country's gone. Doesn't mean the people in it have to act like it." He turned to go, then stopped. "Your rent's paid through the season. If you come back."

I won't come back. We both knew it. "If I come back," I said, "you'll tell me about your daughter's wedding again. All four hours."

His face did something complicated and he clapped the doorframe twice, the way you'd pat a horse, and went downstairs, and I stood there holding bread I hadn't earned in a country that officially wasn't there, and thought: *this is what they're burning.* Not the shrines. This. The whole invisible ledger of small debts and small kindnesses that no registry ever recorded and no edict could erase, because it was never written anywhere except in people. The Crown could strike Thornfell from the maps. It couldn't strike it from Brammel's hands.

Which meant the only way to truly kill a country was to make sure no one was left to remember it.

Huh, I thought. Funny. That sounded like somebody's job description, and I suspected, with a sick lurch, that it had recently been mine.

* * *

I left before dawn, because dawn is when patrols change and roads belong to whoever's awake earliest. The Tannery Quarter was silent, the smoke columns to the north visible even in the dark — they'd burned through the night, working their way down the Pilgrim's Way shrine by shrine, methodical as bookkeeping. By the time I hit the south road the sky behind me had a bruised orange glow to it, and I told myself it was sunrise. It was sunrise. Mostly.

The plan was simple, and simple plans are the only ones that survive contact with roads. South to the Salt Bridge, two days if the weather held. Avoid the main track, use the drover paths. Spend nothing I could steal, steal nothing I could avoid spending. Reach Vetch inside a week, sell nothing, tell no one, become a rumor heading further south. Alone. Clean. Fast.

I was a mile out of town when I felt it through the pack: the box, warm against my spine, whispering. And for the first time it wasn't a prayer.

It was a name.

Mine. Said the way my mother used to say it — the tone she saved for when I'd broken something or lied badly, weary and fond and final — except my mother had been dead eleven years and this voice had never spoken to me before in my life, and it said *Isandro* like it was checking whether the word still fit. Like it was trying the name on. Like it was confirming ownership before the paperwork came through.

I stopped in the middle of the drover path. The wind moved the grass. Somewhere north, another shrine caught, and the sky brightened a shade that had nothing to do with morning.

"Alone," I reminded myself out loud. "Clean. Fast."

The box whispered my name again, patiently, the way you'd call a dog you knew would come eventually.

I walked south. I want that on the record: I walked south, alone, exactly according to plan, and I did not turn around, and I definitely did not notice, three switchbacks down, a figure in travel-stained vestments climbing the path behind me with the unmistakable stride of a woman who had spent her whole life walking toward things that frightened her.

I noticed. Of course I noticed. I'm a thief.

Noticing is the whole job.
