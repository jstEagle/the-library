## 3. What the Priestess Kept

I had served the god of the Hollow Bell for nineteen years, and in all that time it had never once spoken to me. This is not unusual. Most gods of the old rite are quiet by nature — they listen, they keep, they hold your griefs the way a riverbed holds water. You do not pray to them for answers. You pray so that someone, somewhere, remembers you were here.

So when the silence deepened, I told myself it was my fault. Too few candles. Too many doubts. A priestess who secretly resents her vows makes a poor vessel, and the god withdraws from poor vessels the way warmth withdraws from a dying fire.

I was wrong, of course. I would spend a great deal of blood learning exactly how wrong.

* * *

The shrine of the Hollow Bell sat in the crook of two hills above the village of Marrowfen, and it was ugly in the way that only truly loved things are ugly. Its walls were fieldstone and apology. Its bell — hollow, cracked, mute for three hundred years — hung in a tower that leaned like a drunk uncle at a wedding. Pilgrims came anyway. They always came. They brought their dead mothers' voices, their first loves' laughter, the sound of their childhood kitchens, and they left them at the altar the way you'd leave bread for a bird: trusting something small and holy to carry them onward.

My job was to tend what they left. Not to take. Never to take. That distinction was the whole of my faith, and I clung to it with both hands because it was the only rope I had.

On the morning the riders came, I was sweeping ash out of the hearth — ordinary ash, hearth-ash, the kind that doesn't mean anything — when Brother Aldous came up the hill at a run, which should have been my first warning. Aldous was seventy and considered stairs a personal insult.

"Sera." He braced himself on the doorframe, wheezing. "Riders. On the fen road. Six of them. Grey cloaks."

Grey cloaks. The word arrived in my body before it arrived in my mind — a cold drop behind the sternum, an old instinct waking. Inquisition. But the inquisition had been dust for a generation, disbanded, its archives burned, its name spoken only as a curse or a joke depending on how much you'd lost to it. Grey cloaks were history. Grey cloaks were grandmothers' stories.

"Maybe they're just grey," I said. "Cloaks come in grey. It's a common dye."

"They're carrying firepots, Sera."

Ah. Well. That settled the dye question.

* * *

They gave us an hour to evacuate, which was generous by historical standards, and I spent forty minutes of it doing the one thing I could not explain to anyone who hadn't kept a shrine: choosing what to save.

You cannot carry a god. I want that understood. People imagine priests fleeing with idols under their arms, gods tucked into satchels like contraband relics. But the Hollow Bell did not live in the bell. It lived in the keeping — in three centuries of held memories, layered like sediment, pressed into the stones and the altar-slate and the very dust of the floor. If the shrine burned, the god didn't die. It just... lost its house. Lost its shape. Became a voice with no throat, a keeper with nothing left to keep.

Or so I believed then. I have since learned there are worse fates than homelessness.

Aldous wanted to save the bell. Of course he did; it was his life's absurdity, hauling water for a cracked thing that had never rung. I let him organize four villagers around the ropes while I went down into the reliquary vault beneath the altar, where we kept the oldest deposits — memories too fragile or too heavy for open keeping. Names of the drowned. Last words of plague-dead children. A soldier's memory of the moment before a battle that no surviving record otherwise mentions.

The vault was cool and smelled of slate and tallow. And it was wrong.

I don't know how else to say it. Every keeper learns the texture of a full vault the way a midwife knows the weight of a healthy child — not through study but through the hands, through some animal certainty. And the vault felt *light*. Emptied. As though someone had gone through it with a sieve and taken the fine flour and left the husks.

I knelt at the first shelf and put my palm against the oldest stone, the Vow-Slate, where the founding keepers had pressed the shrine's own consecration. Nothing. Not silence — silence is a presence, silence has weight — but absence. A hole shaped like a memory. I checked a second stone. A third. Husks, all of them. Shells of slate that still hummed faintly with the *shape* of what they'd held, the way a bed holds the shape of a sleeper after they rise.

Someone had harvested my god.

I sat down on the cold floor, in my sweeping dress, with riders burning my home above me, and I laughed. I couldn't help it. Nineteen years of self-flagellation over a silent god — the extra vigils, the fasting, the terrible suspicion that I was simply not devout enough to be heard — and the truth was that the god hadn't been ignoring me. The god had been *robbed*. You cannot listen without memories any more than you can sing without a tongue. Something had eaten the Hollow Bell's voice from the inside, quietly, patiently, and left me kneeling at an empty altar blaming myself for the hunger.

The first firepot came through the eastern window while I was still laughing. I think that's why the villagers later said I seemed unhinged. There's a particular madness in discovering your grief was misdirected all along.

* * *

We got the bell out. That much, we managed. Aldous and the four villagers and I hauled it down the hill on a handcart while the shrine went up behind us, and I will tell you honestly that I did not look back, because looking back is a luxury of people whose gods still have somewhere to live.

The riders watched us go. That was the part I hadn't expected. Grey-cloaked, six of them, sitting their horses on the fen road with the patience of men following orders rather than zeal, and they let a cartload of peasants trundle past carrying three hundred years of sacred bronze. One of them, a woman with a burn-scarred jaw, even nodded to me. Courteous. Almost kind.

It was the courtesy that made me stop the cart.

"You'll want the ledgers," she said, before I could speak. "Records of deposit. We've already taken the archive from the motherhouse at Cael Row, but parish shrines keep copies. Sloppy practice, keeping copies."

"You burned it," I said. "Why bother with ledgers?"

"Because burning is for shrines." She said it the way you'd correct a child's arithmetic. "Shrines are vessels. Vessels get emptied and destroyed so the contents can't be re-housed. Ledgers are provenance. Provenance is value." Her eyes moved to the bell on the cart, and something flickered across her face — not reverence. Recognition. The look of a merchant appraising stock. "That one's already been drawn down, hasn't it? Feels light. Someone got to your parish ahead of schedule."

"I don't know what you mean."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't. Keepers never do. You tend the pantry and never ask who eats." She drew her horse alongside the cart, close enough that I could smell the oil on her cloak, and lowered her voice as if offering a kindness. "Your god isn't special, priestess. Half the old rite's been harvested this season. There's a market now. There's been a market for years — little thieves skimming the edges, selling mortal keepsakes to collectors. But lately someone's been buying *upward*. Buying in volume. Feeding something that eats a great deal."

"What something?"

She smiled with the scarred half of her face. "Does it matter? What matters is supply. And supply means thieves. Thieves leave trails, and trails lead to names." She straightened in the saddle, done with charity. "There was a shipment through Thornfell last month. Fen-country memories, batch-stamped from parishes along this very road. Yours among them, I'd wager. The broker's ledger lists a source. A supplier of unusual quality, apparently — mortal-cut, cleanly lifted, no damage. The broker pays well for clean work."

"Give me the name," I said. My voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice.

"Nakamura," she said. "Isandro Nakamura. Memory-thief, works the smuggler roads out of Thornfell. Though I'd hurry, if I were you. When the Crown finishes striking Thornfell from the registries — and that's this week, I'm told — there'll be no law in it at all. No law means no witnesses. No witnesses means the harvest goes faster." She gathered her reins. "Burn what's left, lads," she called to her riders, almost cheerfully, "and let's ride."

I stood in the road holding the cart's handlebar hard enough to split the wood grain, watching six grey cloaks dwindle toward the next parish, the next empty altar, the next husk.

Behind me, Marrowfen's shrine collapsed inward with a sound like a long exhale, and the smoke rose straight up into a windless sky, a grey column visible, I knew, from every farm in the valley. Let them see it. Let every soul who'd ever left a memory on our altar come up the hill tomorrow and find nothing but warm stone, and let them ask why, and let someone point down the road at me.

I had a name. I had a direction. And I had, coiled under my ribs where my faith used to sit, something hotter and far more durable than grief.

* * *

Aldous found me at dusk, three miles on, sitting beside the handcart where the other villagers had made camp. He lowered himself onto the cart's rail with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions, and for a while neither of us spoke. The bell lay between us under sacking, and once, when the wind shifted, the sack moved — just slightly, just enough — as though something inside had turned over in its sleep.

"It's still alive," Aldous said. "The god. You can feel it, can't you?"

"Barely." I fed a stick into the cookfire. "It's like... you know when a man's starving, and he stops begging, and just lies there? Past hunger into something quieter?" I turned the stick. "It's not silent because it's serene anymore, Aldous. It's silent because it's been eaten. Nearly all of it. And whatever's left is holding on to the last scraps the way a drowning man holds a plank."

"Then we find who did it and we make them give it back."

I almost smiled. Nineteen years in the rite and the old man still thought faith was a matter of asking nicely. "And how does a priestess and a retired bell-ringer compel a memory-thief to return stolen goods?"

"We don't," Aldous said placidly. "But you will. I've watched you for nineteen years, girl. You sweep the same corner twice when it bothers you. You rewrote the parish liturgy four times because the phrasing wasn't honest enough. You once made a baron's son apologize to a scullery maid." He patted the sack over the bell, gently, the way you'd pat a sick animal. "Grief sits wrong on you. It curdles. It becomes a plan."

He was right, which annoyed me more than the fire had.

Because here is what I had understood, standing in that road while the smoke went up: the woman with the scarred jaw had given me the name casually, contemptuously, the way you toss a bone to a dog to keep it from biting. She had thought she was telling me about a thief. She had not understood what she was actually telling me.

She was telling me that my god's memories — three hundred years of Marrowfen's dead mothers, drowned fishermen, plague-children's last words, the whole tender sediment of a valley's soul — had been *sold*. Not destroyed. Sold. Which meant they existed somewhere, intact, in somebody's inventory, stacked like cordwood in a broker's vault. Which meant they could be taken back.

And she had told me the thief's name. Isandro Nakamura. Working the smuggler roads of Thornfell — a country being erased from every map and record even as we sat by our little fire, a country about to become a hole in the world where no law reached and anything could be harvested.

Everyone else was fleeing Thornfell. The smart ones, the survivors, the people with functioning instincts — south, away, anywhere with borders and registries and protection.

I looked at the sack over the bell. It stirred again, faintly, like breath.

"I'm going to need a smuggler's map," I said aloud, "and a weapon I don't know how to use, and a great deal of luck."

Aldous snorted. "You'll need more than that. You'll need to find a thief who doesn't want to be found, in a country that won't exist, and convince him to surrender his livelihood out of the goodness of his heart."

"No," I said. "I don't need his goodness. I've had nineteen years of goodness. Goodness sweeps corners and rings no bells." I pulled the sacking back and looked at the Hollow Bell itself, cracked and mute and stubbornly, barely alive, and I made it a promise with the only vocabulary I had left. "What I need is his fear. Everyone has something they've stolen that they can't afford to lose. I'm going to find out what Nakamura can't afford to lose — and then I'm going to stand in front of him holding it."

The wind came down off the fens and carried the last of the smoke east, toward Thornfell, and I swear on the ashes of everything I ever believed that the bell leaned into it. Just slightly. Like a listener turning toward a voice.

Somewhere down those roads, a thief was carrying my god in pieces and didn't even know it.

I intended to introduce myself.
