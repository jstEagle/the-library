## 5. The Child Who Remembers Nothing

Here is the thing about me: I don't know the thing about me.

I know the well has a rope that bites your hands. I know the bread in the blue house is stale but the cheese is fine. I know the word *village*, because there are houses all around me, and I know the word *empty*, because there are no people in them. But I don't know if I lived here. I don't know my mother's face or my father's laugh or whether I ever had either. I don't even know if Wren is a name somebody gave me or a word I made up out of the sound birds make.

I checked every house. That took two days. In one of them there was a mirror, and the person in it was small and brown-eyed and had a scab on their chin, so I decided that was me. It seemed polite to agree with the mirror.

The gods come at dusk.

I call them gods because they are too big for anything else. They don't have shapes exactly. They have *weights*. One of them leans on the eastern hills like a hand pressing down on dough. Another pools in the well water when I look too long, deep under the bucket-line, something vast turning over slowly like a fish that has forgotten how to be small. And at dusk they all drift toward the village square, toward me, slow as smoke, hungry as winter.

They can't touch me. I figured that out the first night, when I stood in the square and let the biggest one pour over me like cold water, and it went *through* me and came out the other side and made a sound I felt in my teeth. A disappointed sound. Like a dog sniffing an empty bowl.

Because that's what I am, apparently. An empty bowl.

The gods want memories. I don't know how I know this, except that everything in me knows it, the way I know the rope bites. They circle and circle and lean against me and find nothing to hold, nothing to drink, nothing to eat, and then they make that sound again and drift off to sulk in the hills until tomorrow.

So we wait each other out, the gods and me. They're patient. I'm stubborn. It's not much of a life, but it's the only one I've got, and I'd like to point out that I'm keeping it in very good condition.

Then two people walked into my village, and everything got complicated.

* * *

I saw them from the bell tower, which is where I watch from, because you can see the roads both ways and because the bell rope is fun to swing on when nobody's around to tell you not to.

One was tall and moved like the road owed them money. The other wore grey robes with the god-marks torn off the hem — I could see the little stitched shapes where they used to be, picked out careful, like someone unsewing their own skin. A priestess who stopped being a priestess, or a priestess pretending hard. Behind them, way back on the southern road, dust hung in the air where dust shouldn't hang. Horses. Many horses, coming slow.

I should have hidden. Hiding is rule number one of being eight and alone in an empty country: when people come, be furniture.

But here's the other thing about me: I wanted to see them. Not for any reason I could name. I just — wanted. It was the strongest feeling I'd had since the mirror, and feelings are food too, and I was starving.

So instead of hiding I climbed down and stood in the middle of the square, right where the biggest god likes to pool at dusk, and I waited for them like I was the mayor.

The tall one saw me first. They stopped so suddenly the priestess walked into their back.

"That's a child," the priestess said.

"It's a trap," the tall one said. "It's always a child. Last time it was a child, I lost three fingers' worth of coin and most of my faith in humanity."

"You have faith in humanity?"

"I have a system."

They argued about me like I wasn't standing right there, which was fine, because while they argued I got to look at them properly. The tall one had a face like a closed door and eyes that kept flicking to the rooftops, counting exits. The priestess had a face like weather about to happen. Both of them looked tired in a way that went all the way down, past tired into something older.

"Hello," I said, because nobody had said hello to me in — well. In as long as I could remember, which, to be fair, is not a very long ruler.

The tall one flinched. Actually flinched, like I'd thrown something.

"Don't," they said.

"Don't what?"

"Don't *talk* to me in that — " They waved a hand at all of me. "Children talk. Children say hello. Children have dirt on their faces and opinions about things. I know what children are. I've robbed enough of them." They paused. "That came out wrong."

"It really did," said the priestess.

"I mean I've *passed through* villages with — never mind. Sera. We are not picking up a child. We are fleeing a country that officially does not exist, pursued by riders who burn shrines, and you want to add a *child*."

"I didn't say anything," the priestess said.

"You were thinking it loudly."

The sun was going down. I knew what that meant. I felt the big god start to gather itself behind the eastern hills, the way it always did, rolling its weight toward the square, toward me, toward the empty bowl it kept hoping would fill.

The tall one felt it too. I watched it hit them — watched them go still in a different way, the stillness of prey. Their head turned east. Their hand found something in their coat pocket and gripped it.

"What," they said quietly, "is *that*."

"That," I said, "is the big one. It comes at dusk. Don't worry, it can't do anything to me."

"Why can't it do anything to you?"

"Because I'm empty."

I said it the way I'd say the sky is blue, and the effect on the two of them was like I'd said the sky was bleeding. The priestess went white. The tall one went very, very still, and then did something strange: they took their hand out of their pocket empty, deliberately, like showing a dog you've got no food.

The god arrived.

I'm used to it by now, but watching these two experience it was almost worth the loneliness. The weight rolled over the square, cold and enormous, and the light went strange and gold-brown like the inside of a bell, and the tall one's knees actually bent. The priestess dropped to the ground and pressed her forehead to the cobbles and started whispering — prayers, I guessed, though hers sounded angry, more argument than asking.

And the god poured through me like it always did, hunting for purchase, finding none, and made its sound. Its big, grinding, disappointed sound. *Empty. Empty again.*

Then it did something new.

It stopped.

It turned — I don't know how else to say it; it doesn't have a front, but it turned — and it looked at the tall one. At the thing in the place where the thing in their pocket had been, even though their pocket was now empty. It looked at Isandro — I learned their name later, from the priestess shouting it — the way a starving man looks at a loaf.

"*Thief*," it said.

Not out loud. Out loud there was only wind. But the word landed in my skull anyway, huge and slow, and I saw from their face it landed in theirs too.

"Oh no," Isandro said. "No no no. I sold that fair and square. Finders keepers is a whole legal framework—"

The god leaned closer. The gold light thickened. And I felt — this is the part I can't explain right — I felt it reach *past* me. Through me. Like I was a window and it was looking at the room behind me, except there is no room behind me, there's never been a room behind me, and for one horrible second I understood what everyone meant when they talked about *before*. There's a hole where before goes. I live in a house with no rooms.

The god wanted the hole.

The god wanted *me*.

"Isandro," the priestess breathed. "It's not circling the child. It's — oh, silent god. It thinks the child is a *vessel*. An empty vessel. That's why it keeps coming back. That's why it can't take anything — there's nothing to take — but if it ever learns how to *put something in*—"

"Put what in?"

"Anything. Everything. Us. Thornfell. Every memory it's swallowed and can't digest." She got up off the cobbles, and her face had gone from white to iron. "An empty mind is the holiest object there is, according to the old rites. A vessel for a god to be born in."

The tall one looked at me. Really looked, for the first time, door-face and all. And I watched something happen behind their eyes that they clearly hated: a war, quick and ugly, between the part of them that counts exits and the part that — well. I didn't know about that part yet. Neither did they, I think.

"We leave at dawn," Isandro said. "South. Alone. Two of us, fast and quiet, the way you survive."

"Yes," said the priestess.

"The child stays."

"Yes," said the priestess.

"Stop agreeing with me when I'm being heartless! Argue!"

"You just said the correct thing. I'm as surprised as you are."

Isandro made a sound like a kettle giving up and stalked to the well and sat on its edge with their back to both of us, which is a very clear kind of body language, the kind that says *conversation over, also I am definitely not going to change my mind, also watch me not change my mind.*

The god drifted back to the hills, sulking. Dusk ended. The bell-light faded. Ordinary dark came, the good kind, the kind without weights in it.

I sat down across from Isandro, on the other side of the well, and ate half a heel of the stale blue-house bread, and offered them the other half.

They stared at it like it might be rigged.

"It's stale," I said. "But the cheese is fine."

"Where did you get cheese?"

"Blue house. Third street. Under the floorboard that isn't nailed down because whoever lived there thought nobody would check."

Something happened to their mouth. It took me a moment to identify it, because I'd only seen it in the mirror once, practicing. It was a smile. A real one, small and crooked and annoyed at itself.

"You looted an entire village," they said, "and organized it by house."

"I organized it by *which houses had better stuff*. There's a difference. The yellow house has blankets. The green house has a knife I can't lift. The church has nothing, someone already cleaned it out, and they left the candles, which is rude, candles are the best part."

"They left the candles." Isandro rubbed their face. "Sera. Sera, are you hearing this."

From the shrine doorway, the priestess said, mildly, "I hear a child who survived alone in an emptied country for gods know how long, with inquisition riders on the roads, and stayed fed and organized and — did you swing on the bell rope? There's wear on it. Fresh wear."

"...Maybe," I said.

"Alone," Isandro repeated, like the word was a stone they were turning over. "How long?"

I shrugged. "Since the beginning."

"Of what?"

"Of me. That's all there is. You're looking at the whole thing." I spread my arms, presenting myself, the complete collection. "No before. I checked. There's a hole where it should go and the gods keep trying to drink out of it and it's very rude of them and also I think maybe sad? I can't tell if it's sad. I don't have anything to compare it to. Maybe everybody's got a hole and they're all just polite about it."

The silence after that lasted a long time. Long enough for the first stars. Isandro looked at me across the well, and their face did several things in a row that they would probably deny under oath, and finally they said, in a voice roughened all the way down:

"Everybody's got a before, kid. Yours was taken. Or — " and here their hand went, without them seeming to notice, to their own coat pocket, to the empty spot where the heavy thing wasn't. "Or traded. Somebody decided your before was worth something, and now it's in a market somewhere, and you're — "

They stopped. They looked at their own hand like it had betrayed them.

"Sera," they said. "We're taking the child."

"Wonderful," said the priestess. "What changed your mind?"

"Nothing changed my mind. My mind is unchanged. This is a purely tactical decision. The gods want the child as a vessel. If we leave the child, the gods get a vessel, and then Thornfell gets a brand-new hungry god wearing an eight-year-old, and that is a problem that follows us south forever. Whereas if we bring the child—" they gestured at me, sweeping, "—we deny the enemy resources. It's logistics."

"It's logistics," Sera agreed solemnly.

"It's entirely logistics."

"Absolutely. Nothing to do with the bread."

"The bread is irrelevant."

I tore the cheese in three. Nobody argued. That's how I knew I was coming with them, before anybody said it — because when you share food with people and they eat it, that's a contract, older than any country, even ones that get erased from maps.

* * *

We left before dawn, while the gods were still sulking in the hills.

Isandro carried me on their shoulders through the fields because I was slow and the dust on the southern road had gotten closer in the night. Up high like that I could feel them thinking. Thinking is loud, when someone carries you. Their thoughts had a shape, tight and prickly, like a hedgehog trying to pretend it's a smooth stone.

"You're doing it again," I said.

"Doing what."

"Deciding to care about me and being mad about it."

"I am *not* — " A pause. The hedgehog bristling. "Kid, I've spent twenty years making sure the only person whose problems are mine is me. It's a discipline. It's kept me alive. You are a walking violation of a very carefully maintained system."

"Good," I said. "Systems are boring. I tried organizing one once. The village. By house."

"That's different."

"How?"

Another pause, longer. From ahead, Sera was singing something low and tuneless under her breath, some hymn with the holy words scratched out and replaced with ordinary ones, and the road ran pale ahead of us toward mountains I'd only seen from the bell tower, mountains I had never once imagined reaching because imagining requires a future and futures need somewhere to stand.

"Because," Isandro said finally, and their voice did the rough thing again, "you organize things so you don't lose them. I organize things so I can leave them. It's the same skill pointing opposite directions." They shifted me higher on their shoulders. "Hold on. The road gets bad past the ridge, and there are riders on it, and I need both hands for lying to them convincingly."

Behind us, far back, the eastern hills shivered — the whole skyline of them, like water disturbed — and I felt the big god notice us leaving. Felt it turn its vast attention from the empty village to the small procession stealing its vessel out from under it. Felt the hunger sharpen into something with intent.

Isandro felt it too. I know because their spine went straight as a bell rope pulled taut.

"Faster," they said, to no one, to all of us. "We move faster now."

And we went, three of us, down roads that weren't on any map anymore, carrying everything we owned — a stolen memory, a broken faith, and a child full of nothing — with a god waking up behind us and learning, hunger by hunger, how to follow.
