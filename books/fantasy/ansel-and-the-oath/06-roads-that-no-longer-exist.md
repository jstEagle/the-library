## 6. Roads That No Longer Exist

The map said there was nothing here.

I mean that literally. I'd stolen a surveyor's chart off a dead man in Lowmark two winters back — one of the good ones, inked on vellum, the kind the Crown paid fortunes for and then burned when the borders moved. Where we were standing, the vellum showed blank parchment and a small notation in a clerk's tidy hand: *uninhabited. no road. no water.*

"No road," I told Wren, kicking a stone off the edge of a track wide enough for three carts abreast. "No water." I pointed at the river glinting below us through the pines, fat and silver and extremely wet. "You see the problem with maps, kid? They're just someone else's memory of a place. And memories lie."

Wren looked at the river, then at me, then back at the river, with the flat patience of an eight-year-old who has decided the adult is saying something stupid but is willing to wait for it to become interesting. "Then how do you know where to go?"

"Because I was here before the map stopped being true." I crouched and drew in the dirt with a stick: three lines converging on a notch between two peaks. "Roads don't stop existing because someone stops writing them down. They just go quiet. Like a song nobody sings for a while. The stones remember even if the clerks don't." I tapped the notch. "That's the Shepherd's Gap. In three days it puts us out past the last inquisition waystation, and from there it's smuggler country all the way south."

"What's a smuggler?"

"A person with an excellent sense of direction and no respect for paperwork."

Sera, behind us with her hood up against the drizzle, made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Three days on the road and she still walked like she was attending her own funeral — spine straight, hands folded, eyes forward, as if grief were a uniform she refused to take off even to sleep. She hadn't forgiven me yet. She might never. But she'd stopped checking whether I was about to run, which from a priestess of the silent god was practically a love letter.

"Teach them properly," she said, "or not at all."

"I'm teaching them properly."

"You're teaching them cynicism."

"Cynicism *is* the proper lesson. Everything after that is decoration."

Wren crouched next to my map-in-dirt and studied it with an intensity that hurt something behind my ribs. The child remembered nothing — not their name before, not a mother's face, not what rain smelled like the first time. Whatever had emptied them had done a thorough job. And yet they learned like a dry field drinks: greedily, silently, everything going somewhere deep where I couldn't see it pool.

"Show me again," Wren said. "The gap."

So I did. I drew the whole route — the switchbacks above the drowned quarry, the ford where the bridge had rotted but the pilings still held a careful walker, the shepherd bothy with its door that only opened if you lifted instead of pushed, because everyone who lived here knew that and every stranger didn't. I named each place twice, once as it was and once as the old-timers called it, because names are rope bridges over forgetting and you want as many ropes as you can get.

Wren repeated every name back without a single mistake.

"Good," I said, and my voice came out rougher than I intended. "That's — good."

* * *

We walked the drowned quarry road at dusk because dusk was when the patrols changed shift, and because the light made the flooded pits look like bowls of hammered copper, and because I wanted Wren to see it while it was beautiful rather than merely useful. Priorities. A thief teaches survival; a parent teaches beauty. I was neither, but I was apparently something adjacent to both, and it sat on me like a coat that fit badly.

"Lesson four," I said. "Never walk a ridge line at any hour you can walk the shadow beside it. Skyline is silhouette. Silhouette is target."

"Why do you number the lessons?"

"So you can complain about them individually later. Lesson five: always complain. Complaints are free intelligence. If someone tells you to shut up and keep walking, you've learned something about them too."

Behind us Sera snorted. Progress.

We dropped into the shadow of the ridge, single file, my hand occasionally finding Wren's shoulder to steer them around loose scree. The child moved well — better than me at eight, certainly better than Sera, who descended slopes like a woman negotiating with an enemy she respected. Somewhere below, faint on the wind, came the sound I'd been listening for all day: iron on leather, a horse shifting weight, the particular cough of a man who'd been riding since dawn and hated his life.

Patrol. Two riders, maybe three, on the old toll road below the quarry. Going the wrong direction to have seen us, but close enough that a careless noise would travel.

I put two fingers on Wren's shoulder and pressed down. The child folded instantly, small and quiet as spilled sand, and something in my chest did a complicated thing I chose not to examine. We waited in the juniper while the riders passed below us — grey cloaks, white stoles, the new inquisition livery that hadn't existed a season ago. One of them carried a brazier on a pole, unlit, and I thought about what they burned shrines *with*, and decided not to think about it.

When the hoofbeats faded, Wren whispered, "Why are they looking for us? We haven't done anything."

Ah. There it was. The question every child asks eventually, and the one Thornfell had spent a season answering in fire.

"They're not looking for us specifically," I said. "They're looking for anyone. That's worse, so let's not dwell on it. Lesson six—"

"Don't dwell."

"See? Learning already."

Sera caught up to us, breathing hard, and for a moment the three of us stood in the blue dusk among the junipers, listening to a country pretend it had never existed. Then Sera said, quietly, "My order used to maintain this road. Before. Pilgrims came up from the coast to the high shrines every midsummer." Her jaw worked. "There were lamps. Every hundred paces, a lamp, lit by whoever passed, so the road was never dark."

"And now?"

"Now it's dark, and the clerks say there was never a road at all." She started walking. "Keep your lessons coming, thief. It seems the mountains are the only ones left keeping our records."

* * *

The stolen memory woke on the second night.

I need to explain what that means, and I'll be honest: I don't fully know. Memory-thieves carry taken goods the way mules carry salt — inert, heavy, indifferent. You wrap a memory in wax and will and it sits in its little packet until you sell it. That's the trade. That's been the trade since before I could hold a pick.

But the memory I'd lifted from the dying woman in chapter one of this disaster — the happiest day of her life, prayers that answered themselves — had stopped behaving like goods. First it grew heavy. Not metaphorically. I weighed it some nights out of professional paranoia, turning the wax packet in my palm, and it gained weight the way a sponge gains water. Then it started whispering.

Not words at first. More like the feeling of words, the shape of speech heard through a wall. On the second night of the road, camped in the shepherd's bothy with the door-lifted-properly shut against the drizzle, it got clearer.

"*Isandro.*"

I sat bolt upright by the cold hearth, knife out, heart slamming. Sera was asleep sitting up, as she did, back to the wall like a woman who expected walls to betray her. Wren was a small warm lump under both our cloaks, because the child had annexed my bedding on night one through the ancient unstoppable diplomacy of being eight and cold.

"*Isandro Nakamura.*"

The whisper came from inside my coat. From the pocket where the packet sat. My own name, spoken in a voice like a woman praying and something else answering — except now the something else was pronouncing *me*, slowly, tasting the syllables, the way you'd read a label on a jar before deciding what was inside.

I took the packet out. Held it at arm's length. It was warm. Wax doesn't get warm. Wax especially doesn't get warm in time with your pulse, which is what this was doing, and I want it noted that I am a professional and my hands did not shake until the third repetition of my name.

"Stop that," I whispered.

"*Isandro.*" Almost fond. Almost patient. "*You kept what isn't yours.*"

"I keep lots of things that aren't mine. It's called employment."

Silence. Then, softer, in the dying woman's voice now, gentle as a hand on a fevered brow: "*It remembers you. It is learning you. What is kept long enough becomes known, and what is known—"*

"—belongs to whoever knows it," I finished, because I'd heard that doctrine my whole life, carved over every shrine door in Thornfell. *Memory belongs to the gods who witness it.* I'd always assumed it was the usual priestly furniture, the kind of sentence that exists to make tithing feel noble.

Sera stirred. "What are you talking to?"

"Nothing. Go back to sleep."

She opened her eyes. In the dark they were very steady. "Isandro. What are you talking to."

I should have lied. Lying to Sera was becoming a habit I couldn't afford, mostly because she saw through them with the serene efficiency of a woman who'd spent decades hearing confessions. So I held up the packet, and I watched her face do the thing faces do when doctrine crawls off the page and sits in your lap.

"That's the memory," she breathed. "From the dying woman. The one you sold a copy of—" She crossed herself, an old reflex, then seemed embarrassed by it. "It spoke?"

"It knows my name."

"The god knows your name." Her voice went thin. "Ossa is waking, and it is reading its inventory, and you are *in the inventory*."

"Thank you, Sera, I had pieced together roughly that much."

"It grows heavier because it is being *claimed*. Every stolen thing Ossa reclaims, it first must learn. You are carrying a piece of a god's attention in your pocket, Isandro, and you have been treating it like contraband."

"Well, yes. Because it *is* contraband. That's the entire basis of my personality."

She stared at me for a long moment, and then — this is the part I'm still thinking about — she laughed. Quietly, exhaustedly, but genuinely, into her hand, shoulders shaking, the first laugh I'd heard from her since her shrine burned. "You are going to die of sarcasm," she said, "before any god gets near you."

"There are worse deaths. I've stolen several."

She lay back down. After a while, in the dark, she said: "Give it to me tomorrow. I'll ward it. Properly, with rites, not whatever wax-and-denial system you use."

And I said yes, because I was tired, and because her hands when she prayed had a steadiness mine lacked, and because — though I'd have bitten my tongue off before admitting it — it was easier to be afraid with someone else holding half the fear.

* * *

On the third morning I taught Wren the mountains properly.

Not the cynical curriculum. The real one. Which plants hold water in their roots. Which scree sings before it slides. How to read weather off the clouds stacking on the western peaks, and how to tell a shepherd's cairn from a grave cairn (count the stones; graves get odd numbers; the living are always even-handed). How to be quiet not like hiding but like belonging — the difference between a stranger in the woods and an animal in the woods.

Wren absorbed it all with that terrifying blank-page hunger, and somewhere along the morning I realized I wasn't teaching survival anymore. I was teaching inheritance. I was handing over everything I knew about a country that had been erased from every map, because somebody had to hold it, and the clerks weren't volunteering, and Sera's god was busy eating the archives, and this small empty-eyed child was the only vault I trusted.

"You're staring," Wren said, not looking up from the cairn they were counting.

"Just checking your work."

"Eleven stones. That's wrong. It's a grave."

"It's a grave." I crouched beside them. "Whoever it was, someone climbed up here to mark them. That's the whole point of cairns. Not the counting. The climbing." I picked up a twelfth stone and set it on top, and Wren, solemn as a magistrate, added a thirteenth. Odd numbers. Done properly.

"Did you bury people?" Wren asked. "Before?"

"Once or twice. Mostly I robbed them afterward, which sounds worse than it is. Dead men don't need rings."

"That's sad."

"It's practical."

"It's both," Wren said, with the terrible precision of children, and went on up the trail, and I stood there a moment with my heart doing that complicated unexamined thing again, and then followed.

* * *

We cleared the Shepherd's Gap at noon, and Thornfell opened below us like a book with half its pages torn out.

I don't know how else to describe it. From the height you could see the whole middle country — the river towns, the shrine roads, the smoke of Vetch far to the east — and threaded through it, everywhere, were absences. Fields that the eye slid off. Villages that were technically still standing but somehow not *there*, the way a word is not there after you've repeated it too long. The erasure wasn't just paperwork anymore. It was getting into the land itself.

"There," Sera said, pointing south, and I followed her finger to a smudge on the horizon that was either cloud or the inquisition burning another wayshrine, and honestly at this point the distinction felt academic.

"We'll skirt east," I said. "Through the Salt Flats. Kestrel country, unfortunately, but the riders won't follow onto the flats — horses sink."

"Kestrel." Sera said the name like spitting out a seed. "Your rival."

"My rival. My creditor. Possibly my killer, depending on his mood and my balance." I checked the sky, the sun, the distance to the flats. "If we move fast we reach the Salt Bridge by tomorrow night. If we move slow, we reach it eventually, colder and hungrier, and he'll still be there, because Kestrel is many things but he has never once failed to be somewhere inconvenient."

Wren tugged my sleeve. "Is he bad?"

"He's owed money and grief. That combination makes most people bad, temporarily." I ruffled the child's hair, an act of pure instinct that surprised us both. "Stay close to me tomorrow and let me do the talking."

"You always do the talking."

"Yes, well. It's my one marketable skill besides theft."

We started down. Behind us the Shepherd's Gap closed its stony mouth, and ahead the Salt Flats shimmered white and blinding, and in my pocket — warded now under Sera's rites, wrapped in prayer and wax and denial — the stolen memory shifted its weight and whispered my name one more time, soft as a lover, patient as debt.

And beneath it, fainter, from further away than a pocket should be able to reach, something vast turned over in its sleep and began, syllable by syllable, to learn the rest of my name — the parts even I no longer knew.
