## 7. The Rival's Toll

There are three ways to take a memory from a person. The kind way is with their permission and a fair price. The honest way is with a knife at their throat and no pretense. And then there's the Nakamura way, which is to smile at you across a card table for six hours while lifting the last twenty years out of your skull like coins from a drunk's pocket.

I've had seven years to think about that night in Vetch. Seven years to replay every hand of cards, every laugh Isandro Nakamura let slip at exactly the right moments, every time they touched my shoulder like we were old friends. Seven years to watch my brother Tam sit by the window of our rented room in Saltreach, hands folded in his lap, waiting — patiently, endlessly, the way a chair waits — for a life that wasn't coming back.

So when I heard that Nakamura was crossing the Salt Bridge with a priestess and a child, I did what any reasonable man would do.

I bought rope, waited three days in the cold, and planned a murder.

* * *

The Salt Bridge is the only crossing over the Greywash gorge that doesn't require you to sell your soul to a ferryman, and even it takes a toll. Two hundred feet of ancient stone slung between cliffs, slick with mineral crust, wide enough for two mules or one bad decision. The wind comes up the gorge like something with an opinion. On the day Nakamura crossed it, the wind was saying: *jump.*

I'd strung my rope low across the span, ankle height, hidden under the salt crust where the stone whitened. Simple. They'd trip, I'd come out of the rocks at the far end, and by the time anyone understood what was happening, I'd have a blade at the soft part of the world's most careful thief.

Except plans are just prayers you make to yourself, and mine were answered badly.

The child went first. Small, dark-haired, walking with the deliberate care of someone who has recently learned that the world contains edges. The priestess next — Sera, I knew her face from before the burning; every shrine-keeper in Thornfell had that same look now, like a woman carrying water in her cupped hands and refusing to notice it leaking. Then Nakamura.

Nakamura stepped over my rope without looking down.

I want to be clear about how insulting this was. Not jumped it. Not stumbled near it. Just lifted one boot and cleared it the way you'd step over a sleeping dog, mid-sentence, talking to the child about — I'd learn later — which clouds meant rain and which ones meant soldiers. My trap, three days of freezing on a cliffside, defeated by basic pedestrian competence.

Fine. I came out from behind the rocks anyway, because pride is also a toll, and I've always paid it gladly.

"Kestrel." Nakamura said my name the way you'd read it off a ledger. No surprise. Of course no surprise. They'd probably smelled me two miles back and crossed anyway, because making my ambush pointless was free entertainment. "You look terrible."

"You look rich," I said, and put the knife out where the wind could appreciate it. "That's a nice coat. Whose is it? I mean originally. Before you wore it home inside your head."

The priestess's hand went to whatever she kept under her robes. The child looked between us with that terrible blank openness children have, the kind that makes you feel like a stain on a window.

"Nakamura," Sera said. "You know this man?"

"Oh, we're old friends." Nakamura's voice did the thing it does — warm, wry, a door left open a crack so you lean toward it and forget it's a door. "Kestrel and I worked a job together once. Didn't we, Kestrel?"

"We did." I took a step forward. The bridge hummed under us. Below, the Greywash chewed its white foam against the rocks, patient as everything else in Thornfell these days. "Tell them about the job, Nakamura. Tell them about the Aldermont house. Tell them what you took."

Something moved behind Nakamura's eyes. Not guilt — I've seen guilt, it's cheap and common, half the smugglers on the roads wear it like a second coat. This was older. Inventory. Nakamura was checking a shelf somewhere deep inside to see if the item I wanted was still there.

"I don't remember the Aldermont house," Nakamura said quietly.

"No," I agreed. "You wouldn't. That's rather the point."

* * *

Here's what happened at the Aldermont house, since apparently I'm the only one who keeps records anymore.

The Aldermonts were old money, the kind that predates the Crown's interest in Thornfell, and old Mrs. Aldermont was dying the slow way, the way that lets a family fight over the estate for years while she watches from her chair. Her son hired me to lift certain memories out of certain relatives' heads — inconvenient deathbed promises, a will that favored the wrong grandchild, that sort of thing. Dirty work, but it paid, and I was young enough to think dirty work was just work.

I needed a partner for the extraction itself. Memory-work on that scale takes two: one to hold the subject steady in the trance, one to make the cut. I hired Nakamura off a recommendation from a fence in Vetch who owed me money. Big mistake number one. Trusting the fence's recommendation instead of checking the knife myself was mistake two. Mistake three was assuming that because we split the fee, we split the risk.

We did the job clean. Four extractions, one night, the whole rotten family lighter by the exact weight of their worst secrets. I held the trance on the grandmother myself — the hardest mind, the deepest vault — and when I came up out of it, sweating, Nakamura was already packing the satchel.

And my brother's mind was already gone.

Not from the job. From after. Because here's the thing nobody tells you about memory-thieving: the memories have weight, and weight wants somewhere to go, and if you're careless — if you're greedy — you don't store what you steal in crystal and vellum like a professional. You hold it in yourself, just for the night, just until the handoff. And a mind holding four strangers' worth of stolen past is a crowded house, and things get shuffled, and sometimes when you reach for your own memories you pull out someone else's, and sometimes —

Sometimes you leave the door open behind you, and something walks out.

Tam was our lookout that night. Twenty-two years old, sharp as a tack, could name every bird in the Greywash valley by its call. When I got back to our camp he was sitting by the fire, smiling at me, pleasant as morning. He didn't know my name. By dawn he didn't know his own. Whatever spilled out of Nakamura's overloaded head that night — whether it leaked through the link we'd used for the trance, whether some of the grandmother's hoarded years found a younger, emptier vessel nearby — it took him. Took everything. Left the body walking, eating, smiling politely at strangers.

The healers called it hollowing. The priests called it punishment. I called it what it was: collateral. Nakamura's greed, priced in my brother.

I hunted them for a year. Lost them. Hated them efficiently for another six. And then the world ended — the Crown struck Thornfell from the registries, the shrines started burning, the inquisition riders came with their ledgers and their torches — and hating became a luxury, and luxuries are the first thing a dead country confiscates.

But then, three weeks ago, a broker in Vetch — a man with too many rings and not enough fingers, if you follow me — let drop a piece of gossip he thought was worthless. He said the old gods were waking up fat and hungry, gorging on stolen memory, and that the supply chain ran through thieves. Through *particular* thieves. Through a certain Nakamura, whose sales had been traced upward, sale by sale, all the way to something with too many names.

And I thought: if Nakamura's stolen goods went up to the gods, maybe other things went up too. Maybe things that were never sold. Maybe things that were *lost* — spilled, dropped, left behind in a campfire's light by a thief carrying too much.

Maybe my brother's mind is in heaven, and heaven can be robbed.

I'm a thief. It's the only prayer I know how to say.

* * *

"So here's how this goes," I told them, there on the bridge, wind trying to shove us all into the gorge together like a drunk uncle at a wedding. "You're going to give me back what you owe, or you're going to go over the edge, and I'm going to search your pockets on the way down."

"That's not how pockets work," Nakamura said.

"It's how they'll work today."

Sera had her hand fully around whatever she carried now, and there was a light under her knuckles that I didn't love. The child had moved — smartly, I noted — to stand behind Nakamura, watching me with those empty eyes, and I'll confess something shameful: looking at Wren was like looking at Tam. Not the face. The *absence*. The polite waiting. Something in my chest turned over like a boat taking water.

"The Aldermont memories are long gone," Nakamura said. "Sold, spent, scattered. If I still had them, do you think I'd be walking erased roads with a priestess and a child? I own nothing, Kestrel. That's the entire problem."

"Liar's comfort."

"It's the truth's comfort. It's uglier." Nakamura spread their hands, and I noticed — professional habit — that they were empty, and that their weight sat balanced on the balls of their feet, and that they'd positioned themselves between me and the child without appearing to move. Old habits. Even now, even hollowed-out, Nakamura protected things. It made me angrier. It's easier to kill a man who's never stood in front of anyone. "Ask your question properly, Kestrel. The real one. The one you climbed down a cliff in the cold to ask."

The wind filled the silence. Somewhere below, water worked at stone.

"What makes you think there's a real one?"

"Because if you only wanted me dead, I'd be dead. You've been behind us since the ferry at Marrow Ford. Three days. You had a hundred chances to put a bolt in my back on open ground, and instead you built a rope trap on a bridge, which means you wanted me stopped, not finished. Stopped means conversation. Conversation means you need something." Nakamura tilted their head. "What did you lose?"

And there it was. Seven years of rehearsed hatred, and the bastard disarmed it by being good at their job.

I should have lied. A good thief lies reflexively, the way other people breathe. But I was tired, and cold, and I'd spent three days watching a child walk carefully along cliff edges, and something in me had gone soft as thawing ground.

"My brother," I said. "Your spill took my brother. The night of the Aldermont job. Your head was too full and it — leaked. Onto him. He's been hollow ever since. Twenty-two years old and he waits by windows for a life that isn't coming." I heard my own voice go flat, which is what it does instead of breaking. "And now I hear the gods are eating stolen memory. Everything sold upward. Everything lost. So either you tell me where my brother's mind went, or I start taking pieces of you until you remember."

Nakamura went very still. People think stillness means shock. In thieves, it usually means recalculation — the fast, silent arithmetic of a life re-weighted.

"I didn't know," Nakamura said finally. "About the spill. About Tam." A pause. "What was his name again?"

"Tam."

"Tam." They said it like they were setting it down somewhere safe. "I didn't know. I swear it on — " They stopped. Smiled, thin and bitter. "I don't have anything to swear on anymore. But I didn't know."

"Then know this now. The broker in Vetch says the gods' larder is full. Sold memories, yes, but also the loose stuff. The leakage. The spillage. Things that fell off carts like yours." I stepped closer. The bridge swayed. "If my brother is in that larder, I'm going in after him. And you're going to show me the door, Nakamura, because you built the cart."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the priestess gets her wish and I get my justice, and neither of us enjoys it."

Sera spoke for the first time in minutes, and her voice had that quality shrine-keepers get when the god goes quiet — iron wrapped in velvet wrapped in grief. "He's telling the truth about the larder. I've felt it. My god's silence has a shape, and the shape is *emptied*. If Kestrel's brother's mind was taken upward, then it sits beside what was taken from my faith." She looked at me, and I saw her decide something she hated deciding. "We could use a knife."

"I could use a reason," I said. "Yours is better than most."

* * *

We stood there on the Salt Bridge for a long moment, four people and a gorge, negotiating the terms of a country that no longer officially existed. I want it recorded that I did not soften. I did not feel the warmth of fellowship. What I felt was arithmetic: Nakamura knew the trade routes and the brokers' names; the priestess knew the old rites and, presumably, how to talk to things that shouldn't be talked to; the child was a mystery I hadn't solved yet but suspected mattered enormously, because Nakamura — who abandoned everyone, who traveled alone by doctrine — was walking at the pace of small legs and shielding small shoulders.

People reveal themselves by what slows them down. Nakamura had slowed down for a child. That was data. Data is better than trust. Trust is just data you haven't checked yet.

"One condition," I said.

"Of course there's a condition."

"When we find this larder — this divine warehouse of stolen years — my brother comes first. Before your god, priestess. Before your redemption, Nakamura. Before whatever the child is. Tam first, or I burn the whole arrangement down and everyone walks away poorer."

"Tam first," Nakamura repeated. And then, quieter, in that voice that had talked me across a card table seven years ago: "For what it's worth — and I know what it's worth, which is nothing — I'm sorry. I didn't know what I was carrying cost so much to carry."

"It's worth nothing," I agreed. "It's also the first time anyone's said it. So. Fine."

I cut my rope down and coiled it, because a thief travels light and grudges travel lighter, and fell into step behind them as the bridge delivered us to the southern cliff. The child — Wren, I'd learn, though the name seemed borrowed, like everything else in this company — fell in beside me without fear, which was either wisdom or the absence of anything to be wise with.

"You're angry," Wren observed, after a mile.

"Yes."

"At the tall one."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because they broke something of mine and don't remember doing it."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Do you remember everything you've broken?"

I opened my mouth to say *of course*, and then seven years of Aldermont ghosts filed past — the cousins stripped bare, the grandmother's dying wishes extracted and sold, the son who'd hired me and gotten exactly the filthy service he'd paid for, and every one of them walking around lighter and worse, and me never once losing sleep, because sleep is for people whose ledgers balance.

"No," I said.

"Then maybe," Wren said, with the terrifying simplicity of the empty, "you're angry at the wrong remembering."

I didn't answer. Ahead of us, Nakamura glanced back — once, quick, checking on the child, finding the child beside *me*, and frowning slightly at that, as if I were weather moving in.

By dusk we made camp in the ruins of a wayshrine, one of the few the inquisition hadn't reached yet, its little god long fled or long eaten, the alcove above the altar holding nothing but a bird's nest and old wax. Sera lit a fire with hands that shook and steadied. Nakamura shared out food with the mechanical fairness of someone settling accounts. Wren slept between all of us like a coin in a purse.

And I sat up on watch, knife across my knees, looking south toward Vetch and the broker with too many rings, and I let myself do the thing I'd forbidden myself for seven years.

I hoped.

Just a little. Just the way you test ice with one foot. Because if the gods were real, and they were full, and full things can be emptied — then somewhere in that vast divine pantry, a twenty-two-year-old boy was still waiting by a window. And I had never robbed anything worth robbing before.

Above the dead altar, the wind moved through the empty alcove, and for half a heartbeat I would have sworn it made a sound like a birdcall — the grey shrike, specifically, the one Tam could name in his sleep, back when he had a sleep worth naming things in.

I decided not to have heard it.

Hope is a toll too. I'd pay it. But I'd pay it armed.
