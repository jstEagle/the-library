## 8. Prayers Answered Badly

I have prayed in better places than this.

I have prayed in the marble hush of the Sanctum of Quiet Hours, where the incense is worth more per ounce than a smuggler's horse and the silence is so expensive it comes with an attendant to guard it. I have prayed in a ditch during a hailstorm, which was less dignified but considerably more honest. And I have prayed, once, memorably, in the bedchamber of a man I was supposed to be absolving and instead was robbing — but that was before my disgrace, and the less said about it the better for everyone.

This wayshrine, though. This wayshrine is a new low even by my standards.

It stands at the crook of a mountain pass where the road forgets itself — one of those roads that officially no longer exists, since the Crown struck Thornfell from every registry like a scribe blotting out a misspelled word. The shrine is older than the registry, older than the Crown, older, if you believe the carvings, than most of what we currently agree to call the world. A ring of standing stones worn smooth as river pebbles, a basin of black water that never freezes, and above it all a carving of a face so eroded by wind that it has become no face at all, which is, I suppose, the most accurate portrait of my god anyone has ever managed.

My god. Listen to me. Six months ago I would have said *the* god, capital G, singular, with a full liturgical apparatus behind the pronoun. Now it's *my* god, the way you'd say *my* fever or *my* bad knee. Something that clings to you and won't leave and gives you nothing but trouble.

Behind me, Isandro Nakamura was doing what Isandro Nakamura always does: leaning against a stone with their arms crossed, radiating the particular boredom of a person who thinks prayer is a scam they haven't figured out how to monetize.

"Let me guess," they said. "You're going to talk at the rocks."

"I'm going to pray."

"Talk. At the rocks." They tipped their head back to look up at the faceless carving. "You know what I stole out of a dying woman's head three weeks ago? Her happiest memory. And do you know what it did? It prayed back at me. Whole prayers, answered before they were finished asking. So forgive me if I've developed some professional skepticism about the practice."

"That memory came from this country," I said. "From someone who grew up under these stones. Of course it prayed. Prayer is load-bearing here. It's not decoration, Nakamura. It's plumbing."

"Charming metaphor."

"I was a priestess. We're trained in metaphor. It's cheaper than miracles."

Wren, who had been crouched at the edge of the black basin making faces at their own reflection, looked up. The child remembers nothing — nothing, not their name's origin, not their mother's face, not whether they like the taste of apples until they try one and report back with the gravity of a scholar — and yet they always seem to know when adults are about to be stupid.

"You're going to ask it for something," Wren said. It wasn't a question.

"I'm going to pray," I said again, with the stubbornness of a woman who has very little left to be stubborn with.

"And it's going to answer," Wren said.

And there it is. The horror of children: they see the shape of a thing before the adults finish arranging their excuses around it.

Kestrel said nothing. Kestrel rarely says anything that isn't a threat or an invoice. He stood apart from us with his back to the wind, watching the pass the way a man watches a wound he doesn't trust to stay closed, and he let his silence say that whatever happened next was my funeral and he intended to attend only as a witness.

I turned back to the stones. I knelt on ground frozen hard enough to bruise. And I did the thing I had sworn off doing in the smoking ruin of my shrine, the thing I had told myself was finished, the thing that had cost me my vows, my order, and every friend I had who hadn't already been murdered by an inquisitor:

I prayed.

Not the sanctioned prayers. Those are formalities, call-and-response, the spiritual equivalent of signing a ledger. I mean real prayer, the naked kind, the kind you only do when everything else has failed, the kind that isn't words so much as a hand reaching into the dark and hoping something on the other side doesn't bite it.

*I don't need much,* I said. *I've stopped asking for much. Just this: show me how to stop them. Show me what Thornfell will be if the old gods wake whole. Let me see it plainly, so I can stand against it. Give me clarity.*

There is a rule in my former profession, taught to novices in their first year, usually by a weary elder over cheap wine: *never pray a question to a hungry thing.* Questions are doors. Doors swing both ways.

I knew the rule. I prayed anyway. Desperation does that; it makes theologians of us all, and terrible ones.

The water in the basin went still.

I don't mean calm. I mean *still*, stillness like held breath, stillness like a room where someone has just stopped moving because they heard their name. The reflections vanished from it — no stones, no sky, no faceless god, no kneeling fool in a stolen cloak. Just black, deep, and attentive.

"Oh," I heard myself say. "Oh, that's not good."

The water showed me Thornfell.

* * *

Here is what I saw first, and I want it on the record that I saw the beautiful version first, because whoever — whatever — answered my prayer understood precisely how to bait a hook.

Thornfell whole. Thornfell remembered. Every erased road unblotted, every burned shrine rebuilt in timber and gold leaf, every name the registries devoured spoken aloud again in the streets. I saw harvests that came in fat and early because the land remembered every season it had ever survived. I saw the plague years healed — not forgotten, *healed* — because a people who remember everything can learn from anything. I saw my own shrine standing again, whitewashed, singing, and I saw myself young inside it, before the disgrace, before the smoke, and I wept in the vision like a woman seeing her own grave filled back in.

That was the bait. That was the honey on the blade. Any priestess would kneel down her whole life for that vision. I nearly did. My knees actually started to go, right there on the frozen ground, and it took Isandro's hand on my shoulder — the memory-thief, of all people, the last person with any right to touch a priestess — to hold me above the water.

"Don't," they said quietly. "Look closer. You're good at that."

So I looked closer. That is, God forgive me, the one skill they never managed to burn out of me.

The harvests came in fat because the farmers had no other seasons left in them. A man who remembers only the harvest cannot imagine famine, and a man who cannot imagine famine plants carelessly, and a land tended carelessly dies — but the gods kept feeding the vision forward, season after golden season, and the people smiled wider and thought less, and their faces began to smooth the way the god's carved face had smoothed, features softening into contentment, contentment into vacancy. Memory is work. The gods were offering to do the work forever. All the people had to give up was having any of their own.

I saw the plague years healed and realized with a lurch that the healers remembered no disease but the one the gods chose, that medicine was becoming liturgy, that a child born in that shining Thornfell would grow up knowing exactly what she was permitted to know and would love it, would *kneel* for it, would report her mother to the temple for telling her a story the calendar didn't include.

I saw my shrine standing again, and I saw what was sung in it: not hymns. Recitations. The past, read aloud by priests who had none of their own, in a tongue that was really just obedience with grammar. And I saw myself, young and white-cowled and radiant, and I understood finally that the vision wasn't showing me a reward. It was showing me a *position*. There was a place reserved in the waking world for useful faithful. The gods had done inventory. They knew what I was for.

"Enough," I whispered.

The vision leaned closer. That's the only way I can describe it — the black water tilted toward me like a listener, like a suitor, like something enormous bending down over something small, and the voice that came out of the basin didn't sound so much as *arrived*, the way cold arrives through a cracked window.

**YOU ASKED FOR CLARITY,** it said. **WE ARE CLARITY. WE ARE EVERYTHING REMEMBERED, AND SO NOTHING IS EVER LOST AGAIN, AND SO NOTHING CAN EVER CHANGE AGAIN. THIS IS THE GIFT.**

"That's a cage," I said. My voice came out steadier than I felt, which is the single useful thing my training ever gave me. "A cage with gilding is still a cage."

**THE CAGED THING IS THE ONE THAT SUFFERS. THE CAGE ITSELF DOES NOT. WE OFFER TO BE THE CAGE FOR YOU. YOU WILL NEVER HAVE TO HOLD YOUR PAST AGAIN. YOU WILL ONLY HAVE TO KNEEL INSIDE IT.**

And then — because I had asked to see what Thornfell would become, and it is a literal-minded thing, a horribly, perfectly literal-minded thing — it showed me the rest. Not the golden version. The version underneath, where the gilding flakes.

I saw villages where every elder sat in perfect peace because they could remember nothing except what the gods recirculated to them, the same summer, the same wedding, the same funeral, cycling through their heads like water through a mill. I saw a woman laugh at her husband's joke for the fortieth time and mean it every time, because for her it was always the first time, and beside her the husband's eyes were wet because he alone, punished for some private doubt, remembered all forty.

I saw the inquisition — our inquisition, the resurrected riders burning shrines across the passes — kneeling in rows, emptied, their orders remembered *for* them, handed to them fresh each dawn by something divine, soldiers who no longer needed to believe in their cause because belief had been issued to them wholesale.

I saw Wren.

I want to be precise here, because this is the part that curdled me, and I am a woman who has watched her life's work burn and described the color of the smoke accurately afterward. In the vision, Wren stood in the center of a restored Thornfell, in a plaza that gleamed, and the child was *full*. Filled to the brim with everyone else's poured-off memories — the sins, the shames, the griefs, the whole sediment of a nation decanted into the one vessel that had come empty. And Wren's face was serene. Serene the way the drowned are serene. And around the child, ten thousand people knelt and gave thanks that someone else now carried what they refused to.

They were going to make that child into a god. Or into a meal. With things like this, in my experience, the distinction is academic.

I tore my eyes away — physically tore them, it felt like pulling roots out of stone — and the vision snapped shut, and the basin was just water again, black and shallow and innocent as a lie told well.

* * *

For a long moment nobody spoke. The wind moved through the pass. Somewhere downslope a crow remarked on the state of the world, unfavorably, as crows do.

Wren broke the silence, because of course Wren did.

"It showed me to you," the child said. "Why would it show me to you?"

"To frighten me."

"Did it?"

I looked at the child. Eight years old, empty as a clean page, holding a heel of bread they'd been saving for Isandro without being asked. "Yes," I said. "But not the way it wanted."

Isandro pushed off the stone. For once they weren't smirking. "So now we know," they said. "They don't want worship. Worship's just the collection plate. What they want is the remembering. All of it. Centralized." Their mouth twisted. "You know what that is? That's a vault. Somebody built these gods a vault and told them it was heaven."

"You'd know," Kestrel said, from his post by the pass. His voice had the flatness of a blade laid flat on a table. "You fed them enough."

"I fed a broker," Isandro said. "I didn't know where the chain went up. Neither did you, when you sold yours."

Kestrel's jaw worked. He didn't deny it, which from Kestrel is a kind of confession, and we both let it lie there between us like a coin neither wants to pick up.

I got to my feet. My knees ached. My hands wouldn't entirely stop shaking, and I made no effort to hide it, because I have learned that fear concealed becomes fear believed, and fear shown is just weather.

"Here is what I know now," I said. "I spent six months thinking my god had abandoned Thornfell. Silent, absent, dead — I mourned it. I was wrong. It wasn't silent because it left. It was silent because it was being *eaten*, piece by piece, sale by sale, and everything it had — every memory of every soul that ever knelt on this soil — has been pooled somewhere by a broker who thinks divinity is an inventory problem." I looked at each of them in turn, even Kestrel. Especially Kestrel. "These things aren't waking. They're being *assembled*. And what they're being assembled into showed me its face today and offered me a job in it."

"The child," Wren said quietly. "It needs an empty vessel. It showed you because it thinks you'll trade me."

"It thinks wrong," I said, faster than I meant to, harder than I meant to.

Something flickered across Isandro's face at that — surprise, maybe, or the beginning of a different arithmetic — but I didn't stop to read it.

"For years I prayed for guidance and heard nothing, and I thought the silence was punishment," I went on. "Now I've heard the answer, and I understand the silence was mercy. A god that answers literally is a god you should starve. So here is my resolution, and you can mock it if you like, Nakamura, you especially: I renounce nothing further tonight, because I already lost it all. But I swear something new, on these stones, in front of witnesses, smugglers and thieves and a child who will actually remember this, which is more than I can say for most congregations I've served."

The wind held its breath. Even the crow shut up.

"What Thornfell becomes is going to be decided by who holds its memories. Not the Crown, not the registry, not the riders, not the broker, and not the things in the dark drinking them down. By us. By the living. Memory belongs to the living — to be given, not taken, not hoarded, not poured into any hungry mouth that asks nicely. And I will walk to whatever door they're hiding at the end of this country, and I will see it shut, and if the price of shutting it is everything the church ever taught me was holy—" I laughed, and it came out ragged, "—then the church and I are finally agreed on something, because I've already paid that once."

Silence. Then Wren crossed the frozen ground, took the saved heel of bread, and put it solemnly in my hand, which from Wren is a sacrament, and I received it as one.

"Well," said Isandro, after a moment. "That was almost a sermon. You're lucky I don't charge for those." But their eyes had gone to the black basin, and stayed there, and their hand had drifted — unconsciously, I'd have sworn it was unconscious — to the inner pocket where they keep the stolen memory that whispers their name.

"We move at first light," Kestrel said, turning back to watch the pass. "And priestess—"

"Yes?"

"Next time you want to know what's coming for us?" He didn't look around. "Ask me. I bill less, and I lie less."

We made camp in the lee of the stones, beneath the faceless carving, and I took the last watch, and I did not pray.

But as the stars wheeled over the pass, I noticed two things, and I will set them down here because a priestess keeps records even after her church burns.

The first: the water in the basin had begun, very slowly, to freeze — from the outside in, as though something at its center had withdrawn, satisfied, digesting what I'd shown it wanted to see.

The second: far down the valley, along a road that appears on no map because there are no maps anymore, torchlight was climbing. Many torches. Moving in formation.

Riders don't travel in formation unless they're looking for someone.

And we were the only souls on the road.
