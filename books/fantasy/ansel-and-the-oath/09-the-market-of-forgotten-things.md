## 9. The Market of Forgotten Things

Vetch smelled the way it always did: like coin left too long in a dead man's pocket. Salt and tallow and something sweeter underneath — burnt sugar, maybe, or the memory of burnt sugar, which in Vetch was often the same thing.

I'd been coming here since I was old enough to lie convincingly, and I still hated it. That's the thing about black markets. People imagine glamour. Velvet tents, hooded brokers, exotic goods on silver trays. What you actually get is a fish market that also sells your grandmother's last summer, and the fish is fresher.

"Stay close," Sera murmured at my shoulder. She had her hood up and her hands folded like she was attending her own funeral, which, given the state of her faith lately, wasn't far off. "This place feels wrong."

"It is wrong. That's why we're here." I adjusted my pack strap. Wren clung to my other sleeve with the grip of a child who had learned, recently and thoroughly, what happened to children who let go. Kestrel brought up the rear, arms crossed, radiating the particular resentment of a man doing me a favor he refused to admit was a favor.

The Market of Forgotten Things opened at dusk, because memories keep better in the dark. Stalls unfolded from carts, awnings bloomed between leaning buildings, and by full dark the whole crooked street of Saltrow was lit with lanterns dyed the color of bruises. Vendors called out their wares the way fishwives call out herring.

"First kisses! Gently used! A wedding day, complete weather!"

"Grief, bottled, vintage of the Border Wars — drink it and understand your enemy!"

"A childhood! Any childhood! We have orphans' and we have princes', all authentic, none returned!"

Wren stopped walking. I felt the small hand tighten on my sleeve before I heard the small voice.

"Is that where memories go?"

I could have lied. Lying to Wren was easy; lying to someone who has no past is like cheating at cards against a player who can't see the deck. But Sera was watching me, and Kestrel was watching me, and there are only so many hours in a day you can spend being the person you've decided to be instead of the person you are.

"That's where some of them go," I said. "The ones people sell. Or the ones people take."

"Did you ever sell one here?"

The lantern light did me a favor and made my face hard to read. "Once or twice."

It had been more than once or twice. And that was the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the wayshrine, since Sera's god went silent, since the whispering memory in my pocket started saying my name in a voice I almost recognized. Every stolen memory I'd ever sold had gone somewhere. Memories don't evaporate. They get bought. And somebody, somewhere up the chain, had been buying enough of them to feed gods.

* * *

The broker I needed was named Marrow-of-the-Lot, which was not his name but his function, the way a hook is called a hook. He traded in bulk. Single memories were trinkets; Marrow dealt in cargoes. If anyone in Vetch knew where a decade of Thornfell's stolen past had flowed, it was him.

Getting an audience required a token, and the token required a theft, so I spent the first hour of the night lifting a jar of preserved longing off a dealer who deserved it — he watered his stock with cheap sentiment, a crime against craft if nothing else. The jar got me past Marrow's curtain, into a back room lit by a single lamp and lined with shelves of labeled glass.

Marrow himself sat behind a desk made of a ship's hatch. He was small, neat, and bald as an egg, with the patient eyes of a man who had watched ten thousand people realize what they'd sold.

"Nakamura." He said it without surprise. In Vetch, surprise is a tax most traders refuse to pay. "I wondered when you'd come crawling up your own supply chain."

"You know why I'm here."

"I know why everyone's here. Everyone wants either to buy back what they sold or to find out who's buying." He laced his fingers. "Which are you?"

"Neither." I set the jar of longing on his desk as payment and kept my voice level. "I want the chain. My sales, the last three years. Where they went after you."

He looked at the jar, then at me, then — briefly, curiously — at Sera, whose posture under the hood had gone very still, the stillness of a woman praying or preparing to hit someone.

"You understand what you're asking," Marrow said. "Chains are the only thing in this market more valuable than product. You're asking me to burn a supplier relationship."

"I'm asking you to name a price."

"And if the price is something you don't want to pay?" His smile was small and dry. "You're a thief, Nakamura. You know better than anyone that everything in this city is for sale except the things that matter, and those go cheapest, because nobody bids."

"Just tell me the price."

"Information about yourself." He said it lightly, the way a surgeon says *this might sting*. "A trade. You give me one true answer for every link in the chain I give you. True answers only. I'll know."

Sera shifted beside me. "What kind of question—"

"The useful kind." Marrow hadn't taken his eyes off me. "Well? You came here to trace your own work. Tracing means looking. Looking means finding. Are you sure you want to find?"

Here's the thing about guilt: it's patient. It doesn't chase you. It sits down across the table and waits for you to arrive, and when you finally do, it offers you tea and asks how you've been, and the worst part is that it genuinely wants to know.

"One question per link," I said. "Ask."

* * *

The first link cost me the truth that I'd sold the dying woman's happiest memory within a week of taking it, and that I'd told myself she wouldn't miss it because she was dying anyway. Marrow wrote that down. I didn't ask what for.

The second link cost me the admission that I kept a ledger — every job, every sale, dates and prices — because some part of me had always known there would be an audit someday. He seemed to enjoy that one.

The third link was where the chain turned upward.

"Your cargo moved twice," Marrow said, consulting nothing, because men like Marrow keep their ledgers behind their eyes. "First to a consolidator in Greyharbor. Then north. Then—" he paused, savoring it, the vulture, "—then it stopped being cargo at all. Do you know what happens to a memory when it's eaten by something older than markets?"

"No," I said. "Tell me."

"It stops being a memory. It becomes material. Mortar. The bricks of a waking thing." He leaned forward. "There's a buyer. There has been for two years now. Buys in volume, pays in old coin, never bargains. Wears a mask of white porcelain, no mouth painted on it. Calls itself the Custodian. And everything it buys goes up the mountain road to the shrines — the old shrines, the ones your priestess's church abandoned, the ones that are starting to *answer prayers* again."

The room got very quiet. Even the lamp flame seemed to lean in.

"Why," Sera said. Her voice came out flat and dangerous. "Why would a broker feed the gods?"

Marrow spread his hands. "Because whoever controls what a nation remembers controls what a nation is. You can't erase a country with soldiers alone — people remember around armies, they sew the holes shut with stories. But starve them of their own past? Sell it off piece by piece until no one can agree on what happened? Then pour the collected past into something hungry and divine, something that will hold it all, hoard it, *own* it—" he shrugged, egg-bald and serene, "—and a country becomes a blank page. Blank pages get rewritten. Ask the Crown. They struck Thornfell from the registries the same season the Custodian started buying."

I thought about arguing. I thought about telling him he was wrong, that it was coincidence, that the world's ordinary rules had cracked but surely not *that* badly. But I had a ledger in my own head, three years of entries, and I could do the arithmetic myself. Every winter I'd sold heavier. Every spring the shrines had gotten quieter. I'd thought it was the church failing. It was the church being *eaten*, and I'd been holding the ladle.

"How much," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "How much of it was mine?"

Marrow tilted his head. "That's another question. That costs another answer."

"What's the question?"

He smiled. "No. The *answer* costs another answer. The question is free: do you actually want to know?"

* * *

We stood outside the curtain afterward in the bruise-colored lantern light, and none of us said anything for a while. Wren had fallen asleep against Kestrel's side somewhere during the negotiation, which said something about either the child's trust or Kestrel's unexpected talent for standing still.

"So," Kestrel said finally. "Your stolen goods fed the gods. Congratulations. You're not just a thief, you're a *supplier*."

"Kestrel," Sera said.

"No, let me finish. Because I want to be clear about the shape of this." His voice was low and even, which from Kestrel was worse than shouting. "My brother's mind went through that same chain. Through people like you, selling to people like Marrow, up to whatever wears the mouthless mask. For years I blamed thieves in general. It turns out I've been traveling with the specific one."

"I didn't know."

"Nobody ever knows. That's the design, isn't it? Keep the supply chain long enough and everyone's hands stay clean-ish." He adjusted Wren's weight on his hip, gentler than his words. "I'm not leaving. I want you to know that too. I'm just done pretending you're not part of the machine."

"That's fair," I said.

"It's not fair. It's just true."

Sera waited until he'd walked a few paces ahead with the sleeping child before she spoke, quietly, under the noise of the market. "Do you believe him? About the Custodian?"

"I believe the mask. Mouthless brokers are a real type — they deal in things that shouldn't be spoken aloud, so they stop speaking altogether. Superstition becomes costume becomes habit." I rubbed my eyes. "And I believe the volume. Two years of heavy buying, all flowing north. Someone's building something out of us, Sera. Out of *me*."

"Then we go north."

"We go north." I laughed, and it came out wrong, hollow as a stolen house. "You understand what I did? I spent fifteen years surviving by selling pieces of other people's lives, and it turns out I was kneading dough. Every coin I ate, every winter I made it through — the gods ate first. My survival fed the catastrophe. Literally. I'm the breadwinner of the apocalypse."

Sera looked at me for a long moment. Under the hood, her face was tired and fierce and, horribly, not contemptuous. That was the worst part. Contempt I could have carried.

"When I was a novice," she said, "an old priest told me that guilt is just grief that hasn't found its work yet. Find the work, Nakamura. Grief makes terrible company but excellent fuel."

"And if the work is impossible?"

"Then you do it anyway, and you complain the entire time, and at the end you're allowed to say you told us so." She pulled her hood lower and started after Kestrel. "Come on. The Custodian buys north of here. There's a caravan leaving at dawn."

I stood a moment longer in the lantern light, listening to the vendors call out their wares. *A childhood! Any childhood!* Somewhere in those jars and bottles and folded cloths was a decade of Thornfell, and somewhere north of Thornfell it was all piled together, brick on brick, building a god.

And somewhere in that pile — I knew it the way you know a scar under new skin — was a room I couldn't remember. A door I couldn't picture. A reason I'd fled this country as a child that I had sold so completely, so long ago, that even the wanting to know had been sold with it.

The Custodian had my past on its shelf.

I meant to take it back.

* * *

We found the caravan master at dawn, a leathery woman named Osk who charged triple for silence and quadruple for questions-not-asked, and by the time the sun cleared the salt flats we were rolling north in a cart of smuggled dye-stone, Wren asleep again between Sera and me, Kestrel sharpening a knife he never seemed to use on anything.

Halfway through the morning, Wren woke, looked at me with those clear, unburdened eyes, and asked the question children ask and adults spend lifetimes dodging.

"If the bad man in the mask has everyone's memories," Wren said, "does that mean nobody owns them anymore? Not even themselves?"

I opened my mouth to give the comforting adult non-answer.

And from the bottom of my pack, muffled and patient, the stolen memory whispered my name — and this time, unmistakably, it whispered it in *my own child's voice*.
