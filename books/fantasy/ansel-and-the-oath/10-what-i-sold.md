## 10. What I Sold

The broker's cellar smelled of tallow and old paper and something sweeter underneath — the smell of memories kept too long in jars. I know that smell the way a drunk knows wine. I've carried it on my own hands for years.

He was tied to his own chair with his own silk, which I felt was poetic, and Kestrel stood behind him sharpening a knife he had no intention of using yet, which I felt was theater. Sera sat across from the man with her hands folded like a woman at prayer, which was worse than either.

"His name is Corvane," she said. "Or that's what Vetch knows him as."

"It's what Vetch knows me as," the broker agreed. His voice was pleasant. That was the unsettling part. Men who traffic in stolen childhoods should sound like grave-dirt and rust. This one sounded like a host offering you a second cup of tea. "Names are inventory too, priestess. Everything is. You'd be amazed what people sell when they're hungry enough. Or frightened enough." His eyes found mine over the gag of his own cravat. "You'd know."

"Untie his mouth," I said, "and I'll consider being amazed."

Kestrel cut the silk. The broker worked his jaw, winced — I'd tied it tighter than strictly necessary — and smiled at me like we were old friends meeting at a funeral.

"Nakamura," he said. "Isandro Nakamura. Do you know how many ledgers I keep? Eleven countries' worth. And yours is the only one where the seller's name made me laugh."

"Glad to entertain."

"You sold me forty-one memories over six years. Forty-one. Do you understand what a *catalogue* that is? Most thieves bring me scraps — a first kiss, a dead mother's voice, the taste of a festival gone wrong. You brought me *seasons*. Whole years, intact, with their weather." He shook his head with genuine professional admiration. "I've bought from kings' confessors who brought less than you did. And you never once asked what they were for."

"I asked once," I said. "Year one. You told me a collector."

"A collector." He tasted the word. "Yes. Let's call it that."

Sera leaned forward. The candlelight did something unkind to her face, hollowing her into the shape she'd been before her shrine burned — all bone and conviction. "The gods," she said. "You've been feeding them."

The broker looked at her the way a man looks at weather he'd been promised would hold. "Priestess of the Silent God. I wondered when you'd come. I hoped it wouldn't be with the knife-man, but here we are." He flexed his bound wrists. "Yes. The gods. Though 'feeding' is such a generous word. I prefer *curating*."

"Curating," Kestrel repeated flatly, from behind him. The knife stopped moving. That was somehow louder.

"A nation is only its memory," the broker said, and now the pleasantness dropped away like a mask slipping off sweat, and what was under it was older and much more patient. "Thornfell remembers rebellions. Thornfell remembers shrines. Thornfell remembers that it has *gods*, and gods are terribly inconvenient for anyone who wants a country quiet. So you starve them. You take their worship apart piece by piece — not the temples, those burn easily enough, but the *remembering*. Every prayer is a memory of needing something. Take the need, take the god." He shrugged inside his bonds. "The Crown struck Thornfell from the registries because a country that can't remember itself can't object to anything. And the old gods wake starving, and a starving god will eat whatever remembering is left lying around. It's elegant. I'm rather proud of it."

"You're proud," Sera said, "of making my god go silent."

"I'm proud of *economics*, priestess. Your god went silent because your church sold its archive to pay a war debt eighty years ago, and the archive passed through nine hands before it reached me, and by then it was just — product." He turned back to me. "Ask him. Ask your thief what he thought he was selling."

"I know what I sold," I said. "Memories. People's. Willingly traded, mostly. I don't take what isn't given." Even saying it, I heard the hollowness under the words, the way you hear the hollow under a floorboard when you're the one standing on it. Six years of telling myself the trade was clean because the coin flowed downhill to the desperate instead of up to me.

"Willingly," the broker said softly. "Mostly. And the rest?"

"The rest were mine."

The cellar went quiet. Tallow dripped somewhere. Wren was upstairs with the Vetch fence-woman who owed me, learning to play knucklebones badly, and I was suddenly, fiercely glad the child wasn't hearing this.

"Yours," Sera repeated.

"I sold my own years when the work was thin. Everyone does, eventually. The good thieves last longest because we have the most to sell." I kept my voice level, which took more effort than any lockpick ever had. "A winter here. A bad year there. Things I didn't want anyway."

"And what did you not want?" the broker asked, gentle as a surgeon. "Tell them what you told me, the night you sold the lot. The big one. The sealed one. You came in half-frozen, seventeen years old if you were that, with a memory wrapped in oilcloth and your hands shaking so hard I made you sit down. And you said—"

"Don't."

"—you said: *take all of it. Take everything before I was twelve. I don't care what it costs. I just want to stop running.*"

The candle flame bent. Or I did. Hard to say, from the inside.

"That's not true," I said.

"It's in my ledger. Verbatim. I have an excellent memory, Nakamura. It's the whole of my stock-in-trade. Unlike some people in this room." He smiled again, and this time there was nothing of the host in it. "You sold me your childhood. All of it. Before your twelfth year. And I want you to appreciate the craftsmanship of what happened next, because I certainly do: I didn't feed it to the gods. Not at first. A complete childhood is rare currency. But three years ago the Crown's new friends came asking for material with *resonance* — memories anchored to Thornfell soil, soaked in the old rites, the kind that wake things. And whose childhood do you suppose was soaked deepest? Whose mother sang the old songs at the old shrine? Whose family kept the spring rites when everyone else had gone to the new church?"

"Stop," I said again, and my voice came out wrong, scraped.

"Yours," he said. "It was always going to be yours. You think it's coincidence that Ossa woke wearing your face? Gods don't choose faces at random, thief. They wear what they've eaten. Ossa has been wearing you since the day it swallowed your twelfth birthday."

I don't remember crossing the room. I remember the broker's chair tipping, remember my fist against his collarbone, remember Kestrel hauling me off by both arms while Sera said my name in the voice priests use for the dying. The broker lay among the chair slats, breathing carefully around what was probably a cracked rib, and he was still smiling.

"There it is," he wheezed. "There's the boy who wanted to stop running. Still running. Still selling pieces of himself to buy the road south. You never left that cellar, Nakamura. You've been walking out of it for twenty years."

"Isandro." Sera's hand on my shoulder. Warm. Steady. An anchor thrown to someone who hadn't admitted they were drowning. "Breathe."

I breathed. It felt borrowed.

* * *

We gave him water and let him talk, because that was the terrible thing — he *wanted* to talk. Men who curate other people's pasts hoard their own like dragons. We sat in the wreckage of the chair and made him spend.

"The rites," Sera said. "The spring rites. Describe them."

"I don't have them. I sold them upward two years ago. But I catalogued them first — I catalogue everything; it's how I sleep at night, knowing exactly what I am." He recited it like scripture, eyes half-closed: "Dawn, river-shrine, four voices. A woman singing the naming-song. A man — the father? — refusing to sing. A child hiding under the altar cloth watching through a tear in the weave. And then—" he paused, and for one moment the broker's composure flickered, showing me something almost like pity, "—and then smoke. Riders. Screaming. The child runs. The child runs for eleven years, priestess, in one direction or another, and every road out of Thornfell is a road away from that smoke."

"Why riders?" Sera asked quietly. "Who burns a spring rite?"

"The Crown's first inquisition. Before your church called it heresy, they called it *cleanup*. The old rites weren't suppressed, they were *harvested* — the fear of them, the memory of them, bottled and stored. Someone was building a reserve even then. I merely became the middleman." He looked at me. "Your family died keeping the old names alive, thief. And you sold their keeping to the thing that killed them. Do you want to know the exquisite part?"

"No," I said.

"Ossa doesn't just *have* your childhood. It *is* your childhood. When it wears your face, it isn't mockery — it's inheritance. Every skill in your hands, every habit in your step, the way you hum the naming-song when you think no one's listening — you think those are yours? They're lien property. You've been living in a house the bank owns." He settled deeper into his ruin of a chair. "And here's the clause you'll hate most: the reason you can't remember why you fled isn't damage. It's design. I sold your childhood *whole*, sealed, uncracked. Nothing leaks from a sealed jar. Which means somewhere inside that god is a twelve-year-old who watched riders come up the river valley, and that child knows exactly why you ran, and who you ran from, and what you saw — and it will trade that knowledge for exactly one thing."

The cellar was very cold. Or I was.

"What thing," Sera said, when I couldn't.

"The vessel." He said it simply, the way you'd say *the price is four crowns*. "The empty mind. The child with nothing in it. A god that eats memory needs somewhere to put what it takes, and an empty vessel is worth more than a kingdom right now. Ossa will give Nakamura back every year of that childhood — the smoke, the screaming, the reason, all of it, whole — for the price of one small blank soul delivered to the gate."

Upstairs, faintly, through the floorboards, I heard Wren laugh. Lost a round of knucklebones. Laughing anyway, the way children do when losing is still a game.

"They won't give the child," Kestrel said. It wasn't a question and it wasn't to me, exactly. It was to the room, staking a claim on behalf of all of us.

"No," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. Like a memory of a voice. "No, they won't."

"Then you have a choice," the broker said, almost kindly, which was the cruelest register he owned. "The child, or the truth. Because I promise you, thief — Ossa will make the offer at the gate, and it will wear your mother's voice when it does, and it will sing the naming-song, and you will *want* it. Twenty years of wanting, uncorked all at once. I've seen men trade kingdoms for less. I've *sold* men kingdoms for less."

Sera stood. She'd gone very still, the way she went still in chapter after chapter of her faith being dismantled, and when she spoke it was to the broker but her hand was on my shoulder, gripping hard enough to leave marks I was grateful for.

"You said the childhood is sealed. Unbroken."

"Perfectly preserved. The gods are careful eaters."

"Then it can be returned." Her fingers tightened. "Not traded. *Returned*. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference, and your ledger doesn't have a column for it."

The broker laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him, wincing around his rib. "Oh, priestess. Returned to whom? The owners are dead. The land is erased. The boy who sold it signed it away with his own blood in my book — legally, spiritually, irrevocably. Memory follows ownership, and ownership follows the signature, and the signature says *Nakamura, all of it, take everything before I was twelve*." He spread his bound hands. "Unless, of course, the signer withdraws the sale. And the only way to withdraw a sale to a god—"

"—is to stand in front of it," Sera finished, "and take it back out of its mouth."

Nobody said anything. The tallow dripped. Upstairs, Wren laughed again — winning this time, maybe, or laughing at losing better.

Kestrel broke the silence, sheathing his knife with a click that sounded like a decision. "So we walk to the gate. We were walking to the gate anyway."

"We were walking to the Door of Unmaking," Sera said. "Which opens for those who surrender what they cannot bear to lose." She looked down at me, and her eyes were doing the thing I'd seen once before, in the ashes of her shrine — grief and fury fused into something that could carry a person a very long way. "And now we know what you cannot bear to lose, Isandro. It isn't a memory. It's the child upstairs. So we make sure the door gets one and the god gets the other, and we get the order right."

I looked at my hands. Thief's hands. Lien property. Somewhere inside a starving god was a twelve-year-old me, curled around a secret I'd bled to forget, waiting to be bartered against an eight-year-old who remembered nothing at all.

My past wasn't lost. That was the vertigo of it — not gone, not mourned, just *held*, hostage, weaponized, wearing my own face and humming my mother's song. For twenty years I'd grieved a childhood I thought was dead. It wasn't dead. It was the enemy. It had been the enemy the whole time, and it knew my name because I'd given it my name, in ink, in blood, in a cellar very much like this one.

"Get up," I told the broker.

He raised his eyebrows. "Excuse me?"

"You're coming with us. To the gate. You're going to stand in front of the thing you built and read me my own ledger, line by line, every memory I sold, in order — because if I'm going to take my life back out of a god's mouth, I want to know exactly what I'm reaching for." I pulled him up by the collar, gently, considering the rib. "And Corvane? If Ossa offers me my childhood for the child, and I so much as hesitate — Sera has permission to do whatever priests do."

"She renounced the priesthood," Kestrel said.

"Then she improvises."

The broker straightened his ruined cravat with his bound hands, and for the first time since we'd broken down his door, he looked genuinely uncertain — a curator realizing the collection had grown teeth.

"You should know," he said, as Sera pushed him toward the stairs, "the ledger's last entry. The one I haven't mentioned. Dated three nights ago."

"Read it," I said.

"*Incoming shipment, Thornfell route, seized by inquisition riders at the Salt Bridge ford.*" He met my eyes in the dark of the stairwell. "*Contents: one memory, sealed, labeled in the seller's hand —* 'everything before I was twelve.' *Condition: damaged. The seal is already open.*"

The stairwell tilted. Somewhere behind my ribs, twenty years of running changed direction.

"Someone opened it," I said. "Someone opened my childhood. Who?"

The broker smiled, and this time there was no tea-host in it at all.

"Who do you think?" he said. "It's waking up, Nakamura. And it's looking for you."
