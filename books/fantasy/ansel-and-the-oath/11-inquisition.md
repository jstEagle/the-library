## 11. Inquisition

I want it on the record — whoever keeps records anymore, now that Thornfell has been struck from every ledger in creation — that I never intended to be good at betrayal.

I intended to be *terrible* at it. Half-hearted. The kind of traitor who trips over his own bootlaces and warns the targets by accident. But it turns out I'm good at everything I hate, which has been the organizing principle of my life since my brother stopped knowing my name.

It happened like this: I was on watch, third watch, the hour when the mountains go grey and honest people are still asleep and thieves are too tired to lie well. We'd made camp in a hollow below the Salt Bridge road, the four of us — Nakamura brooding by the dead fire, Sera praying to a god who'd gone quiet as a robbed grave, Wren asleep against Isandro's pack like a stray cat who'd decided ownership was a two-way street. And me, walking the perimeter, doing arithmetic I'd been doing for weeks.

The arithmetic went like this. My brother Tam had a mind full of holes where a heist of mine used to be. The memories had been taken from him — not erased, *taken*, lifted clean and sold upward, because in Thornfell even grief has a supply chain. And somewhere above the smugglers and the brokers there was something divine eating what I'd lost. So the math said: follow the memories up. Which meant staying with Nakamura and their little congregation of strays.

The other math said: the inquisition pays better, doesn't sleep rough, and knows exactly where the memories are kept.

I was on the second sum when a branch snapped behind me, which is embarrassing, because I would have heard anyone else at forty paces. Either they were very good or I was very tired, and only one of those turned out to be true.

"Kestrel of the Salt Bridge," said a voice like a drawer of knives being opened carefully.

There were six of them when I turned around. Grey riders, grey cloaks, faces wrapped to the eyes so the cold couldn't get in and neither could sympathy. Inquisition. Their horses stood in the dark like patient furniture. One of them held a lantern with the shutter half-closed, letting out a blade of light that lay across my chest like a sash.

"We've been looking for you," the speaker said. He dismounted slowly, the way men do when they're certain you're not going anywhere. "Or rather, we've been looking for your companions. You were a convenience."

"A compliment," I said. "Most people have to buy dinner first."

He let that hang. Under the wrap, I could see enough of his face to know he didn't blink much. Inquisitors don't, in my experience. Blinking is an admission that the world changes.

"Here is the shape of your night," he said. "You will lead us to the thief Nakamura. You will stand aside while we take them, the priestess, and the child. In exchange—" he made a small gesture, and one of the riders brought forward a case, long and narrow, opened it on a flat rock like a reliquary.

Inside, packed in grey wool, were vials. Dozens of them, each one stoppered and sealed with wax stamped with the inquisition's sun-and-shears. And I knew. The way you know your own handwriting. The way you know the smell of your childhood kitchen. Memory, in glass — and among all that stolen light, glowing a slightly different color than the rest, warm as lamplight through a window you used to live behind—

"Tam's," I said. My voice came out wrong. Flat, like someone else doing an impression of me at my worst moment.

"All of him," the inquisitor said. "Every year they took. Restored whole. He'll know you again. He'll know what happened on the bridge, why he climbed down instead of running, whose hand he was reaching for." A pause, calibrated. "He remembers falling, Kestrel. He doesn't remember catching."

I should tell you about the bridge, I suppose, since apparently we're telling everything tonight. Two years ago. A heist gone sideways on the Salt Bridge itself — a memory too heavy for one carrier, split between two brothers, and when the wardens came Tam went over the rail rather than let them take his half. I got my half clear. He hit the water. He lived, mostly. What the fall didn't break, the loss did: whatever the memory held had been knitted into everything around it, and when I pulled my thread out of him to save his life — no. When *they* pulled theirs. I've told myself both versions. Neither of them lets me sleep.

Afterward his mind had a Tam-shaped hole in it. He looked at me with perfect politeness, the way you look at a landlord. And then someone had come through the recovery wards buying damaged minds for scrap prices, and Tam's hole had been worth money to somebody, and I'd spent two years following that money up the chain.

Now the chain had followed me back down, holding the top link out like a dog treat.

"You'll forgive me," I said, "if I don't weep with gratitude. Your people burned three shrines in Vetch last month. You're not in the restoration business. You're in the bait business."

"I'm in the obedience business," he said, unbothered. "The restoration is real. I've seen the manifest. The vials are genuine — check them yourself if you know how, and I'm told you do." He let the lantern light slide off me. "Refuse, and we take them anyway, along with your friends, and you can watch your brother die ignorant at whichever charity house the wind blows him to. Accept, and he wakes up whole. It is not a complicated choice. Complicated choices are for people with more souls than you're currently using."

And here's the thing I keep circling: the choice *wasn't* complicated. That was the horror of it. Four weeks earlier I'd have signed in blood and asked for a pen for the blood. Four weeks earlier I was a man with one debt and no witnesses.

But four weeks of road does things to you. Wren had learned to whistle through their fingers and insisted on demonstrating at dawn, badly, every dawn. Sera had started saving me the heel of the bread without being asked, because she'd noticed I liked it, because that's what priests do when their god goes silent — they practice noticing on whoever's nearby. And Nakamura —

Nakamura, who I'd ambushed and robbed and sworn to kill, back when I was a better person, had sat across from me two nights ago and said, without any preamble, in the dark: *Your brother. On the bridge. You've never told anyone which side of the rope you were on.* Not an accusation. Just an inventory. The way a fellow thief looks at your scars and says, ah, that one's from a lockpick, that one's from a lover. Professional recognition. The closest thing to kindness our trade has.

So the choice wasn't complicated. It was just expensive either way, which is different.

"I'll need until mid-morning," I heard myself say. "They don't travel the open road after the bridge checkpoint. There's a goat track; it adds hours. If you come at dawn they'll hear the horses on the scree."

The inquisitor studied me for a long moment. I gave him what he wanted to see: a man doing math with his brother's name in the left column. It wasn't even acting. Both columns had my brother's name in them. That's the trick nobody tells you about betrayal — done properly, it looks exactly like love from the outside, because it's made of the same material and weighs the same.

"Mid-morning," he agreed. "And Kestrel — we'll be close enough to see everything. If you warn them, the vials go back in the case and the case goes back in the fire."

"Understood," I said, and it was the truth, which is the best kind of lie to build on.

* * *

I didn't sleep. Obviously.

I sat by the banked fire and watched Wren breathe and tried to construct a version of events where everyone got out. It was bad architecture. Every draft collapsed. Take the deal: Nakamura in irons, Sera in irons, Wren — Wren most of all, because I'd heard the inquisitor's riders talk in that casual way soldiers talk about cargo, and the child's empty head was described as *the vessel*, and nothing good has ever been done to anything called a vessel.

Not take the deal: Tam stays broken forever, and I become the man who chose strangers over his own blood, which is the one crime Thornfell's underworld actually punishes. There are ballads about that kind of man. None of them end well and all of them rhyme.

Sera woke somewhere in the black hours. She didn't speak. She just looked at me across the coals with those priestess eyes that had seen my whole hand of cards weeks ago and never once called my bluff, and after a while she said, quietly:

"My god went silent because its memories were stolen. I spent a long time believing that silence was abandonment. It's remarkable," she added, feeding a twig to the embers, "how much cruelty starts with misreading silence."

"If you've got a sermon in you, save it," I said. "I'm not a religious man."

"No," she agreed. "But you're a man standing at an altar, deciding. That's close enough that I'll pray for you anyway. You won't notice. It's non-denominational."

"What do you pray *to*?" I asked, before I could stop myself. "Yours is deaf."

"To the part of it that's still listening," she said. "Something always is. Even stolen things leave the shape of themselves behind. Even empty rooms echo." She looked at me directly then. "Whatever you're carrying, Kestrel — whatever they put on you tonight — remember that a debt paid in other people's blood isn't paid. It's refinanced. The interest is worse."

I told her I didn't know what she was talking about. She nodded with terrible patience and went back to sleep, and I sat there feeling read like a ledger, and hated her, in the specific way you hate people who are right on a schedule.

Dawn came up grey. Wren woke, whistled through their fingers — three notes, all wrong, triumphant — and asked me if today we'd reach the pass with the stone cairns shaped like fat men. I said maybe. Nakamura drank their bitter tea and looked at me with those thief's eyes that catalogued everything, and I wondered — not for the first time — whether they already knew. Whether they'd known since the hollow, since the way I hadn't slept, since whatever micro-tells I give off that I don't know about. Thieves read each other. It's the whole job.

If they knew, they said nothing. Maybe they trusted me. Maybe they'd just decided I wasn't worth the worry, which stung worse.

We broke camp and took the goat track, and I led them toward it.

* * *

The place I'd chosen — and I use *chosen* loosely, the way you choose which tooth hurts — was a defile below the old cairns where the track narrows between two rock walls. Perfect ground for an ambush, which is why I picked it, which is why picking it was unforgivable. I walked point. Behind me: Nakamura, easy in their body, humming something under their breath. Sera, staff in hand, using it mostly as a walking stick, which is what she wanted people to think. Wren riding on the packhorse, narrating a story to themselves in a whisper — some stitched-together thing borrowed from the commune tales and Sera's psalms and Isandro's swear words, because that's where a child gets a self when they weren't issued one.

And ahead of me, six grey riders who thought they were hidden, and a seventh thing I felt before I saw — a wrongness in the air like the hush before a shrine bell.

I stopped. Nakamura stopped. The defile waited.

"So," I said.

"So," said Nakamura, and I'll be damned if they didn't sound almost fond.

"They're on both walls," I said. "Six riders, plus whatever's making the air taste like a locked church. They want you, the priestess, and the vessel. They offered me my brother in a box." I turned around. This was the hard part, the actual work, harder than any heist: standing still and letting them see all of me. "I took the deal. Then I sat by your fire all night trying to find the version where it was forgivable, and I couldn't, so here's the other version. Late. Unpaid for. Take it or don't."

Silence, except Wren's horse shifting.

Then Nakamura did the last thing I expected, which was laugh — short, sharp, no humor in it but no hatred either. "Kestrel. You absolute ruin of a man. We knew before midnight."

"—what?"

"The inquisition doesn't snap branches," they said. "Sera heard the prayer-cadence in their lantern light. I heard the horses chewing with their lips closed — cavalry habit. You were the only one surprised." They shrugged. "We let you walk point anyway. Wanted to see which way you'd turn at the narrows."

"That's—" I groped for a word. "*Insane.*"

"It's the whole trade," Nakamura said. "You stake everything on reading one person right. I've done it for coin my whole life. Figured I'd try it for something else once." They glanced past me at the walls, already calculating angles, hands loose, voice dropping into business. "Talk fast. What's their formation?"

"Four high, two mounted closing the rear gap. The thing in front — I don't know what it is. It smells like the inside of the recovery wards."

"Sera!" Nakamura called, without turning. "Front's yours. Whatever it is."

"It's a reader," Sera said calmly, unshouldering her staff, and there was something in her voice I hadn't heard before — not the quiet grief, the *other* thing, the thing the burning shrines had planted. "A bound one. Poor creature. Don't look into its eyes unless you want your last good afternoon appraised."

And then everything happened at once, because that's what ambushes are for.

Riders came off both walls — ropes and scree, fast, professional. The reader-thing drifted into the defile's mouth, and it was worse than I'd imagined: man-shaped, unwrapped, its face moving like water remembering being a face, and everywhere it looked the air went blank. I saw one rider glance at it and forget what decade it was.

Wren screamed — not fear. Fury. And the gods that circle that child like moths circled *hard*, and the nearest rider's horse simply refused the wall, screaming, and threw him onto the rocks.

Sera met the reader. I won't pretend I understood what I saw. She spoke something old into it — not a prayer, a *bill of sale reversed*, words like *this mind belongs to itself* — and the thing shuddered, and a rider behind it clutched his skull and wept, suddenly, hugely, like a man given back ten years he'd been robbed of, and forgot which side he was on because he'd just remembered which side he'd *been* on, years ago, when the inquisition took his sister's memories for the quota.

That's when I understood the whole rotten machine. That's when I stopped doing arithmetic entirely.

Nakamura was already moving through the smoke and scree like the answer to a question nobody asked out loud, and I went with them, because there was a case on a saddle — I'd seen which saddle — and there were vials in the case, and one of the vials was warm as lamplight through a kitchen window.

I killed no one. I want that recorded too. I broke a jaw with a pommel and dropped a man off a ledge into a snowdrift he'd thank me for later, and I got the case, and Nakamura covered me the whole way without being asked, without a word, the way you cover someone you've decided to keep.

Afterward — riders scattered, reader dissolved into ordinary air leaving one confused freed prisoner babbling apologies, Wren whistling victory through their bleeding fingers — we stood in the defile breathing white breath, and I set the case down between us and opened it.

Nakamura looked at the vials. Looked at me.

"That's your price," they said. "Your brother. Whole. You could walk out of Thornfell rich in the only currency that ever mattered to you. Nobody would blame you."

"Yeah," I said. My hands were shaking. I closed the case. Picked it up. "Problem is, I've recently learned where restored minds get sold *next*. Everything goes up the chain eventually. Everything except—" I jerked my chin at the case, at Wren, at Sera, at all of it. "Except this. Whatever this is. Apparently I'm carrying it now."

Sera smiled like a woman watching a stubborn convert finally step into the water. Wren asked if they could hold the box because it looked important, and I said ask me again when you're older, and Nakamura just nodded once, thief to thief, the highest honor my miserable trade confers.

We started up toward the pass, toward the Door, toward whatever ends this story has in stock.

And far behind us, at the mouth of the defile, the air folded — once, politely, like a page turning — and something wearing a familiar silhouette stood where the reader had been, watching us go, and I heard Nakamura's breath catch in a way I'd never heard before.

Because whatever had just found our trail, it moved exactly the way Nakamura moves.
