## 12. The God Who Claims Me

There is a particular kind of silence that comes before a god speaks. I know this now the way I know the weight of a coin purse by feel, or the exact moment a mark's smile turns from hospitality to suspicion. It isn't an absence of sound. It's a presence of *attention* — the whole world leaning in to listen to something that hasn't said a word yet.

I felt it first at dusk, three days out of Vetch, on a road that technically did not exist and therefore could not legally contain us.

"Stop walking," I said.

Kestrel didn't stop walking. Kestrel never stopped walking unless you made it expensive for him not to. "Why?"

"Because the birds stopped."

He glanced up. The pines stood black against a bruised-orange sky, and there wasn't a single wingbeat in any of them. No crows arguing over territory. No thrushes doing their evening inventory of worms. Just stillness, spreading outward from somewhere ahead like ink through water.

Sera had already gone quiet — her version of quiet, which was different from everyone else's. Hers was the quiet of a woman listening with her whole spine. "It's here," she said. "It's been following us since Vetch."

"You could have mentioned that," I said.

"I wanted to be sure."

"And now you're sure?"

She looked at me, and I saw something in her face I'd only seen once before, at her burning shrine: grief so old it had fossilized into something harder than fear. "Now I'm sure."

Wren tugged my sleeve. The kid had learned, in our weeks together, to read the weather of adult panic the way farm children read clouds. "Isandro. Why does the road feel like it's looking at me?"

Because it's looking at all of us, I almost said. Because we're standing in the palm of something. But what came out was: "Stay behind me, sprout."

Wren stayed behind me. Kestrel finally stopped walking, which told me more about how bad this was than any omen. And then the air ahead of us folded — there's no better word for it, the way twilight just *folded*, like cloth taken up by careful hands — and something stepped out of the fold wearing my face.

Not approximately my face. Not a flattering portrait or a nightmare distortion. My face. The one I shaved in cracked tavern mirrors. The scar through my left eyebrow that I'd gotten at nine years old falling out of a plum tree — except I couldn't remember falling out of a plum tree, because I'd sold the memory of my childhood years ago, hadn't I, and yet the scar was *there*, on its face, exactly where it lived on mine.

"Well," I said, because wit is the last thing I own outright and I guard it jealously. "Either I've died and this is the afterlife's idea of a joke, or—"

"*Or,*" said the thing with my mouth, and hearing my own voice come out of my own face from six feet away was like watching someone else pick your pocket in slow motion, "you are finally meeting your creditor."

Behind me, Sera whispered a word in Old Liturgical. It was not a prayer. It was closer to a curse, or possibly a diagnosis.

The god — and I knew it was a god, the way you know a storm is a storm and not just wind — looked at me with my own eyes. They were brown. Mine are brown. There was a fleck of amber in the left iris that I'd never noticed before, because who studies their own eyes? Apparently gods do. Apparently gods study every detail of what they own.

"You're Ossa," Sera said. She stepped forward, past me, arms loose at her sides — a priestess's posture, open, unarmed, though I'd seen her break a man's wrist from that exact stance. "You wear stolen faces. That's your nature now, isn't it? You are made of what was taken."

Ossa turned its head — my head — toward her, and smiled with my smile, the crooked one I use when I want a mark to think I'm harmless. "Priestess. Your god ate well off my table for centuries and called it communion. Do not lecture me on theft." It turned back to me. "But we are not here for you, little sister of silence. We are here for the thief."

"The thief has a name," Kestrel said flatly.

"It has several. Nakamura. Vetch's 'gray ghost.' The child who ran from Thornfell seventeen years ago and could not say why." Ossa tilted my head, studying me the way I studied locked boxes. "Do you wonder about that, Isandro? Why you ran? You've built a whole self around the shape of that absence. A person shaped like a hole."

My mouth was dry. "Everyone has gaps."

"Not everyone's gaps have *provenance*. Not everyone's gaps come with a bill of sale." It raised my hand — my hand, with the burn scar across two knuckles from a forge I'd never been able to picture — and began counting on my fingers. "Let us review the ledger. At fourteen, you stole the memory of a fisherman's drowned son and sold it for bread money. At sixteen, a bride's wedding day. At nineteen, a dying man's entire boyhood, forty-one years, sold in three lots. Shall I continue? I have the receipts."

"Those were theirs," I said. "I took them fair. Well. Unfair. But fairly *unfair*. That's the trade."

"Yes," Ossa said softly, with my voice, gentle as a mother's. "And everything taken becomes ours. That is the oldest law, older than your Crown, older than your church. What is seized belongs to the seizer. What is sold belongs to the buyer. And you, Isandro Nakamura, have spent your whole life selling." It spread my hands wide, a showman's gesture, and the road behind it rippled with half-seen shapes — faces, hundreds of them, flickering like reflections in disturbed water. Every memory I'd ever lifted, wearing the people they'd been peeled from. "We are the sum of your career. We woke hungry because you fed us. And now we have come to collect what was always ours by right."

"Memories," I said. "Fine. Take them. I don't have many worth the trouble."

"Oh, thief." My own face looked at me with something like pity, and pity from yourself is the worst kind. "We do not want the memories in your *head*. Those are mostly borrowed anyway, secondhand goods, other people's leavings. We want what the memories bought." It gestured at me — at all of me. "Your hands remember lockpicking. Where did that skill live before you learned it? In memories. Of teaching, of practice, of failure. Your tongue remembers four languages. Your body remembers the plum tree you fell from — oh yes, we hold that one, the scar is ours, the pain was filed under your name and paid for twice. Your wariness. Your tenderness — yes, we've seen how you look at the empty-headed child when you think no one is watching; that softness came from somewhere, from someone, and we hold the somewhere. Everything you are is built from remembered things. And every remembered thing you ever touched, touched you back. You are collateral, Isandro. You have been collateral since your first theft. We are simply the first creditor honest enough to call in the lien."

The road swayed. Or I did. Hard to say.

"That's not how ownership works," Sera said sharply.

"It is precisely how ownership works," Ossa replied, pleasant as a tax collector. "Your church wrote the doctrine. Memory belongs to heaven; flesh is merely its custodian. We are merely *foreclosing*."

Wren made a small sound behind me. Ossa's gaze slid past my shoulder — my gaze, sliding — and landed on the child, and for one moment the god's expression changed. Hunger is too crude a word. It was the look of a man finding water in a desert.

"And there it is," Ossa murmured. "The empty cup. So patient. So *clean*." Its attention lingered on Wren a heartbeat too long, and every animal instinct I owned stood up and screamed, and then the god looked back at me and the moment passed, smooth as a card trick. "Another matter. Another appointment. Today is about you."

"Then let's conclude business," I said, and my voice only shook a little, which I counted as a professional triumph. "What do you want? State terms. I'm a trader. I understand terms."

"Terms." Ossa savored the word. "Very well. First: acknowledge the debt. Say aloud that what you took, you took, and what you took is ours. Words are deeds among the old gods; your confession will bind the contract tighter than any chain."

"No," Sera breathed. "Isandro, don't—"

"Second," Ossa continued, ignoring her, "you will cease trading. A debtor does not keep borrowing. From this night forward, no memory passes through your hands — given, taken, or sold. You will become, in effect, what the child is: a vessel that holds only what falls into it."

"And third?" My mouth had gone ahead without consulting me. Bad habit. One of mine. Possibly one of *theirs* now.

"Third," said the god wearing my face, and here it leaned close, close enough that I could smell my own smell on it — woodsmoke and cheap soap and the specific sweat of fear — "third, you will stop running from Thornfell. You will turn around. You will walk back into the country that erased itself, and you will stand in the place where you stood as a child on the night you fled, and you will remember why."

"I don't remember why."

"We know," Ossa said, with infinite gentleness. "We hold the why. It is the cornerstone of our house. It is the sweetest thing you ever sold us, though you don't recall selling it — you were small, and terrified, and a broker in Vetch offered you forgetting in exchange for survival, and you took the deal the way children take all deals: without reading them." It straightened. "Give us the child, Isandro. Give us the empty vessel, and we will return your childhood whole. Every day. Your mother's voice. The reason you ran. The name of the thing you saw. Everything you swore never to give up — returned, in exchange for one small blank slate that wants for nothing, misses nothing, and belongs to no one."

The silence afterward had weight, like snow.

I became aware, distantly, of Kestrel's hand near his knife. Of Sera's lips moving through some silent argument with a god that had already stopped listening. Of Wren's small fingers gripping the back of my coat — not scared, just holding on, the way the kid held on whenever the world got loud, because I'd taught them that, hadn't I, taught them *hold on to me and count to a hundred*, and teaching requires memory, and whose memory was that, mine or the god's, and did it even matter anymore—

"No," I said.

Ossa waited.

"I said no. Keep the childhood. Keep the ledger. Keep the lien, the contract, the receipts, whatever liturgical paperwork you dragged out here." I stepped sideways, deliberately, until my body was fully between the god and the child. "You can have my skills. Take the lockpicking, take the languages, take the plum tree and the burn scar and the crooked smile. Strip me down to a stranger if that's what collection looks like. But the empty cup stays empty, and it stays *with me*, and if you reach for it I will spend every scrap of myself you haven't repossessed making you regret the trip."

Something moved behind my own eyes on the god's face. Not anger. Something worse — *recalculation*.

"Interesting," Ossa said quietly. "That answer was not in your file."

"Maybe your files are incomplete."

"No." The god smiled my smile again, but slower now, and it didn't reach my eyes, which — fair enough, it never reached mine either. "Maybe the file is outdated. Maybe some small part of you has acquired things we do not hold. Freely given things carry no lien, thief. Remember that. It will matter soon." It stepped backward into the fold of the air, my face dissolving into the dark like a reflection leaving water. "We will not press the claim tonight. Creditors can wait. Debts cannot. Walk south, if you like. Run to your Door of Unmaking. But know that every step you take on remembered roads, you walk on ours — and when you arrive at the door that takes what you cannot bear to lose, ask yourselves who has been paying *our* toll all along."

The fold closed. The birds came back all at once, a riot of indignant evening noise, as if the whole forest had been holding its breath and had opinions about it.

For a long moment none of us moved.

Then Wren's grip tightened on my coat. "Isandro," the kid said, very calmly, in the way people are calm when they are absolutely not calm, "it had your face. When it talked about me. It was smiling with *your* face."

"I know, sprout."

"If it takes your face, how will I know you?"

That question went through me like a blade finding the gap in armor. I crouched down to the kid's level. My knees hurt. Small mercies: pain was probably still mine.

"You'll know me," I said, "because I'll be the one telling you to hold on and count to a hundred. Gods don't count. Gods only collect."

Wren considered this with the terrifying seriousness of an eight-year-old auditing an ontology. "Okay. One. Two. Three..."

Kestrel sheathed his knife. "It knew about the Door," he said. "It *wants* us at the Door. That was not a threat, Nakamura, that was an invitation."

"I heard it."

"So we don't go."

"We go." Sera's voice was iron. She was staring at the place where the god had stood. "Don't you see? It said freely given things carry no lien. It's afraid of that. It told us its weakness because it couldn't help itself — greed always shows you the shape of what it fears." She turned to me, and her eyes were wet and furious and certain. "It needs you to arrive alone, or broken, or bargaining. So we arrive together, whole, and we give each other everything it thinks it owns."

"That's either brilliant or suicidal," Kestrel said.

"With this crew," I said, standing up, knees cracking, Wren reaching twenty-seven, "historically it's both."

We walked on, south, down a road that existed nowhere except in smugglers' memories — and I caught myself wondering, with a chill I couldn't shake, whether the road remembered us back, and whose property that made us.

Behind us, faint as a held breath, something with my laugh laughed once, softly, and did not follow.

Yet.

* * *

We made camp that night in the shell of a waystation whose name had been struck from maps so thoroughly that even the ghosts seemed unsure of the address. Kestrel took first watch and pretended not to check on Wren every ten minutes. Sera sat awake by the cold fire, mouthing the renunciation she'd begun drafting in her head — I could see the words taking shape behind her eyes like storm fronts.

And I lay on my back under unfamiliar stars and tried to take inventory of myself, item by item, the way I'd inventory a mark's house. Hands: present. Fear: present, abundant. The crooked smile: unaccounted for. The reason I ran: missing, lien held by a god wearing my face.

Somewhere past midnight, Wren crept over and lay down against my side without asking, the way the kid did now, the way that had become normal, the way that meant something no ledger had a column for.

"Forty-one, forty-two," Wren murmured, still counting, hours later, keeping the tally going like a lamp kept lit.

I closed my eyes and held on.
