## 13. Sanctuary of the Mapless

The first thing you should know about me is that I don't remember anything. Not a little, not mostly, not "except for one terrible thing." Nothing. My head is like a bowl someone washed out and forgot to fill. Isandro says that's not a bad way to be, but Isandro says a lot of comforting things in a voice that means the opposite, so I've learned to check.

The second thing you should know is that the people in the valley below us had no reason at all to take me in, and they did it before Isandro even finished explaining who we were.

We had been walking for — I don't know how long. I don't have a before, exactly, so time is a thing that happens to other people. But my feet had opinions, and my feet said it had been a long time. We came down out of the mountains along a road that wasn't on any map, which Isandro said was the only kind of road left worth walking, and Sera said that was a heresy and also true, and Kestrel said nothing, because Kestrel saves his words like coins he doesn't trust the bank with.

Then Isandro stopped, held up a hand, and said, "Smoke. Cooking smoke. Not burning."

I didn't understand the difference yet. I do now.

The valley was small and green and full of gardens, which seemed impossible, because everything else in Thornfell had been burned or emptied or struck off the world. There were maybe forty houses, none of them built the same way, like people had arrived from forty different countries and each brought a piece of home and nailed it up. There were goats. There was a bell tower with no bell in it, just a rope hanging down into nothing.

And there was a woman standing in the road in front of us with a pitchfork, who looked at four filthy travelers — a thief, a disgraced priestess, a man with a scar like a river delta across his face, and me — and lowered the pitchfork and said, "You'll be wanting supper, then."

Not *who are you*. Not *what do you want*. Not even *are you dangerous*, which, honestly, Kestrel alone should have earned us that question.

"You'll be wanting supper, then." As if it was the most obvious thing in the world. As if we were expected. As if there had never been any question at all.

I said, "Yes, please," because I am polite even with a head like an empty bowl, and the woman laughed like I'd said something wise.

* * *

Her name was Odile, and she had been a cartographer, back when there were maps and a country that appeared on them. She'd been erased from the records of her own city for drawing a map the government didn't like — a map that showed where the mass graves were. So she'd come here, to a valley that had been struck off the rolls three regimes ago, where the erased of every era had been drifting like ash and settling like snow.

There was a man who had been a judge, struck from the rolls for ruling against a lord. A woman who had been a historian, whose entire life's work was burned, who had rewritten as much of it as she could from memory and then taught it to children until the children knew it better than she did. A family who had fled a purge two generations back and whose grandchildren had never seen a city. A man who had once been a name on a list, and that was all, and he'd decided if he was going to be a list-entry he'd at least choose which list, and he'd chosen this one.

"You're all mapless," Isandro said that first night, in the long hall where everyone ate, and there was a silence, and then Odile said, "We prefer *unmapped*. A mapless thing is lost. An unmapped thing is free."

Isandro looked at her for a long moment. Isandro does that — looks at people like they're locks and Isandro is deciding which tools to use. But this time I don't think it was that. I think Isandro was looking at a person who had lost everything official about themselves and was still standing, and it scared Isandro more than the inquisition did, because it meant it was possible.

I didn't understand any of this at the time. I was eating bread. The bread was very good. You have to understand, when your head is empty, bread is not a small thing. Bread is enormous.

* * *

Here is the thing about the valley, the thing that made it different from every other place we'd hidden, and I didn't understand it until the second night, and even then I only understood it with my skin, not my head.

Every night, after supper, they told each other's stories.

Not *stories*, like made-up ones. Their stories. Each other's. The judge would stand up — he was old and his hands shook — and tell the story of the historian's trial, because she couldn't tell it herself without her voice going strange, so he told it for her, and he'd been in the courtroom, and he told it exactly, every word, and when he finished she would nod once, like a door being locked.

The historian told the story of the family who'd fled the purge, because the grandmother who'd lived it had died the winter before, and the family stood in a row while the historian gave them back their own past in words, and the grandchildren listened with their mouths open like I did, because it was the first time they were hearing it too.

And so on, around the hall, night after night. Everyone held a piece of everyone else. Nobody held only their own.

"It's how we keep it," Odile told us. "Memory, I mean. A memory kept alone is a candle in a wind. A memory kept by forty people is a bonfire. The Crown can strike my name from every registry in the world. It cannot strike it from Marta's telling of it, or the judge's, or that child's — " she pointed at me — "once I give it to her."

I felt Isandro go still next to me. Not moving-still. The other kind. The kind where someone is standing very carefully on the edge of something.

"That's a dangerous way to live," Isandro said.

"It's the only way that's ever worked," Odile said, and poured more tea, and that was that.

* * *

It was Sera who noticed me first. Sera notices everything; she was a priestess, and even a disgraced one watches people the way shepherds watch weather. On the third night, while the judge was telling the story of a boy who'd once lived here — a boy who'd grown up and left and whose mother told his story every night so he'd have somewhere to come back to — Sera leaned down to me.

"You're not listening the way they listen," she said.

"How do they listen?"

"Like they're catching something. Like it's being handed to them and they have to hold on. You're listening like it's a fire you're warming your hands at."

I thought about that. "I don't have anywhere to put them," I said. "The stories. I don't have a shelf."

Sera was quiet a moment. "No shelf," she said. "No shelf at all."

"No. I told you. I don't remember anything."

"I know what you told me." She said it gently, but there was something underneath it, the way there always was with Sera, a wire under the cloth. "I've been wondering, Wren, what it means. The gods circle you. They circled you in that village, and they circled you at the wayshrine, and they'd circle you now if there were any left in this valley to do it. An empty mind. Do you know what that is, to them?"

"Food?"

"A *vessel*," Sera said. "A clean cup. Something they could pour themselves into." She looked at me for a long time, and I looked back, because I didn't know what else to do with a look like that. "But that's their reading of you. I've been working on another."

"What's mine?"

"That you are not empty," Sera said. "That you are *unwritten*. And there is a difference, child, the same difference Odile draws between mapless and unmapped. Empty means something was taken. Unwritten means nothing has claimed you yet. The gods think you're a cup. I think you're a page."

I didn't fully understand, but I understood the important part, which was that she wasn't afraid of me, and she wasn't sorry for me, and both of those were new.

* * *

It was Odile who gave me the first story. She did it the way she did everything, plainly, without asking permission, the fourth night, when supper was done and the hall was filling with the low murmur of people arranging themselves for the telling.

"Tonight we have guests," she announced, "and one of them is new in a way none of us has been, and I've been thinking about what we owe her."

"We owe her nothing," said the judge, but kindly, the way you say a thing to test it.

"We owe her a shelf," Odile said. "That's the whole law of this valley. Everyone holds something for someone. So." She turned to me, and the whole hall turned with her, forty faces, firelight on them, and I wanted to hide behind Kestrel, but Kestrel was watching too, and there was nowhere to hide from that particular look. "Wren. I'm going to tell you a story, and it's yours now. Not mine anymore. Once I give it, I only keep my copy, the way we all do. But the story itself — the keeping of it — that's yours. And if I die, or forget, or the inquisition burns this valley down, you'll still have it. That's the bargain. Do you accept?"

Forty people waiting. A fire cracking. My hands around a cup of tea gone cold because I'd forgotten to drink it.

"Yes," I said.

So Odile told me a story. It was about a girl named Odile, actually, which I thought at first was a mistake, but it wasn't. It was about a girl who loved maps, who learned to draw coastlines before she learned to write her own name, who grew up and drew a map of where the bodies were buried because someone had to, because a country that doesn't mark its graves will dig more of them. And how they erased her for it. And how she walked for a long time. And how she found a valley.

"And then she found a child with no past," Odile said, "and gave her a story, so that the child would have one thing that was hers, that no god and no crown and no inquisition could take, because it lived in her now, freely given and freely held. And that was the first story Wren ever kept. And she kept it her whole life."

The hall was quiet. I was crying, which surprised me, because I didn't know I could do that about something that wasn't even mine, that had happened to someone else, that had happened to *her*. But that's the thing about a given story. It's not someone else's. That's the whole trick of it.

"Say the last part back to me," Odile said.

"And she kept it her whole life," I said.

"Good," said Odile. "Now it's true."

* * *

After that, they came to me one at a time. I didn't ask them to. I think Odile told people, the way things travel in a small place, and I think — I understand this better now — that giving me a story was a kind of gift they'd been waiting years to give anyone. You can't hand someone a candle if they already have a lantern. I was the first person any of them had met in a long time with no light at all.

The judge gave me the story of his last ruling — the one that got him erased — every word of it, and made me repeat the important sentence back until I had it exactly: *The law belongs to the people it protects, or it belongs to no one.*

Marta the historian gave me the names of eleven villages that no longer appeared on any map, and made me say them back, and told me that if I was the only person alive who knew them, then the villages were still real, and so was I.

Kestrel — and this I did not expect, I want you to understand, Kestrel, who counted his words like coins — Kestrel sat down next to me one evening on the woodpile and said, "My brother's name was Tam." And that was all. And then he said, "That's yours now. Don't lose it." And got up and left, and I sat there holding *Tam* like it was made of glass, because I had seen what it cost him to set it down.

And Isandro. Isandro took the longest.

Isandro watched all of it from the edge of the hall every night, arms crossed, wearing that face that means *I am not moved by this* — the face Isandro wears when Isandro is most moved by something. And on the sixth night, after the tellings were done and everyone had gone to bed, Isandro sat down beside me on the bench where I was saying the eleven villages to myself, one more time, in the dark.

"You know they're using you," Isandro said. It wasn't cruel. It was careful. Isandro is always careful with me, the way you're careful with a thing you're afraid of breaking. "The stories. It's sweet, but it's also — they need keepers, Wren. That's the whole machine. Everyone holds a piece of everyone else so no one can be erased. It's beautiful and it's a trap. You're eight. You didn't choose this."

"I didn't choose anything," I said. "I don't have anything to choose *with*. That's what you keep not understanding." I said it more sharply than I meant to. Isandro flinched, actually flinched, and I felt bad, and also not bad, because it was true. "You all keep saying I'm empty like it's a wound. Sera says I'm a page. Odile says I'm unmapped. But you — you look at me like I'm a hole."

Isandro was quiet for a long time. The fire in the hall had burned down to a red breathing.

"When I was your age," Isandro said, and stopped, and I knew — the way you know things with your skin — that Isandro didn't know the rest of the sentence. That Isandro had reached for a memory and found the shelf bare, and the bareness was Isandro's own doing, sold away years ago to someone whose name Isandro didn't know yet.

"You don't remember either," I said. "When you were my age."

"No," Isandro said. "I don't."

"Then how do you know it's better? Having one?"

Another long quiet. Then Isandro laughed — a small, real laugh, one of maybe three I'd heard — and put a hand on my head, briefly, like a blessing that didn't know it was one.

"I don't," Isandro said. "That's the honest answer. I don't know. But here's what I know about you, Wren, and you can keep this one too, since everyone's handing them out. Six days ago you were a child alone in an emptied village, and every instinct I have — every single one, and I have very good instincts — told me to walk past you. And I didn't. I've been trying to work out why ever since. And tonight, watching forty people give you their histories for free, I think I finally have it." Isandro looked at me, and the firelight was in Isandro's eyes, and for once there was no lock-picking in the look at all. Just looking. "It's because you're the only person I've ever met with nothing to hide. Everyone else in the world is a locked room, Wren. I should know. I break into them for a living. You're the first open door I've ever stood in front of. And it turns out I didn't want to steal anything. I just wanted to come in out of the weather."

I didn't say anything. I was holding the words, the way Odile taught me. *Once I give it, it's yours.*

"Say it back," Isandro said, and there was a smile in it, but underneath the smile, something that needed to hear it.

"You wanted to come in out of the weather," I said.

"Good," Isandro said, and stood, and paused. "Keep that one safe, Wren. It's worth more than you know. It's the only true thing about me I have left."

And Isandro went to bed, and I sat in the dark saying the eleven villages, and the judge's sentence, and *Tam*, and the girl who loved maps, and the thief who wanted to come in out of the weather, over and over, quietly, the way you count a treasure.

* * *

I should have known it couldn't last. Nothing in Thornfell lasts, except the stories, and even they have to be carried somewhere.

It was the ninth night. The tellings were done, the hall was emptying, and I was half asleep against Sera's shoulder when the bell rope in the tower — the rope that hung down into nothing, the rope that had never once rung since we arrived — moved.

There was no bell. But the rope moved, and the whole valley heard it anyway, a sound with no source, a tolling that came up through the ground and out of the walls, and every person in that hall went white at exactly the same moment, because they had been erased people for a long time, and they knew what it meant when something knocked on a door that wasn't supposed to exist.

Odile stood up. "Someone's asking for sanctuary," she said. "Or something's asking to come in."

And out past the gardens, at the mouth of the valley, the air began to glow — a soft, patient, gold light, like a lamp being carried by someone in no hurry at all — and Sera's hand closed hard on my shoulder, and she said, in a voice I'd never heard her use, a voice from before she was disgraced:

"Get Isandro. It found us. *It's found Wren.*"
