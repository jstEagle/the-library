## 14. Sera's Heresy

The Sanctuary of the Mapless smelled of woodsmoke and lentils and forty people who had decided, against all evidence, that they were not going anywhere.

I had lived in a shrine for eleven years. I knew what sanctity was supposed to smell like — incense, beeswax, the faint mineral cold of stone that had been prayed over so long it had given up being ordinary rock. This place smelled like supper. And yet when Wren fell asleep between a one-eyed ferryman and a woman who had been erased from three separate countries before she turned thirty, I felt something in my chest I had not felt since my ordination, and I resented it immediately, because I had been trained to recognize grace and this wasn't supposed to be it.

It was the stories that did it. Every night, after the meal, the commune gathered and told each other's histories aloud. Not their own — each other's. Marta recited the ferryman's crossing of the Ilse with the river frozen mid-thaw, and the ferryman recited how Marta's grandmother had smuggled seed potatoes in a false-bottomed coffin, and so on around the fire until every person present had been remembered by someone else out loud, in front of witnesses, as if memory were a legal document requiring countersignatures.

Which, I realized on the fourth night, was exactly what it was.

I want to be precise about the moment everything broke, because for years afterward — for whatever "afterward" means now — people asked me when I stopped believing, and I could never give them a clean answer. So: it was the fourth night, by the fire, when old Ferro told Wren's story.

Wren's story was very short. *This is Wren. Wren is eight. Wren remembers nothing, which means everything Wren does from tonight onward is the beginning of Wren.* That was all. The commune said it like a liturgy anyway, and Wren glowed like a lamp, and I felt my eyes sting, and then — because I am, at core, a very bad believer — my mind did the thing minds do when they are finally quiet. It started asking questions.

*Who owns that story?*

Ferro had just spoken Wren into continuity. By every doctrine I had ever sworn to, that act belonged to the gods. Memory was divine property, held in trust by the living, tithed back at death or taken earlier if the gods were hungry. I had taught that. I had taught children that. I had stood in my little shrine above the valley and told grieving mothers that their husbands' faces no longer belonged to them, that the gods had need of them, that grief was a form of rent.

And Ossa was coming for Wren because Wren's empty head was, in the theology I had memorized at fourteen, *available property.*

I got up from the fire without excusing myself, walked out past the goat pen, and threw up behind the woodpile.

* * *

"You're doing the face," Kestrel said, later.

I was sitting on the sanctuary's low wall in the dark, and Kestrel — because he had the spiritual sensitivity of a flint strike and the personal boundaries of a tick — had followed me out with two cups of something that claimed to be tea.

"I don't have a face."

"Everyone has a face. Yours is currently the one people make when they've found the body." He handed me a cup. "Isandro does it too. It's very annoying in two people."

I drank the not-tea. It tasted like hot regret. "Kestrel, when you were a boy — before your brother — did you go to shrine?"

He snorted. "Thornfell shrines don't take Salt Bridge boys. We paid our respects at crossroads stones and hoped the old ones weren't listening." He looked at me sideways. "Why."

"Because I need to know what someone outside the church thinks the doctrine says, and I need you to answer honestly, and you're incapable of flattering me."

"That's true," he agreed comfortably. "The doctrine says memories belong to the gods. Everyone knows that. Even the mapless know that; it's why they do the fire thing, saying each other's names — keeps the gods from collecting. You lot made up a loophole and the peasants found a better one." He shrugged. "Why?"

"Because 'you lot' is doing a great deal of work in that sentence," I said, "and because I've spent three days trying to find the loophole, and there isn't one."

I laid it out for him the way I'd once laid it out for novices, except backwards, inside-out, the way you lay out a corpse. The Doctrine of Trust: memory is lent to the living. The Rite of Return: at death, memory goes home to the god. The Tithe of Sorrows: in famine, plague, or judgment, the faithful surrender memories early, willingly, to feed the god who protects them. Every clause benign. Every clause reasonable. Every clause assuming that a soul's history was an object that could be owned, transferred, borrowed against — which meant that somewhere, in principle, there existed a legitimate counterparty to receive such transfers.

Ossa hadn't broken the doctrine. Ossa was the doctrine, grown teeth. A broker buys stolen memories from thieves like Isandro, feeds them upward through rites my own church wrote, and the god wakes fat on a nation's childhoods — and every priest who ever explained to a widow that her husband's smile now belonged to Something Else had been, technically, correct. Technically faithful. Technically an accessory.

"And here's the part I can't stop chewing," I said. "If memory belongs to gods, then Ossa has a claim on Wren. Not a metaphorical claim. A doctrinal one. Legal-spiritual, Isandro calls it. If I still believe what I swore—"

"Then you have to let it happen," Kestrel finished, flat. He turned his cup slowly. "Huh. My brother believed all that. Said the rites over his food every morning even after—" He stopped. He didn't talk about Tamon's eyes, the day they went vacant. "So what are you going to do?"

"I genuinely don't know," I said. "That's the problem. Everything I am is made of those vows. Take them out and there's nothing underneath. I'm not a person who became a priestess, Kestrel. Being a priestess ate the person."

He considered this with the same expression he used for assessing bridges. "You know what I'd say, if I were the sort to say soft things."

"You're not."

"No. But the mapless would say it. They'd say: you've got it backwards. Nobody eats the person. The person is what's left when everything else is eaten." He stood, stretched, and delivered his actual contribution to my crisis of faith: "Also, you're going to freeze out here, and Isandro will blame me, and I'll have to listen to a speech about it. Come inside."

I stayed on the wall a while longer anyway. Below, in the valley dark, Thornfell went on being legally nowhere. Somewhere north of us, a god wearing Isandro's face was walking, patient as winter. And somewhere inside me, a girl of fourteen knelt on cold shrine stones with her hands open, receiving the silence of her god as a mystery, never once asking the obvious question.

*I should have asked why the god was silent. I was trained to call silence holy.*

* * *

In the morning I asked Sefa — the commune's eldest, the woman of three erasures — whether they had a place where a person could make a formal statement. She looked at me for a long moment with her milky eyes, and then she said, "We have the fire," as though that settled anything, which apparently it did.

Word travels strangely in a commune. By midmorning everyone knew something was happening, though nobody knew what, and they arranged themselves around the cold fire pit with the unselfconscious solemnity of people who had watched regimes fall out of kitchen windows. Isandro stood near the back with Wren on a crate beside them, wearing the carefully bored expression Isandro wore whenever feelings threatened. Kestrel leaned on the doorpost of the cookhouse, arms folded, pretending not to attend.

Sefa nodded to me. I stepped into the fire pit, into the circle, where the ash was cold underfoot, and I discovered that eleven years of homiletics abandon you completely when you finally have something true to say.

"My name is Sera, daughter of Ilse-vale," I began, and stopped, because my voice cracked like a novice's. Fine. Let it crack. Cracking was honest. I started again.

"I was ordained at fourteen. I took four vows. I want to say them aloud, because I think you have to hear what you're unmaking before you unmake it, and because some of you may want to know exactly what kind of woman has been traveling with you.

"I vowed poverty, which I kept. I vowed obedience, which I kept until keeping it would have killed these people, and I told myself that breaking it was tragedy rather than relief, which was a lie. I vowed guardianship of the silent god's shrine, which burned, and I grieved it as a marriage grieves a house fire — wrongly, as it turns out, because the house was never the point.

"And I vowed the Doctrine of Trust. That all memory belongs to the gods, lent to the living, returned at death or surrendered in need. I taught it. I comforted the bereaved with it. When my god went silent, I assumed the fault was mine, because the doctrine says silence is always the fault of the faithless." I looked at Isandro. "Then I learned my god's memories had been stolen. Sold. Fed to something older. And I thought: the thief is the problem. Cut out the thief and the wound closes.

"But the thief only sold what the doctrine said was sellable. You cannot fence what nobody owns. Ossa did not corrupt my faith. Ossa *is* my faith — its logic carried to its end, a god built entirely out of ownership. And that doctrine says that Wren—" Wren went very still on the crate. "—that this child's emptiness is not innocence but vacancy. Available space. Inventory awaiting collection.

"I have searched for three days for a way to keep my vows and stand between that child and the god. There is none. The vows are the road the god is walking down. I helped pave it."

Marta, the potato-smuggler's granddaughter, said quietly, "Then stop paving."

"Yes," I said. "Here is the renunciation, and I want it witnessed, because I know some of you keep records in your heads, and I want this in somebody's head forever:

"I, Sera of Ilse-vale, renounce my vows entire. Poverty, obedience, guardianship, trust — undone, unsworn, returned like a stolen coat. I declare the Doctrine of Trust to be false from its first word. Memory does not belong to gods. It never did. Memory belongs to the living — to the one who made it and the ones who help carry it. It is not property. It is not tithe. It cannot be sold, bought, brokered, fed, or collected, and any power that claims otherwise is not a god but a thief wearing a god's face, and I have more standing than most to recognize the profession."

Kestrel made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

"Therefore," I said, and here my voice steadied, which surprised me, "I take a new vow, in this fire pit, before these witnesses. What the mapless already practice, what the peasant crossroads-stones already knew, what my church spent eleven centuries explaining away: that we keep each other. That memory is held in common by consent, given freely or not at all, and that the proper response to a god demanding what isn't given is to tell it no — loudly, in company, and forever if necessary. I will not pray to what owns. I will tell stories to what lives. That's my whole faith now. It fits in a fire pit. It doesn't need incense."

Silence. Long enough that I heard the goats.

Then Ferro, the one-eyed ferryman, cleared his throat and said, in the tone of a man announcing the next course, "*This is Sera. Sera was a priestess. Sera renounced it. Sera stays.*" And the whole commune answered, as they answered every night at the fire: *"Witnessed."*

I am not ashamed to say I sat down on the cold ash and put my face in my hands. Eleven years of identity, gone in nine sentences, and underneath it, instead of the nothing I had feared — ash, and people, and a child climbing off a crate to come sit pressed against my side, warm as a coal, asking, "Does that mean you'll tell me a story? Because Ferro tells mine wrong. He makes the nothing sound nice. It's not nice. It's scary."

"It is scary," I agreed, and put my arm around her. "I'll tell it right."

* * *

Liberation, it turns out, feels like an amputation that works. The limb is gone; the pain is real; and yet somehow you can move.

I spent the afternoon doing the small terrible arithmetic of my new poverty. No office. No rite. No god to bring my doubts to at night, which was, I confess, the hardest loss — not worship, exactly, but the habit of having somewhere to put things too heavy to hold. For eleven years, every fear had a recipient. Now the fears just sat in me, mine, undeliverable.

And yet. When Marta needed someone to hold the story of her grandmother's coffin while she was sick with fever, I held it, and gave it back to her whole when she woke. When Wren woke screaming from a dream — Wren's *first* dream, an event the entire commune treated with the gravity of a royal birth — it was my shoulder Wren cried on, and afterward Wren told me the dream three times to make sure it stuck, and I understood that this was prayer now. Receiving. Keeping. Returning.

Near dusk Isandro found me repointing the goat-pen wall, because a renounced priestess with no duties will invent them, and sat down on a stump with the air of someone about to discuss the weather and absolutely not about to do anything of the kind.

"So," Isandro said. "You're unemployed."

"Voluntarily destitute. It's traditional."

"Mm." A pause. Isandro turned a pebble over in those quick thief's fingers. "When we get to the Door. When Ossa catches us, because it will catch us. You know it's going to use me against you. My face. Maybe worse — maybe it'll offer you your god back, whole and loud, if you hand me over. I've been doing the arithmetic on what I'm worth to everyone in this company and I want you to know I've priced myself honestly."

"You haven't," I said. "You've priced yourself at zero and called it honesty. It's the oldest lie in the thief's confession, right after *no one got hurt.*"

Something crossed Isandro's face — the flicker of a person caught, quickly suppressed. "The point is—"

"The point is that I didn't burn my vows to trade one ownership for another," I said. "I don't follow gods and I don't follow Isandro Nakamura. I follow the vow I made this morning. Which means when Ossa wears your face and offers me miracles, I will tell it no, and I will not require your permission, and I will not accept your self-appraisal as scripture." I jabbed the trowel at the wall. "Hold this stone."

Isandro held the stone. After a moment: "Nobody's talked to me like that since my mother."

"She sounds wise."

"She sold me to a memory-broker," Isandro said, mildly, "so probably not, but thank you."

I sat back. The stone held. And that was when Wren came running up from the watch post, breathless, hair full of burrs, with the particular terrible brightness children get when they have seen something they cannot yet name.

"Sera. Sera, there's a light on the north road. Ferro says it's just a shepherd but Ferro's wrong, because shepherds don't glow." Wren grabbed my sleeve. "And it's walking like Isandro walks. Exactly like Isandro walks. Why would it walk like Isandro?"

Isandro had gone very still beside the half-built wall, and the evening had suddenly grown teeth.

"Wren," I said, taking the child's hand and rising, "go fetch Kestrel and Marta. Quickly, and don't run through the goats." Wren tore off toward the cookhouse. I looked north, where the light came on steady through the dusk, patient as winter, wearing a familiar stride.

"Well," I said. "It seems we're about to find out whether my new faith holds weight."

Isandro stood, dusting off their hands. "For what it's worth — I hope it does."

"So do I," I said. "Pray anyway. Old habits."

And we went down to meet the god.
