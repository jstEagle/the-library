## 15. The Door of Unmaking

The map Sera carried was not a map. It was a hymn.

She'd explained this three times on the climb, and each time I'd nodded the way you nod at a drunk uncle explaining his investment strategy — politely, while privately calculating how much rope we had left. But somewhere past the tree line, where Thornfell's erased roads gave up even pretending to exist and the mountain became pure argument, I understood what she meant. The way up wasn't marked in ink. It was marked in verses. *Where the shepherd's grief grows cold, turn east. Where the bell forgot its tongue, descend.* Every line described a place by what it had lost, which, given recent events, made it the most accurate cartography in the country.

"Left," Sera said, touching the page. "*Where the mother's name was washed from stone.*"

"There's no path."

"There is. It just doesn't remember itself yet." She started walking, and the snow — I swear this — arranged itself into footprints ahead of her. Old ones. Someone else's, decades gone, preserved like flies in amber because the mountain had nothing better to hold onto.

Wren walked between us, small hand fisted in my coat hem, breath steaming in little determined puffs. They hadn't spoken since dawn. Not frightened-quiet. Listening-quiet. Lately Wren did a lot of listening to things that weren't there, and lately the things that weren't there had stopped being subtle about it.

"They're singing again," Wren said finally.

"The gods?"

"No." A pause, the particular pause of an eight-year-old deciding whether adults can handle the truth. "The door."

Kestrel, bringing up the rear with his crossbow slung and his face doing its usual impression of a man who has never once been glad to be anywhere, snorted. "Doors don't sing."

"This one does," Wren said.

And the terrible thing — the thing I'd been swallowing for two days like a stone that kept growing — was that I could hear it too. Faint. Under the wind. A note held so long it stopped being sound and became pressure, the way grief becomes pressure, the way debt becomes pressure. We were walking toward something that knew we were coming.

*Good*, said the memory in my pocket. My own childhood, stolen back from a broker's vault, wrapped in oilcloth against my ribs where it had grown from the weight of a coin purse to the weight of a sleeping cat to the weight of a sleeping ox. *Hurry. You've missed so much.*

"Ignore it," I muttered.

"I didn't say anything," Kestrel said.

"Not you."

* * *

We came over the shoulder of the mountain at dusk, and every one of us stopped at once, which never happens. Groups don't stop in unison unless something has reached out and put its hand flat on all their chests simultaneously.

The Door of Unmaking stood in a cleft of black rock, and it was not a door the way a thing is a door. It was more like the idea of a door that had gotten tired of waiting for a wall and simply decided to stand alone. Tall as a chapel. No frame, no hinges, no handle. Just a standing surface of grey stone shot through with veins of light that moved like slow water under ice — or like memories, surfacing and sinking, surfacing and sinking.

The ground before it was bare earth, swept clean by centuries of wind, except for one thing: offerings. Not coins, not flowers. Objects. A child's shoe. A wedding ring fused to a scrap of veil. A soldier's letter, opened and re-folded so many times the paper had gone soft as cloth. Hundreds of them, arranged with a care that hurt to look at, each one placed by someone who had chosen not to pass through after all — or who had passed through and left behind the part of themselves they couldn't carry.

Or couldn't bear to lose, depending on how you read the old stories. The stories disagreed. Stories usually do. That's how you know they're alive.

"It's real," Sera whispered. She'd spent her whole life serving a silent god inside a church that taught the Door was metaphor. Allegory. A nice thought for harvest festivals. Her hands were shaking, and she pressed them together like a woman holding a bird she wasn't sure she wanted to keep. "It's actually—"

"Yeah," I said. "It's actually."

Kestrel set down his pack with exaggerated care, the way he did everything when he was terrified, which was always. "So we knock?"

"You don't knock," Wren said. Everyone looked at them. Wren shrugged, eight years old and ancient. "You pay."

* * *

We camped within sight of it, because none of us could make ourselves turn our backs on the thing, and because the light it shed was warmer than any fire we could build. It didn't flicker. It breathed.

I took first watch, mostly because I wanted time alone with my pocket.

The oilcloth bundle sat in my lap, and I let myself feel it properly for the first time since Vetch. My childhood. Seven years of a person I used to be, wrapped in waxed linen, humming against my ribs like a second heart that resented me. Somewhere in there was a kitchen. A mother's voice, probably. The reason I ran. The reason I'd built myself out of other people's leftovers, because mine had been sold off piece by piece to keep me fed, and the last piece — the one I swore on every dead god's name I'd never give back — was this one.

Ossa wore my face now. Claimed my habits, my skills, my loves, itemized like a ledger. And the only way to break that claim, the only currency the Door would take that Ossa couldn't touch—

No. Not yet. There had to be another way. There was always another way; that was the whole religion of being a thief.

"You're going to do it," Sera said quietly. She'd come to sit beside me without making a sound, which for a former priestess of a silent god was apparently a professional skill. "Pay with yourself."

"I'm going to sleep. Eventually. After I finish this watch."

"Isandro."

"Sera."

She looked at the Door. Its light moved across her face, and for a moment I saw her young — younger than she'd ever let herself be, some novice kneeling in a shrine that still had a roof. "My whole life, I was taught that memory belongs to the gods. That we are stewards, not owners. That forgetting is holy." Her jaw tightened. "I renounced that doctrine in front of a burning wayshrine and it was the easiest prayer I ever said. So believe me when I tell you: your past is not a tithe. It's not owed. Whatever you decide at that door, decide it as a free person, not a debtor."

"That's very inspiring. Write it down. I'll sell it."

She almost smiled. Then: "Wren asked me what happens if you go through and forget us."

The question landed harder than any blade had all journey. I looked across the fire to where Wren slept curled against Kestrel's pack, and Kestrel sat propped against a stone pretending to sleep but clearly listening, because Kestrel always listened, betrayal being a skill that improves the hearing.

"What did you tell them?"

"The truth. That no one knows. That the Door takes what you cannot bear to lose, and gives back—" she gestured vaguely at the world beyond the cleft, the south, the elsewhere, "—whatever's left."

"And what's left, Sera? If it takes everything?"

"Then you're new," she said. "And the people who love you introduce themselves."

* * *

Morning came grey and cold, and we stood before the Door like four people at a graveside who couldn't agree whose grave it was.

Kestrel went first. Of course he did. Kestrel did everything first when it involved pain, because waiting was worse than bleeding and he'd told me once that the only discipline his father taught him was how to flinch forward instead of back.

He walked up to the stone and it — responded. The veins of light gathered toward him, curious, hungry, and I felt the pressure of its attention like a hand on the back of my neck. When Kestrel spoke, his voice came out scraped raw.

"My brother's name was Tam." He swallowed. "They took his mind, years ago. A heist gone wrong. My fault. His memories were sold upward — to your kind of customers." The light pulsed. "I want to trade. His grief. All of it. Everything he lost and everything it cost him. Take it, and let him wake up light."

The Door was silent a long moment. Long enough that Kestrel's shoulders began to shake, long enough that Wren reached out and took his sleeve.

Then the light moved through him — I watched it happen, a slow silver tide passing through his body like water through a net — and Kestrel made a sound I will never repeat to anyone, not under torture, not drunk, not dying. Half sob, half relief, entirely private. When it passed, he stood straighter than I'd ever seen him stand.

"It didn't take Tam," he said hoarsely, turning back to us. His eyes were wet and astonished. "It took the *weight*. He'll still remember. But it won't—" He laughed, cracked and disbelieving. "It won't crush him anymore."

The Door's surface rippled, and a seam appeared. Thin as a hair. An invitation.

Kestrel looked at it. Looked at us. "Go on, then. I'll wait for you lot. Somebody has to guard the exit. It's the only job I'm qualified for."

Sera went next.

She paid differently. She knelt before the Door like a communicant and offered it her vows — twenty years of certainty, of liturgy, of belonging to something larger that turned out to be a cage with good lighting. The Door took them gently, the way you'd take a heavy coat off someone's shoulders, and Sera rose lighter and visibly unsure of who she was without them, and visibly willing to find out.

That left Wren.

Wren stood before the Door, and the Door — I swear this, and I don't swear often — *leaned toward them*. The light in its veins surged, all of it, every current pulling toward that small empty figure like rivers finding the sea. Because that was the truth we'd been running from since the emptied village: Wren's mind wasn't just blank. It was *vacant*. The last vessel. The thing Ossa needed to pour a nation's stolen past into and become something no one could unmake.

"No," I said. Out loud. To a door.

The Door regarded me. If stone can be patient, it was. If stone can be hungry, it was that too.

"They have nothing to pay," Sera said sharply, stepping in front of Wren. "There's nothing to take."

The light dimmed. Considered. And then, horribly, it began to show things — images rising in its surface like fish coming up to feed. Wren's future. A life. A name learned, a trade, a first love, gray hair. Everything Wren could become, laid out like goods at market. And beside it, the price: all of it. Pay with the whole unwritten book. Walk through as no one, and become someone on the far side, or stay here and be filled.

Wren looked at those images for a long time. Then they looked up at me, and their face did something no eight-year-old's face should know how to do.

"If I pay," Wren said, "will you still be there? On the other side? Will you know me?"

"Wren—"

"Because I don't have anything else. I checked. I checked and checked." Their chin trembled and their voice didn't. "But if you're there, then I'm not paying with nothing. Am I. I'm paying with the part that knows you."

Something in my chest tore along a seam I hadn't known was there.

And that's when I felt it — not heard, *felt* — the memory against my ribs stop whispering and start *singing*, my own childhood, seven lost years, the exact weight of everything I could not bear to lose, and the Door's light swung toward me like a lighthouse finding a shipwreck, and through the cleft behind us, down the impossible road, came the sound of riders. Many riders. And beneath the hoofbeats, a voice wearing my own voice, calling my name with the intimacy of a creditor.

Ossa had arrived. And the Door was open exactly one payment wide.

"Get behind me," I said to Wren, and reached for the oilcloth.
