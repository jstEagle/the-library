## 16. Kestrel's Bargain

I'd faced down inquisition riders with a knife between my teeth and a lie on my tongue. I'd walked into Vetch's memory-markets knowing half the brokers there wanted me dead and the other half wanted my hands. I'd watched my brother forget my name over the course of a single winter and kept smiling through it, because that's what you do.

None of it prepared me for a door that wanted to talk.

It stood at the end of a valley that wasn't on any map — which, given that Thornfell itself had been struck from every map in creation, felt almost redundant. Grey stone, no hinges, no handle, no seam. Just a lintel carved with words so old they'd worn smooth, and a presence behind them like a held breath.

Sera called it holy. Isandro called it a tollbooth with delusions of grandeur. Wren called it *the quiet place*, which was somehow worse than either.

Me? I stood in front of it for a long time before anyone noticed I hadn't moved, because I already knew what it was going to ask me for.

* * *

We'd made camp in the valley's mouth the night before, under a sky so full of stars it looked like someone had spilled the whole treasury. The gods' hunger had been chasing us for days — you could feel it now like weather, a pressure behind the eyes, a whispering at the edge of hearing that resolved, if you let it, into fragments of other people's lives. A wedding. A funeral. A woman learning to swim. The old gods were gorging themselves on Thornfell's past, and the leftovers sloshed around in the air like bilge water.

Kestrel, don't think about it, I told myself. Thinking about memories around these things is like bleeding around sharks.

Wren sat by the fire drawing shapes in the ash. The kid still remembered nothing — not their name before we gave them one, not the village we found them in, not the faces of whatever parents had left them behind. And yet Wren slept easier than any of us. There's a kind of peace in an empty room, I suppose. No ghosts rattling around in there.

Isandro was sharpening a knife that didn't need sharpening, which is what they did instead of talking about feelings. Sera was praying, quietly, in the way she did now — not asking for anything, just talking, like letters to a friend who'd stopped writing back.

And I was looking at the fire and doing arithmetic I'd been avoiding for three hundred miles.

The door took what you couldn't bear to lose. That was the legend, anyway, and everything we'd seen since reaching the valley suggested the legends here were less decoration than instruction manual. Sera had been circling it all evening like she was working up to something. Isandro had that look — the one where they were carrying something heavy in their pocket and pretending it was lint.

Me, I knew exactly what I couldn't bear to lose. It just happened to be something I'd already lost, which made the whole thing either very simple or very cruel, depending on how you looked at it.

"Kestrel." Sera's voice, soft. "You've been staring at that fire like it owes you money."

"It does," I said. "Everything owes me money."

She sat down beside me. She'd gotten good at that lately — sitting next to people without demanding they perform their souls. Disgraced priesthood had apparently taught her useful skills after all.

"You're thinking about him," she said.

"I'm always thinking about him. That's the problem. One of us should get to stop."

Tam. My brother Tam, who'd had a laugh like a cart going downhill and a memory like a steel trap, until a heist gone wrong cracked the trap open and everything spilled out. My fault. My plan, my miscalculation, my hand on his shoulder when the memory-thief's backlash hit. He'd spent six years since then in our aunt's house in the lowlands, pleasant as milk, recognizing nothing, grieving nothing — because you can't grieve what you can't remember losing.

Except he did grieve. That was the thing nobody understood. The mind forgets; the body keeps the books. He'd stand at windows for hours. He cried some evenings without knowing why, and when our aunt asked what was wrong, he'd say *nothing*, and mean it, and be wrong.

When I'd traced the stolen memories upward — through fences and brokers and finally to something divine — I'd hoped, stupidly, that I could buy him back. Restore him. Make it right.

Then I'd seen what the gods did with what they ate. They didn't restore anything. They wore it. They turned a man's wedding day into a weapon and his mother's lullaby into a leash. Giving Tam's memories back through *that* channel wouldn't heal him. It would hollow him out and fill the hole with god.

So. Arithmetic.

"Whatever you're planning," Sera said quietly, "don't do it alone."

"That's rich, coming from a priestess. Isn't suffering alone sort of your brand?"

"Our brand was *silence*," she said. "I'm retired." She put her hand over mine, brief and warm, and went back to her prayers, and I sat there hating her a little for being kind, because kindness makes it harder to keep your armor buckled.

* * *

Morning came grey and cold, and the door was waiting, and one by one we approached it.

Not all at once. The door had opinions about crowds. Sera went first, jaw set like a woman walking to her own trial, and when she came back twenty minutes later her face was wet and her shoulders were lighter, and she said only, "It listens. Really listens. Be careful what you confess." Then she walked off down the valley to be alone, which I respected deeply.

Wren went next, holding Isandro's hand, and came back puzzled and unburdened. "It asked what I loved," Wren reported, "and I said *them*, and pointed, and it made a sound like a bell being pleased." Kids. They make the impossible sound like a Tuesday.

That left Isandro and me standing in front of a slab of ancient stone, both of us carrying things we hadn't shown each other yet.

"After you," I said.

"Don't trust me?"

"I trust you exactly as far as I can throw you, Nakamura, and you're heavier than you look." But my heart wasn't in it, and they knew it, and they touched my arm — actually touched it, deliberately, which for Isandro was roughly equivalent to a marriage proposal — and then they stepped forward and the door took them somewhere, or took something from them, and they came back looking like a person who'd set down a load they'd forgotten they were carrying.

They didn't tell me what they'd paid. I didn't ask. Thieves' etiquette: never inventory another thief's pockets.

Then it was me.

I walked up to the door and it was warm, which I hadn't expected, and it hummed against my palm like a sleeping animal, and a voice that wasn't a voice said, in the space behind my teeth:

***What cannot you bear to lose?***

And here's the thing. Here's the whole rotten beautiful thing.

I opened my mouth to say *my brother*, because that was the true answer, the obvious answer, the answer I'd been carrying across a country that legally didn't exist. I can't bear to lose Tam. I lost him years ago and I've been dragging the loss around ever since like a ball and chain, feeding it, watering it, letting it grow roots in every plan I made and every road I walked. Revenge against the thief who broke him. Debt against myself. Every choice for six years had been shaped around that absence, the way water shapes itself around a stone.

But the door didn't want the obvious answer. The door listened — really listened, like Sera said — and what it heard underneath my answer was the truth, and the truth was this:

*I can't bear for him to keep hurting.*

Because restoring Tam's memories — even cleanly, even without gods involved — would have meant giving him back the grief. The full weight of it. Six years of stolen time crashing down at once, plus the original wound, plus the knowledge that his own brother had caused it and then spent those same six years hunting restitution instead of visiting. I'd told myself restoration was mercy. It wasn't. It was me wanting my brother back, wanting my guilt absolved, wanting the world rearranged so I didn't have to live with what I'd done.

The door offered me another shape.

Give it the memories. Not to the gods — the door was older than the gods, hungrier in a different direction, a mouth that closed rather than opened. Give it Tam's stolen past, all of it, the wedding and the laugh and the winter of forgetting and the grief itself. Let the door swallow the pain whole. Tam would never remember what he lost — but neither would he ache for it anymore. The body's ledger would finally balance. He'd wake tomorrow light.

And I would lose him forever. Not to death, which at least leaves a grave to visit. To peace. He'd be happy, and whole, and mine no longer — because a brother is partly made of shared memory, and I'd be giving away every thread that tied us.

The door waited. Doors are patient. It's people who aren't.

"Yeah," I said, out loud, to a rock, like a madman. "Yeah, okay. Take it. Take all of it. Not for me — for him. He doesn't get a vote, so I'm casting it, and I'm casting it for *mercy*, not for *me*. You hear me? Whatever you are. Make sure it hurts him less. That's the price. That's the only term."

The hum deepened. Warmth spread up my arm, into my chest, and I braced for it to hurt.

It didn't hurt. That was the worst part. It felt like setting down luggage I'd carried so long my spine had grown around it. It felt like the morning after a fever breaks. Somewhere very far away, I felt — I swear I felt — a man in a lowland house stop mid-pace by a window, blink, and smile at nothing, at sunlight, at the simple fact of being alive and unhaunted.

My knees went out from under me. I sat down hard in the frost-grass and laughed, or sobbed, or did that ugly thing in between that people do, and the door hummed above me like it was satisfied, like it had heard ten thousand such bargains and honored every honest one.

* * *

Isandro found me there. Of course they did. They have a talent for finding people at their worst moments; I think it's a professional courtesy among thieves.

"So," they said, crouching beside me. "You paid."

"Paid in full. No refunds."

"What did it take?"

I looked at them. Isandro Nakamura, who'd spent this whole miserable pilgrimage pretending not to care about any of us, watching me with those careful eyes, and I realized I could lie to them. It would be easy. It's always easy.

"He'll be free," is what I said instead. "My brother. He won't remember me, or the heist, or any of it — but he won't hurt anymore either. That was the trade. Grief for grief. I figured somebody in our family should finally get out of debt."

Isandro was quiet for a long moment. Then: "That's the dumbest, bravest thing I've ever heard, and I once watched a man fight a bear to win back a hat."

"The hat was iconic."

"Kestrel." They said my name carefully, like handling something breakable. "For what it's worth — and I know it isn't worth much coming from me — he'd have chosen it too. If he'd been able to choose."

"That's the thing," I said, and my voice cracked straight down the middle. "He never got to choose anything, after what I did. So I chose for him. Last time. I promise you, last time." I wiped my face with my sleeve, rough, businesslike, done. "Anyway. That's my ledger closed. Clean slate. Which means I've got nothing left to lose, Nakamura, and in my experience that's when I get dangerous."

Something flickered across Isandro's face then — a shadow, quick as a pickpocket. They glanced toward the valley's mouth, where the air had begun, very faintly, to shimmer. Not weather. Not heat-haze. Something approaching along roads that no longer existed, wearing a pressure we'd all come to dread.

Sera appeared at my elbow, pale. "Ossa," she said. "It's found us. And it hasn't come alone."

Down the valley, between the grey stones, the first white cloaks of the inquisition caught the light like a row of pulled knives — and ahead of them, wearing a face I recognized from campfires and card games and three hundred miles of grudging loyalty, walked a god.

Isandro's face. Smiling with Isandro's mouth.

"Well," I said, and reached for my knife out of pure habit, though we both knew steel wouldn't matter now. "Clean slate lasted what, four minutes? New record."

The god raised Isandro's hand in greeting, friendly as a merchant, and the valley began to fill with the sound of our own names.
