## 17. Ossa at the Gate

The Door of Unmaking looked, in the end, like a door.

I had spent three weeks imagining it as something grander — a wound in the world, a ring of standing stones humming with the names of the forgotten, a hole shaped exactly like everything I'd ever lost. Instead it was set into the cliffside above the Ashen Vale like a cellar entrance: grey stone, grey frame, no handle, no hinges, no keyhole. Just a seam in the rock so straight and so patient that your eyes kept sliding off it, the way they slide off a stranger's face in a crowd.

Sera touched it once and pulled her hand back as if burned.

"It's not locked," she said. "It's *waiting*."

"Everything worth avoiding is," Kestrel said.

We had come up the last of the mapless roads by night, Wren asleep against my back the whole climb, a warm dead weight that I had stopped resenting somewhere around day nine and started needing somewhere around day twelve. Nobody said that out loud. Found families are built on things nobody says out loud.

Wren woke when we stopped, slid down, and stood in front of the door with their head tilted, the way they did with everything new.

"It's empty," Wren said.

"Doors usually are," Kestrel said.

"No." Wren pressed one small palm flat against the stone. "Inside. It's empty like me."

That should have frightened us more than it did. But we were four days past the commune, full of other people's stories, running on bread and stubbornness, and there is a kind of tired that makes even horror feel like weather. Sera began unpacking the ritual texts she'd renounced and then, in a move she refused to discuss, stolen from her own burning shrine anyway. Kestrel checked the sightlines. I stood behind Wren with my hand on their shoulder and let myself believe — for one entire, stupid minute — that we had made it.

That was when the valley below lit up with torches.

* * *

There were forty of them, maybe fifty, riders in the grey-and-silver of the resurrected Inquisition, fanning out across the vale floor with the drilled precision of people who have never once wondered whether they're the bad guys. Their banners were new; their doctrine was old. Somewhere down there a horn sounded, low and flat, the note they used for heretics and tax evaders and, lately, anyone whose paperwork hadn't survived the erasure of an entire country.

"They can't have followed us," Kestrel said. "Those roads don't exist."

"We don't exist either," I said. "And here we are."

He grunted. It was the closest he came to admitting I'd made a point.

Sera stepped to the cliff edge, and I watched her do the thing she did now instead of praying: she looked at the riders and named them. "They're not searching for us. They came straight here. They knew where the Door is." Her jaw tightened. "Someone told them."

"Or something," I said, because I had felt it coming for two days — a pressure behind my eyes, a familiarity in the wind, the specific sensation of being *remembered* by something that shouldn't have a memory to remember me with.

Wren's hand found mine. Small, cold, certain.

"He's here," Wren said.

And the torchlight below went out — all of it, every flame in the valley, forty fires snuffed in the same instant, not blown but *taken*, the way you take a coin off a table — and in the dark that followed, something began to walk up the switchback road toward us without hurrying, because nothing that owns time ever hurries.

Ossa arrived wearing my face.

I will never get used to writing that sentence, and I hope I never have to live it again. It came up the last stretch of road in a body assembled from borrowed light and older dark, and the face it wore was mine — not the face I see in mirrors, which lies professionally, but the face underneath, the one I'd forgotten I had, the one I sold. It smiled with my mouth. It tilted its head with my habit, my tell, the little leftward lean I do when I'm deciding how much truth a situation can afford.

"Thief," it said, in my voice, pitched down into something geological. "You brought my vessel to my door."

"Wren isn't yours," Sera said, stepping forward, arms spread — the old priestess stance, repurposed, weaponized. "Nothing is yours. That's what the Door is *for*."

Ossa didn't even look at her. Gods are rude that way. Its eyes stayed on me, and looking into them was like looking into a room in my own house where someone else had rearranged the furniture.

"You've been busy," it said. "You found the broker. You learned what you sold." It smiled again, gently, horribly. "Do you know what I admire about you, Isandro Nakamura? You survive on other people's pasts, and yet you never once asked the only question that mattered: *why were you willing to sell your own?*"

"I needed the coin."

"You needed to forget." The god climbed the last step and stopped ten feet away, close enough that I could smell it — woodsmoke and my mother's kitchen and the specific dust of a house I couldn't picture. Behind it, the inquisition riders remounted the switchbacks in silence, torches relit, forming a crescent around the plateau. No escape down. There had never been any escape down. "And I have been keeping it safe for you. Every year of it. The house. The river. The night you ran. I have carried your childhood like a lantern through all this hungry dark, waiting to give it back."

My hands were shaking. I put them in my pockets, where the ghost of a dying woman's happiest memory still whispered prayers that answered themselves, and I made my voice come out level.

"What's it cost?"

"There it is," Ossa said warmly. "There's my thief. Always checking the price before the goods." It turned, at last, and regarded Wren — and I moved without deciding to, putting myself between them, and the god laughed with my laugh and let me. "No harm. No harm at all. Only this: the gods are waking, and waking requires vessels. Your little friend is the finest ever made — a mind with nothing in it, a cup that has never been poured full. Give Wren to us, freely, and I return your childhood whole. Every memory. Every year. You will know why you fled Thornfell. You will know your mother's voice. You will know *yourself* — the self you have been renting rooms in your whole life without ever meeting the landlord."

The vale was very quiet. Even the wind had the decency to hold its breath.

"And if I refuse?" I asked, though I already knew, because I'd been the one holding the ledger in a hundred smuggler dens, and I knew every way a deal turns into a threat.

"Then we take Thornfell's remaining past, and the inquisition takes you, and the child comes to us eventually anyway — screaming, or empty, it makes no difference to a god. Whereas—" Ossa spread my hands, magnanimous, "—whereas if you say yes, everyone walks away. Your priestess keeps her new heresy. The rival keeps his lightened brother. And you keep—" it leaned closer, and its breath smelled like the inside of my own skull, "—everything you swore never to lose. Everything you built this whole miserable pilgrimage to protect. *Yourself.*"

* * *

Here is the thing nobody tells you about temptation: it doesn't arrive looking like a devil. It arrives looking like an invoice already paid.

I stood there and I *felt* it — the deal settling over me like a coat cut perfectly to my measurements. All I had to do was hand over a child who wasn't mine, whom I'd known for less than a season, whom I'd told myself a dozen times on the road was cargo, a liability, a stray I'd regret picking up. All I had to do was become whole. I could almost taste it: my mother's name, the reason for the running, the shape of the child I had been before I taught myself to be no one. Twenty years of walking around a hole in my own center, and the god was offering to fill it, free, in exchange for a single transaction — the only kind of transaction I'd ever been good at.

And Wren stood behind me, holding the hem of my coat, saying nothing, because Wren trusted me, and trust, I have since learned, is the heaviest thing a person can carry without knowing its weight.

"You're doing the arithmetic," Ossa observed. "I can hear it. One child against four lives. One memory against a nation's. You've made colder trades for warmer meals."

"That's true," I said.

Its smile widened. My smile. I hated it on my face.

"But here's your error," I went on, and my voice cracked in the middle, and I let it, because some things should crack. "You think I'm a thief because I take things. That's not the trade. Any crow can take. A thief is someone who decides what things are *worth* — and you just priced a person against a memory, and that's how I know you were never a god at all. You're just a hoarder with good lighting."

Something flickered across my own face — not anger. Hunger. Worse.

"The child," Ossa said, and the warmth drained out of my voice until what remained was the sound a glacier makes, "is the last vessel. Without it, we wake half-formed, and half-formed gods devour blindly. Give it to us and be remembered forever as the one who fed the dawn. Refuse, and be forgotten with everything else." It straightened. Behind it, fifty riders drew steel in one motion, a sound like the country itself clearing its throat. "Choose, thief. The Door opens for payment, and I am prepared to pay *you*."

"I have a counteroffer," I said.

"Isandro," Sera said quietly, warningly.

"It's a short one." I reached back and put my hand on Wren's head, and Wren leaned into it, and I looked the god in my own eyes and lied to it the way I had never managed to lie to anyone I loved: steadily. "You can go to hell, and take my childhood with you. It's clearly been keeping bad company."

For a moment, nothing. The wind resumed. Somewhere below, a horse shifted.

Then Ossa laughed — really laughed, my laugh, the ugly honking one I do when something actually gets me — and the laugh curdled halfway through into something vast and disappointed, and the temperature of the whole plateau dropped as though the valley had exhaled.

"Pity," it said. "I had hoped to buy. But I was born from theft, thief. Buying was always the courtesy. Taking is the trade."

The riders came up the slope at a walk, then a trot, then a run, and Sera was shouting something about the Door, and Kestrel was swearing in three dialects and reaching for his knives, and Wren's grip on my coat tightened to a fist —

— and the seam in the grey stone began, very softly, to glow.

Not bright. Not dramatic. Just a thin line of light along the Door's impossible edge, the color of the moment before you remember something, and a voice that was not a voice said, in the space behind all our ribs:

***What will you pay?***

Ossa stopped. For the first time since it had climbed out of whatever pit gods crawl from, it looked uncertain — my face, wearing doubt badly, like a borrowed coat two sizes wrong.

"No," it said. "No. Not yet. They haven't chosen. The terms are *mine*—"

"Terms," said the Door, in the voice of every memory anyone had ever given away, "are for sellers. This is a door. It only knows gifts."

And I understood, all at once, standing between a god and a child with fifty blades coming up the hill, what the Door had been waiting for. Not payment. Not surrender. It wanted what none of us had ever been offered and none of us knew how to ask for.

It wanted someone to give something away *freely* — and I had nothing left that was mine.

Except, I realized, turning to look at the ragged, furious, impossible people beside me —

maybe I wasn't the only one who got to decide what I was worth.
