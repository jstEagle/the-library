## 18. The Refusal

### Isandro

The Door of Unmaking stood in a valley that had no name because names were the first thing it ate.

I want to be clear about that: we didn't find the Door. The Door permitted us to arrive. There's a difference, and the difference is the kind of thing you feel in your teeth. For three days we had walked through country that grew less real with every mile — roads softening into suggestion, trees going gray at the edges like old photographs — and then the valley opened and there it was: a slab of black stone standing alone in the grass, no frame, no wall, no hinge. Just a door, upright, patient, utterly indifferent to the fact that nothing stood behind it.

Sera knelt. Of course she did. She'd been kneeling to things her whole life; at least this one had the courtesy to actually exist.

Wren asked the obvious question, because Wren always did. "What's behind it?"

"Nothing," I said. "That's rather the point."

"Nothing behind a door is just a wall."

I looked at the child. Eight years old, empty as a winter field, and sharper than anyone I'd robbed in twenty years of thieving. "It's not a door *into* somewhere, little one. It's a door *out*. Out of Thornfell. Out of the reach of gods who think your head is their pantry." I crouched, put us eye to eye. "But it doesn't open for coin or blood or prayer. It opens for payment. And the price isn't things."

Kestrel spat, which was his version of amen. "What, then?"

"What you cannot bear to lose," Sera said quietly. She'd read every fragment of scripture the burning shrines had left behind, and some of them had apparently survived the fire along with her faith. "The door takes what you hold tightest. That's the toll. That's the whole theology of it, if it has one. You walk through lighter than you came, and what you leave behind stays here, in a country that's being eaten anyway."

We all went quiet after that, the way people do when they're privately inventorying themselves.

Because here's what none of us said out loud: I already knew what I'd pay. I'd known since Vetch, since the broker's mask came off and I learned that my own childhood sat in a god's gullet like a swallowed stone. Ossa wanted Wren. Ossa had offered me my past back, whole and warm, in exchange for an eight-year-old with nothing inside them but room. And the Door would take anything I gave it — including, I suspected, exactly the thing Ossa held over me.

Give the Door my childhood. Break Ossa's claim by paying it away before the god could trade on it. Walk out of Thornfell hollow, but walk out with Wren.

Simple. Clean. Terrible.

I'd have done it that night if the others hadn't been watching. Which, I've since decided, is the entire purpose of other people.

* * *

### Sera

I have knelt before altars since I was six years old. I know the architecture of surrender from the inside — the way you arrange your body into an offering, the way you learn to call your own smallness devotion. Twenty years of it. And in all that time, no god ever asked me for something I could see. They took prayers, incense, obedience. Abstractions. It's easy to give away what you can't hold.

So when I understood what Isandro meant to do — and I understood it the way you understand weather, before the clouds even gather — I was furious in a way that felt almost holy.

They meant to pay the Door with themselves. Not with their crimes, not with their skills, not with any of the sharp useful pieces. With the childhood they'd only just learned they'd lost. They meant to hand the last proof that they had ever been a person — a small frightened person who ran from something terrible — to a slab of rock, so that a child they'd known for a season could walk out of a dying country.

And they meant to do it without telling anyone. Without letting anyone stop them. Without, I realized with a clarity that burned worse than my shrine had, ever once considering that they might be worth saving too.

I found them at the fire that night, sitting apart, doing that thing they do where they pretend to sharpen a knife that is already sharp.

"You're going to pay it yourself," I said.

Isandro didn't look up. "You should sleep, priestess."

"Don't." My voice cracked on it. I let it crack. Renouncing vows teaches you that some cracks are doors. "Don't do the thing where you make yourself small and call it strategy. I spent two decades watching men do that from pulpits. I know the posture."

Now they looked up. In the firelight their face was all angles and exhaustion, and underneath the exhaustion, something young. Something that had been waiting twenty years for someone to notice it.

"Sera—"

"I renounced my god," I said, "because he believed memory belonged to him. To the divine. To anything bigger and older and hungrier than the person living it. And I will not stand in this valley and watch you perform the same heresy against yourself." I sat down across the fire from them, deliberately, the way you plant a flag. "So hear me. If the Door wants a piece of you, it can take a piece of me instead. Freely given. That's the difference between us and Ossa, Isandro. Everything the god takes, it takes. What I'm offering, I offer."

"That's not how the toll works—"

"We don't know how the toll works. No one does. Everyone who ever wrote about it was guessing, and most of them were priests, and I am professionally qualified to say that half of theology is confident guessing." I pulled the cord from around my neck — the little knot of keepsakes I'd carried since the shrine burned, ash-stained cloth, a splinter of altar wood. "But I know this. A gift given freely weighs nothing on the giver. Let's find out what it weighs on a door."

For a long moment Isandro said nothing. Then, very quietly, in a voice I suspect they hadn't used since childhood:

"Why? You hated me when you found me. You cornered me in a den full of cutthroats to tell me so."

"I did hate you." I smiled, and it hurt, and I let it hurt. "Then I watched you carry a sleeping child four miles through inquisition territory because putting them down would wake them. People are allowed to change, Nakamura. It's the one doctrine I still believe."

### Wren

I don't remember things. That's the whole shape of me. Other people are made of yesterdays, stacked up like stones, and I'm made of now, and now, and now. Sera says that's not emptiness. Sera says it's a room with the windows open. But rooms with open windows get cold, and sometimes at night I feel the old gods circling outside me like wolves smell a house with no fire in it.

Isandro taught me the mountains. That's mine now. Not remembered — kept. There's a difference. Remembering is having something happen to you. Keeping is choosing to hold it. Every morning I keep the mountains again, on purpose, the way you'd keep a bird that might fly off.

And tonight everyone was being strange and sad around the fire, and I don't have yesterdays to hide in, so I just walked over and sat down in the middle of it.

"Isandro's going to give the door their whole self," I announced, because nobody else was saying it out loud and secrets rot when you leave them in the dark. "Aren't you."

The silence got very loud. Kestrel choked on whatever he was chewing. Sera closed her eyes like she was praying, except she told me she doesn't pray anymore, so maybe she was counting.

"Wren," Isandro started.

"It's a bad plan," I said. "I'm eight and even I can tell it's a bad plan. If you give the door everything, there won't be enough of you left to walk through. You'll go out the other side like smoke." I climbed onto the log beside them, close, because cold rooms need fires and I've decided Isandro is one of mine. "So I'm giving you some of me."

"You don't *have*—" Kestrel began.

"I have *now*," I said. "I have lots of nows. Nobody wants those, so they're cheap, but they're mine." I took Isandro's hand — their knife-hand, the dangerous one — and pressed it flat between both of mine, the way Sera does when she's serious. "I give you the morning the mountains came over the ridge and you said *there*, like the word was a place you'd been looking for. I give you the taste of the honey bread in Vetch, the burnt edge, you ate the burnt edge and pretended you liked it better. I give you the song you hum when you think we're asleep. All freely given. Take them to the door. Tell it a child paid part of your toll, and see if it dares argue with me."

Nobody laughed. But something happened to the air around the fire — it loosened, the way a knot loosens — and I felt the nows leave me gently, like birds off a wire, and I felt them land somewhere warm.

Isandro's eyes were wet. I didn't say anything about it. Some things you keep by not keeping them.

* * *

### Kestrel

I'll tell you what I know about debts, since I've spent eleven years collecting them: a debt unpaid doesn't sit still. It grows interest. It gets into your blood. My brother Tam went empty because of a heist I planned, and I have carried that hole in him like a stone in my boot for a decade, and when I finally traced the theft upward — past brokers, past fences, past every dirty rung of the ladder — I found it had been sold to something divine, and I understood that some debts don't get settled. They get *transferred*. Upward. Forever.

Ossa holds my brother's mind the way a landlord holds a deed.

So understand me when I say I know exactly what it costs to consider giving a piece of yourself to a power that eats. I stood at the Salt Bridge with a blade at Isandro's throat ready to take repayment in flesh, and now here I was, three weeks later, about to do the single stupidest generous thing of my life.

"Oi." I waited until the brat finished their speech and the priestess finished hers, because I wasn't going second to a child. "Nakamura. Look at me."

They looked. Gods, they looked *tired*. Twenty years of stolen other people's happiness, and it turns out the thief was the poorest man on the road.

"I owe you a debt of blood," I said. "Everyone knows it. I've said it myself, drunk, twice." I crouched so we were level, which for me is a concession on par with kneeling. "But a debt of blood is a debt taken. Taken things bind you. So I'm not paying a debt tonight. I'm making a trade, free and clear, and you're going to accept it because refusing a fair trade is an insult in every country on every map, including the ones that got erased."

"Kestrel—"

"Shut up. I'm talking." I pressed two fingers to my own temple, the way you do when the thing you're handling is delicate. "I give you the last evening with Tam before the heist. Both of us on the roof of the counting-house, splitting a bottle of plum wine, laughing at my plan because it was a bad plan and we knew it and we did it anyway. He had a laugh like a door falling off its hinges. I give you that. The whole hour. The wine, the cold, his stupid hat. Freely given, freely held. When you walk through that door, you walk through carrying my brother's best night — so that somewhere on the far side of unmaking, one good hour of Tam survives, even if it lives in a stranger."

My voice went rough on the last of it. I let it. Eleven years I'd hoarded that memory like a dragon on one coin, and it turns out a coin only spends if you let it go.

"And here's the trade," I said. "You carry Tam's laughter out of Thornfell. That's the payment. In exchange, you don't hand that door your whole self like a martyr at an altar. You pay *enough*. You pay what breaks the god's claim and not one drop more. Because if you walk out of here empty, Nakamura, then Ossa wins anyway — it'll have unmade you without lifting a finger, and I'll have traded my brother's ghost for nothing."

Isandro stared at me. Behind them, Sera had gone very still, and Wren was watching with that open-window look, and I realized — with the particular horror of a hard man caught mid-softness — that all three of them had already decided.

"This is mutiny," Isandro said.

"Aye," I agreed. "First one I've ever led. Always thought I'd be better at taking orders."

* * *

### Isandro

Here is what I learned that night, in a nameless valley under a door that eats: you cannot refuse a gift without insulting the giver, and I had run out of insults.

Sera gave me the morning of her ordination — not the triumph of it, but the terror, the moment before the anointing when she'd stood in the dark behind the altar and admitted to herself that she didn't believe, and chose the vows anyway, because the believing could come later or never but the serving was hers to choose. "So you know," she said, pressing it into my hands like bread, "that identity isn't what happened to you. It's what you decide next. I've decided twice now. It works."

Wren gave me three nows, bright and weightless as coins fresh from the mint.

Kestrel gave me Tam's laughter, and I felt it settle into me like a coal into a banked fire, and I understood that I would carry a dead man's joy across the edge of the world, and that this was not a burden. This was the opposite of a burden. This was what burdens were for.

Four gifts. Freely given. None of them taken, none of them owed, none of them mine by right — which meant, for the first time in my life, everything I carried was actually mine.

"You understand," I said at last, and my voice betrayed me, so I let it, "that this changes nothing about the mathematics. Ossa's claim runs through my childhood. The Door breaks claims. Someone pays."

"Yes," Sera said. "Someone. We've established that. We're simply informing you that *someone* is plural."

"The door takes what you cannot bear to lose," Wren recited, solemn as scripture. "Maybe it takes what lots of people can't bear to lose together. Maybe that's heavier."

I looked at the Door. Black stone, patient, indifferent. And I swear — I swear on every stolen thing I ever sold — that it seemed, in that moment, slightly less indifferent. As though it had been listening. As though it had been waiting, all these centuries, not for a sacrifice but for exactly this: a handful of ruined people insisting, against all theology, that a self is not property to be surrendered but a story to be handed on.

"All right," I said. "All right. Tomorrow, we go to the door together, and we find out what it makes of a family."

I said *family*. Out loud. Nobody corrected me. That was the worst kindness of the whole night.

And then, because the world has timing like a bad comedian, the grass at the valley's mouth began to move — not with wind, but with footsteps, hundreds of them, in perfect unison — and out of the dark came the white masks of the inquisition, and walking ahead of them, wearing my face, smiling my smile, came the god.

Ossa had arrived to collect.

And it was carrying something in its cupped hands, wrapped in swaddling cloth, glowing faintly gold.

Something that smelled, impossibly, of my mother's kitchen.
