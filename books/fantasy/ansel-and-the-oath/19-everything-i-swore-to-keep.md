## 19. Everything I Swore to Keep

The god wore my face, and it was smiling with my mouth, and I hated how well the smile fit.

"Last offer," Ossa said. Its voice came out of a throat I knew better than any throat in the world — mine, but pitched lower, resonant, the way mine sounded inside my own skull before it ever reached air. "Your childhood. Whole. Every year of it, every taste and terror, restored to you as if nothing had ever been taken. All you have to do is give me the child."

Wren stood behind me. I could hear them breathing — quick, shallow, the breathing of an eight-year-old who had learned far too recently what it meant when grown-ups went quiet like this. Kestrel was at my left shoulder, blade drawn, which was about as useful as a spoon against a glacier but I appreciated the sentiment. Sera was praying. I could tell because her lips were moving and nothing was happening, which these days was the only honest kind of prayer.

Behind Ossa, filling the pass below the Door of Unmaking, stood the inquisition. Forty riders, maybe fifty, torches guttering in the mountain wind, their white-and-ash surcoats the only bright thing in all that dark. They hadn't moved. They didn't need to. The god was doing the negotiating, and gods don't send advance men.

"No," I said.

"You haven't heard the terms fully—"

"I heard them the first time." My mouth was dry. My hands, traitorous things, were steady — they always were during a job, which is the cruelest joke my body ever played on me. "You want me to hand over a child who has never done anything to anyone except exist empty, so that you can pour your stolen country into them like wine into a cup. And in exchange you'll give me back the years I sold."

"Yes."

"The years I don't remember missing."

"Yes," Ossa said gently, and that gentleness was the worst part, because it was *my* gentleness, the one I used on marks, on Wren, on myself at three in the morning. "You've built a whole person out of the hole where those years used to be, Isandro. Clever work. Load-bearing grief. But wouldn't you like to know what's under it?"

And here is the thing I will not pretend I didn't feel: I wanted it. Gods, I wanted it. There is a room inside every thief where we keep the shape of the thing we stole from ourselves, and I had lived thirty-odd years furnishing around that room, telling myself the door was locked for good reasons. Why does a child flee a country alone? Why does a memory-thief — someone who could buy back almost anything, who had handled more pasts than most people have *had* — never once go looking for their own? Because some part of me, the part that kept its hands steady, already knew the price of looking.

"I'm not giving you Wren," I said.

"Then keep your hole," Ossa said, and my own face did something disappointed and patient, and I wanted to punch myself in it. "We will take the country instead. We are nearly finished eating, Isandro. Do you know what Thornfell tastes like, from the inside? It tastes like the moment before a name is forgotten. We have eaten the registries, the shrines, the roads. Soon there will be nothing left to remember it *with*, and then your Door will open onto nothing, because there will be no one left to walk through it."

Sera stepped forward. Her shrine-gone face was lit by torchlight and fury. "You are not gods," she said. "You are *debt collectors*. You take what was never given."

"We take what was *sold*," Ossa said, and turned my eyes — my eyes — to me. "Sold by thieves. Prayed to us by priests. Forgotten by mothers. Everything in us was given up freely by someone who didn't want it anymore. That is the oldest law there is, little priestess. Older than your church. Ownership flows from surrender."

"That's not law," Sera said. "That's just what scavengers say."

But something in me went very still, because Ossa was right about the mechanism even if it lied about everything else. That's how theft works. Not by force — force is amateur hour. Theft works by finding the moment a thing is loose, unwanted, unguarded, and calling that moment *consent*. I had built a career on it. I had told myself a thousand times: *they weren't using it, they were dying anyway, it would have rotted in them.* And now here was a god wearing my face, reciting my own professional ethics back at me as scripture.

Wren tugged my sleeve.

I looked down. In the torchlight the kid's face was pale and set, and their eyes — those terrible blank eyes that the old gods circled like wolves around a lamb that couldn't be smelled — were fixed on Ossa with something I hadn't seen in them before. Recognition. Not of the god. Of the *hunger*. Whatever emptiness lived in Wren knew its cousins when it saw them.

"Isandro," Wren said. "What's a childhood?"

The question went through me like a blade through wet paper.

Because I didn't know. That was the whole horror of my life, compressed into a child's question. I knew other people's childhoods intimately — I'd carried a hundred of them, warm as bread, through cold smuggler roads. I knew what childhoods *felt like* from the inside of strangers. Mine was a locked room I'd stopped knocking on.

"I don't know," I said honestly. "I lost mine before I could miss it."

"Was it yours?"

"It was mine," Ossa said.

"It was *mine*," I said, louder than I meant to, and the word cracked something open, and I understood — finally, stupidly, at the end of everything — what I had to do.

Not the deal. Never the deal. You don't pay a blackmailer once; you pay them forever, and you pay in things you love until there's nothing left of you but the paying. Sera had taught me that without meaning to, back in the smuggler den, when she chose partnership over murder and called it strategy. Kestrel had taught me that on the Salt Bridge, when he broke his own trap rather than feed his brother to the machine that ate minds. Even Wren taught it, every day, by existing: *you are not what was taken from you.*

There was another way. There had always been another way, and I had been too much of a thief to see it, because thieves think in acquisitions and the answer was a *return*.

Ownership flows from surrender, the god had said. Fine. Two could play at that theology.

"Sera," I said. "When I give something back — truly give it, not sell it, not trade it — whose is it?"

Her breath caught. I watched her get there, watched the doctrine die behind her eyes and something older and truer take its place. "Theirs," she whispered. "Freely returned, it belongs to... to whoever it came from. Or—" her eyes went wide, "—or to no one. A gift refused leaves the giver holding it. A gift *unmade*—"

"A gift unmade belongs to the road," I finished, because some smuggler proverb was apparently going to save the world today, and isn't that always how it goes.

Ossa had gone still. My face, wearing caution. "What are you doing?"

"Reading the fine print," I said. "You claimed everything I ever stole. Every memory that passed through my hands carries your lien, yes? Your legal-spiritual paperwork. Including the ones I sold upward. Including—" and here my heart slammed against my ribs like a prisoner against a door, "—including the ones that were *mine first*."

"You sold your childhood to Vetch's broker twenty-six years ago," Ossa said. "It is ours. It feeds us. It is the keystone of our claim on you."

"Then watch me default."

I reached into my coat, into the inner pocket where I kept the dying woman's prayer-memory and, folded beneath it, the thing I had taken from the broker's vault in Vetch: a sliver of glassy dark, cold as a held breath, humming faintly. My childhood. Recovered, traced, bought back with blood and guilt and Kestrel's grudging sword-work. I had carried it for weeks telling myself I was deciding whether to destroy it or drink it. Both options had felt like theft. Both *were*.

There was a third option. The one nobody takes. The one that costs everything and buys nothing, which is the only currency the old laws respect.

"Isandro, don't—" Kestrel started.

"Isandro, *wait*—" Sera said.

Wren just took my hand.

That decided it, if anything needed deciding. You don't let go of a hand like that to go chasing after a past. You hold on, and you let the past go.

"Here's my counteroffer," I told my own face. "I return it. Freely. No sale, no trade, no lien. A gift given back to the void it came from. And per your own oldest law — ownership flows from surrender — when I surrender it *to no one*, it belongs to no one. Not to you. Not to me. It stops being property at all. It stops being *food*."

"You would starve yourself to spite us," Ossa said. For the first time, my face looked afraid, and I confess it was the single most satisfying thing I have ever seen.

"I would starve myself to free a country," I said. "Spite is just the seasoning."

I broke the seal.

* * *

They tell you returning a stolen memory is quiet. They lie, or they've never done it, or they're gods, which amounts to the same thing.

It was not quiet. It was a dam breaking in reverse — the flood going *up*, out of me, and then turning around and coming *back*, because a childhood is not a thing you can hand to the void without the void handing back the having-of-it. For one endless moment I was both the giver and the given, the thief and the stolen, standing in the pass below the Door of Unmaking while twenty-six years poured through me like light through a cracked bell.

I was four, and the kitchen smelled of cedar smoke and burnt honey, and my mother sang off-key on purpose because she thought it was funny.

I was six, and I hid under the shrine stairs with a stolen sweet, and the priestess found me and didn't tell, and winked, and I learned that mercy could look like complicity.

I was eight—

I was eight, and I was in the deep shrine, the one under the hill, where I should never have been, following a cat or a dare or both, and I saw them.

The figures in grey. Not inquisition — older, quieter, wrong in a way the eye kept sliding off. They moved through the god-hall like clerks through a countinghouse, and they *harvested*. I watched them lay hands on the sleeping shrine-gods — my mother's god, the wink-priestess's silent god, all of them, drowsing in their stone like bears in winter — and I watched them *draw out* the memories. Centuries of devotion, of answered prayers, of names and harvests and small mercies, siphoned into vessels of that same glassy dark I would one day carry, and sell, and be sold.

And I remembered why I ran. Not courage. Not witness. A child of eight saw gods being robbed and did the only thing children can do with an unbearable truth: they fled, and forgot, and built a life on top of the grave of it. I fled because one of the grey figures had *looked at me*. Had seen the small boy in the shadows with his mouth open around a scream. Had smiled — my smile, the family resemblance suddenly obscene — and put one finger to its lips.

*I had been sworn to silence by a thief before I knew the word for theft.*

That was the oath under all my oaths. That was the load-bearing grief. That was why I became what I became: a person who takes memories, because taking them was the only relationship with memory I was ever permitted. You don't choose your first crime. Sometimes your first crime is chosen *for* you, at eight years old, by a smiling stranger in the dark under a hill.

The memories kept coming, and I kept screaming, and somewhere outside the flood Sera was shouting words that might have been a prayer or a curse or my name, and Kestrel was holding me upright by the collar like a drowned man, and Wren —

Wren was pressed against my side, small and solid, saying, over and over, "I've got you. I've got you. I've got you," which is a strange thing for a child with no memories to know how to say, unless love doesn't require a past, which I had suspected for a while and now knew to the marrow.

And Ossa screamed too.

Because the keystone was cracking. I felt it leave me — felt the claim dissolve like frost in sun — and across the pass the god staggered, my face flickering through a hundred borrowed expressions, my childhood tearing loose from whatever engine it had been turning inside the divine machinery. Starved of its anchor, the lien on everything else I'd ever touched began to fray. I could *see* it fraying — threads of pale light snapping above the inquisition riders, above the burning lowlands, above the whole vast architecture of stolen remembering.

"You *fool*," Ossa gasped, in a voice that was losing my timbre, going thin, going many-voiced, going hungry. "Do you know what you've witnessed? Do you know what still sleeps under the hill, half-drained, waiting for its thieves to come back and finish—"

"I know," I said, and my voice came out wrecked and new. "I was there."

The god stared at me with what remained of my eyes. And in them I saw the calculation turn, the hunger pivot, the whole divine attention swing away from Wren — the empty vessel, the last cup — and toward the thing it suddenly wanted far more than a child:

The witness. The testimony. The last living memory of the original theft.

It lunged.

* * *

Later — seconds later, though it felt like a life, and I suppose it was one — I would understand that this was the moment everything balanced. The god coming for me with forty riders at its back and a nation's worth of stolen past coiled in its gut. Me, hollowed out, kneeling in the frost with my found family's hands on me and one truth burning in the space where my childhood used to live.

The truth was mine now. Truly mine. Returned, witnessed, *given* — and therefore, by the oldest law, unownable. Ossa couldn't take what had been surrendered to no one. It could only destroy the vessel that carried it.

Which meant I had exactly one move left, and it was the same move as before, scaled up: give it away again. Give it to everyone. Make the testimony unkillable by making it *shared*.

"Wren," I rasped. "Kestrel. Sera. Come here. Hold on to me. All of you."

"Why?" Kestrel asked, blade out, watching the god close the distance like an avalanche with a face.

"Because I'm about to tell you a story," I said, "and stories belong to whoever hears them."

Ossa was ten paces away. Five. The riders were spurring forward. Behind us, ancient and indifferent, the Door of Unmaking waited in the mountain's shoulder, opening — as it only opens — for those ready to surrender what they cannot bear to lose.

I opened my mouth to speak the truth that would either save us or unmake me entirely.

And the ground under the hill began, very softly, to sing.
