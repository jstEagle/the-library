## 20. The Unmaking

Here is a thing no one tells you about giving away your childhood: it doesn't leave all at once.

It leaves the way water leaves a cracked cup — slowly at first, in threads you don't notice, and then all at once, everywhere, soaking into something that drinks greedily.

I stood on the threshold of the Door of Unmaking with my hands open and my past pouring out of them, and I watched a god drink.

Ossa wore my face while it did it. That was the worst part. Not the size of it — though it had grown since the salt flats, since Vetch, since it had first leaned out of a broker's mask and said *mine* — but the face. My face, stretched across something vast and hungry, going slack with satisfaction like a man at a table after too much wine.

"That's it," it murmured with my mouth. "That's the taste of you."

"You're welcome," I said, because if I didn't say something flippant right then I was going to scream, and screaming felt like losing.

Behind me, Sera was chanting — not prayer anymore; she'd burned that bridge herself, formally, with a heretic's calm, three days ago. What she chanted now was older than prayer. A counting. Names of the dead of Thornfell, spoken aloud so they'd stay spoken. Kestrel had his knife out, which for Kestrel was practically an embrace. And Wren —

Wren was holding my sleeve. Eight years old, empty as fresh snow, looking up at me with the only kind of faith I'd ever trusted: the faith of someone who has decided, without evidence, that you are worth following.

"Hold on," I told them. "This might get strange."

"Everything's already strange," Wren said. "Go ahead."

So I let go.

* * *

You should understand what I was giving back, because I only understood it in pieces myself, even then.

In chapter nineteen — no. Forgive me. In the moment before this one, when the memories came home to roost before flying off again, I learned why I'd fled Thornfell as a child. I saw the shrine beneath my grandmother's house. I saw the men with the broker's masks, siphoning a god's sleep into lead-lined jars. I saw that I had watched, and been seen watching, and run, and that everything I'd built my life on afterward — the thieving, the roads, the careful loneliness — had been one long act of hiding from what I knew.

The gods hadn't woken on their own. They'd been force-fed. And the first course, the appetizer that taught them the flavor of human years, had been sold to their broker by a child who grew up to be me.

Ossa's claim on me had never been metaphor. It was bookkeeping. Every memory I'd ever stolen and sold passed through the same ledgers, up the same chain, into the same mouths. My skills were theirs. My loves were theirs. My body's habits — the way I palmed a coin, the way I flinched at bells — all of it sat in their vaults under seal.

But a claim requires collateral. And collateral can be returned.

That was the trick. That was the whole stupid, beautiful trick: the Door of Unmaking opens for those who surrender what they cannot bear to lose, and what I could not bear to lose was the thing being used against me. Give the childhood back to its thief-in-chief, close the account, and the lien dissolves.

Simple. Clean. Like cutting off your own hand to escape a trap, except the hand was *me*, all of me, every year before the age of nine.

The last of it went out of me standing there, and I'll tell you honestly what it felt like: it felt like setting down a pack I'd forgotten I was carrying. It felt like the morning after a fever breaks. It felt like nothing, which is the most frightening thing emptiness can feel like.

"There," I said. My voice sounded odd. Younger? No — younger would have meant keeping something. It sounded *unowned*. "Paid in full. Check your ledgers."

Ossa checked.

I watched it check. Watched my own face frown with my own frown, watch my own eyes go distant the way mine go distant when I'm counting coin. Somewhere behind that borrowed face, vast mechanisms turned over, found the account closed, found the vault empty, found —

Found nothing.

"No," Ossa said.

"Yeah," I said. "No."

* * *

A god is made of what it eats. I'd known that abstractly since the wayshrine, since Sera's prayer got answered by something that granted her wish the way a monkey grants a wish — literally, maliciously, with a monkey's understanding of what wishes are *for*. The old gods of Thornfell weren't creators. They were accumulations. Centuries of offered memory, offered devotion, offered fear, packed dense enough to think.

And I had just taken back the densest meal any of them had tasted in a generation.

Ossa unraveled from the edges inward.

It started small. The god's left hand — a hand made of stacked memories, I realized, of harvests and funerals and first kisses, thousands of them pressed together until they held a shape — the left hand simply stopped agreeing to be a hand. Fingers drifted apart like smoke deciding it had better things to do. Then the arm. Then the shoulders of it, the great mantle of stolen years sloughing off in flakes that fell upward instead of down, because memories don't obey gravity once nobody's holding them.

My face went last.

It looked at me with my own eyes while it died, and here is the thing I will carry to whatever grave accepts me: it looked *grateful*. Or maybe that was just my face doing what faces do, mirroring, reflecting. Maybe a god can't be grateful. Maybe all I saw was myself, finally, without anything wearing me.

"Thief," Ossa said — and it wasn't accusation. It was recognition. One professional acknowledging another. We had both spent our existence taking what wasn't given. The difference between us was that I'd learned to stop.

"Yeah," I said. "Guilty. Go hungry."

And Ossa, wearing my face one final instant, smiled my smile, and said, in a voice like a ledger closing:

"*No one owns you now.*"

Then it was weather. Then it was less than weather. A smell of rain on dust, a pressure change, a sense of some enormous attention folding itself shut like a book whose reader has fallen asleep mid-sentence — and gone.

The silence afterward rang.

* * *

The inquisition lasted another four minutes.

I want that on the record, because history — the unofficial kind, the kind told in communes and smuggler dens — tends to give villains longer deaths than they deserve. Four minutes. That's how long the riders at the Door of Unmaking kept functioning after their god's pantry emptied.

Kestrel spotted it first. He always spots things first; it's his most annoying virtue. "Look at the sergeant," he said, low. "Look at his face."

The sergeant — a broad, gray man named Halloway, whom we'd been dodging since the Salt Bridge, whose orders had been burned into him by something older than the Crown — the sergeant was standing very still beside his horse with an expression I recognized intimately. It was the expression of a man reaching for a memory and finding the shelf bare.

"What were my orders?" he asked the air.

Nobody answered him. His lieutenant looked at him with the same dawning blankness. Around the clearing, thirty riders in gray began, one by one, to have the same conversation with themselves.

"I was sent to — to —"

"There was a door. Why is there a door?"

"Whose horse is this?"

It should have been funny. Some of it was. One young rider dismounted, walked to the edge of the clearing, picked up a stone, examined it with tremendous scholarly interest, and put it back down, apparently having resolved whatever crisis the stone presented. But mostly it was terrible, watching men come apart not in blood but in *purpose*, their whole identity — inquisitor, hunter, sword of a silent church — evaporating because the thing that gave them meaning had just been eaten by its own appetite.

Sera stepped toward them. I grabbed her arm.

"They'll scatter," she said.

"They'll scatter," I agreed. "Or they'll panic, and panicked men with crossbows are still panicked men with crossbows. Let them go."

They went. In ones and twos, then in streams, wheeling their horses south and east and west, each following whatever thread of self remained. Halloway lingered longest. He looked straight at me — or through me, rather; I don't think he saw anything he could name — and then he mounted up and rode toward a horizon he'd have to invent from scratch.

Four minutes. Then Thornfell's legal erasure had no enforcement arm, and the country that officially did not exist continued, stubbornly, to exist anyway.

"Well," said Kestrel. He cleaned his knife on his sleeve and put it away. "That was anticlimactic."

"You wanted a battle?"

"I wanted *something*. Twenty years of running from these bastards and they forget their own names in front of me?" He shook his head. "Feels like theft."

"It is," I said. "We stole their god. Best heist of my career, and I didn't even get to keep anything."

"That," said Kestrel, almost smiling, "is what makes it art."

* * *

The Door of Unmaking stood open.

It had stood open since the moment I began paying, and I understood now that it had always been less a door than a question. *What can you not bear to lose?* Answer honestly, pay truly, and pass. Everyone pays differently. Kestrel had paid his brother's grief-memories two days ago, and I still caught him sometimes touching his own chest like a man checking whether a wound has really closed. Sera had paid her vows. Wren —

Wren had paid nothing, because Wren had nothing, and the Door, which is fair in the way only ancient indifferent things are fair, had accepted that. An empty vessel owes no toll. Sometimes I think that's the whole secret of the child: everyone else in Thornfell was mortgaged to their past, and Wren walked free among us like a coin that had never been spent.

Now it was my turn to walk through, and I stood at the threshold doing inventory, because that's what thieves do, and some habits survive even the loss of the self that formed them.

What did I have?

Not my childhood. Given back. Not my skills — those had been Ossa's collateral, and Ossa's collapse had scattered them; I could feel gaps where lockpicking used to live, where the smuggler's cant lived, where the memory of my mother's voice had been sitting so quietly I'd forgotten it was there until it wasn't. Not my name, even. *Isandro Nakamura* belonged to a person with a past, and I had just donated mine to a dying god.

What I had was this:

Sera's gift. From the night at the sanctuary, when she'd pressed her palm to my forehead and given me, freely, the memory of her first sunrise over the shrine at Vel Marra — the bell, the frost, the certainty that someone was listening. She'd said, "Hold this. It's yours now. I give it." And the Door had weighed it and found it true.

Kestrel's gift. A tavern fight in Grayharbor, badly started, bravely finished — the memory of standing back to back with a stranger and realizing halfway through that the stranger had become a friend. He'd spat as he gave it, because Kestrel gives nothing gracefully, but he'd given it.

And Wren's gift, which was the strangest and the lightest: the memory of the day we met. The emptied village, the circling gods, the moment a hollow-eyed child had looked at a hardened thief and decided, with the total authority of eight years old, *this one is safe.* Wren had given me my own kindness, back, as a keepsake. I have thought about that every day since. I expect to think about it every day hence.

Three memories. That was the cargo. That was everything Isandro Nakamura carried through the Door of Unmaking: a sunrise, a brawl, and a small hand taking hold of a sleeve.

It was more than I deserved. It was exactly enough.

"You don't have to do this alone," Sera said beside me. "The Door takes what you surrender. It doesn't take what people hand you."

"I know."

"You're crying."

"Am I?" I touched my cheek. Wet. Interesting. Apparently the body grieves even when the self has been repossessed. "Must be allergies. All this unraveling in the air."

"Isandro—"

"Don't." I held up a hand. Then, gentler: "Don't say goodbye like it's a funeral. I'm walking through a door. You're coming with me. Whatever's on the other side, I'll still know you. I'll know you *better*, maybe — you're most of what I've got left."

She laughed, wetly, and hugged me hard enough to crack a rib, and I let her, because some debts you pay gladly.

Wren tugged my sleeve. "What happens if you walk through and you're nobody?"

"Then I'll be nobody with excellent friends," I said. "Which is a considerable promotion over who I was last month."

"And if you forget us?"

I crouched to their height. The Door hummed behind me, patient as geology.

"Wren. Listen. I gave back everything I *took*. Everything anyone ever stole from me, I returned. Do you understand what that means?" I tapped their chest, lightly, over the heart. "It means the only memories I'm carrying are ones that were *given*. Freely. By people who chose me. Things given freely don't work like stolen things. Stolen things fade. Given things root."

"Like seeds," Wren said.

"Like seeds. Now come on. Hold my sleeve. It's tradition."

* * *

I won't pretend I remember the crossing clearly. The Door of Unmaking is not a place; it's a subtraction. You step toward it and the world politely removes itself, layer by layer — sound first, then color, then the sense of having a body, then the sense of there having ever been a body to lose — and in that white and weightless nowhere, I felt the last of the old Isandro finish draining out of me. Not painfully. Honestly. The way a lie feels when you finally stop telling it.

For one endless instant I was nothing at all.

And here is the miracle, the one I'll spend the rest of my life failing to deserve: the nothing was not empty.

There was a sunrise in it. Frost on a shrine bell, and a woman's certainty that someone was listening. There was a tavern brawl, and a stranger's shoulder against mine, and the slow astonishment of trust. There was a small hand gripping a sleeve, and a voice saying *this one is safe.*

Given things root.

I came out the other side breathing, blinking, standing in ordinary daylight on grass that grew in a country with no name yet, and the first thing I did was turn around to count my family — priestess, rival, child, all present, all real — and the second thing I did was laugh, because I couldn't remember my own name, and it didn't hurt. It didn't hurt at all. It felt like a page torn clean out of a ledger, and every remaining line written in someone else's loving hand.

"So," said Kestrel, squinting at me. "Who are you, then?"

I opened my mouth to answer.

And that — who I was about to become, and the oath I was about to swear at the first small shrine we found — is a story for the next road. Because the grass was green, the sun was up, and somebody had to decide what to call a person made entirely of gifts.

I had a few ideas.
