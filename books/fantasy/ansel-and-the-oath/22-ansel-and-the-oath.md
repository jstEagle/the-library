## 22. Ansel and the Oath

The shrine was small enough to miss if you weren't looking for it, which I suppose was the point.

It sat where the smuggler road bent around a granite shoulder of the southern hills, a stone no bigger than a well-cap, worn smooth on top by centuries of travelers' palms. No god's name carved into it. No doctrine nailed to its face. Just the stone, and the wind coming up off the low country smelling of rain that hadn't fallen yet, and me, standing in front of it with wet boots and an empty pocket where a stolen memory used to live.

I'd been walking for eleven days. Long enough for my feet to stop complaining and start negotiating. Long enough to learn the shape of what I carried — because I did carry something now, even after everything. Not my own past; that was gone, spent at the Door like a coin too heavy to spend any other way. What I carried were the gifts. Sera's memory of her first prayer, whispered into a shrine that never answered, and how she'd kept praying anyway. Kestrel's memory of his brother laughing before the grief got in — he'd cut that one loose himself, freely, the way you'd open a birdcage knowing the bird might not come back. Wren's memory of the morning they found us: not fear, Wren had said, but *the moment I decided you were mine.* An eight-year-old giving away their first decision like it cost nothing.

It cost everything. That's the thing about gifts nobody tells you. The free ones are the expensive ones.

I set my hand flat on the shrine stone. It was cold, then warm, the way stones are when they've been holding the sun all day and give it back grudgingly. Somewhere behind me, north, Thornfell went on being nowhere — a country struck from every registry, surviving now only in mouths and memories and the stubborn nightly storytelling of people who refused to be unmade. Somewhere ahead of me, south, there were maps again. Laws. Names that meant something on paper.

And here, in between, there was this stone, and me, and a choice I'd been carrying in my chest since the Door closed behind us.

Here's what nobody tells you about losing your whole past: you don't feel light. You feel *drafty*. Like a house with the windows knocked out. The wind moves through you and there's nothing to catch it — no childhood to snag on, no grudges, no first love, no reason you flinch at certain sounds or crave certain foods. I walked south and I was a person-shaped absence, and every night when we made camp I'd ask the others, carefully, casually, *what was I like?* And Sera would look at me with that priestess patience she'd earned by burning down her own faith, and say, "You were tired, Isandro. You were so tired. But you were kind in the places it counted, and you pretended not to be."

I wanted to keep that. Not as memory — I couldn't hold memory anymore, not mine, not really; the ones I carried were given, and given things sit differently in you, like furniture someone else chose. I wanted to keep it as *promise*.

Because here's the other thing nobody tells you: an empty person is dangerous. Ossa had been empty once, I think. A god is just a hunger that learned to speak. And I knew exactly how hunger worked, because I'd fed on other people's pasts for fifteen years and called it survival. If I walked south hollow, something would eventually walk into the hollow. It always does. The world abhors a vacancy.

So I came to the shrine. And I took a new name.

Not out of the air. Out of Wren. On the road, the child had taken to calling me something — not Isandro, because Isandro belonged to the thief, to the debts, to the woman whose dying happiness I'd pocketed for coin, to the broker in Vetch, to the god wearing my face. Wren had heard the name somewhere, maybe from a story told at the commune of the mapless, maybe invented whole from the place where children invent things, and had started using it the way you'd test a new boot: *Ansel, look. Ansel, are we close? Ansel, do thieves go to hell or just to Vetch?*

Ansel. It fit the way borrowed clothes fit at first — wrong in the shoulders — and then, after some miles, right.

I knelt at the stone. My knees cracked. Fifteen years of roads will do that.

"I don't know who I was," I said to the stone, because gods were gone from it and that seemed honest. "I know pieces of what I did. I stole from the dying. I sold a child's worth of myself to a broker who fed it to a hungry thing, and the hungry thing ate a country's memory while I bought boots. I'm not asking forgiveness. Forgiveness needs a self to forgive, and I spent mine."

The wind moved through the pass. Somewhere below, a shepherd's dog barked at nothing, or at everything.

"But I know what I want to be," I went on. "That's new. That's the first genuinely new thing I've ever owned." I pressed my palm harder against the stone. "So. Oath. Never again to take what isn't given. Not memories, not names, not trust, not time. If it comes to me, it comes freely or it doesn't come at all. And everything that *is* given to me—" my voice caught, drafty old house, "—everything given, I'll carry like it's holy. Because it is. It's the only holiness left, now that the gods have proven themselves to be just bigger thieves."

I stayed there a while. Long enough for the cold to work through my trousers and into my knees, long enough for the dog to stop barking. Then I stood, and it was done, and nothing dramatic happened, which is how you know an oath is real. Dramatic oaths are theater. Real ones are just words said at a rock by a tired person, and then the long work of meaning them.

* * *

Sera found me three days later, or I found her — with Sera it was always ambiguous who was pursuing whom.

She was at a crossroads inn south of the hills, sitting with her back to the wall out of fifteen years of habit she hadn't lost with her vows, eating soup like it had personally wronged her. Kestrel was across from her, arguing with the innkeeper about whether his coin was legal, which was a fair question given that his coin was minted by a country that officially didn't exist. The innkeeper, to his credit, was taking it, because Kestrel had that face — the face of a man who has already calculated seventeen ways to make your disagreement expensive.

Wren saw me first. Eight years old, and sharper than both of us put together. They came off the bench at a run and hit me at waist height, and I held on, and I noticed — the way I'd been noticing for eleven days — that I could feel it. The gift-memory of them, *the moment I decided you were mine*, sitting warm in my chest like a coal that wouldn't go out.

"You're late," Wren said into my coat.

"Roads are longer than they used to be."

"That's not how roads work."

"It's how *my* roads work."

Sera looked up. She studied me the way she used to study scripture — looking for the flaw, the heresy, the thing underneath. "You stopped," she said. "At the shrine on the pass. The old waystone."

"How could you possibly know that?"

"Because you're standing differently." She said it plainly, no mysticism in it, which somehow made it worse. "Thieves stand like they're about to leave. You're standing like you arrived somewhere."

Kestrel snorted. "He's been reborn. Next he'll want a ceremony."

"A name," I said. "I took a name. Ansel."

Silence, the specific silence of people deciding whether to laugh. Then Wren, indignant: "*I* gave you that name!"

"And I accepted it," I said. "Freely given, freely taken. First transaction of my new life conducted entirely above board. There were witnesses." I nodded at the stone, far behind us, and at the sky, and at whatever remained in the world to witness things.

Kestrel turned back to his coin argument with the expression of a man conceding defeat in a war he refuses to acknowledge. Sera smiled — small, real, the smile of a woman who had watched her whole faith burn and found, in the ashes, something worth kneeling to anyway.

"Ansel," she said, tasting it. "It suits you."

"It's too big for me yet."

"Good," she said. "Names should be too big. You grow into them. That's the whole point of promises."

We ate. The soup was terrible. It was the best meal of my life, or would have been, if I'd had a life to measure against — and the strange grace of it was that I didn't need one. Best meal so far. That was enough. That was, I was learning, the entire secret: *so far*. Every day a *so far*, and the so-far stretching ahead like a road that exists again.

* * *

The traveler came through two days later, at another wayside shrine — smaller than the pass stone, barely a cairn, where the road dropped toward the river country and the first proper maps.

I was alone at it; the others had gone ahead to argue with a ferryman. He was an old man, road-worn, leading a mule loaded with salt, and he stopped at the cairn the way travelers do, to touch it, to mark that he'd passed. He saw me standing there and nodded, companionable, the nod of the road.

"Going south?" he asked.

"That's the direction."

"From where?"

And there it was. The question I'd been waiting for without knowing I was waiting. From where, stranger? Where does a person come from, when they've paid their origin to a door? When their childhood is ash in a dead god's mouth and their name is a gift from a child?

I thought about lying. It would have been easy. *A village up north. Small place, you wouldn't know it.* And it would even have been true, in its way — Thornfell, the village that was a country, the place you wouldn't know because no map would show it to you ever again.

But I'd sworn an oath at a rock. Real oaths are tested in the boring moments. That's the whole design of them.

"No one," I said. "Yet."

The old man looked at me. Mule shifted. Wind came up the valley carrying river-smell and the promise of rain, and somewhere far north a country made of stories went on telling itself into existence, one voice at a time.

He nodded slowly, and I watched him decide I was either a madman or a pilgrim, and either way not worth the trouble. "Well," he said, touching the cairn stone. "Everyone's no one till somebody remembers them. Safe roads, son."

He moved off down toward the river, mule bells chiming, and I stood a moment longer with my hand on the stone.

Everyone's no one till somebody remembers them.

I had four somebodies waiting at a ferry crossing who remembered me — not the thief, not the debt, not the god's claim, but the person I was becoming, which they'd each contributed a piece to, freely, the way you build anything worth building. I had a name too big for me. I had an oath with the long work still ahead of it, because oaths aren't sworn once; they're sworn every morning, at every crossroads, every time taking would be easier than asking.

I turned south. The rain finally came, soft, warm, the kind that means the season is turning.

And far behind me — so far I might have imagined it, and maybe I did, and maybe that was allowed now — I thought I heard the waystone at the pass humming. Not a god. Nothing hungry. Just the sound a stone makes when a thousand travelers' hands have polished it warm, and one more pair has joined them.

I walked on. No one, yet.

Yet was doing a lot of work in that sentence. I intended to let it work for the rest of my life.
