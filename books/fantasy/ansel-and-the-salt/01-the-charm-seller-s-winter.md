## 1. The Charm Seller's Winter

The frost came to Corvane before the bells did.

Rosalind Eastwick knew this the way she knew her own hands—by long acquaintance, by the small aches that arrived each winter like relatives who never wrote ahead. The city's bells rang late in cold weather; the bell-warden was old and slow to climb, and so for a quarter hour each morning Corvane lay in a hush broken only by the creak of cartwheels on frozen mud and the thin complaint of gulls over the fish market. In that hush Rosalind set up her stall: two trestle boards, a striped awning gone gray with age, and a tray of goods arranged with the care of a woman laying out evidence for a trial she hoped would never convene.

Luck-knots first. Red cord, seven turns, tied blind. Anyone could tie them; she sold perhaps forty in a good week, mostly to fishermen's wives and girls with sweethearts going downriver. Then the fever-salts, coarse white grains in twist-paper packets, three coppers apiece. *Sprinkle in the broth,* said the hand-lettered card, *and let the fever find its way out.* It was true, as far as it went. The salts did ease fever. They did it by means no card could say aloud, and that was the whole architecture of her life: everything true, nothing admitted.

She had kept the Salt Guild alive for eleven years by making it boring.

That was the trick no one taught you—that survival was not a matter of hiding but of being beneath notice, of being the woman with the knots and the salts, the one whose stall smelled faintly of brine and dried lavender, the one who complained about the price of cord and the damp in her bones. Magic, real magic, had been outlawed before Rosalind was born, back when Veyra was struck from the maps and then from the memory of everyone too polite or too frightened to keep it. What remained were parlor tricks. What remained was salt.

"Eastwick." A voice like a boot heel on gravel. "You've got my knot."

Marta Cole, herring-wife, stood at the front of the stall with her shawl pulled up to her ears and her breath steaming. Rosalind found the packet without looking—she knew her stock the way a priest knows his psalms—and counted change into Marta's chapped palm.

"Third one this season," Marta said, low. "For my boy's boat. You'll not tell me red string keeps a hull together."

"I'll tell you it costs two coppers," Rosalind said, "and hope is cheaper than timber."

Marta snorted, which was the local equivalent of a blessing, and shuffled off toward the fish rows. Rosalind watched her go, then let her gaze travel the square the way she did every morning, unhurried, a woman merely taking in the day. The bellpost with its curling proclamations from the crown. The pie-seller's brazier smoking sweetly. The soldiers at the far arch—only two today, stamping their feet, more interested in warmth than in watching. And the ordinary tide of Corvane flowing past her stall: apprentices, grandmothers, a knife-grinder ringing his wheel, all of them passing the charm-seller without a second glance.

Invisible. That was the word, though she never said it even inside her own head. Invisible was what happened to other people, in stories. She preferred *unremarked*. Unremarked was something you could work at.

The morning went on. She sold a luck-knot to a bride whose mother hovered behind her with the expression of a woman paying for insurance against her own doubts. She sold fever-salts twice, once to a man who asked too many questions about where they came from—"Sea salt, pressed and dried," Rosalind said, with the flat patience of someone answering for the ten-thousandth time—and once to a young mother whose baby had the wet rattle of a chest-cold, and whom Rosalind sent off with an extra packet and no charge, because some economies were not about coin.

It was near midday when the child came.

He appeared at the edge of the stall the way children do, materializing out of the crowd's knees, a boy of perhaps eight with a wool cap too big for him and a runny nose he wiped on his sleeve with the efficiency of long practice. He studied the tray with the grave concentration of a merchant assessing cargo.

"How much for a knot?"

"Two coppers."

He dug in his pocket. His face, while he dug, went through a small storm of expressions—the particular theater of a child discovering that money does not behave. He produced a bent nail, a button, a length of string (Rosalind repressed the urge to tell him he could make his own luck-knot with that), and finally, from some deeper fold where treasures lived, a single coin.

He set it on the tray between them.

Rosalind looked at it, and the market went quiet—not actually quiet; the noise went on, the gulls and the grinder's wheel and the pie-seller calling her wares—but quiet in the way a room goes quiet inside your skull when something impossible walks through it.

The coin was silver, worn nearly smooth, older than any coin the crown minted now. And stamped into its face, half-erased by decades of thumbs, was a crest: a tower sinking into waves, its topmost window still visible above the waterline, as if the building itself were drowning and refusing to stop looking out.

The drowned crest of Veyra.

Twenty years since she had seen it. Twenty years since Tam Wren, over a candle stub in the guildhouse cellar, had shown her the last of the old coins and told her to memorize it and then melt the memory into the same place where she kept everything else that could hang her. *No one mints it. No one spends it. If you see it again, girl, it means it has come out of the ground, or out of the sea, or out of a dead man's hoard—and none of those places let things go for free.*

The boy watched her with bright, untroubled eyes. "Is it enough? Gran says it's old but it's still silver."

"It's enough," Rosalind heard herself say. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, later—the voice holding steady while everything under it shifted like sand at ebb tide. She took the coin, slid it into her apron pocket rather than the cash box, and tied the boy's luck-knot with fingers that did not tremble, seven turns, blind, the way she'd done ten thousand times. "There. For you or for someone you love?"

"For our boat." He clutched it. "Gran says the sea's been wrong lately. Talking."

"Talking," Rosalind repeated.

"At night. When the fog comes in." He shrugged, the great shrug of the very young, for whom the sea speaking is a fact among facts. "Gran says it's just wind in the caves. But she bought a knot anyway, didn't she."

"She did," Rosalind said. "Run home before the light goes."

He ran. She watched him until the crowd folded him away, and then she stood a moment longer with her hands flat on the trestle boards, feeling the frost's ghost come up through the wood, and made herself do the next thing and the next thing after that: straighten the tray, count the change, greet the pie-seller's boy who came by for his mother's weekly knot. Work. Work was the wall she had built her whole life inside, stone by stone, and she laid another course of it now, and another, until the bell rang for afternoon and the light began to fail and there was nothing left to do but pack up and walk home past the soldiers at the arch, who did not look at her, because she was nobody, because she was unremarked, because the trick held, it always held—

Except that her hand kept drifting to her apron pocket, where the coin sat against her hip like a coal that hadn't finished burning.

* * *

The guildhouse was not called the guildhouse. It was called the Saltcellar, which was both a joke and a perfect disguise, because it had been a salt warehouse once, back when Corvane traded honestly in brine and fish, and it still smelled of it—a deep mineral coldness in the stones that no amount of years could wash out. Rosalind had rented it officially as storage for her stock. Officially, it held barrels of curing herring and crates of dried kelp. Unofficially, it held the last three members of the Salt Guild, and beneath the floor, behind a door that opened only to a word spoken in the old tongue, it held the hoard.

Tam Wren was in the counting room when she came in, bent over the ledger by lamplight, his pen scratching with the dry regularity of a beetle in a wall. He was seventy if he was a day, narrow as a salt-packer's hook, with eyes the pale gray of weathered rope. He did not look up.

"You're late," he said.

"The soldiers were at the arch."

"The soldiers are always at the arch." He made his mark. "Ivo's back. Sulking in the cellar. Says the nets he charmed tore anyway and the fisherwoman wants her copper back."

"Did he give it back?"

"He gave it back and then argued with her about gratitude for twenty minutes, which I'm told ended with her throwing a mackerel at him." Tam's pen paused. "The boy has his grandmother's gift for making friends."

Rosalind unwound her scarf, hung her coat, went through the motions of the evening—lamp trimmed, shutters checked, the kettle set on the little stove that was technically against three separate fire ordinances. Only when the tea was poured and they were both seated across the scarred counting table did she put her hand in her apron pocket and set the coin down between them.

Tam looked at it for a long time.

He did not touch it. That was the first thing she noticed, and it frightened her more than anything else, because Tam Wren touched everything—he was a man who handled the world to understand it, who read ledgers with ink-stained fingers and old maps with reverence. Now his hands stayed flat on the table on either side of the coin, framing it, the way a physician frames a wound.

"Where," he said quietly, "did this come from?"

"A boy paid me with it. Eight years old, maybe. Wool cap. Said his gran gave it to him—said it was old but still silver." Rosalind wrapped her hands around her tea cup, needing the heat. "Tam, no one spends these. You told me yourself. They come out of hoards, out of graves, out of the ground. So either some dead man's cache got looted and passed hand to hand until it reached a child in the fish market—"

"Or someone is spending them on purpose," Tam finished. "Putting them back into the world. Into *circulation*." He said the word like it tasted of iron. "Twenty years, Rosalind. Twenty years we have kept the crest buried, and now it is being handed to children in exchange for luck-knots."

"Maybe it means nothing. Coins turn up. Things surface."

"Things surface," Tam agreed. "That is precisely what frightens me. Salt surfaces when the tide changes." He finally picked up the coin, turned it in the lamplight, and the drowned tower caught the flame and seemed almost to gleam wetly, as if the sea on it had never dried. "Do you know what I did today? Inventory. Boring, necessary inventory, the kind that keeps us alive. And do you know what I found?" He set the coin down with exaggerated gentleness. "The third barrel in the north rack. The one we sealed four winters ago. The seal-word is still spoken, the wax is still whole—but the weight is wrong. Lighter. By a measure I cannot account for. As if some of it had *gone somewhere*."

Rosalind felt the cold come up through the floor then, the real cold, the one that had nothing to do with winter. "Salt doesn't walk."

"No," Tam said. "But it remembers. And lately I have begun to wonder whether remembering is a kind of wanting." He rubbed his eyes with thumb and forefinger, and for a moment he looked every year of his age, a tired clerk in a cold room instead of the keeper of the last magic in a kingdom that wanted it dead. "Your boy with the coin. Did he say anything else?"

"He said the sea's been talking at night. Fog nights. His gran thinks it's wind in the caves." Rosalind turned her cup. "Ivo heard it too. Two weeks ago, coming back along the shingle. He wouldn't tell me what it said."

"Of course he wouldn't. He's sixteen and he thinks feelings are a form of weakness." Tam almost smiled, then didn't. "The sea talks in fog. It talked in fog the year Veyra went under, if the oldest accounts are true. It talked for a month afterward, saying the names of the drowned streets." He pushed back his chair and stood, joints cracking like green kindling, and crossed to the window, where the frost had feathered the glass and beyond it the dark of Corvane lay scattered with a few hundred stubborn lights. "When I was a young man, before your time, the elders used to say the country wasn't destroyed. Erased, yes—from the maps, from the records, from every mouth that could be made afraid. But you cannot erase a place, Rosalind. You can only erase the *knowing* of it. The place itself goes on underneath, the way a name goes on under whitewash. Waiting for someone to read it again."

"And the coin is someone reading it."

"The coin is someone *spending* it," Tam said. "Which is worse. Reading is hunger. Spending is intent." He turned from the window, and the lamplight caught his pale eyes and made them briefly fierce. "Tomorrow you will sell your knots and your salts and you will smile at everyone and be gloriously, magnificently dull. And you will listen. Every customer, every scrap of market talk—who has new coin, who has come into money, who has moved into the old quarters, what the soldiers grumble about on the arch. Something is surfacing, girl. I want to know what it is before it knows we're watching."

Rosalind looked at the coin on the table between them, at the little drowning tower with its one lit window, and thought of the boy running home through the crowd with his luck-knot clutched in his fist, carrying a piece of a country that had never existed, guarded by a grandmother who bought charms against a talking sea.

"We should tell Ivo," she said. "And Odile's letter said—"

"Odile's letter can wait a day. One day." Tam held up a finger. "Let me think. Let me remember what the elders said about coins that come home. There was a phrase—" He frowned, groping, and Rosalind watched an old man reach for something buried deep and find the shelf empty. "There was a phrase," he said again, slower, troubled now in a new way. "About what it meant when the salt began to move on its own. I knew it once. I taught it to apprentices."

The lamp flickered. Neither of them moved.

"Sleep on it," Rosalind said at last, gently, and took the coin, and felt it warm in her palm—warm, though it had sat in cold air all evening—as though it had recently been held by a living hand, or as though it remembered being part of something that had burned.

Upstairs, in her narrow room under the eaves, she set the coin on the sill where the frost ferns grew, and lay in the dark listening to Corvane settle around her: cart wheels, a drunk singing, the far-off boom of the harbor chain. And beneath it all, so faint she might have invented it, a sound like many voices under water, saying something patient and old, over and over, like a lesson being learned by heart.

She did not sleep. In the morning she would stand at her stall and be unremarkable, and she would listen, and she would watch the faces of her neighbors for whatever was rising in them like silt in stirred water.

The coin sat on the sill, catching the last of the streetlight, and the drowned tower's single window seemed to hold it, the way a house holds a lamp for someone who has not yet come home.
