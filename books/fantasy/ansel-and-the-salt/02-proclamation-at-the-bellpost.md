## 2. Proclamation at the Bellpost

The bells woke Corvane before the light did.

Rosalind heard them from her room above the stall—six strokes, then a seventh, which was wrong. The dawn bells had rung six for as long as she had lived in the city, and the seventh stroke hung in the cold air like a splinter in a thumb. She lay listening to it fade, and beneath it, fainter, the sound of boots. Many boots. Moving through the streets in the organized, unhurried rhythm of men who had been paid to be somewhere before the market woke.

She dressed in the dark. She did not light the lamp. Twenty years of selling charms in a city that had forgotten why it feared them had taught her that the body learns warnings before the mind consents to them, and her body was telling her, plainly, that this was not a day for light.

Down in the market square, the Bellpost had grown a paper skin.

It stood at the square's heart, an iron post older than any of the buildings around it, hung with a hundred years of notices—tithes, curfews, the prices of bread, the names of debtors. This morning it wore something new, pasted up in four places, the paste still wet and glistening in the lantern-light of the criers who stood around it looking uncomfortable in their new uniforms. The paper was heavy stock, cream-colored, the kind used for weddings and death announcements. Rosalind read it twice, because the first time her eyes refused the words.

*By order of His Majesty King Aldric, First of His Name, Protector of the Realm and Rooter-Out of Hidden Corruptions:*

*Let it be known that the practice of the gift—being the working of wonders by unlawful means—is hereby declared a corruption of the blood and a treason of the soul.*

*All persons bearing said gift shall present themselves at the registrar's office within thirty days to be counted, catalogued, and marked.*

*Those who register shall be permitted to live.*

*Those who do not shall be hunted.*

*Informers presenting true knowledge of an unregistered bearer shall be rewarded from the Crown's stores: one measure of salt for every name proven true.*

Salt.

The word sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone. Of all the payments they might have chosen—coin, land, pardon—they had chosen salt. And not just any salt, she understood with a cold, descending certainty. The criers were unloading barrels even now, rolling them across the cobbles to a table set up beside the Bellpost, prying the lids, and filling small linen pouches with coarse white crystals that caught the lantern-light the way nothing ordinary catches light. Payment in kind. Payment in the very thing the crown did not know it was paying out. Somewhere in the palace cellars were the seized hoards of a hundred small guilds, gathered up over decades by men who thought they were confiscating seasoning, and now Aldric was scattering it through the city like seed grain, one pouch at a time, to anyone who would sell a neighbor.

Rosalind turned her collar up and went to open her stall.

* * *

The market that morning was a study in what people do with their eyes.

They came, at first, because markets are habits older than fear. The fishwives laid out their silver rows. The baker's boy stacked his loaves. The rag-and-bone man wheeled his cart down the row with the particular squeak that meant he was open for trade. Rosalind set out her own goods with hands that knew the work: the luck-knots in their red thread, the fever-salts in their twist of paper, the little stitched birds that were supposed to carry bad dreams out of a house and into the river.

Her stall was the third from the Bellpost. It had been the third from the Bellpost for nineteen years, and in all that time she had built her life on a simple architecture of smallness. Sell nothing that truly works. Charge little enough that no one bothers to cheat you. Be the charm-woman, the harmless one, the widow Eastwick with her parlor tricks and her kind eyes. The guild had survived the erasure of Veyra by becoming invisible, and invisibility, Rosalind had learned, was not a cloak you put on. It was a discipline you performed daily, stitch by stitch, coin by coin, until even you half-believed the performance.

By midmorning, the performance began to fail.

It was not anything anyone said. That was the terrible part. A woman bought a luck-knot for her son's journey and would not meet Rosalind's eyes while she paid. The baker, who for a decade had traded her day-old loaves for a fever-salt when his children coughed, suddenly had no day-old loaves. Two men she had known since they were boys stood at the corner of her stall, and one of them said, quietly, to the other, in a voice pitched just loud enough: "My cousin says the registrar pays a full measure. A full measure, for a name."

The other man looked at Rosalind.

It lasted half a heartbeat. Then he looked away, and the two of them walked on, and Rosalind stood among her charms and felt the market rearrange itself around her like water around a stone. Not hostility. Nothing so honest as hostility. Just a new attention, a weighing, a hundred small calculations happening behind a hundred pairs of eyes: *how well do I know her, how much is a measure of salt, would anyone know it was me.*

She had spent twenty years making herself unremarkable, and she understood, standing there in the gray morning with her red-threaded knots laid out like small hanged things, that it had all rested on a foundation she had never examined: that the crown did not care. That the erasure of Veyra had been an ending, a closed book, a war won so thoroughly that no one would ever reopen it. She had built her whole life on the assumption that the past was dead. And the past, it turned out, had only been sleeping, and a king with a new crown and old fears had come along and posted it on the Bellpost in cream-colored paper.

The child came at noon.

She was perhaps eight, barefoot despite the cold, with the sharp-faced hunger of the river districts. She had bought from Rosalind before—a stitched bird, once, paid for with buttons. This time she stood at the stall turning something over in her fist, and when Rosalind held out her hand the child dropped a coin into it and snatched a fever-salt and ran, the way river children run, as if the whole world were a place to escape from.

Rosalind looked down at the coin.

It was old, worn nearly smooth, the edges soft as cloth. But the stamp survived, and her breath went out of her in one long, controlled exhale that she was grateful no one was close enough to see. A crest: a tower half-drowned, waves rising to its windows, and beneath it a line of script in an alphabet that had not officially existed for twenty years, because the country that wrote it had been struck from every map and every record and every gravestone in the kingdom.

Veyra's drowned crest. The coin of a country that was never here.

She closed her fist around it. The metal was cold, then warm, then cold again, and she had the sudden vertiginous sense that the coin was not old at all—that it had come *through* something, from *somewhere*, and that somewhere was reaching toward her across twenty years of careful silence. She looked up, scanning the market for the child, but the girl had dissolved into the crowd the way river children do.

When she looked back down at her stall, the man from the corner—the one who had said *a full measure*—was watching her fist.

* * *

She did not go home. She went, first, to the tallow-renderer's off Tanner's Row, because a woman buying tallow was a woman with a practical errand, and practical women were not interesting. She bought enough tallow for a year of candles. Then she bought, from three separate shops, a clay brazier, a bundle of kindling, and a jug of the harsh spirit the fishermen drank, and she carried all of it home through streets that seemed, in the lengthening afternoon, to have grown more eyes than windows.

The ledger had been her mother's before it was hers.

It was a plain thing, bound in gray board, its spine cracked white with use. Twenty years of transactions in her careful hand, and her mother's fifteen before that, and her grandmother's in the faded ink of the earliest pages. *Fever-salt, one twist, to the cooper's wife, for a hen. Luck-knot, red thread, for the ferryman's daughter, for a mended net.* Nothing in it was true magic. That was the joke of it, the bitter joke she had appreciated only in the last few years: the ledger recorded twenty years of harmless lies, thread and tallow and table salt blessed with nothing but confidence. The real guild had never touched paper.

But the real guild had *customers*, and the ledger recorded those too, in a cipher of her grandmother's devising, and if Serah Vole or anyone like her ever laid hands on it, they would not need the gift to read it. They would need only patience.

She burned it in the brazier on her hearth, page by page, feeding the fire with the spirit so the flames ran blue and hot. The cipher ink burned green. She watched her grandmother's handwriting curl and go, and her mother's, and her own, and she thought: *this is what invisibility costs, when it fails. Everything you wrote down. Everything you kept.* The ash rose up the chimney in a gray whisper, and she stood a long time in the small heat, her hands out, like a woman warming herself at a funeral.

Then she washed her face, put on her good shawl, and went out into the dark to warn the last of the guild.

* * *

Tam Wren kept a room above a chandler's shop in the Salt Quarter, which was either the safest place in Corvane or the most dangerous, depending on how you read the crown's sense of humor. He was seventy-one and moved like a man made of knotted rope, and when Rosalind knocked the pattern—two, pause, three—he opened the door before the third knock fell.

"I heard the bells," he said. "Seven strokes. Come in, girl, and don't stand in the stairwell like a fool."

The room was small and smelled of tallow and old paper, though Tam kept no paper anymore; he kept everything in his head, which was the guild's true archive, and which was why the guild could not afford to lose him. He poured her a cup of barley tea without asking, and she drank it scalding, because her hands wanted something to hold.

"It's salt," she said. "They're paying informers in salt."

Tam went very still. It was the stillness of a man who has spent a lifetime learning to be still, and Rosalind watched it settle over him like a lid over a well.

"How much do they have?" he asked.

"Barrels. A dozen at least, at the Bellpost alone. Coarse white, and Tam—" she hesitated. "It *shone*. Not the way salt shines. The way—"

"I know the way." He set down his cup. His hands, she noticed, were not entirely steady. "Seized hoards. From the little guilds. The knot-makers of Harrow Fen, the salt-singers on the coast, all the small cells that went quiet one by one over the years. The crown has been sitting on it for decades, thinking it was seasoning." He laughed, one dry syllable with no joy in it. "And now Aldric wants to spend it buying neighbors. Do you understand what that means, Rosalind? Every pouch he pays out is a pouch walking around the city in someone's pocket. Every pouch is a piece of—"

He stopped. He looked at her, and she saw him decide something, weighing it the way he weighed everything, and she felt the weight of the decision land on her before he spoke.

"Of Veyra," she said. "I know. I've always known it was more than salt. I've never known *what*."

"And I've never told you," Tam said, "because not knowing kept you safe, and keeping you safe was the only duty I had left." He rose, slowly, joints cracking like green wood, and went to the window, and stood looking down at the Salt Quarter's narrow lane. "That duty's changing shape tonight. Go and see to the others first. Ivo will do something stupid—he does everything stupid when he's frightened, it's his gift and his curse both. And Mira—Mira will want to stand and fight, and she must not, not yet, not until we've talked. Tell them all the same thing: the stall closes tomorrow. No selling. No meeting. Nothing written, nothing spoken, nothing that leaves a trail. Three days, and then come back here, and I'll tell you what the guild kept."

"The guild kept something," Rosalind said. It was not a question.

"Girl," Tam said, without turning from the window, "the guild has kept the same thing for twenty years. It's why there's a guild at all. Now go."

* * *

Ivo was not hard to find, because Ivo was exactly where she had told him never to be: down by the river wall, watching the Bellpost's criers from a distance, his thin body rigid with the particular fury of the young who have just discovered the world can be taken from them.

"You're selling fear," he said, before she was even close enough to touch him. "They're going to sell us to each other, Rosalind. With *our own salt*. They're going to pay people with—" his voice cracked and climbed, and around them, she saw it happen: the river water in the mooring basin shivered. Small waves, no wind. The moored boats knocked their hulls together like nervous teeth.

"Ivo." She took his wrist. His skin was hot. "Breathe. Down. *Down.*"

He breathed. It took a while. The water stilled by degrees, and the boats went quiet, and an old fisherman two moorings down looked at the water, then at Ivo, then at the water again, with an expression Rosalind could not read and did not want to.

"Come away from the river," she said quietly. "Come away from every place you've ever loved, for three days. That's the instruction. Tam will explain everything in three days, and until then you do nothing, you see no one, you are a ghost. Can you be a ghost, Ivo?"

He looked at her, and he was nineteen and frightened and furious, and what he said was: "Can *you*? You've been a ghost for twenty years. Look what it got us."

The words went in under her ribs, where she would feel them for days. She let go of his wrist. "Three days," she said. "The chandler's on Tanner's Row. Knock two, pause, three. And Ivo—whatever you feel, the river is not the place to feel it. Not anymore."

* * *

Mira's house had a blue door and a garden that was dead for the winter, and Mira herself opened the door before Rosalind finished knocking, because Mira always knew.

"Seven bells," Mira said. "I counted them in my sleep and woke up knowing. Come in, love, before the street grows ideas."

Inside, the house was warm and smelled of rosemary and lamp oil, and Mira—sixty, broad as a door, with hands that could mend nets or break jaws—sat Rosalind down and fed her bread and salted fish and made her eat all of it, because Mira's answer to every crisis from fire to funerals was that no one should face it hungry. Only when the plate was empty did she say, "You want me to run."

"I want you to be careful."

"I'm sixty years old and I've been careful my whole life and it's bought me a blue door and a dead garden." Mira's voice was level, but her hands, folded on the table, were white-knuckled. "There are people on my street who'll sell my name for a pouch of salt, Rosalind. People I've known thirty years. I felt it today at the well—the *looking*. So don't tell me careful. Tell me the plan."

"There isn't one yet. Tam has it. Whatever the guild kept—there's something, Mira. Something he's never told me, in twenty years. In three days he tells us all."

Mira was quiet a long moment. The lamp between them guttered and steadied. "Twenty years," she said at last, softly. "He kept it from you twenty years, and now he's going to hand it to you in an evening, like it's a loaf of bread." She shook her head slowly. "It won't be a loaf of bread, Rosalind. Things kept that long go strange in the keeping."

Rosalind walked home through streets gone quiet and cold, past the dark shape of the Bellpost with its cream-colored papers fluttering, and she thought about what Mira had said, and about the coin in her pocket with its drowned tower, and about the fisherman's unreadable face by the river. The city felt different now, and it would never feel the same again: every window a question, every neighbor an account book, every kindness possibly a price being calculated.

At her door she paused. From the market square, faint with distance, she could hear the criers still calling the proclamation into the dark, the words worn smooth with repetition: *register, or be hunted. One measure of salt for every name proven true.*

Three days, Tam had said. Three days, and then she would learn what the guild kept.

She did not know, yet, that she would wish he had kept it longer.
