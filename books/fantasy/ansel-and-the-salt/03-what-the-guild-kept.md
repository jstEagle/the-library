## 3. What the Guild Kept

Tam Wren had kept the guild's records for forty-one years, and in all that time he had learned one rule above the others: *never let them ask twice.* A question asked once could be deflected with a smile and a bad knee. A question asked twice meant the asker had already decided to know.

Rosalind Eastwick had asked twice.

She stood now in the back room of his house on Tanner's Lane, where he kept the things that were not for sale and never had been: a press of ledgers bound in oilcloth, a jar of buttons that had once been someone's eyes, and above the hearth, framed behind glass gone amber with age, a map of a country that did not exist.

"You burned your ledger," she said. It was not a question. Word traveled fast through three surviving members of a dying guild.

"I burned the ledger I kept for you," Tam said. "The one with prices in it." He lowered himself into his chair by the fire, joints announcing each inch of descent like a man reading aloud from a list of grievances. "Sit down, girl. What I have to show you takes longer than standing."

She sat on the stool across from him, hands folded in her lap the way she did when she was frightened and wanted no one to see it. Twenty years he had watched her run the market stall—luck-knots woven from horsehair and thread, fever-salts that were mostly rosemary and confidence—and twenty years he had admired how completely she had made herself dull. Rosalind Eastwick, seller of trinkets. Rosalind Eastwick, widow's daughter, unremarkable, harmless. She had built her invisibility the way masons build walls, one careful stone at a time, and yesterday the crown had posted its decree at the Bellpost and knocked the whole thing down before noon.

"They'll come for us," she said. "Serah Vole doesn't post decrees she doesn't intend to enforce."

"Vole." Tam tasted the name. "I knew a witchfinder when I was young. Before Vole. He hanged my teacher's brother from the bridge at Corvane and called it sanitation." He reached for the poker and stirred the fire, though it needed no stirring. "They're all the same, these finders. They believe so hard it makes them honest."

"Tam."

"I'm getting there." He set the poker down. "You paid a boy today. At the stall. A coin with a crest on it."

Her stillness told him everything. "Where did you hear—"

"Ivo told me. He came by the way Ivo comes by everything—breathless, half an hour late, and certain the world is ending." Tam almost smiled. "He said you went white as whey and gave him back his luck-knot for free."

"It was a drowned crest," Rosalind said quietly. "A scallop shell over three waves. That coin hasn't been struck in twenty years. It hasn't been *legal* in twenty years."

"And yet there it was, in a child's hand, in Corvane market, on the very day the king declared war on everyone like us." Tam leaned forward. "You don't believe in coincidence, Rosalind. You never have. It's why you've survived."

"No," she said. "I don't."

"Then let me tell you what the guild kept." His voice caught on the old words, words he had rehearsed for decades against a day he had prayed would never come, and found they sounded smaller out loud than they had in his head. "Not the charms. Not the knots and the salts we sell to gouty aldermen. The real keeping. The reason there is a Salt Guild at all."

* * *

He took down the framed map first.

It was not large—a hand's breadth wider than a hearth-shelf—but the moment it left the wall the room seemed to change around it, the way a room changes when a window is opened onto a different weather. The parchment was heavy, salt-stiffened at the corners, drawn in ink that had faded from black to the brown of dried blood. Coastlines. Harbors. Roads running inland to hills marked with names in no alphabet used anymore.

"Do you know what this is?" Tam asked.

Rosalind didn't answer. Her eyes had gone to the cartouche in the lower corner, where a scallop shell floated over three waves, and her lips moved without sound, reading the name beneath it.

"Say it," Tam said. "Out loud. It's been too long since anyone said it."

"Veyra," she whispered.

"Louder."

"*Veyra.*"

"There." He laid the map on the table between them, weighting the curling corners with the button jar and an inkwell. "Twenty years ago, King Aldric's predecessor—the old king, Maravel, may the sea keep what's left of him—decided that Veyra had never existed. Not conquered. Not lost. *Never.* Treaties rewritten. Records burned. Maps scraped and redrawn. People who remembered were given a choice between forgetting and the gallows, and most of them chose gallows with more dignity than the king expected." He tapped the coastline with one knuckle. "And the sea rose that same winter and took the whole eastern shore, every town, every harbor, as if the water itself had agreed to the erasure. They say it was a storm. I've always thought storms are what the sea calls its opinions."

"But the guild—" Rosalind began.

"The guild was Veyran before it was anything else. Salt-gatherers. Tide-readers. The gifted of the coast, who worked small and worked quiet because big magic draws big attention." Tam settled back. "When Maravel erased the country, our founders understood something the court didn't. You can burn a library. You can rewrite a treaty. But memory isn't only in paper and minds. Memory has weight. Memory can be *harvested*."

"That's nonsense," Rosalind said, but her voice wavered on it.

"It is. Glorious, working nonsense." He pushed himself up again, slower this time, and went to the press of oilcloth-bound ledgers. From the bottom shelf—not the records, never the records—he drew out a box. Plain pine, iron-hinged, older than he was. He set it on the table beside the map and rested his hand on the lid without opening it.

"When the last free Veyrans saw what was coming, they did the only thing left. They gathered up their memories—all of them, the weddings and the funerals and the names of streets and the taste of their mothers' bread—and they poured them into salt. Crystallized them. Every grain in our vaults, every hoard the guild has hidden in wells and chapels and cellar-walls from here to the Gray Reach, is a piece of a country that the king said never was." He looked up at her. "We haven't been selling charms for twenty years, Rosalind. We've been selling *cover stories*, so that no one would look too closely at what we actually carry."

Rosalind stared at the box. In the firelight her face had gone the color of the parchment. "The fever-salts. The luck-salt. The little twists of paper I've sold by the hundred—"

"Some of those are just salt, child. Rosemary and confidence, like I said. But the core stock—the stock we never let leave the vault except under seal—that's true salt. Veyra salt. And it does more than sit in a jar being old."

"Show me."

* * *

He opened the box.

Inside, nested in rotted velvet, lay a twist of gray linen no bigger than a sparrow. Tam unwound it with the care of a man handling a sleeping adder, and in his palm appeared a single grain of salt. It should have been nothing—a speck, a crumb, invisible at arm's length. But this grain caught the firelight and held it, and held it, and did not let it go, glowing faintly from within like a coal buried in ash.

"This grain," Tam said, "is the memory of a woman named Sefa Ilves, who died the winter the sea came. It holds the last hour of her life: her kitchen, her daughter's voice, the sound of rain on a roof in a town that is now a sandbar two miles off the coast." He extended his hand toward Rosalind. "Touch it. Just touch. Don't grip it, don't close your fist. And whatever you see, remember that it already happened, and cannot hurt you, however much it wants to."

She hesitated. He watched her weigh it—twenty years of caution against the thing she had spent twenty years pretending not to want. Then she reached out one finger and touched the grain.

The change was immediate and terrible. Her breath went in sharp. Her eyes fixed on some middle distance where no wall hung. Her lips parted, and what came out was not a word but a sound—a small domestic sound, a kettle perhaps, or a spoon against a bowl—and then, barely audible:

"Oh. Oh, the light. The light through the window, it's *gold*—"

"Come back," Tam said gently. "Come back, Rosalind. It's not yours. Come home."

She snatched her finger away as if scalded and sat shaking, both hands pressed flat to the table as though the room might otherwise tip. Tears ran freely down her face and she did not wipe them, seemed not to notice them.

"A kitchen," she said. "There was a kitchen. Someone was singing. The song—I almost knew the song, Tam, it was right *there*—" She looked up at him, and her eyes were wet and furious and alive in a way he hadn't seen since she was a girl. "That wasn't a vision. That was a *place*. I was *in* it."

"Now you understand what we keep." He rewrapped the grain with steady hands, though his heart was hammering. Forty-one years, and he had shown that grain to four living people. Five, now. "Every hoard is full of that. Streets. Harvests. Voices. The whole country, ground fine and stored against forgetting. Our founders believed that someday the erasure would fail—kings die, after all, and lies rot faster than truth—and that the salt could be given back. Restored. The memories returned to the world, and Veyra remembered into existence again."

"How?" Rosalind asked. "How do you give a memory back to a country that's underwater?"

Tam looked at the map. His gaze traveled along the coast, past the scraped margins where Maravel's censors had torn away the neighboring kingdoms' claims, to the far northeastern edge where the parchment darkened into smudged blue-gray wash. There, in ink so faint it might have been shadow, a staircase descended into the sea. Steps, drawn one below another, until the water took them.

"Because the founders didn't only hide the salt," he said. "They built a door."

* * *

"The Door of Brine," Rosalind repeated. She had gotten up at some point without noticing and stood now at the window, looking out at Tanner's Lane, at the ordinary evening of an occupied city—lamplighters, a dog, a woman beating a rug—as if checking that the world still worked. "That sounds like something out of Ivo's storybooks."

"So does a country made of salt, and you touched one this hour." Tam remained seated. Standing cost him; revelations cost more. "The Door stands at a place called the Saltsteps, in the Gray Reach, where the old coast went under. It opens onto the old roads—not the roads as they are now, drowned and nameless, but as they were *kept*. As the salt remembers them. Walk through, and you walk into Veyra. Not the living country—it's gone, that's past praying for—but its echo. Its shape. Everything the salt holds, made solid enough to stand on."

"And the founders built this? A handful of tide-readers?"

"The founders had help from something in the sea that liked them better than it likes kings." He waved the question away before she could sharpen it. "The how doesn't matter tonight. What matters is this: the Door needs salt to open. True salt, freely carried, willingly given. The hoards aren't treasure, Rosalind. They're *keys*. Scattered keys, hidden in pieces so that no one person could ever gather enough to open the Door alone—because the founders feared exactly what you'd fear. They feared a king finding it."

Rosalind turned from the window. "Aldric."

"Aldric." The name sat in the room like a stone dropped in a well. "Maravel erased Veyra. Aldric hunts the gifted. Put those together and tell me what a man like that would do with a door into a country made of memory. He wouldn't open it to go home. He'd open it to *own* it. A kingdom no one can tax, no one can rebel in, ruled by whoever holds the key." Tam's hands had closed into fists atop his knees without his permission. "That is why the guild hid the hoards apart. Why the locations were split between keepers who never met. Why I have spent forty-one years being a dull old clerk with a bad knee and a fondness for buttons."

"And now?"

"Now the decree is on the Bellpost, and Serah Vole is paying informers in salt—which means someone at court already knows salt matters, which means we are later than late." He drew a long breath. "There are three hoards whose locations I hold. One more was kept by Merris Hale, and Merris is dead two years, and I never learned whether she passed hers on. There may be others I don't know. The founders were thorough about secrecy and careless about documentation, which I have complained about to their graves for four decades."

Rosalind came back to the table. She looked down at the map—at Veyra, whole and named, its harbors crowded with tiny inked ships—and Tam watched her the way he had once watched his own daughter decide whether to jump from the high rock into cold water.

"You're going to tell me to gather them," she said. "The hoards. All of them."

"I'm going to tell you what I was told, forty-one years ago, by a dying woman with salt-burned hands." He met her eyes. "I was told: *when the hunting starts, the keeper gives the burden to the one who can carry it without wanting to.* Not the strongest. Not the boldest. The one who doesn't want it. Because wanting is how kings get made, and the Door must never belong to a king."

"I don't want it," Rosalind said immediately.

"No," Tam agreed. "You don't. That's rather the point." He slid the map across the table to her, and the sound of parchment on wood was very loud in the small room. "The hoard under the flooded chapel at Greyford, outside the walls—the one we pass every year at midwinter and pretend is a ruin. That's the nearest. After that, the well at Cinderharrow. After that—" he hesitated, and the hesitation cost him visibly, "—after that, the Gray Reach itself. And Rosalind. Child. Listen to me."

She looked up.

"Once you start gathering, you cannot stop. The salt calls to itself, the way water finds water. Every grain you carry will make the next one easier to find and harder to hide. By the end, you will not be able to put it down. You will be the guild's keeper in fact, not just in name, and everything that means—every death, every betrayal, every choice—will come to you and knock." He pushed the map fully into her hands. "I am eighty-one years old. I can't run. I can't fight. I can barely stand. But I can tell you the truth, and I can tell it plainly, which is more than most keepers ever got."

Rosalind looked down at the map in her hands. The scallop shell over three waves. The drowned stair. The names of towns that had never officially existed, written in a dead woman's careful hand.

"If I take this," she said slowly, "then I'm not a charm-seller anymore. Whatever happens. Even if we burn the ledgers and close the stall and scatter—even then. I'd be the woman who knows where Veyra is."

"Yes."

"And they'll hunt me for it. Vole will hunt me for it."

"Yes."

Rosalind closed her eyes. For a long moment the only sound was the fire settling and, distantly, the lamplighter's bell moving up Tanner's Lane. When she opened her eyes again, something in them had changed—not hardened, Tam thought, but *deepened*, the way a well deepens when you finally reach water.

"Then we'd better move tonight," she said. "Before Vole works out that the informer's price tells her everything she needs to know." She was already folding the map, already turning practical, already becoming the woman the burden required—and Tam felt grief and relief arrive together, twins, holding hands. "Ivo can get us out through the tannery gate. We'll need the chapel hoard before dawn."

"One thing more." Tam reached into the pine box one last time and pressed something small into her palm: a single grain of true salt, wrapped in its own twist of linen. "Carry that always. If the worst comes—if they take you—swallow it. The memory will fill you so completely there'll be no room left for them to question. It's painless. It's even, I'm told, rather beautiful."

Rosalind looked at the tiny packet in her hand, then at the old man in the chair, and did the thing he had not expected: she bent and kissed his forehead, salt-dry skin, forty-one years of secrets.

"Thank you for telling me plainly," she said.

"Don't thank me," Tam Wren said. "Mourn me, if there's time. Now go. And send Ivo back for the ledgers—they're heavier than they look, and I intend to be difficult about which ones burn."

She went. He heard the street door, heard her low voice speaking to Ivo in the lane, heard footsteps recede toward the tannery district and the flooded chapel and the beginning of everything.

Then Tam Wren sat alone with his banked fire, his jar of buttons, and the great silence where forty-one years of keeping used to be, and discovered that a burden handed off weighs nothing at all—except everywhere it has been.
