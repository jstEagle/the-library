## 4. Serah Vole Comes to Market

The frost had come to Corvane overnight, and Serah Vole arrived at the Tallowmarket before the bell rang, when the stalls were still being dressed and the braziers were little more than smoke and intention. She liked markets at this hour. They showed their seams. A market in full cry was a thousand lies shouted at once; a market waking was only tired people arranging their goods with honest hands, and honest hands told her everything.

She wore no uniform. The crown's witchfinders did not wear one—Aldric's ministers had decided early that fear traveled better in plain cloth—and so she came dressed as what she appeared to be: a gray woman of middle years with a ledger under her arm and ink stains on her fingers, the sort of person who might be a tax assessor or a clerk sent down from the chancery. People looked at her and saw nothing worth remembering. That was the first trick of the trade, and it was not a trick at all. It was simply true. She had spent thirty years making herself unremarkable, and by now she could stand in a crowd like water standing in a cup.

The proclamation had gone up on the Bellpost three days ago. She had written part of it herself—the phrasing about informers being paid in salt had been hers, though the chancery had balked until she explained the arithmetic. Salt was the one currency every household understood. You could refuse gold on principle; you could not refuse salt when your children were crying for bread. By now she knew exactly how many names a promise of salt would buy in a city this size, and it was more than enough.

Her assistant, Pell, hovered at her shoulder with his slate, breathing through his mouth the way he always did when he was nervous. He was twenty-two and earnest and would make a fine witchfinder someday if he learned to stop flinching.

"Begin where?" he asked.

"Where the luck is sold," she said.

* * *

They found the charm-sellers clustered along the eastern row, where the light came up off the river and made the trinkets glitter: knotted cords and hagstones, fever-salts in twistets of paper, love-charms pressed from wax, little tin medals stamped with saints whose names had been sanded off two reigns ago. Serah walked the row slowly, unhurried, letting the sellers see her coming. That was important. Fear needed time to arrive and settle, like weather. If you pounced, they lied out of panic, and panicked lies were noisy and useless. If you strolled, they lied out of habit, and habitual lies had patterns, and patterns could be read.

She stopped first at a stall run by a woman with flour on her forearms who sold, ostensibly, honey-cakes, and also sold knots of red thread tied in a pattern Serah recognized from three provinces away.

"Good morning," Serah said pleasantly. "Cold enough to crack stones."

"Aye, mistress." The woman's eyes went to the ledger and back. "Buying or selling?"

"Buying, mostly." Serah picked up one of the red-thread knots and turned it over. Seven loops. She counted them without appearing to count them. "What does this do?"

"Keeps the fever off. Old custom. My grandmother tied them."

"Your grandmother." Serah smiled. "And your grandmother's grandmother?"

The woman's hands went still on her tray. "Also old," she said carefully.

"Mm." Serah set the knot down and opened her ledger against the stall's edge, and let the woman watch her write something—not her name; just a mark, a small careful cross. "You understand the new decree."

"I've heard talk."

"The crown asks very little. Registration isn't punishment. It's protection—for the gifted themselves, most of all. Unregistered gifts frighten people, and frightened people do regrettable things. We'd hate for anything regrettable to happen over a fever-knot." She said it warmly, almost kindly, and watched the woman's throat move as she swallowed. "If you remember any old words your grandmothers used with those threads—just the words, mind, not the working—you come find me. There's salt in it for you. Good white salt."

"I don't recall any words," the woman said.

"No," Serah agreed pleasantly. "I expect you don't." She left another mark in the ledger and moved on.

Pell trailed her. "She was lying," he whispered.

"Of course she was lying. Everyone here is lying. That's not the interesting part." Serah paused at a stall of dried herbs, lifted a bundle of something bitter, put it down. "The interesting part is *what kind* of lie. Did you notice? She didn't say 'there's nothing in it.' She said 'my grandmother tied them.' She gave me an ancestry. When people are hiding a gift, they hide it inside family—it's the one hiding place the law can't burn down. Note the stall. If the decree holds, half this row will register within the month, and the ones who don't will be the interesting half."

"And if they don't come in?"

Serah looked at him. He was young enough that the question still sounded like a question. "Then we come for them," she said gently. "That's all the decree means, Pell. It's a list of doors, and we knock on the ones that stay shut."

* * *

By midmorning the market had filled, and the frost had turned to a thin cold rain that made the awnings drum. Serah worked the row methodically, stall by stall, and kept her ledger open on her forearm like a falconer's cuff. She was good at this and knew she was good at it, and the knowledge sat in her the way a carpenter's calluses sit—not pride, exactly. Just structure. The world was full of small dangerous fires, and someone had to walk the rows with wet hands and put them out before they caught the thatch. Her mother had died in a fire like that, in the border years, when a hedge-witch's charm-war had escalated between two villages until neither remembered what had started it. Serah had been nine. She had never once, in thirty years, doubted what she was for.

It was at the third hour, near the well, that she saw the boy.

He was minding a stall—a narrow one, hung with luck-knots and paper twistets of what the sign called *fever-salts*, though Serah had already sampled three such twistets that morning and found them all to be ordinary rock salt with a pinch of dried thyme. Parlor tricks. The whole guild trade had gone soft decades ago, ground down into superstition and placebo, which was precisely why the chancery had let it live. But the sign said *Salt Guild*, in faded paint, and the Salt Guild was the oldest continuous trade-charter in Corvane, and old charters were where old things hid.

The boy was perhaps sixteen, sharp-boned, dark-haired, with the restless hands of someone who could not leave objects alone. He was retying a luck-knot while he watched the crowd, and he was watching the crowd the wrong way—not the way a seller watches buyers, but the way a rabbit watches treeline.

Serah drifted toward the stall without hurrying. Around her the market roared and steamed. Somewhere a fishwife was shouting prices; somewhere a child was crying about a spilled bag of chestnuts. Ordinary noise. She catalogued it and let it pass beneath her attention like water under a bridge.

"You're the Salt Guild," she said, arriving. "I thought there'd be more of you."

The boy started—visibly, badly—and then caught himself and arranged a seller's smile that fit him like a borrowed coat. "Guild's mostly retired folk these days, mistress. It's just me and my master today. Can I interest you in a knot? Keeps fevers off. Or—" his eyes flicked to her ledger and something moved behind them, quick as a fish turning—"or fever-salts. Good for the blood, my master says."

"Your master. And who is your master?"

"Rosalind Eastwick." He said the name smoothly enough, but his hands had stopped moving. The half-tied knot hung slack between his fingers. "She'll be back directly. She's gone to the wool-seller's."

"Will she?" Serah glanced down the row. The stall beside the boy's—an empty one, trestles bare, a folded canvas leaning against it—had the look of a place recently abandoned in a hurry. A brazier still smoked there. "Convenient. I've questions for the Eastwick woman. In the meantime, I'll ask you." She opened the ledger. "Name?"

"Ivo."

"Ivo what?"

"Just Ivo." His chin came up a fraction. Underneath the fear there was stubbornness; she noted it, the way she noted load-bearing beams. Stubbornness was useful. Stubbornness made people hold still.

"Just Ivo," Serah repeated, writing it. "Registered under the new decree?"

The pause lasted half a heartbeat too long. "We're tradesfolk, mistress. Charming's not magic. Everyone knows that. It's knots and salt and saying the old words for luck. Like knocking on wood."

"Like knocking on wood," Serah agreed. "And yet the decree doesn't ask whether your gift is large or small. It asks whether you have one." She leaned slightly forward, and lowered her voice—not to menace him, but to include him, to make it a confidence shared between reasonable people. This was the technique Pell could never learn from watching: you did not hunt the gifted by making them afraid of you. You hunted them by making them afraid *for* you. "There are people in this city, Ivo, who have gifts they've hidden since childhood. Small ones. Harmless ones. And because they hid them, they never learned control, and because they never learned control, last winter in Harrow Fen a boy like you burned down four houses and killed a sleeping family. Nobody wanted that. Nobody intended it. That is what registration prevents. Do you understand? I'm not your enemy. I'm the reason you're alive."

Something crossed the boy's face then—something fast and complicated, guilt and anger and a terrible yearning all at once—and Serah, who had spent thirty years reading faces the way sailors read water, felt the temperature of the morning change.

Because his hands had begun to steam.

It was subtle. The rain had soaked everyone; the boy's sleeves were wet like everyone's; but the water on his knuckles was lifting, bead by bead, rising off his skin in a faint silver mist that curled upward and vanished, and the damp knot in his hands had gone dry and stiff, and the air around the stall smelled suddenly of hot stone, of a hearth, of summer—

Then a market cart jolted past, a wheel screaming on its axle, and the boy flinched, and it was gone. Steam became nothing. Damp became damp. He stood there holding a limp red knot, breathing hard through his nose, staring at her with the fixed blankness of a cornered animal deciding whether to bolt.

Serah did not move. She did not raise her voice. Inside her chest her heart was going like a drum, steady and slow, the way it always did at the finding—this was the moment, the one moment in all the gray routine of the work that was worth the work, the instant when the world's skin parted and showed the fire underneath.

"There it is," she said softly.

"Mistress?" His voice cracked on it.

"You should breathe," she told him kindly. "Slowly. It answers to the pulse—I know it does. Whatever it is, whatever your master has been teaching you to smother, it comes up the same way fear does. So. Slowly." She kept her eyes on his and her hand flat and open on the ledger, showing him she held no iron, and behind her mild face she was doing rapid, ruthless arithmetic: distance to the well, distance to the crowd, whether Pell had his whistle, whether the boy would run or flare, and which outcome she preferred. Runners could be followed. Flares drew crowds, and crowds complicated everything. "Now. I'm going to ask you one question, and I want you to think very carefully, because your answer decides what happens next, and I would like what happens next to be nothing at all. Have you registered with the crown?"

The boy's lips parted.

And then a voice came from behind her, level and pleasant and carrying just enough weight to land on both of them at once.

"He has."

* * *

Serah turned.

The woman was plain-dressed, forty or thereabouts, with rain-darkened hair going gray at the temples and the capable hands of someone who had worked a stall for twenty years. She carried a bolt of undyed wool under one arm as though she had genuinely been to the wool-seller's, and her face was arranged into the particular calm of deep water—nothing moving on the surface, everything moving underneath.

"Rosalind Eastwick," Serah said. Not a question.

"You have me at an advantage, mistress." The woman set the wool down on the trestle and began, unhurried, to fold it—as if folding were the most natural thing to do while a witchfinder stood in front of her. "You have my apprentice looking like he's seen a ghost, and yourself holding a ledger, and me knowing what that ledger means. Rosalind Eastwick. Keeper of the Salt Guild charter, such as it is. Charms and knots and nothing else, I promise you. Now—what seems to be the trouble?"

"The trouble," Serah said, "is that your apprentice was steaming."

The word dropped into the space between them, and Serah watched it land. Eastwick didn't flinch. Didn't blink. But her hands paused—one heartbeat, no more—in the folding of the wool, and her eyes went, briefly, involuntarily, to the boy's sleeves. Checking. Looking for the evidence herself.

Which meant she hadn't seen it. Which meant either she was blind, or the boy had buried it fast, or—

Or the gift answered to fear, and the thing the boy feared most had just walked up behind him wearing an apron.

"Damp wool," Eastwick said mildly. "Steam off wet cloth by a brazier. Boys are careless with fire." She smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the edges. "Mistress witchfinder—I know why you're here. The Bellpost. We've all read it. And I'll tell you plainly, so you can write it in that book of yours: the Salt Guild sells parlor tricks. Rock salt and thyme. Red thread and knots. If there were any true gift left in this guild, it died with the old country, and good riddance, says I—we lost enough to it." Her voice did not rise. It stayed level as a shelf. But underneath the levelness Serah heard something else, something pitched below hearing, a frequency of grief so old it had become structural, like rot in a beam that holds up a roof.

"Lost enough to it," Serah repeated.

"We all did. Ask anyone my age." Eastwick met her eyes, and for a moment—half a breath—the mask slipped, not into fear but into something worse: recognition. The look of a woman measuring another woman across a table and finding her formidable. "You're thorough. I can see that. You'll go down this row and mark every stall, and some poor souls will give you their neighbors' names for a sack of salt, and you'll knock on doors, and some of the doors will hide nothing and some will hide a granny who murmurs over bruises. And you'll call it prevention." She picked the wool back up. "I won't argue with you, mistress. I'm too old and too tired. But I'll say one thing and then I'll sell you a fever-knot at cost, because business is business."

"Say it."

"When you knock on those doors," Eastwick said quietly, "remember that the people behind them are the ones who mended your city's walls after the war. Who kept the wells sweet. Who sat up all night with your soldiers' children when the fever came through, before anyone knew what a fever-salt even was. The gift built half of what you're standing in, and the gift remembers being thanked." She held out a red knot. "At cost."

Serah took it. Their fingers did not touch. She paid the coin, exact, and wrote in her ledger—Eastwick watched her do it, and did not stop her—the name, the stall, the date, and beneath it, after a moment's consideration, a single small cross.

"What's the cross mean?" the boy asked. Ivo. Just Ivo. His voice had steadied, but his hands were still wrapped around each other like something keeping itself closed.

"It means I'll be back," Serah said.

* * *

She finished the row by dusk. Eleven stalls marked, four registrations promised, one honey-cake seller whose grandmother's grandmother had "also been old." Pell tallied it all on his slate with the solemnity of a monk copying scripture, and when they reached the Bellpost he finally asked the question he'd been carrying since noon.

"You believe her? The Eastwick woman?"

Serah stood a while in the falling rain, looking back down the market row. The lamps were being lit one by one, and the steam of a hundred suppers rose through the yellow light, and somewhere in all that ordinary warmth a boy was banking a brazier with hands that could boil rainwater, and a woman was folding wool with the patience of someone who had been hiding something for twenty years and was very, very good at it.

"She lied to me eleven times," Serah said. "Counted them. But here's what matters, Pell: she lied *well*. Amateurs lie badly, and frightened amateurs lie worst of all. That woman has been lying to witchfinders since before you were born—which means she had practice long before the decree, which means she had something worth practicing on." She turned up her collar. "And the boy. Write this down. The gift flared when I pressed him—but it *died* the moment she spoke. Not faded. Died. Someone taught that boy suppression, and taught him well, and there is exactly one institution in this kingdom old enough to teach it."

"The Salt Guild." Pell's voice had gone quiet.

"The Salt Guild," Serah agreed. "Which the chancery believes has been toothless for a generation. Which the chancery is wrong about." She touched the red knot in her pocket, dry as bone, warm as a held hand. "Tomorrow we pull the charter rolls. Every name ever licensed under the guild seal, every address, every apprentice bound in the last forty years. If Rosalind Eastwick is keeping a school of the gift behind a charm-stall, I want to know how many students she's graduated—and where they all went."

Above them, on the Bellpost, the decree fluttered in the wet wind, and the rain came down on Corvane like a patient thing, and Serah Vole walked home through it already composing, in her head, the letter she would send to the chancery:

*The Salt Guild lives. Recommend immediate inquiry.*
