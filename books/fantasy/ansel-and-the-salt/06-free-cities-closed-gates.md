## 6. Free Cities, Closed Gates

The road to the free cities was a river running the wrong way.

Ivo had never seen so many people in one place, and every one of them was going nowhere. They filled the causeway from ditch to ditch—carts with their wheels wrapped in rags to quiet them, families walking with their beds on their backs, a man carrying a goat like a child, its legs folded under his arm, both of them staring ahead with the same flat patience. The dust hung over the whole procession like a second sky, and everything smelled of it: dust and sweat and woodsmoke from cookfires lit at the roadside because there was no inn that would open, no barn that wasn't already full, no wall left unclaimed by someone sleeping against it.

Ivo walked at the rear of their little line of six and counted the ways the world had changed since Corvane. Three days ago he had been an apprentice with salt-burned fingers and a master who scolded him for measuring wrong. Now he was a fugitive with a hood pulled low, walking beside a dead man's ledger, and the whole country was spilling out of its houses like water from a cracked jar.

"Keep your eyes down," Rosalind said, without turning her head. She had a way of speaking that carried exactly as far as she wanted it to and no farther. "You're looking at people."

"They're looking at us."

"Of course they are. We're the only ones not crying." She adjusted the strap of her pack across her shoulder. Under the sacking, the first hoard rode against her spine—the chapel salt, wrapped in oilcloth, warm as a sleeping animal. Ivo could feel it too, even three bodies away. He always felt it now. It hummed against his gift like a struck bell held near another bell, and his bones wanted to answer.

He looked down at the road, which was what she'd asked, and saw a wooden toy half-crushed into the mud—a horse, carved badly, one leg snapped. Someone's child had dropped it somewhere between a burning village and this endless gray afternoon, and no one had gone back for it. He stepped around it. Then, hating himself, he stepped back and picked it up and put it in his pocket, where it pressed against his hip like a small accusation.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen milestone, one of the old stones that still bore two sets of letters, the king's distances chiseled over older marks that no one read anymore. Tam would have known what the older marks said. Ivo caught himself turning to ask him and the question died in his throat with its answer.

The guild's last members sat close around a fire no bigger than a cupped hand. Rosalind. Old Maren, whose hands shook unless she was working them. Brecc, broad and silent, who had carried the chapel hoard up out of the flooded dark without one word of complaint and had barely spoken since. Odile Fen was not yet with them—that debt lay waiting in Marrowgate—and so they were six, counting Ivo, five if you didn't, and some days he wasn't sure which way the count went.

"We should travel at night," Ivo said. It came out harder than he meant. Everything came out harder than he meant lately; his voice felt like a tool he hadn't learned to hold properly. "Everyone's watching the roads in daylight. The witchfinder's riders move in daylight."

"And walk into ditches in the dark," Maren said, "with every refugee between here and Marrowgate thinking we're thieves come for their bundles." She was winding thread around a luck-knot, the motion automatic, her fingers remembering a trade that had been safe for forty years and wasn't anymore. "There are ten thousand people on this road, boy. Ten thousand pairs of eyes. You want to be invisible, you hide inside the crowd, not outside it."

"The crowd is the danger." He heard his own voice rise and couldn't stop it. "You haven't seen what I've seen. In Corvane, before we left—a woman dragged out of the tannery row because her neighbor said she'd whispered a fever out of her son. Whispered it, Maren. That's all. And they—" His hands did something in the air that he wished they wouldn't. "Informers get paid in salt. Our salt. Our name. Somebody on this road knows the Salt Guild's face, and the crown is buying."

Rosalind looked at him across the small fire. Her face in the firelight was all planes and patience, and it made him furious, how patient she could be while the world burned.

"You think I don't know that?" she said quietly.

"I think you know it and you keep walking like a market-woman going to market. Slow. Careful. Polite. Serah Vole isn't polite. She'll ride through this crowd like a scythe and she won't stop to count who's crying either."

Brecc shifted, and the ground seemed to shift with him. "The boy's not wrong about Vole," he rumbled.

"The boy is not wrong," Rosalind agreed, and Ivo braced for the *but*, and it came, patient as tide. "But speed is its own confession. A cart that hurries gets stopped. A family that hurries gets remembered. We are six travelers of no account, going to Marrowgate to sell charms, and we will arrive when we arrive, and we will be boring every single step of the way, because boring is the armor that has kept this guild alive for twenty years."

"Boring got Tam killed."

The words were out before he could weigh them, and the fire crackled into the silence after, loud as a shout. Maren's thread went still. Brecc looked at the flames. Rosalind didn't flinch—Ivo had learned she almost never flinched—but something moved behind her eyes, deep down, like a fish under ice.

"That's true," she said at last. "Boring got Tam killed. And caution kept the other four of us alive long enough to bury him. I'd rather carry his death than spend yours buying back my pride." She poked the fire apart with a stick, breaking it into embers, ending the day. "Sleep. We reach the Graywash ferry by noon tomorrow, and Marrowgate the day after, if the gates are still open."

"If," Ivo said.

"*If,*" she agreed, and lay down with her back to him, and the conversation was over, the way her conversations were always over, closed like a shop shutter, and he lay in the dark smelling dust and woodsmoke and the faint mineral breath of the salt, and hated how much he loved these people, and how little they trusted him to help save them.

* * *

Marrowgate announced itself a day early, the way free cities did—not with walls, which came later, but with smoke. A hundred chimneys' worth, hanging over the marshes like a gray banner. Then the walls themselves, black stone furred with moss, older than the kingdom, raised by cities that had never sworn to any crown and were proud of it. Ivo had seen them once as a small child, from his father's shoulders, and remembered thinking they looked like the edge of the world.

Now they looked like a door closing.

The refugees had told them on the road, in fragments, each one more bitter than the last: Marrowgate had shut its gates four days ago. Vell Harbor the week before. The League of Free Cities, which had spent two centuries boasting that no king's decree ever crossed its thresholds, had discovered fear, and fear crossed every threshold ever built.

Still they joined the crowd at the Southgate, because where else was there? The mass of people funneled toward the gate like sediment toward a drain, and the gate itself stood shut, oak and iron, with archers on the wall above and a crier on a platform below reading from a scroll in a voice worn smooth with repetition:

*"By order of the Council of Nine: Marrowgate is closed to all persons until further notice. No entry will be granted to bearers of unregistered gifts. Any person found concealing a gifted individual within the city will be expelled with their household. The Council regrets the necessity. The Council acts for the safety of all."*

*"The Council regrets the necessity,"* someone in the crowd repeated, in a voice like a knife being drawn slowly. *"The Council eats warm bread behind those walls, that's what the Council does."*

Ivo stood on his toes and looked past the heads. There were people at the gate itself—not guards only, but clerks at a trestle table set just outside the bars, taking names, sorting. He watched a family ahead of them approach the table: a mother, two children, an old man. The clerk asked questions. The old man answered. One of the children sneezed, a small ordinary sneeze, and the clerk's quill stopped moving, and the guards' hands moved to their belts, and the family was waved aside into a pen off to the left where perhaps thirty people stood waiting under a canvas awning, and Ivo understood with a cold slide in his stomach that the pen was not a queue. Nothing good waited under that awning.

"Don't stare," Rosalind murmured.

"I'm done not staring." His voice was shaking. He let it shake. "Look at them, Rosalind. Look at what 'until further notice' means. Those people have been standing there since dawn. Where do they sleep? Where do they—" He stopped, because the answer was visible: nowhere. The ditch beyond the pen was already dotted with makeshift shelters, the beginnings of a town of the refused, growing like mold against the city's beautiful black stones.

"There are other gates," Maren said. "The marsh post. The river gate for barges."

"With what coin do we buy a barge?" Brecc asked. "And the marsh post has been closed longest—they say a gifted girl drowned herself in the Graywash rather than be taken by the pen."

Ivo turned. "Then we stop knocking politely." He pulled the broken wooden horse from his pocket and held it up, absurdly, evidence of nothing. "Do you see it yet? Aldric doesn't need to catch us. The cities are doing his work for him. Every gate that closes makes the roads narrower. Every pen like that one teaches ten thousand people that reporting a neighbor might be the price of getting inside. We're being herded, and we're herding ourselves, and you want to be *boring*—"

"I want us alive," Rosalind said, sharp now, the patience finally cracking enough to show the iron under it. "That's my whole strategy, Ivo. Alive. Not glorious. Not safe, even—alive. And alive means we do not stand at a closed gate arguing doctrine within earshot of clerks who are paid to listen."

But a clerk *was* listening. Ivo saw it happen the way you see a cart begin to slip its brake—one detail out of place. At the trestle table, a thin man with ink-stained fingers had lifted his head. Not at Rosalind's voice. At Maren's hands. Maren, without thinking, had taken up her thread again during the argument, worrying a luck-knot into shape, and her shaking fingers had gone steady, and the knot was forming too fast, too perfectly, the pattern closing like a small blooming flower, and anyone who knew anything about anything knew that knots didn't tie themselves.

The clerk rose. He touched the elbow of a guard and said something, and the guard looked, and looked again, and began to move along the table's edge—not hurrying, scanning faces, working his way toward them through the packed crowd with the unhurried confidence of a man who had all day and a wall full of archers.

"Rosalind." Ivo touched her sleeve. "Don't turn around. Left of the table. Walking."

She didn't turn around. He watched her not turn around, watched her take inventory by reflection alone—in the polished boss of a shield, in a puddle, in the dark glass of a clerk's inkpot—and then, softly, in the voice that carried exactly as far as she wanted: "Maren. Hands."

Maren's fingers stilled mid-knot. Too late. The guard had seen enough; the seeing was done; it lived in him now like a splinter. He was twenty feet away. Fifteen. Around them the crowd pressed, oblivious, hungry, each person a wall of backs and bundles, and Ivo felt his gift wake in his chest the way it always did when fear came calling—heat rising under his skin, the air beginning to taste of storms, static crawling along his arms, wanting out, wanting to *do* something, and he knew, from the tannery row, from the woman who had only whispered, what it would cost if it got out. If it flared here, in front of a wall of archers and a table of clerks, they were all dead or penned, and the salt would go into a crown vault, and everything Tam had died holding would end at a trestle table outside a coward's gate.

"Walk," Rosalind breathed. "Away from the gate. Slowly. We are leaving, disappointed, like everyone else. We are nobody. Boring, Ivo. Be boring."

He was the worst boring person in the world. He walked anyway, matching her pace, feeling the guard's attention drag across his shoulders like a hooked pole. Behind them, the crier took up his scroll again—*the Council regrets the necessity*—and the crowd groaned and surged and argued, and somewhere in that churn of bodies the guard lost his line of sight, lost it, regained it, lost it again, and then a cart loaded with marsh hay rolled between them and stayed there, rolling slow, blocking everything, and when it passed the guard had stopped, uncertain, scanning a sea of dusty anonymous faces none of which were theirs, because Rosalind had steered them down a lane of tents with the smooth inevitability of water finding a crack.

They walked until the gate was out of sight. Then a while longer. Then they stopped in the shadow of a derelict tollhouse, and Maren sat down abruptly on a milestone and put her head between her knees, and Brecc stood over her like a wall, and Ivo's gift finally drained out of him and left him hollow and ringing.

"That," Rosalind said, "is why we are boring."

"That," Ivo said, "is why boring is dying. Did you see the pen? Did you actually look at it? That's not a gate closing, Rosalind. Gates reopen. That's a net drawing shut, and every city in the League is tying the same knot, and we're standing outside it with a bag full of the one thing in the world that can undo all of this, arguing about whether to hurry."

Rosalind was quiet for a long moment. Beyond the tollhouse, the marshes stretched gray and silver toward the horizon, and somewhere out there, down drowned stairs in the Gray Reach, a Door waited that none of them had ever seen and all of them now carried.

"You want to hurry," she said finally. Not mocking. Measuring.

"I want to *live*," Ivo said. "And living isn't the same as surviving. Tam taught me that. He used to say the guild wasn't the salt. It was the people the salt belonged to. And the people are in pens, Rosalind. All of them. Everywhere. And we're the only ones carrying anything worth anything, and we're walking at the speed of a Sunday stroll."

She looked at him for a long time, and he couldn't read her, and that was the wound of it—he had given this woman three years of his life, slept under her counter, learned her craft, loved her like a mother he'd never had, and in the moment that mattered he could not tell whether she saw him or merely tolerated him.

"Tomorrow," she said at last, "we go north along the marsh causeway instead of joining the queue at the river gate. There's a smuggler's landing at Sallowmere. Odile Fen owes the guild a life, and debts are paid in person." She shouldered her pack, and the salt shifted against her spine, and hummed. "You'll get your hurry, Ivo. Pray to whatever you pray to that we're not too late for Marrowgate's other gates—or that what's waiting at Sallowmere isn't worse than the pen."

Behind them, far off, the crier's voice drifted over the marsh grass one last time, worn smooth, regretting the necessity. And beneath it, so faint Ivo might have imagined it, the salt in Rosalind's pack gave a single low pulse, like a heart answering its name.
