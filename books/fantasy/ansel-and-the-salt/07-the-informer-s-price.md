## 7. The Informer's Price

The marsh road began where the king's road ended, and the difference between them was the difference between being seen and being forgotten.

Rosalind had always preferred the second.

They left Corvane before dawn, four of them moving through the fish-gate while the tide still held its breath: Rosalind, Tam Wren, Ivo, and old Maren, who had carried salt for the guild for forty years and complained about her knees for thirty-nine of them. The cart was a fish-cart, because nothing drew the eye less than a woman hauling herring inland at first light. The smell did its work. Soldiers let them pass with their scarves over their faces, waving them on as if hurrying away something unpleasant.

Under the herring, wrapped in oilcloth and packed in barrels that had once held brine, lay eleven pounds of true salt—the first hoard, pulled dripping from the drowned chapel three nights past. Eleven pounds of crystallized memory. Eleven pounds of a country that had never existed, according to every map and every decree and every schoolbook in Corvane.

Eleven pounds of the reason Serah Vole would burn the world to find them.

"Keep your eyes down," Rosalind murmured, though no one needed telling. "We're fishwives. We're nobody."

"I've been nobody for sixty years," Maren said. "I'm rather good at it."

Tam said nothing. He sat beside her on the cart's bench with the guild's map folded against his chest, inside his coat, inside his shirt, against his skin—because Tam trusted paper more than people and skin more than either. He was seventy-one years old and he smelled of ink and vinegar, and when the marsh opened up around them—all grey reed and brown water and a sky so low it seemed apologetic—he finally spoke.

"You know what my master told me, when I took the ledger?" he said. "He said: the guild survives not because we hide well, but because we are small. A thing too small to be worth finding. Remember that, Rosalind. The day we become worth finding is the day we begin to die."

"We're already worth finding," she said. "The proclamation saw to that."

"The proclamation makes us worth catching." He shook his head slowly. "Worth finding is different. Worth finding requires someone who knows where to look."

She thought about that for the next hour, as the wheels sucked through mud and the gulls argued overhead, and she decided it was the kind of thing old men said to frighten the young into caution. She was wrong. She would spend the rest of her life knowing exactly how wrong she had been, and on which mile of the marsh road the wrongness had ended.

* * *

Doss had joined the guild nine years ago, a broad-shouldered drayman with a gift for warming cold rooms—not magic, not quite, just a knack, the kind of small talent the guild had always gathered in like stray cats. He'd left two winters back, angry about something no one could quite name, and gone to work the docks at Sallow Ford. He'd sent a card at Midwinter. *Thinking of you all. D.*

Rosalind had thought nothing of it. That was the thing she would remember afterward, the thing that would sit in her chest like a swallowed stone: she had thought *nothing of it*, because Doss had been one of theirs, and one of theirs was safe, and the whole architecture of her life rested on that single assumption.

They stopped at the ferry crossing at Sallow Ford near midday, because the marsh road forked there—one branch running east toward the free cities through open fen, the other north along the river through country Aldric's patrols had begun to walk. Odile Fen's letter, sent ahead in cipher, waited at the ferry house. It named the crossing where they should meet the smuggler who would take them down the coast. It also warned, in Odile's flat careful hand: *Ask for me by no name. There are ears even in Marrowgate.*

The ferry house was a low stone building with a turf roof and a fire that never went out, kept by a woman named Bette who asked no questions because questions were bad for trade. Rosalind left the others with the cart in the lean-to and went in alone to collect the letter.

Doss was sitting by the fire.

For one absurd moment she simply looked at him, cataloguing the details like a charm-maker checking her stock: the same heavy hands, the same beard going grey at the chin, the same way of hunching toward warmth as if warmth were something that might be taken from him. Then she smiled, because nine years of habit do not die in an afternoon, and said, "Doss. Of all the crossings in all the marshes."

"Rosalind." He did not smile back.

That was the first wrong thing. The second was the way his eyes went past her shoulder, out the smoke-hole of a window, toward the lean-to where the cart stood. The third was the salt dish on the table beside him—a little crock of coarse white crystals, crown-stamped, the payment the proclamation promised to informers—and the way his hand lay over it, not hiding it, but *keeping* it, the way a man keeps a thing he has already spent.

She understood all of it in perhaps two heartbeats, standing in the doorway with the peat smoke stinging her eyes. And in those two heartbeats she made the mistake that cost Tam Wren his life: she turned to run.

"Doss," she said, instead of screaming, because even then, even knowing, some part of her could not believe it, could not say the word *traitor* aloud in a room that smelled of peat and herring and nine years of shared suppers. "Whatever they gave you—"

"They gave me my life." His voice was flat and terrible in its calm. "And salt, Rosalind. Real salt. They had a grain of it, from your chapel hoard—they showed me what it does." For just a moment something moved behind his eyes, something awed and broken, and she understood that whatever Serah Vole had shown him, it had frightened him more than any gallows could. "You should have told us. All of you, all these years—you should have told us what we were carrying. I warmed cold rooms for you. I was a *stray cat*. And the whole time the truth was salt on your tongue and you fed us parlor tricks."

"There wasn't time—"

"There's never time." He stood. He was not armed; she noticed that distantly, absurdly. He didn't need to be. "The witchfinder has twenty men in the fen already. She'll take the east fork. Go north, if you go anywhere." And then, quieter, almost gentle, which was somehow the worst of it: "I'm sorry it was me. But it was always going to be someone."

Rosalind ran.

She burst from the ferry house shouting, and the world came apart with the efficiency of a thing long rehearsed. Men rose out of the reeds—not twenty, only six, but six was enough—who had been waiting since dawn for a fish-cart that was not a fish-cart. Tam was already moving, God keep him, already hauling himself up onto the bench, already slapping the mule's rump with the flat of his hand, shouting *north, north, the north fork*—and the cart lurched forward with Ivo clinging to the barrels and Maren beside him, and an arrow took Tam Wren through the chest before the cart had gone thirty yards.

She heard it before she understood it. A sound like a door closing.

Then she was running through mud that grabbed her boots like living hands, and the marsh was full of shouting, and somewhere behind her Ivo was screaming her name, and the cart was careening along the north fork with an old man dying on its bench and the map against his heart.

An arm caught her. A soldier's gauntlet, iron-rimmed, wrenching her half off her feet—and then Maren was there, sixty years of nobody, swinging the herring barrel's lid like a shield and driving the sharpened fish-gaff she had carried since Corvane straight into the gap beneath the man's arm. He went down. Maren did not apologize. "Run, girl," she snarled, blood on her knuckles, and Rosalind ran.

* * *

They buried him at dusk, or what passed for burial when the ground itself was water.

There was a hummock in the fen a mile up the north fork, a low green island crowded with reeds, and they dug into it with the fish-gaff and a broken oar until they had a hollow deep enough. Tam Wren weighed almost nothing by then. Death had taken him lightly, the way he had done everything—quietly, without fuss, without wasting anyone's time.

He had lived long enough to give her the map.

She would replay those minutes for the rest of her life: the cart grinding to a halt under a black alder, Ivo howling for help, Maren pressing her hands to the wound as if pressure could argue with an arrowhead. Tam's face grey as ash, his lips flecked pink. And his hand—his ink-stained, vinegar-smelling hand—dragging at his own collar, fumbling buttons, until Rosalind understood and reached inside his shirt and drew out the oilcloth packet with the map inside, warm from his skin.

His fingers closed over hers around it. His grip was astonishing. Seventy-one years old, an arrow through the lung, and his grip was astonishing.

"Not the ledger," he rasped. "Anyone can keep a ledger. You—" A bubble of blood at the corner of his mouth; he wiped it away with the back of his hand, fastidious even now. "You keep the *map*. You know what I mean. Not roads. The whole of it. Where the hoards sleep. What they are. What they're *for*." His eyes found hers and held them, and in them she saw no fear at all, only a kind of fierce, exhausted relief, like a man setting down a weight carried too long. "I kept it because I was too much a coward to carry it. You're not a coward, Rosalind Eastwick. That's the trouble with you. That's why it has to be you."

"Don't," she said. "Don't talk like a eulogy. We'll get you to Marrowgate, there are healers—"

"There are healers," he agreed gravely, "and I am dead. Let's not insult each other." Something that was almost a laugh cost him dearly; he paid it and kept talking, faster now, spending himself like a miser forced open. "Odile Fen. Trust her debts, not her friendship—she'll keep faith because she owes, and owing lasts longer than loving. The Gray Reach. The Door is real, girl, I saw it once, when I was younger than Ivo—I stood on the Saltsteps and I heard it breathing. Whatever it asks of you—" His hand tightened. "It will ask. Doors like that don't open for free. When it asks, think of the guild, not of yourself. Think of the small ones. Ivo. The ones who come after."

"Ivo," Rosalind called, desperate, wanting anything but this alone. "Ivo, come say—"

But Ivo was ten feet away with his fists pressed to his mouth, shaking his head, refusing, sixteen years old and unable to watch, and she could not blame him, and she loved him for it and hated him for it, and Tam said, softly, "Let the boy be. Grief needs somewhere to stand. Let him stand over there a while."

Then his grip failed, all at once, like a knot slipping, and his hand fell away from hers, and Tam Wren—record-keeper of the Salt Guild for fifty years, keeper of eleven hoards and one impossible truth—looked past her shoulder at the darkening fen and said, in the tone of a man noting a correction in a margin:

"It remembers us, you know. The salt. Every grain. We were never erased. We were only... filed away." His eyes came back to her, unfocused now, seeing something further than the reeds. "File us back, Rosalind. File us back where we belong."

And then he was done, and the fen held its silence around them, and the only sound was the water lapping at the hummock's edge, patient as a clerk waiting for a signature.

They dug. They buried him with his coat buttoned to the throat and Maren's prayer, which was older than the kingdom and technically illegal, spoken low over the disturbed earth. Ivo cut a sprig of marsh-myrtle and laid it on the grave because Tam had liked the smell of it, and then the boy walked to the cart and sat among the barrels and did not speak again for hours.

Maren found Rosalind standing at the water's edge with the oilcloth packet in both hands.

"Well," the old woman said. "Keeper."

"Don't." The word came out cracked. "I sold charms, Maren. Luck-knots. Fever-salts. I kept us *small*. That was the whole of my genius—that and a good stall location. Tam kept the truth because he was Tam. Ivo's a child. You're—"

"Sixty years of carrying salt and complaining about my knees. Aye, I'm no keeper." Maren looked out at the fen, where the last light was drowning. "Neither am I dead. Neither are you. The guild is four people and a map now, Rosalind, and one of the four is a boy who nearly ran to Aldric's envoy last week, and one of the four just killed a soldier with a fish-gaff, and one of the four is holding the only map to every hoard of Veyra's memory left in the world." She turned, and her eyes in the dusk were pale and steady as a gull's. "So it's you. It was always going to be you. Tam knew it. I expect you knew it too, and hoped you'd die before it mattered."

Rosalind looked down at the packet. Inside the oilcloth, she knew without opening it, the ink would be fine and cramped and utterly certain, every hoard marked, every route noted, in a hand that would never add another line. Somewhere behind them, six soldiers' worth of silence, and beyond that the witchfinder with her twenty men and her polite voice, already turning east along the empty fork, already learning that the fish-cart had gone north.

Already learning that Doss had lied to her—or that Rosalind had learned to lie faster.

No. Doss hadn't lied. Doss had sent them north because some splinter of the man who had warmed cold rooms for nine years had still been in there, buried under the pardon and the salt and the fear. He had bought his life and spent a piece of it buying theirs.

"North fork meets the river at Greywick," Maren said. "Smuggler's mooring, if Odile's letter holds true. Two days' walk if the cart holds, three if it doesn't." She put out her hand—not for the map, Rosalind noticed. Just her hand, palm up, an old woman's offer in the gathering dark. "Come along, Keeper. The dead have the watching now. We've got the walking."

Rosalind took her hand. Then she untied the oilcloth, just enough to see the top edge of the map—to see Tam's fine cramped certainty marching across the vellum, hoard after hoard after hoard, and at the bottom right, in fresher ink, in letters pressed hard enough to score the page, the words he must have written the night the proclamation went up, when he had known, even then, what was coming:

*THE GRAY REACH. THE SALTSTEPS. IF ALL ELSE FAILS, THE SEA KEEPS WHAT WE CANNOT.*

Behind them, far back along the marsh road, a bird startled up out of the reeds where no bird should have been resting at dusk.

Someone was walking the north fork, and they were not hurrying.
