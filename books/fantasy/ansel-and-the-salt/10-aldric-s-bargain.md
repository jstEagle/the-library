## 10. Aldric's Bargain

The messenger came in at dusk, half-frozen and half-drowned, dragged between two of Aldric's riders like a sack of wet grain.

Aldric watched from the high window of the hunting lodge at Vell's Crossing as they brought him across the courtyard. Snow had begun again—the third day of it—and the white lay thick on the flagstones, so that the man's trail behind him was a dark seam stitched through clean cloth. The lodge had been a royal property once, before the wars, before the erasure; now it was a waystation on the road to nowhere, which was precisely why Aldric used it. Nowhere was where he did his best thinking.

He went down himself to meet the prisoner. His guards looked surprised—he was not a king who came down to prisoners—but they said nothing. They were learning, slowly, when silence served them better than flattery.

The messenger was young. That was the first thing. Younger than Aldric had expected, with river-mud dried in the creases of his neck and a rope-burn at his wrists where someone had bound him before the soldiers ever found him. He knelt because his legs would no longer hold him, and he looked up at Aldric with the particular expression Aldric had spent thirty years learning to read: terror braided with calculation. A boy deciding how much truth would keep him alive.

"You're the one who carried word to the Salt Guild," Aldric said. "From me."

"Your Majesty—" The boy's voice cracked. "I didn't know what the letter said. I swear it. I only carried—"

"Of course you didn't know." Aldric crouched, unhurried, until their eyes were level. Up close the boy smelled of marsh water and horse sweat and, beneath it, faintly, of salt—not guild salt, just the sea, just the ordinary brine of a life lived near the Gray Reach. "No one ever knows what my letters say. That is rather the point of letters."

He let the silence do its work. It always did. Around them the courtyard filled with the small sounds of a garrison settling into evening: the stamp of horses, the clatter of a cook-pot, snow ticking against the shutters. Somewhere beyond the walls, the marsh road ran south toward Marrowgate, and somewhere along it, five ragged people were carrying the last hoard of a dead country toward a door Aldric had spent three decades trying to find again.

"Your name," he said.

"Tollen, sire."

"And who gave you the letter, Tollen?"

The boy hesitated. Aldric watched him weigh loyalty against survival and found the arithmetic almost touching. Almost.

"A woman," Tollen said finally. "Older. Gray at the temples. She paid in silver and she said—" He stopped.

"Say it."

"She said if I was caught, I should tell whoever caught me nothing, because anyone who could catch me would already know everything worth telling."

Aldric laughed. It surprised even him—a short, dry sound, like a stick snapping underfoot. "That sounds like Rosalind Eastwick."

* * *

He took supper alone in the lodge's long room, at a table built for forty, laid for one. The fire was good. The wine was better than the lodge deserved. And on the table beside his cup sat the letter he had written eleven days ago and sent down the marsh road by hand after hand, each courier chosen for ordinariness, each instructed to be caught.

It had worked. Of course it had worked. Rosalind Eastwick was many things—careful, stubborn, decent in the small grinding way of shopkeepers and survivors—but she was not cruel, and she would not have let a boy hang for carrying paper. She would have taken the letter. She would have read it. And now, somewhere on the road to Marrowgate, she was doing exactly what Aldric needed her to do: thinking about him.

He turned the second copy of the letter over in his fingers. He had written it in his own hand, which was a risk, but some things required the risk. The wax seal bore no crest—only a thumbprint pressed deep while the wax was soft, and within the thumbprint, a single grain of true salt, fused there by heat no ordinary fire could make.

She would have known what that meant. She had to have known. Tam Wren had been the guild's memory for fifty years, and Tam Wren had been there, in the beginning, in the year Aldric still had another name.

He poured more wine and let the remembering come, because he had learned long ago that fighting it only made it worse.

* * *

His name then had been Ansel.

He had been fourteen, and hungry in the specific way of clever boys whose cleverness has nowhere to go, and Tam Wren had found him picking pockets outside the Corvane fish market with hands that made coins feel warmer than they should. Not stolen-warm. Gifted-warm. Tam had taken him by the wrist, turned his palm up, and looked at the coin sitting there, and said, "You've been doing this long?"

"Long enough."

"Do you know why the coin likes you?"

Ansel had shrugged. "I'm good."

"No," Tam said, and there had been something in the old man's face—not pity, worse than pity, a kind of grief worn smooth by decades of handling—"you're *remembered*. There's a difference."

They had brought him to the guildhouse behind the tanneries, the one with the crooked chimney and the cellar that smelled of the sea though the sea was forty miles away. They had taught him knots and salves and the small harmless charms that kept the guild fed and invisible. And late at night, when the others slept, Tam had shown him the locked drawer, and the jar inside it, and the salt that was not salt.

"What is it?" Ansel had asked.

"It's Veyra," Tam said. "What's left of her. When they erased the country, boy, they couldn't erase everything. Some of it crystallized. Grief does that. Memory does that. Every grain in every hoard we keep is a piece of a place that the maps say never existed—and it *remembers*. Hold it long enough, and it will show you streets. Show you faces. Show you the Door."

"The Door of Brine," Ansel whispered. Even then, even at fourteen, he had heard the stories the old members told when they thought the apprentices asleep. The stair beneath the sea. The country behind the country.

"Yes," Tam said. "And you will never touch it again, because the last apprentice who went looking for it nearly unmade us all."

But that was the trouble with showing a hungry boy a locked drawer. You might as well hand him the key and call it a test.

* * *

Aldric set the letter down. His hands were steady. They were always steady now; the shaking had gone out of them years ago, along with most other things.

He had not meant what happened. That was the sentence he had carried longest, the one he had polished smooth as sea glass through thirty years of telling it to himself in the dark. He had not meant to open the drawer. He had not meant to take the grain. He had certainly not meant to follow the salt's whispering all the way to the coast, to stand on the Gray Reach in the fog with the tide going out and the drowned stair rising black beneath the water like the spine of something sleeping.

He had meant to look. Only to look.

But the Door had been sealed, and the lock had wanted a memory freely given, and fourteen-year-old Ansel—hungry, clever, remembered—had given it the first thing it asked for. His mother's voice, singing. He could still recall the shape of the giving: how the memory left him not like something stolen but like something *poured*, warm and total, and how the stair had begun to gleam, and how the water had parted—

And how Tam Wren and three guild elders had come out of the fog like avenging weather and pulled him back from the steps, screaming, and held him while the Door closed, and told him what he had done. The lock demanded a memory freely given, yes—but it also demanded an anchor. A living gifted soul, willing, to hold the Door from the far side while it stood open. The salt had taken his gift as surety. Had wound itself through him, through the marrow of him, and for six days he had stood on the wrong side of the world in a gray echo-country built of other people's memories, feeling the Door strain against him like a mooring line in a storm, until the elders broke the anchoring from their side—at cost, at terrible cost, two of them dying of it—and cast him out.

Cast out. That was the word they used. Not *saved*, though saving him was what it was. Not *forgiven*, though he had been fourteen and the salt had sung to him in his mother's voice. Cast out, with his gift half-torn from him and a scar across his palm where the salt had entered and never fully left, and a new understanding planted in him like a seed in wet ground:

The Door could be opened. The Door had been opened. Which meant it could be opened *again*, by someone who understood it, who had stood inside it, who knew what it cost and what it paid.

They thought they had punished him. They had educated him.

* * *

The fire shifted. A log collapsed into embers, throwing sparks up the chimney like startled birds. Aldric rose and walked the length of the room, past the tapestries of hunts he had never cared about, to the window where the snow fell steadily over the courtyard, over the marsh road, over the whole gray breadth of a kingdom built on top of a grave.

People misunderstood him. He allowed it; misunderstanding was armor. Serah Vole believed he hunted the gifted out of piety or fear or the simple tyrant's hunger for control, and he let her believe it, because a witchfinder who thinks she understands her king is a witchfinder who sleeps well. The court believed he was a usurper—ambitious, ruthless, a soldier-king who had climbed over corpses to a throne that wasn't his. Also useful. Let them think the crown was the point.

The crown was not the point. The crown was a tool, the same way the hunt was a tool, the same way fear was a tool. You could not gather what was scattered unless you first made the scattered afraid enough to run, and running things leave trails. Every gifted soul flushed out of Corvane's cellars and attics was another thread leading back toward the guilds, the hoards, the old roads. The purge was a net, and Aldric had been hauling it in for years.

And now the net had brought him Rosalind Eastwick.

He had watched her for longer than she knew. A charm-seller with a stall in the Corvane market, selling luck-knots and fever-salts, harmless as bread—except that nothing about the Salt Guild had ever been harmless, and a woman who kept a dead guild breathing for twenty years under a usurper's nose was not harmless either. She was careful. She was kind in the way that made people loyal without knowing why. And she had the hoards—or access to them, through Tam Wren, through whatever remained of the old network.

She was, in short, everything he had been. Before.

* * *

He returned to the table and read his own letter once more, though he knew it by heart.

*Rosalind Eastwick, keeper of what your guild keeps.*

*I know what the salt is. I knew before you did. I was guild before you were born, and I was cast out of it for the crime of finding the Door you are now walking toward, and I am the only living soul who knows what waits on the other side of it—and what it will demand.*

*You have heard the stories. Streets of remembered stone. A country that the world says never was. You have carried the hoard through snow and ambush and betrayal to reach it, and you believe that opening it will save your people.*

*It will not. Not alone. The Door takes an anchor. Ask Tam Wren what that means, if he still lives to tell you. Ask him what it cost to pull me back.*

*But it need not take one of yours. Come to me. Bring the hoard. Together we can open the Door and hold it open—not with a sacrifice, but with a kingdom's strength behind it. Veyra restored. Not an echo. Not a grave. A country, whole, where the gifted can walk in daylight and no child is ever again taught to be ashamed of warm coins and singing kettles.*

*You want a homeland. So do I. I am offering you the only one that can exist.*

*Rule it with me.*

*A.*

He had signed it with the old name's initial. A small cruelty, or a small honesty—he had never decided which. Ansel. Let her sit with that. Let her wonder, as she must be wondering, whether the letter was a trap or a truth, and discover that the worst answer was: both.

Because here was the thing Aldric understood about Rosalind Eastwick, the thing that made her dangerous where Serah Vole was merely thorough: she actually wanted it. Not power. Not vengeance. She wanted what he wanted—a place where her people could stop hiding. Every charm she sold, every ledger she burned, every night she spent moving hoards through the dark was in service of the same longing that had led a fourteen-year-old boy down a drowned stair in the fog.

That was why she was dangerous. A woman who wanted nothing could be bought with nothing and threatened with nothing. But a woman who wanted a homeland could be offered one. And if she refused—if she chose pride, or principle, or the guild's old cowardice dressed up as wisdom—then she would refuse it not for herself but for her people, and her people would know it, and the knowing would crack the guild open from the inside, the way it had cracked open once before, thirty years ago, when the elders voted to cast out a boy rather than admit what he'd found.

Temptation was not a knife. Temptation was a mirror. You showed a person what they already wanted, held steady, until they could not look away.

* * *

Near midnight, a knock. Captain Reyes, snow on her shoulders, exhaustion in her face she was too disciplined to speak of.

"The prisoner, sire. What are your orders?"

"He eats. He sleeps. In the morning he goes free."

Reyes blinked. "Free, Your Majesty?"

"Give him a horse and three days' provisions and turn him loose on the Marrowgate road. Unharmed. Untouched. And make certain he knows it was mercy—my mercy—that freed him."

"Sire, if he carries word back to the guild—"

"He will." Aldric smiled, and saw the captain suppress a shiver, and filed that away too; even his own officers still flinched at him, and flinching men were honest men. "That is the entire purpose of letting him go. Everything that boy tells them will be true. The kindness was real. The offer is real. Tell them the king wept over the letter—I don't care, tell them anything—so long as they understand that Rosalind Eastwick holds in her hands a choice, and that I am waiting, patiently, for her answer."

"And if her answer is no?"

Aldric crossed to the window. Below, the sentries moved through the snowfall, lanterns swinging, and beyond the wall the marsh road ran black and gleaming toward the coast, toward the Gray Reach, toward the stair he had climbed once as a boy and would climb again as a king.

"If her answer is no," he said quietly, "then she will open the Door anyway—because she has come too far to do otherwise—and she will learn, standing on those steps, that the lock demands what she cannot pay. And when she turns, desperate, with the water rising and my armies at her back, she will find that my offer is still standing. Still patient. Still open, like a door."

He touched his scarred palm, briefly, the way a man touches an old wound to confirm it still aches.

"Every road leads to the Saltsteps, Captain. I built it so. Go and see the boy fed."

When she had gone, he sat alone again with the cooling fire and the falling snow, and let himself remember—not the burning, not the casting-out, but the moment before all of it: the guildhouse cellar, lamplight on glass, Tam Wren's rough hand closing his fingers around a single grain of true salt.

*Taste it,* the old man had said. *Tell me what you remember.*

And Ansel had put it on his tongue, and the whole lost country had bloomed behind his eyes—white walls, green terraces, a harbor full of boats the color of ripe fruit—and he had wept without knowing why, and Tam had watched him weep with that smooth-worn grief on his face, and said:

"Now you know what we're keeping. And now you understand why we can never open it."

Thirty years, and the old man had been wrong about only one thing.

They could open it. Anyone could open it. All it ever took was everything you had.

Aldric finished the wine, and in the morning the messenger rode east with his mercy and his message, and the snow kept falling on the road to Marrowgate, where a charm-seller was reading a king's handwriting and learning, grain by grain, that the man hunting her people had once been one of them—and that he was offering her, in his own cold patient hand, everything she had ever wanted.
