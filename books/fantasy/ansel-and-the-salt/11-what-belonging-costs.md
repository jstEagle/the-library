## 11. What Belonging Costs

The envoy came to Ivo the way fog comes to a harbor—without announcement, without shape at first, and then all at once everywhere.

He had gone out alone. That was the first lie he told himself: that it was an errand, that Rosalind had asked him to walk the perimeter of their camp above the river bend and count the horses in the far meadow. She hadn't asked. He had volunteered, and she had looked at him for a moment too long before nodding, and he had carried that look down through the birches like a stone in his boot.

Three weeks since Marrowgate. Three weeks of Odile's maps spread on oilcloth, of Tam's map—Tam's dead hands' map—weighted at the corners with river stones. Three weeks of Rosalind saying *we move at night, we stay small, we trust no one*, and Ivo hearing underneath every word the thing she never said aloud: *you are the youngest, so you are the last to know.*

The envoy was waiting by the water. Of course he was. They always knew.

He was not what Ivo expected. No crown livery, no witchfinder's gray. Just a man in a fisherman's oilskin, mending a net that needed no mending, his hands moving in the old way—the gifted way, the way that made rope knot itself and wood stop leaking. When Ivo came close enough, the man spoke without looking up.

"You walk like someone listening for footsteps."

"I walk like someone who doesn't want any."

"Mm." The man set down the net. He was perhaps fifty, weathered to the color of driftwood, with eyes the pale gray of the Gray Reach itself. "Ivo. That's your name. The king knows it. He remembers being young and gifted in a country that hated the word. He thought you'd want to hear from someone who understands."

Ivo's heart did something complicated—a stumble, a leap, a drop. "I'm not going anywhere with you."

"No," the man agreed pleasantly. "You're standing here, though. That's already more than the others gave me."

*The others.* So they had come to Tam first, before the marsh road. To Doss, after. The knowledge sat in Ivo's stomach like swallowed ice. Everyone in this war got visited eventually. Everyone got measured. He wondered what weight they'd assigned him.

"What does he want?"

"To talk. Only that. There's a boat." The man nodded downstream, where a skiff waited among the reeds with a lantern already lit inside it, warm as a held breath. "Ten minutes of his time, and then you can row back and tell Rosalind Eastwick everything, if that's what you want. He said you'd understand why he can't come to her directly. She's the keeper now. Keepers have obligations. You—" the man smiled, and it was a kind smile, which was somehow worse than a cruel one would have been, "—you're still deciding who you are. Kings remember what that's like."

Every sensible part of Ivo said turn around. Every sensible part of him listed the reasons: it was a trap, it was always a trap, Serah Vole might be behind the birches, the lantern might be a signal fire, the whole riverbank might be crawling with Aldric's men.

But underneath the sensible parts, in the place where the gift lived—that warm, restless, unquiet place behind his sternum that flared when he was frightened and sang when he was happy and had never once, not once in nineteen years, been *welcome* anywhere—underneath all of it, something leaned toward the lantern like a plant toward light.

He got in the boat.

* * *

Aldric received him in a farmhouse that couldn't have been more ordinary: low beams, a peat fire, a table scarred by generations of knives. The king himself wore no crown. He was smaller than the coins made him look, broader through the shoulders, with a face that might once have been handsome and had since been rearranged by decades of getting what he wanted. His hair was going gray at the temples in a way that seemed deliberate, curated, like everything else about him.

"Sit," Aldric said, pouring tea from a brown pot as though this were a market stall and he were a vendor and Ivo a customer with money in his pocket. "You don't drink? More for me." He drank. He watched Ivo over the rim with eyes that missed nothing, cataloguing everything, and Ivo understood suddenly how a man like this had risen: not by force first, but by attention. By making every person he met feel like the only person in the room worth knowing.

"You flared," Aldric said. "In Corvane. At the bellpost, when the proclamation went up. My witchfinder felt it from three streets away and spent a week hunting the source. She found a boy selling luck-knots instead, because a woman named Eastwick is very good at hiding things." He set down the cup. "But she felt you, Ivo. Do you understand what that means? Your gift is loud. Loud gifts get people hanged. And yet here you are, alive, because she hid you—and I'd wager my throne she's hidden other things from you too."

"She hasn't hidden anything."

"No?" Aldric smiled. It transformed his face into something almost gentle. "Ask her sometime about the guild rolls. Ask her who was admitted and who was turned away. Ask her why the Salt Guild took dues from the harbor-gifted for sixty years and never once let them past the back room." He leaned forward. "I was an apprentice there, boy. Before your mother drew breath. I know exactly what that family looks like from the outside of its walls."

The words went in like splinters, and Ivo hated himself for how deep they sank. Because it wasn't true—not all of it, surely not all of it—but some of it rang against things he had felt and never named. The way Rosalind decided things without asking. The way Tam's stories always centered the founding members, the blood-line members, the ones whose grandmothers had walked out of Veyra. The way Ivo himself, orphaned, harbor-born, taken in at twelve because his gift was useful, had spent seven years being *included* without ever quite being *inside*.

"You want a homeland," Aldric said quietly. "Everyone gifted wants one. A place where the word isn't a death sentence. Where a boy can light a lamp with a whisper and his neighbor says *thank you* instead of fetching the witchfinder." He turned his cup slowly on the table. "Veyra was that place. Not perfect—I won't insult you with lies—but ours. Mine, when I was young enough to believe in it. And it can be again. Not a memory-country behind a door. A real one. Restored. With laws written fresh, and the first law being this: *the gift is legal.*"

"And you rule it."

"I hold it together until it can stand. Yes." Aldric met his eyes without flinching, and that was the most frightening thing yet—the absence of shame. "You think I should pretend otherwise? Every kingdom has a king, boy. The only question is whether yours will be one of us or one of them. Rosalind Eastwick will open that door and hand Veyra to ghosts. I'm offering it to the living. To *you*."

"The salt—"

"—is power. Power hoarded by four frightened old people in a guild that's been dying for twenty years, spent on parlor tricks while our people swing from gibbets." Aldric's voice stayed level, but something moved beneath it, tectonic. "I'm not asking you to betray anyone. I'm asking you to choose. That's all belonging ever is, in the end. A choice, made openly, by someone who wants you." He rose, crossed the room, and pressed something into Ivo's hand: a single grain of salt, warm as a heartbeat, humming faintly with a memory that wasn't his. "Hold onto that. If you want to talk again, it will show you where. If you don't—throw it in the river and live the life she's planned for you. Hiding. Running. Being *tolerated*."

Ivo stood in the doorway a long moment before leaving. Behind him, unhurried, the king of nothing finished his tea.

* * *

Rosalind was waiting at the camp's edge when he came back up through the birches, and he knew instantly that she knew—not because of anything on her face, which was carefully, terribly still, but because Odile stood behind her with her arms folded, and because the camp had that particular silence of people pretending very hard not to listen.

"How long have you known?" Ivo asked.

"Since the fisherman started mending a net with no holes in it." Her voice was flat. "Odile saw him an hour ago. I told her to wait. I wanted—" She stopped. Started again. "I wanted to see if you'd come back."

The cruelty of it landed before he could brace. "You *tested* me."

"I needed to know."

"You needed to know! What, whether I was Doss? Whether I'd sell the route, sell the map, sell *you*?" His voice cracked upward, and the gift flared with it—he felt the birch leaves shiver overhead, felt the fire in the near pit lean sideways, and saw Rosalind's eyes flick to it, tracking, managing, *containing* him even now. "There it is. Look at that. Even angry, I'm a thing to be handled."

"That's not fair."

"Isn't it?" He laughed, and it came out ugly. "Then tell me what I am to this guild, Rosalind. Tell me honestly. Am I family? Or am I staff?"

Behind her, Odile shifted. Somewhere by the wagons, Maren had stopped packing and was watching with her hands very still. Good, Ivo thought wildly. Let them all hear it. Let it be public, the way his exclusion had been public, the way every decision about his life had been made in rooms he wasn't invited into.

"Ivo." Rosalind stepped closer, lowering her voice, and that too was a wound—the instinct to make it private, to protect the guild's face rather than his. "Whatever he said to you—"

"He said he was one of you." The words came out of Ivo like something breaking loose. "Aldric. He was an apprentice. Before you, before Tam even, maybe. He said the guild took harbor-dues for sixty years and never opened the back room to the people paying them. He said—" and here his voice did fail him, briefly, and he hated that most of all, "—he said he knew what it looks like from outside the walls. And the worst part, Rosalind, the part I can't stop chewing on, is that I *believed* him. Not about the kingdom. About the walls."

Silence. The river talked to itself below the bank. Rosalind's face had gone through shock into something older and sadder, and when she finally spoke, it was quiet enough that only he could hear it.

"The back room was closed to everyone. That's not—it wasn't about you."

"It was always about *something*. There was always a reason I was last to know. Too young. Too new. Too loud." He gestured at the air between them, at the faint shimmer his anger left there. "This. This is why. You've been afraid of me since the day you took me in. Don't shake your head—you have. You love me, maybe, the way you love a candle in a high wind: carefully, and with one hand ready to cup the flame out."

Maren made a small sound. Rosalind didn't turn. "That's not what I've done."

"Then what have you done? Tell me what the plan is. Tell me what happens to me when we reach the Saltsteps. Tell me what a harbor-born orphan with a loud gift gets in Rosalind Eastwick's Veyra." He was crying now, furiously, and refused to wipe his face. "Because Aldric had an answer. It was a rotten answer, it was a *king's* answer, but it was an answer, and it was addressed to *me*. Nobody else in twenty years has ever assumed I was the one being spoken to."

Rosalind stood very still. When she answered, her own eyes were wet, and it cost him something to see it, and he made himself keep looking anyway.

"You're right," she said.

The words knocked the wind out of him. He had come armed for denial.

"You're right that the guild had walls inside its walls. You're right that I kept you at arm's length and called it protection. You're right that I was afraid of your gift, and that fear is just cowardice wearing armor." Her voice shook and held. "And you're right that Aldric offered you a place, because that's what men like him do—they find the hungriest person in the room and feed them first. But Ivo. Listen to me. Whatever he promised you, ask him what happened to the last apprentice he fed. Ask him what he did to *this* country when his hunger didn't get him a throne fast enough. Belonging that's sold to you belongs to the seller."

"And yours is free? Yours, where I wait outside doors?"

"No." She said it simply, terribly. "Mine isn't free either. It costs obedience, and patience, and years of being small, and I have demanded all of those from you because I was afraid, and I am sorry." She reached out—not to touch him, but to hold her open palm between them, an offering, a question. "I can't give you Veyra, Ivo. I don't know anymore if Veyra is a thing anyone can be given. But I can give you this: no more last-to-know. No more doors. From tonight, you sit at the map with the rest of us, and if you still choose to walk away after that—if you take his salt and go—then go knowing it was a choice between two families, not a rescue from one."

For a long moment Ivo couldn't speak. The grain of salt sat in his pocket, warm against his hip, patient as a debt.

He didn't take her hand. But he didn't leave, either, and after a while she lowered her arm, and they stood there in the birch-shadow with the whole guild watching and pretending not to, two people who loved each other badly across a gap that honesty had widened and might, someday, bridge.

It was Odile who broke it—striding up from the riverbank with her coat flapping, a scrap of paper in her fist, her face gray as old lead.

"Rosalind." The cartographer's voice was stripped down to wire. "The fisherman's net. The one with no holes." She thrust the paper at them: a sketch, quick and precise, of the skiff, the lantern, the mooring. "I mapped coastlines for thirty years. You learn what boats mean. That skiff wasn't ferrying an envoy." She swallowed. "It was taking soundings. Depth marks, current lines, along the whole reach. They're not hunting us anymore."

Ivo felt the salt-grain pulse once against his skin, warm as a second heartbeat.

"They're charting the way to the Door."
