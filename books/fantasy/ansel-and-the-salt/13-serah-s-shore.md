## 13. Serah's Shore

The sea did not kill her. That was the first insult.

Serah Vole had been prepared to die—had, in the last grey moment before the *Vigilant* broke her back on the shoals, made a kind of peace with it. She had thought of the ledger in her cabin going down with her, all those neat columns of names, and she had thought: at least the arithmetic ends clean. Then the water took her instead, rolled her like a stone in a riverbed, and spat her out onto shingle that scraped her palms raw, under a sky the color of old pewter.

She lay there a long time, listening to the wreckage settle. The *Vigilant* was gone—she could see its bones now, mast and spar and a stretch of painted hull snagged on the rocks a hundred yards out, gulls already claiming it. Of her crew she could see nothing but a boot, turning slowly in the shallows, and then not even that. Eleven men. She would write their names later, if she lived. She always wrote their names. It was the one entry in her ledgers that was never arithmetic.

Her left arm was wrong. Not broken—she knew broken—but wrenched at the shoulder, hanging half an inch from where it ought to hang, and every breath pulled a hot wire through her ribs. She catalogued the injuries the way she catalogued everything, because cataloguing was the wall she built between herself and the world, and the world tonight was a black sea full of drowned men.

She got up. She walked. There was no decision in it; her body simply refused to lie down on enemy ground, and every ground that was not Corvane's heart was enemy ground now. Inland rose a line of low hills, dark against the less-dark sky, and somewhere beyond them, she was almost certain, lay the coast road that ran toward the Gray Reach—and toward the Salt Guild, and Rosalind Eastwick, and the door that must not be opened.

She walked with her arm cradled like a stolen thing.

* * *

The village announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke, fish guts, tar, the green rot of the tideline. It crouched in a fold of the hills as if it were hiding from the sea rather than living off it—a dozen houses of grey stone, roofs weighted with net-stones against the wind, a single lamp burning in what might have been a tavern or a chapel. Smoke rose from chimneys at an hour when honest folk were asleep.

Serah stood at the edge of the lamplight and considered her options, which were these: knock, or fall down. Her legs were making the second option increasingly persuasive.

She chose the door. She chose it with her spine straight and her good hand resting on the hilt of the knife that had somehow stayed with her through the drowning, because a witchfinder arrives armed even when she arrives half-dead, especially when she arrives half-dead. She knocked twice.

The man who opened it was old, broad as a barrel, with a beard gone to wire and eyes the pale grey of the sea he worked. He looked at her—at the crown's grey coat, salt-stiffened, at the witchfinder's pin still fastened at her collar, absurdly intact—and Serah watched him decide something. She braced for the shout, the running feet, the pitchforks. Villages like this fed the hunt willingly enough when informers were paid in salt.

"Come in out of the wet," he said.

That was the second insult.

* * *

They put her by the fire. A woman who introduced herself only as Maren—grey-streaked hair, forearms scarred from decades of lines and hooks—cut the ruined coat off her shoulder with a fish knife and worked the joint back into place while Serah bit down on a leather strap and refused, absolutely refused, to make a sound. Maren bound the shoulder with linen that smelled of some green herb, and set a cup in front of her: broth, thick with barley and dried fish, hot enough to hurt going down.

"You'll be wanting dry clothes," Maren said. "There's my husband's spare shirt. He won't mind. He's out."

"Out," Serah repeated. Her voice came out scraped. "At this hour."

"Fishing," Maren said, in the tone of a woman stating the sun rises.

Serah drank the broth. She told herself she was accepting sustenance as strategy—a witchfinder who collapsed in a ditch served no one, least of all the crown. She told herself the warmth spreading through her chest was merely thermal. She was very good at telling herself things; it was most of her profession.

It was the boy who broke the night open.

He came in near midnight, maybe ten years old, soaked to the waist, carrying a creel heavy with fish that should not have been there. Herring ran deep this time of year; everyone on this coast knew it. But the creel was full, and the boy was grinning, and he knelt by the fire saying, "Gran, Gran, I found the school, they were right up under the point—" and then he saw Serah and stopped as if he'd hit glass.

Maren said, mildly, "Mind your manners, Tam. This is a guest."

And the boy—frightened, wet, ten years old, caught—did the thing that Serah Vole had spent nineteen years training herself to see. He *let go*. She felt it pass through the room like a draft under a door: a small unclenching, a loosening of the air, the way a room feels just after a bell stops ringing. The fire flared a half-inch and settled. The fish smell sharpened, impossibly fresh, as though the herring were still swimming.

The boy had been frightened, and his gift had slipped its leash, exactly as gifts do. Exactly as Ivo's gift had flared in the market at Corvane, three weeks and a lifetime ago. Serah had written two pages in her ledger about that flare. She had known, with surgical certainty, what she had seen.

She knew what she had seen now, too.

The boy stood dripping, watching her, waiting to be denounced. His grandmother went on stirring the pot. And Serah Vole sat by the fire with the crown's pin at her throat and did nothing at all.

* * *

She should have risen. She understood that with perfect clarity, sitting there in a dead man's borrowed shirt. Procedure was procedure: mark the child, question the household, send word to the nearest garrison, add four names to a ledger. The law did not care that her arm hung in a sling and her ship was on the rocks and her crew were food for gulls. The law was the one thing that survived shipwrecks. She had built her whole life on that belief—that the law was the keel, and everything else was weather.

Instead she said, "Your grandson fishes at midnight."

"He's got the knack," Maren said, unbothered, ladling more broth. "Finds the schools. His mother had it too, before she passed. Half this village has a knack for something. You don't survive the Gray Reach without one."

"You're confessing," Serah said slowly, "to a witchfinder. In your own kitchen."

Maren looked at her then, really looked, with those flat sea-grey eyes, and said: "You ate our food. You're wearing our shirt. If you mean to burn us, say so now and I'll get the boy out the back while you finish your supper." She stirred the pot. "But I don't think you will. I've fed soldiers and smugglers and one pirate, and I can tell the difference between a woman with a law and a woman with a wound. Yours is the wound kind."

Serah looked into the fire and said nothing, which was itself an answer, and they both knew it, and that was the third insult of the night—the worst one, because it was aimed at her and landed true.

* * *

She slept in the loft above the byre, on straw that smelled of animal and salt, and dreamed of Veyra.

She had never seen Veyra. No one living had; it had been erased before her grandmother was born, struck from maps and ledgers and baptisms, its name a crime to speak aloud. But the dream was specific anyway: white streets going down to a bright water, bells, a market where women sold knots of blue thread, and everywhere, everywhere, the smell of salt—not the dead salt of the shingle outside, but a living salt, warm, almost sweet, a salt that seemed to be *remembering*. In the dream she stood in that market holding a single grain of it on her tongue, and the grain unfolded inside her like a flower made of other people's lives—

She woke with tears drying on her face and no memory of crying, and lay in the straw feeling violated by her own sleeping mind. *This is how it starts,* she told herself. *This is what the gift does. It gets into the soft places. It wears your face.* Nineteen years of doctrine, recited like a rosary: magic is theft, magic takes what it does not pay for, every charm is a debt collected from someone weaker. She had burned people on less than a dream.

But the dream had cost her nothing. And the boy's fish had fed her. And the net—

The net. She hadn't let herself think about the net yet.

She had seen it that first morning, propped on the seawall for mending: a great grey web of a thing, torn along one whole panel, and beside it an old woman running her fingers along the damage, lips moving, not quite words. And the tear closing. Not mending—there was no needle, no twine—but *closing*, the mesh drawing together like a wound knitting, slow as frost forming on a window. The old woman had glanced up, seen Serah watching, and simply nodded, the way one workman nods to another across a yard. As if it were nothing. As if it were Tuesday.

A fishing net mended by a whispered word. That was the corruption. That was the abomination the crown had spent a generation drowning in blood to stamp out. Serah stood on the seawall in the wind and tried to locate the evil in it, the way she had once located it in a luck-knot sold for a copper, in a fever-salt pressed into a dying child's palm, in a frightened apprentice whose hands glowed. Find the theft. Find the victim. Name the debt.

The net had been torn. Now it was whole. Fishermen would eat. Somewhere out past the point, a ten-year-old boy was finding herring with a knack his dead mother gave him, so his grandmother's table would not be bare. Serah turned the problem over and over like a stone, looking underneath for the rot, and found only the underside of the stone, wet and ordinary and clean.

*This is how they get you,* she thought. *This is the seduction. Small magics, humble magics, until one day the small ones are the large ones and someone is anchoring doors in the sea.* She held to that. She held to it the way a drowning sailor holds to a spar—knowing, even as she gripped it, that the sea was very large and the spar was very small.

* * *

On the third day, walking the shore to test her shoulder, she found the wreck of her convictions washed up alongside everything else.

It was a body. One of her eleven—one of the young ones, Pell, who had signed on at Marrowgate and laughed too easily. The sea had laid him out on the sand with strange tenderness, arms at his sides, face to the sky. And around him, kneeling in the shallows, were three villagers—an old man Serah recognized from the tavern, a girl of perhaps fifteen, and Maren herself—doing something to the dead.

Not robbing him. Not defiling him. The girl had her hands hovering over Pell's chest, palms down, trembling with effort, and her lips were moving, and the water in the rock pools around them had gone perfectly, unnaturally still. And Pell—

Pell breathed.

One breath. A long, rattling, impossible breath, drawn eighteen hours after the sea finished with him. His chest lifted and fell once, and his mouth moved, and though Serah was too far to hear any words, she saw the shape of them, and the girl bent close to catch whatever he said, and then the breath went out of him again and did not come back, and the girl slumped forward into the shallows, sobbing, spent, empty-handed.

They had tried to call him back. They had spent themselves trying, knowing it would fail, because the alternative was letting him go unheard. The old man gathered the girl up. Maren closed Pell's eyes and folded his hands, and looked up, and found Serah standing twenty yards away with the wind pulling her coat, and did not look away, and did not apologize.

Serah walked down the sand. She knelt beside the body of her own crewman, in front of the woman who should have been her prisoner, and asked, "What did he say?"

"He said thank you," Maren said. "For trying." A pause. "He said the salt was warm."

Something went through Serah then that had no name in any of her ledgers. She had interrogated mothers. She had signed orders that emptied streets. She had believed, with the whole iron weight of her, that she was the dam against a flood—that every name in her book was a floodgate held shut, and the misery she caused was arithmetic, regrettable but necessary, the price of a kingdom that did not drown.

And here was the flood, and it was a girl weeping over a stranger because no one should die without being spoken to. Here was the corruption, and it tasted like barley broth and smelled like mended nets.

"I have to report this village," she heard herself say. It came out flat, procedural, the voice of the keel. "You understand that. What I've seen—I'm required—"

"You'll report what you choose," Maren said. "Like every woman does." She wiped the sand off her skirts. "The Gray Reach fog's lifting tomorrow. If you're walking north, walk at dawn; the tide serves early. There's bread in your pack. And Serah—" the old woman used her name as if she'd known it all along, which she probably had, from the pin, from the way fear travels ahead of a reputation— "whatever you're hunting up that coast, ask yourself who taught you to hunt it, and what they were afraid of."

* * *

She left at dawn, as instructed, with bread in her pack and her shoulder healing wrong and her ledger—salvaged, miraculously, in its oilskin wrap—feeling heavier than it had ever been.

She did not complete the crisis. She wants that recorded, if anything of her is ever recorded: she did not kneel in the sand and renounce nineteen years. She did not tear out the pages. On the contrary, walking north along the Grey coast with the Saltsteps somewhere ahead of her, she took out the ledger at the first halt and wrote, in her precise, upright hand, an account of the wreck, the eleven names, the village, the boy, the net, the girl, the breath. All of it. Evidence, faithfully recorded. Because evidence was the last honest thing she owned, and because she trusted—needed to trust—that when the evidence was finally laid out entire, the conclusion would hold. That the doctrine would survive contact with the facts, as it always had, as it must.

She wrote it all down and closed the book, and told herself the slight tremor in her hand was the cold.

But she could not undo it either. That was the trap the shore had sprung on her, and she knew it now, walking north: you cannot un-eat the broth, un-wear the shirt, un-hear a dead man say *the salt was warm*. The knowledge was in her the way the sea was in the wreck of the *Vigilant*—soaked through, permanent, waiting. Whatever she decided at the end of this road, she would decide it *knowing*, and there was no longer any version of her that did not know.

Ahead, the coastline bent westward into the Grey Reach, and the fog over the water had a strange luminous quality, as if lit from below. And there—she stopped, and shaded her eyes, and made herself look twice—there, far out on the grey water, a sail. Then another. Warships' lines, moving with purpose, flying no colors she could read at this distance.

Not hers. Not the crown's ships out of Corvane, which should have been behind her, scattered with the storm.

Which meant they were Aldric's, running ahead of the guild, racing for the same drowned stair she was racing for—and somewhere between her and that stair, on foot, with a hoard of the true salt and a keeper who had beaten her twice already, was Rosalind Eastwick.

Serah closed the ledger, tightened the strap of her pack, and began to run.
