## 14. The Saltsteps

They found the Door on the third morning of the fog, when Odile Fen stopped the boat and said, in the flat voice of a woman reading her own death warrant, "Here. Or near enough. The sea has moved since I was young, but not far."

The Gray Reach had swallowed the horizon days ago. There was no sky to speak of, only a paler gray above a darker one, and the boat moved through it like a needle through wet cloth. The salt they carried had grown warm in its wrappings—Tam's old map, folded against Rosalind's ribs, had grown warm too, until she'd stopped being able to tell whether it was the salt or her own blood heating it. Ivo said the sea spoke to him in his sleep. Mera said the same, though neither of them would repeat what it said. Rosalind had not slept in two nights, and in the fog she heard it too: not words, exactly, but the cadence of words, a voice speaking just below the surface of the water, patient as tide.

"Odile," Rosalind said. "How do you know it's here?"

"Because the birds stopped." Odile pointed her chin at the mast, at the empty rigging. "Gulls follow a boat for the scraps. They've been with us since Marrowgate. They left an hour ago, all at once, like a curtain drawn." She spat over the side. "Nothing that flies will come near a door. My grandmother said so, when I mapped the coast for her. I thought it was a story to keep children off the rocks."

"Your grandmother was Veyran," Mera said quietly. It was not a question. They had all begun saying it that way in the last week—not accusing, not even curious anymore. Simply naming, the way you name a fever.

Odile's jaw worked. "Half. On the side that didn't matter, according to the crown." She turned the tiller. "There. Do you see it?"

At first Rosalind saw nothing. Then the fog thinned for a breath—just a breath, as though the gray itself had blinked—and she saw the rocks: a horseshoe of black stone rising from the water like the ribs of something vast and drowned, enclosing a pool of sea so still it looked solid, green-dark, lit from below by no light Rosalind could name.

And in the middle of the pool, descending into it, were steps.

* * *

They beached the boat on the outer shelf of rock and walked the last stretch, the water never rising above their ankles though the pool's center looked deep enough to drown a cathedral. The steps began at the water's edge—broad, shallow steps of white stone, salt-crusted, worn in the middle by the passage of feet that had been dead for twenty years and more. They went down. Rosalind counted eleven before the water took them, and still they went on beneath it, pale and clear as bone, descending into the green dark.

Nobody spoke. Ivo's hand found Rosalind's sleeve and gripped it, and she let him. Mera stood with her arms wrapped around the satchel of salt as if it were a child. Odile stayed by the boat. She had said, in Marrowgate, that she would bring them here and no further, that she had debts to the guild but not her soul, and Rosalind had not argued. Some doors a person should not have to walk through twice.

"It's warm," Ivo said. His voice came out strange, hushed, the way people speak in chapels. "The water. Rosalind—it's warm as bathwater. In the Reach. In open sea."

Rosalind knelt and put her hand in. He was right. The cold of the Gray Reach had been in their bones for days, and here the water lay against her skin like something living, like a palm held out. And when she drew her hand back, the salt water clung to her fingers in a fine crystalline film that did not drip away but sat there, glittering, until she wiped it on her coat.

"The salt remembers," Tam had told her, in the back room of the shop, in the last calm hour of the old world. "That's all it does, girl. It remembers. Everything else we ever told ourselves about it was decoration." He had been dying even then, though none of them knew it yet, and he had pressed the map into her hands the way a man hands over a debt. "The Door of Brine isn't a door the way you're thinking. It's a wound. Veyra didn't vanish. It was *unmade*—struck from the memory of the world, every map burned, every name scraped from every stone. But the sea keeps what the land is made to forget. The salt is Veyra's memory, crystallized. And where enough of it gathered—" he had tapped the map, the little horseshoe of ink he'd drawn from Odile's stolen charts, "—the sea never quite forgot how to be a country."

Now Rosalind stood on the Saltsteps with the whole of what remained of the guild's hoard in a satchel on Mera's back, and she understood that they had not come to find a door. They had come to return something. The thought arrived whole, the way the worst thoughts do, and she set it down inside herself carefully, to be dealt with later, or never.

The lock was at the waterline.

* * *

It was not a lock the way locks are made in Corvane—no iron, no keyhole, no mechanism. It was simply the eleventh step, the last dry one, and set into its face a depression the size of two cupped hands, smooth as a basin, rimmed with characters worn nearly to ghosts. Rosalind knew the script. Every guild member did, though none of them could read it; it was the script on the drowned crest on the coin the child had paid her with, a lifetime ago, in a market that felt like another country's memory. Which, she supposed, it was.

Odile, from the boat, called out: "My grandmother's charts had a word for it. *Selvane.* The giving-stone. She wrote that the old roads would open to a freely given memory, and never to a taken one. She thought it was poetry."

"Everything true is poetry until it works," Mera said. She was crying without any noise, the way she did everything, efficiently, without disturbing anyone. "Rosalind. It has to be one of us, doesn't it. It has to be given."

Rosalind looked at the basin. It was empty and it was waiting, and the sea around the steps had gone utterly still, and the voice beneath the water had stopped speaking, as though it too were listening.

"Freely given," she repeated. "Not taken." She thought of Tam. She thought of the ledger she had burned, of twenty years of small charms and small lies, of luck-knots that did nothing and fever-salts that did a little, of the whole careful architecture of hiding she had built her life inside, and how it had all come down to this: a stone basin at the edge of a drowned country, asking for the one thing she had never sold, never traded, never even spoken aloud.

Her earliest memory of her mother.

She was four years old, or nearly. There was a kitchen with a blue door—the blue of a certain hour of evening that Rosalind had never found again in any sky—and her mother was salting something, working coarse salt between her fingers over a bowl, and she was singing. Not a song anyone had words for anymore. A song in the old language, the one that got you looked at twice even then, and her mother's hands moved in time with it, and the salt fell through the lamplight like slow snow, and her mother looked up and saw Rosalind watching and did not stop singing. That was the whole of it. That was everything. A blue door, falling salt, a woman who saw her child watching and did not stop.

Her mother had been taken in the first purges, the ones before Aldric, the quiet ones nobody called purges yet. Rosalind had been six. She had kept this memory the way you keep a coal alive under ash—never blowing on it, never letting it flare, just keeping it, for thirty years, against a cold that had no other remedy.

And now a stone wanted it.

"Rosalind." Ivo's hand on her arm. "You don't have to. We can find another way. There's always—"

"There isn't," Rosalind said, and was surprised at how steady her voice came out. "Ivo. Listen to the water. It's been waiting for us since before you were born. Everything else we have is borrowed. This—" she touched her own temple, lightly, "—this is the only thing I own outright."

She knelt on the eleventh step. The stone was warm beneath her knees. She cupped her hands and held them over the basin, and she did not know the mechanics of it, the grammar of the giving, so she simply did the only thing she knew how to do, the thing she had done at a hundred market stalls: she offered.

She gave it the blue door. She gave it the lamplight and the falling salt, the slow snow of it, the way it caught the light on the way down. She gave it the song, note by note, though she had not let herself hear it in thirty years and it came back whole, every turn of it, as though it had been waiting inside her all this time, patient as the sea. She gave it her mother's hands, the salt caught in the creases of her knuckles. And she gave it the last thing, the truest thing, the thing she had not known was part of the memory until this moment: her mother looking up, seeing her child watching, and singing on. *You are allowed to see,* that look had said. *You are allowed to know what you are.*

The basin filled with light.

It was not a bright light. It was the color of the memory itself—lamplight gold, evening blue—and it rose from the stone like breath from a mouth, and the characters around the rim woke one by one, filling with that gold-blue glow, and Rosalind heard, from everywhere and nowhere, from the water and the rock and the inside of her own ribs, the song.

Her mother's song, in her mother's voice.

She did not remember deciding to weep. She was weeping, and the salt of her tears ran down her chin and dripped onto the step, and where each drop fell the stone glowed briefly, softly, as though the Door were catching even those, even the grief, and keeping it.

"Rosalind—" Mera's voice, cracked. "Look. *Look.*"

The sea was opening.

* * *

It did not roar. That was the terrible, beautiful thing. The water on the steps simply began to part, the way a curtain parts, folding back from the descending stair in two slow silver walls, and the stair went down and down, lit now from within by that gold-blue glow, and the air that rose up out of it smelled of bread baking and woodsmoke and a particular kind of rain on a particular kind of stone, and Rosalind knew that smell, had never smelled it before in her life and knew it absolutely, the way you know a face in a dream.

"Odile," Rosalind called, and her voice shook and she let it. "You should see this."

"No," Odile said, from the boat, and then, after a long moment, quieter, "Describe it to me. That's enough. That will have to be enough."

So Rosalind described it, kneeling on the eleventh step, while the guild's survivors gathered around her—Mera with the satchel, Ivo with his eyes enormous and wet, old Wenna gripping the rock with both hands as though the world had tilted, which it had. She described the stair going down into the parted sea, and the light, and the smell of bread and rain.

And then, at the bottom of the stair, where the water stood folded back on either side like the walls of a corridor, they saw it.

Veyra.

Not Veyra. That was the thing Rosalind understood in the first breath and could not stop understanding, over and over, with each step down. It was not the country. The country was gone; the country had been *unmade*; nothing under heaven could give back what the world had been made to forget. But the sea had not forgotten. The sea had kept the shape of it, the way a bed keeps the shape of a sleeper, the way salt keeps the shape of whatever it dries upon—and what lay below them, at the foot of the Saltsteps, was that shape: a country built of memory itself.

Gray streets under a gray-gold sky. Rooftops of slate that shimmered like fish scales. A harbor with boats at anchor, and the boats were real enough, and the water they floated on was not water at all but a kind of shining stillness, and the figures on the docks—there were figures, distant, moving, going about the business of an evening—were made of the same gold-blue light as the lock, as though the salt remembered the people too, remembered them walking and hauling and laughing, and could not bear to let even that go.

A bell was ringing somewhere. It rang the way the song had sung—whole, unbroken, as though twenty years of erasure had been a single night's sleep it was now waking from.

"It's not real," Wenna whispered. She had come down three steps without noticing; her knuckles were white on Ivo's arm. "It can't be real. It's the salt. It's all the salt, all the hoards, all the memories, it's—" her voice failed and she tried again. "It's everyone we ever lost, still walking around in there."

"Or the shape of them," Mera said. "The memory of them." She was holding the satchel against her chest and the satchel was glowing through the canvas, softly, in rhythm with the distant bell. "The sea kept what the land was made to forget. Tam told us. This is what keeping looks like."

Ivo went down the steps first. Rosalind would remember that always—how the boy simply walked, no hesitation, no ceremony, down into the parted sea toward the gray country, because for him, she understood, there was no question to ask. He had spent his whole life in a kingdom that called what he was a crime. Here was a country where what he was was the *air*.

The moment his foot touched the last step and met the harbor cobbles, the light of the nearest figure turned toward him. It had no face, exactly. It had the *idea* of a face, the way the streets had the idea of streets. But it inclined its head, the way a shopkeeper nods to a customer, the way a neighbor nods to a neighbor, and Ivo made a sound Rosalind had never heard him make, a small broken sound like a boy of eight who has been lost a very long time and has just, against all odds, seen his own front door.

Rosalind followed him down.

The air of the echo-country was warm. That was the thing no one had warned her about, because no one had known: it was warm, and it smelled of bread, and under her boots the cobbles gave slightly, springy, like ground after rain, like the memory of ground. The bell rang on. Down the gray street, lamps were being lit by figures of light, one by one, in windows of houses that had been burned or pulled down or struck from every deed and chart in Corvane, and here they stood, and here their windows glowed.

Mera came down with the satchel. Wenna came down last, backwards, watching the sea-walls that stood folded open behind them, as if she did not trust the door to stay.

And it was then, standing on the cobbles of a country that did not exist, holding the last salt of Veyra in a canvas bag while its own memory walked and rang bells around her, that Rosalind felt it—the thing Tam had tried to tell her in the back room, the thing she had not been ready to hear.

The satchel was pulling. Gently, patiently, the way a tide pulls. The salt wanted to be here. The salt had *always* wanted to be here. Every hoard they had gathered, every grain they had carried through ambush and storm and betrayal, had not been fleeing toward this place.

It had been coming home.

"Rosalind." Ivo was standing at the mouth of a street she did not know, looking back at her, and his face in the gray-gold light was the face of someone standing at the edge of everything he had ever wanted. "The bell. It stopped."

She listened. He was right. The silence was enormous.

And then, from far down that street—from the direction of the harbor, from the direction of the Door itself, from the gray country's one border, its one wound—came a second sound. Footsteps. Many of them. And a voice, thin across the remembered air, a voice she had heard only once, decades ago, from a boy being dragged out of the guild's back room, but knew instantly, the way you know a scar:

"Rosalind Eastwick." King Aldric's voice. "You brought it home. Now we can talk."
