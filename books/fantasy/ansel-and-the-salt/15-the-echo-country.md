## 15. The Echo Country

The light in Veyra came from everywhere and nowhere.

Ivo noticed it first as they climbed the last of the Saltsteps — the stair that had been a door, the sea that had been a wall — and stepped out onto grass that was not quite grass. It gave beneath his boots like moss after rain, and it was the color of old pewter, and it did not bend in any wind because there was no wind. The light fell on them from all directions at once, sourceless and even, the way light falls underwater or inside a pearl.

"Is it morning?" Odile Fen asked behind him, her voice hushed in a way Ivo had never heard from her. She had mapped this country once, with ink and memory, and now she stood inside her own cartography like a woman walking into a painting she'd made blindfolded.

"It's whatever it remembers of morning," Rosalind said.

They were nine now. Nine survivors out of a guild that had once filled three market stalls and a warehouse and a chapel's worth of hidden hoards. Tam was not among them, and neither were the two who'd been lost to the storm on the Gray Reach, and Ivo felt their absence the way you feel a missing tooth — the tongue keeps going back to the gap. He carried one of the salt casks himself, slung across his shoulders in a harness of rope, and the salt within it was warm against his spine. Warmer than it had ever been. It hummed, very faintly, the way a struck bell hums after the sound has technically ended.

Before them, down a gentle slope of pewter grass, lay a city.

It was Veyra. Odile said the name aloud, half-swallowed, and Tam's map — which Rosalind carried now, wrapped in oilcloth against her chest — had called it by its true name too, though Ivo had never learned to say it right. The city rose along a harbor that held no ships, streets climbing terraced hills toward a citadel whose towers were slender as candles. And everything — the walls, the roofs, the cobbles, the little bridges over canals that gleamed without water — everything was made of gray. Not dead gray. Living gray, if such a thing could be: gray shot through with faint movement, like smoke seen through frosted glass, like the shimmer on a fish's flank.

"It's the salt," Rosalind said quietly. "All of it. Every grain we ever sold, every hoard we hid, everything the guild drew from the sea for two hundred years. It came home."

* * *

They walked into the city because there was nothing else to do. That was the honest reason, and Ivo admitted it to himself as they passed under an archway carved with drowned crests — the same crest that had been stamped on a child's coin in Corvane, a lifetime ago, in another chapter of his life. You could not stand before a whole country and not walk into it. Curiosity was a kind of gravity.

The streets remembered feet. That was the second thing he understood, and it frightened him more than the sourceless light. Where they walked, the cobbles warmed slightly, and faint impressions bloomed around their steps — not footprints exactly, but echoes of footprints, ghost-pressures of other walkers, long processions of them, vendors and children and soldiers and lovers, all the traffic of a city that had been erased so thoroughly that even its name had been scraped from maps and tongues. The city remembered being walked upon. Their walking reminded it.

And then the voices started.

Not voices, precisely. More like the memory of voices — a murmur rising from the walls themselves, wordless at first, the way you hear a crowd through a closed window. But as they went deeper, as the streets narrowed and the gray buildings leaned close overhead, the murmur resolved. A woman calling someone in to supper, in a language Ivo didn't know but somehow almost understood, the way you almost understand a song. Children counting a skipping rhyme. A man laughing, low and helpless, at something he'd been told. A bell ringing somewhere — not alarm, just the hour, just the ordinary tolling of an ordinary evening in a country that had believed it would have ten thousand more evenings.

"Stop," Odile whispered, and pressed her hand flat against a wall. Her eyes had gone wet. "This is the fishmarket quarter. This is — I sat here. I sat on this step and ate figs and argued with a man about tide-tables, and I thought I would come back the next year, and—" Her voice failed. She took her hand off the wall as if it had burned her, and looked at her palm, and Ivo saw that the gray had left a faint shimmer on her skin, like salt-dust.

"The salt remembers," Rosalind said. "That's all this is. Memory given shape." But her voice wasn't steady either, and Ivo watched her touch the oilcloth bundle at her chest — Tam's map, Tam's life's work, Tam's grave unmarked on a marsh road — and he understood that she was holding herself together with both hands.

They made camp that night — if night it was; the sourceless light dimmed slowly, graciously, as if the country remembered what dusk was for — in a square where a fountain stood dry. The fountain's basin was full of salt. Not scattered grains but a smooth white crust, thick as snowfall, and when Ivo knelt beside it and touched it, the memories came up through his fingers like heat through iron.

He saw a wedding. He saw the bride's face clearly, a girl no older than him, laughing while her sisters threw grain — real grain, wheat, gold-colored — and the whole square was full of people throwing it, and the sound of the cheering was so vivid that Ivo flinched. Then the vision turned, the way dreams turn, and he saw the same square empty, the same buildings graying, the people gone — not dead, worse than dead, *unwritten*, erased so completely that the world had forgotten it had ever needed to kill them — and the salt creeping in over the cobbles like frost claiming a windowpane.

He pulled his hand back. His heart was slamming.

"Ivo." Rosalind crouched beside him. She was older than him by twenty years and looked it tonight, every year of it, the firelight — the memory-light — carving lines the journey had dug. "Don't drink it all at once. The salt gives, but it takes too. You feel that?"

"Yes."

"That's why the guild never told. That's why we sold luck-knots and fever-salts and let Corvane believe we were charlatans. Because this—" she gestured at the sleeping square, the murmuring walls, the whole impossible breathing country — "this is not a thing you can carry and stay whole. You have to take it in sips."

But even as she said it, Ivo saw her looking past him, up the terraced streets toward the citadel, and her expression was not caution. It was hunger. Grief and hunger braided together, and he recognized it because he wore the same expression, he suspected, whenever he looked at anything he wanted.

* * *

It was on the third day — or third dimming, since days here were only a courtesy the country paid to habit — that Ivo found the truth.

He had wandered alone. That was the argument they'd all been having in small ways since the Gray Reach: whether they moved as one body or nine separate hungers. Rosalind wanted discipline, watch-rotations, the Door guarded. Ivo wanted to *know*, and knowing meant walking, and walking meant alone, and alone was where he had always done his best surviving anyway. So while the others slept in a merchant's house whose rooms still remembered their furniture — gray outlines of chairs and beds, soft as breath — Ivo climbed.

The citadel at the top of the city was less a fortress than a lighthouse, he decided. Its tower was hollow, open to the sourceless sky, and in its center, descending below the floor, was a shaft. And in the shaft was the sea.

Not the Gray Reach they had sailed. Something older, darker, utterly still — a column of black water filling the shaft's lower reaches like ink in a well, and rising from it, impossibly, the Saltsteps themselves, continuing downward. He lay on his stomach at the shaft's edge and looked down, and the water looked back, and he felt it notice him the way you feel a stranger's gaze between your shoulder blades.

The salt in his pack — he'd taken to carrying a pouch of loose grains, a habit from the road — grew hot against his hip. Hot enough to sting. And the water began to speak.

Not in the murmur of the streets. In his own voice.

*Ivo.* It said his name the way his mother used to say it, before the fever, before the workhouse, before the guild found him starving behind a Corvane tannery with sparks jumping off his fingers and everyone ready to call it devilry. *Ivo. You're home.*

"I'm not," he said aloud, and his voice cracked in the empty tower. "This isn't home. This is a memory wearing a country's clothes."

*Isn't that what home always is?* the water said, reasonable, patient, terribly kind. *A memory you keep choosing?*

He should have run then. He knew that later, with the clarity hindsight gives like a lamp. Instead he knelt at the edge of the shaft and put his hand into the black water.

It was warm. Blood-warm. And the moment his skin broke its surface, the whole shaft lit from beneath — a slow gold bloom, like sunrise underwater — and the visions poured into him, but different now, ordered, deliberate, as if something had stopped dreaming and started *talking*. He saw the Saltsteps as they had been: crowded, thronged, pilgrims descending into the sea and returning with salt in their hands and wonder on their faces. He saw the keepers of the Door — robed figures, generations of them, standing in this very tower — and he saw what they did.

One of them stood in the water. Always one. Never two.

The visions showed him with terrible patience: the keeper standing waist-deep in the black sea, arms open, and the gold light flowing *through* them, drawn up out of their body the way a wick draws flame, and the Door above — the Door of Brine, the passage back to Corvane's gray skies — held wide, stable, permanent, anchored. Anchored the way a ship is anchored. By something that cannot leave.

He saw what happened when the keeper grew tired. When the keeper's gift thinned, frayed, guttered like a candle down to its socket. The light dimmed. The Door shuddered. And the other keepers — gently, sorrowfully, with rituals and hymns and hands laid on shoulders — helped the old one sink, and brought a new one down into the water, and the light blazed again.

*Now you understand,* the sea said, in his mother's voice, in Tam's voice, in Rosalind's voice, cycling through them like a peddler showing wares. *You see how it works. You see what it costs. And you are gifted, Ivo. You are strong. You could hold it for fifty years. Sixty. A whole lifetime of being the reason a country exists.*

"How long has it been empty?" he whispered. His hand was still in the water. He couldn't remember deciding to leave it there.

*A hundred years,* said the sea. *Since your Aldric tore himself free and fled up the stairs, and the Door began to close, and the country began to forget itself faster and faster. Look at the edges of things, Ivo. Look at the harbor. Look at the far hills. They are already thin.*

And he looked, and it was true. The city's center was dense with memory, vivid, humming — but the outskirts blurred. Streets out toward the walls faded into gray fog that was not weather but *absence*, the country forgetting its own extremities like an old man losing the ends of his fingers. Even in the three days they'd been here, the fog had crept closer. He was sure of it. The fountain square they'd camped in — he tried to picture it and found the picture already smudged, the bride's face harder to hold than it had been.

The country was dying. Had been dying for a century. And it could live — truly live, doors open, roads running down to Corvane, a homeland for every gifted child hunted through every kingdom — if someone stood in the water.

Forever.

Ivo took his hand out of the sea. The gold light sank. He sat in the hollow tower for a long time, in the courteous false dusk, listening to the water say nothing now, having shown him everything it wished to show, patient as only a trap can be patient.

Then he went down the terraced streets to find Rosalind, and with every step he argued with himself, and lost.

Because here was the thing he could not unsay to his own heart: he had spent his whole life belonging nowhere. Not in Corvane, where his gift was a crime. Not in the guild, entirely — he loved them, God help him, he loved them like family, but he had always felt the seams, the way Rosalind's generation kept the deep knowledge among themselves, the way Tam had chosen *him* almost by accident, the way there had always been members who mattered more and members who carried casks. He had followed them across a kingdom and a sea because they were the closest thing to home he had. And now home stood actual and golden-lit before him, asking only one small thing, one willing body, one life —

— and wasn't a life the exact price every homeland in history had ever charged? Paid by someone. Usually someone young.

*Fifty years,* the sea had said. *Sixty.*

He was nineteen.

* * *

He found Rosalind at the fountain square, awake, sitting on the dry basin's rim with Tam's map open across her knees, though there was nothing left on it to consult. The others slept in the memory-furniture house. The false stars — gray points, arranged in constellations some dead astronomer had loved — hung over the terraces.

"You've been to the tower," she said, before he'd said a word. She looked up at him, and her face in the gray starlight was terrible with foreknowledge. "I can see it in you. What did it show you?"

"It showed me how the Door stays open." His voice came out flat. He was proud of that, later — flat, controlled, a courier delivering facts instead of a boy delivering a wound. "It needs a keeper. A living gifted person, standing in the water, feeding it. Forever. That's the anchor. That's the whole architecture of paradise — it runs on a prisoner."

Rosalind closed the map slowly. "And it offered you."

"It didn't offer. It just... explained. Very reasonably." He laughed, and hated the sound. "In my mother's voice, Rosalind. It wears voices. It knows exactly what each of us is starving for and it serves it up like soup."

For a long moment she said nothing. The fountain's salt crust glittered. Somewhere up a side street, a wall murmured a fragment of lullaby to no one.

"We seal it," Rosalind said at last. "We take what we came for — the hoards, the memories, whatever can be carried — and we seal the Door behind us, and no one ever stands in that water. No one. Not you, not me, not Aldric, not a stranger, not a saint. We let Veyra rest."

"Let it *die*, you mean. Let it finish fading. All of this—" he swept his arm at the sleeping city, the murmuring streets, the whole breathing echo of a murdered country — "goes out like a lamp. And we go back to cellars and luck-knots and burning ledgers, and the next Ivo gets caught by the next witchfinder, and there's no door anywhere, ever, for anyone."

"Yes," Rosalind said simply. "That is what I mean."

"And that's *enough* for you?"

"No," she said, and the word came out of her like a tooth pulled. "It is not enough. It is not nearly enough, and it is unjust, and I am furious about it, and I will spend the rest of my life furious about it. But I buried Tam on a marsh road, Ivo, and I buried the guild's whole quiet world in one afternoon in Corvane, and I have learned the difference between a door and a mouth." She stood, and she was not tall, but she seemed it. "That water is a mouth. It smiles and it feeds you and it says *home*, and it does not tell you that home means never leaving. I will not feed it. Especially not the person I—" She stopped. Recomposed herself, brick by brick, the way she always did. "Especially not you."

Ivo stood in the gray starlight with the sea's warmth still ghosting his hand, and the argument he'd lost on the stairs down from the tower — *isn't a life the price of every homeland* — rose in him again, and this time he almost said it aloud. Almost told her that maybe some prices were worth paying, that maybe she'd carried guilt so long she'd forgotten the difference between sacrifice and suicide, that maybe the choice belonged to the one standing in the water and no one else.

He didn't say it. But he didn't unthink it either, and the thought stayed in him like a splinter, working deeper.

Below them, far down the terraced city, at the edge of the fading harbor where the fog of forgetting gnawed at the quays, a light appeared. Small, moving, wrong — a torch-flame burning *white*, cold, disciplined. Torchlight from the world of maps and kings.

And behind it, faint on the salt-warm air, came the sound of many boots on the Saltsteps, descending.

"They're here," Ivo said. "Rosalind — Aldric is here."
