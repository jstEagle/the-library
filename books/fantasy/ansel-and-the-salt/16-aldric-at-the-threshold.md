## 16. Aldric at the Threshold

The echo-country had a way of making time feel like weather.

Rosalind felt it first as a pressure behind her eyes, the same dull ache that came before storms on the Gray Reach. The streets of remembered Veyra stretched around them in shades of gray and salt-white—houses built of half-remembered stone, windows that held the suggestion of light rather than light itself, doorways that opened onto rooms no one could quite describe afterward. Everything here was almost. Almost real. Almost warm. Almost home.

The guild had made camp in what Ivo swore had been a market square. There was a fountain at its center that ran with water the color of old glass, and when Rosalind cupped her hands in it, she tasted brine and something sweeter beneath—honey, perhaps, or the memory of honey, from a woman who had once sold sweetmeats near this square and whose name the salt still carried.

Tam would have known her name. Tam was three weeks dead, and Rosalind still caught herself turning to speak to him, still felt the map's weight in the satchel against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

"Keeper." Odile Fen stood at the edge of the square, her cartographer's eyes narrowed at the horizon—which was not a horizon at all, but a soft gray blur where remembered streets gave way to remembered sea. "The fog's moving wrong."

Fog did not move in the echo-country; it simply was, hanging between the buildings like breath held too long. But Odile was right. Something out there was pushing it aside, parting it in a long straight line, the way a ship parts water.

Rosalind rose. Her knees ached. She was fifty-one years old and she had been walking through a dead country for two days, and every part of her body wanted to lie down in the gray grass and sleep until the world made sense again.

"How far?" she asked.

"If it's what I think it is," Odile said, "close enough that we should have moved an hour ago."

Ivo came running from the eastern street, his young face tight with a fear he was trying—and failing—to master. Behind him, the other survivors gathered: Maren with her burned hands, old Pell who hadn't spoken since Tam died, the twins Sef and Ansel clutching their small share of the hoard like children holding bread. Twenty-two of them had left Corvane. Eleven knelt now in the ghost of a market square, and the salt they carried hummed against their skin like a hive of bees thinking one thought.

"It's soldiers," Ivo said. "On the shore. On *our* shore." His voice cracked on the word, and Rosalind saw how much it cost him—the echo-country had given him something none of them had expected, a place where his gift didn't need hiding, where the air itself seemed to approve of him. He had walked these streets touching everything, laughing once, actually laughing, and now his face was doing the arithmetic of loss faster than Rosalind wanted to watch.

"How many?"

"Fifty. Maybe more. And banners." He swallowed. "Not the crown's banners, Keeper. Older ones. Torn ones. They look like—" He stopped, as if saying it might make it worse.

"Like Veyra's," Rosalind finished quietly.

* * *

They watched from the upper floor of a house that remembered being a counting-house, its windows tall and clouded, its floors solid only where enough people had loved them. Below, on the pale strand where the Saltsteps emerged from the sea, Aldric's vanguard was making camp with the terrible efficiency of men who had crossed a country that did not exist to do so.

But it was not the crown's army. That was the thing Rosalind kept returning to, the thing that turned her stomach. These soldiers wore no livery of Corvane. Their armor was mismatched, ancient, patched with leather and prayer. Their banners—if you could call them banners—were strips of cloth dyed the deep blue-green of the drowned crest, and they hung limp in air that did not stir, so that they looked less like flags than like bandages over old wounds.

"They followed us," Maren whispered. "Through the Reach. Through the fog. How does anyone follow anyone *here*?"

"I don't think they followed us," Rosalind said. The thought had been forming since the shore, cold and certain as a stone settling into mud. "I think the salt called them. Or—" She hesitated. "Or something in them answered."

Because she had seen it now, watching through the clouded glass: the soldiers moved strangely in the echo-light. They paused at doorways and stared at nothing with wet eyes. One man knelt in the gray grass and pressed his palm flat against it, over and over, like a man trying to wake someone sleeping. Another had taken off his helm and was turning slowly in place, mouth open, breathing the dead air as if it were the first sweet breath after a lifetime underwater.

These were not Aldric's hardened regulars. These were the marked. The gifted. The hunted. Men and women who had registered or fled or hidden, gathered up by a king who promised them—

Promised them what?

The answer came walking up the strand alone.

* * *

He had aged, but not the way kings age, soft and ringed. Aldric had aged the way driftwood ages: stripped, silvered, harder than the tree ever was. He wore no crown. He wore a soldier's oilskin gone gray with salt, and his hair was cut short and practical, and he walked with a slight hitch in his left leg that Rosalind found herself cataloguing without meaning to—the way you catalogue anything, when your life may shortly depend on knowing it.

Twenty paces from the counting-house door, he stopped. He did not call out. He did not send a herald. He simply stood there in the gray light, looking up at the window where eleven fugitives pressed themselves against the glass, and waited, with the patience of a man who had already waited thirty years and could wait thirty more.

"Keeper Eastwick," he said at last. His voice carried strangely in the echo-air, arriving both too soon and too late, doubled by the streets. "You have your mother's habit of watching from high windows."

Rosalind's blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with fear and everything to do with recognition.

She went down. She told herself it was because someone had to, because a keeper does not hide behind glass while a king stands in the open, because Ivo would have gone if she didn't and Ivo was too young and too angry and too full of the echo-country's promise to face whatever this was. All true. None of it the truth. The truth was simpler and worse: he had said *your mother*, and she needed to know how he knew.

The strand was cold under her boots. Up close, the vanguard's camp smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and, faintly, unmistakably, of the sea after a storm—that clean sharp scent Ivo would one day tell a child meant *Veyra*. Aldric watched her come with eyes the color of shallow water over sand, and there was salt crusted white in the creases of his knuckles, and scars on his forearms that were not scars at all but pale branching lines, like frost, like roots, like the veins of a leaf seen against the sun.

Rosalind knew those marks. Every guild child raised on the old stories knew them, though the stories never named them. *Anchor-marks,* Tam had called them once, late, drunk, in a voice he'd never used sober. *The Door writes itself on whoever holds it shut.*

"You were anchored," she said. No greeting. No preamble. The words fell out of her like stones from a torn sack.

Something moved behind the king's eyes—not pain exactly, but the place where pain had been quarried out and built into something load-bearing. "Thirty-one years," Aldric said. "Give or take a winter. I stopped counting after the twentieth, because counting is a way of hoping, and hope down there—" He gestured vaguely toward the sea, toward the Saltsteps, toward the gray depths where the Door of Brine waited. "Hope is a kind of drowning."

"You were a boy."

"I was fourteen." He said it flatly, factually, the way he might report troop numbers. "An apprentice of the Salt Guild, third-rank, marked for the anchor-work because my gift was strong and my family was nobody and the guild needed a door held open more than it needed me whole. You know the old ways, Keeper. Your Tam knew them better than anyone—he wrote half the protocols himself. A living gifted soul, freely bound, keeps the Door of Brine open so the guild can pass between countries. Indefinitely. Eternally, if the anchor doesn't break." His mouth twisted. "Mine broke. Or I broke it. There's a difference, and I've had thirty years to decide which, and I still haven't."

Behind her, Rosalind heard footsteps—Ivo, Odile, the others coming down despite her raised hand, drawn by the gravity of him. She did not turn. She watched Aldric watch them, and saw his gaze catch on Ivo and soften into something that looked, horribly, like tenderness.

"He has the marks of a strong one," Aldric said. "You should be careful with him. The guild was never careful with boys like us."

"The guild cast you out," Rosalind said. "That's the story. That's the story *we* tell."

"The guild cast me out because I escaped," Aldric said, "and an escaped anchor is a loose thread, and loose threads unravel protocols. They didn't hunt me. They didn't even chase me. Tam Wren signed the severance papers himself and wished me luck in a voice like a man closing a coffin lid." He took a step closer, and Rosalind made herself not step back. "Your guild erased me, Keeper. Before your king ever erased a country, your guild taught me how erasure works. You write a person out of the ledger, and then you write the ledger out of the world, and then you sell charms in a market stall for twenty years and call it survival." His voice did not rise. It didn't need to. "I am not your opposite, Rosalind Eastwick. I am what your guild made and threw away. I am the price you paid and pretended not to count."

* * *

They spoke for an hour on the strand, or what felt like an hour; time moved like weather here, and Rosalind could not have sworn to any measure of it. The vanguard kept their distance, watching their king speak with the guild's keeper, and Rosalind understood at last why Aldric had brought these particular soldiers, why their banners were torn echoes of a drowned crest.

"These are the ones who fled the registration," she said. "The ones Serah Vole hunted. You gathered them."

"I offered them what no one else would offer." Aldric stood with his back to the sea, and the gray light made him look carved rather than born. "A homeland. A country where their gift is law instead of crime. Not this—" he spread a hand at the echo-streets, the almost-houses, the fog that never lifted— "not this gray sketch of a place. The *real* Veyra. Restored. Re-anchored. Made permanent."

"That's not possible."

"It is possible," Aldric said, with the terrible calm of a man reciting scripture he had personally verified, "because I have stood inside the Door, Keeper. I know what it wants. It wants an anchor—a living will, freely given, holding it open against the pressure of forgetting. One soul, and the Door stays open forever, and everything the salt remembers pours back into the world. Streets. Harvests. Voices. Children born under a sky that remembers their names." He leaned forward, and for a moment the king fell away and Rosalind saw the fourteen-year-old underneath, the apprentice in the dark, the boy who had been written out of a ledger. "One soul, Rosalind. Against a nation. Name me the arithmetic that calls that a cruelty."

"And that soul is you."

"My marks are spent. The Door burned me out—it takes what it takes and leaves the rest. I cannot anchor it again even if I wanted to, and believe me, there were years I wanted to." He smiled, and it was the worst thing Rosalind had seen in a journey full of worst things, because it was genuine. "No. The anchor must be new. Young. Strong. Someone the salt already loves." His gaze drifted past her shoulder, and Rosalind did not need to turn to know it had found Ivo. "Someone with a long life ahead of them to spend."

"We won't."

"You haven't heard the offer yet."

"There's nothing you can offer that—"

"A kingdom." Aldric said it simply, without theater, which was somehow more obscene than if he'd shouted it. "Veyra restored, with me as its king and the guild as its keepers. Your people safe, honored, *legal*. Every gifted soul in Corvane given passage through the Door to a country that will never again appear on a list of things erased. And you—" his voice dropped, and the doubling echo made it seem to come from all directions, from the streets, from the sea, from twenty years ago— "you spend the rest of your life as the woman who brought her people home. Isn't that what Tam's map was for? Isn't that what you buried him carrying? I am offering you the ending you have been walking toward since the day you burned your ledger, Rosalind Eastwick. All I ask in return is that you choose *which* of your people pays for it."

The strand was silent. The fog hung. Somewhere behind her, Ivo's breath caught audibly, and Rosalind thought: *there it is.* The trap was never the bargain. The trap was that the bargain was fair—or nearly fair, or fair enough that refusing it required a kind of courage that had no name in any language the guild had kept.

"You were anchored once," she said quietly. "You know what it costs. You know better than any living soul what it costs. And you'd ask it of a boy of nineteen."

"I'd ask it of whoever consents," Aldric said. "Freely. That is the protocol. That was always the protocol." A pause, thin as ice. "And if no one consents—then the Door closes, and stays closed, and every soul in my vanguard goes back to Corvane to be hunted, and you go back to selling fever-salts until Serah Vole finds you, and Veyra sleeps in the salt forever. I have lived with the Door closed, Keeper. I have lived on the *outside* of it. I will burn every free city to ash before I live on the outside one more decade."

There it was. Underneath the scripture, underneath the tenderness, the iron: he had not come to negotiate. He had come to collect.

Aldric straightened his oilskin shoulders and became, visibly, a king again. "You have until the tide turns," he said. "Then my people and I descend the Saltsteps, and we open the Door ourselves—with an anchor or without one. Without one, it will fight us. Without one, some of my people will die prying it open, and the country it gives us will be thin and starving and half-real." He met her eyes, and the boy surfaced one last time, brief and drowning. "With one, it will be *home*. Choose, Keeper. You're better at choosing than you think. You've been choosing who survives since the day you took Tam's map."

He turned and walked back up the strand toward his waiting vanguard, his hitching gait steady, his torn blue-green banner limp above him, and the fog closed behind him like a page turning.

Rosalind stood on the cold sand with her guild gathered at her back and the sea speaking its drowned voices at her feet, and she understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the history she had inherited and the tyranny she had fled were the same history, grown from the same root, watered by the same salt.

Ivo stepped up beside her. His jaw was set in the shape of a decision.

"Keeper," he said. "I've been thinking about what he said. About consent. About—"

"No," Rosalind said.

"You haven't even heard—"

"*No.*" She took his wrist, hard, feeling the pulse beneath the skin, young and fast and alive. "Not yet. Not here. Not while he's watching." Because it was true, and because it bought her time, and because the alternative was to say what she actually meant: *not you, never you, I will drown this whole gray country myself before I let it have you.*

Above the strand, the fog was beginning to move again—parting, this time, in the east, where the echo-sea lay flat and gleaming and wrong.

And out of that parted fog, black against the gray, came sails.
