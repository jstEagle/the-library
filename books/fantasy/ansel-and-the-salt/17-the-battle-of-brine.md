## 17. The Battle of Brine

Serah Vole had spent her life learning to read people the way scholars read dead languages—slowly, syllable by syllable, until the meaning gave itself up. She had read smugglers and saints, children who lied about their gifts and magistrates who lied about everything else. She had thought herself fluent in every dialect of human want.

She had not been fluent enough to read a king.

The realization had come to her three days out from Marrowgate, standing in the hold of the *Cormorant's Tithe* with Doss's confession still souring her stomach like old milk. Aldric's vanguard did not move like an arm of the crown. It moved like a man's private hunger given uniforms. The supply trains carried no surveyors' chains, no boundary stakes, none of the instruments a king would need to claim new territory for Corvane. They carried rope. Anchoring rope, hundreds of coils of it, tarred and heavy, and iron collars stamped with no sigil at all—as if the king could not bear even his own crest to touch what he intended.

And they carried chains sized for a person.

Serah had held one of those chains in her gloved hands and understood, with the cold certainty she usually reserved for catching a liar mid-sentence, that Aldric had never meant to bring Veyra back for Corvane. He meant to keep it. A country behind a door, opened only by his hand, anchored by whatever gifted soul he chained to the threshold—and she, Serah Vole, witchfinder of the realm, had been sharpening his knife for him since the first proclamation went up on the bellposts.

She had burned nothing. She had told no one. There was no one left to tell who would not report her for treason, which was, she reflected, its own kind of joke: the woman who had hunted every hidden thing in the kingdom now harbored the largest secret of all.

So she had done the arithmetic that witches were supposed to be incapable of, and chosen the lesser evil by weight.

* * *

The Gray Reach wore fog like a mourner wears black—not as ornament but as identity. Serah stood at the prow of the lead longboat and watched the drowned staircase resolve out of the mist by degrees, stone step below stone step, descending into water so flat it looked less like sea than like glass laid over something sleeping. The Saltsteps. Odile Fen's maps had named it; the salt in Rosalind Eastwick's saddlebags had sung toward it, if the reports of her own scouts could be believed—and they could, because her scouts kept touching their tongues to the air afterward and weeping without knowing why.

Her soldiers felt it too. She heard them murmuring behind her, the way men murmur in cathedrals or before executions. The fog was warm here. That was the wrongness none of them could stop remarking on: fog should be cold, and this was warm as breath, warm as a room where someone has just been sitting.

"Finder Vole." Captain Brase, her second, a solid man with a face like a closed ledger. He nodded toward the steps. "Movement on the heights."

Serah raised her spyglass. Along the cliff above the stair, torches were kindling one by one, unhurried, ceremonial, like candles lit for a vigil. Aldric's vanguard had arrived in the night and taken the high ground, and they were not hiding it. They wanted to be seen. That, more than anything, confirmed what the chains had told her: this was not a battle the king expected to fight. This was a coronation he expected to interrupt.

"How many?"

"Two hundred on the ridge. More coming down the coast road. And—" Brase hesitated, which he almost never did. "There are figures on the steps themselves, ma'am. Below the tide line. Standing on the stones under the water. Not drowning."

She took the glass again and found them: perhaps a dozen shapes, robed or unrobed, standing ankle-deep, knee-deep, chest-deep in the flat gray sea, motionless as pilings. The guild. Waiting. Either they did not fear the water or the water did not consider them intruders, and both possibilities sat in Serah's chest like swallowed stones.

"And the guild's boat?" she asked, though she already knew.

"Run aground on the shingle east of the steps. They came ashore an hour ago. Eastwick is on the beach."

Of course she was. Serah lowered the glass. Somewhere down there, on a strip of gray sand under a sky the color of dishwater, the woman she had chased across half a kingdom was preparing to open a door that should never have existed, and Serah Vole had sailed through a storm to make sure she got the chance.

If any of her superiors in the crown's service could see her now, they would not need a trial. They would simply look at her the way she had once looked at the guilty, and that would be enough.

"Signal the fleet," she said. "We go in under the guild's position. We hold the beach between Eastwick and the ridge." She paused, and made herself say the rest of it plainly, because her soldiers deserved plain words even when the words were madness. "We are not fighting for the crown today. We are fighting against the man wearing it. Anyone whose oath cannot bend that far may stay aboard, and I will write no name down."

No one stayed aboard. She chose not to examine what that meant about her, about them, about the strange gravity of the place that made oaths feel like small things. She turned her face toward the shore and let the warm fog close over the fleet like a hand.

* * *

The beach was littered with the sea's leavings: kelp in black ropes, driftwood bleached to bone, crab shells split and hollow. And salt—salt everywhere, crusted white on the stones, glinting in the wrack like frost that refused to melt. Serah waded ashore through shallows that whispered against her boots, and the whisper was almost language, almost a voice saying something patient and enormous, and she fixed her eyes on the guild's fire and did not listen.

Rosalind Eastwick rose from beside the fire to meet her. Up close, after all these months of pursuit, the charm seller looked smaller than her reputation—gray-streaked hair escaping its braid, hands cracked from salt water, eyes that held the particular steadiness of someone who had already decided how she would die and found it a relief.

"Witchfinder." No fear in it. Only a tired courtesy, the way one greets weather.

"Eastwick." Serah stopped an arm's length away—the distance she'd have kept from anything dangerous, though she could no longer have said which of them filled that category. "Aldric holds the ridge. Two hundred men tonight, more by morning. He has anchoring rope and prisoner chains, and his orders are to take the Door alive and everyone near it otherwise." She let that land. "I am offering you my company. Forty-one soldiers. We will hold the beach while you do—whatever it is you intend to do down those stairs."

Rosalind studied her for a long moment. Behind her, the others had gathered: the boy Ivo with his jaw set like a man twice his age, the cartographer Fen clutching her satchel of maps as though paper might save anyone, a handful of guild survivors with salt-burned hands. Tam Wren was not among them. Serah knew about the marsh road. She had read Doss's confession twice, once for intelligence and once, in the dark of her cabin, for penance.

"You know what he wants," Rosalind said quietly. "You've known since Marrowgate. Why didn't you turn back? Why didn't you arrest us on the sand and be done with it?"

"Because arresting you gives him what he wants, and stopping you gives him what he wants, and I have run out of ways to serve a king who is not one." Serah heard her own voice come out flat and hard, and was grateful for it. Flat was safe. "Do not mistake this for absolution. I burned the registry lists myself. I trained the informers. Everything your people suffered, my signature is somewhere underneath it. I am not asking to stand beside you. I am asking to stand in front of you, which is different, and I will not pretend otherwise."

Something moved through the guild members behind Rosalind—not warmth, nothing so generous, but a kind of grim recalculation, the look of people deciding which wolf to stand nearer to. The boy Ivo spat into the sand and said nothing, which Serah understood to be the most generous available response.

"You'll want to know the ground," Rosalind said at last, and turned to the stairs, and began to talk.

* * *

They fought as the light failed, because Aldric's vanguard came down the ridge in the last hour of dusk, torches guttering in the warm fog, and the battle that followed had no proper name in any of Serah's manuals. The Saltsteps did not permit formations. The beach funneled into the stair, and the stair descended in wide flooded terraces, and the fighting spread across them like spilled water finding every channel at once.

Serah fought on the third terrace, where the sea came to her thighs and the water was blood-warm and full of voices. That was the worst of it—not the steel, not the press of bodies, but the water itself, which remembered. Every time a man fell, the sea around him seemed to gather the falling in, and for a heartbeat Serah saw things that were not there: a harbor with white towers, a woman laughing on a balcony, orchards burning in rows, a child's hand pressed flat against a window. Veyra, leaking out of the water into her skull, frame by frame, whether she willed it or no.

*Hold the terrace,* she told her soldiers, and her voice sounded far away to her own ears, thin over the murmur of the deep. *Hold the terrace.*

They held. Brase died holding it—a pike through the throat on the fourth step, his ledger-face finally open, finally surprised—and Serah stepped over him and did not stop, because stopping was death and feeling was worse, and she had been practicing not-feeling since she was nine years old and the fever took her sister and no prayer and no physician and no law could call her back.

The guild fought too, and that she had not expected. She had imagined them cowering behind her line, the way the powerless always cowered in her experience. Instead they fought like people defending a grave. The boy Ivo moved through the melee with light coiling off his fingers—actual light, raw and untrained, flaring whenever fear gripped him, and fear gripped him often, and each flare threw back a wave of Aldric's men like wind through wheat. Rosalind Eastwick fought with a boat hook and no magic at all, which struck Serah, in some detached corner of her mind, as the most honest thing she had witnessed in twenty years of service.

And the sea helped them. Serah saw it happen and could not afterwards deny it: waves rising against the attackers alone, pulling at their legs like hands; a swell that swept three soldiers off the fifth terrace and set them down, coughing and unharmed, on the shingle far from the fight, disarmed by the water's indifference rather than its mercy. The drowned country beneath them was choosing. Everyone on those stairs understood it, and understanding broke the vanguard faster than any blade.

By full dark, the ridge was silent and the beach was strewn with the cost of holding it, and Serah Vole stood waist-deep on the lowest terrace with her sword arm shaking and her lungs full of the taste of brine, watching Rosalind Eastwick descend the final steps toward the door.

* * *

They gathered at the threshold—all that remained of two armies and one guild, perhaps sixty souls standing on a drowned staircase in the middle of the night sea, breathing hard, bleeding freely, listening to the water speak.

The Door was not a door. That was the last cruelty of it. It was simply a place where the sea ended—not froze, not stilled, but *ended*, a vertical wall of dark water hanging above the ninth step as though the ocean had been cut with a knife and the cut had healed wrong. Through it, faintly, showed a gray country: hills the color of ash, a road going nowhere, a sky without sun or stars, all of it trembling like a reflection in disturbed water.

Aldric's men had broken and fled inland, or drowned, or knelt on the terraces with their hands laced behind their heads, done with kings. The fog had thickened until the world was thirty feet wide. In that narrow world there was only the Door, the guild, and the witchfinder.

Rosalind stood before the wall of water with a grain of salt held out on her palm, and Serah watched her hesitate—not from cowardice, she realized, but from grief, the way one hesitates before reading a dead friend's last letter.

"Eastwick." Serah's voice came out rougher than she intended. She cleared her throat. "When this is done—if it can be done—I will file no report. I will burn what remains of the registry. I will tell the crown the Door collapsed and the sea took the proof." She paused, and forced out the sentence she had crossed half a kingdom to avoid saying. "It will not undo what I did. I know that. I am not asking you to forgive it. I am telling you that from tonight, the hunt is over, and I am the one ending it, and you may think whatever you like of me for as long as you live."

Rosalind turned. In the trembling gray light of the Door, her face looked carved from the same stuff as the echo-country beyond—weathered, faded, enduring. "I don't think anything of you yet, witchfinder," she said. "Ask me again when the salt is settled." Then, softer, almost kindly, which was somehow harder to bear: "Your captain fought well. So did you. Whatever else is true, that's true."

She turned back to the Door. The boy Ivo moved to stand at her shoulder, and Serah saw him glance at the hanging wall of water with an expression she recognized from a hundred interrogations—the expression of someone about to volunteer for something terrible—and she saw Rosalind see it too, and grip the boy's wrist, hard, without looking away from the Door.

Beyond the threshold, the gray country waited, trembling, patient, built entirely out of things the world had agreed to forget.

And Serah Vole, who had spent her whole life closing doors, stood guard at the last one that mattered, sword drawn, and watched Rosalind Eastwick lift the salt toward the water, and begin to speak.
