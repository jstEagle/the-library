## 18. The Anchor

The Door was dying, and Ivo could feel it the way you feel a fever coming on—first as a warmth behind the eyes, then as a wrongness in the blood.

He stood at the top of the Saltsteps with the wind tearing at his coat, watching the gray echo of Veyra flicker below him like a lamp running out of oil. The streets were still there—the remembered streets, built of salt and longing—but they had begun to stutter. A lamppost that had burned with a soft white flame for two days now guttered and went dark, then flared again, then went dark for good. A window box of blue flowers he had touched once, marveling, crumbled into grains that the sea-wind carried away. Somewhere down those streets a bell rang in a tower that no longer quite existed, its sound arriving late and thin, like an apology from someone already leaving.

"It's faster than Tam's notes said," Odile Fen murmured beside him. The old cartographer had her journal braced against her knee, sketching the stair even now, even as it failed, because that was what Odile did with grief—she documented it. "The lock took Rosalind's memory and opened wide, but an open door is not a held door. It needs weight. It needs something that won't be pulled back out."

"Something living," Ivo said.

Odile didn't answer. That was answer enough.

Below them, on the middle steps where the sea met the stone, the others were gathered around the last of the hoard—eleven barrels and three strongboxes of true salt, all that remained after the storm on the Gray Reach had drunk its share. Rosalind crouched over them with her sleeves rolled up, her hands moving among the crystals, and even from this distance Ivo could see the salt reaching for her fingers the way flowers turn toward light. It knew her. It had known her since Corvane, since the market stall, since the day she'd pressed a luck-knot into his palm and told him, in her dry market voice, *You're going to need more than luck, boy.*

He had been fifteen then. He was seventeen now, and the whole of the time between had been one long road: the flooded chapel, the marsh ambush, Tam dying with the map pressed into his own hands before pressing it into Rosalind's, the gates of Marrowgate, the fog, the storm, the stairs. He had spent that road angry half the time and grateful the other half, and he had never once stopped searching for the thing every orphan searches for, which is not a home exactly but a place where being yourself is not a crime.

The echo-country below him was that place. Or it had been, for two days.

Now it was closing, and someone would have to hold it open forever, and Ivo had already decided who.

* * *

He told Rosalind at dusk, when the others had gone to the ruined chapel above the cliff to sleep, because the dead deserved their privacy and so did the living.

She was sitting on the lowest step with her boots off, her feet in the water, which was madness—it was nearly winter and the Gray Reach ate heat like a debt collector eats coin—but the water here was strange. Warm where the salt remembered summer. She had been sitting there for an hour, he guessed, letting the sea speak against her ankles, listening to the drowned voices that lived in the brine. Her hair had gone fully gray in the months since Corvane. She wore it loose now, which she never used to do, and it made her look younger and older at once, like a woman standing in two times.

"Ivo." She said his name without turning. "I can hear you thinking from here. It sounds like a millstone."

"Then you know what I'm going to say."

"I know what you're going to say." She patted the wet stone beside her. "Sit down anyway."

He sat. The water lapped his boots and soaked through to his socks and he didn't care. Below them, through the clear dark water, the ghost-streets of Veyra glimmered like a second sky seen from underneath.

"The Door needs an anchor," he said. "A living gifted person, given freely, holding it from inside. Forever. I've read Tam's notes. I've felt it pulling. And I—" His voice cracked and he hated it and pushed on anyway. "I volunteer."

Rosalind was quiet for a long moment. The sea breathed against the steps. Far off, in the failing city below, that bell rang again, thinner than before.

"No," she said.

"You haven't even—"

"I don't need to hear the argument, Ivo. I wrote the argument myself, twenty years ago, in a ledger I burned." She turned to look at him then, and her eyes in the low light were the color of wet slate. "You think I don't know every word of it by heart? *I am young. I am strong. My gift is small enough to spare. I have no family to grieve me.* You'd say all of it. You've been rehearsing it for days. I've watched you rehearsing it."

"Then you know it's true," Ivo said, and his anger came up hot, the way it always did, the way it had carried him through every hard mile of this road. "I *am* young. I *am* strong. My gift *is* small—I mend things, Rosalind. Nets and hinges and broken cups. What use is that in Corvane? What use is that anywhere but here, in a country made of things that need mending?" He gestured wildly at the water, at the glimmering streets. "Look at it! Look what's down there! A whole country where I'm not a criminal. Where none of us are. And it's *closing*, and I can hold it open, and you're telling me no like I'm asking to skip chores."

"Yes," Rosalind said. "I am telling you no. Like I'm your mother. Which I am not, and never claimed to be, and yet here we are."

"That's not fair."

"Nothing about this is fair. Fairness drowned with Veyra." She pulled her feet from the water and turned to face him fully, and he saw that her hands were shaking—not with cold. With something she was holding down. "Do you want to know why I'm saying no? Not the ledger reasons. The real ones."

He said nothing. The sea said nothing. Even the bell below had finally gone silent.

"When I found you," she said, "you were sleeping in the ash-house behind the tannery. Do you remember? You were eleven, maybe twelve, and you'd mended every hinge in the building without being asked, because you couldn't stand the sound of them squeaking. You'd mended other children's shoes. You'd mended a birdcage and left the door open so the bird could choose. And when I woke you, you flinched—you flinched like I was going to hurt you—and then you apologized. For existing. In a room full of kindnesses you'd done in secret." Her voice caught, steadied. "And I thought: *there*. There is the thing the kingdom cannot bear. Not magic. Magic is everywhere, cheap as rain. What they cannot bear is a child who gives without being asked and expects to be punished for it."

"Rosalind—"

"Let me finish, because I will only say this once." She took his face in both her hands, the way she had done once, years ago, when he'd had a fever and she'd sat up all night with him. Her palms smelled of salt and lamp oil and the inside of the guild's old storeroom, which was to say they smelled like every safe place he had ever known. "You have spent your whole life looking for somewhere to belong. I know. I watched you look. Every town we passed, you counted the windows. Every gate, you asked if they'd take a mender. And when the Door opened and you saw Veyra, I watched your face, and I thought, *ah, there it is, he's found it*—and I was glad, Ivo, I was so glad for you. But belonging is not a place. It never was. Belonging is people who refuse to let you disappear. And if I let you walk down those stairs and hold that door until your bones are salt—if I let you trade everyone who loves you for a street that remembers you—" Her thumb brushed his cheekbone, rough and gentle. "Then I would be teaching you the exact lie Aldric believes. That a person can be spent like coin. That love is a currency. No."

"Aldric would do it," Ivo whispered. "He'd take the bargain in a heartbeat."

"And look what it made him." Rosalind's voice dropped to almost nothing. "He anchored himself to that door once, or tried to, and tore free, and everything he has done since—the purges, the hunt, Serah Vole, all of it—is the shape of a man trying to buy back a door he should never have touched. You want to belong somewhere so badly you're willing to become the lock. I won't allow it. Not because your life is worth more than Veyra. Because *we* are worth more than Veyra. All of us. Together. That's the whole point of a guild, boy. We were never a warehouse. We were a promise."

Ivo's eyes were burning. He blinked hard and looked out at the water, at the drowned glow beneath it, and the anger in him curdled into something worse, which was understanding.

"Then what?" he asked. "If not me, then who? Someone has to hold it. You heard Odile. You felt it failing. By tomorrow night the streets will be gone and the Door will seal and everything Tam died carrying—that map, those hoards, twenty years of hiding salt in chapel walls and well bottoms—all of it will have been for a door that shut in our faces."

"I know," Rosalind said.

"So who?"

She was quiet again. But this time it was a different quiet—heavy, deliberate, the quiet of a woman setting down a weight she has carried alone so that others can see how large it truly is.

"There may be another way," she said slowly. "Tam's notes mention it only in passing—a line about the hoard itself, about where the salt was drawn from. The sea it came from. This sea." She looked down at the black water between her feet. "Salt remembers, Ivo. That's all it has ever done. Maybe memory doesn't need a prisoner to hold it. Maybe it just needs to go home."

"That's a guess."

"It's a hope," she corrected. "And tomorrow, if the gods of lost countries have any mercy left, it will be more than that. Tonight—" she squeezed his hands once, hard, and let go— "tonight you will eat something, and sleep, and stop rehearsing martyrdoms. That's an order from your keeper."

"You're not my keeper yet," he muttered. "Tam was the keeper."

"Tam is dead, and the map is in my hands, and the guild is four people and a cartographer." She rose, stiffly, joints cracking like the ice on a winter puddle, and for a moment she looked every year of her age and every year of her burdens. Then she looked down at him, and the look was the same one she'd given him in the ash-house, twelve years old and apologizing for existing. "But you're right about one thing. One day it will be yours. The ledger, the hoard, the keeping. Not because you volunteered to die for it. Because you'll be the one who stayed."

She walked back up the steps toward the chapel, and Ivo stayed where he was, boots in the warm-cold water, watching the drowned city breathe its fading light.

* * *

He did not sleep.

He lay in the chapel among the others—Odile snoring softly against a fallen saint, Marta and Coss curled together under one cloak the way they had been since the marsh road—and he listened to the sea through the broken roof, and he thought about doors.

Every door he had ever mended squeaked because its hinges wanted oiling, and he had oiled thousands of them, and he had learned something in the doing that no one had ever asked him to learn: a door does not want to be held open. Held open, it strains. Held open, the wood warps and the frame cracks and eventually the whole thing tears loose. A door wants to swing—open, closed, open—as it was made to do. The trick was never to force it. The trick was to fix what was broken so it could move freely, and trust whoever came next to choose which side of it to stand on.

Rosalind was wrong about one thing, he thought, staring up through the broken roof at the stars. She thought he hadn't understood her refusal. But he had. That was the terrible part. He had understood it completely, and it had felt—for the first time in seventeen years, in ash-houses and tannery yards and roads full of refugees—like being loved with his eyes open. Not wanted for his gift. Not needed for his youth. Refused. Refused the way you refuse to let a child play with fire, which is the only proof that someone intends for you to have a future.

So he wouldn't anchor the Door.

But he was a mender. And the Door was breaking. And there were hours yet before dawn.

He rose without a sound, the way he'd learned to in the ash-house, and slipped out into the wind.

* * *

The Saltsteps at midnight were a different country. The moon was down; the fog off the Gray Reach had come in thick and tasting of iron; and the failing light of echo-Veyra pulsed beneath the water like a heart deciding whether to keep beating. Ivo went down the steps barefoot, coatless, his gift rising in his chest with every step—that small warm hum, the mender's hum, the sound of hinges wanting oil.

At the waterline he knelt and put both hands flat on the stone.

The Door answered immediately. It always had, since the first day; the salt knew its own. But tonight the answering was ragged, a stutter, a pulse missing beats. Through his palms he could feel the whole vast structure of it—the lock Rosalind's memory had opened, the streets it held up, the bell tower and the flower boxes and the blue door of a house where, somewhere in the memory-landscape, a woman was still singing a song in a language the maps had been forbidden to carry. All of it straining. All of it held open past its making, warping, cracking, ready to tear loose and take its memories down with it.

*I can't hold you,* he told it silently. *She won't let me, and she's right, and I hate that she's right.*

The Door pulsed again, dimmer.

*But I can mend you,* he said. *Not forever. Not the way they wanted. Just—so you can swing. So whatever happens next, you close clean instead of breaking.*

He closed his eyes and gave the hum everything he had.

It was not much. He had always known it was not much. A mender's gift, good for nets and hinges and broken cups. But he poured it down through his palms into the salt-slick stone, into the grain of the ancient stair, and the hum spread the way warmth spreads through a frozen house when the first fire is lit—one beam, then another, then the bones of the whole place remembering what it was to be sound. Far below, the bell tower straightened. The flower boxes bloomed one last full time, blue as a vein, blue as the crest on a coin a child had once paid him in Corvane. The singing woman's song grew briefly, achingly clear, rising up through the water like breath through a keyhole.

And then, gently, the Door began to close.

Not collapse. Not tear. *Close*—the way a well-mended door closes, smooth and true, the streets folding into the sea like a hand folding closed, the memories sinking home into the brine that had dreamed them. Ivo knelt on the steps with his hands on the stone and his tears freezing in the wind, and he felt the whole gray country pass beneath his palms on its way down, and it did not feel like losing. It felt like laying something to rest properly, which was the only funeral anyone had ever given Veyra.

The last thing through was the song. Then the water was only water, black and deep and ordinary, and the Saltsteps were only stairs, descending into the Gray Reach like any drowned ruin on any coast, and the hum in Ivo's chest guttered out and left him hollow and shaking and strangely, impossibly light.

He sat back on his heels. Above him, on the cliff path, a lantern was coming down fast, bobbing, urgent—and behind it a voice calling his name, raw with fear, a voice he knew better than any country's.

Rosalind reached the waterline at a run, lantern high, Marta and Coss and Odile stumbling behind her with whatever weapons sleepers grab. She skidded to a stop on the wet stone, took in his soaked knees, his empty hands, his face—

"What did you do?" she whispered. "Ivo. *What did you do?*"

"Not what you think." His voice came out cracked. He looked up at her, at all of them, at the small stubborn family that had refused to let him disappear. "I fixed the hinges. That's all. I fixed the hinges so it could close the way it was meant to." He swallowed. "But Rosalind—when it closed, I felt where everything went. The memories. The hoard's memories, all of them, sinking. They didn't scatter." He pointed at the black water, and his hand was steady now, steadier than it had ever been. "They went *down*. All the way down. To where the salt was first drawn from. There's something down there. Something that takes them home."

Rosalind stared at the sea. The lantern flame bent in the wind, and in its light her face was unreadable—except that Ivo had spent twelve years learning to read it, and what he saw there was not grief.

It was a woman realizing the door she meant to break open might, instead, be fed.

"Get the others," she said quietly, to Marta. "Wake everyone. Bring every barrel, every box, every grain we have left." She looked down at Ivo, and she held out her hand, and he took it, and she pulled him to his feet as though he weighed nothing at all. "We're going to need the whole hoard by dawn."
