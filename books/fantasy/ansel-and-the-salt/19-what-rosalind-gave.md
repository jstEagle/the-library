## 19. What Rosalind Gave

The Door was failing.

Rosalind felt it the way one feels a fever breaking—not as relief but as a kind of grief, a heat draining out of the world. The gray streets of echo-Veyra had begun to soften at their edges, the remembered rooftops going vague as breath on glass, and beneath her feet the Saltsteps hummed with a note that was no longer a note at all but a fraying, a thread pulled loose from its weaving.

She stood at the top of the stair with the hoard in her arms.

It had come to her in the way that true things come—quietly, sideways, while she was looking at something else. Ivo had been kneeling at the threshold, his hands pressed flat to the salt-crusted stone, trying to hold the Door open by will alone, and she had seen the grain of true salt in his palm glowing like a coal, dimming, glowing again in time with his heartbeat. *Anchor it,* he had said, when she'd understood what he meant to do. *I can anchor it. I'm strong enough.* And she had said no—had said it with everything in her, with twenty years of keeping children safe by making them small—and then, in the silence after the refusal, she had looked down at the sacks and boxes and jars of the guild's whole inheritance, every hoard they'd bled to gather, and the thought had arrived whole, the way Veyra itself must have arrived once, out of nothing, out of sea.

The salt had been drawn from this water. Every grain of it. Tam had told her that much before the marsh road took him: *it's not treasure, girl, it's what's left over—the country crystallized, drop by drop, the way the sea leaves salt behind when it withdraws.* And if the sea had left it, then the sea could take it back. Not one living soul anchored to a doorway forever. Not Ivo, not anyone. All of it—every memory, every voice, every street and kitchen and wedding song pressed into crystal across two hundred years of hiding—returned to the water that remembered how to hold them.

The Door would not stay open. That was the cost, and she had stopped pretending there was a version of this without a cost. But it would not need a prisoner either.

*You're certain?* Odile had asked her, at the top of the stair, the old cartographer's face gray with spray and exhaustion. Odile had mapped Veyra once, had loved it in the fierce unsentimental way cartographers love places, and Rosalind had watched her understand before the words were finished. Certain? No. She was not certain of anything except that she would not trade a boy for a country.

"I'm certain," she said now, aloud, to no one, because saying it made it heavier and she needed it heavy. She needed to feel the full weight of what she carried down.

The hoard weighed less than a child. That was the terrible arithmetic of it. Two hundred years of a nation, all its salt gathered from chapel cisterns and smugglers' caches and the floor of a burned granary, and it fit against her chest in oilcloth and rope, light as bread, light as ash.

* * *

She started down the Saltsteps alone.

The others tried to stop her—Ivo loudest, because he was young and because refusing to be saved is its own kind of violence—but she had spent twenty years being the one who said *no, we wait, we hide, we sell luck-knots and keep our heads down*, and there was no arguing left in any of them for her. Tam was dead. Doss had sold them. The guild was four people and a cartographer standing in fog at the top of a drowned staircase, and its last keeper was walking away from them into the sea with everything they had ever been in her arms.

"Take me with you," Ivo said, and his voice cracked on *with*, and she heard in it every road they'd walked since Corvane, every gate closing, every night he'd asked where he belonged and gotten silence or a lesson instead of an answer.

"No," she said. "Someone has to remember me wrong, afterward. It should be you."

He laughed—a broken, furious sound—and then he did the thing that undid her: he stepped aside. He lifted his hands off the threshold stone and let her pass, and the look he gave her was not forgiveness and not permission but something rarer, the look of someone watching a door close on purpose.

The steps went down farther than stairs should go. They were cut from black rock furred with white salt, and each one sang faintly under her boots, a different tone, descending through a scale no living musician had written. The water rose around her ankles, then her knees, and it was warm—warmer than the Gray Reach had any right to be, warm as bathwater, warm as blood—and where it touched the oilcloth wrapping the hoard, the salt inside began to answer. She felt it through her ribs. A low calling, like a mother hearing her own name in a crowd of strangers' voices.

And the voices came.

They came up through the water the way they had come in the drowned chapel, but here there was no wall between the remembering and the world. A woman singing a spinning song in a dialect Rosalind didn't know and somehow understood. Children counting fish on a dock. An argument about the price of olives, rising and falling, rising and falling, the oldest music there is. A man saying *I'll be home before the lamps are lit* to someone who never answered, because the recording ends where the memory ended, and some memories end badly.

"Wait," Rosalind whispered to them, absurdly, as if the dead could hurry or slow. "Wait, I'm bringing you home."

The fog thickened. The gray echo-country flickered around her like a lamp low on oil—she could see it still, at the edges, the remembered city standing in the shallows of its own echo, gulls that were only the idea of gulls wheeling over roofs that were only the idea of roofs. It was beautiful. That was the trap of it; it had always been beautiful; Aldric had looked at this beauty and wanted to own it, and Serah Vole had looked at it and wanted to cage it, and Rosalind Eastwick looked at it and understood, finally, why her grandmother had taught her the first charm she ever learned: *a thing kept is a thing changed.*

She was halfway down when Aldric came.

* * *

She heard him before she saw him—boots on stone above, fast and wrong-footed, a man running down stairs he had climbed once before, decades ago, with different intentions. Then he was there on the steps below her, having come around through the water, and the fog parted around him like a curtain, and she saw what the journey had made of the king.

His fine coat was ruined. His hands were cut from the salt rock—he had climbed, she realized, he had climbed the outside of the staircase like a man possessed—and his eyes had gone to the hoard in her arms with an expression she recognized because she had seen it in market stalls her whole life: hunger wearing the mask of reason.

"You don't understand what you're holding," he said. His voice was still beautiful. That was the worst of him; even broken, even half-mad, the beauty held. "That's not salt, Eastwick. That's a country. You are carrying a country in your arms, and you mean to drown it."

"It came from the water," she said. "I'm giving it back."

"To *what*?" He laughed, and the laugh skinned something off him, showed the boy underneath, the apprentice cast out before she was born. "Water doesn't want. Water doesn't keep. I have been to the bottom of this stair, woman. I know what's down there. Nothing. A lock and a dark and a Door that eats whoever holds it open—that's what Veyra is now, that's what your precious guild preserved all these years, a hunger with a hymn sung over it. Give it to me." He put out his hands, and they were shaking. "Give it to me and I will open it properly. I will anchor it the way it was meant to be anchored—with the hoard itself, with all of it, and no one has to stay. No one has to be the prisoner. Don't you see? I found the third way. I've had forty years to find the third way."

And the awful thing, the thing she would carry out of this place along with her own life, was that she believed he believed it. Somewhere in forty years of exile and usurpation and burning, Aldric had built himself a mathematics in which the Door could be fed without devouring, in which a king could hold a country open by wanting it enough. Half-mad, yes. But the madness had a logic, and the logic was love—the same love she felt, curdled by possession, turned inward, turned to rot.

"You tried to anchor it once," she said quietly. "Before you escaped. Before the scars."

His face closed like a fist.

"The salt remembers," she said. "It remembers you, Aldric. It's been remembering you the whole way down." She shifted the hoard in her arms, and the grains inside stirred, and the water around them lit faintly, phosphorescent, full of drifting motes of light that might have been salt dissolving or might have been faces. "It remembers a boy who sat on this stair for three days because he thought if he loved the Door hard enough it would love him back. It remembers what it cost him to leave."

"Don't." One word, and all the music gone out of his voice.

"You weren't cast out," she said. "You ran. Because it asked for what you wouldn't give, and you called that betrayal so you'd never have to call it mercy."

He moved then—fast, faster than a man his age should move, lunging up the steps at her with both hands out, and she did the only thing she could do, the thing she had been doing her whole life in smaller ways: she turned her body between the treasure and the grasping hands, and she kept walking down.

The water took her to the waist. The cold of it was a shock and then a comfort, and the hoard grew warm against her chest, warmer, almost hot, the salt crying out in frequencies below sound. Behind her Aldric shouted her name—her true name, her guild name, names she hadn't heard spoken aloud in years—and then the water was at her shoulders and the gray echo-country was dissolving around her in long soft ribbons, streets unspooling into mist, the spinning song unraveling into single notes, and she understood that she did not have far to go. The sea had come up to meet her. The sea had been climbing the stairs to meet her the whole time.

*Let go,* said the water, in no language, in every language, in her mother's voice for one unbearable instant. *Let go, let go, let go.*

* * *

At the bottom—or where the bottom would have been, where the last step vanished into green-black depth—she stopped, treading water, the hoard heavy now as lead, as a sleeping child, as everything.

Aldric had not followed her into the deep. He stood on the final dry step, chest heaving, hair plastered to his skull, and the madness in his face had cracked into something else, something younger. He looked, suddenly, exactly like what he had been: an apprentice at the end of his strength, begging the Door for a homeland.

"There's another way," he said, and now he was pleading. "Eastwick. Rosalind. There has to be another way. There always was, for people like us—for the ones who wouldn't kneel, who wouldn't register, who wouldn't *disappear*. We built the guild so there would be another way. You don't get to end it. You don't get to decide for all of us that the story stops here."

"You're right," she said, and saw him flinch at the agreement. "I don't get to decide. None of us does. That's the point. That was always the point." She loosened the first knot. "Veyra doesn't belong to the crown, and it doesn't belong to the guild, and it doesn't belong to me. It belongs to the water it came from, and to anyone who tastes the air after a storm and remembers. Let everyone keep that much. Let no one own more."

"You'll kill it," he whispered.

"No," Rosalind said. "I'll set it down."

She opened the oilcloth.

The salt rose out of her arms like a living thing—like a flock, like a choir, like breath finally exhaled after two hundred years held. It went up silver in the dark water, ten thousand grains, ten million, each one a kitchen fire, a christening, a harvest, a quarrel, a lullaby, and the sea received it the way the sea receives rivers: utterly, without ceremony, with a patience older than countries. The light spread. It spread through the water and up the staircase and out into the Gray Reach until the whole drowned world glowed like a lamp under silk, and the voices came back—not fading now but *arriving*, all of them at once, the spinning song and the fish-counting and the olive-sellers and the man who never got home before the lamps were lit, and the last one, the newest, the deepest: a woman's voice, warm and tired and amused, saying a name Rosalind had not heard since she was four years old.

Her mother's voice. Her earliest memory, given freely to the lock, coming home.

Rosalind cried then, in the salt water, where no one could tell the difference, and it was not grief, or it was not only grief. It was the feeling of setting down a weight she had mistaken for her spine.

Above her, on the last step, King Aldric sank slowly to his knees in the flooding light, and reached out one hand toward the brightness as it rose past him, and did not try to stop it, and did not speak, and did not, Rosalind thought, look like a king at all. He looked like a man watching the tide take something he had spent his whole life trying to hold, and finding, in the last moment, that his hands had forgotten how to close.

The glow began to fade. The echo-country went with it—gently, gently, the remembered roofs softening into sea-mist, the idea of gulls becoming real gulls somewhere far above, the streets returning to the water that had dreamed them. The Door of Brine sighed shut like an old woman settling into sleep.

And Rosalind Eastwick, keeper of nothing, swam for the surface and the air and the small gray light of the living world, while below her, in the darkening green, a man knelt alone on the last step of a staircase that was becoming only stone—

—and the water began, very softly, to sing to him.
