## 1. Charms for the Fearful

The luck-knots never sold as well as the fear-charms, which was a pity, because Rafael could make a luck-knot in her sleep and often did, her fingers working the three strands of grey wool into their braided pattern while her mind wandered anywhere else. Fear-charms took real work. A fear-charm was a hollow thing—a dried rowan berry, a pinch of salt, a scrap of iron nail wrapped in red thread—but it had to *look* like something. It had to look like protection.

People in Corvane did not buy hope anymore. They bought walls they could hang around their necks.

"Three coppers for the small, five for the large," she said to the fisherman who had stopped at her stall without seeming to decide to, the way most of her customers arrived. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with hands like knotted rope and eyes that had recently done a great deal of crying. Gulls wheeled overhead in the pale morning, screaming their usual grievances at the harbor below the market terraces.

The fisherman picked up a luck-knot, turned it over, put it down. Picked up a fear-charm, turned it over, and did not put it down.

"My wife bought one of these," he said. "Before."

Rafael kept her face kind and neutral, the expression she practiced in the cracked bit of mirror behind her stall. "Before," she agreed softly. Everyone in Corvane had a *before*. Twelve years since the Edict, since the covenant-sigils were pulled down from the temple doors and the old gods went quiet as stones. Twelve years since magic became a word you only said with your back against a wall and your voice low.

"She swore it worked," the fisherman said. He turned the charm in his huge fingers, careful, the way you'd handle an egg. "Salt and a berry in some thread, and she swore it worked. Said she could feel it sitting warm against her heart."

"It's a good charm," Rafael said, which was a lie, because it was salt and a berry in some thread, but it felt like the right kind of lie—the kind that let people keep loving the dead without feeling foolish about it.

"She's gone now. Three weeks past." He set the charm down and picked up the luck-knot again instead, holding it out. "For my boat. And—" His voice did something complicated. "There's a gull."

"A gull."

"Landed on my rail the day we buried her. Won't leave. Won't eat proper either—picks at the fish heads and stares at me like it's waiting for something." He looked embarrassed, this mountain of a man, embarrassed and desperate. "It's dying, I think. I'd pay for a charm for it. Silly, I know. But Marta loved the gulls, and I can't just watch the thing starve on my rail like it's got an appointment to join her."

*Oh,* Rafael thought. *Oh, don't do this to me.*

She looked at the gulls wheeling over the harbor, then at the man's ruined face, and thought about the arithmetic of her life. Rent on the shop above came due in nine days. Her flour sack held perhaps four days of meals if she ate thin. The Warden's patrols had doubled this month—something about trouble at the shrines, whispers she'd been carefully not listening to—and the penalty for what she carried in her blood was not fine or pillory but the quiet room beneath the Wardenhouse from which nobody came back up the stairs.

"Two coppers for the knot," she said. "And take the charm for the gull free. It won't work, mind—" *it will absolutely work, but not because of the charm* — "but it won't hurt, and your wife liked them, so."

He paid without haggling, which told her how much he was hurting, and shuffled off toward the harbor stairs with his luck-knot clutched like a lifeline.

* * *

Rafael told herself she would finish her tea, count her till, and go home. She told herself this while she drank the tea too fast, and while she counted eleven coppers twice hoping the number would improve, and while she packed up her remaining charms with movements that were already carrying her down toward the water before her mind had formally agreed to any of it.

This was the trouble with having a secret gift: it didn't stay where you put it. It followed you down harbor stairs.

She found the fisherman's boat by finding the fisherman, who sat on the gunwale of a broad-bellied trawler called the *Marta's Luck*, which answered several questions at once. On the rail, hunched into itself like an old man in winter, sat the gull.

It was dying. She could see that from ten feet off—not the dramatic flailing kind of dying but the slow, folded-in kind, feathers gone dull as old pewter, the bright yellow of its bill faded to the color of weak tea. It watched the fisherman with one round eye, patient and terrible, and Rafael felt the old ache open in her chest, the one she'd been stuffing shut since she was seven years old.

Because here was the truth of Rafael Ishikawa, charm-seller, poverty-keeper, invisible woman of the Corvane market terraces: she could talk to beasts.

Not in words. Not the way stories told it, with animals sitting up and declaiming. It was more like... a warmth. She made a sound—a murmur, a hum, half a breath of shaped air—and meaning traveled inside the sound, and the beast understood, and answered in its own fashion, and she understood that. Her mother had called it the family ear. Her mother had also taught her, before anything else, the first rule of it: *Never. Not where anyone can see. Not ever.*

Twelve years of never. Twelve years of murmuring to Sable the wharf-rat in the dark of her cellar, twelve years of humming comfort to cart-horses and pretending to be simply fond of animals, twelve years of being safe, which was another way of saying alone.

The gull's eye found her. Beasts always knew. They'd turn toward her across a crowded market like compass needles, and she'd have to pretend to be examining fish or boots or nothing at all.

She crouched by the rail, out of the fisherman's line of sight, and made the smallest sound in the world.

*Little one. What happened to you?*

The answer came back the way it always did—not words exactly, but a shape of feeling, and inside the feeling, images. Cold water. A storm with a name she couldn't hold. And grief—grief so vast and formless that Rafael's throat closed to touch even the edge of it. The gull had lost its flock in the storm. Or the gull had *been* something else once, near the storm, near whatever answered prayers these days—

No. That was nonsense. She was projecting. Grieving widower, grieving bird, sad morning; anyone would get morbid.

*You're starving yourself,* she murmured, warmth flowing through the sound like honey through water. *That's not honoring anyone. Your person's person wouldn't want it.*

The gull made a low sound, almost a purr, and leaned toward her hand.

*Eat,* she told it. *Live. Grieve sideways, not downward.* It was what her mother used to say, and saying it made her eyes sting unexpectedly. *Grieve sideways.*

The gull blinked once, slow, and Rafael felt something shift in it—a small turning, like a boat coming about. Then it snapped a fish head off the pile by the mast with sudden decisive violence, and the fisherman made a strangled noise of relief.

"Bless you," he said, and Rafael froze with her hand still out, her lips still barely parted, the last warmth of the murmur still fading in the air between them. "Whatever you said to it—I know it's silly, but it's like it understood you."

"It's the charm," Rafael said quickly, too quickly, patting the red-threaded berry she hadn't actually given him yet. "Rowan. Very restful for birds."

He laughed, wet-eyed and grateful, and pressed two extra coppers into her hand, and Rafael walked home through the twisting streets of Corvane with her heart hammering against her ribs like something trying to escape a locked room.

Stupid. Stupid, stupid. In daylight. Ten feet from a grown man. What was the point of twelve years of silence if she spent it in a single careless afternoon?

But under the fear, quieter and more shameful, was the other thing. The gull had *answered* her. Warmth had moved between her and another living creature, and for one moment she had not been alone in her own skull. She hated how much she wanted that again. Safety, her mother used to say, is a small room with a strong door, and you should be grateful for it. Her mother had never mentioned that you could starve in a small room, or that gratitude could go stale like bread left too long.

* * *

Her shop occupied the ground floor of a leaning house on Candlewick Lane, and it sold, officially, charms for the fearful: salt-wrappings, iron nails on red thread, hag-stones, dried rowan sprigs, little plaques of ash wood carved with entirely decorative knots that promised absolutely nothing and cost accordingly. Above the shop was her rooms. Below the shop was the cellar, and below the cellar was the part of her life she didn't think about during daylight.

Tonight, though, she sat in the shop after dark with her meager supper of barley porridge, listening to the rain start up against the shutters, and the silence of the city pressed in on her the way it had been pressing in all week. Something was wrong with Corvane lately. She couldn't have named it—the streets looked the same, the patrols marched the same, the shrine-candles burned the same—but the silences between sounds had gotten deeper, like the whole city was holding a breath.

Even Sable had remarked on it, and Sable remarked on very little besides food and the relative merits of different floorboards.

"You feel it too?" Rafael said to the darkness under the stair, and there was a scrabble, and the wharf-rat emerged, sleek and grey and missing half an ear from some ancient disagreement, and sat up regarding Rafael with black bead eyes full of opinions.

Sable said—in the way of feelings and pictures, though over fifteen years of friendship Rafael had translated the beast-tongue into something nearly as good as words—that the walls felt wrong. That the spaces between the walls felt crowded. That the rats were leaving the oldest parts of the city, down where the roots grew, and rats did not leave good territory lightly.

"That's cheerful," Rafael said. "Any other portents? Frogs of unusual size? Blood in the flour?"

Sable sent back an image of cheese, which was Sable's answer to most anxieties, and Rafael shared her crust and tried not to think about the deep places under the city, and failed, because thinking about things she was avoiding was her one unbroken talent.

It was later—candle guttering, rain steady, the lane empty—that she heard the bells.

Not the hour bells. The shrine bells, which did not ring. Had not rung in twelve years. The shrine bells of Corvane had been silenced along with everything else, their ropes cut, their clappers wrapped, and every child knew their mute dark shapes hanging in the temple towers like wasps' nests nobody would knock down.

They rang now. All of them at once, all over the city, a sound like struck glass and underneath it something lower, something that made her back teeth ache, and Rafael stood up so fast her stool went over, and Sable came out of the wall with every hair on end.

In the morning, she learned what the bells meant. A blind boy on Chandler's Row—one of the shrine-touched, born without sight, whose mother lit candles at the silent altar every week of his eight years—had woken seeing. The whole street was talking about it. The mother was calling it a miracle. The neighbors were calling it a miracle. Even the Warden's men, when they came asking questions with flat faces, called it a miracle in the tone of voice people use for things they intend to arrest.

Rafael stood in the market crowd and listened, cold to the bone despite the sun, as the story spread and swelled. The boy could see. Perfectly. Better than perfectly.

And his eyes, people whispered—his eyes reflected the moon. Both moons. Though last night there had been only one.

The crowd murmured with wonder. Rafael looked at the faces around her, upturned and hungry for magic to be real again, and felt the loneliness in her chest crack open like river ice, and underneath the loneliness, rising slowly, dread.

Because she knew what it cost when prayers were answered. She'd been raised on the old stories her mother told in the cellar-dark, stories with teeth in them. Gifts from the old gods came with hooks in them, and hooks came with lines, and lines led down to something with a mouth.

The gulls screamed over the harbor. Somewhere across the terraces, a bell that shouldn't ring hung still and silent, and Rafael went back to her stall and began, with hands that almost didn't shake, to make fear-charms.

They were going to sell very well indeed.
