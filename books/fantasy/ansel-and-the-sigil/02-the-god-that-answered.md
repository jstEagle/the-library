## 2. The God That Answered

The morning the blind boy of Tanner's Row woke up seeing, Rafael Ishikawa was arguing with a jar of pickled eels.

"You're not going to sell," she told them. "I know it, you know it, and yet here we both are, sitting in a shop nobody visits, pretending otherwise."

The eels said nothing, which was more courtesy than most of Corvane offered her before noon.

She'd opened the shutters early because the light had been strange — a greenish, expectant sort of light, like the city was holding its breath — and because rent was due to Mistress Ovel in four days and Rafael's coin-box held exactly enough to buy her one more night of not thinking about it. The charm-shop had been her mother's before it was hers: *Ishikawa Charms & Small Blessings*, the sign's gold leaf flaking away until it read, if you squinted kindly, *Ishikawa Charms & Small*.

Her mother would have hated what the shop had become. Or loved it. It depended on which mother you meant — the one who'd hummed over her workbench threading luck-knots with hands that never once trembled, or the one who'd gone quiet and gray in the last years, checking the door twice, three times, as though the street itself might come in and arrest them for something they hadn't done yet.

Rafael sold charms now. Harmless ones. A knot of braided cord to keep fevers down — because fever broke on its own, mostly, and the knot gave people somewhere to put their hope. A river-stone painted with a wren for safe travel. Dried lavender against nightmares, which worked about as well as lavender ever did, which is to say it smelled nice while you suffered.

And underneath all of it, folded up small behind her ribs where no Warden's iron could find it, she kept the other thing. The true thing. The word that made a sick cat sit up and wash its ears. The low whistle that called sparrows down to eat crumbs from her palm and stay, absurdly, like guests at a table. The language under language, the one every beast spoke and no human was supposed to hear.

Twelve years since magic had been outlawed in Corvane. Twelve years since the old gods had gone silent mid-prayer, mid-festival, mid-*everything*, and the Wardens had decided that whatever remained of the old power must be hunted out of the walls like rats. Twelve years of being careful. She was very good at careful. Careful was practically her trade.

So when the shouting started on Tanner's Row, she did what any careful person would do.

She went straight toward it.

---

The crowd had gathered outside the Vess family's tenement, and it had that particular texture crowds get when joy and fear have gotten tangled together and can't be pulled apart. Rafael slipped along the edge of it, small and unremarkable in her patched brown coat, and heard the story assembling itself out of a hundred mouths:

—*the boy, Tam Vess, born without eyes that worked, seven years old—*

—*his grandmother prayed at the little shrine on Cooper's Lane, the cracked one, the one nobody tends—*

—*she prayed all night, they say, and this morning he—*

Rafael found a gap between a fishwife and a man selling hot chestnuts (a crime of opportunism she respected) and looked through it.

Tam Vess stood in his doorway in his nightshirt, blinking.

Blinking like someone learning the motion from scratch. His mother crouched in front of him, weeping into her apron, and the boy kept touching her face with both hands, then pulling back to look at it, then touching it again, as though confirming that sight and touch agreed with each other. He laughed — a startled, hiccupping laugh — and the crowd made a sound like a wave going over stones.

"Say something, Tam," his mother begged. "Tell me what you see."

"You," Tam said. "I see you. I see—" He turned his head, drinking in the street, the rooftops, the pale green sky. "Everything."

And then he turned toward Rafael.

Not toward the crowd. Toward *her*, specifically, across forty faces, and Rafael felt the hair rise on her arms the way it did when a hawk took an interest in a sparrow.

"It's bright," Tam said, frowning. "Why is it so bright? Why does everyone have two shadows?"

Murmurs. Laughter, a little ragged. Someone said the boy's eyes were still learning their work, give him time.

But Rafael had gone cold all over, because she knew what lived in bright places, and she knew what cast second shadows, and she knew — with the certainty of a woman who had spent twelve years listening to things that were not supposed to talk — that the boy was looking at her not because she was standing oddly or wearing a red scarf.

He was looking at her because she was *lit*. From inside. Like a lantern seen through a curtain.

*There you are,* said something, in a voice that was not the boy's voice, though it used the boy's mouth to shape the words, soft and wondering and terribly pleased. *There you are, little speaker.*

Rafael did not run. Running was for guilty people, and she was only a charm-seller who had come out to see a miracle like everybody else. She smiled the small polite smile of a stranger in a crowd, and she stepped backward, and she let the crowd close between herself and the doorway like water closing over a dropped stone.

But her heart was hammering a rhythm she hadn't let it hammer in twelve years, and it was saying, in the oldest part of her, the part that had never learned to be careful:

*It answered. Something answered.*

---

She didn't go back to the shop. Not right away. Instead she walked, fast, through streets that seemed to be filling up with the same news the way a cellar fills with water — seeping in everywhere at once, unstoppable, changing everything it touched.

At the corner of Bellows Street, an old woman had set up a folding table with a chipped bowl of water on it and was telling anyone who'd listen that she'd prayed to Saint Aldery for rain and by the saints, look at those clouds, look at them. The clouds did look promising. They also looked, to Rafael's eye, like ordinary autumn clouds, but try telling that to a woman who had spent thirty years praying into silence and had just heard the first syllable of a reply.

Outside the tannery, two men argued in fierce whispers about whether it was holy or whether it was a trick, whether they should kneel or whether they should fetch a Warden, and Rafael noticed — filed away, the way she filed everything — that neither of them suggested it might be *both*.

By the fountain in Chandler's Square, children were playing a new game. One would squeeze her eyes shut and pray aloud — *please, please, please* — for something small and greedy: a honey-bun, a ribbon, a puppy. Then she'd open her eyes and shriek with laughter, because nothing happened, because the world was still the world. But they played it again. And again. And the game had a hunger in it already, a little engine of wanting, and Rafael watched it and thought: *that's how it starts. That's exactly how it starts.*

Because here was the thing nobody in Corvane remembered anymore, the thing that had been scrubbed out of the schoolrooms and burned off the monuments: prayer was not a letter posted to nowhere. Prayer was a *door*. And doors, her mother had told her once, in a voice Rafael had never been able to forget, do not care who knocks from the other side.

*When the gods were loud,* her mother had said, kneeling in the cellar among her jars and cords, *you could pray and be heard by something that loved you. When the gods went quiet, the doors stayed shut, and that was terrible, yes. But child — a shut door cannot be opened by the wrong thing.*

Rafael had been nine. She hadn't understood. She'd thought her mother was being gloomy, the way mothers were.

She understood now.

---

The shrine on Cooper's Lane was exactly as unimpressive as advertised: a waist-high alcove in a brick wall, roofed with a slab of slate gone green with moss, housing a statue maybe two feet tall. The statue was supposed to be the Quiet Mother, one of the lesser godlings, patron of menders and grandmothers and anyone whose work was done twice. Her face had weathered to a smooth blankness. Someone had left a stub of candle and a saucer of milk, long since skinned over.

Rafael came at dusk, when the lane was empty, and stood in front of the shrine with her hands jammed in her pockets, feeling profoundly foolish.

"Hello," she said quietly. To a statue. To a blank-faced lump of stone. If a neighbor saw her she'd claim she was inspecting the mortar. "Hello. I don't suppose you're in there."

Nothing. Of course nothing. Stone was stone; she'd known stone her whole life, and stone held exactly one opinion, which was *no*.

But she knelt anyway, because her knees decided before her pride could object, and she laid her palm flat on the shrine's base the way she'd lay a hand on a frightened animal, and she listened the way she listened — not with her ears, but with that folded-up thing behind her ribs, the gift she'd spent half a lifetime pretending wasn't there.

For a long moment: nothing. The ordinary hum of the city. Rats in a wall two houses down, complaining about a cat. A dog on a chain in a yard, dreaming of running. The small, dense, animal chatter of Corvane, which most people walked through deaf and Rafael heard the way other people heard music.

Then, beneath all of it — faint, far, like a voice calling up from the bottom of a well —

*singing.*

Not the gods' singing. She'd been too young to remember the gods' singing, but she'd heard her mother describe it often enough: vast and orderly, a thousand voices in agreement, a cathedral of sound. This was not that. This was thin and sweet and *off*, a tune that almost resolved and never did, the way a lure is almost food.

It was coming from below. From under the city. From wherever shrines went when they died, or wherever prayers pooled when nobody drank them.

Rafael snatched her hand back as if the stone had burned her.

The milk in the saucer had moved. She was nearly sure the milk in the saucer had moved — rippled outward from the center in a slow ring, the way water moves when something far beneath it exhales.

"Right," she said aloud, to the empty lane, in the brisk tone she used for eels and other problems that wouldn't cooperate. "Right. No. Absolutely not."

She stood, brushed off her knees, and walked home at a pace just short of undignified.

---

That night she sat up late at her workbench, not working. The lamp guttered. Sable — a rat of advanced years and frankly scandalous opinions, who had adopted Rafael sometime around her sixteenth winter and never explained why — sat on the shelf above her, tail curled around his feet, watching her with black bead eyes.

"You felt it too," Rafael said. It wasn't really a question. Sable had been twitchy all evening, whiskers forward, staring at the wall as though the wall owed him money.

Sable's whiskers twitched. He made no answer, because answering was the line, the bright uncrossable line, the one thing that would turn a harmless charm-witch into kindling for a Warden's pyre. In twelve years he had never spoken a human word to her, and she had never asked him to, and the not-asking was its own kind of covenant between them.

"I'm being silly," Rafael said. "A boy can see. That's a good thing. Blind boys waking up able to see is the sort of thing that happens in the stories, the *good* ones, the ones with festivals at the end." She turned a luck-knot over in her fingers without seeing it. "It's just — the second shadow. And the way he looked at me. And the milk moved, Sable. Milk doesn't move."

Sable blinked slowly, which in rat meant either *I agree* or *you're boring me*, and after twelve years Rafael still couldn't always tell.

"And everyone's *happy*," she went on, and heard her own voice crack on it, and laughed at herself, a small unhappy sound. "That's the worst part. Everyone's happy, and I'm the only one standing in the square with my skin crawling, and I can't even say why, because saying why means saying *how I know*, and saying how I know means—" She stopped. Set the knot down. "Means the pyre."

The lamp flame leaned sideways, though the window was shut.

And in the silence after, Rafael heard it again — not with her ears, not quite, but in the place where she heard beasts. Faint. Far below. That thin, sweet, unresolved singing, closer than it had been at the shrine, or perhaps only louder, patient, unhurried, the way a fisherman is patient with a line in dark water.

*Little speaker,* it seemed to say, from a hundred miles down and a hand's breadth away. *We hear you. We remember you. We are coming up.*

Rafael pinched out the lamp.

In the dark, Sable came down from the shelf and pressed himself against her wrist, warm and trembling slightly, and stayed there while the singing sang on beneath the city, beneath the shop, beneath the floorboards of everything she'd built her small safe life on.

She did not sleep. And near dawn, boots rang on the cobbles outside — hard-heeled, official, wrong for the hour — and a voice called out in the grey light, asking the street at large a question that stopped her heart:

"Which of you lives at the charm-shop? The Ishikawa shop? Open in the name of the Wardens."
