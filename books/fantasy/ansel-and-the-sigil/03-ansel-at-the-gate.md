## 3. Ansel at the Gate

The road into Corvane smelled of salt, tar, and fear.

Ansel Vey had walked enough roads to know the difference between a city that was merely poor and one that was frightened, and Corvane was the second kind. Fear had a texture. It made people walk faster and talk quieter. It made them lock their shutters against the sea wind as though the wind itself might be listening. It made the gate-guards check papers twice and smile never.

She joined the line of carts and pilgrims shuffling toward the gate, hood up, staff in hand, looking for all the world like what she appeared to be: a tired traveler with dust in her boots and nothing worth stealing. Twelve years of practice at looking like nobody. She'd gotten so good at it that sometimes she startled herself in mirrors.

"Name and business," said the gate-guard, a young man with a pimple on his chin and the eyes of someone who had been told, recently and firmly, what to watch for.

"Ansel Vey. Charm-seller."

He looked at her staff. It was just a staff—ash wood, unremarkable, its only magic being that it had carried her this far without complaint—but he looked at it the way men look at snakes. "Charms are licensed goods. You'll register with the Wardens' office within three days or forfeit your stock."

"I wouldn't dream of otherwise," Ansel said, and smiled the smile of a woman who dreamed of nothing but taxes.

He waved her through, and she passed under the gate arch into Corvane proper, and there it was again—that smell of fear under the fish-smell, faint as a wrong note in a familiar song. She had felt it before, in other cities, further along the coast. A shrine defaced here. A prayer whispered and then hastily unsaid there. And always, always, the same rumor traveling ahead of her like gulls after a fishing boat:

*The gods are answering again.*

Twelve years ago, they had gone silent. Not gradually, not fading like a candle guttering out—all at once, mid-breath, like a choir cut off by a slammed door. Ansel knew why better than anyone alive. She had been the door.

And now something was answering prayers again, and whatever it was, it wasn't the old gods come home. The old gods had been vast and strange and occasionally cruel, but they had been *bound*—woven into the covenant, their hunger knotted into law. What answered prayers now answered *wrong*. A well in Harrowmere that granted wishes and took years. A hearth-spirit in Dunlow that kept a family's fire burning while it slowly ate the family's shadows. Prayer was a door, and doors opened from both sides, and something on the far side had learned to knock.

So Ansel had come to Corvane, because Corvane was where the trail thickened, and because beneath Corvane lay the ruins where the covenant had first been sworn—the Undercity of Roots, if the old maps could be believed, though the old maps were mostly ash and wishful thinking.

First, though, the market. Because Ansel had learned something in twelve years of hunting answered prayers: the source was rarely a god. The source was usually a person. Someone small and desperate who had prayed too loudly, or listened too closely, or—in the worst cases—someone born with an ear that had never stopped hearing.

Someone like her.

* * *

The Fishmarket of Corvane occupied a long crescent between the harbor wall and the tanners' row, which meant it smelled like a war between two armies, and the fish were losing. Stalls leaned together under awnings patched with sailcloth. Gulls screamed overhead with the entitlement of landlords. And everywhere, charms.

That was the thing about outlawed magic: it didn't die, it went domestic. When the covenant broke and the old orders burned their own libraries, magic hadn't vanished—it had shrunk. It hid in knots tied a certain way, in words murmured over cradles, in iron nails hammered above doorframes by men who would have denied under torture that they believed in anything at all. Charm-selling was legal precisely because it was understood to be nonsense. The Wardens tolerated luck-knots the way a farmer tolerates a scarecrow: harmless, comforting, and proof that everyone agreed the crows weren't real.

Ansel drifted through the crowd with her hands in her pockets, listening.

Not with her ears. With the other thing. The sense she'd been born with and had spent a lifetime pretending she hadn't—the sense that heard the hum of living things, the low chord of beasts and birds and the smaller crawling nations, each species singing its own narrow, stubborn note. In most cities the chord was muffled, drowned under human noise. Here it came through clearly, and threaded through it, faint as a hair pulled from a weave, was something else.

A voice. Human-shaped, but pitched toward the beasts. Murmuring.

Ansel followed it the way water follows a slope, without deciding to, past stalls of dried cod and rope and chandlery, until she reached a corner stall barely wider than a doorway, hung with luck-knots and hag-stones and little woven birds that swayed in the harbor wind.

Behind the counter stood a woman perhaps thirty, dark-haired, sleeves rolled, with ink stains on her fingers and the particular posture of someone who has spent years making herself look smaller than she is. She was selling a luck-knot to a fisherman—a big weathered man whose face had the specific ruin of recent grief—and she was doing it kindly, which caught Ansel's attention before anything magical did. Kindness in a charm-seller was rarer than magic.

"Third knot from the left is for safe return," the woman was saying. "Tie it to the mast, not the rail. Rails get wet, and wet knots forget what they're for."

"That's not—" The fisherman stopped himself. "No. Of course. Thank you, Rafael."

Rafael. Ansel filed the name away.

The fisherman paid, and then he hesitated, and his gaze went up—to the gulls wheeling over the market, and then, specifically, to one gull sitting hunched on a bollard nearby, feathers dull, head drooping in a way that even a stranger could read. Dying things made themselves legible; it was one of the cruelties of the world.

"My brother's bird," the fisherman said quietly. "Old Salt. He followed my brother's boat eleven years. My brother went down in the spring, and the bird... he won't eat. He just sits where the boat used to tie off." He rubbed his neck, embarrassed. "I know it's foolish. I just thought—if any charm could..."

The woman—Rafael—looked at the gull for a long moment. And Ansel saw it happen. Saw the woman's shoulders drop half an inch, saw her glance left and right with the quick economy of long habit, saw her lean across the counter as if adjusting the fisherman's knot and murmur something under her breath.

Something in *the old speech*. The beast-tongue. The words that predated the covenant, that every creature with wings or whiskers understood without being taught.

Ansel stopped walking.

The gull lifted its head. Its dull eye brightened. It made a sound—not a squawk, something softer, almost conversational—and Rafael Ishikawa (for Ansel was already certain of the surname; the face matched the wanted-sketches she'd memorized a decade ago, the charm-shop license, the whole quiet careful life) nodded once, gravely, as though receiving important news.

"What did you say to it?" the fisherman asked.

"Nothing. Told you the knot needed adjusting." She smiled. It was a good smile. It was also, Ansel noted, a practiced one—the smile of a woman who had lied so often and so gently that the lies had worn smooth as river stones.

The fisherman left, lighter somehow, glancing back once at the gull, which had begun, tentatively, to preen.

And Rafael turned to restock her shelf of hag-stones, and found Ansel standing at her counter.

"One luck-knot," Ansel said, because it seemed polite to buy something before ruining a woman's life.

"For what purpose?" Rafael asked. Her voice was level. Her hands were not; they'd gone still in the way that stillness goes when it's holding something down.

"Safe travels."

"Third from the left." Rafael took one down, and her eyes flicked up—assessing, quick, cataloguing—and Ansel watched the exact moment the assessment finished and the alarm began. She saw it in the microsecond pause, the tiny recalibration behind the eyes. *This customer knows things.* "You're not from Corvane."

"No. I'm from further than that." Ansel set a coin on the counter. Then, softly, in the old speech, in the language of fur and feather and scale, she said: *"Your gull will live. But you should be more careful who watches you speak."*

Rafael went white.

It was a remarkable transformation to watch—the color draining from her face in orderly stages, the shopkeeper vanishing layer by layer until what stood behind the counter was something rawer and much older: a child caught mid-crime, except the crime was existing. Her hand crept toward the counter's edge. Not toward a weapon. Toward the little brass bell, Ansel realized. Toward some signal.

"Don't," Ansel said, in plain trade-tongue now, keeping her voice pleasant, her hood shadowing her face. "Not the bell, not the Warden's whistle, none of it. I'm not here to report you. I'm the last person in the world who would."

"You're—" Rafael's voice cracked and steadied. "There's no law against mumbling."

"There isn't. There's a law against what you *are*, though, and we both know it, and we both know I know it." Ansel leaned in slightly. Beneath the hood, she let her own senses open—let Rafael feel, if the woman had the sensitivity Ansel suspected, the hum of her, the double-voicedness of a person who spoke beast and human both. "My name is Ansel Vey. I was a keeper of the covenant, before there was a covenant to keep. And three days ago, in a village north of here, a blind girl's mother prayed at a roadside shrine for her daughter's sight—and by morning the girl could see. Except her eyes reflect a second moon, mistress charm-seller. A moon that isn't there. So I've come to find out what's answering prayers in this city, and I think you may already have heard it too."

* * *

They did not talk in the market. Rafael closed the stall with the brisk efficiency of someone fleeing, hung the CLOSED sign that Ansel suspected spent most of its life hanging, and led her through a maze of alleys with the confidence of a woman who had mapped every escape route in Corvane out of pure professional necessity. They ended up in a cramped room above the charm-shop, lit by one window and smelling of hemp cord and dried lavender, and Rafael put the table between them like a fence between two dogs who hadn't decided yet.

"Talk fast," Rafael said. "And then leave."

"You're welcome to call the Wardens instead."

"I'm considering it."

"You're not, though." Ansel sat down uninvited, which was a gamble, and noted with interest that Rafael didn't order her back up. "Because calling the Wardens means explaining how you knew I was telling the truth. And that explanation ends with you in irons."

Rafael's jaw tightened. "Who are you? Really."

"I told you. Ansel Vey. Covenant-keeper, formerly of the Ashvale chapter." She paused. "Disgraced. If you've heard the name, it'll have been attached to the word *oathbreaker*, and the stories will all be true, and several worse ones besides."

Something moved behind Rafael's eyes—not recognition exactly. Recognition of a *category*. Ansel knew that look. It was the look of a woman meeting another woman's ghost.

"The covenant broke twelve years ago," Rafael said carefully. "Everyone says the gods simply faded. Old power wears out. Like everything."

"Everyone says that because it's kinder than the truth." Ansel turned her luck-knot over in her fingers. Third from the left. For safe travels. She almost laughed. "The covenant didn't fade. It was shattered. Deliberately. By a keeper who thought she was saving the world, and who has spent twelve years discovering, one ruined city at a time, exactly how wrong she was."

Silence. From the street below came the cries of gulls and fishmongers, the ordinary music of a city that didn't know what was coming.

"And you're her," Rafael said. Not a question.

"I'm her."

"Why tell me that? You don't know me."

"No," Ansel agreed. "But I know what you are. And I know what it costs, being the only one of something." She set the luck-knot down on the table between them, a small absurd peace offering. "I spent twelve years thinking I was the last person alive who could hear the old speech. Who remembered the oaths. Who knew the covenant was broken rather than dead. And then I walked into your market and watched you lie sweetly to a grieving fisherman while whispering comfort to his dying gull in a language that officially doesn't exist."

Rafael had gone very still. The stillness of prey, and also—Ansel recognized it, hated recognizing it—the stillness of someone hearing a door open after years alone in a locked room.

"You're wrong," Rafael said.

"About which part?"

"All of it. Any of it. Take your pick." But her voice shook on the last word, and her eyes went, involuntarily, to a shadowed corner of the room near the rafters—and Ansel, whose ears had never once in her life obeyed the instruction *don't listen*, heard the small scrabble of claws and the particular breathing of a rat.

A rat that was, she realized with a jolt, humming. Low and contented. In the old speech.

Rafael followed her gaze and closed her eyes as if praying for a rockslide.

"Sable," she said, in the tone of a woman addressing a wayward nephew. "*Don't.*"

The rat emerged anyway. It climbed down the curtain with the shameless agility of its kind, trotted across the floorboards, and sat up on the table beside the luck-knot, whiskers twitching, regarding Ansel with small black eyes full of ancient and unimpressed intelligence.

Then it looked at Rafael, and in a voice like dry leaves and gravel, in the old speech that no human throat should understand and no beast should dare speak plainly before a stranger, it said:

*"Rafael. She tells truth. I smell it. Truth smells like rain."*

The room went very quiet.

Rafael stared at her rat. Her rat stared back, entirely unrepentant. And Ansel watched twelve years of careful, patient, exhausting concealment collapse in a single sentence—not because Ansel had exposed her, but because her own friend, her own small confederate, had simply decided the hiding was over.

"He talks," Ansel said. "Openly. To you. In front of strangers." Something rose in her chest that she hadn't felt in years and didn't have a name for—hope wearing grief's old coat. "Mistress Ishikawa. Do you have any idea what you are?"

"Yes," Rafael said quietly. "Illegal."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's the only answer that's kept me alive." She sank onto the stool behind her, all the fight going out of her at once, and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. For a long moment she just breathed. When she lowered her hands, she looked older and younger simultaneously, the way people do when a weight shifts. "If what you say is true—if the covenant was broken, not faded—then everything I've been afraid of for twelve years is worse than I thought."

"Worse how?"

"Because I've been hearing it too." Rafael's voice dropped. "The answers. Prayers getting... replied to. Wrong replies. There's a shrine on Lantern Row—something answered a prayer there two nights ago, and what it gave back wasn't a gift, it was a *hook*. And the beasts can hear it, Ansel. All of them. Sable hasn't slept properly in a week. The rats are leaving the lower districts. The gulls won't fly at night." She looked up, and her eyes were fierce and terrified in equal measure. "Whatever is answering—it's not a god. The beasts know what gods sound like. And they're saying this isn't one."

Ansel felt the old cold settle into her bones, the cold she'd been carrying since Ashvale, since the day she'd torn the covenant apart with her own hands and called it salvation.

"I know," she said. "That's why I'm here. That's why I need you."

"You don't need me. You need a Warden, a priest, an army—"

"I need someone who can hear what the beasts hear. Because the beasts remember what the gods were *before* worship, and that memory is the only map we have to what's coming." Ansel held out her hand across the table, palm up—an oath-gesture, old as the covenant itself, meaningless now and offered anyway. "Help me find the source. Help me reforge what I broke. And I swear to you, on what's left of my name, that when it's done, you will never have to hide again."

Rafael looked at the offered hand. At the rat. At the window, and the city beyond it, walled and fearful and praying in whispers to something hungry.

Sable sniffed the air, and said, with the grave certainty of a creature who had never once considered the consequences of his opinions:

*"We go. I know the way down."*

Rafael closed her eyes.

"Down," she repeated. "Of course it's down. It's always down."
