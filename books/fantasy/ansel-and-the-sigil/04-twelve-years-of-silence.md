## 4. Twelve Years of Silence

The knock came at the third hour past midnight, and Rafael knew before she opened the door that it was trouble, because nobody knocked at the third hour past midnight unless they were dying, drunk, or dangerous.

She lay still in her narrow bed above the charm-shop and listened. The knock came again—three measured raps, patient as a debt collector. Not the frantic hammering of someone whose child was burning with fever. Not the sloppy percussion of a dockhand who'd spent his wages on plum wine.

*Dangerous,* then.

Rafael slid out from under the blanket, bare feet finding the cold floorboards by memory, and took the iron poker from beside the hearth. Old habit. The gift was faster—the gift was always faster—but the gift was also a noose, and she'd spent twelve years learning to reach for iron first.

"Who's there?"

"A traveler," said a woman's voice. Low, even, unhurried. "I need a charm."

"We're closed."

"The sign says otherwise." A pause. "The sign says *Ishikawa Charms: Luck, Love, Safe Passage.* It's very confident for a shop that closes at sundown."

Rafael looked at the door. Oak, iron-banded, older than she was. Through the gap beneath it she could see lamplight spilling across the threshold, gold and steady.

"It's midnight," she said. "Whatever you need can wait for morning."

"It's been waiting twelve years," the voice said. "It can wait another minute. But not much longer than that."

The poker suddenly felt very small in Rafael's hand.

Because she knew that voice. Not well—not the way you know a friend—but the way you know a story you were told once, at an age when stories went into you like water into sand. A story about a covenant-keeper named Ansel Vane, who had walked out of the ruins of the Amberfall shrine at nineteen years old, alone, unburned, while every other keeper in the city lay as still as their own statues.

They'd said she broke it. They'd said she *shattered* it, though no one could say how, and the gods had gone quiet within the season, and the dragons had stopped answering, and the world had settled into its long gray silence like an old man into a chair he never meant to get up from again.

And now that voice was on her doorstep asking for a charm.

Rafael set down the poker. Picked it up again. Set it down.

She opened the door.

* * *

Ansel Vane was smaller than the stories made her. That was Rafael's first thought, absurd and disloyal. Twelve years of whispered legend had built her into something tall and terrible, a woman carved from oath-stone, and instead here was a person of ordinary height standing in the cold with a travel cloak gone green at the seams and mud to her knees, holding a lantern like she wasn't entirely sure what to do with it.

Her eyes were the problem. Everything else about her said *tired road-worn nobody*, but her eyes moved over Rafael's face with a terrible, gentle attention, the way a physician reads a body.

"You're Rafael Ishikawa," she said.

"I'm closed," Rafael said.

"You sold a luck-knot yesterday. To a fisherman. Corren Tallow, off the *Merry Adela*." Ansel's voice stayed level, almost apologetic. "Gray wool, three knots, tied left over right. You told him to hang it on his mast and think of his daughter each time the wind caught it."

"That's—" Rafael's mouth had gone dry. "That's just salesmanship. You give them something to hold onto. The knot is wool. The luck is theirs."

"Mm." Ansel stepped forward—not aggressively, just inevitably, the way weather steps forward—and Rafael found herself backing up to let her in, which she would later be furious about. "And this morning, before dawn, you stood on the harbor wall and spoke to a gull with a broken wing. And the gull answered you."

The shop felt very small all at once. The shelves of charms—horse-brass bells, knotted threads, river-stones drilled and strung—all of it suddenly looked like what it was: a disguise so old she'd forgotten she was wearing it.

"I talk to birds," Rafael said. "Lots of people talk to birds."

"Lots of people talk *at* birds." Ansel set the lantern on the counter. Her hands were steady. That was somehow worse than if they'd shaken. "The bird talked back. I watched it happen from the customs house roof. It tilted its head and made a sound no gull makes, and then it flew, wing whole, and you stood there looking like a woman watching her own funeral procession go by."

"I don't know what you think you saw—"

"Your lips moved. Your shoulders did the thing shoulders do when the gift comes up through them—I'd forgotten that part, actually, the way it sits in the body, like swallowing sunlight. And then you looked around to see if anyone had noticed, which is the most heartbreaking habit in the world, and I would know, because I had it too, once."

"Stop." The word came out sharper than Rafael intended, and she let it stay sharp, because sharp was armor. "You come into my shop at midnight, after twelve years, and you want me to stand here and confess to a crime that gets people burned? You want me to hand you my life because you *watched my shoulders*?"

"No," Ansel said quietly. "I want you to tell me why the prayers are being answered."

And there it was. The thing under everything, the thing Rafael had been stepping around for two weeks like a hole in the floor. The blind boy at the Lantern Shrine who could see. The widow on Tanner's Row whose dead husband's voice had come through the well. The fevered child in the Weavers' Quarter who woke whole, laughing, with a word in his mouth that made the candle flames lean toward him.

Everyone in Corvane was whispering about miracles. Everyone was lighting candles again, dusting off shrines, daring to hope.

Rafael had heard one of those answers herself, once, through Sable—and it hadn't sounded like hope. It had sounded like hunger wearing hope's coat.

"I don't know anything about that," she said.

"Don't you?" Ansel looked at her, and there was something in her face now besides attention—something rawer, almost pleading. "Twelve years ago the gods went silent. Every keeper in every city felt it happen, like a bell going dark mid-ring. And everyone agreed on the story: the covenant faded. Old things fade. It was sad and natural and nobody was to blame, least of all the girl who walked out of Amberfall alive." She laughed, short and bitter. "I believed it myself for about four years. Then I started listening, really listening, to what was left—the beasts, the small spirits, the things too lowly for the gods to bother silencing. And they all tell the same story, Rafael. The covenant didn't fade. It was *broken*. Snapped clean, on purpose, by someone who knew exactly what they were doing. And whatever broke it is waking back up now, and it isn't coming back kind."

"I'm sorry," Rafael said. "That sounds like a hard story. But it's not mine."

They stood there in the lamplight, two liars facing each other across a counter full of harmless charms, and Rafael thought: *she'll go. She'll take the lantern and go, and I'll bolt this door, and tomorrow I'll sell a love-charm to a baker's apprentice and everything will be exactly as safe as it was.*

Which was when Sable said, from the shelf by the window, in a small dry voice like a page turning:

"Rafael. Don't lie to her. Lying makes your ears go pink."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted four heartbeats. Rafael counted them, because counting was the only thing keeping her upright.

Sable sat on the shelf where she'd lived for six years—a rat, gray as ash, with a torn ear and the unbothered dignity of a duchess—and washed her face with both paws as though she hadn't just committed treason against everything Rafael had built.

"Sable," Rafael said, very carefully, "we've *talked* about this."

"We have," Sable agreed. "You said, and I quote, *never speak in front of anyone, ever, or we both die horribly.* I have reflected on this instruction at length." She paused in her washing. "But she already knows. And you've been lonely for twelve years, and it's making you stupid, and I'm tired of watching."

"*Sable—*"

"She has kind hands," Sable said, nodding toward Ansel. "Beasts can tell. Also she smells like dragon-ash, which is either very good or very bad, but definitely interesting."

Ansel hadn't moved. She was staring at the rat with an expression Rafael had never seen on a human face before—wonder and grief tangled together so tightly you couldn't find the seam. When she finally spoke, her voice had lost all its careful evenness.

"What's her name?"

"...Sable."

"Sable." Ansel said it the way you'd say the name of someone you'd mourned. She crouched slowly, bringing herself level with the shelf, and held out one hand—not reaching, just offering, palm up, the way you offer to a creature that might bite or might bless you. "Hello, Sable. My name is Ansel. I used to be able to do what she does. Before."

"Before what?" Sable asked.

"Before I broke the world."

The words dropped into the room like stones into a well. Rafael gripped the edge of the counter. "You—what? You just said—you just admitted it? To a *rat*?"

"Rats keep better secrets than people," Sable said primly.

"They do," Ansel agreed, and something in her face cracked open, and Rafael understood with a lurch that this woman had been carrying her confession alone for twelve years, the way Rafael had been carrying her gift alone, and that they were both so starved for someone to *know* that they'd confess to anything with ears.

"You broke the covenant," Rafael said. It wasn't a question anymore. The denial had gone out of her like air from a punctured lung, and what rushed in behind it was terrifyingly close to relief. "Amberfall. That was you. The silence—that's *you*. Twelve years of dead shrines and empty skies and dragons that won't answer, and it was you."

"Yes."

"And now the prayers are being answered again, and that's—"

"Also probably me. Or rather, the hole I made." Ansel straightened up. Up close, in the lamplight, Rafael could see how tired she was—not road-tired, bone-tired, the tiredness of someone who had been walking toward this conversation for over a decade. "When you break a seal, you don't remove what was behind it. You just open the way. The gods didn't die, Rafael. They went *quiet*—held shut, muffled, the way you'd wrap a snarling dog's muzzle. Whatever bargain kept them wrapped is coming undone. And what's leaking through isn't the old gods. It's the thing that wears them."

"The blind boy," Rafael whispered. "His eyes. There's a second moon in them."

Ansel went very still. "Show me."

"I can't show you, he's a *child*, his mother would—"

"Then describe him. Exactly."

So Rafael did. The boy, Tomas, seven years old, born blind, whose mother had prayed every night at the Lantern Shrine for two years. Who had woken three days ago seeing. Whose eyes, when the light caught them, held a small silver disc that no one else could see, floating where a reflection should be—except there had been no moon in the shrine that night, and the disc never blinked, and sometimes, Rafael had heard from the shrine-keeper's cousin, the boy spoke in his sleep in a voice that wasn't his, thanking people. Thanking everyone. Over and over. *Thank you, thank you, thank you.*

Ansel listened without moving. When Rafael finished, she sat down on the shop's one stool, abruptly, like a puppet with cut strings.

"That's Hollow-Voice," she said. "Or the start of it. It answers prayers because prayers are doors. Every answered prayer opens the believer a little wider." She rubbed her face with both hands. "I came to Corvane following rumors. I hoped I was wrong. I hoped it was just... echoes. Seams stretching. But a second moon in a child's eyes—" She looked up, and her eyes were wet, and furious, and afraid. "It's starting again. What I stopped. Or tried to stop. And I can't do it alone, because I couldn't do it alone last time either, that's the whole reason everything went wrong, and the only other person in this city with the gift is a charm-seller who talks to rats and lies about it."

"I don't lie about it," Rafael said automatically. "I just... decline to mention it."

"You lie about it beautifully," Sable said. "For the record, I've always admired the craftsmanship."

Rafael laughed. It burst out of her before she could stop it—cracked, half-hysterical, twelve years of held breath escaping all at once—and she pressed her hand over her mouth, and laughed again, and then, horribly, felt tears start, hot and fast, because the laughter had broken something and now everything was coming through the crack.

Being known, it turned out, felt like this. Like setting down a weight so heavy you'd forgotten it was heavy, and discovering that your arms still worked, and hating yourself for the relief, because the weight had been the only thing keeping you safe.

"Oh no," she managed. "No, stop, this is—this is the worst night of my life—"

"It gets worse," Ansel said, with the ghost of a smile. "I promise you, it gets significantly worse."

"Rafael," Sable said, and her voice had changed—no duchess now, no humor, just the small grave clarity that beasts kept for important things. "Ask her what broke the covenant. Ask her what she traded. Because I've smelled it on her since she walked in, under the dragon-ash." The rat's whiskers trembled. "She smells like a bargain that hasn't finished being paid."

The laughter died in Rafael's chest.

Ansel met her eyes across the lamplit counter, and didn't look away, and said, "There's a cellar under this shop. Under your mother's old charm-shop. I've walked a long way to ask you this, so I'll only ask once: has anything in this building ever hummed at you? Anything buried?"

Rafael's blood went cold, because yes. Yes, it had. For twelve years, some nights, through the floorboards, faint as a heartbeat under snow—she'd told herself it was the river, the pipes, her own pulse.

She looked down at the trapdoor behind the counter, the one she'd never once opened, that her mother had told her on her deathbed never to touch, with a grip on Rafael's wrist that had left bruises.

"It hums," Rafael said. "Every night. Like it's counting."

Ansel rose from the stool. Somewhere far above the city, thunder rolled across a sky that had held no storms in twelve years.

"Then we should open it," she said, "before whatever's counting finishes."
