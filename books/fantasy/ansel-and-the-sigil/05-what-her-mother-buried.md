## 5. What Her Mother Buried

The cellar door had been locked for three years, and Rafael had spent those three years pretending that was a coincidence.

It wasn't, of course. Nothing about her mother had ever been a coincidence. Maren Ishikawa had been a woman who salted her thresholds against bad luck and her letters against snooping, who labeled every jar in the shop in a hand so precise it looked printed, and who had died on a Tuesday with her accounts balanced and her affairs in order and one instruction left behind, delivered to Rafael by the solicitor with the mild apologetic air of a man handing over something slightly damp:

*"The cellar stays shut until you're ready. You'll know when."*

Rafael had not known when. She had known grief, hunger, the particular humiliation of haggling over lamp-oil, and — as of two days ago — she had known Ansel's eyes on her across a market crowd, steady and terrible, the look of someone who had recognized a thing Rafael had spent twelve years making invisible.

She supposed that counted as *ready*.

The key was where it had always been hidden, which was to say nowhere sensible: inside the hollow handle of the good ladle, wrapped in oilcloth, because her mother had believed that thieves never stole kitchenware. Rafael sat on the cellar steps for a long moment after the lock gave, listening to the shop above her creak through its evening settling, and told herself she was only going down to inventory the stores. Candle wax. Dried nettle. The ordinary stock of an ordinary charm-witch selling ordinary nothing.

The lamp caught the stairs one at a time. Twelve of them. She'd counted them as a child, sliding down on a tea tray while her mother shouted up through the floorboards that if she broke her neck, at least do it quietly, the neighbors were sensitive.

The cellar was exactly as she remembered it, which was the first wrong thing. Cellars did not stay still. They grew mold, they shed plaster, they collected the slow sediment of years. This room had been held. Every jar stood in its row. Every label faced out. In the corner, the workbench waited under a sheet of canvas so clean it might have been folded yesterday, and dust lay over everything else like snow over a field — everywhere except a narrow path from the stairs to the bench, swept bare, as though someone had walked it recently, or as though the room itself had been keeping the way clear.

"Mother," Rafael said to the empty dark, "you could not have left me *one* normal inheritance? A recipe book? A slightly cursed teapot?"

The dark declined to answer, which was more courtesy than most gods showed these days.

She lifted the canvas off the bench. Beneath it lay tools she knew — pliers, wire, the little bone-handled knife for cutting knot-cords — and things she didn't. A mortar stained with something black that had soaked into the stone and would not scrub out. A ledger, closed, its cover stamped with no title at all. And beneath the ledger, half-tucked into shadow, a stone the size of a fist.

It was warm.

Not sun-warm, not hearth-warm. Warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, warmth with a pulse in it, faint and slow, and when Rafael picked it up the pulse quickened against her palm like a small frightened heart deciding whether to trust her. The stone was gray river-rock to look at, unremarkable, the kind of thing a child would skip across water. But carved into its face was a spiral, worn nearly smooth, and the moment her thumb found the groove of it, the hair on her arms rose and every jar in the cellar rattled once, softly, all together, like a held breath let go.

She set it down very carefully. Then she picked it up again, because she was her mother's daughter and fear had never once stopped her hands from working.

The ledger opened to a page near the middle, as if it had been waiting there. Her mother's handwriting marched across it, neat as fence posts:

*Day 1. The bargain is struck. I have done a terrible thing and I have done it well.*

Rafael read it standing up. Then her knees informed her that standing was optional, and she read it sitting down on the cold flagstones with the sigil-stone cradled in her lap like something rescued from a fire.

*Day 1,* the entry began again, properly this time, beneath a date Rafael did not need to count backward to understand. Twelve years ago. The year the gods went quiet.

---

She had been nine. She remembered the year in pieces, the way children remember — in smells and wrongnesses rather than events. She remembered that the luck-knots her mother tied had begun to hold better than knots had any right to hold. She remembered the fish coming back to Corvane's harbor in numbers the old men called miraculous and her mother called, very quietly, *enough*. She remembered her mother burning papers in the shop's stove at odd hours, feeding the flames handfuls at a time, watching the door while she did it.

And she remembered the night her mother came home smelling of rain and smoke and knelt down on the shop floor to be at eye level with her, which her mother almost never did, because Maren Ishikawa believed kneeling was for prayer and prayer was for other people.

"Rafi. If anything ever happens to me — if anyone ever asks you what I did, or why — you tell them I sold charms. That's all. Can you remember that?"

"You sell charms," nine-year-old Rafael had said, puzzled. "That's true anyway."

Her mother had laughed — a short, startled sound, like a hiccup wearing a disguise — and pressed their foreheads together. "Yes," she'd said. "That's true anyway. Good girl. Keep it true."

Then she'd stood up, brushed off her skirts, and gone upstairs to make dinner, and Rafael had thought no more about it, because she was nine and dinner was happening.

---

*Day 3.* The ledger's ink had changed color partway through, brown-black to plain black, as though written in haste or in different blood. *The Voice came to me as I asked. It is not what the stories say gods are. It is hungry in a way I did not expect. It offered silence and I took silence, because the alternative was my daughter.*

*Understand this, whoever reads: they wanted her. Not harmed — kept. The old powers had marked Rafi before she could walk. Beast-speakers are how covenants are heard, and a covenant can only be broken or remade through the ear that hears both sides. They meant to raise her as their instrument. I have made instead a bargain: the gods withdraw, all of them, everywhere, and in exchange the girl is passed over. Unmarked. Unheard. Ordinary.*

*I have bought my daughter a small life with the world's great voice. Let any judge of me weigh that honestly before they cast the first stone.*

Rafael's hands had gone numb around the stone. She read on anyway. Her mother had taught her that too — finish the page before you weep, tears warp the ink.

*Day 9. It is done. The last shrine has gone dark. The priests are calling it a fading, a natural dying-out, and the Wardens are already saying magic was only ever superstition dressed in ceremony. Good. Let them believe it faded. A thing that fades might return; a thing that never existed cannot be mourned.*

*But I write this so that someone, someday, knows the truth: the gods did not die. They were silenced. By me. For her.*

*And silences, I am learning, are not empty. Something fills them. Something is already filling this one. I hear it some nights, down here, in the walls. It sounds like prayer being answered by whatever is nearest. I have bound the sigil-stone of ash to keep it sleeping, but binding is not banishing, and my hands are not what they were.*

*If the silence begins to break — if prayers start being answered again, wrongly, hungrily — then the bargain is failing, and everything I traded for your small life will come due at once. There are four sigils, Rafi. Ash, tide, bone, ember. Scattered, as I scattered them, so no one hand could gather them. The covenant they belong to was sworn beneath Corvane, in the roots, before the city had a name. Find the keeper. The covenant-keeper will know what must be done, though I pray to no one now that you never have to learn it.*

*Forgive me. Or don't. Either way, keep the shop. It was always yours.*

Below that, in smaller writing, cramped against the bottom margin as though her mother had been running out of paper or courage:

*P.S. The rat is not a pet. She knows. Feed her anyway.*

Rafael laughed. It came out cracked down the middle, half sob, and she let it, alone in the dark where no Warden could hear and no neighbor could report and the only witness was a stone with a heartbeat and a dead woman's honest arithmetic.

Twelve years. Twelve years of hiding in plain sight, of harmless charms and lowered eyes, of murmuring comfort to gulls and rats and once, memorably, a very opinionated mule — and none of it had been caution. It had been *payment*. Her whole careful, small, safe life had been purchased with the world's voice, and the bill had come due the week a blind child woke up seeing with a second moon swimming in his eyes.

"I know," she said aloud, to her mother, to the cellar, to nobody. "I know what you did. I'd have done it too."

That was the worst part. Not anger. She reached for anger and found only recognition, the terrible mirror of it: a mother, a daughter, a bargain. She would have burned the heavens down for a child. Her mother had merely arranged a quieter fire.

The sigil-stone pulsed in her palm, patient as a sleeping dog. *Ash*, the ledger said. One of four. Her mother had hidden them scattered — and yet here was the first one, sitting in the one place in Corvane no one had been permitted to enter for three years, guarded by a locked door and a dead woman's will.

*You left it for me,* Rafael realized. *You didn't scatter it far at all. You left it where only I would find it, when I was ready. You absolute, impossible woman.*

Above her, through the floorboards, came a sound that did not belong to the shop's evening settling. A knock. Two knocks, then a pause, then two more — deliberate, unhurried, the knock of someone who expected to be answered and had all night to wait.

Rafael blew out the lamp and climbed the twelve stairs with the stone warm against her chest and her mother's confession folded into her shirt, and when she opened the shop door, Ansel stood in the lamplight of the street with rain in her hair and Sable riding her shoulder like a small gray general who had switched sides.

"We need to talk," Ansel said. "Your rat insisted."

Sable, from her perch, said clearly: *"Cellar. Now. Before the walls repeat what they heard."*

Rafael looked at the rat. She looked at the stranger who had hunted her gift across a market square. She looked back down the dark stairwell, toward the swept path and the pulsing stone and the ledger full of her mother's terrible, loving arithmetic.

"Come in," she said, stepping aside. "And close the door quickly. My house has started eavesdropping."
