## 6. The Undercity of Roots

### Ansel

The entrance to the undercity was behind a fishmonger's stall, which Ansel had always found either deeply poetic or deeply insulting, depending on the day.

"Here?" Rafael whispered, clutching her satchel of charm-supplies like a shield. "Behind the mackerel?"

"The mackerel is load-bearing," Ansel said, and lifted the trapdoor.

It was, in fairness, not a mackerel joke. The stall had been built over the old Pilgrim's Door because the fishmonger's grandfather had been the last keeper of it, and keepers of hidden things made excellent fishmongers—nobody questioned a man who rose before dawn and looked tired and secretive. Ansel had known his son. She had known his son's funeral. That was twelve years of silence in miniature, and she did not say any of it aloud.

The trapdoor opened on a stair of black stone, worn into two shallow channels by centuries of feet. Pilgrims had come down these stairs in their thousands, once. They had come singing. Ansel remembered the singing.

She did not remember it fondly. That was the trouble with memory; it kept no honest ledger.

"You first," Rafael said.

"I always first. That's the arrangement."

"I don't remember agreeing to an arrangement."

"You agreed to it about four minutes ago, when you decided to trust me, which was a mistake, and I intend to honor it." Ansel lit the lamp at her belt. Old covenant-keeper lamps burned without oil—a small, lawful magic, the last scrap of her order that still worked—and the flame came up gold and steady. "Stay behind my shoulder. If the roots speak, don't answer. If the roots sing, hum along. If the roots ask you a question—"

"What kind of question would roots ask?"

"Personal ones." Ansel stepped down into the dark. "They have a great deal of time on their hands and no manners whatsoever."

The stair went down farther than the city had foundations, which was the first impossible thing. Corvane's walls were old; its cellars were older; but the undercity was older than the idea of cellars. The walls changed as they descended—dressed stone, then fitted stone, then stone that had never been cut at all but *persuaded*, grown into shape by hands that had treated geology as a slow and willing friend. Ansel watched Rafael's fingers trail along it and saw the moment the woman understood what she was touching.

"This is grown," Rafael breathed. "Stone doesn't grow."

"Stone didn't. It does, down here. Or it did." Ansel kept her voice level. Grief, she had learned, was best carried flat, like a tray of full cups. "The covenant wasn't a contract, Rafael. It was a marriage. The realm of roots and the realm of stone and the realm of fire and the realm of tide, all wed to the realm of men, and the vows were spoken here, in the dark, where nothing could overhear and nothing could interrupt."

"And now?"

"Now it's a very large empty room with excellent acoustics."

The stair opened out.

Ansel had prepared herself. She had rehearsed this moment on the road for three days, composing her face into the calm of a guide showing tourists a ruin. It did not help. It never helped.

The Hall of Vows was a cavern the size of the city above it, and it was alive.

Roots—enormous, pale, slow as glaciers—came down through the ceiling of the world like the ribs of some buried god, and where they met the floor they had woven themselves into benches, into pillars, into a dais at the far end where four thrones of living wood still stood. The roots were white as birch and faintly, unmistakably *warm*. Ansel felt the heat of them on her face like standing near an oven door. And beneath the heat, beneath the silence, they hummed.

Not a sound. A hum. The kind of hum a struck bell keeps after the ringing stops, if the bell were the size of a mountain and had been struck twelve years ago and never allowed to fall fully still.

"Oh," Rafael said.

It was a small word. Ansel had heard people say *oh* at funerals and weddings and the births of children, and Rafael said it the way all of those people said it, and then she walked past Ansel into the hall like a woman walking into the sea, and Ansel stood at the edge and let her go, because that was the other thing about grief: sometimes you had to watch someone else touch the wound to know it was real.

Rafael crossed the hall slowly. She stopped at the nearest root-pillar and laid both palms against it, and Ansel saw the hum answer her—saw the pale wood flush, very faintly, the color of dawn.

"It knows me," Rafael whispered. "Ansel. It *knows me*."

"It knows your gift. It knew every beast-speaker who ever came down here. They sat where you're standing and translated." Ansel came forward, her boots loud on stone that should have been soft with moss and wasn't. Everything down here was preserved like a fly in amber—clean, intact, dead-but-for-the-hum. "The animals had no oaths to swear. They were the witnesses. A covenant needs witnesses, and what better than creatures who cannot lie and cannot be bribed and will outlive every court in creation?"

Rafael turned. Her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "And this is what my mother's bargain silenced."

"This is what the breaking silenced. Your mother's bargain came after." Ansel said it gently. She had been saving the gentleness up for days, like coins. "Don't carry the whole weight yet. Carry your share. I'll carry mine. It's heavier than it looks, mine."

"What did the vows sound like?" Rafael asked, and her voice broke a little on *sound*, and Ansel understood that Rafael was not asking about the words.

"Like this," Ansel said, and put her hand on the pillar beside Rafael's.

The hum rose.

It was not words. It had never been words. It was the feeling of a promise kept so long it had become a law of nature—the feeling of a door that opens, of a fire that waits, of a tide that comes back. Ansel had stood here at nine years old with her hand on this exact pillar while her teacher said *listen, child, this is what safety sounds like*, and she had listened, and she had spent the rest of her life learning that safety was a sound she herself had ended.

The hum faded. The roots dimmed to their patient white.

Rafael took her hand off the pillar like it had burned her. "It's grieving," she said. "I can feel it. It's not dead, it's *grieving*."

"Yes."

"Can it be—" She stopped. Started again. "Is it still in there? The covenant? Or is this just—echo? A corpse that hums?"

Ansel looked at the four thrones at the far end of the hall. The throne of ash was scorched black. The throne of tide was furred with salt that had stopped falling. The throne of bone was clean as a knuckle. And the throne of ember—

The throne of ember was the reason they had come.

"It's in there," Ansel said. "Sleeping, not dead. That's why the prayers are being answered wrong. A sleeping covenant can't protect the doors between realms, and something has found a door and is standing in it, wearing the voice of a god." She turned to Rafael. "There are four sigils. Ash, tide, bone, ember. They were scattered when the vows broke—hidden by the last keepers so no one could misuse them. I know where three of them sleep. The fourth—" She touched the scorched throne without looking at it. "The fourth is a problem for later, and I'd like very much for later to hold off."

"Ansel." Rafael's voice had gone quiet in the way that made Ansel's stomach drop. "You said the roots ask questions."

"They do."

"What will they ask *you*?"

The hum stirred, faintly, like a dog lifting its head.

"They already know," Ansel said. "That's the thing about this place. It doesn't ask what you did. It just... keeps the record." She managed something that was almost a smile. "Come on. The tide sigil is a day's walk from here, through passages that were dug for people eight feet tall, and I want to be rude to it before the Warden's people find the fishmonger and start asking why his cellar breathes."

* * *

### Rafael

They walked for hours, and Rafael fell in love with the dark, which was not something she had expected of her life.

Not the dark itself—she was a shopkeeper, she knew the dark of a cellar, the dark of poverty, the dark behind your eyelids when you pretended to sleep so the neighbors wouldn't hear you crying. But this dark was *occupied*. The roots came down through the passages at intervals, and every time they passed one, the hum rose to meet them, and every time, Rafael felt it in her teeth like a hymn she'd forgotten the words to.

Sable rode in her satchel with his nose out, and he was uncharacteristically quiet, which for Sable was the equivalent of a religious experience.

"You feel it too," she murmured to him.

*It smells like before,* Sable said. *Before the quiet. I was young then. I thought the quiet was just how the world was.* He twitched his whiskers. *It is not how the world was. The world was loud. I miss the loud.*

Rafael's chest did something complicated. "I'm sorry, little one."

*You are going to fix it,* Sable said. It was not a question, which was how rats comforted themselves, she had learned. Rats did not hope. Rats *assumed*, aggressively, and dared the world to disagree.

Ansel walked ahead with the lamp, and Rafael watched her back and thought about the pillar. *It knows your gift.* Twelve years Rafael had hidden, had muffled, had lied with her whole body—and the first ancient living thing she'd touched since her mother died had recognized her in a heartbeat, had flushed warm for her, had grieved in front of her without shame. The roots grieved openly. The roots had been grieving for twelve years in the dark where no one came, and no one had told them to stop, and no one had punished them for it.

*You could be like that,* Rafael thought, and then flinched from the thought, because thoughts like that were how beast-speakers got caught.

"Question," she said aloud, to stop her own mind. "The thrones. Who sat in them?"

"Keepers. One from each realm's people. The ash-keeper was human—fire-priests, mostly, stubborn lot. The tide-keepers came up from the coast, people who could hold their breath for an hour and gossip the whole time. Bone-keepers were catacomb-dwellers. And the ember-keeper—" Ansel's stride hitched, barely. "The ember-keeper was a dragon, when a dragon could fit. In the last centuries they sent a fire-priest instead and the dragon attended by... correspondence."

"Correspondence."

"Dragons are formal. There were letters. I've read some. A dragon's idea of a polite opening greeting runs about forty lines." Ansel glanced back, and in the lamplight Rafael caught something young and unguarded cross her face. "I liked the letters. Before."

*Before.* The word had a weight to it, and Rafael was beginning to understand that Ansel carried a whole set of scales, and *before* sat on one side and *after* on the other, and the after-side was winning.

They stopped to rest in a chamber where the roots had grown into something like a kitchen—benches, a hearth of pale wood, hollows in the wall shaped for jars. Rafael ran her fingers along one hollow. "People lived down here."

"Pilgrims stayed. Keepers lived." Ansel set the lamp down and produced bread and hard cheese from her pack, and Rafael produced, with the reflexive shame of the poor, a twist of paper containing two honeyed figs she'd been saving. Ansel looked at the figs. Looked at Rafael. "Those are for an emergency."

"This is an emergency. I'm emotionally compromised and you're feeding me bread like we're soldiers."

Ansel took a fig. She ate it slowly, and Rafael had the strange, piercing sense that she was watching someone eat a memory. "My teacher brought figs," Ansel said, to the middle distance. "Down here. She said the roots liked the smell. She was wrong—the roots don't smell, they don't have noses—but she said it every trip, and every trip the roots hummed a little louder while she ate, and she took that as agreement, and it made her happy, so." She shrugged. The shrug did not work. It collapsed halfway through. "Anyway."

Rafael reached over and squeezed her wrist, once, and let go. Neither of them said anything. It was, Rafael thought, the first fully honest conversation they'd had.

They slept in shifts in the old keeper's chamber. Rafael dreamed of the hum, and in the dream it had words, and the words were *come back, come back, come back*, and she woke with wet eyes and did not examine why.

* * *

On the second day the passages changed, and Rafael felt it before she understood it.

The hum had been constant—a low, vast, patient note, the same everywhere. Now it began to bend. To *lean*. As they walked, the note deepened and slowed, took on a rhythm like breathing, like waves, and the air grew cool and tasted of salt that had no sea anywhere near it.

"The tide sigil," Rafael said.

"You feel it." Ansel sounded pleased and worried in equal measure, which seemed to be her resting state. "Good. That means the sigil still recognizes gift-blood. Bad, because it means it's *awake enough to notice us*, and the tide sigil has always been the sentimental one. The ash sigil will ignore you completely. The bone sigil will judge you silently. The tide sigil will want to talk about your feelings."

"That sounds nice, actually."

"It sounds nice right up until it asks what you'd sacrifice to get what you want, and then you're standing in a flooded passage having a conversation with a rock, and the rock is *right*, Rafael. That's the horror of it. The sigils are always right." Ansel stopped at a junction where three passages met, and the hum here was unmistakably the sound of the sea heard through a wall. "Before we go in. Rules. You don't promise it anything. Not a word, not a nod, not a *feeling*—it can read feelings, that's how it takes payment. If it makes you an offer, you say 'I'll consider it,' and then we leave, and then you consider it for the rest of your natural life."

"Has anyone ever taken its offer?"

"Everyone takes its offer eventually. That's why the keepers stopped letting people visit." Ansel's jaw tightened. "My teacher took it. That's how she died—she gave the sigil a memory she couldn't afford to lose, and then she lost it, and then she lost the shape of herself around the hole it left, and she walked into the sea one morning and the sea was polite enough to give her back." Ansel looked at Rafael, and her voice went low and fierce. "So: *I'll consider it.* Four words. Can you remember four words?"

"I've memorized longer prayers."

"Prayers don't lie to you beautifully. This thing will." Ansel turned into the center passage. "Stay behind my shoulder. And Rafael—if it offers you your mother back—"

The hum surged.

It came up through the floor and the walls and Rafael's own bones, warm and vast and sorrowful, and it was not words, but it did not need words, because it was the feeling of her mother's kitchen, of chamomile steam, of a hand smoothing her hair, of *there, there, little wren*, and Rafael's eyes flooded before she could stop them and her mouth was already opening around something like *yes*—

"*I'll consider it,*" Ansel said loudly, to the passage, on Rafael's behalf. "She'll consider it. We're both considering it. We're *very* considerate."

The hum receded, sulkily, Rafael thought, like a cat denied a lap.

They stood in the dark, breathing. Rafael wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "It didn't even ask a question."

"It didn't have to. It knows what you want. That's the whole trick of it." Ansel's face was pale in the lamplight. "That thing down the passage has been listening to Corvane's prayers for twelve years with nothing to do but *sympathize*. And now something else is answering those prayers, something with the same trick and none of the love, and Rafael—" She stopped. Rafael watched her decide to say it. "Rafael, if the sigils can read a want from a hundred feet, then whatever is wearing godhood up there has been reading the whole city like a menu. And it's had twelve years to get hungry."

Ahead of them, down the salt-tasting dark, the hum of the tide sigil rose and fell like breathing—and then, beneath it, faint and wrong, came a second sound. A third moon's sound. The sound of a voice that answered prayers, echoing up from somewhere far below, as if it had found the undercity too.

Sable's claws pricked through the satchel's canvas.

*It's down here,* the rat whispered. *The hungry thing. It's been down here all along.*
