## 7. Sable's Bargain

The rats came at dusk, the way they always did when they had something worth saying.

Rafael was counting her coin—counting being a generous word for what amounted to arranging nine coppers and a button into the saddest little parade Corvane had ever seen—when she heard the scratch of small feet on her windowsill. She didn't look up right away. Looking up too eagerly was how you got caught caring, and caring, in Rafael Ishikawa's long and careful education, was the first step toward ruin.

But her heart did that stupid hopeful lurch it always did, because there were only so many visitors who came by window rather than door, and all of them had whiskers.

"Sable," she said, opening the shutter. "You're late."

*We're punctual,* said Sable, pouring herself over the sill like spilled ink with opinions. She was a brown rat of considerable dignity, one ear notched from an old trap and one eye clouded white, which gave her the look of a tiny pirate who had seen things. *The sewers flooded on the east side. Half my cousins are swimming for their lives and the other half are complaining about it.*

"Both halves sound like you."

*Flattery,* Sable said, settling onto the table beside the coin parade and regarding it with open pity. *From a woman with nine coppers and a button. The button isn't even a good button.*

"It's a lucky button."

*It's a button.*

Rafael swept the coins into her palm and the button back into its jar, where it had lived for six years, untested but theoretically fortunate. "You didn't come through a flooded sewer to insult my finances. What is it?"

Sable groomed her whiskers, which Rafael had learned over eleven years of friendship meant *I'm deciding how much to tell you*, and which had never once ended well.

*It's the prayers,* Sable said at last. *The answered ones.*

Rafael went still. Outside, the evening bells rang from the Warden's tower, flat and toneless, the way everything in Corvane rang since the gods went quiet. Twelve years of silence. Twelve years of harmless charms and hidden words and a city that had learned to pray to nothing and expect nothing back.

And then, three weeks ago, the prayers had started getting answers.

"I know about the prayers," Rafael said carefully. "Everyone knows about the prayers. The blind boy who can see now. The woman on Tanner's Row whose wells filled with sweet water. The Warden's posted rewards for anyone who reports—" She stopped. "What about them?"

*My cousins live under Tanner's Row.* Sable's voice had gone very quiet, and Sable doing quiet was worse than Sable doing loud. *They say the water in those wells tastes wrong. Not spoiled. Wrong. Like it's listening. And the blind boy—the one with the second moon in his eyes—he walks past the drains at night and every rat within thirty feet goes flat to the stone and doesn't breathe until he's gone.*

"He's a child."

*He's a doorway,* Sable said. *There's a difference, and the difference has teeth.*

Rafael sat down on her stool. Her shop—the shop, she corrected herself, her mother's shop, which she merely inhabited the way you inhabit an old coat—was dim around her, shelves of luck-knots and fever-wards and jars of nothing-in-particular casting soft familiar shadows. It had felt safe an hour ago. It felt like paper now, paper walls between her and something walking the streets above the sewers.

"Tell me what your cousins told you," she said. "All of it."

*That's why we're here.* Sable hopped down from the table, and Rafael realized with a small cold shock that the rat wasn't alone. The shadows along the baseboard shifted, and out of them came others: a lean gray rat named Bracken who spoke only in short sentences; a fat old tomcat called Duchess who technically belonged to the baker next door and technically obeyed no one; a starling with a broken wing feather who perched on the curtain rod and refused, on principle, to enter buildings any faster than necessary.

The whole parliament of them. Rafael's chest did something complicated.

"You're all here," she said. "That's either very good or very bad."

*Yes,* said Bracken.

*Bad,* said the starling, whose name was Pip and whose contributions to any conversation were reliably the least helpful available.

Duchess said nothing, because Duchess communicated exclusively through the weight of her stare, and her stare currently weighed approximately as much as a collapsed roof.

Sable climbed onto the table again, facing Rafael directly, tail curled around her feet like a gavel. *Here is what the beasts know, and here is why the beasts know it and the humans don't: we remember. That's the whole of our magic, girl, the thing your kind outlawed and hunted and burned us for harboring—we remember longer than you do. Dogs remember the old shrines. Crows remember the old names. Rats remember everything, because everyone forgets rats, and forgotten things keep their shape.*

"And what do you remember?"

*I remember what answered prayers used to feel like.* Sable's whiskers trembled. *Warmth. Like lying in a sunbeam that knows your name. When the old covenant held, and a god answered a prayer, it was—it was being handed bread by someone who loved you. My grandmother's grandmother described it. Every beast line passed it down, the way yours passed down charm-songs.*

Rafael's hands had gone cold around her cup. "And the ones now?"

Sable looked at Bracken. Bracken looked at Duchess. Duchess, magnificently, looked at Rafael.

*Cold,* Sable said. *The answers now feel like being swallowed. The water on Tanner's Row fills because something wants the people there grateful. Grateful people stop asking questions. The boy sees because something wants eyes in the world that report back to it. Every prayer that gets answered is a hook, Rafael. Something below the city is fishing, and it's using miracles for bait.*

"The old gods wouldn't—"

*The old gods are silent.* Bracken's voice, flat as slate. *Silent means gone, or silent means sealed. Either way, this isn't them. This is something wearing their shape the way a lure-fish wears light.*

*A predator wearing godhood,* Sable said simply. *That's what we've taken to calling it. We don't name it properly. Naming invites.*

The room seemed to lean in around Rafael. She thought of the blind boy's eyes, that second moon floating in each iris, and how the whole street had wept with joy, and how she herself had stood in that crowd feeling wonder curdle slowly into dread without knowing why. Because her gift had known before she did. Beasts knew first. They always had.

"There's more," she said. "You didn't bring the entire neighborhood to tell me what I already half-knew."

Sable was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had lost its piratical crispness and gained something older and wearier.

*There's a woman asking after you. Dark coat, grey eyes, walks like someone who used to carry a sword and misses it. She's been to the shrine district, the fish market, the tanner's row. Asking about charmsellers. Asking about anyone who talks to animals.* A pause. *She stood outside your shop yesterday for the length of a bell. I watched her from the gutter.*

Ansel. Rafael's stomach dropped through the floorboards, through the cellar, through whatever lay beneath the cellar, which after this week she was no longer confident was just dirt. The disgraced covenant-keeper with the scarred hands and the eyes that had seen Rafael murmur to a dying gull across a crowded market and *known*.

"She's not hunting me," Rafael said. "Not exactly. She thinks I might be able to help her. There's a covenant—a pact between realms—and it broke twelve years ago, and these answered prayers are leaking through the crack." She heard herself and almost laughed. "Listen to me. Two months ago my biggest problem was whether the barley merchant would extend my credit."

*The covenant,* Sable repeated. Something flickered behind her one good eye. *The beasts have songs about the covenant. Old ones. Half-eaten ones.*

"Then maybe you can help us. Ansel says the covenant was sworn somewhere beneath the city—in ruins under Corvane, some place where roots still hum with the old oaths. She needs to find it. I need—" Rafael gestured vaguely at the air, at the wrongness pressing against the walls of the world. "I need to know if my mother's sigil-stone fits into any of this, and I need the thing on Tanner's Row to stop eating people's gratitude, and mostly I need to sleep more than four hours a night."

*Us,* said Pip from the curtain rod. *She said us. Rats, note the pronoun.*

*Noted,* said Bracken.

"We," Rafael corrected, ears going hot. "Whatever. You know what I mean."

Sable paced the length of the table, three lengths, five, tail lashing. Rafael knew that walk. That was the walk Sable did when she was about to demand something outrageous, and Rafael braced herself for the usual request—cheese, specifically, the expensive smoked kind from the hill market, which Sable considered the only cheese worth the word.

Instead Sable stopped, turned, and said:

*I'll guide you. Me and mine. Down through the undercity, past the drowned chapel, to the root-halls where the oaths hum. We know ways down that no human has walked in a hundred years.*

Rafael blinked. "Just like that?"

*No,* said Sable. *Not just like that. Nothing is just like that, girl, least of all from a rat.*

"Name your price. Cheese. The smoked kind, I assume, and enough of it that the baker suspects arson."

*No cheese.* Sable sat back on her haunches, and for a moment she looked very old, older than a rat had any right to be, older than the silence itself. *A memory.*

The word landed strangely in the warm little room. Rafael laughed, uncertain. "A memory. Mine? Sable, most of my memories are embarrassing and the rest are poor. You'd be robbing a beggar."

*Yours,* Sable confirmed. *One memory. Chosen by me. Given freely—that part matters, freely given or it sours—and once given, it's gone. You won't know what it was. You won't miss it, exactly. You'll just find a smooth place in your mind where something used to be, like a river stone, and you'll carry it and never know why.*

The humor drained out of Rafael's mouth. "Why would you want that? You've seen my memories. You've *lived* in my memories. You were there for the porridge incident of last winter."

*Memories aren't food, Rafael. Not for us.* Sable's voice dropped low, and the other beasts went still around her, even Pip, even Duchess's monumental stare. *Memory is the oldest currency there is. Older than coin, older than kings. The old covenant itself was paid for in memories—do you think oaths hold themselves? Someone remembered the terms, generation after generation, and the remembering was the payment. That's why beasts kept the covenant alive as long as it lasted. We're rich, girl. We're made of remembering.*

She padded closer, close enough that Rafael could see the fine scars on her ears, the map of a hard small life.

*And I'm asking for one of yours because what's coming will ask for far more, and I'd rather take my fee now, honestly, than watch you pay it later to something with teeth. Consider it tuition. Consider it insurance. Consider it a rat looking out for the only human who ever talked to her like she was somebody.*

"That's a terrible sales pitch."

*I learned commerce from you.*

Rafael looked at the little circle of beasts. Bracken, stoic. Pip, preening nervously. Duchess, who had finally blinked, which among cats was roughly equivalent to a standing ovation. And Sable, waiting, patient as a debt.

One memory. One smooth stone where something used to be.

"What kind of memory?" Rafael asked quietly. "Would you take a bad one? I have a truly excellent bad one involving a wedding, a goat, and the mayor's wife."

*Chosen by me,* Sable said gently. *That's the price of the price.*

"And if I say no?"

*Then we guide you anyway,* Sable said, surprising her. *Because the thing below the city is a threat to every burrow and nest and den from here to the mountains, and beasts don't get to opt out of the world ending. But we'd guide you as strangers. And I'd regret it. Both parts.*

Rafael stood. Walked to the window. Below, Corvane was lighting its lamps one by one, ten thousand small fires against a dark that had recently started answering back. Somewhere out there, a woman with a sword-shaped walk was asking questions about her. Somewhere below, something cold was handing out miracles like a fisherman chumming water.

Her mother had left her a sigil-stone and a confession and a cellar full of secrets. Apparently the family business was paying prices.

"All right," Rafael said, turning around. "One memory. Freely given. But Sable—"

*Hmm?*

"If you take the memory of how to make my famous honey-buns, I will personally invent rat traps that offer apologies."

Sable's whiskers curved into what was unmistakably, unforgivably, a smile.

*Deal,* she said. *Now sit down, close your eyes, and try to think of something boring. Trust me, girl—*

She reached up, and placed one small dark paw against Rafael's temple, gentle as a key finding its lock.

*—you're going to want to be sitting.*

* * *

Later—how much later she couldn't have said; the lamps had burned lower and the bells had rung twice—Rafael opened her eyes to a room that felt subtly, maddeningly incomplete, like a book with one page pressed shut forever.

Sable sat on the table, watching her with an expression Rafael couldn't read and, she suspected, had been built specifically to be unreadable.

"Well?" Rafael asked. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "What did you take?"

*Can't say,* said Sable. *Saying returns it.*

"Was it important?"

*Everything's important,* Sable said. *That's rather the point.* She hopped down, and behind her the parliament stirred—Bracken flowing toward the baseboard, Pip dropping from the rod, Duchess rising with the groan of a small furry avalanche. *Rest tonight. Tomorrow, when the sun's up, meet the woman at the old shrine gate. We'll take you down through the drowned chapel, and from there to the roots.*

*Sable.* Rafael caught her before she reached the sill. *One question. Honest answer. Is the memory-payment about what you need—or about what I'll need to survive what's coming?*

For a moment the little pirate face was utterly still.

*Yes,* said Sable, and slipped out into the dark.

Rafael stood alone in her mother's shop, running her tongue over the smooth new stone in her mind, trying to guess its shape from the hollow it left behind. Outside, far off toward Tanner's Row, a child's voice rose in the night—not crying, not laughing, but reciting something, over and over, in a cadence that made every hair on her arms stand up.

And beneath her feet, faint as a heartbeat, she could have sworn the cellar floor was humming.
