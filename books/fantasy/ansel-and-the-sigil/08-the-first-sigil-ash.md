## 8. The First Sigil: Ash

The temple had burned so long ago that the city had grown around its ruin like a tree around a nail.

Ansel stood at the edge of the Ashen Quarter and looked up at what remained of the Sanctum of the Second Flame: a ribcage of blackened stone, open to the sky, its broken spire leaning like a drunk against a newer tenement that had been built flush to it sometime in the last century. Laundry lines crossed the gap where the nave had been. Someone had strung a hammock between two fallen pillars, and a cat—no, not a cat, Ansel corrected herself; a small fire-sprite wearing the shape of a cat, which was worse—was asleep in it, purring smoke.

"You're doing the face," Rafael said beside her.

"I don't have a face."

"You absolutely do. The one where you look like you're about to apologize to a building."

Ansel opened her mouth to deny it, considered the evidence of her own expression, and closed it again. Twelve years of exile had taught her many things, most of them humiliating, and one of them was that Rafael Ishikawa could read her better than any covenant-script she'd ever studied. It was inconvenient. It was also, if she was honest in the privacy of her own skull, the first thing in twelve years that had made her feel less like a ghost.

"It's just a temple," Rafael said, but her voice had gone careful, the way it did when she was being kind on purpose. "Ruined. Empty. We go in, we find the sigil, we leave before the Warden's patrols circle back. In and out. Like thieves."

"We are thieves."

"Speak for yourself. I'm a charm-seller conducting an inventory audit."

Sable emerged from the hood of Rafael's cloak, whiskers twitching, and regarded the ruin with the flat disdain only a rat can achieve. *It smells wrong,* she said. Her voice arrived in Ansel's head as a dry rustle, like paper turning. *Old burn. Old burn under old burn. And something underneath that isn't burn at all.*

"That's reassuring," Ansel said.

*Nothing I say is meant to be reassuring. I am a rat.*

They went in through what had once been the pilgrims' door—a tall arch now half-blocked by rubble, its lintel carved with flames that some pious mason had chiseled blind during the Silence, gouging out the old god's name rather than let it stand unprayed-to. Ansel ran her thumb over the gouges as she passed. The stone was smooth there, worn by years of nervous fingers. Everyone who came this way had touched the wound in the carving. Everyone had wanted, briefly and guiltily, to know what had been taken from them.

She knew what had been taken. She had taken it.

The interior opened up around them, vast and hollow. Sunlight fell through the collapsed roof in dusty columns, lighting a floor of cracked mosaic: the old covenant-wheel, its four quarters rendered in tile—ash, tide, bone, ember—with the figures of keepers kneeling at each point. Most of the faces were shattered. Someone had spent years, Ansel thought, methodically breaking every human face in the wheel while leaving the sigils untouched. Fear did that. Fear always aimed for the eyes first.

Rafael crouched by the mosaic, brushing grit from the ash-quarter with her sleeve. "This is where they swore it," she said quietly. Not a question. She'd read her mother's confession enough times to know the geography of everything Ansel had destroyed.

"One of the places," Ansel said. "There were seven sanctums. This was the second." She heard her own voice go flat and formal, the covenant-keeper's recitation voice, and hated it. "The flame here was tended by an order of—"

"Ansel."

"—an order of ash-keepers who believed that endings were holy, that every fire contained its own—"

"*Ansel.*" Rafael stood up. Grit dusted her knees. She wasn't looking at the mosaic anymore. She was looking at Ansel with that steady, level patience that was somehow far more frightening than anger. "You're lecturing. You lecture when you're hiding something. You've been doing it since the cellar."

"I am not hiding anything. I am providing context."

"You've provided context for forty minutes straight. On the walk over you explained the tax history of the Ashen Quarter. Nobody knows the tax history of the Ashen Quarter. Even the tax collectors don't know the tax history of the Ashen Quarter."

From the hood, Sable made a sound that in a larger animal would have been a snort.

Ansel looked at the mosaic instead of at Rafael. The kneeling keeper at the ash-point had lost her face, but her hands remained, tiled in pale stone, cupped together as if holding something invisible. Something that had been taken from her.

Here it was, then. The place she'd been walking toward for three days without letting herself admit it. She'd told herself the route through Corvane was about avoiding patrols. It hadn't been. There were four ways into the Quarter and she'd chosen the one that passed the Sanctum, because some cowardly part of her had decided it was better to get this over with than to have Rafael find the truth later, in some worse place, in some darker hour, when trust would cost more.

"The sigil is beneath the altar," Ansel said. "And before we go down, there's something you need to hear from me, standing up, in the light, rather than finding it written somewhere in the dark."

Rafael folded her arms. Waited. She was very good at waiting. It occurred to Ansel, not for the first time, that Rafael Ishikawa had spent twelve years being patient with a silent world, and that patience was simply what she was made of now, the way other people were made of blood.

"I broke the covenant here," Ansel said.

The words came out smaller than she'd rehearsed them. Somewhere above, the laundry lines creaked. A pigeon resettled in the broken spire.

"Not metaphorically. Not—the way everyone says it now, *the covenant broke, the gods went quiet,* like weather, like something that happened to us." She made herself keep going. "I broke it. Here. In this room, at that altar, twelve years ago this autumn. I took the four sigils apart and I spoke the unbinding, and I did it on purpose, and I would—" her voice caught, and she pushed through it, "—I would probably do it again, which is either the worst or the best thing about me, I genuinely no longer know."

Rafael didn't move. Didn't speak. The sunlight moved a fraction across the floor between them, slow as syrup.

"There was a god," Ansel said. "A real one, bound properly under the old covenant, one of the small local ones—the Kindly Drowned, they called him, a river-god, he kept the fish runs healthy and drowned maybe one child a generation, which sounds monstrous until you understand the bargain kept the whole river from ever flooding. That was the covenant. Small gods, small hungers, held tight. And then the drought year came, and the river shrank, and the Kindly Drowned got hungry, and hungry gods under a binding start to *pull.*" She looked at her hands. "He started pulling from the other end of his binding. From the town. First the well water tasted of iron. Then livestock. Then—"

She stopped. Rafael said nothing, which was its own kind of mercy.

"My sister," Ansel said. "Maren. Eleven years old. The god pulled her thin the way you'd pull thread through a needle. The healers couldn't name it. The covenant-keepers—I was one, the youngest ever sworn—we *knew*, and we debated. We sat in council for nine days debating whether the terms of the binding permitted intervention, because the terms said the god's hunger was lawful, and Maren was dying in our own hospice while we cited subclauses." A laugh came out of her, dry and awful. "So on the ninth night I came here, where the master sigils were kept, and I unbound the whole thing. All of it. Every god in every sanctum, loosed at once, because I reasoned—and I want you to appreciate the quality of this reasoning—that a god with no binding has no purchase. Unbound power dissipates. That's what the texts said. Unbound power *dissipates.*"

"And it didn't," Rafael said softly.

"No. It pooled. It went feral. The small gods didn't dissipate—they sank, down into the roots of things, down into the deep places, and they've been down there starving for twelve years. And the dragons—" Ansel's throat closed. She made herself finish. "Dragon-fire was the fuel the covenant burned. With the covenant unbound, the fire had nothing to feed, and it began to gutter. Everything you've read in your mother's confession, everything Sable's kin remember—it traces back to this room. To me. To a girl who couldn't watch her sister die and so killed the sky instead."

Silence. The pigeon cooed. Down in the hammock outside, the fire-sprite-cat dreamed, exhaling little rings of smoke.

"Maren?" Rafael asked at last. Very gently.

"Lived." Ansel wiped her face with the heel of her hand, furious at herself for the wetness there. "That's the joke. The god's hunger broke when the binding broke—he lost his grip on her within the hour. She grew up, married a cooper, has two children, and prays every week to gods I silenced, and I have never once been able to tell her why the prayers stopped echoing. I saved my sister and damned the world, and I have spent twelve years trying to work out the arithmetic of that, and the answer keeps coming back *insoluble.*"

Rafael crossed the ruined floor. Ansel braced for anger—for the slap, the curse, the turning-away. She had earned all of it.

Instead Rafael took her hands. They were charm-maker's hands, nicked and ink-stained, and they were trembling slightly, and they held on anyway.

"You broke it to save someone," Rafael said. "My mother buried it to save everyone, and it still rotted. There's no clean version of this. There's just people doing terrible arithmetic in the dark." Her mouth twisted. "Believe me. My family wrote the other half of your sin, and we had twelve comfortable years to feel good about it while you starved in exile."

"That doesn't make it—"

"No. It doesn't make it fine. You'll carry it. Good. Carry it *with* me, though, instead of alone in front of me like a wall." Rafael squeezed her hands. "You should have told me at the gate."

"I know."

"You should have told me at the cellar."

"I know."

"You will tell me the next thing immediately, even if it's stupid, *especially* if it's stupid."

Ansel huffed a laugh despite everything. "There's a chance the altar guardian is still active and will attempt to judge us both, and I may be constitutionally incapable of passing a moral examination."

"See? Stupid. Told immediately. Growth." But Rafael's grip tightened, and her voice dropped. "Thank you. For saying it in the light."

*Touching,* Sable said from the hood, dripping sarcasm like rain from eaves. *Truly. When you're finished, the floor is opening.*

* * *

The altar had split down the middle when the Sanctum burned—or when Ansel had unbound it; the two events were the same night, and she had never determined which caused which—and beneath the split lay a stair, narrow and steep, descending into air that smelled of cold hearthstone and, faintly, unbearably, of incense. Twelve years dead, and the ash-keepers' incense still haunted the dark. Ansel's eyes stung.

She lit no torch. The sigil chamber of a sanctum answered to blood-oath and intent, not flame, and besides—she remembered, with a lurch, that she had walked these stairs before, by this same darkness, guided by the same pale seep of light from below. The ash-sigil glowed. It had always glowed, even in the burning. Fire remembered its own.

"Stay behind me," she said.

"Absolutely not," said Rafael, and stepped past her onto the stairs.

The chamber at the bottom was round, domed, intact—the fire had never reached it, because the fire had been *inside* it, born of it. The walls were lined with niches, and in each niche sat an urn, and the urns held the ashes of every ash-keeper who had ever served the Sanctum, two hundred years of them, each marked with a name-plate gone green with age. At the room's center, on a plinth of fused glass—sand melted by the first lighting of the sigil, ages ago—sat a stone the size of a heart.

It was grey. It was ordinary-looking. It was the color of a hearth the morning after, of a field after the burning, of every ending that makes room for a beginning. And it pulsed, very slowly, like something breathing in its sleep.

Ansel's feet would not move.

She had known this would happen. She had confessed upstairs precisely so that this moment wouldn't own her, and here it was, owning her anyway. Because the last time she had stood on this exact flagstone, she had spoken the unbinding with Maren's name in her mouth like a prayer to no one, and the sigil had flared white, and the dome had rung like a struck bell, and every urn in every niche had rattled as if the dead were trying to sit up.

"Ansel." Rafael's voice, low. "Your hands."

Ansel looked down. Her hands were shaking. Of course they were.

*It remembers you,* Sable said, from the safety of Rafael's shoulder. The rat's voice had lost its irony. *Stones like this don't forget. Neither do the dead. Walk forward anyway. That's the whole of the craft, in my experience. Walking forward anyway.*

"Right," Ansel said aloud, to the chamber, to the urns, to the girl she had been. "Yes. I'm the one who did it. I'm the one who's sorry. And I'm the one who's going to pick you up, old friend, and carry you to a caldera, and try—at last—to put right what I put wrong. Judge me if you must. Everyone else has. You might as well join the queue."

She crossed the floor. The pulsing light steadied as she approached—not warming, exactly, but attending. She reached out, and her fingers closed on the ash-stone, and it was warm as a sleeping bird.

Nothing struck her down. No voice pronounced judgment. The urns stayed silent in their niches.

But as she lifted it free of the plinth, the sigil's glow shifted—just once—from grey to gold, and in that gold light Ansel saw, for half a heartbeat, the mosaic upstairs whole again: the covenant-wheel complete, four keepers kneeling, faces unbroken, and one of the tiled faces turned toward the stairs with an expression that was unmistakably, impossibly, *waiting.*

Then it was grey again, breathing its slow breath in her palm.

"Well?" Rafael whispered.

"It didn't refuse me." Ansel found she was smiling, and that the smile hurt. "That's either forgiveness or curiosity. With stones, it's usually curiosity."

*One,* said Sable. *Three remain. Tide, bone, ember. And I remind you both that the Warden's patrol turns past this quarter at dusk, and dusk, in case you've forgotten what the sky looks like, is approximately now.*

Above them, faint through the broken dome, came the sound of boots on rubble—more than two pairs, moving in the disciplined rhythm of a sweep-line, and threaded among the footfalls, thin and clear, the high silver note of a prayer-whistle. The Warden's instrument. The tool they used to call magic out of hiding, the way a fowler calls birds.

Rafael's eyes met Ansel's in the sigil's grey light, wide and dark.

"They're not sweeping for smugglers," she breathed. "That whistle's tuned. Someone's told them exactly what to listen for."

Ansel closed her fist around the first sigil of the new covenant and felt it pulse against her palm, patient as embers, and understood that the hunt had already begun—and that it had begun, somehow, with *her* name in someone's mouth.
