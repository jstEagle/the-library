## 9. The Warden of Prayers

The Warden came to the market on a Tuesday, which Rafael thought was deeply unfair of her. Tuesdays were slow days. Tuesdays were the days Rafael could afford to lose.

She saw the grey coat before she saw the face — that particular slate-colored wool the Wardens wore, cut close so no sleeve could hide a charm or a blade or a hand mid-sleight. The coat moved through the fishmongers' row like a needle through cloth, and the crowd parted around it without seeming to notice they were doing it, the way water parts around a keel.

Rafael's hands kept working. That was the first rule of the stall: hands keep working. She braided a luck-knot out of river-grass and did not look up, and she listened with every part of herself except the parts that were visible.

"—three answered prayers in the Saltrow district alone," the Warden was saying, to old Maren at the pie cart, of all people. Maren, who had once thrown a boot at a tax collector and missed on purpose. "The blind child on Candlemaker's Lane. The well on Tanner's Step that sweetened overnight. And last week, a widow's dead husband's coat came back warm."

"And what's wrong with any of that?" Maren asked, in the tone of a woman who would defend the concept of warm coats to the death.

"Nothing," said the Warden. "That's what worries me."

Rafael risked one glance. Up close, the Warden was younger than the coat suggested — maybe thirty, with a long, serious face and hair scraped back so tightly it looked less like a hairstyle than an apology for having hair at all. Her eyes were doing arithmetic on everything they touched. Stall by stall. Face by face.

They passed within six feet of Rafael's table. Rafael smiled the smile she used on customers who haggled past all reason — pleasant, vacant, faintly apologetic about existing — and the Warden's gaze slid over her like weather over stone.

Then Sable chose that exact moment to stick his head out of Rafael's basket and say, loudly, in the speech of rats, which is not a language human ears are built to parse but is *not silence*: "You're sweating. Why are you sweating? Is it the cheese? Did you eat my cheese?"

The Warden stopped walking.

Rafael covered the basket with a flour sack in one motion so smooth it deserved its own ballad, and said, to no one, "Rats. Terrible problem this year." Her voice came out steady. Somewhere inside, a small Rafael was screaming with professional admiration for how steady it came out.

"Mm," said the Warden.

She moved on. Rafael watched the grey coat recede down the row and did not breathe fully until it turned the corner by the chandlery.

"You're welcome," said Sable, from under the flour sack.

"You nearly got us both hanged."

"I got us both *noticed*, which is different, and also I stand by the cheese question. You owe me an answer."

* * *

That evening Rafael went looking for trouble, because trouble had stopped coming to her and she'd learned that when trouble changes its schedule, it's planning something.

She found the first sign at the shrine on Candlemaker's Lane. The blind child's mother stood at the little altar with her son's hand in hers, and she wasn't praying. She was arguing — quietly, desperately, the way you argue with someone who has already made up their mind and is only waiting for you to finish so they can say no again.

"It won't stop asking," she was saying, to the priestess, to the air, to anyone. "Every night since he could see. It asks him what he wants next. It *waits* for an answer. What kind of god waits?"

Rafael bought a candle she didn't need and lingered, the way you do, and heard the rest: the child described the voice as patient. Kind, even. But he'd started sleeping with his new eyes covered, because when he looked at the dark, the dark looked back with something in it that counted.

The second sign was chalked on the door of her own shop when she got home. Not a mark she recognized — a small neat spiral, precise as a signature — and beneath it, in a clerk's tidy hand:

*Mrs. Ishikawa. A matter of civic record-keeping. Nothing more. Please attend the Warden's office at your convenience.*

At your convenience. As if convenience were a thing that existed for people whose names ended up in Warden ledgers.

Sable read it over her shoulder from atop the doorframe, upside down, which he insisted was more comfortable. "That's a summons," he said. "I've seen them before. In the old days, before your time, a spiral on the door meant—"

"I know what it means."

"You don't, actually, because you were a child, and children think spirals are just spirals. Whereas I am old enough to remember when—" He paused. Rats pause dramatically; it's one of their few indulgences. "When they took people away for exactly this."

Rafael stood in the street a long moment, holding the note as if it might bite. Around her, Corvane settled into its evening noises — shutters, supper-smoke, someone's terrible fiddle two lanes over. An ordinary city pretending very hard.

"They're tracing it backward," she said. "From the prayers. The blind boy, the well, the coat. Someone's drawing lines between them, and the lines all point somewhere."

"Where do they point?"

Rafael looked at her own front door, with its little bell and its peeling paint and the luck-knots she hung there every season for show, harmless, decorative, *legal*.

"Here," she said. "Eventually. Everything points here."

* * *

She didn't go to the Warden's office at her convenience. She went at dawn, uninvited, which Sable called "strategically insane" and Rafael called "better to be the question than the answer."

The Warden's office occupied the ground floor of the Assay House, where the city weighed imported goods for hidden charms. Fitting. Inside smelled of paper, ink, and cold iron — the iron was everywhere, in the door hinges, the lamp stands, a long rod mounted above the clerk's desk like a sword of office. Iron didn't hurt Rafael. It just made her gift feel muffled, like hearing a song through a wall.

The Warden was at her desk, eating breakfast — bread and hard cheese, no ceremony — and she waved Rafael into the chair across from her without surprise.

"Mrs. Ishikawa."

"Rafael. Just Rafael. The shop isn't married either."

Something flickered at the corner of the Warden's mouth. It might have been the ancestor of a smile, three generations removed. "Warden Sereth Vane. You'll have seen my note."

"I came early. Convenience and I aren't speaking."

Vane set down her bread. She studied Rafael the way Rafael studied knots — looking for the loose end. "Do you know what I do all day, Rafael-just-Rafael?"

"Paperwork, I'd guess. There's a great deal of paperwork in evidence."

"I listen." Vane tapped her ear. "Twelve years ago, when the gods went quiet, the city stopped needing witch-hunters and started needing listeners. Because magic didn't die, whatever the priests say. It just went underground and got quiet too. So we listen. For murmurs. For words spoken to animals. For prayers that get *answered*." She leaned back. "Three answered prayers in one month, in a city that hasn't heard a real answer in twelve years. That's not a blessing, Mrs.—Rafael. That's a leak. And leaks have sources."

"And you think I'm the source."

"I think you're *near* the source. Which is worse, for you." Vane pulled a ledger toward her and opened it, and Rafael's stomach dropped, because there on the page, in columns, was her life laid out in other people's testimony: *the charm-seller on Bellows Street, talks to her basket; the fisherman Aldous Pell, bought a luck-knot the day his gull turned better; heard whispering at the Candlemaker shrine, woman, dark coat.* "You sell charms. Harmless ones, everyone says. River-grass and ribbon. And yet everywhere the answers appear, there's a woman with a basket nearby, and the animals near that woman behave like — well. Like they're being *consulted*."

Rafael folded her hands so they wouldn't shake. "If I could make wells sweet and blind boys see, Warden, I wouldn't be selling luck-knots for two coppers."

"No," Vane agreed. "You'd be dead, most likely. Or rich. Or both, briefly." She closed the ledger, but her hand stayed flat on it, keeping it shut the way you'd keep a lid on something. "Here's what I actually want. Not you. I want the thing answering the prayers. Because I've been listening for twelve years, and I know what a blessing sounds like, and these aren't blessings. These are *bait*. Something out there is feeding this city small miracles the way a fisherman feeds a spot before setting hooks. And my duty — my actual duty, the one I swore — is to Corvane, not to the law. The law says report every whisperer. My conscience says the whisperer might be the only person in this city who can hear the hook coming."

Rafael stared at her. "You're telling me you don't believe in the law you enforce."

"I believe in it the way I believe in a boat. Useful, until the sea disagrees with it." Vane's eyes were tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "I caught something last night. An animal. A rat, if you can believe it, sitting on a shrine roof at midnight, *listening to the prayer-wells*. Animals don't do that. Animals flee the places where the answers come from. This one was taking notes."

The floor tilted gently under Rafael's chair.

"Grey fur," Vane continued, "left ear nicked, extremely rude demeanor according to my sergeant, who attempted to apprehend it and was bitten."

"That tracks," Rafael said faintly.

"He's in a cage in the back room. Iron bars." Vane watched Rafael's face with the patience of a woman who had already won. "He's been chattering nonstop since dawn. My sergeant says it's nonsense noise. But I've spent twelve years learning to hear patterns in noise, and that rat is repeating one phrase over and over, and the phrase is a name."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere upstairs, the Assay House scales clinked.

"*Bellows Street,*" said Vane. "Over and over. Your street, your shop. He's leading us to you, whether he means to or not. Or—" and here she tilted her head, sharp as a kestrel, "—he's leading us to you *on purpose*, because he knows I'll listen, and he wants me to find the one person in Corvane who might understand what's happening before the city does something stupid and grateful and worships itself straight into a trap."

Rafael stood up. Her legs held, which she considered a personal triumph. "Show him to me."

"In exchange for what?"

"The truth," Rafael said. "All of it. About what's answering the prayers. Even the parts that will get me hanged. That's my price, Warden. The truth, and then you decide what your conscience does with it."

For a long moment Vane looked at her — really looked, past the charm-seller, past the vacant market smile, down to whatever it was she'd been hunting all along. Then she took an iron key from her desk drawer, and Rafael noticed her hand wasn't quite steady either.

"Fair warning," Vane said, standing. "Your rat has opinions about you. Loud ones. My sergeant wrote some of them down."

"Oh no."

"Oh yes." Vane unlocked the back room, and the chatter inside stopped mid-word, the way it does when someone recognizes footsteps. "He's been demanding cheese. Specifically *your* cheese. He claims you owe him."

"I don't owe him cheese. I owe him an *answer* about the cheese, which is different, and the answer is no."

The cage in the back room was small and iron-barred, and Sable sat in the middle of it with his tail wrapped around his feet like a disapproving teapot, and when he saw Rafael his whiskers went rigid with relief he would deny to his dying day.

"Took you long enough," he said. "Do you have any idea what iron does to the nose? It's like smelling through a sock. Also, the big one bites. Ask me how I know."

"Sable," Rafael said softly, "what did you tell them?"

"Nothing. Everything. Depends who's asking." His black eyes flicked to Vane, then back, and when he spoke again his voice had gone low and quick, the voice he used for things that mattered. "Rafael. Listen. I wasn't spying on the shrines for fun. The prayer-wells — the places where the answers pool — they're getting *deeper*. Whatever's answering isn't scattered anymore. It's gathering. Pulling in. And last night I heard it say something, clear as church bells, and I've been trying to lead these iron-coats to you ever since because you're the only one who'll believe me."

Rafael knelt by the cage, ignoring the iron's dull pressure against her gift. "Say it."

Sable's whiskers trembled.

"It said: '*Bring me the daughter of the bargain, before the fire goes out.*'"

The words landed in the room like a dropped lantern. Vane looked between them — the woman and the rat — and Rafael watched twelve years of careful listening rearrange themselves behind the Warden's eyes into a single, terrible conclusion.

"Daughter of the bargain," Vane repeated slowly. "Whose bargain?"

And Sable, who had survived twelve years in the walls of Corvane by knowing exactly which silences to keep, broke the biggest one he had.

"Hers," he said. "Her mother's. The one that silenced the gods."
