## 10. Fire That Remembers

The mountain had a name once. Ansel had learned it as a girl, kneeling in a covenant-school with chalk dust on her knuckles: *Vael-Korrath*, the Cradle of Breath, where the first fire was sworn and the first oath was lit. Now the maps called it nothing at all. The cartographers had stopped drawing it the way people stop speaking of a relative who disgraced himself — politely, thoroughly, as if it had never existed.

Ansel climbed it anyway.

Three days out of Corvane, following a thread of warmth only she could feel — the covenant-keeper's sense, the one gift they hadn't managed to burn out of her along with everything else — the trail had grown stranger. Warmth in winter. Snow that slid off the rocks before it could settle. A smell like a hearth left burning in an empty house, which was either hope or the worst kind of warning, and Ansel had long ago stopped being able to tell those apart.

Behind her, Rafael picked her way up the scree with the grim competence of someone who had spent twelve years hauling charm-crates up stairs for a living, and Sable rode in the hood of Rafael's coat like a small furry admiral surveying his fleet.

"You know," Sable said, "in my professional opinion, mountains are overrated."

"You're a rat," Ansel said. "Your professional opinion is about cheese."

"My professional opinion is *broadly applicable*." He adjusted his position in the hood. "Also, I'd like it noted that when we all freeze to death on this rock, I want it recorded that I objected. Verbally. Several times."

"Noted," Rafael said, breath fogging. "For the historical record."

"Thank you. History has been unkind to rats. We were there for every important moment and credited for none of them."

Ansel almost smiled. It was an odd sensation, smiling while climbing toward what she was fairly sure was the end of the world, but the last week had been full of odd sensations. Walking beside Rafael Ishikawa was one of them. The woman had spent their entire acquaintance insisting she was nobody — a charm-seller, a coward, a keeper of small safe secrets — and then kept doing things that no coward would do. She had opened her mother's cellar. She had paid a memory to a rat without flinching. She had looked at the sigil of ash in Ansel's palm and said, *we'll need the rest*, as if saving the world were a market errand she'd simply added to her list, between lamp oil and thread.

Ansel had broken a covenant between realms. She knew what that cost. She could not fathom how Rafael made it look like carrying groceries.

"The warmth's stronger here," Rafael said suddenly. She crouched, pressed her bare palm flat against a slab of black stone, and frowned. "It's not heat, exactly. It's more like—" She searched for the word. "Like standing near someone who's talking in their sleep."

Ansel stopped walking.

That was exactly what it was. That was *precisely* what it was, and the fact that Rafael — a beast-speaker who had never sworn a covenant, never trained, never touched the old rites — could feel it meant the dragon's sleep-talk had gotten loud enough for anyone with even a whisper of the gift to hear.

Which meant Emberling was dying faster than Ansel had dared to fear.

"We should hurry," she said, and hated how thin her voice came out.

* * *

They reached the caldera rim near dusk, and Ansel made them all stop and be quiet, because some things deserved silence and this was one of them.

The crater below was vast — a bowl of dark stone two miles across, its walls streaked with ancient channels where fire had once run like rivers. And at the bottom, coiled around a spire of fused glass that might have been a mountain's heart or a throne or simply a place to rest, lay the last dragon.

Emberling was smaller than Ansel remembered. That was the first cruelty. In the old days — in the illustrations in the school-books, in the one time she had seen a dragon fly overhead as a child, a shadow across the sun that had made an entire city kneel in the streets — dragons had been *vast*. This one was perhaps the length of three wagons, her scales gone dull as old iron, her wings folded tight against her sides like a bird conserving warmth. The light beneath those scales was the worst part. It pulsed. Faintly. Irregularly. Like embers under ash, like a heartbeat that kept forgetting itself and having to be reminded.

"Oh," Rafael whispered. Her hand found Sable in her hood, or maybe Sable's paw found her hand; from outside the coat it was impossible to say. "Oh, she's *alone*."

"She's been alone for twelve years," Ansel said. "She's been alone since—"

"Since you broke the covenant." Rafael said it gently, without accusation, which somehow made it worse. "I know. You told me. On the road. Twice."

"Three times," said Sable. "You told it three times. The middle version was the most flattering, if you're taking requests."

"I'm not," Ansel said.

"Shame. In the middle version you also rescued an orphaned fox."

Ansel descended into the caldera before she could answer, because there was only so much gentle truth a person could absorb on an empty stomach, and because the dragon had lifted her head.

Emberling's eyes opened — slow, like doors in a house long shut — and they were the color of the sigil Ansel carried. Ash-grey ringed with dying gold. The great head swung toward them with a ponderous, heartbreaking care, and a voice filled the crater. Not spoken. *Felt*, the way thunder is felt in the ribs, the way an organ note is felt in the floorboards.

*A keeper.* The voice was dry as paper, warm as a banked stove, tired beyond any weariness Ansel had words for. *I smell the ash-sigil on you. I smell the breaking on you too. They always travel together, don't they. The mending and the wound.*

"I'm sorry," Ansel said. It was not adequate. Nothing she had ever said to a dragon had been adequate, and she suspected that was rather the point of dragons.

*Sorry.* A plume of smoke curled from Emberling's nostrils, grey and thin, and dissipated. *I have had twelve years of sorry. The mountain is full of it. It settles like snow.*

Rafael stepped forward, and Ansel watched something remarkable happen: the dragon's head turned from her — from the keeper, from the guilt, from the whole weight of the broken covenant — and fixed instead on Rafael, with an expression that on a human face would have been recognition, and on a dragon's face was something older than that.

*And you. Little speaker. You smell of beasts and cellars and a mother's bargain.* A rumble moved through the great body — it took Ansel a moment to identify it as a laugh, and another moment to realize how sad the laugh was. *You smell of the debt coming due.*

Rafael went very still. "What debt?"

*Do not play innocent with me, child. Your mother did not bury her secrets deep enough. Nothing buries deep enough for fire. Fire remembers.* The dragon's eyes half-closed. *Fire remembers everything. That is what it is *for*.*

Ansel's stomach dropped through the stone of the caldera. "Emberling. What do you mean, fire remembers? What does it remember?"

But the dragon had begun the thing Ansel had come three hundred miles dreading, the thing she had seen once before at the death of another dragon, in another life: the telling. Dragons did not die quietly. They died *out loud*, pouring their hoarded memory into the air around them, because a dragon's memory was not contained in flesh — it was fuel, it was the very substance of their fire, and when the flesh failed the memories escaped in wisps and fragments, visible as heat-shimmer, audible as murmurs.

The air above Emberling began to shimmer. And in the shimmer, images.

A city burning — no, wait, Ansel realized, not burning. *Lit.* A city being lit, street by street, window by window, each flame kindled by a passing shape of wings and gold, and in every window a family cheering, because in the old days dragon-fire lighting your hearth was a blessing, was the covenant made visible, was the gods' own warmth shared out among mortals—

A hall of roots and stone, the undercity beneath Corvane, alive with voices — human voices, beast voices, voices that were neither — all swearing together, all *one* voice, the covenant fresh and blazing—

A woman with Rafael's jaw and none of Rafael's warmth, standing in a cellar, holding a stone, whispering terms to something in the dark—

And then, abruptly, a memory Ansel knew. Hers. Twelve years ago. The god with too many mouths. Her own hands tearing the sigil-stone free. The silence that fell afterward like snow that never stopped falling.

The shimmer flickered and showed it back to her — her own worst moment, reflected in a dying dragon's escaping fire — and Ansel found she could not look away. Perhaps that was right. Perhaps that was penance, watching it again.

*Stop,* Emberling said, and the images guttered out. *Forgive me. When the fire runs low, it spills. I cannot always choose what leaks.* Her great eye rolled toward Ansel, and there was — Ansel would swear this until her own dying day — gentleness in it. *You carry it heavily, little breaker. Heavier than most. Most breakers tell themselves stories. You refuse to.*

"It doesn't help," Ansel said. "Telling myself stories. I tried. I'm bad at it."

*Mm.* The dragon settled her chin onto her foreclaws, and the pulse beneath her scales stuttered, caught, resumed. Slower now. *Then let me tell you a true thing instead, since you seem to collect them. Do you know why the covenant needs dragon-fire? Not symbolically. Actually. Why?*

Ansel opened her mouth. Closed it. She had been taught the rites, the words, the order of swearing — but the *why* had been considered advanced study, reserved for keepers of the third rank, and she had shattered the covenant before reaching it.

"Because fire remembers," Rafael said quietly. She was staring at the shimmering air where the images had been. "You said it. Fire remembers everything. So the covenant isn't just an agreement. It's a *memory*. The realms swore to each other, and the fire holds the memory of the swearing, and as long as the fire burns, neither side can forget what they owe." She looked up at Ansel, and her eyes were wide and horrified and brilliant. "That's why the gods went silent. It's not that they left. It's that the *remembering* stopped. Hollow-Voice answers prayers because it's the only thing still listening — it's wearing the shape of godhood because we've all forgotten what real ones sounded like."

Sable poked his head out of the hood. "I did try to tell you beasts remember what gods were before worship. Nobody listens to rats. This is a chronic condition of the large mammals."

"Emberling," Ansel said, and her voice cracked down the middle. "How long? How long does your fire last?"

The dragon was quiet for a long moment. Wind moved through the caldera, stirring ash that had been ash for twelve years.

*I have been rationing,* she said at last. *Sleeping more. Burning less. A flame can be starved slowly, if it is disciplined. But discipline ends, little keeper. Even mine.* Her eye fixed on Ansel. *When the last of my fire goes out, the covenant cannot be reforged. Not by four sigils. Not by forty. There will be nothing left to hold the memory. The door between realms will not lock — it will simply cease to have ever been a door.*

"How long?" Ansel asked again, and made herself stand still for the answer.

*One moon.* The words fell into the crater like stones into a well. *Perhaps less. My fire gutters fastest when I dream, and lately I dream of the old days, and dreaming costs me. I am spending my last warmth remembering, because forgetting would be easier and I refuse. Let that be said of me at the end: the last dragon refused to forget.*

Silence. Even Sable had nothing to say, which Ansel suspected was unprecedented in the history of rodents.

Then Rafael stepped forward, right up to the dragon's enormous lowered head, close enough that Emberling's breath stirred her hair, and did the thing that Ansel was beginning to understand Rafael Ishikawa always did, the thing that made no strategic sense whatsoever and was somehow the only correct move:

She put her hand flat against the dragon's snout.

"You're not spending your last warmth alone," Rafael said. "We've got two sigils. We'll get the other two. Tide and bone and ember — and ember's yours, isn't it? The fourth sigil. It was always yours." Her voice wobbled and steadied. "So you hold on. You ration and you dream and you spend what you must, but you *hold on*, because we are going to come back down into this crater with all four, and you are going to blaze one more time. One more. That's all I'm asking."

Emberling regarded her for a long, long moment. Then the great eye closed, and when it opened again there was — faint, guttering, but unmistakably present — a spark of gold in it that had not been there before.

*Nobody has asked anything of me in twelve years,* the dragon said. *They only ask about me. There is a difference. I had forgotten the difference.* The pulse beneath her scales steadied, just slightly. Just for a moment. *Very well, little speaker. One more blaze. Hold me to it.*

They climbed back up the caldera wall in the blue dark, and camped just below the rim, because none of them could bear to leave entirely. Ansel sat with her back against a warm stone — the whole mountain was warm now, faintly, like a body fighting fever — and watched Rafael feed crumbs to a rat and argue with him about whether courage counted if you complained the entire time.

"It counts," Rafael insisted.

"It counts *less*," Sable said. "Courage with commentary is a different virtue. Scholars agree."

"What scholars?"

"Me. I'm the scholar."

Ansel hugged her knees and listened to them and let herself feel, for exactly one minute, something dangerously close to hope. Two sigils. A dragon holding on. A beast-speaker who bargained with the world like it owed her money and somehow kept winning.

Then she looked north, toward the coast, where the tide-sigil waited in whatever drowned shrine held it — and south, toward Corvane, where the Warden was surely pulling threads by now, tracing answered prayers back through the market, back toward a charm-shop, back toward them.

One moon. Maybe less.

"Rafael," she said. "About the tide-sigil. There's something you need to know before we go. About what it costs."

Rafael looked up, crumbs forgotten, and in the firelight her face was open and unafraid, and Ansel thought: *she has no idea. She thinks the price will be coin, or comfort, or a few days of danger. She has already paid once and thinks she knows the size of these things.*

Ansel had stood in front of a god and torn a covenant apart. She knew the size of these things.

"The sea keeps accounts," she said, "the way fire keeps memories. Exactly. And forever."

Below them, faint as a sleeping heartbeat, the last dragon's fire pulsed in the dark — and pulsed again — and held.
