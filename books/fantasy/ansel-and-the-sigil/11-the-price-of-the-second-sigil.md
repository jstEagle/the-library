## 11. The Price of the Second Sigil

The sea did not knock. It simply arrived.

One moment Rafael and Ansel were picking their way down the cliff path below Corvane's western wall, Sable riding in the crook of her elbow like a smug gray scarf, and the next the path ended in a beach that had no business existing—a crescent of black sand tucked beneath the cliffs, lit by a moon that had already set behind them. The water at its edge was not moving. It stood in a long glassy wall, tall as three men, and it waited.

"Well," said Ansel. "That's new."

"It's been waiting," Rafael said. She could feel it in her teeth, the way she felt weather coming and the moods of animals: patience, vast and old and slightly offended. "It's been waiting a long time for someone to come."

Sable's whiskers twitched against her wrist. *Tide-sigil,* the rat said. *Smells like my grandmother's attic. Old and damp and full of things nobody wanted.*

"Your grandmother had an attic?"

*Everyone has an attic, Rafael. Some of us are just honest about what we keep in ours.*

Ansel had gone still in the way she went still before anything dangerous—which, Rafael was learning, was most things. Her hand rested on the strap of the satchel where the sigil of ash slept wrapped in oilcloth, warm as a hearthstone even through the leather. Two sigils now. Ash and—soon, if the sea allowed—tide. Half the covenant. Halfway to reforging the pact that kept the hungry god-thing called Hollow-Voice from wearing every answered prayer in Corvane like a stolen coat.

Halfway, and the price list was getting longer.

"The ash sigil took blood," Rafael said quietly. "What does the tide take?"

Ansel didn't answer right away. She was looking at the standing water with the expression of a woman reading a bill she'd hoped would be smaller. "Covenant magic runs on exchange," she said finally. "Fire takes fuel. Bone takes acknowledgment—you have to look at death and say yes, I see you. And tide..." She exhaled. "Tide takes memory. A true one. Freely given, fully surrendered. You don't get it back."

Rafael laughed. It came out wrong, thin as thread. "That's convenient. I've got plenty of memories. Twelve years of hiding, mostly. Take your pick."

"Rafael." Ansel turned to face her, and the dead moonlight caught the scar along her jaw—the one Rafael still hadn't asked about, because some stories you let people tell in their own time. "It doesn't take your pick. It takes what matters most. That's how it knows the payment is true. You can't lie to the tide. Ask any fisherman."

"That's grim."

"That's the sea."

*She's right,* Sable offered helpfully. *I once watched a crab try to negotiate with a wave. The wave won.*

"Thank you, Sable."

*I'm here all week.*

Rafael walked toward the water. The sand was cold through her boots, and each step felt like walking into a room where someone had just stopped talking about you. Up close, the standing wave wasn't water at all, exactly—it was water the way a portrait is a person. Somewhere inside it, deep in the green-dark, something glinted. A shape like a coin. Like a stone worn smooth by centuries of salt.

The second sigil. Suspended in the tide like a pearl in a closed fist.

"How do I—" she began.

"You know how," Ansel said behind her. Her voice had gone rough. "You've known since the cellar. Since your mother's confession. Every step of this has been you paying for a world that put a price on your head, and I have hated watching you pay it, and I will hate watching you pay this, and I am going to stand right here while you do it anyway, because you asked me to stop making your choices for you."

Rafael looked back at her. Ansel Ishikawa-no-longer, disgraced keeper, oath-breaker, the woman who had shattered heaven to save one city and broken it instead—stood on the black sand with her fists clenched like a girl at her own trial.

"When did I ask you that?" Rafael asked.

"The night we found the ash sigil. You said, 'Stop apologizing and start trusting me.' I've been trying. It's very difficult. I'm told practice helps."

Despite everything—the cold, the waiting water, the coin-shaped dread sitting in her chest—Rafael smiled. "You're terrible at it."

"I'm aware."

*Touching,* said Sable. *Truly. Now would someone please negotiate with the ocean before I grow gills?*

* * *

Here is what Rafael knew about her mother, Mara Ishikawa, who had died on an ordinary Tuesday with flour on her hands and a half-finished luck-knot on the table:

She had made the bargain. She had silenced the gods. She had traded the old covenant's voice for time—for years bought off some hunger so vast that even the confession, written in careful shopkeeper's script on the back of a charm inventory, could barely hold its shape. *They were eating us,* the letter said. *Prayer by prayer. So I gave them silence instead, and silence is only a slower meal.* And then she had come upstairs, Rafael supposed, and rolled dough, and taught her daughter which knots meant luck and which meant love and which meant goodbye, and never once said a word.

Twelve years Rafael had spent being angry at gods, at Wardens, at a city that outlawed her gift. It had taken a letter in a cellar to teach her that the first liar in her life had loved her enough to lie well.

And here was the thing she had not told Ansel. Could not, yet. The thing she carried up the cliff path and into ruins and out again like a stone in her boot:

Her mother's face was already fading.

Not magically. Ordinarily. Grief worked like that—cruel in its smallness. Rafael could remember the flour on her hands, the exact pitch of her humming, the way she said *Rafi* with the second syllable always half-swallowed. But when she reached for the face itself, the whole of it, the living map of it—her mother's eyes, the crease beside her mouth when she laughed—she found fog. She found a sketch where there should have been a portrait. She had been losing her mother by inches for twelve years, and no one had warned her, because no one ever warns you about the ordinary thefts.

So when the tide rose up before her and asked—not in words, but in pressure, in pull, the way the moon pulls—*a true memory, freely given, what matters most*—

Rafael understood, with terrible clarity, what it would choose.

*Don't,* said Sable, very quietly, from her shoulder. No joke now. The rat's claws pricked gently through her sleeve. *Rafael. There are other memories. There are so many. Give it the fish market. Give it the smell of the tannery, honestly, no one needs that one—*

"It has to be true," Rafael said. "It has to matter most. That's the whole point. If I pay it something cheap, the sigil stays a trinket, and a trinket doesn't reforge a covenant, and Hollow-Voice keeps answering prayers until there's nothing left of this city but answered people." She swallowed. "I've spent twelve years selling charms that do nothing because doing something was illegal. This one does something. This one costs what it says it costs. Maybe that's what real magic is. Maybe that's why they outlawed it."

*Rafi—*

"Hold my arm," she said. "If I fall forward, don't let me drown. I know you're small. Improvise."

* * *

She stepped into the water.

It did not swallow her. It received her. The standing wave parted around her like a held breath, and she walked down into green twilight where the light came from everywhere and nowhere, and the sigil hung before her—a disc of pale stone veined with silver, turning slowly, patient as erosion.

She reached for it. It did not resist. It simply waited for the rest of the transaction.

*A true memory,* said the tide, in the voice water makes against a hull, against a shore, against the inside of a shell held to your ear as a child. *Freely given. Fully surrendered.*

"I know," Rafael said aloud, and her voice came out in bubbles that rose like tiny moons. "Take it."

The tide reached into her.

It was not violent. That was the worst part. It was gentle, the way her mother had been gentle peeling a splinter from her palm when she was six—*look away, Rafi, count the knots on the door*—and Rafael felt the tide find the place where her mother lived in her, the whole country of her, humming and flour-dusted and safe, and it lifted that country out of her the way you lift a sleeping child, carefully, completely, without waking anyone.

She tried to hold on. Of course she tried. She grabbed for the flour and the humming and the word *Rafi*, and she kept those—she would learn later, gasping on the sand, that she kept those, the edges, the sound, the love itself—but the center, the face, the map of her mother's living eyes looking back at her across a kitchen table saying *you are not wrong to be what you are, little beast-speaker, you are only early*—

Gone.

Not darkened. Not blurred. *Absent*, the way a tooth is absent. Her mind kept reaching for it and finding smooth socket, healed over, as though there had never been anything there at all. She could remember that her mother had a face. Everyone's mother has a face. She simply could not remember this one. She could remember loving her without being able to picture who she had loved.

The sigil pressed itself into her palm, cool and heavy and final, and the tide set her down on the black sand like something it was finished with.

* * *

She came back to herself in pieces. Sand under her cheek. Ansel's coat around her shoulders, smelling of woodsmoke and old leather. Sable curled against her collarbone, trembling slightly, whiskers wet—whether from spray or something else, Rafael chose not to examine.

"You paid it," Ansel said. Not a question.

"I paid it."

"What did it—"

"Don't." Rafael sat up too fast and the world tilted politely. "Please. Don't make me say it while it's still bleeding. Ask me in a year. Ask me if I can name what I lost. Right now I can't, and that's the cruelest part—I keep reaching for it and there's nothing to reach for, and I know there used to be something, and knowing that is worse than grief, because at least grief knows what it's crying about."

Ansel was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "My mother used to sing while she cooked. Badly. Off-key with total confidence. I broke the covenant twelve years ago and I can still hear her doing it, and some nights that's the only reason I get up." Her voice cracked on the last words. "I'm not telling you that to compare losses. I'm telling you because you shouldn't be alone inside this, and I don't know any other way in than through mine."

Rafael looked at her—at this woman the city had exiled, whom the covenant had broken, who had confessed her guilt in a burned temple and then picked up the pieces anyway—and did the bravest thing she'd done all day, braver than walking into the sea.

She leaned her head against Ansel's shoulder and let herself be held.

For a while, none of them spoke. The standing wave had lowered; ordinary surf now ran up the black sand and retreated, running like it had somewhere to be. In Rafael's satchel, two sigils lay together—ash and tide—and she could feel them faintly through the canvas, warm and cool, like two hands almost touching.

*For what it's worth,* Sable said at last, softly, *the rat remembers her. Your mother. She fed me cheese off the good plate once and told me if I ever told anyone she'd deny it to her grave. She had a laugh like a door hinge that learned to sing. I'll keep that for you. Rats are excellent at keeping things. It's most of what we do.*

Rafael laughed—a wet, broken, genuine laugh—and scratched the small gray head. "Thank you, Sable."

*Don't mention it. Literally. Ever. I have a reputation.*

* * *

They climbed the cliff path as the false dawn came up gray over Corvane's walls. Rafael's legs shook. Her head ached in a way that wasn't pain exactly—more like a room in her house she kept walking past and couldn't enter, couldn't remember why it mattered, only that the door was locked and her palms remembered knocking.

Two sigils. Half the covenant. And somewhere ahead, a third waited in bone, and a fourth in ember, and Emberling's fire guttered in its caldera like a candle in a doorway, and Hollow-Voice moved through Corvane wearing the voices of the desperate.

At the top of the path, Ansel stopped.

"Rafael. Before the catacombs—before the bone sigil—there's something you need to hear. All of it. What I did, twelve years ago. Why the covenant broke. Why your mother's bargain was even possible." She was staring at the city walls, not at Rafael, and her hands were shaking. "Because the first dead keeper judged me worthy down there, and I need someone who isn't dead to decide if I am. And because—" her breath caught— "because if you're going to keep paying prices for this, you deserve to know exactly whose debt you're paying."

The wind came off the sea, carrying salt and something older underneath it, something that sounded almost like a voice learning to pray.

Rafael tightened her grip on the satchel, on the two sigils, on the empty room in her own memory, and said, "Tell me everything."
