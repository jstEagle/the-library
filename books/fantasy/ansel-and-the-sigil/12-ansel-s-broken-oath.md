## 12. Ansel's Broken Oath

Twelve years before Corvane had walls worth the name, there was a city called Vessany that smelled of salt and bread and, on festival days, of crushed mint scattered over cobblestones. Ansel had been twenty-three years old, newly sworn to the covenant, and so certain of everything that she would later spend a decade learning how wrong certainty could be.

She remembered it in pieces. That was the trouble with guilt: it did not keep its memories whole. It broke them into shards and handed them back one at a time, usually at night, usually when she'd finally found somewhere warm to sleep.

The first shard was always the same. The mint on the stones. Her boots slipping slightly on it as she ran toward the temple steps, and her mentor Halvard shouting behind her — *Ansel, wait, you don't understand what you're* — and her not waiting, because she was twenty-three and waiting was for people who hadn't been chosen.

* * *

Vessany had no wall then. It didn't need one; it had the covenant. Every city on the coast had a keeper in those days, a man or woman sworn between realms, someone who could stand at the threshold where the gods still listened and speak on behalf of the small, warm, mortal world. Ansel had been chosen at nineteen, which was young, and told repeatedly that she was young, which she had taken as flattery.

Her gift was listening. Not beast-speaking — that was rarer, and stranger, and belonged mostly to families like the Ishikawas, though she wouldn't learn that for years yet. Ansel's gift was hearing the covenant itself: the great slow hum of the oath that held the realms apart but conversing, god and mortal speaking through a veil so that neither burned the other. When she closed her eyes she could hear it the way sailors hear weather coming. A vast chord, patient and old.

On the morning of the last day of Vessany, the chord had gone wrong.

Not silent. She would have given anything, later, for silence. No — it had gone *hungry*. One voice inside the harmony had begun to swell, the way a note swells when a singer leans into it, except this note leaned and leaned and never stopped, and underneath it Ansel heard something that made her stomach drop: the sound of an appetite opening.

"It's one of them," Halvard said. He was seventy, half-deaf in one ear, and he had never once in four years raised his voice at her. He raised it now. "One of the old ones has turned its face toward us. We can hold the veil. We've held worse."

"Have we?" Ansel asked. Because she could hear what he couldn't, or perhaps he could hear it too and was lying to keep her steady, and either way the swelling note was already inside the harbor. Fishermen were kneeling on their decks, praying — actual prayers, the kind that went somewhere — and the thing behind the veil was answering each one by tasting it.

A god had come to Vessany the way a tide comes to a tidepool. Slowly. Curiously. With every intention of drinking it dry.

* * *

Here is what they taught covenant-keepers, and here is what Ansel did instead.

They taught: the veil holds because both sides consent. The gods agreed, long ago, to speak and not touch; mortals agreed to worship and not demand. The covenant was not a wall. It was a conversation with rules, and rules were sacred, and no single keeper had the right to end a conversation older than language.

Ansel stood on the temple steps with the hungry note filling the sky like a storm front made of attention, and she looked down at the harbor, at the little boats, at a fisherman's daughter she knew by name — Petra, eight years old, who sold mussels and cheated shamelessly at dice — and she thought: *the rules are going to eat her.*

So she did the only thing a person does when the law is slow and the danger is fast. She broke it.

There was a word. There is always a word; that's the cruelty of covenants, that unmaking is always simpler than making. The sigil-stone of Vessany sat in its cradle at the temple's heart, humming with the local thread of the great chord, and Ansel put her hands on it and spoke the severance — the word keepers learned first and were meant to die before using — and she tore Vessany's thread out of the covenant entirely.

She expected the god to be locked out. That was the theory. Cut the thread, close the door, save the city.

What actually happened was that the chord collapsed.

Every thread, everywhere. Because the covenant was not a wall, it was a web, and she had not cut one strand — she had cut the strand that tensioned all the others. She felt it go, felt the great patient harmony unravel outward from her hands like a knitted sweater catching on a nail, thread after thread after thread going slack and dark across the whole wide world, and the last thing she heard before the silence fell was the god behind the veil laughing — not cruelly, which would have been bearable, but *delighted*, the way a child is delighted when a toy breaks in an interesting way.

Then: nothing. The first true silence in ten thousand years.

And Vessany lived. That was the joke of it. That was the filthy, unbearable joke. The god, deprived of the veil, of the channel, of the conversation it had been savoring, lost interest — the way a drinker loses interest in a cup when the well goes dry. It drifted off to starve slowly in the space between realms, and Petra grew up, and Vessany grew walls, and everyone said the plague of unanswered prayers had passed, and Ansel stood in the temple holding a dead stone and understood exactly two things:

She had saved them.

She had killed everything else.

* * *

The dragons died slowly, which was somehow worse than if they'd died quickly.

Nobody explained it to her at first; the other keepers stopped speaking to her at all, which she deserved, and so she had to work it out herself from the old texts, sitting in a borrowed room in a city that wasn't hers, reading by tallow light with her hands shaking.

Dragon-fire fed the covenant. Not metaphorically — literally, chemically, mystically, whatever word you liked; the flame of the elder wyrms was the fuel the oaths burned. A covenant is a promise kept alive, and promises are fire, and fire needs feeding. With the web severed there was nothing left to feed, and the dragons — who had banked their flames low for centuries, trusting the covenant to carry the warmth — began, one by one, to gutter.

She read the reports. Greywing of the Northern Reaches, flame gone blue, then clear, then out. The river-dragon Sarn, who had once let children slide down her tail at midsummer, coiling around her own cooling eggs. Emberling, youngest of them, barely more than a large clever lizard by wyrm-standards, retreating to a caldera in the mountains to burn alone and slow, hoarding heat like a miser hoards coins, trying to keep one coal alive in a world going cold.

Ansel wrote letters. Forty of them, to every remaining keeper, apologizing, explaining, offering her life, her name, her sigil-hand, anything. Three replies came. Two were curses. The third was from Halvard, one line only:

*You were right about the girl. You were wrong about everything else. Come home when you can forgive yourself, because no one else will do it for you.*

She didn't go home. Forgiveness sounded, from inside, like getting away with it.

* * *

For twelve years she hunted the aftermath.

Because the silence had not stayed clean. Silence never does. Things moved into it — things that had always envied the gods their listeners, lesser hungers, clever hungers, things that had learned to wear the shape of an answered prayer the way a fisherman wears a drowned man's coat. Ansel tracked them. She had no covenant left to enforce, so she enforced the memory of one, carrying a dead sigil-stone from town to town, finding the places where prayers got answered wrong and burning them out root and branch.

It was grim work, badly paid, and she was, by any honest accounting, a war criminal with a walking stick. But she was good at it, and being good at something keeps a person upright longer than being sorry does.

She learned things in those years. She learned that the severed threads hadn't vanished — they'd gone dormant, curled up in the deep places, in roots and catacombs and calderas, waiting. She learned that the old sigils — ash, tide, bone, ember — had scattered when the web broke, rolled into the dark corners of the world like dropped coins. And she learned, three winters ago, the thing that had put her on the road to Corvane with her heart hammering:

The silence was ending. Not healing — *ending*. Prayers were being answered again, here and there, and every answer she investigated had teeth.

Something had found the frayed edge of the old web and was pulling threads through, one at a time, wearing godhood like a stolen cloak. And the only way to stop a thief wearing a stolen cloak was to make the cloak real again — reforge the covenant, relight the fire, gather the sigils before the last dragon's heart went out.

Which required, the texts said, a keeper. Or — the texts hesitated here, embarrassed — *or the one who broke it, whose hands still remember the shape of the web.*

Her hands. Of course. The universe had a sense of humor; she'd just spent twelve years failing to find it funny.

* * *

Corvane was a mean little walled city that hated magic, which Ansel found refreshing after a decade of towns that hated magic *and* pretended otherwise. She arrived in autumn, registered under a false name at a rooming house run by a woman who charged double for discretion and triple for breakfast, and began asking careful questions in the market about answered prayers.

She expected to find a cult, or a smuggled relic, or some hedge-witch dabbling in things that would get her hanged. What she did not expect, on her third morning, threading through the charm-sellers' row, was to stop dead in front of a stall of luck-knots and hear — faintly, unmistakably, beneath the market noise —

a gull. Speaking. In the old tongue of beasts, the tongue that predated the covenant, the tongue that had never needed the veil because it was older than veils.

And then a woman's voice answering it. Low. Careful. Tender, in the way people are tender when tenderness is illegal.

Ansel stood very still among the crowd, holding a paper bag of chestnuts she had no intention of eating, and felt twelve years of loneliness crack down the middle like river ice in spring.

Because she knew that voice's *shape*, even without knowing the woman. She knew what it was to speak into silence and have something answer. She knew the particular care of a person who has spent years hiding the best part of themselves in plain sight.

She watched the charm-seller — dark-haired, quick-eyed, poor in the way of people who give money away — press a knot into a grieving fisherman's hand and murmur something to the gull perched on his shoulder, and the gull bowed its head as if receiving a blessing, and nobody saw, nobody ever saw, and Ansel's chest hurt.

*Two of us,* she thought. *After all this time. Two.*

She should have approached gently. She had planned to approach gently. She had rehearsed, on the walk over, several openings ranging from diplomatic to charming.

Instead, the next morning, she cornered the woman by the shrine where a blind child had inexplicably begun to see — with eyes that reflected a second moon — and said, with all the grace of a collapsing bookshelf:

"You spoke to the gull."

The woman's face did something remarkable: it became completely blank while somehow communicating terror, defiance, and a request that Ansel fall into the harbor.

"I sell charms," the woman said.

"You speak Beast. I heard you. I've spent twelve years listening for that exact sound, and I heard it, and I would very much like to not be hanged alone, so—" Ansel stopped. Breathed. Started over, quieter. "My name is Ansel. I was a covenant-keeper. Before."

Something flickered in the woman's eyes — recognition, or its cousin. "Before," she repeated.

"Before the silence." Ansel glanced at the shrine, at the child being fussed over by his mother, at the second moon swimming in his new-made pupils. "You've seen what's coming back. So have I. And I think — I *think* — that you and I are the only two people in this city who know the difference between a miracle and a mouth."

The charm-seller looked at her for a long moment. Behind her, a rat with unusually expressive whiskers poked its head out of her basket and regarded Ansel with what could only be described as professional skepticism.

"Rafael," the woman said at last. "Rafael Ishikawa. And if you're lying to me, the rats will know. They always know."

"The rats," Ansel said, "have excellent taste."

She meant it lightly. It came out heavy, the way true things do. Because standing there in the cold market with a stranger who hid the same wound she carried, Ansel felt the old shard turn over in her chest — the mint on the stones, the severed thread, the delighted laugh — and she understood that before this was done, Rafael Ishikawa would know what she had done. All of it. The saving and the killing, braided into one act, performed by one pair of hands.

And some stubborn, drowning part of her hoped — against all evidence, against all justice — that knowing it, Rafael might stay anyway.

The rat in the basket chittered something short and sharp.

Rafael went pale. "Sable says," she translated slowly, "that the roots under the city have started humming again. Old oaths. Dead words." She looked up at Ansel, and her eyes were frightened and fierce in equal measure. "You'd better tell me what you did, keeper. From the beginning."

Ansel closed her eyes. Twelve years of running from the beginning.

"All right," she said. "From the beginning."
