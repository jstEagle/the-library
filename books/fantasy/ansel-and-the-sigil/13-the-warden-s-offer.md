## 13. The Warden's Offer

The summons came at dawn, folded into a luck-knot and left on my doorstep like a joke only the Warden's office would find funny.

I stood in the gray light with the paper in my hands and Sable's empty cage at my back, and I thought: *they know.* Of course they knew. The Warden's men had taken Sable four days ago, and I had spent those four days telling myself they'd taken her because she was a rat, a small crime, a footnote. Rats were not people. Rats were not evidence. I had repeated this to myself the way you press on a bruise to prove it doesn't hurt.

The summons said only: *Mistress Ishikawa. The Office of Wardens requests your presence at the ninth bell. Come alone. Come willing.*

*Come willing.* As if I had a choice that wasn't painted to look like one.

I fed the gull on my windowsill — the fisherman's gull, the one I'd spoken to, the one who had told me in his cracked old voice that the sea had been whispering wrong things lately, that the water dreamed of teeth. He cocked his head at me.

"You look terrible," he said.

"Thank you. I've been working on it."

"You should eat something. You should also not go where that paper wants you to go."

"You know about the paper?"

"I can read," the gull said, with dignity. "I lived above a schoolhouse for six years. And I can smell ink and fear, and this paper has both."

I gave him the heel of yesterday's bread because he'd earned it, and because arguing with him was easier than deciding anything.

* * *

The Office of Wardens sat at the heart of Corvane like a stone in a fruit — you could pretend it wasn't there until you bit into it. It had been a temple once, back when temples were allowed to be temples. The bell tower still stood, but the bell had been recast into shackles for the city gates, and the windows were filled with lead stamped with the sigil of the Compact: an open hand, pressed flat. *Nothing moves. Nothing speaks. Nothing answers.*

The Warden was waiting for me in a room that had clearly once been a chapel. Someone had scraped the old carvings off the walls, but the shapes of them remained, ghosts in the plaster — vines, wings, a river of small running figures. She had put her desk directly over where the altar must have been, which told me most of what I needed to know about her before she said a word.

She was younger than I expected. Gray at the temples already, though — the kind of gray that comes from a job, not from years. Her uniform was immaculate. Her eyes were the problem. They were kind eyes, and that frightened me more than cruel ones would have. Cruel eyes you can brace against. Kind eyes make you want to confess.

"Rafael Ishikawa," she said. "Charm-seller. Third stall from the fountain, market row. Luck-knots, fever-wards, traveler's blessings. No complaints on file in eleven years of trade." She smiled. "An exemplary record."

"Thank you," I said. "I work hard."

"Sit down, mistress."

There was one chair. There was always one chair. I sat.

On her desk, between an inkwell and a stack of reports, sat a cage. A small one. Wire and wood, the kind sold for songbirds, and inside it —

"Sable," I said, before I could stop myself.

She looked terrible. Rats are not built for wire cages; their paws don't understand the floor. She sat in the corner with her whiskers drooped, and when she saw me she made a sound I had never heard her make — small, and flat, and ashamed.

"Hello, Rafe," she said.

The Warden did not flinch. That was the worst part. She watched the rat speak and she did not flinch, did not cross herself, did not reach for the iron poker standing by the hearth. She had heard it before. Many times, I suspected. She had built her whole career on hearing it.

"Four days ago my officers confiscated this animal during a routine sweep of the undercity approaches," the Warden said. "It was carrying a message. In its mouth. A written message, which is unusual enough. But when questioned—" she paused, delicately, "—it *answered.*"

"She," I said. "Sable is a she."

"Forgive me." The Warden's smile didn't move. "It answered, mistress. In full sentences. With opinions." She opened a drawer and drew out a sheet of paper, covered in a careful clerk's hand. "It has been very forthcoming. It told us about the market. About the gull. About a woman who murmurs to beasts when she thinks no one is watching. It told us about the shrine on Candlemaker's Lane, and the child with two moons in his eyes. It told us about the ruins beneath the city, and the roots that hum, and—" she glanced at the paper, and her voice went quieter, "—about a covenant-keeper. A woman named Ansel, who came through your gate twelve years ago and never left your heart, if the rat is to be believed. Rats are very romantic, apparently."

Sable made a small mortified noise.

"It told us all of this," the Warden continued, "and then it spent two days telling us nothing at all, no matter what we did. There are techniques, mistress Ishikawa. I won't describe them. But there is a limit to what a small body will hold, and this one reached it, and then it simply looked at my officers with those little black eyes and said, *'You can hurt me all you like. I still won't tell you where she sleeps.'*"

My throat closed. I looked at Sable, and Sable looked at the floor, and I understood suddenly and completely that everything the Warden had just described — the message, the confession, the silence after — had been a performance. Sable had *let* herself be taken. Sable had fed the Warden exactly what the Warden was supposed to hear, and had held the rest behind her small teeth at a cost I didn't want to imagine.

Which meant the message in her mouth had been meant for me. Which meant —

*The catacombs,* I thought. *The third sigil. Bone.* Sable had been trying to tell me where to go next, and had gotten herself caged to do it, because a rat carrying a paper is suspicious and a rat caught carrying a paper is merely a rat.

I kept my face still. Eleven years of practice. I could feel the Warden reading it anyway, the way you read weather.

"So," the Warden said. "Here is what happens now. Option one." She folded her hands. "I process the rat's testimony. You are arrested for beast-speaker practice under the Compact, tried, and — I will be honest with you, because I think you're a woman who prefers honesty — the sentence is the deep cells under the Office, where we keep the ones who *answer.* There are forty-one of them down there, mistress. Did you know that? Forty-one people who talk to animals, or hear prayers, or do the other small forbidden things. Forty-one people the city pretends don't exist."

I hadn't known. Forty-one. All this time I'd thought I was nearly alone, that Ansel and I were the last of something. We were the last of something *loud.* There was a whole quiet congregation beneath my feet.

"Option two," the Warden said.

She rose, went to the window, and stood looking out at the city. Morning smoke rising from a thousand chimneys. The market assembling itself in the square below, stall by stall, the great slow tide of ordinary life.

"Twelve years ago," she said, "I took this office. Do you know why the Compact exists, mistress Ishikawa? Not because magic is evil. Magic is a door, and doors are not evil. The Compact exists because of what came through the door *before* we closed it. The answered prayers. The things that wore godhood like a coat that didn't fit. My grandmother watched a saint walk into her village and grant every wish asked of it, and by winter there was no village. There was a place where wishes had been, and something else wearing the wishes." She turned back to me. "So we pressed the hand flat. Nothing moves. Nothing speaks. Nothing answers. And it *worked,* mistress. For twelve years it worked. Corvane is the safest city in the world precisely because it is the most frightened one, and I have spent my whole career keeping it frightened in the correct amounts."

"And now the prayers are being answered again," I said. "And it's not working."

The kindness in her eyes flickered, and beneath it I saw the other thing — the tiredness, the fear, the weight of a woman holding a door shut with her shoulder.

"No," she said. "It isn't."

* * *

She poured tea. That was the strange part. She poured two cups from a chipped brown pot, the way any neighbor would, and set one in front of me, and for a moment we were just two tired women in a scraped-out chapel drinking terrible tea.

"I'm going to offer you a bargain," she said. "And I want you to understand that it is a genuine one. I keep my bargains. Ask anyone in this office."

"I've heard rats say the same thing," I said, "and they were lying."

The Warden almost smiled. "The rat goes free. Today. No record, no mark, no cell. You go free. Your shop, your stall, your luck-knots, your small gray life — all of it, untouched, guaranteed, in writing if you like. In exchange, you bring me the covenant-keeper."

The tea went cold in my mouth.

"Ansel," I said.

"Ansel." She said the name like she'd been holding it a long time. "She broke the covenant, mistress. Before it was even law — she broke the *old* one, the true one, and everything since has been us patching the hole she made. The gods going silent. The dragons dying. The answered prayers coming back wrong, like water through a cracked dam. Every fear this city has ever had, she is the reason for it. And she is here, in Corvane, now, gathering the sigils." She leaned forward. "You know where she is. You know what she intends. And I am offering you the only trade anyone will ever offer you: her freedom for yours. Her life, if it comes to that, for yours."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then option one. The deep cells. For you and, eventually, for her — we'll find her anyway, mistress. We're already looking. You'd only be delaying it, and paying for the delay with your own life." She sat back. "I don't say this to threaten you. I say it because I've read your file, and your file is eleven years of a woman being *careful.* Careful with her words. Careful with her gift. Careful with her heart. You have spent your whole adult life buying safety with silence, and it has worked, hasn't it? You're alive. You're free. You have your stall and your gull and your—" she gestured at the cage, "—your rat. A small life. A safe one. I am offering to let you keep it. All you have to do is give me the one person who makes it unsafe."

And there it was. The thing she'd built the whole room around.

Because the terrible truth — the truth I sat with while the tea went cold and Sable watched me with her black button eyes — was that the Warden was right. Not about Ansel. About *me.* I *had* bought safety with silence. Eleven years of it. I had a whole architecture of smallness, load-bearing walls of caution, and I knew every beam of it by heart. Don't speak where windows face the street. Don't charm anything bigger than a sparrow. Don't let anyone close enough to be leverage. Don't love anyone the state could take.

And then Ansel had walked through my gate with her broken oath and her stubborn, grieving face, and every wall I'd built had developed a door.

*She is the reason for every fear this city has.* Maybe. But she was also the reason I had spoken aloud in twelve years. The reason a rat knew my name. The reason the gull on my sill had someone to complain to. The reason I had stood in the ruins under the city and felt the dead oaths hum and thought, *oh — this is what I've been starving for,* the way you only learn you're hungry when you finally smell bread.

The Warden was offering me my small life back. My warm, walled, well-lit small life.

And all it would cost was the first true thing I'd had since my mother died.

"You know what she's trying to do," I said. "The sigils. You know, and you're not trying to stop the reforging — you're trying to stop *her.* Why?"

Something moved behind the Warden's face. Something tired.

"Because I read the same histories you have," she said quietly. "And the histories say that the last time the covenant was reforged, it cost the keepers everything they were. And the time before that. The covenant is a fire, mistress Ishikawa, and it has only ever burned *people* for fuel. I have forty-one souls in my cells who prove what this city does to the ones who answer. I will not hand the old gods two more." She stood. "Give me Ansel, and no one else has to burn. Refuse, and everyone burns — you first, and her after, and the city somewhere in the middle. That is the whole of the arithmetic. I'm sorry. I know it isn't kind. Kindness is for after the door is held."

Sable moved in her cage. Just a shift of weight, but I'd known her eleven years, and that shift said: *Rafe. Whatever you're going to do, do it like you mean it.*

I set down the cup.

"Here's my counter-offer," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "You let Sable go. Now, today, into my hands. And in exchange, I'll tell you something true, for free, no bargain attached. Something a Warden ought to know."

The Warden's eyes narrowed. "I'm listening."

"The thing answering prayers under this city isn't a god," I said. "It's a *predator.* It wears godhood the way your grandmother's saint wore it — like a coat that doesn't fit. It has been offering people what they want most. Safety. Legitimacy. A small warm life where no one ever has to be afraid." I held her gaze. "You should know, Warden, because it's coming for you too. And when it does, it's going to offer you exactly what you just offered me. Your door, held. Your city, safe. Everything you've spent your life for, handed to you — if you just stop holding the door yourself and let something else hold it. And it will sound like *rest.*"

The room was very quiet. Down in the square, the market was waking — a cart wheel, a gull crying, the ordinary music of the frightened, careful, stubborn world.

"That," the Warden said at last, "is sedition."

"That's a freebie," I said. "The bargain stands or falls on its own."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she looked at Sable, and something in her face — I'll swear this on my mother's sigil-stone — something in her face *grieved.*

"Take your rat," she said.

She unlocked the cage herself. Sable climbed onto my palm, trembling, lighter than she should have been, and pressed her small hot forehead against my thumb.

"Walk out," the Warden said, turning back to her window. "Go home, mistress Ishikawa. Sell your charms. And when the covenant-keeper comes to you next — because she will, rats are terrible gossips — you tell her that the Office of Wardens knows about the catacombs. We know about the bone. And we will be there."

I stopped at the door.

"You could come with us," I said. "You could hold the door *with* us. That's an option too. It's the one where nobody burns alone."

She didn't turn around. But she didn't answer either, and I have thought about that silence every day since.

* * *

Sable didn't speak until we were three streets from the Office, tucked in my coat pocket, her whiskers twitching against my wrist.

"You knew," I said. "You let them take you."

"I had to get the message to you somehow. Paper under the door is watched. Rats are—" she yawned, enormous, exhausted, "—rats are invisible. Until they aren't."

"They hurt you."

"They *tried.*" A pause. "Rafe, listen. The catacombs. Under the Chapel of the Quiet Dead, there's a stair that isn't on any map. The first keeper is down there. The bone sigil. Ansel knows the way but she can't go alone — the dead keeper has to *judge* her, and judgment goes easier with a witness who isn't afraid of her." Another pause, longer. "There's something else. Something I found out in the cells, listening to the Warden's clerks talk. They're not the only ones hunting Ansel. The Hollow-Voice knows about the catacombs too. It's been *answering* people down there, Rafe. The dead are starting to pray."

I stood in the morning crowd, in the city that had outlawed me, holding a rescued rat and a whole day's worth of courage I hadn't had an hour ago, and I turned toward the Chapel of the Quiet Dead.

"Then we'd better hurry," I said. "Ansel's going to walk straight into a judgment she doesn't believe she can survive. And I intend to be standing next to her when she finds out she's wrong."

Sable's whiskers brushed my wrist, once, like a hand squeezing back.
