## 14. The Third Sigil: Bone

The stairs down into the catacombs had been built for people who expected to come back up.

Rafael noticed this because the stairs going *down* were wide and even and lit by old glowglass sconces that still burned with a patient amber light, while every few landings there was a niche carved into the wall — a place to rest, a place to catch your breath, a place where some long-dead mason had clearly thought, *yes, this is where a tired person will want to pause.* The kindness of it made her throat tight. Whoever had carved these stairs had believed in return trips the way farmers believe in spring.

Nobody had used them in a hundred years.

"Watch the fourth step," Ansel said from below, her voice bouncing off stone. "It's a liar."

Rafael tested it. It was indeed a liar; it gave half an inch under her boot with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

"You've been down here before," Rafael said.

"Once." Ansel didn't turn around. Her lantern swung in her hand, throwing her shadow long and broken against the wall ahead of them. "When I was younger and stupider, which is a sentence I could apply to most of my life."

Sable rode on Rafael's shoulder, her small claws kneading absently at the collar of Rafael's coat, the way she did when she was nervous and pretending not to be. The rat had been quiet since they'd entered the undercity — quieter than usual, which for Sable was saying something, since her usual involved narrating everything from the quality of the air (*stale, dusty, insulting*) to the quality of Ansel's boots (*overpriced, left-heeled, tragic*).

Now she only pressed her whiskers against Rafael's ear and whispered, "The dead here are listening."

"I know," Rafael whispered back.

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be."

They passed the first of the alcoves, and Rafael made herself look. A keeper lay inside on a shelf of stone, hands folded over a sword hilt gone to rust, robes rotted to lace. Bones, mostly. But around the skull, faint as breath on cold glass, something still glimmered — a ring of light, like a halo that had forgotten how to be one.

"The covenant-keepers," Ansel said softly. "Every one of them swore their bones to the pact. They're supposed to be anchors. Ballast." She paused at the next alcove, and Rafael saw her jaw tighten. "Most of them are just bones now. The oath went out of them when I—"

"When you broke it," Rafael finished. She hadn't meant to say it so plainly. Twelve years of silence had taught her to speak in charms and misdirections, and here was Ansel, walking into the dark carrying her guilt like a second lantern.

Ansel's shoulders moved. It might have been a shrug. It might have been something else entirely.

"They kept their word longer than I kept mine," she said. "That's all."

Ansel's POV

The catacombs of Corvane smelled the way Ansel remembered: dust and candle-tallow and, underneath it, something older and stranger — the smell of oaths. People thought oaths were words. Oaths were *material*. They soaked into stone the way wine soaked into wood, and a century after the last covenant-keeper had walked these halls, Ansel could still taste it on the back of her tongue, bitter and binding.

She hated that she'd missed it.

Twelve years of exile, twelve years of telling herself the covenant was a cage and she'd done the world a favor by breaking it, and the whole time some animal part of her had been homesick for the weight of it. For belonging to something larger than her own survival.

"Here." She stopped before a door that wasn't a door — a slab of black basalt set flush into the wall, unmarked except for four shallow depressions arranged in a diamond. Ash. Tide. Ember. And at the bottom, worn deepest of all: bone.

"The first keeper's crypt?" Rafael asked.

"The first keeper's *throne*," Ansel corrected. "Maren of the Hollow Hill refused to be buried. Said death was just another watch shift." Despite everything, Ansel felt her mouth twitch. "She was the sort of woman who argued with gods and won on points."

"And the sigil?"

"Maren took it with her. Bone answers to bone." Ansel set her palm flat against the basalt. It was warm. That was wrong — nothing down here should have been warm — and the warmth of it made her heart hammer like a fist on a locked door. "There's a rite. You knock, in the old pattern, and if your need is true, she lets you in."

"And if your need isn't true?"

"She keeps you." Ansel knocked: once, twice, three-and-one. "Permanently. As furniture."

The basalt shuddered. Dust sifted down like gray snow. And then, with a groan of stone remembering how to move, the door swung inward onto darkness that breathed.

Rafael's POV

The crypt beyond the door was round, and it was full of light.

Not torchlight, not glowglass — a soft white radiance that came from everywhere and nowhere, pooling on the floor like milk. In its center stood a chair of black iron, and in the chair sat a skeleton wearing the rotted grandeur of the first covenant, and the skeleton's eye sockets held two points of that same white fire.

Rafael had faced down the Warden's men. She had spoken with beasts her whole life in secret, in whispers, in the spaces between heartbeats. None of it had prepared her for being *seen* by the dead.

"Well," said Maren of the Hollow Hill, and her voice was dry leaves and door hinges and, underneath, a woman's voice, wry and tired and awake. "Visitors. It's been — hm. Let me consult my accounting." The skull tilted. "One hundred and eleven years, four months, and nine days. Give or take a war."

"You've been counting," Ansel said.

"Somebody had to. Everyone else stopped." The white fires flicked to Rafael, and Rafael felt them go through her like needles through cloth, sorting thread. "Ah. A beast-speaker. Hidden, hidden, hidden — twelve years of hiding, girl, you've got more knots in you than a sailor's rope. And—" the fires narrowed, "—you carry two sigils already. Ash and tide. Who told you to do that?"

"No one," Rafael said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "We're trying to reforge the covenant. Before the dragon-fire goes out."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on the chest.

Then Maren laughed. It was a terrible, wonderful sound — a dead woman laughing in a room full of light — and it went on until Rafael found herself smiling despite everything, because the laugh was *warm*, and warmth had been in short supply lately.

"Reforge it," Maren said, wiping at her nonexistent eyes with a hand that was mostly metacarpals. "Oh, that's rich. That's *magnificent*. Do you know who broke it, girl? Do you know what she did?"

"Yes," Rafael said.

"Do you know *why*?"

"...Yes."

"Then you're either very brave or very badly informed." The white fires turned, slow as lighthouse beams, to Ansel. And Rafael watched Ansel stand there and take it — stand in the light of the first keeper's judgment with her chin up and her hands open at her sides, no weapon, no excuse, nothing between her and the dead but the truth.

"Say it," Maren said gently. Gently, which was somehow worse than any accusation. "I have been sitting in this chair for a hundred and eleven years with nothing to do but remember. Say it out loud. Dead ears are patient ears."

Ansel's throat worked.

"I broke the covenant," she said. "Twelve years ago. There was a god — a real one, or close enough — coming through the veil at Vell's Crossing. It was going to eat the city. Every prayer in it, every soul, everything. I had minutes. Maybe less. So I shattered the anchor-stone, and the veil closed, and the god starved on the far side." Her voice cracked and she let it. "And every other god starved too. Because the anchor was the hearth, and I put out the hearth to burn down one wolf. I saved one city and silenced the world, and I have spent twelve years deciding whether that makes me a hero or the worst criminal in history, and I still don't know."

The light held steady on her.

Rafael realized she was holding her breath. She let it out slowly. Beside her ear, Sable had gone utterly still.

"You don't know," Maren repeated. "Good."

"*Good?*"

"Anyone who knows is lying or dangerous, and usually both." The skeleton rose from the chair — rose the way smoke rises, unhurried, inevitable — and crossed the floor to stand before Ansel, close enough that the white fire in her sockets reflected in Ansel's eyes. "I judged keepers for sixty years, girl. I heard every kind of confession. The ones who said *I know exactly what I am* were always wrong, and the ones who said *I don't know* were usually right, and the ones who stood there with their hands open instead of reaching for a defense—" the bony finger tapped Ansel's open palm, once, "—those were the rare ones. Those were the ones worth swearing anything to."

"I'm not asking for absolution," Ansel said hoarsely.

"No. You're asking for a job. Which is better." Maren turned, robes whispering like paper, and gestured toward the iron chair. Behind it, in the wall, Rafael saw it now: a recess, and within the recess, resting on a cushion of woven hair, a sigil-stone the color of old ivory, marked with a spiral like a fingerprint. Like the whorls of an ear. Like something listening.

"Bone is the sigil of keeping," Maren said. "Of holding the line when holding the line costs everything. Ash remembers, tide endures, ember burns — but bone *stays*. It stays when staying is stupid. It stays when everyone else has gone home." The white fires came back to Ansel. "You stayed twelve years in exile doing small good deeds and calling yourself damned. That's the stupidest kind of staying there is. It's also the kind I swore my bones to."

"You can't mean to give it to her," Rafael blurted.

Maren's skull turned toward her, amused. "No?"

"She broke the covenant! The whole thing, the entire — everything my mother spent her life hiding, everything we're bleeding to fix, it traces back to *her*, and you're going to just — hand her the third piece like she's earned it?"

"Yes," said Maren.

"But—"

"Girl." The dead keeper drifted closer, and her voice dropped into something almost tender. "If we only gave the tools of mending to those who never broke anything, the world would have no menders at all. Only breakers, and bystanders." She looked back at Ansel. "Besides. I've watched her since she walked in. She hasn't looked away from you once. Not in fear. In *accounting*. She's checking your work." The fires flickered, and Rafael would have sworn the skull smiled. "That's what trust looks like when it's honest. Not certainty. Supervision."

Rafael felt her face go hot. "I—that's not—"

"It's exactly that," Sable said from her shoulder, smugly. "I've been counting. You've checked on her eleven times since the door opened."

"*Traitor,*" Rafael hissed, in rat-tongue, which fooled no one.

Ansel's POV

The sigil of bone weighed nothing and everything.

When Ansel lifted it from its cushion, the ivory stone sat in her palm like a bird's egg, warm as a sleeping animal. And then the warmth spread — up her wrist, her arm, into her chest, into the hollow places where the old oath had been ripped out twelve years ago — and for one vertiginous moment Ansel felt *held*. Held the way stone holds a foundation. Held the way the dead hold their promises.

Maren stood before her, and the white fires in her sockets had dimmed to embers, and Ansel understood, with a lurch, that giving the sigil was costing her. That the light Ansel had taken for eternal was, in fact, a candle, and the candle was nearly done.

"You're spending yourself," Ansel said. "Giving us this. You're—"

"I'm clocking out." Maren's voice was thinner now, papery, but the wryness held. "A hundred and eleven years of overtime, girl. I've earned it. Besides—" the skeletal hand rose and closed Ansel's fingers tighter around the stone, "—a judge is useless without a court. Reforge the covenant. Build something worth judging. Then come back and tell me about it, and I'll decide whether to haunt you."

"That's not how haunting works."

"Everything works differently once I'm through with it." Maren stepped back. The light in her sockets guttered, steadied, guttered again. "Last words. I get last words, I've waited long enough." The fires fixed on both of them — on Ansel, and on Rafael, standing at the edge of the milk-white pool with a rat on her shoulder and her chin set at the exact angle of someone determined not to hope. "You two. Whatever you are to each other — and it's none of my business, and I'm making it my business — don't waste the staying. Staying's the whole trick. Everything else is decoration."

The light went out.

The skeleton settled back into its chair with a sigh of dust, hands folding over the rusted sword, and the crypt went dark except for the ivory glow of the sigil in Ansel's hand and the amber of Rafael's charm-lantern, which she'd apparently been clutching the entire time like a talisman.

For a long moment neither of them spoke.

"Heavy?" Rafael asked finally, nodding at the stone.

"No," Ansel said. Then, honestly: "Yes. Both. It's very good at being both."

Rafael came closer. Close enough that Ansel could see the shine of the lantern-light in her eyes, and the exhaustion under it, and the stubbornness under *that*. She looked at the sigil for a long moment. Then she reached out — hesitated — and laid two fingers briefly against the back of Ansel's hand, around the stone.

"Worthy of a second chance," she said quietly. "Her words, not mine. But I'm... supervising."

"I heard."

"Eleven times."

"Fourteen," said Sable. "You missed three."

Rafael turned red to the ears, and Ansel — for the first time in twelve years, in a crypt beneath a city that had outlawed everything she loved — laughed. Really laughed. It came out rusty and cracked, a hinge unused, but it came out, and Rafael's startled smile in response was worth more than the sigil, though Ansel would have died before saying so.

They climbed back toward the door, and the basalt groaned shut behind them, and the stairs stretched upward, kind and even, built by people who believed in return trips.

It was Sable who broke the mood, as Sable was contractually obligated to do.

"Small problem," the rat announced, hackles lifting, whiskers flat. "Someone's been following us since the third landing. Two-legged. Heavy boots. Warden-issue boots." Her nose twitched toward the dark above. "And they've been listening to *everything*."

Above them, faint but unmistakable, came the click of a crossbow being cocked in the dark.

Ansel's hand closed around the sigil of bone, and the stairwell lights began, one by one, to die.
