## 16. The God of Answered Prayers

Ansel had faced down three kinds of terror in her life: the kind that roared, the kind that whispered, and the kind that was polite.

The first two she could manage. She had once stared down a storm-wyrm on the cliffs of Vess with nothing but a broken oath and a very sharp stick, and she had sat through more whispered threats in the covenant halls than she cared to count—old keepers murmuring about her failures behind cupped hands, as though grief were contagious and she the vector.

But politeness terrified her. Politeness meant the thing across from you had studied how people worked. Politeness meant it knew exactly which door to knock on, and it would wait, hands folded, until you opened it yourself.

Hollow-Voice knocked politely.

* * *

They had made camp in the shell of the old tithe-house above the Tanners' Row, because Sable insisted that rats could smell god-stink the way humans smelled rot, and the tithe-house stank least. Ansel didn't fully believe the rat's methodology, but she had learned over the past weeks that dismissing Sable's advice was like dismissing weather advice from someone standing outside in the rain. You were welcome to your skepticism. You'd still get wet.

Rafael was asleep by the fire, curled around her pack the way she always did, as if even unconscious she expected to have to run. Ansel kept the first watch, sharpening a knife she didn't need to sharpen, because sharpening gave her hands something honest to do while her mind did dishonest things.

The fire guttered.

Not the way fires normally guttered—not toward the wind, not toward damp wood. It guttered *inward*, flames folding into themselves like a hand closing, and for one heartbeat the whole camp went dim and silver, lit by something that wasn't fire at all.

Then the light came back up, warm and gold and wrong, and there was a man sitting on the far side of the hearth.

He looked like a pilgrim. That was the first thing Ansel noticed, and the thing that frightened her most. He wore the dusty gray of a road-tired traveler, boots scuffed, a walking staff leaned against the wall beside him with the casualness of a man who had earned his blisters honestly. His face was unremarkable in the specific way that faces in crowds are unremarkable—the face you'd forget before you finished looking at it. Middle-aged, kind lines around the eyes, a small scar through one eyebrow that suggested a story he'd tell you if you asked, and he seemed so genuinely hopeful you might.

"Forgive the intrusion," he said. His voice was warm. It was the voice of a favorite uncle, a beloved teacher, the innkeeper who always saved you the corner table. "I know how it looks. A stranger by your fire at night. But I've been walking a long time, and I saw the smoke, and I thought—well. Company is company."

Ansel's knife was in her hand. She had no memory of drawing it. "Sable," she said quietly.

"Oh, the rat's fine," the man said, with a little laugh. "Sleeping. Very deeply. Rats dream wonderfully, did you know? All that running." He spread his hands. "You can wake her if it comforts you. I'd rather you were comfortable."

"Who are you?"

"You know who I am." And here was the terrible thing: he said it without menace, without triumph, almost with sympathy, the way you'd remind an old friend of a name they'd misplaced. "You've been hunting me for weeks, Ansel Vane. Disgraced keeper, breaker of covenants, last of the Oathbound—or second-to-last, now, counting the charming young woman asleep beside your fire. You know me. You simply know me by my work."

"The answered prayers."

"Mm." He nodded, pleased, as if she'd identified a wine correctly. "Though I prefer to think of them as *answered*. The prayers themselves are other people's work. I only do the answering part. It's the part everyone forgets has to be done by somebody." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight catching the scar. "Do you know how long I waited? Do you have any idea what it is to be a god with no one to answer? Twelve years of silence, Ansel. Twelve years of mothers praying over fevered children and getting nothing back. I heard every single prayer. Every one. And I could do *nothing*, because your covenant—" he gestured at her, gently, the way you'd gesture at a wound, "—had cut the wires."

"I didn't cut them to hurt anyone."

"No," he agreed readily. "You cut them to save a city. I know. I was there, in a manner of speaking. I watched you make the choice, and I want you to understand—I have never blamed you. Not once." He smiled, and the smile was so full of understanding that Ansel felt her chest tighten with something dangerously close to relief. "That's the difference between me and the others, you see. The old gods judged. Constantly. Every prayer was weighed, every petitioner measured against some ancient standard no mortal could ever meet. *Answered prayers* came with conditions, tithes, humiliations. I don't do any of that." He spread his hands again, open, empty. "I just say yes."

"That's not true," Ansel said. "The blind boy. The child who sees now. What does he see when he looks up?"

The pause was very small. Most people wouldn't have caught it. Ansel had spent twelve years learning to catch pauses.

"He sees," Hollow-Voice said warmly, "wonderfully."

"And the fisherman's wife, in Lowharbor, whose husband came home from a sea that took him nine years ago. He came home, didn't he. Walked through the door dripping. And she was so happy she didn't ask why he never ate."

"She was happy," Hollow-Voice said, and his voice was still warm, but underneath the warmth, for just a moment, Ansel heard something vast shift its weight, the way the sea shifts under a calm surface. "She prayed for nine years. Who are you to begrudge her the answer?"

Between them, the fire folded inward again, and in the silver half-dark Ansel saw him clearly for one instant—not the pilgrim, but the shape wearing the pilgrim, tall past sense, its edges soft the way a voice is soft, its face a suggestion rather than a fact. Then the flame rose and he was a tired traveler again, rubbing his hands over the coals.

"I'm not here for you, anyway," he said. "I'm here for Rafael."

Something cold walked down Ansel's spine. "If you touch her—"

"*Touch* her?" He laughed, genuinely delighted, and the laugh was so human it made her ill. "No, no. Listen to me. Listen." He leaned forward, and his voice dropped into confidence, into intimacy, into the register of a man sharing a secret he'd been keeping for your own good. "Do you know what she wants? Truly? Because I've listened to her heart for weeks now, the way I listen to all hearts, and hers is the clearest I've heard in a century. She wants to stop hiding. That's all. That's the whole of it. Twelve years of selling luck-knots to people whose luck she could actually change, twelve years of whispering to gulls in alleys and flinching when footsteps come, twelve years of being *smaller than she is*—and why? Because the law says her gift is a crime. Because the covenant that should have sheltered her broke, and the world decided that everything holy must therefore be hunted." He shook his head slowly, sorrowfully. "I can fix that."

"You can't fix anything. Everything you fix comes back wrong."

"Does it?" He tilted his head. "The mother in Tanner's Row whose daughter's cough stopped midwinter—she lights a candle for me every seventh day. Is that wrong? The ferryman who hasn't lost a passenger since spring—he leaves bread at my shrine. Wrong? Ansel." He said her name like a hand on her shoulder. "You've spent twelve years telling yourself the silence was mercy. It wasn't. It was abandonment. I am the first thing in twelve years that has *answered these people*, and they love me for it, and their love is not wrong. Their love is the truest thing left in Corvane."

Behind her, Ansel heard the small sounds of Rafael stirring, and panic flared bright and useless in her chest.

"She doesn't need you," Ansel said.

"Everyone needs me. That's rather the point of gods." He sighed, and the sigh carried the weariness of centuries, and it was performed, Ansel knew it was performed, and knowing did nothing whatsoever to blunt it. "But here is what I actually came to say, and then I'll go, because I'm courteous, whatever you've heard." He looked past her, at the sleeping shape by the fire, and his expression softened into something that looked unbearably like tenderness. "I'm going to offer her everything. Legitimacy—a city where her gift is a *profession*, honored, licensed, celebrated. Safety—no Warden, no cell, no more flinching. And love." His eyes came back to Ansel's, gentle and pitiless. "Love without sacrifice. Because that's the part that breaks my heart, keeper. She's falling for you. And you—both of you—are already rehearsing what it will cost. I can hear it. The sigils take memories. The dragon-fire takes more. You're both lying awake doing arithmetic with each other, deciding in advance which pieces of yourselves you can afford to lose. That isn't love. That's bookkeeping."

The words went in like a splinter, deep, and Ansel couldn't find the edge of them to pull them out.

"I offer her a life where love costs nothing," Hollow-Voice said. "Where she says yes to me and the yes costs nothing, ever, forever. No sigils. No calderas. No empty chairs across fires. Just warmth, and safety, and a city that finally, *finally* says her name out loud instead of making her whisper it." He stood, unhurried, and took up his staff. "And she'll say yes. Not because I trick her. Because it will feel like coming home. That's the difference between me and the old gods, Ansel, and I need you to carry this back to whoever's left of your order: the old gods demanded worship. I only offer it a place to rest."

He moved toward the door, and then paused, and turned back with the small apologetic smile of a guest remembering his manners.

"One more thing. When she refuses—and she may, she's stubborn, I find it charming—there will be a moment, soon, in public, where she'll have to choose whether to be seen. Truly seen. Beast-speaker, criminal, miracle, all of it, in front of the whole city." His scarred eyebrow lifted. "I won't force her. I never force anyone. I'll simply arrange the moment, and let her choose between the small safe life and the large dangerous one, with every eye in Corvane watching, and the Warden's men already moving through the crowd." He tapped his staff once against the floorboards, softly, like a heartbeat. "Choose your side carefully, keeper. One of us is offering her a home."

Then the fire folded inward, and the silver light passed through the room like water through cloth, and when the flames rose again the chair across the hearth was empty, and the dust on the floorboards showed no footprints at all.

* * *

Ansel sat very still for a long time.

The knife was still in her hand. She set it down, picked it up again, set it down. Her thoughts kept circling the same wound: *bookkeeping.* Was that what they'd been doing? She thought of Rafael at the tide-sigil, giving up her mother's face with a steadiness that had frightened Ansel more than any scream would have. She thought of herself, lying awake, composing the sentence she'd say if Rafael ever asked what the ember sigil would cost—composing it carefully, so that Rafael would volunteer before she understood, so that the sacrifice would be *clean.*

Clean. As if sacrifice could be clean. As if love were a ledger you balanced before the auditors came.

"Ansel."

Rafael was awake, propped on one elbow, hair sleep-mussed, watching her with those level dark eyes that missed nothing. "How long have you been sitting like that?"

"A while."

"Sable woke up growling at the air and then went back to sleep," Rafael said. "That usually means something talked to us." She sat up properly, pulling her blanket around her shoulders, and looked at the empty chair, and then at Ansel, and Ansel watched her arrive at the truth the way she arrived at everything—quietly, completely, without wasted motion. "It was him, wasn't it. Hollow-Voice."

"Yes."

"What did he want?"

Ansel opened her mouth to lie—to soften it, to edit it, to do the protective arithmetic she'd been doing since the tithe-house—and stopped. Because the god had been right about one thing, and it stuck in her throat like a fishbone: they had to stop doing sums. If they were going to walk into whatever came next, they had to stop balancing accounts and start telling each other the whole ugly totals.

"He offered you everything," Ansel said. "A lawful gift. A safe life. Love that costs nothing." She held Rafael's gaze. "And he's going to give you the choosing of it in front of the entire city, soon, with the Warden watching. He wanted me to know. He wanted me to doubt."

Rafael was quiet for a moment. Then, horribly, she smiled—that crooked, tired, entirely unsaintly smile that Ansel had privately catalogued among the things worth saving a world for.

"Love that costs nothing," Rafael repeated. "From a god who answers prayers by drowning fishermen and putting moons in children's eyes." She shook her head. "He really did his research, though. That's the awful part. He found the exact shape of the hole in me and offered to pour himself into it."

"Rafael—"

"But here's what he got wrong." She leaned forward, and the firelight caught her face, and there was nothing hidden in it at all. "He thinks the cost is the problem. He thinks if you take away the price, you get the same love, just cheaper." She reached across the fire and took Ansel's hand—callused, knife-scarred, still holding the memory of the knife. "The price is *mine* to pay. That's the whole point. Nobody gets to buy my surrender, not even with a bargain that looks like a homecoming. Especially not then."

Outside, far off across the sleeping city, a bell began to ring—not the hour bell, not the alarm bell, but the Warden's summons bell, rolling out over the rooftops in long urgent strokes. Voices stirred in the streets below. Somewhere, dogs began to bark, and Ansel would have sworn, would have staked her ruined oath on it, that beneath the barking she heard rats squealing in a pattern too organized to be fear.

Rafael stood, already reaching for her pack, already listening.

"That bell means market square," she said. "At dawn. Something's gathering."

Ansel rose, and the cold certainty settled over her like a mantle: the polite god had arranged his moment after all.

"It's time to choose," she said.
