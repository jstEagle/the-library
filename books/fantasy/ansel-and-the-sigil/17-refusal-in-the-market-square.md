## 17. Refusal in the Market Square

The morning Rafael decided to ruin her life began, as most ruinous mornings do, with bread.

She had queued at Ferro's stall the way she had every morning for twelve years—same spot, same sourdough, same exchange of coins for warmth—and she was three steps away with the loaf tucked under her arm when the screaming started.

Not frightened screaming. That was the terrible part. It was *joyful* screaming, high and bright and wrong, the sound of a city discovering a miracle it hadn't asked for.

Rafael turned and saw them gathered at the fountain square: a knot of people around a woman holding up a child. The boy couldn't have been more than six. He was laughing. Everyone around him was weeping and laughing both, and above them all, faint as breath on glass, hung a light that did not come from the sun.

It came from *below*. From the well at the square's center, the old dry well that had been capped with iron since before Rafael was born. The light seeped up through the seams of the cap like water through a failing dam.

"His legs," someone was saying. "He couldn't walk since the fever. Look at him *stand*."

"Answered," another voice breathed. "It answered her prayer. Three nights she prayed at the shrine, and it *answered*."

Rafael stood very still with her bread going cold under her arm and felt the familiar dread settle into her chest like a stone dropped into a well. She knew what answered prayers looked like now. She'd seen the blind child with two moons in his eyes. She'd heard about the widow whose dead husband's voice sang to her from the chimney every night, sweet and loving, until she stopped eating, stopped sleeping, stopped anything but sitting by the hearth with her face turned up toward the flue.

Hollow-Voice gave people exactly what they asked for. That was the horror of it. No god worth the name would be so *obedient*.

And now it had found a child.

* * *

She should have walked away. She wants, later, to be honest about this: she thought about walking away. She had a shop full of harmless charms, a cellar full of dangerous truths, four sigils hidden in oilcloth beneath her floorboards, and a dragon guttering toward death on a mountain, and every survival instinct she had honed over twelve years of careful, small, invisible living told her to put her head down and go home.

But the boy's mother caught sight of Rafael standing at the edge of the crowd—the charm-seller, the woman everyone knew, quiet and reliable—and something desperate kindled in her face.

"You," she called out. "Mistress Ishikawa. You know the old ways, don't you? You sell the luck-knots. Tell me—is it true? Is it *him*? Is my boy truly healed?"

The crowd turned. A hundred faces. More. And among them, near the fountain's edge, a face that made Rafael's stomach drop through the cobbles: the Warden, gray-cloaked, still, watching her with the patient attention of a cat watching a mouse hole.

Say nothing, every instinct said. Say it's a blessing. Say the gods are kind. Go home.

What came out of her mouth instead was: "May I see him?"

The crowd parted. The mother thrust the boy forward, radiant, triumphant. And Rafael knelt down on the cold stones, took the child's small hands in hers, and looked into his eyes.

They were brown. Warm, ordinary, beautiful brown eyes.

And behind them, reflected deep in each pupil like a figure at the far end of a corridor, stood something tall and thin and smiling, wearing the boy's healed body the way a thief wears a stolen coat.

"Oh," Rafael said softly. "Oh, you poor thing."

"He can *walk*," the mother said, sharp with sudden fear. "Whatever you're seeing—"

"I see a miracle," Rafael said loudly, for the crowd, for the Warden, for anyone listening. "A true blessing." And then, barely a breath, in the tongue that had no business passing her lips in daylight, in public, in front of a hundred witnesses:

*Little one. Can you hear me?*

The boy's smile flickered. For half a heartbeat his face went ancient and exhausted, an old soul crammed into young flesh, and he answered in the same beast-tongue, the words coming out shaped wrong for a human mouth, wet and clicking:

*It sings to me at night. It says I'm its favorite. It says if I keep being good, Mother won't be sad anymore.*

Rafael's blood went to ice water.

*Does it hurt you?*

*It doesn't need to hurt,* the boy said, with the terrible patience of the very old. *It just needs me to say yes.*

* * *

Later—much later, when people asked her how she'd found the courage, though no one ever actually asked because no one wanted to admit they'd been there—Rafael would try to explain that it wasn't courage at all. It was arithmetic.

Twelve years ago, her mother had traded the voices of the gods for her daughter's life, and the bill had come due, and the thing collecting it wore godhood like a mask. Ansel had broken a covenant to save one city and doomed the world. Sable had paid in memories. Emberling was dying in fire. Everyone in this story, Rafael understood suddenly, standing in the market square with a possessed child's hands in hers, had spent their whole selves trying to buy safety for someone else—and safety, it turned out, was the one thing Hollow-Voice sold best.

*It just needs me to say yes.*

Well. Rafael Ishikawa had spent twelve years saying no. Quietly. In cellars. In whispers to gulls and rats and one memorable, disastrous conversation with a milk cow who had opinions about her singing. She had said no so quietly for so long that she'd nearly forgotten what it sounded like out loud.

The Warden was watching. The crowd was watching. The law said that what she was about to do was death, or worse—the gray cells, the quiet trials, the names read out at autumn assizes. Her mother had died keeping this secret. Her mother had *killed herself*, in the slow way, the way of small lives and swallowed words, keeping this secret.

Rafael looked at the boy. The boy looked back with something new in his expression—not hope, exactly. Recognition. The look of a creature meeting the first person in its whole small life who could hear it.

*Will you make it stop?* he asked.

And Rafael Ishikawa, charm-seller, coward, keeper of secrets, stood up in front of the entire city of Corvane and spoke beast-tongue loud enough to raise the dead.

"Yes," she said—in both tongues at once, human words and animal speech braided together, a sound no human throat should make, a sound that made the crowd gasp and stumble backward like a wave recoiling from rock. "I hear you, little brother. And I am telling you: *say no.*"

The light from the well surged. The iron cap groaned. And out of the dark below, filling the square, warm as a hearth and vast as a cathedral, came the voice Rafael had been dreading since the night the prayers started answering themselves.

*You would take this from him?* Hollow-Voice asked, gentle, wounded, reasonable. *He was lame. Now he dances. You would rather he crawl?*

"I'd rather he be *his*," Rafael said, and her voice cracked in the middle and she let it crack, let the whole city see it. "You don't heal. You *keep*. I've seen what you keep. The widow by the chimney. The moons-eyed child. You give them what they ask for until asking is all they are."

*I offer love,* the voice said, sorrowful, magnificent. *No conditions. No sacrifice. No memories bartered, no oaths sworn, no dragons burning. Just yes. Forever, yes. Isn't that what you've always wanted, little speaker? To be loved without paying for it?*

And here was the worst of it, the part Rafael would carry like a scar: for one full heartbeat, she *wanted*. She wanted it the way you want sleep, the way you want your mother's kitchen, the way you want the years back. Somewhere behind her, she sensed Ansel pushing through the crowd, too late, always arriving just after the choice was already made—but it didn't matter, because the choice wasn't Ansel's, and it never had been.

"No," Rafael said.

Simple. Small. The smallest word in any language, in every language, in beast-tongue and human speech and whatever tongue gods use when they lie.

"No," she said again, louder, turning to face the crowd, facing the Warden, facing the well, facing twelve years of her own careful silence. "I have hidden what I am since I was a girl. I have sold luck-knots and bitten my tongue and watched this city outlaw wonder, and I told myself it was safe, and it was *never safe*, because fear is a door and something has been knocking." She lifted the boy's hands, still clasped in hers. "This child said yes because he was six years old and sad and someone finally answered. So I will answer too—loudly, publicly, in front of God and Warden and everybody: *Corvane, hear me.* There are speakers among you. There always were. We talk to your gulls and your dogs and the rats in your walls, and the beasts remember what the gods were before you built shrines, and if you hunt us, you blind yourselves. If you silence us, the only voice left answering your prayers will be *that one*."

She pointed at the well.

The square went silent enough to hear the bread cooling in her arms.

Then the boy laughed—a real laugh, his own laugh, six years old and gap-toothed—and the light beneath the well-cap flickered, guttered, and drew back down into the dark like a tide going out. Whatever grip had held him loosened. He swayed; his mother caught him; and Rafael felt the small warm weight of his attention pass out of the deep place and back into the ordinary world, blinking, dazed, *his*.

*Another time, little speaker,* the dark murmured, almost fondly. *You'll tire of saying no. Everyone does.*

"Maybe," Rafael said. "But not today."

* * *

The Warden did not arrest her.

That was somehow worse. The Warden simply walked through the stunned crowd, unhurried, and stopped an arm's length away, and looked at Rafael with an expression she couldn't read—not triumph, not fury. Something almost like respect, which frightened her more than either.

"Mistress Ishikawa," the Warden said. "Do you know what you've done?"

"I saved a child."

"You confessed to capital heresy in front of two hundred witnesses." The Warden glanced around the square—at the people who had not fled, who were standing in loose, uncertain clusters, looking at Rafael not with horror but with something older and more confused, something that might, given time and hunger, become hope. "And not one of them is calling for the gray cells. Do you understand how much trouble that is? For *me*?"

Behind the Warden, Ansel reached her side, breathing hard, sword-hand empty because drawing steel here would start a riot, eyes wide with a horror and pride she didn't bother hiding. Sable was nowhere visible, which meant the rat was everywhere visible, if you knew how to look.

"The dragon-fire gutters," Rafael said quietly, only for the Warden. "In days, maybe hours. When it dies, that thing under the well stops pretending to be kind. Help us, or get out of our way. Those are your choices."

The Warden held her gaze for a long moment. Then, so softly only Rafael could hear it:

"My grandmother talked to cats. They took her when I was seven." A pause. "Caldera road, west gate. The night watch changes at moonrise. I was never here."

The Warden turned and strode away through the crowd, gray cloak swallowing her, and Rafael exhaled a breath she felt she'd been holding for twelve years.

Ansel stared at her. "You—" She stopped. Started again. "You absolute *idiot*. Magnificent. Reckless. Idiot."

"I know," Rafael said, and discovered she was laughing, slightly hysterical, the loaf of bread still clutched under her arm like a shield. "I know. Come on. We have a dragon to save, and I believe I promised the whole city a revolution before breakfast."

Above them, on the rooftops, a thousand pigeons had gone silent—and every one of them, Rafael could feel it now, openly, without shame, was listening.

Far to the west, past the walls, past the mountains, something ancient felt the covenant's fourth sigil stir in its sleep—and opened one ember-bright eye.
