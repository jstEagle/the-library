## 18. Guttering

The mountain had been weeping ash for three days before they reached the caldera.

Ansel felt it first in her teeth—a fine grit that settled between them no matter how she clenched her jaw—and then in the air itself, which tasted of spent fires and old copper. The road up from the valley was black glass in places, fused by heat so ancient the trees had grown around it and been burned back down again, generation after generation, a forest of charred stumps all leaning away from the summit like courtiers bowing to a dying king.

Rafael walked ahead of her. She'd been walking ahead of her for two days now, ever since the third sigil—bone—had cooled against Rafael's sternum where she wore it on its cord, and Ansel couldn't decide if the distance was grief or strategy or simply the way Rafael moved through the world lately: like someone carrying something breakable and refusing to say what it was.

"You're doing the thing," Ansel said.

"What thing."

"The thing where you walk like the mountain owes you money."

Rafael glanced back over her shoulder. Her face was smudged with ash except for two clean crescents under her eyes where she'd pushed her hair back, which gave her the look of a raccoon who had recently come into an inheritance. "I'm concentrating."

"On what?"

"Whether the mountain owes me money."

Despite everything—the Warden's hunters somewhere below them on the switchbacks, the predator-god's answered prayers festering in every shrine in Corvane, the four days of hard travel on bread gone green at one corner—Ansel laughed. It came out of her sideways, startled, the way laughter always did around Rafael Ishikawa, as if her body kept forgetting to be afraid when Rafael was within earshot.

"There," Rafael said, satisfied. "You were making your grim face. It frightens the mules."

"We don't have a mule."

"That's how frightening it is."

They crested the last ridge as the sun went down behind them, and Ansel stopped laughing.

The caldera opened below like a bowl held up to a sky already going dark, and at the bottom of it lay Emberling.

She was smaller than Ansel remembered. That was the first cruelty of it. Twelve years ago, when Ansel had stood at the covenant-stone in the undercity and shattered the pact between realms, dragon-fire had been a fact of the world, vast and ambient, like weather. You did not think about the size of dragons because their fire made them larger than size. Now Emberling lay coiled in the caldera's throat, and Ansel could see all of her at once—the whole length of her, dull copper gone gray at the wing-joints, her flanks rising and falling in breaths so slow and shallow that Ansel counted three of them before she was sure the dragon still breathed.

Around her, the caldera floor was littered with scales. Not shed ones. Fallen ones, loosened like leaves out of season, drifting whenever her breath stirred them. They clicked and whispered across the stone in the wind, and the sound they made was almost exactly the sound of the ash-forest above, and Ansel understood suddenly why the trees leaned away.

"She's dying," Rafael said quietly.

"She's guttering." Ansel started down the slope, loose scree sliding under her boots. "There's a difference. A candle gutters right before—" She stopped herself. Right before it goes out was not a sentence that helped anyone.

* * *

Emberling's eye opened before they were halfway down.

It was the color of a banked coal, deep orange shot through with veins of near-black, and it tracked them with a slowness that might have been dignity or might have been exhaustion. When they reached the caldera floor proper, close enough to feel the faint warmth coming off her hide like a hearth seen through a wall, the eye swiveled fully toward Ansel, and a voice filled the crater—not loud, not booming, but *everywhere*, the way heat is everywhere in a closed room.

**The oath-breaker returns.**

"I know," Ansel said. She did not bow. Bowing felt like something you did to gods, and Emberling was not a god; she was older than gods and had never once pretended otherwise, which was the whole reason the covenant had been sworn with dragon-fire instead of prayer. "I'm not here to apologize. Apologies are cheap and I've already spent twelve years paying interest. I'm here to finish it."

**Finish it,** the dragon repeated. Something moved in the deep of her eye—not anger. Amusement, maybe, or the memory of amusement, worn smooth like a river stone. **The little keeper comes to finish things. Tell me, oath-breaker: do you finish them well, or merely completely?**

"Well, I hope," Ansel said. "Completely, certainly. I have references. Most of them are angry."

Beside her, Rafael made a small sound that was absolutely a suppressed laugh, and Emberling's nostril flared—a gust of warm air smelling of cinders and, underneath it, impossibly, of honey—and the great head turned from Ansel to Rafael with the ponderous grace of a ship coming about.

**And this is the beast-speaker.**

Rafael went very still. Ansel watched her do it, watched the old habit lock down over her like shutters in a storm, hands flattening against her thighs, chin tucking, the whole body trying to become furniture. Twelve years of hiding, and even here, at the bottom of a dying volcano in front of the last creature alive who had known her gift when it was legal—still, the hiding came first.

"He can't hurt you," Ansel said gently. "He's mostly spine and opinions."

**Mostly,** Emberling agreed, with terrible dignity.

Rafael exhaled. Her shoulders came down half an inch. And then, in a voice barely louder than the wind, she said, "You knew my grandmother."

The eye widened. Just slightly. But Ansel saw it, and saw too the way Rafael's hand drifted up to the cord at her neck where the sigils hung—ash, tide, bone—three smooth stones warm against her skin.

**Maren's girl,** Emberling said. **Yes. I knew Maren. Maren brought me honeycakes and bad news in equal measure, and I could never decide which I liked less.**

"The bad news," Rafael said. "Definitely the bad news. She once told my mother the harvest would fail and then ate the offering table."

**A reasonable allocation of resources.**

And there it was again, that laugh of Rafael's, startled out of her, and Ansel thought—not for the first time, but with new urgency—that she would burn down every remaining certainty she had to keep hearing it.

Which was, as it turned out, roughly the price of admission.

* * *

Emberling told them about the fourth sigil while the stars came out overhead, sharp and cold in the thin mountain air.

The sigil of ember was not hidden. That was the first surprise. It sat in plain sight, a stone the size of a fist resting in a hollow of the caldera floor not ten paces from the dragon's jaw, and it was dark. Not dormant—dark, the way a wick is dark, waiting. Ash had demanded they walk through what they regretted. Tide had taken payment. Bone had judged them. Ember, Emberling said, only asked one question:

**What will you give up willingly?**

"A memory," Ansel said. "That's the pattern. Tide took Rafael's mother's face. So ember takes—"

**Not so fast, little keeper.** The dragon's breath stirred the fallen scales around them into a slow silver whisper. **Tide took what was offered. Tide is patient and takes whatever grief is nearest, the way water finds the lowest ground. Ember is not water. Ember is fire. Fire does not take what is nearest. Fire takes what is given freely, wholly, knowing—and it burns until nothing of it remains.**

Ansel frowned. "Meaning?"

**Meaning the sigil of ember cannot be lit by a memory you would miss.** The coal-dark eye fixed on her. **Any fool can give up a memory they'd miss. That is only loss, and loss is common as dirt. To light the last sigil, you must give up a memory you cannot bear to lose. One you would trade the world to keep. And you must give it gladly.**

The wind moved through the crater. Somewhere far below, faint as a heartbeat, Ansel could feel the undercity humming through the mountain's bones—the dead oaths in the living roots, waiting.

"And if no one gives it?" Rafael asked. Her voice was steady. Too steady. Ansel had heard that steadiness before, in the market, in the cellar beneath the charm shop, every time Rafael Ishawa decided to be brave in advance of feeling anything about it.

**Then the fire goes out,** Emberling said simply. **And Hollow-Voice inherits everything that prayed to us. Every answered prayer becomes his answer. Every child with moonlit eyes becomes his eyes.** A pause, long enough for the scales to whisper twice around the caldera. **I have held the door for twelve years past my strength, beast-speaker. I am asking you not to make those years wasted.**

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the dragon's flank, because it was warm there, and because neither of them suggested leaving.

Ansel woke once in the deep of night and found Rafael sitting up, knees drawn to her chest, watching the dark shape of the sigil-stone catch the starlight. The other three sigils glowed faintly at her throat—ash a sullen red, tide a restless blue-green, bone a clean pale white—but the fourth was just a rock, and Rafael was looking at it the way Ansel had once looked at a letter bearing the covenant's seal, back when seals still meant safety.

"Go to sleep," Ansel murmured.

"In a minute."

"You said that an hour ago."

"I'm a liar with a schedule." But Rafael lay back down, and after a while her breathing slowed, and Ansel lay awake beside her listening to it and thinking about memories she could not bear to lose, and finding the list embarrassingly short and embarrassingly specific, and finally slept.

* * *

In the morning, Rafael was gone.

Not gone-gone. Her bedroll was still there, her pack still there, Sable—her rat, her familiar, her small gray shadow—sat on the folded blanket washing his face with an air of elaborate unconcern that Ansel had learned, over these weeks, to read as either innocence or complicity, with odds running heavily toward complicity.

"Sable," Ansel said. "Where is she?"

The rat paused mid-wash. Looked at her with his bright bead eyes. Said, in his dry little voice, "At the stone."

Ansel was moving before the sentence finished.

The sigil of ember sat in its hollow of volcanic glass, and Rafael knelt before it with both hands cupped around the dark stone, and she was talking to it—low, urgent, the way she talked to frightened animals—and the moment Ansel saw her face she knew, with the sick lurch of a stair missing in the dark, that she was too late.

"Rafael." Her own voice came out wrong. Thin. "Rafael, stop. Whatever you're doing, stop."

"It's fine." Rafael didn't turn around. "I've got it. I worked it out last night. It doesn't want a *kind* of memory, Ansel, it wants a *true* one, freely given, and I have one ready, and it's—it's fine—"

"What memory."

"It doesn't matter what memory."

"*What memory.*"

Rafael turned then. And Ansel saw it in her face before she said a word—the set of her jaw, the shine in her eyes she was fighting like a person bailing a boat, the terrible gentleness—and Ansel knew, knew the way you know a bone is broken before the pain arrives.

"No," Ansel said.

"It has to be something I can't bear to lose," Rafael said. "That's the requirement. That's the whole requirement. And there's exactly one thing in the world I can't bear to lose, and it's not my mother's face anymore, and it's not my gift, and it's not—" Her voice cracked and she pressed on through the crack like a cart through mud. "It's you. It's every version of you. The gate, and the market, and the cellar, and the roots, and the temple, and the catacombs, and the caldera, and every stupid joke about mules we don't have. That's the memory, Ansel. All of it. You."

The world went very quiet. Even the wind seemed to hold its breath. On the slope above, Sable had gone still, whiskers trembling.

"No," Ansel said again, and crossed the space between them in four strides and caught Rafael's wrists, and the sigils clinked together between them, ash and tide and bone, three-fifths of a miracle. "No. We'll find another way. There's always another way, I've had twelve years to learn every way there isn't—"

"There isn't one." Rafael's voice was soft now, and steady, the real kind of steady, the kind that comes after the fear has been felt all the way through. "You taught me that. In the temple, when you froze—you said guilt is just love with nowhere to go. Well. This is mine going somewhere."

"This isn't love, this is arithmetic—"

"This is the best trade I will ever make." And she smiled, and it was the smile from the market, from the luck-knots, from every small kindness she'd sold to frightened people while hiding the largest kindness of all inside herself. "You broke a covenant to save a city. I'm breaking my own heart to save everything. We're very symmetrical. Someone should write a ballad."

"Rafael—"

"Besides," she said, and her thumb moved, just slightly, against Ansel's wrist, "you'll tell me. Afterwards. You're annoyingly good at telling stories. You'll start at the beginning, and you'll be insufferable about the details, and I'll fall in love with you again because apparently I have terrible taste and excellent instincts." Her eyes were streaming now, freely, and she wasn't wiping them. "That's the deal. That's my price for the price. You promise me."

The sigil-stone was warming under Rafael's hands. Ansel could see it—the first thread of orange waking in the dark stone, hungry, waiting, wanting what Rafael had already decided to give.

"Don't," Ansel whispered. It was not an argument. It was not a plea, even. It was just the truest word she had.

"I'm not losing you," Rafael said. "I'm lending you to the future. And the future pays its debts." She lifted her chin toward the great gray shape of Emberling, whose eye had opened again, coal-bright, watching. "Dragon. Take it. Before I get sentimental and try to haggle."

**Gladly,** said Emberling, and the fire rose.

Later—though *later* is a strange country, and its borders shift—Ansel would remember it this way: Rafael's hands opening. The light going up like a struck match in a dark room. Rafael turning to her one last time with ash in her hair and tears on her face and saying, clear as a bell across the roaring caldera:

"Oh—*there* you are. Hello. Have we met?"

And the fourth sigil blazing in her palm like a small captive sunrise, and the mountain itself beginning, very slowly, to wake.
