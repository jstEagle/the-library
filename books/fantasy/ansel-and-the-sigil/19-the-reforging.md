## 19. The Reforging

**Ansel**

The mountain had a heartbeat.

Ansel felt it through her boots before she saw the caldera—a slow, arrhythmic thud beneath the rock, like something enormous turning over in its sleep and not quite managing it. Twelve years ago she had climbed this same slope with fire in her blood and certainty in her chest, and she had thought herself the most decisive person alive. Now she knew better. Certainty was just fear wearing armor.

Rafael climbed beside her, the four sigils wrapped in oilcloth against her back. Ash, tide, bone, and the last one—ember—still dark, still waiting for what it waited for. Ansel had tried three times on the road to ask her again if she understood the price. Three times Rafael had smiled that maddening, gentle smile and said, "Ask me after."

"After" was a luxury they might not have.

The wind at this altitude smelled of sulfur and old snow. Below them, the caldera opened like a cupped hand, and in its center, coiled around a spire of black glass, lay the dragon.

Emberling was smaller than Ansel remembered. That was the cruelty of it—the stories all spoke of dragons as mountains with wings, as storms given scales, but the truth was that a dragon was only as large as its fire, and Emberling's fire had been starving for twelve years. The great body was dim now, dull as banked coals, the ribs showing through skin like embers through ash. One eye opened as they approached. It was the color of a candle about to go out.

*You came,* Emberling said. The voice arrived not through the ears but somewhere behind the sternum, warm and vast and terribly tired. *The little beast-speaker and the oath-breaker. I had begun to think the world had forgotten how to keep appointments.*

"We're late," Ansel said. "I'm sorry."

*You are always sorry, keeper. It is nearly your defining feature.* The dragon's breath stirred the ash on the caldera floor into small ghosts. *But you came. That is more than the gods did.*

Rafael knelt at the edge of the great coiled body and set her palm flat against a scale the size of a dinner plate. "We brought them," she said softly. "All four."

*I know. I can smell them. Ash and tide and bone.* A pause, long enough that Ansel's stomach tightened. *And ember, which is not yet lit.*

"It needs—" Ansel began.

*I know what it needs.* The eye closed. Opened again. *I have known since before either of you were born. Fire that remembers requires a memory worth burning. There has never been any other way. Your mother knew it too, little speaker. Why do you think she built her bargain out of forgetting?*

Ansel looked at Rafael. Rafael was looking at the sigil of ember where it sat in its wrapping, and her face was doing something complicated and quiet, the way a lake does when something moves beneath it.

"Rafael," Ansel said carefully. "What did Sable give back to you? At the catacombs. You never told me."

"The memory I paid him with." Rafael's voice was light. Too light. "He didn't spend it. He said the predator-god fears beasts because beasts remember."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have time for." She stood, and began unwrapping the sigils, laying them out on the black glass spire in the old pattern—ash at the north, tide at the west, bone at the east, ember at the heart. Her hands were steady. Ansel hated how steady they were. Steady hands meant someone had decided something and wasn't going to be talked out of it, and Ansel had spent twelve years being talked out of nothing by no one, alone, and she recognized the posture like she recognized her own reflection.

Below them, far below, in the roots of the mountain, something laughed.

It was not a sound so much as a temperature—a cold that arrived inside the bones ahead of the ears. The ash on the caldera floor shivered. Emberling's remaining eye snapped wide open, and for the first time in twelve years, Ansel saw fear move across a dragon's face.

*It found us,* Emberling said. *Of course it did. Prayer always knows where prayer is answered.*

* * *

**Rafael**

Hollow-Voice rose out of the caldera's throat the way smoke rises out of a snuffed candle—not climbing, exactly, but *remembering upward*, taking shape from the dark the way a word takes shape from silence. It had stopped pretending to be beautiful. That was the worst part. In Corvane it had worn the faces of answered prayers; here, at the source, it wore nothing at all, just a hunger with edges, a mouth made of every wish anyone had ever whispered into a shrine and regretted.

*Rafael Ishikawa,* it said, and her name in its mouth sounded like a door opening onto a warm room. *Little seller of charms. Little hider of gifts. Look how far you've climbed. Look what you're about to do.*

She kept her hands moving. Sigil of ash to the north point. Tide to the west. Bone to the east. Her fingers wanted to shake and she did not let them, because her mother had taught her that charm-work was ninety percent confidence and ten percent string, and this was only a very large charm.

*You could stop,* Hollow-Voice said, gentler now, the way a hand is gentle on the back of a neck. *You know what the fourth sigil costs. I heard you learn it. I hear everything learned near my altars. Give me the covenant instead. Let me wear the old shape. I will make magic lawful tomorrow—I will make it *celebrated*. No more hiding your gift in market stalls. No more murmuring to gulls in alleys. Every beast in the world will speak your name aloud, and no Warden will ever come for you again.*

For one treacherous half-second, Rafael let herself see it: the shop with the shutters open, sunlight, a sign with her own name on it, legal and bright, Sable on the windowsill in full view of the street. Safety. Legitimacy. Everything she had folded up small and hidden for twelve years, unfolded at once.

Then she looked at Ansel.

Ansel stood between her and the rising dark with a broken oath-sword in her hands—the old covenant-blade, reforged from the shards she'd carried for twelve years like a penance—and her stance said everything her face was trying not to: *I will stand here until I am nothing, and I will not ask you to save me, because I already cost you enough.*

"No," Rafael said.

*No?*

"I've heard that offer before. From a rat. From a Warden. From my own mother, in a letter I wasn't supposed to find." She placed the third sigil. Her voice cracked and she let it crack, because hiding things was over, hiding things was *finished*, and there was a strange fierce joy in that even now. "Everyone offers me the safe small life. And everyone who offers it wants something eaten in exchange. So no. But thank you. Genuinely. It was a lovely offer. My mother would have been proud of the craftsmanship."

Something in the dark went very still.

Then Hollow-Voice screamed, and the mountain screamed with it.

* * *

**Ansel**

The attack came as answers.

That was the horror of fighting a god of answered prayers—it never struck first. It *responded*. Ansel swung the oath-sword at the advancing dark and the dark answered the swing, arriving behind her blade, folding around it like water around a stone. She called on the sigil of ash and the ash answered by going cold. She shouted for Rafael to run and the shout was answered by her own voice coming back to her from every direction, from the rock, from the sky, from inside her own skull, saying *run, run, run* until the words lost their meaning.

*This is what worship feels like,* Hollow-Voice observed, almost kindly, as it pressed her down against the black glass. *This is what your gods took from the faithful, all those centuries. Did you ever wonder why the old prayers felt so heavy?*

Ansel's knees hit the caldera floor. Through the chaos she could see Rafael at the spire, hands bleeding, singing the binding-cantos her mother had left hidden in the cellar wall—old words, true words, words that made the air itself remember—and she could see the dark reaching for her, patient as erosion.

And Ansel understood, with the terrible clarity that only arrives at the exact moment it is least useful, that she was going to die here, and that Rafael was going to pay the fourth price anyway, alone, and that twelve years ago she had shattered a world to save one city and was about to watch it happen again in miniature, helplessly, from the floor.

"No," she said out loud, to no one, to herself, to the girl she had been.

She got one knee under her. Then the other. The dark pressed down and she stood up inside it, which should not have been possible, and would not have been possible, except—

Except that beasts remember what gods were before worship.

Sable had told them that in the catacombs, whiskers twitching, utterly matter-of-fact, and Ansel had nodded along without understanding. She understood now. Worship was a leash humans put on power. Before worship, the powers had simply been *neighbors*—vast, dangerous, but bound by the same courtesy as anything else alive. Hollow-Voice was strong because it fed on wanting. But Ansel, in this moment, kneeling in the throat of a mountain, did not want anything.

She was done wanting. She had spent twelve years wanting absolution and it had gotten her nothing but appetite. What she had instead was older than wanting. It was a debt, freely carried. It was a promise she intended to keep whether or not anyone forgave her.

She raised the oath-sword—not to strike, but the way you raise a hand in greeting.

"I don't want anything from you," she told Hollow-Voice. "I'm just going to stop you. Those are different things. You wouldn't understand. You've never met anyone who meant it."

The dark hesitated.

And in that hesitation, high above, the dragon drew breath.

* * *

**Rafael**

Later—much later, in a life with a hole in it shaped like this moment—Rafael would remember the fire.

Not the heat. The *attention* of it. Emberling uncoiled from the caldera floor like a sentence finally finished, and the fire that came out of the old dragon was not destruction. It was recitation. It was every oath ever sworn in this place, every covenant-word spoken by every keeper across a thousand years, all of it stored in dragon-fire the way songs are stored in throats, released now in one final blazing verse. The walls of the caldera lit up with shapes—hands clasping hands, roots threading through stone, wings spread over cities—and Rafael, mid-canto, weeping without noticing, laid the fourth sigil on the spire's heart.

Ember. Unlit. Waiting.

*I will tell you what it costs one more time,* Emberling said, fire pouring past them both like a river deciding to flow uphill. *A memory, willingly given, that you cannot spare. Not a bad memory. A load-bearing one. The fire will take the thing that holds you up.*

"I know," Rafael said.

*Say what it is. Aloud. The fire must hear it named or it will choose badly, and the fire chooses like fire—fast and greedy.*

Rafael looked at Ansel—at Ansel standing unbowed inside the dark, sword raised, hair whipping in the furnace-wind, looking at her with an expression Rafael had spent two months learning to read and had maybe, possibly, almost learned to love.

She thought: *if I name it, I lose her before I finish losing her.*

She thought: *my mother named her bargain aloud too, and look what her silence cost.*

"Hold the line," Rafael called to Ansel, and her voice only broke a little. "Both hands. Don't let it reach the spire for thirty seconds."

"Thirty seconds," Ansel said, and grinned—actually grinned, wild and terrified and luminous—and turned back to the dark. "Rafael Ishikawa, whatever you're about to do—"

"Don't watch."

"That's the one request I'm going to refuse."

So Ansel watched, and Rafael spoke the name of the price aloud, quietly, into the roar:

"My memories of her. All of them. Every one. From the market to the gate to the cellar to tonight. Take the whole shape of her out of me—that's the memory that holds me up. That's the one worth burning."

The sigil of ember caught.

It did not catch like flame catches paper. It caught like dawn catches a valley—all at once and then gradually, light spreading through the black glass spire, down through the roots, out along lines Rafael suddenly realized had been there all along, running under Corvane, under the whole world, dead wires waking one after another after another. The covenant catching flame. Somewhere beneath the city, shrines that had been silent for twelve years shivered and hummed. Somewhere above, in a walled garden, a blind child's second moon blinked out like a lamp at bedtime.

Hollow-Voice shrieked—a real sound now, a cornered sound—as the answering-fire poured over it, and the god of answered prayers discovered the one prayer it could not survive: a wish made with no wanting in it at all, just a promise, kept.

Emberling blazed. For eleven seconds—Rafael counted, because counting was the last work her hands knew how to do—the dragon was everything the stories promised, a sun with wings, a storm given scales, and the caldera rang like a bell struck by morning.

On the twelfth second, the fire guttered, and settled, and went out.

And Rafael Ishikawa turned to look at the woman standing between her and the settling ash, sword lowered, ash in her hair, staring back at her with an expression of such careful, guarded hope that Rafael felt a pang of pure, inexplicable grief—

—for a stranger.

"Is it done?" Rafael asked. Her voice sounded strange to her. Light. Unburdened. Like a room after furniture has been carried out. "Did we do it?"

Ansel's face did something complicated and quiet, the way a lake does when something moves beneath it.

"Yes," Ansel said. "We did it."

"Oh good." Rafael looked down at her bleeding palms, at the four sigils fused now into a single ring of warm stone, and frowned slightly, the way one frowns at a word on the tip of the tongue. "I'm sorry—it's strange. I feel like I've forgotten something important. Like I left a kettle on, somewhere, years ago."

Across the caldera, in the deep quiet where a dragon used to be, something small and silver stirred in the ash, and began, weakly, to laugh.

"You have no idea," said Ansel Halloran, and sat down heavily on the black glass, and began, very softly, to cry.
