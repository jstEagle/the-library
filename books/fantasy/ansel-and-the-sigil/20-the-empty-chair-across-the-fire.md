## 20. The Empty Chair Across the Fire

The morning after the world was saved, Rafael woke up and could not remember why her pillow was damp.

She lay still for a while, listening to the sounds of Corvane waking—the cart wheels on cobbles, a gull screaming its opinion of the fish market, someone two floors down arguing cheerfully with a kettle. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a city that had not ended. She should have been grateful. She *was* grateful. It was just that gratitude sat strangely in her chest, like a coat that fit but belonged to someone else.

Her hands were bandaged. That was the first real clue. Not burned, exactly—marked. Thin lines traced across both palms in patterns she almost recognized, silver-pale against her skin, like scars from holding something very hot for a very long time because letting go was not an option.

She sat up. The room was unfamiliar, which was strange, because it was also unmistakably *hers*. Her mother's old charm-boxes lined the shelf. Her spare shawl hung on the peg. A chipped blue mug sat on the table beside a plate of bread and honey, cut neatly, the way you'd cut food for someone you were worried about.

And there was a rat asleep in her boot.

"Sable?" she said, and then blinked, because she had no idea how she knew that name. It arrived in her mouth fully formed, the way a sneeze arrives—no thought involved at all.

The rat opened one eye. Something moved across its small face that Rafael could only describe as careful.

"You're awake," Sable said. "Good. Don't get up too fast. Everyone says that after, and everyone does it anyway, and then they're sick on the floor."

"After what?"

Sable was quiet for a moment. In that moment Rafael noticed other things: the smell of woodsmoke baked so deep into her clothes it would never come out. The way the light through the window looked different—cleaner, somehow, as though the air itself had been washed. And beneath everything, faint as a pulse under skin, a hum she felt in her teeth rather than heard. Roots, she thought, absurdly. As if the whole city were growing somewhere beneath her, humming.

"The covenant," Sable said finally. "It holds. Magic is lawful. The Warden resigned—loudly, in the market square, which was the most entertaining thing I have ever witnessed, and I once watched a bishop fall into a fountain."

Rafael waited. She had the distinct feeling of standing at the edge of a hole in the ground, knowing it was deep, refusing to look down.

"And me?" she asked. "What did I do?"

"You did what was needed," Sable said. "That's all I'll say. The rest isn't mine to give back."

*Give back.* The words snagged on something. Rafael reached for them the way you reach for a stair in the dark that isn't where your foot expects it—and found nothing. Just smooth, empty dark, and a strange ache behind her eyes, like a muscle cramp from clenching something invisible for years.

"There's a person," she said slowly. "Isn't there. Someone I—" She pressed her hand flat over her sternum, where the ache lived. "There's a shape where someone used to be."

Sable looked at her for a long time. Rats are not built for pity; their faces don't have the architecture for it. But Rafael saw the rat's whiskers droop, and understood that this was the closest thing to weeping a rat could manage.

"Eat your bread," Sable said gently. "Honey helps. It doesn't fix anything, but honey never pretended to."

* * *

Corvane had changed overnight, or perhaps Rafael had changed and the city had simply stopped hiding.

Magic was lawful. She kept testing the sentence in her mouth like a coin bitten to check if it was gold. Children ran through the market with paper charms that actually worked—a boy's kite steered itself out of a chimney, to the boy's shrieking delight and the chimney-sweep's fury. An old woman sold weather-charms from a stall that would have gotten her arrested a month ago. Two guards stood outside the shrine district not to hunt practitioners but to keep the crowds orderly, looking profoundly bored, which Rafael decided was the most beautiful thing she'd ever seen a guard do.

At the shrines, people prayed. And the answers that came back were *quiet*. Small. A fisherman prayed for calm water and got calm water, ordinary and gray and safe. A mother prayed over a feverish child and by evening the fever broke the way fevers do, naturally, without a second moon blooming anywhere in anyone's eyes.

Rafael walked through all of it feeling like a ghost at her own funeral feast. People nodded to her in the street. Some of them bowed. A woman selling apples refused her coin with such ferocity that Rafael ended up leaving with four apples, half a loaf, and a sprig of dried lavender she hadn't asked for, while the woman called after her, "You know what you did! We all know!"—and Rafael smiled and thanked her and had absolutely no idea what she knew.

That was the shape of it, she was learning. Everyone in Corvane remembered except her. She was the one blank space in a city full of witnesses, and the witnesses were kind about it, which was somehow worse than if they'd been cruel. Kindness required explanations. Explanations required a story. And every time someone started to tell it, they'd glance past her shoulder at—

At nothing. At whoever happened to be standing there.

Except that it wasn't nothing. Because three times that day, three separate people had glanced past her shoulder, found whoever stood there, and gone silent in a particular way—not fearful, not reverent, but *careful*, the way you're careful around a wound that's healing.

The third time, Rafael turned around.

A woman stood at the edge of the market square, near the fountain. Tall, gray-cloaked, travel-worn, with the kind of face that had been handsome before grief got done renovating it. She was watching Rafael with an expression Rafael couldn't read at all—which was remarkable, because Rafael had spent her whole life reading faces. It was her trade, nearly. Fear, greed, longing, guilt: she could pick them out of a crowd like ripe fruit.

This woman's face held none of those. It held patience. Enormous, disciplined, bottomless patience, the kind that has made a home inside itself and furnished the rooms.

Their eyes met. The woman didn't wave. Didn't approach. She just lifted one hand—an acknowledgment, brief as a salute between soldiers—and turned away, and was gone into the crowd.

Rafael stood holding her four apples and her lavender and felt the ache behind her eyes bloom into something close to tears, and had no idea why.

"Honey," she muttered to herself. "I need more honey."

* * *

Her shop—her mother's shop, her shop—was exactly as she'd left it, apparently years ago, though it felt like yesterday. Charms in the window. Luck-knots, fever-wards, the little braided cords fishermen swore by. Dust on everything. On the counter, a ledger lay open, and Rafael flipped through it and found her own handwriting recording sales right up until some weeks ago, when the entries stopped mid-page.

On the last written page, under the final entry, someone else had written a note in a strong, slanted hand:

*Rafael — gone to the caldera. If you're reading this, either I failed to convince you to stay, or you failed to listen, and honestly it will be the second one. Whatever happens: thank you. For everything. Especially the things you won't remember giving.*

There was no signature. There was a small drawing instead, next to the unsigned note—a sigil, sketched quickly, of a flame inside a circle of ash.

Rafael stared at it until her vision blurred. Her hand rose without her permission and touched the page, fingertips resting just below the little flame, and the ache in her chest pulled so hard and so suddenly that she had to sit down on the stool behind the counter and breathe through it like a labor pain.

"I knew you," she said aloud, to the empty shop. "Didn't I. Whoever wrote this—I knew you."

From the shelf, Sable said, "Yes."

"Who?"

"That's not mine to tell."

"Everyone keeps saying that. Whose is it, then? Whose is any of it?"

Sable came down the shelf and sat on the ledger, directly on the unsigned note, which Rafael suspected was deliberate. "Yours," the rat said. "You gave it. Freely. Which means nobody gets to hand it back to you like change from a purchase—not me, not the city, not even the person who misses you most in this world. You want to know why I'm sure? Because I offered. Afterward. I offered to return what I took, and she said no."

"She." Rafael seized the pronoun like a drowning woman seizing driftwood. "Tell me about her."

"No."

"Sable—"

"Ask her yourself." The rat groomed one paw with maddening serenity. "She's easy to find. She's always nearby. She thinks I don't notice, but I am a rat, dear; noticing is my entire profession."

* * *

She began leaving the door open.

It wasn't a strategy, exactly—or maybe it was, in the way that all of Rafael's life had been strategies, charms and knots and hidden gifts, safety built out of small deceptions. This was the opposite. This was a door propped wide with a brick, morning to dusk, so that anyone passing could see in: a woman at a workbench, relearning her own craft, tying luck-knots with hands that remembered the motions even when she didn't remember learning them.

The woman in the gray cloak passed the shop eleven times over five days. Rafael counted.

On the eleventh pass, the woman stopped. Stood in the doorway with her hand on the frame, not entering, as if the doorway were a border she'd sworn not to cross.

"You're the one who wrote in my ledger," Rafael said. She kept her voice light, working-woman's voice, the voice she used on frightened customers. "Flame in a circle of ash. No signature."

"Yes," the woman said. Her voice was low and rough, like a road much traveled.

"What was your name? Before you signed nothing?"

Something flickered across the woman's face—pain, quickly governed, filed away by that same terrible discipline. "Ansel," she said. "My name is Ansel."

"Ansel," Rafael repeated, and the name landed in her chest and sank straight through, all the way down, into some cellar of her she hadn't known she had, and struck a spark off the floor there. Nothing came of it. No memory. Just the spark, and the dark, and the ache flaring bright and nameless, and to her horror her eyes filled and spilled over, right there at her own counter, in front of a total stranger.

"I'm sorry," Rafael said, dashing her cheek with her wrist. "I don't—that happens. I don't know why. Apparently I cry at names now. It's very undignified. Please forget you saw it."

Ansel's mouth did something complicated that wanted to be a smile and mostly managed it. "I'm good at forgetting," she said, and then winced, as if the joke had cost her. "No. I'm not. Forget I said that too."

They stood there. The kettle on the shop's little stove ticked as it cooled. Somewhere above, a gull screamed its endless editorial.

"Would you—" Rafael began, and stopped, and started again, because she had spent twelve years being afraid and she was tired of it, whatever the fear had been about. "There's a chair. Across the fire, in the back. My mother used to keep it for customers who stayed late. It's empty. It's been empty since I woke up, and I keep looking at it, and I don't know why, but I think maybe you do."

Ansel looked past her, into the back room, at the chair. Rafael watched her look at it and saw the patience strain against itself like a dog at a rope.

"Not yet," Ansel said quietly. "Not yet. But soon. I promise you, Rafael—soon." She stepped back from the doorway, out of the light. "For now—may I buy a luck-knot? Just one. I'll pay full price."

"It's the least I can do for a stranger," Rafael said, and heard, too late, how the words landed—saw them land, in the small collapse around Ansel's eyes—and reached across the counter before she could think better of it and caught the woman's hand.

The sigil-scars on their palms met. Ansel's matched hers exactly, line for line, twin marks from holding the same hot thing and refusing to let go.

Neither of them moved for a long moment.

"Full price," Ansel said at last, gently freeing her hand, laying coins on the counter, taking the knot. At the door she paused. "The honey helps, you know. With the ache. It doesn't fix anything."

"How did you know I—"

"Everyone knows," Ansel said. "It's Corvane's worst-kept secret that you take your bread with honey now. You never used to." And she was gone, gray cloak swallowed by the market crowd, before Rafael could ask what *used to* meant.

Rafael looked down at the coins. Full price, exact to the copper, and beneath them, folded small, a scrap of paper she hadn't seen Ansel place.

She unfolded it. Inside, in that same strong slanted hand, was a single line:

*Ask me about the gull. Start there.*

Rafael stood in her open doorway a long time, the paper trembling in her fingers, the empty chair waiting behind her in the firelight—and then went to find out what, in all the world, a gull had to do with anything.
