## 21. A Stranger Who Stays

The trouble with staying in a city where everyone remembered your crimes was that they remembered them *wrong*.

"Three coppers for the luck-knot," Ansel said, tying off the last loop of cord. "And no, before you ask, it won't make your wife forgive you. It'll make her *notice* you've been trying. The forgiveness part is your own work."

The fisherman—Bram, third visit this week—counted coins onto the stall with the gravity of a man paying a ransom. "The charm-witch said knots were useless."

"The charm-witch," Ansel said, "sells knots that work and lets people believe they don't, because believing they don't keeps the price honest. I sell knots that work and say so. Between us, we cover the whole market."

Bram grinned, pocketed his knot, and went off toward the harbor, and Ansel watched him go with the particular satisfaction of a woman who had once held the fate of two realms in her hands and now found equal meaning in a fisherman's marriage.

The stall was Rafael's. Had been Rafael's since before the covenant, since before the silence, since before everything. It sat in the long shadow of the old shrine district, wedged between a candlemaker and a woman who sold pickled eggs with the ferocity of a border garrison. The awning had been patched so many times it resembled a quilt that had survived a war. Ansel had offered—once, carefully—to buy better canvas. Rafael had looked at her the way she looked at everyone now: politely, kindly, with the flat friendly attention one gives a stranger on a coach.

"I like the patches," she'd said. "My mother put in the blue one."

And that was the whole of it, wasn't it. Rafael remembered her mother. Rafael remembered Sable, remembered the Warden, remembered the taste of every meal and the name of every street. She remembered the night the sky above the caldera burned white and the ground hummed with a covenant reborn.

She remembered everything except Ansel.

Ansel had given twelve years to the memory of what she'd broken. She could spare a few more to the memory of what she'd found.

* * *

The first week, she'd told herself she was staying for practical reasons. Magic was lawful now—lawful, taxed, licensed, and inspected by a Warden's office that had pivoted from hunting practitioners to regulating them with the enthusiasm of a bureaucrat handed an empire. Someone with actual covenant training ought to help. The charm-stalls of Corvane were full of well-meaning frauds selling knots that did nothing and wards that leaked. Ansel's conscience, which had been through a war and come out the other side with opinions, could not abide a leaking ward.

The second week, she'd admitted to herself that she was staying because Rafael laughed at her jokes. Not all of them. Maybe one in four. But a laugh from a woman who had forgotten you was a strange and specific grace—it meant the joke had to stand on its own. Ansel had spent a lifetime being forgiven for things she'd done. Being found funny by a stranger was somehow harder to earn.

The third week, she started telling stories.

Not all at once. That would have been cruelty dressed as honesty—here is everything you gave up, stranger, isn't it heavy, isn't it terrible, aren't you sorry now? No. Ansel told them the way you'd feed a fire in winter. One piece at a time. A stick, then a look at the flames, then another stick.

"There's a rat in the undercity," she mentioned one gray morning, sorting charms, "who once made me pay a memory for directions. Worst toll I ever paid. I still don't know what I gave up."

Rafael looked up from her thread. "A rat charged you a memory?"

"Sable. She's—well, she's Sable. You'd like her. You did like her." Ansel kept her eyes on the cord. "She has standards. Very high ones. She once refused payment from a bishop because she said his memories smelled of candle wax and cowardice."

Rafael laughed—one of the four—and something in Ansel's chest unclenched a fraction. "What did she smell on yours?"

"Regret, mostly. She said it was a common vintage."

* * *

The rhythm of it settled like sediment. Mornings, they opened the stall. Rafael handled the customers because customers trusted her—had always trusted her, would trust her until the sun burned out, that was simply a fact of Rafael the way gravity was a fact. Ansel handled the actual magic, now that magic could be handled in the open. She'd taught Rafael three wards the first month, and Rafael had learned them the way water learns a riverbed: completely, and as though she'd always known.

"You're a quick study," Ansel said, and immediately regretted it, because *of course she is*, she was a beast-speaker raised in hiding, she'd been studying her whole life.

"I had a good teacher," Rafael said. "My mother. And you're not bad either."

It was nothing. It was a compliment between colleagues. Ansel carried it home that evening like a coal in a cupped hand and set it down carefully among the others she was collecting: the time Rafael had saved her the last honey-bun without being asked. The time Rafael had hummed while working and the sound had made the charms on the stall rattle in sympathy, and Rafael had frowned at them and said, "That's new," and Ansel had said, "That's you," and Rafael had gone pink and said nothing, but hummed a little louder the next day, on purpose, watching the charms dance.

She didn't know why she hummed louder. Ansel knew. Ansel kept the knowledge folded away like a charm in a pocket, taken out only at night, in the rented room above the candlemaker's, where she would hold it up to the light of her little lamp and let herself feel the whole weight of it: *she is still herself. Everything they took, she is still herself.*

* * *

In the second month, Sable came back.

Ansel found her one dawn sitting on the stall's counter, tail wrapped around her feet like a judgment, regarding Ansel with eyes the color of old copper.

"You're still here," Sable said. "I gave it six months. I had a wager with a gull."

"Did you win?"

"I never win wagers involving your sentimentality. It's the one market where you consistently outperform." The rat groomed one paw, elaborately casual. "How is she?"

"She's well. She's—" Ansel searched. "She's *lighter*. Do you understand? Before, she carried it all like a woman carrying water uphill. Every day, the fear, the hiding, the weight of her family's secret. Now she carries nothing. She laughs in the street. She talks to pigeons openly and the pigeons talk back and the city guard just nods and walks on." Ansel's throat tightened. "I did the arithmetic a hundred ways, Sable. Every sum comes out the same. What she paid bought her a life better than the one she had. She just doesn't know the price."

Sable was quiet for a long moment. Rats, Ansel had learned, could be quiet in a way that filled a room.

"And you?" Sable said at last. "What do you carry?"

"The stories."

"Then tell them well." The rat hopped down, paused at the edge of the counter. "And Ansel. When she starts to remember—because she will, beasts remember in their bodies before their heads, and she's spent more time with beasts than with people—don't rush to the ending. Let her arrive at it herself. A memory you're handed is a stone. A memory you find is a door."

Then she was gone into the gutter-traffic of the morning, and Ansel stood in the cold with a rat's wisdom in her hands and no idea what to do with it except the obvious, which was to keep going.

* * *

So she kept going.

She told the story of the blind child with the second moon in his eyes, and how Rafael had stood in the shrine square with her fists clenched and said, "That's not an answer, that's a *mouth*," and how right she'd been. Rafael listened with her head tipped, the way she listened to everything now, and said, "I don't know why that makes me so angry. The child's fine now. He's in my prayers every week."

"Because you fought for him," Ansel said. "You just don't remember the fighting."

She told the story of the Warden, and how the Warden had once offered Rafael her freedom for a betrayal, and how Rafael had walked out of that office and straight into the market square and spoken with beasts in front of two hundred people, because some choices aren't choices at all, they're just the shape of who you are finally becoming visible. Rafael was quiet after that one. That evening she'd asked, out of nowhere, over the stew: "Do you think a person could be brave and not know it? Like—brave in a room with no witnesses, even from herself?"

"Yes," Ansel said. "I think that's the only kind of bravery that counts."

"Hm," said Rafael, and ladled more stew into Ansel's bowl, and Ansel understood that she had just been paid in a currency Rafael didn't know she was minting.

She told the story of Emberling, the last dragon, guttering in the caldera like a lamp out of oil. She told it carefully, because it was the door to the hardest room. She told about the fire that remembers, about the four sigils—ash and tide and bone and ember—and about the caldera's rim at dawn, and the god made of answered prayers coming down on them like a landslide with a voice.

And Rafael, stirring the coals, said: "I dream about the caldera. Not the fight. The quiet after. There's someone standing next to me in the dream and I can't see their face but I know—I *know*—that the quiet was the best moment of my life. Isn't that strange? The best moment, and I can't even tell you who was in it."

"No," Ansel said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "I don't think it's strange at all."

* * *

Winter came down off the mountains and settled over Corvane like a cat on a warm hearth, and the stall did brisk business in wards against frost and charms for travelers, and Ansel learned the small liturgy of Rafael's days. How she counted the till twice, always, and got a different number both times, and shrugged. How she fed the shrine-cats on the corner and scolded them for begging and gave them the best scraps. How she still, sometimes, in the middle of a sentence, would stop and look at Ansel with an expression like a woman trying to recall a word in another language—*there, on the tip of the soul*—and then shake her head and go on.

Ansel learned to love that expression. That was the part no one warned you about: that devotion could live in the gaps. That a person could become dearer to you in what they'd lost than in what they kept.

One night in the deep of the season, Rafael closed the stall early because of snow, and they walked back through the shrine district together, and the new lamps—the magic-bright ones, lawful now, humming softly on their posts—threw their shadows long and doubled on the snow.

"Tell me the one about the ash temple again," Rafael said. "The one where you froze."

"You've heard that one four times."

"I like it four times." Snow caught in her hair and didn't melt. "You stood in the burned temple and you couldn't move, and I—someone—put a hand on your shoulder and said, 'You don't have to be forgiven to be useful.'"

"You said that," Ansel said quietly. "You said, 'Ansel. You don't have to be forgiven to be useful. Forgiveness can catch up.'"

Rafael stopped walking.

The snow went on falling. The lamps hummed. And Rafael stood there with her breath making small ghosts in the air, and her face did the thing—the expression, the reaching, the word on the tip of the soul—and this time she didn't shake her head and go on.

"I said that," she repeated. Not a question. Not quite.

"You said that."

"And my name is—" Rafael pressed her palm flat against her own chest, as though checking for a heartbeat she'd misplaced. "When you said it just now. *Ansel.* My name. I felt it in my hand. Like—" she laughed, bewildered, a little frightened, a little alight— "like a knot I tied a long time ago and forgot, and my fingers still know how."

Ansel's heart was doing something undignified. She kept her voice level, mostly. "Sable says the body remembers first. Beasts remember in their bones before their heads. You've spent your whole life listening to beasts."

Rafael looked down at her open palm, at the snow landing in it, and slowly closed her fingers around nothing—around something.

"Ansel," she said, trying the name out loud, her own name, and shivered. "It's warm. Why is it warm?"

"Because," Ansel said, "you paid more for it than anyone will ever know. And things paid for at that price don't go cold. They just wait."

Rafael stood in the falling snow with her fist closed around a warmth that had no source, and looked at Ansel for a long, long moment—and Ansel, who had broken a covenant and reforged one, who had faced a hungry god and a dying dragon and her own worst self, stood there and was afraid of nothing so much as being asked a question.

"Tell me the story from the beginning," Rafael said at last. "Not the temple. Not the caldera. The *beginning*. The market. The gull. The luck-knot." Her eyes were bright and wet and certain. "Start where I started."

And Ansel smiled, and offered her arm against the snow, and began:

"It was a cold morning, and a fisherman came to your stall to buy a knot for his dying gull—"
