## 22. The Sigil in Her Palm

The morning the ash sigil grew warm in Rafael's palm was also the morning she burned the porridge.

These two facts were not related, probably. But Rafael Ishikawa had learned, over the past season, to pay attention to small wrongnesses—the way a fisherman watches a sky that has gone too quiet before a storm. So when the smell of scorched oats drifted up from the shop's little hearth and she found herself standing at the counter with no memory of having set the pot on at all, she did not panic. She simply noted it, rescued what remained of the porridge, and opened her palm.

The sigil sat there like a chip of night sky, black and faintly starred, no bigger than a thumbnail. It had been warm for weeks now—warm the way a stone is warm after a cat has slept on it—but today it *hummed*. Not sound, exactly. More like the feeling of standing near someone humming while they work: you don't hear the tune, but your chest picks it up anyway.

"You're doing something," she told it. "I'd like to know what."

The sigil, being a sigil, said nothing. But the warmth spread, up her wrist, into the hollow of her throat, and settled somewhere behind her sternum with the unmistakable weight of a word remembered by the body—a word whose meaning had been paid away, but whose shape still lived in her hands.

Rafael closed her fingers around it and breathed.

Outside the window, Corvane was waking up loud. That was the thing about the city now that still caught her off guard some mornings: the noise of it. Not just cart wheels and fishmongers, but voices raised without fear. A woman two doors down arguing cheerfully with her chimney-sweep familiar about soot deposits. Children on the corner teaching a hedgehog to roll over for coppers. Magic lawful. Beasts speaking in the open. The old gods answering prayers again—*properly* answering, with the careful gravity of something that had learned its lesson—and the shrines crowded every seventh day with people who wept not from hunger but from relief.

And through all of it, Rafael sold charms.

Not harmless charms anymore. Real ones. Luck-knots that actually bent luck, weather-bells that actually called rain, grief-tokens woven from gull feather and salt thread that let the bereaved say one true goodbye. Business was good. Better than good. She had hired a boy named Tam to mind the counter three days a week, mostly because he chattered constantly and it kept the shop from feeling like the inside of her own skull.

"Morning, miss!" Tam announced, bursting in with a basket of buns and his ferret, Duchess, riding his shoulder like a small entitled admiral. "Duchess says there's a queue forming already. Also she says you owe her a sardine from Tuesday, which I told her was slander, but she's holding a grudge."

"Tell Duchess I'll settle my debts at closing," Rafael said, "and tell the queue the luck-knots are two coppers, the weather-bells are five, and if anyone asks for a love charm again, the answer is still no, because love charms are *fraud*, Tam, and I have standards."

"She says your standards are why the shop smells like burnt oats."

"Tell Duchess—"

"I heard," said a voice from the doorway, dry as autumn paper. "Everyone in Corvane heard. The gulls are discussing it."

Ansel stood in the door with flour on her sleeves and that particular expression she wore—careful, watchful, kind at the edges where most people weren't looking. She carried a paper sack that smelled of fresh bread, because she always did on mornings like this, though Rafael could not have said what made a morning *like this*. Ansel simply appeared on them, bread in hand, as if she'd been waiting down the street for a sign only she could read.

"You're early," Rafael said.

"I'm punctual. You're late to your own life." Ansel set the sack on the counter, then paused, her eyes flicking—quick, almost involuntary—to Rafael's closed fist. To the sigil inside it. Something moved across her face, there and gone, like a fish under ice. "Warm again?"

"Warm again." Rafael opened her hand. The sigil lay in her palm, black and patient. "It's never done this before. The humming."

"No," Ansel said quietly. "I don't imagine it has."

There it was again—that fish under the ice. Rafael had learned to recognize it over these months, the way Ansel flinched from certain words, certain hours, certain songs the street musicians played. She flinched and then smoothed it over, every time, with the discipline of someone who had made a promise to herself and intended to keep it even if it killed her slowly.

Rafael had asked her once, months ago, over wine: *Why do you stay? We barely knew each other before the caldera. You could go anywhere now. Magic's lawful. Half the covenant-houses would take you back.*

Ansel had looked into her cup for a long time and said, *I'm where I'm needed,* which was an answer shaped exactly like a non-answer, and Rafael had let it go, because some part of her—the part behind her sternum, the part that hummed—had understood that pressing would be like pressing on a wound to see how deep it went.

"Sit," Rafael said now. "Have bread. Tell me—"

"—the story?" Ansel's mouth curved. "You've heard it eleven times."

"Twelve. And you always skip the middle. You always skip from 'we found the dragon' to 'we swore the covenant,' and I know, I *know* there's more in between, because nobody swears a world-saving covenant in a straight line. Nobody's life works like that." Rafael poured tea, pushed a cup across. "Besides. It's my favorite part. The part where you were terrible."

"I was never terrible."

"You shattered a covenant to save a city and doomed the dragons. That's at least *adjacent* to terrible."

"That," Ansel said, settling onto her stool with the sigh of the chronically put-upon, "is adjacent to *grieving*. There's a difference. Small one. Fine distinction. Ask any scholar." She tore the loaf, handed Rafael the heel the way she always did, and began, as she always began:

"Twelve years ago, there was a god in the harbor district. Not one of the old gods—a hunger wearing a god's name. It answered prayers, you see. That was how it fed. Every prayer it answered, it took a piece of the answerer. And it was growing, and the covenant-keepers were losing, and I—" here the pause, the flinch, smoothed over, "—I broke the pact between realms to starve it. Cut the line between the gods and the world. Silence instead of slaughter. I thought I was saving everyone."

"And instead you silenced the gods and doomed the dragons."

"And met a charm-seller who talked to rats," Ansel said, "who yelled at me in a cellar full of roots, which, for the record, no one had ever done before, and I found it very bracing."

"Someone had to."

"Someone did." Ansel turned her cup slowly on its saucer. Outside, Duchess could be heard extorting a sardine from a customer. "You want the middle, then. The part I skip."

"If you're offering."

"I'm offering." Ansel breathed out. "The middle is the ember sigil. The fourth one. It could only be lit by a willing sacrifice of memory, and Emberling's fire was guttering, and there wasn't time to explain, so you—" the pause stretched, longer than usual, long enough that Rafael's tea went cool in her hands, "—you volunteered without telling me what it would cost. And it cost everything. Every hour, every argument, every—" Her voice did something complicated and came back level. "It cost you *me*, Rafael. The covenant took our whole history as its fuel. You lit the fourth sigil with us. And when the fire caught and Hollow-Voice burned and the covenant held—you woke up on the caldera rim and looked at me like I was a stranger."

The shop was very quiet. Even Duchess, outside, had stopped mid-extortion.

"I do that sometimes," Rafael said carefully. "Look at you like a stranger."

"I know."

"It's not personal. There's just—when I look at you, there's a shape where a feeling should be. Like a word I can almost remember. My body knows you. My hands know you. I burned the porridge this morning because I was standing there listening to the sigil hum and thinking about you, and I couldn't have told you why, and that's—" She laughed, short and helpless. "That's maddening, Ansel. You tell me this story twelve times and every time it lands like hearing about someone else's life, except my chest hurts afterward, and I don't know why it hurts, and I *hate* not knowing why things hurt. I run a shop. Knowing why things hurt is half my trade."

Ansel was watching her with an expression Rafael had never quite seen before—not the careful mask, not the dry humor, but something raw underneath both, held very still, the way you hold still when a wild thing has come close and you don't want to frighten it.

"The Warden's clerks offered to restore it, you know," Rafael went on, because now that the dam had cracked she couldn't stop. "After the covenant. Memory-work is lawful now; there's a whole office for it. They said the covenant's records might hold an echo of what was taken. I said no."

"Why?" Ansel asked. Very softly.

"Because you asked me not to. You said—" Rafael frowned, reaching. "You said the price had been paid freely and unspending it would be theft. From whom, you wouldn't say. From me, I think. Or from you. Or from the covenant itself." She set down her cup. "But I'm tired of honoring a bargain I can't remember making. And this morning the sigil hummed, and my chest knew a word my head doesn't, and I thought—"

She stopped. Because her fist had been closed around the sigil this whole time, and it had grown warmer, warmer than stone-in-sun, warm as a living thing, and when she opened her hand the black chip of night sky was glowing at its heart with a single point of ember-light. Tiny. Steady. A coal refusing to go out.

"Oh," Rafael said.

"What is it?"

"It's showing me—" She squinted into the light, and the light showed her—not pictures, nothing so simple—just *feeling*, pouring upward through her arm like heat through iron. A market stall. Salt wind. A gull's last breath. A rat saying her name. A woman freezing in a burned temple, guilt coming off her like smoke. Laughter in an undercity of roots. Terror in a caldera. Love—love like a load-bearing wall, love that had held up a whole year of her life without her knowing, love that had paid for the sky.

The feelings had no faces attached. The memories themselves were gone; those had been spent, fairly, and she would not steal them back. But the *shape* of them remained, the way a riverbed remains when the river moves on, and the sigil was pouring water into the old bed and letting her feel, just for a moment, just as an echo, where everything used to flow.

Toward Ansel. All of it. Every channel of it, toward the woman sitting across the counter with flour on her sleeves, holding herself so carefully still.

"Rafael," Ansel said, and her voice had gone rough. "What's it doing?"

"Telling me the truth I already paid for." Rafael laughed, and it came out wet. "It can't give the memories back. They're spent—they're *in the sky*, Ansel, they're in the covenant, they're the reason the gods answer properly now. But it can tell me what they weighed." She looked up. "They weighed everything. Do you understand? Everything I felt for a year, and it all ran toward you, and I gave it up to save a world that outlawed me, and the world is saved, and the sky is *full of us*—"

"Rafael—"

"And I don't remember you." She said it plainly, because it deserved plainness. "I don't remember you, and I never will, unless someone spends another miracle on me, and I won't ask that. But I know this much: my body remembers you. My hands remember you. This morning I burned porridge thinking about you and I couldn't say why, and now I can: because some part of me has been waiting all year for you to stop keeping your distance and start telling me the truth with your whole face instead of in installments over tea."

Ansel's composure cracked—clean down the middle, like river ice in spring. "I promised myself I wouldn't ask anything of you. Nothing. You paid enough. I was going to just—stay. Sell charms beside you. Tell you the story until it was just a story. Grow old being your strange punctual friend with the bread."

"That's the loneliest thing I've ever heard."

"It had dignity."

"It had *misery* with good posture." Rafael reached across the counter and took Ansel's hand—floury, scarred, trembling slightly—and pressed the warm sigil into it, folding her fingers over. "Feel that? That's us. That's what's left after the fire. A coal. Not the blaze—we lost the blaze, I know, I know we did—but coals keep, Ansel. Coals wait. Coals are how every fire I've ever lit in that hearth got started." Her voice steadied. "So here's what I want. Not the memories back. Not the past. I want what you do with a coal."

Ansel looked down at their hands. When she spoke, her voice was barely above the hum of the sigil. "Which is?"

"You feed it. Carefully. Patiently. You build the fire small and slow and you don't rush it, and you let it become whatever it becomes." Rafael smiled—wobbly, terrified, certain. "Start again from the beginning. Not the story—I've got the story, twelve times over. *Us.* Introduce yourself. Badly, ideally; I want to know if you were awkward the first time too. Buy me a bun from Tam's basket and pretend it's a grand gesture. Walk me home along the seawall. Court me, Ansel Halloran-come-lately, like we've got all the time the covenant bought us. Because we do. That's the whole point of what we paid. We *do*."

For a long moment Ansel didn't move. Then she laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her, the first fully unguarded sound Rafael had ever heard her make—and pressed her free hand over her eyes.

"Twelve years ago I was awkward," she admitted, through her fingers. "Terribly. I knocked over a fruit stand trying to talk to you."

"*Did* you?"

"Duchess witnessed it. She's never let me forget it. Ask her sometime; she does impressions."

"I'm going to ask her *today*." Rafael was laughing too now, tears and all, and the sigil glowed between their joined hands like a small stubborn sunrise. "Go on, then. Beginning. Properly. Hello, stranger, I like your flour."

Ansel lifted her head. And there it was at last—no mask, no careful distance, just a woman looking at the person she loved, who did not yet remember loving her, and choosing—with her whole chest, with the coal-glow of a reforged covenant warming both their palms—to begin again anyway.

"Hello, stranger," Ansel said. "I like your shop. I hear the charm-seller talks to beasts. I've always thought that sounded like the bravest thing in the world."

Outside, the bells of the restored shrines rang the hour, and the old gods listened, and answered gently, and the sky—full of a year's worth of spent love, burning on—held.
