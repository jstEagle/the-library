## 1. The Date That Shouldn't Exist

The storm found her an hour past the last cairn, as storms in the Thornfells always did—suddenly, and with the particular malice of weather that had been waiting for someone specific.

Rosalind Lockridge pulled the mule off the switchback trail by its halter and squinted through the white. The pass was still three miles from the saddle, and the saddle was another two from shelter on the far side, and neither of those distances mattered now. Snow came sideways, thick as wool, erasing the trail behind her even as she stood on it. In twenty minutes there would be no trail at all. In forty there would be no Rosalind.

She knew these mountains the way other people knew their own hands. She knew which ledges held snow and which shed it, knew the smell of the air two hours before a whiteout, knew that the third cairn on this stretch had been rebuilt crooked by a shepherd named Aldous who'd died eleven years ago and whose sheep-paths she still used because dead men kept the best secrets. But knowing the mountain was not the same as arguing with it, and tonight the mountain was not interested in discussion.

So: the waystation.

Every smuggler who worked the Thornfell passes knew about the old Imperial waystations—the relay posts of an empire that had crumbled before her grandmother's grandmother drew breath. Most were rubble. A few had been claimed by goats or worse. And one, tucked into a fold of rock below the Widow's Tooth where no map marked it and no road reached it, still had a roof, half a door, and a chimney that drew if you knew which draft to block with your bedroll.

Rosalind had slept in it perhaps thirty times over nine years of runs. She had never once thought of it as anything but four walls and luck.

She got the mule under the broken lintel first, then herself, then spent ten minutes with numb fingers rigging the oilcloth across the doorway gap so the wind screamed against it instead of through it. The interior was black and smelled of cold stone and old smoke and, faintly, of the juniper branches some long-dead traveler had once burned here—a sweetness underneath the rot, like a memory the room couldn't quite let go of.

She didn't light a lamp. Lighting a lamp in a place you meant to leave before dawn was a habit for people who expected to be found. Instead she felt her way along the wall to the corner where she'd cached a blanket and a brick of pressed tea on her last run through, back in autumn, and found both exactly where she'd left them, wrapped in waxed cloth, untouched.

That was the whole architecture of her life, really. Things left carefully. Things found unchanged. A network of caches and false names and routes that existed nowhere but inside her skull, arranged so that if any single thread of it were cut, the rest would simply go on without her, unremarked. Nine years running freight for the Emberwright Guild through passes the guild's own caravans had forgotten how to cross. Nine years of being paid in coin that left no mark and lodging in places that kept no register. She could walk into any town between Thornfell and the coast and be no one within the hour.

People thought invisibility was a burden. Rosalind had built hers brick by brick, and she loved it the way you love a wall you've mortared yourself—with your hands, in cold weather, knowing every stone.

The mule stamped and blew warm air down her neck. "Yes," she murmured, working the pack straps loose. "I know. Supper."

She got the beast settled against the inner wall, out of the worst of the drafts, and dug the dried apples and grain from the pannier. While the mule ate, Rosalind ate too, sitting on her heels with her back to the hearthstone, listening to the storm try the oilcloth like a creditor at a door.

It was while she was chewing the last apple, idly scanning the room by the grey ghost-light that leaked through the roof gaps, that she noticed the hearth.

Not the hearth itself—she knew the hearth, a broad flat slab of granite with a flue above it choked by a century of bird nests. What she noticed was that the hearthstone sat wrong.

She'd have sworn it always sat flush. But flush stones don't show a seam, and there was a seam now, a hairline dark line tracing the slab's edge, visible only because the wind had shifted the snow-load on the roof and a thin blade of light fell across the floor at an angle it never reached by day.

Rosalind sat very still for a long moment, apple gone dry in her mouth.

Then she got up, knelt, and put her fingers into the seam.

The stone moved easier than it should have. It was not a hearthstone at all, or not only one—it was a lid, cut to sit over a cavity in the floor, and beneath it lay a space lined with lead sheeting, dry as a tomb, and in the space lay a book.

She told herself all the sensible things. Smugglers find things; that is most of what smuggling is. Someone had cached it and died, or cached it and meant to come back, and either way it wasn't hers and the smart move—the move she'd have lectured any green runner on—was to note the location, take nothing, and mention it to no one.

Her hands lifted the book out anyway.

It was heavy the way old things are heavy—not just with paper but with time, with everything that had happened around it while it sat still. Vellum covers, stiff as bark, scarred with water-stains. No clasp. No title. When she opened it, the smell rose up out of the pages like breath from a cellar: dust and tallow and something faintly metallic, like a key long kept in a hand.

The Vellum Codex.

She knew the name the way everyone raised within a hundred miles of the Spire knew it—a story mothers used to frighten children into wearing their amulets. *The book that writes itself. The book the Emberwrights guard. The book that knows when the fire fails.* She had assumed, in the vague way one assumes such things, that it was a story, and that the actual object, if it had ever existed, was locked behind guild doors in Lowmark, tended by the four or five grey-robed keepers who remained of an order that had once been an army.

And here it was, under a hearthstone in a ruin, in her hands, because a blade of stormlight had fallen at an angle it never reached by day.

*Later,* she would think. *Later I will understand why it was here, and I will hate that understanding.* But that night she only turned pages.

They were dense with script in a hand she didn't recognize—small, upright, unhurried, the writing of someone who believed every word mattered. Dates marched down the margins. She couldn't read the older entries; the language shifted partway through the book, sliding from the high imperial script into something closer to the trade-tongue, and the dates grew nearer, decade by decade, the way footsteps grow nearer in the dark.

She understood suddenly, with a lurch, what she was holding. Not a chronicle. A ledger of appointments.

Entries in the early pages spanned centuries apart. Then a century. Then fifty years. Then, near the end, the intervals collapsed entirely, and the dates crowded together like men pressing toward a gate.

The last written entry stopped three-quarters down the final page.

Below it, blank vellum waited.

Rosalind turned back to the front, hunting for a date she could check against something, anything—and found that the entries weren't dated by year alone. Each bore a full reckoning: season, moon, day. And the last completed entry, the one that ended mid-page with a stroke of ink that seemed almost reluctant, carried a reckoning she did know, because it was burned into every child in the borderlands alongside the fear of the Hollow:

*The night the fire guttered.* Ninety-nine years ago tomorrow, give or take what the calendar-menders had stolen since.

Which meant the next line—the one unwritten, the one waiting on the blank vellum below—belonged to a hundredth anniversary.

Belonged, in fact, to tomorrow.

She laughed. It came out wrong, a single dry note with no humor in it, swallowed by the wind. Because that was the thing about stories—they stayed stories right up until they didn't, and the moment they stopped being stories was always the moment you noticed your own hands shaking.

She made herself turn back to the final page. Made herself look at the blank space below the last entry.

The vellum was not blank.

The words were there—she was certain they had not been there when she'd first opened the book, certain with the certainty of a woman who had spent nine years noticing what changed in a room, because noticing was how smugglers stayed alive—but they were there now, pale as breath on glass, darkening even as she watched, ink surfacing through the vellum the way a body surfaces through dark water.

Small, upright, unhurried script. Tomorrow's reckoning.

And beneath the date, a description.

She read it standing, though her knees wanted otherwise. She read it twice, then a third time, moving her lips, because some part of her mind had decided that if she read it aloud it would become merely words, and words could be survived.

*A woman comes up from the south with a grey mule. She carries the ember and does not yet know she carries it. Her hair is dark, cut short for the hood. There is a scar at the left eyebrow, from a wire, from a fence she was climbing over in the year she was fourteen. She has been no one for nine years. She believes this keeps her safe.*

Rosalind's hand went to her eyebrow before she could stop it.

The scar was small—a white thread through the darker skin, a souvenir of a wire fence on a tenant farm she'd run away from at fourteen, a night she had never told anyone, not in nine years, not in the whole architecture of her careful invisible life. It was the kind of detail that lived only in her body. She had never spoken it. She had barely let herself think it.

The ink finished drying as she stared at it. Below the description, one more line surfaced, and this one was not a description at all.

*She shelters in the waystation below the Widow's Tooth, and finds this book, and reads this page.*

The wind hit the oilcloth hard enough to snap the rope on one side. Cold air poured in, carrying snow, carrying the smell of the high rocks, and Rosalind stood in the middle of the ruined waystation with a self-writing book open in her hands and the absolute, ice-water knowledge that she had just done the last thing the page said she would do—that she was doing it now, that the sentence was completing itself around her like a trap closing, and that somewhere in the storm outside, whatever had written those words might already know it had caught her.

She dropped the codex onto the hearthstone. Then, immediately, appalled at herself, picked it up again—because leaving it was impossible now; leaving it meant walking out into the world as a woman described in a prophecy, a woman who *found this book*, and the only defense she had ever owned was that no record of her existed anywhere.

Except one did now. One had written itself.

She wrapped the codex in the waxed cloth that had held her tea, shoved it deep into the bottom of her pack beneath the spare shirt and the coil of silk cord, and sat down against the wall with her knife drawn and her knees drawn up, watching the doorway, watching the oilcloth shudder, watching the storm.

Sleep did not come. Somewhere past midnight the wind eased, the way mountain winds do, dropping all at once like a held breath released, and in the sudden quiet she heard the mule breathing, and the drip of meltwater, and nothing else.

*She has been no one for nine years.*

That was the line that kept returning, the barb buried deepest. Not the ember—she didn't carry any ember, unless the guild's little brazier counted, and that was a legend, a coal said to be dragon-fire that the last Emberwrights fed and mourned in their empty hall in Lowmark. Not the scar, though the scar was worse, the scar was a violation. But *she has been no one for nine years*—that was the line that made her feel seen, truly seen, for the first time since she was a girl on a tenant farm deciding to disappear. Someone—or something—had been counting. Had known exactly how long. Had weighed her invisibility and found it a fact worth recording, a rung in a ladder of sentences leading somewhere she had not agreed to go.

Dawn came grey and thin. The storm had spent itself; the pass glittered, treacherous and beautiful, the world scrubbed clean and indifferent. Rosalind broke her cache's seal, took the blanket, left the tea brick and a coin—an old habit, payment for what she took, though she suspected the coin's future owner was already written down somewhere too.

At the doorway she stopped, pack on her shoulder, and looked back at the hearthstone lying open on the floor like a mouth.

Nine years of routes ran through her head, every fork and fallback. The southern trail to Lowmark was four days. The guildhall sat at the edge of town, grey and shrinking, kept alive by four old people and a brazier that was mostly ash and stubbornness. She had delivered to it forty times and never once asked what the Emberwrights actually guarded, because asking questions was how invisible people became visible.

The codex pressed against her spine through the pack's canvas, heavier than paper had any right to be.

If the book was a forgery—if some clever fraudster had planted it, playing a game whose shape she couldn't yet see—then the guild's scribes would know. Forgers left traces. And if it wasn't a forgery—

If it wasn't a forgery, then tomorrow had a date in it with her name implied, and the only people alive who might understand what that meant wore grey robes and tended a dying flame.

"Damn it," Rosalind said quietly, to the mule, to the mountains, to whoever had written her into a book.

The mule flicked an ear.

She stepped out into the blue morning shadow of the Widow's Tooth and turned south, toward Lowmark, toward the one place she had spent nine years making sure she entered unnoticed—carrying the one object in the world that made notice unavoidable.

Behind her, in the ruined waystation, the hearthstone lay open and empty, and the storm-wind moved through the broken roof, turning the first pages of nothing at all.

* * *

Two days later, on the ridge above Lowmark, Rosalind saw the smoke.

Lowmark's chimneys smoked in winter, of course—dozens of threads of woodsmoke rising straight in the still air. But this smoke was different: a single column from the guildhall's ancient stack, and it was burning black.

She had seen the Emberwrights' fire maybe six times in nine years, always at a distance, always at dusk: a thin grey ribbon, the smoke of a coal coaxed along on reverence and almost nothing else. Old Maren, the scribe, had once told her—unprompted, in that drifting way the very old speak—that the brazier's smoke had been white when the order was strong. *White for a living flame,* she'd said, looking at nothing. *Grey for a dying one. Black—*

Maren had never finished the sentence. Rosalind had never asked.

Now the column stood against the afternoon sky, black as a scratch of ink on vellum, and Rosalind stood on the ridge with the codex against her spine and understood that the book had not finished with her either.

She adjusted her hood, checked that her face was shadowed, and started down toward the town where she was no one, carrying the thing that said otherwise.
