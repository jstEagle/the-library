## 2. Emberwrights, Last of Their Name

The Emberwright guildhall had once been a fortress.

Rosalind had heard the stories all her life—how the walls had stood forty feet high, how dragon-fire burned in braziers at every corner and the whole valley could read by their glow on winter nights. Now what remained of it crouched at the mouth of Cindervale like an old dog that no one had the heart to drown: roofline sagging where two storms had argued over it, banners long since traded for firewood, the great iron gates rusted open because there was nothing left inside worth closing them against.

She came down off the ridge road an hour past midnight, codex wrapped in oilcloth against her back, and stopped where the light should have been.

Should have been. In her earliest memories of Thornfell—the ones from before her mother died, so few and half-dissolved she couldn't always tell if they were real—the guildhall had thrown light clear across the valley floor. She remembered, or thought she remembered, standing on her mother's shoulders in a crowd, watching fire climb out of a stone bowl as if it were alive and glad about it.

Tonight the hall showed exactly one flame. It sat behind the cracked shutters of the great hall's east window, thin as a candle seen through smoked glass, guttering every few breaths as though it kept forgetting itself.

Rosalind stood in the dark a long moment with her breath smoking in front of her.

*You're being ridiculous,* she told herself. *It's a brazier. They let it get low at night. That's all.*

But her hands wouldn't stop shaking, and it wasn't entirely the cold. The date was still written behind her eyes. *Tomorrow.* And beneath it, in ink gone brown as old blood: *the bearer comes down from the high passes, carrying what is left.*

She'd read it four times on the way down, holding the codex open by lightning-flash and moonlight, telling herself each time she'd imagined it—that some smuggler's joke had gotten bound into a century-old book, that someone had known her route, her face, her habit of coming down through the Widow's Stair when honest people took the low road. There were explanations. There were always explanations. She had built a whole life out of finding them.

None of the explanations explained the handwriting.

She didn't know whose it was, but she knew it wasn't a forgery's hand. Forgers hurry. That was the first thing you learned moving goods nobody official wanted moved—forgeries are made to be believed quickly, and they show their seams. This script had no seams. It was patient, disciplined, old-fashioned in its flourishes, and it had described her with the flat accuracy of someone reading from a list: *grey-eyed, storm-marked, carries the ember without knowing she carries it yet.*

Nobody knew she was grey-eyed. Nobody saw her face long enough to count colors in it.

The gates groaned when she pushed through. The courtyard smelled of wet ash and old stone, and somewhere under that, faintly, something else—a scent like a forge left banked too long, metal and heat remembering themselves. She crossed to the great hall doors, hesitated with her hand flat against the wood, and heard voices inside. Four voices, maybe five. Quiet ones. The voices of people who had learned not to speak loudly in an empty room.

She went in.

* * *

The great hall of the Emberwrights could seat three hundred at feast-table. Its rafters had once hung with standards of the sixty hearth-tenders' lines, each banner blessed by dragon-breath. Now a screen of patched timber had been dragged across the far third of it, boxing in a space hardly bigger than her mother's old kitchen, and everything beyond the screen was given over to dust and shadow and the small industrious sounds of mice.

Inside the box, four people sat around a brazier.

That was her first coherent thought: *four.* She had known the order was dying; everyone in Thornfell knew it, the way you know a neighbor is ill. But knowing a thing and seeing it were different animals. Sixty lines of hearth-tenders, an order that had kept the mountain passes warm and warded since before the province had a name, and here was what remained: four people, one brazier, and a fire so small it looked embarrassed to be burning.

An old woman sat closest to it, wrapped in three layers of shawl, a scribe's ink-stained fingers held out to the warmth. A heavyset man with a smith's burn-scars stood at a workbench behind her, hammering something with tremendous gentleness, each strike soft as an apology. Two younger ones—a man and a woman in matching grey cloaks—played at dice near the wall, though neither seemed interested in the throw. And above them all, on the dais where the Guildmaster's chair stood, a fifth figure sat apart, reading by the brazier-light.

Corvane.

He hadn't changed much in the six years since Rosalind had last delivered to him directly. Tall, spare, precise; silver at the temples worn like rank; hands folded on the ledger open before him as though the whole world were an account he was auditing. He looked up when the door opened, and his gaze found her in the dark of the doorway with the unhurried certainty of a man who was rarely surprised.

"Lockridge." Her name in his mouth sounded like an item being checked off. "You're late."

"The Widow's Stair was iced." She stayed by the door. Habit. "I brought your north-route receipts. And—" Her hand went to the oilcloth bundle at her back, almost of its own accord. "And something else."

"You'll want to warm first." He said it kindly, in the way of a man who had learned kindness as a professional tool. "Maren, make room for our courier."

The old woman in the shawls shifted along her bench, and Rosalind saw her properly for the first time. Maren. She knew the name the way everyone did—oldest scribe in the order, keeper of records nobody consulted anymore. Her eyes were pale and quick, and they went not to Rosalind's face but to the bundle on her back, and stayed there.

"That's a book," Maren said. Her voice was dry as the paper she worked. "Wrapped like contraband."

"It is contraband," Rosalind said. "Or it isn't anything. I don't know which."

She crossed the hall, unwound the oilcloth, and laid the Vellum Codex on the bench between the scribe and the fire. The leather cover, cracked and pale as frost-bitten skin, drank the brazier-light. The smith set down his hammer. The dice-players abandoned their game. Four faces and one reflected in Corvane's spectacles, all turned toward the book as if it might open itself.

"It was in the ruined waystation above Greywater Falls," Rosalind said. "The one that burned before I was born. Sealed under a floor slab, wrapped in wax cloth, dry as the day it was put there." She made herself keep going, level, factual—a smuggler's report, nothing more. "The last page bears tomorrow's date. Below the date is a passage describing—" She stopped. Started again. "Describing me."

Silence. One of the young ones laughed, a single nervous syllable, and choked it off.

Corvane reached across and drew the codex toward himself with two fingers, tilting it to the light the way a moneylender tilts a suspect coin. He turned pages. His expression never moved. Rosalind watched him arrive at the final leaf, watched his eyes travel the lines, watched him close the book with a soft clap of leather.

"A clever fake," he said.

"Guildmaster—" began the smith.

"Hear me." Corvane folded his hands again. "Vellum can be aged with smoke and buttermilk in a fortnight. Script can be antiqued with oak-gall and iron salts. Someone with a grudge—or a sense of humor—plants this in a ruin on a route our courier is known to use, knowing she'll bring it straight to us like a cat with a dead bird." He looked at Rosalind, and there was something in his face she couldn't parse—not mockery, something wearier. "You've done good work for this guild six years, Lockridge. Don't undo it chasing ghosts."

"I don't chase ghosts." Her voice came out harder than she intended. "I read. The page names me. Grey-eyed, storm-marked, coming down from the high passes tonight. Which is precisely what I did. If it's a forgery, then someone forged it before I ever decided on tonight's route—and I didn't decide until dusk."

"Then they guessed."

"Read it aloud," Maren said quietly.

Every head turned to her. The old scribe had not touched the codex. Her hands were still extended to the fire, but they had gone still, and Rosalind saw that her breathing had changed—slower, deeper, the breathing of someone bracing.

"No," said Corvane. "We don't perform superstitions in this hall. Not anymore. That practice ended, Maren, and you agreed it ended."

"I agreed it was expensive," Maren said. "That's a different word." She pulled her hands from the warmth and reached for the book at last, and Corvane—Rosalind marked this, stored it away in the cold ledgers of her own mind—Corvane let him let her, without protest, only watching. Maren turned to the final page and bent close, and for a long moment the only sound in the screened-off world was the guttering of the brazier and the tick of cooling metal from the smith's bench.

Then Maren began to shake.

It started in her hands. The fine tremor of age became something else, became a shudder that climbed her arms and reached her shoulders, and when she lifted her head her pale eyes were swimming.

"This is Elira's hand," she said.

The name landed in the hall like a dropped hammer. Rosalind felt it hit her somewhere beneath the ribs and go through.

"What did you say?" Her own voice sounded very far away.

"Elira's hand." Maren's thumb hovered over the page without touching it, the reverence of a woman who had spent her life handling things that mattered more than her comfort. "The letterforms. The way she looped her ascenders—I taught half the order's scribes and none of them wrote like this, because none of them *were* her. This is the Vellum Codex itself, lost these hundred years, and this page is in Elira Lockridge's writing. And—" she stopped, and looked up at Rosalind, and her face did something complicated and terrible with itself, some grief arriving decades late. "And you have her eyes."

"My mother died when I was six." The words came out flat, automatic, the answer to a question asked so often in childhood that the shape of it had worn smooth. "Fever. In the winter. There's a grave in Lowmarsh with her name on it."

"Yes," Maren said softly. "There is a grave in Lowmarsh."

"What does that mean? What—" Rosalind rounded on Corvane instead, because looking at Maren's face was suddenly unbearable. "You heard her. The codex is real. The prophecy—"

"The codex is real," Corvane agreed, and his voice was still calm, still audit-flat, and that was the moment Rosalind understood she was looking at a frightened man. Frightened men sound calm. It was the same tone he used on debtors. "Real, yes. Old, yes. And wrong, Lockridge. Dead wrong, the way all prophecies are wrong, eventually, because the thing they were written for has already happened." He rose from the Guildmaster's chair, and in the brazier-light his shadow climbed the patched screen and bent against the rafters. "Do you know why the Emberwrights are four souls and one fire? Because the dragons went silent three generations ago. Because the renewal rites have come and gone and the Hollow has stayed exactly where it always was—at the edge of maps, in miners' stories, nowhere real. Because we tend a brazier and call it the last dragon-fire because calling it that is all that keeps us from admitting we are a charity case with better costumes."

The words fell into the room and none of the other Emberwrights contradicted them. That was the worst part, Rosalind thought. The smith looking at his boots. The dice-players studying their knuckles. Believers who had already buried their belief and attended the funeral.

"The Hollow is real," Maren said. Very quietly. Not arguing. Reporting.

"The Hollow is a story we tell to justify coal deliveries," Corvane said. "I will grant that the book is genuine, Maren, since you vouch for the hand. Genuine books can hold mad prophecies. Elira herself—" and here he paused, just slightly, a hitch Rosalind would turn over a hundred times in the nights to come— "Elira was a fervent woman in a fading order. She wrote what she needed to believe. That doesn't obligate us to believe it after her."

"You knew my mother." Rosalind said it to his face. Not a question.

"Everyone in this hall knew Elira. She was Hearthkeeper of the First Flame." He gathered the ledger off the dais and closed it. "Which is why I know how much of her life was spent chasing exactly this kind of page. I'm sorry, Lockridge. Take the book if you want it. It's worth nothing and less. But I won't have this order spend its last strength on a dead woman's nightmares." He moved toward the door, then stopped beside her, and lowered his voice so only she could hear. "You look like her. That's a hard thing to be in this valley, and I'm sorry for it. But being someone's daughter is not a destiny. Go home. Sleep. Bring me receipts next week like a sensible woman."

He walked out. His footsteps faded down the cloister, even and unbothered, and the hall door thudded somewhere far away.

* * *

For a while nobody spoke. Then the smith—heavy, scarred, gentle—cleared his throat and said, "Well," and that broke something loose. One of the young ones dragged a stool over for Rosalind as if she'd been expected. Maren drew the codex into her lap and shielded it with both arms like a sleeping child.

"He's not wrong about the order," the smith said. "Four of us. The First Flame hasn't held a steady burn in nine years. We feed it everything the rites call for—rowan, salt, the old oils—and it eats and eats and stays hungry." He nodded at the brazier, and Rosalind looked at it properly for the first time.

Up close, the fire was wrong. She had seen a thousand fires, carried coals through three winters of passes in ways no sane courier would. Fire wanted to climb. This one lay low in its bowl like a coal wrapped in ash, orange at the heart, and every so often it flared a finger's width and dimmed again, rhythmic, almost—she searched for the word and disliked the one that came—almost like breathing. Like something vast trying to fit itself into too small a space, and failing, and trying again.

"Is it really dragon-fire?" she asked. "Truly?"

"No one alive has seen the true thing," Maren said. "This is what was saved. A spark carried out of the last forging, a hundred years ago, when the Spire went cold." She smoothed the codex's cover with a thumb. "They called the ones who carried it the Last Watch. There were seven. This bowl is theirs."

"And now there's four of you tending it."

"Now there is four of us," Maren agreed. "And tomorrow's date is written in a dead woman's hand, saying a bearer comes down from the high passes." She looked up, and her pale eyes held Rosalind's without mercy and without cruelty. "You could walk away, girl. Corvane would be relieved. Most nights, I'd be relieved too. Clinging to a dying purpose is easier when nobody brings it news."

"Why didn't you?" Rosalind asked. "Walk away, I mean. All of you."

The smith answered first, with a shrug that meant *where else would I go.* The young ones glanced at each other. But Maren kept looking at Rosalind, and after a moment she said, "Because the fire answers the rites. Dimly. Badly—but it answers. A thing that answers is a thing that's listening. And a thing that's listening is waiting."

She opened the codex again, to the last page, and turned it around so the brazier-light fell across the brown ink. Tomorrow's date. The description. And below it, in the same patient hand, a line Rosalind had read four times on the mountain and still couldn't make mean anything but dread:

*The bearer comes down from the high passes, carrying what is left.*

"Carrying what is left," Maren repeated. Her eyes went to the brazier. To the codex. Back to Rosalind. "Of what, Elira never says. Of the fire. Of the covenant. Of time, perhaps." She closed the book gently. "There's a village at the mountain's edge, girl—Hollowbrook, they call it, poor joke of a name. It sits on the road to the Spire. My knees won't carry me there until morning, but if that page means what I'm afraid it means—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Rosalind had spent six years learning to read the things people didn't say; it was practically her trade.

"If the bearer's coming," Rosalind said slowly, "then something must be coming to meet her."

Maren nodded once, and the fire behind her guttered, and breathed, and guttered again, and far off beyond the hall walls—so far off it might have been wind, but none of them believed it was wind—something in the high passes cried out with a voice that had no throat.
