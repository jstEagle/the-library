## 3. What the Hollow Leaves

Maren had copied the Vellum Codex three times in her life, and each time her hand had shaken.

The first was as an apprentice, forty years ago, when Guildmaster Ondrell had ordered fresh copies made against the day the original crumbled to dust. The second was for the archive at Lowmark, though she'd never learned whether it arrived—the courier vanished somewhere past the Salt Road, and no one had gone looking. The third time was last night, by candlelight, with Rosalind Lockridge asleep on a bench across the guildhall and the codex open between Maren's palms like something freshly wounded.

She hadn't slept. She doubted she would again soon.

The handwriting was Elira's.

Maren had known that within a breath of seeing the final page, and knowing it had done something to her chest that felt like a door closing. She had spent twenty-five years telling herself the woman's death was clean—a fever, a fall, one of the small anonymous ends the mountains handed out without malice. She had told herself that story so often it had worn smooth as river stone. And now here was Elira's hand again, slanting and sure, recording a date that had not yet come and a description of a girl who sat sleeping ten feet away, smelling faintly of road dust and pine tar, with a smuggler's calluses on hands that were supposed to belong to a scribe's prophecy.

Maren set down her pen. The brazier in the center of the hall guttered, and she watched it the way she watched it every hour of every day—with the particular terror of a woman guarding the last coal of a fire that had once warmed the world.

*It holds,* she told herself. *It still holds.*

But it held the way a dying man holds a conversation: shorter each time, with longer silences between.

Footsteps on the stair. Not Rosalind—she moved like smoke, you never heard her unless she wanted to be heard. These steps were heavy and deliberate, and Maren closed the codex before Corvane reached the bottom of the stairs, an old reflex, though she wasn't sure anymore why she bothered hiding things from him. Perhaps because hiding things from Corvane had become, over the years, the only honest work left in the Emberwright Guild.

"You're up early." He crossed the hall, gray-bearded, immaculate even at dawn, his guildmaster's chain polished to a dull shine. His eyes went to the covered codex and stayed there a half-second too long. "Still chasing ghosts?"

"I'm reading," Maren said. "It's what scribes do."

"What I do," Corvane said, "is keep four old people fed and a roof on this hall, and I don't need a smuggler's fairy stories complicating that." He said it lightly, but Maren had known him thirty years and could hear the wire under the silk. "The girl leaves today. Give her back whatever trinket she brought us and let her go."

"She brought us the Vellum Codex."

"She brought us a book." He turned toward the brazier, held his hands over its warmth the way a man warms himself at any ordinary fire, and Maren felt something cold move through her that had nothing to do with drafts. Thirty years, and he still warmed his hands at it like it was kindling and oak. "A book some forger aged in a cheese cellar. You've seen the tricks, Maren. Tea stains. Ash rub. There's always someone selling the past to fools."

"Yes," Maren said quietly. "There always is."

He looked at her then, and for a moment—just a moment—something moved behind his face that she couldn't name. Fear, maybe. Or the memory of fear, worn down to habit. Then he smiled his guildmaster's smile and climbed back up the stairs, and Maren sat alone with the codex and the ember and the terrible arithmetic of what she knew.

Because here was the thing Maren had never written down, not once in forty years, not in any copy, not in any margin: the covenant did not fail all at once. It failed the way a wall fails. A crack here. A stone there. And someone, sometime in the last hundred years, had been selling the stones.

* * *

Rosalind woke the way Maren imagined wolves woke—eyes open before the body finished moving, hand already finding the knife under the bench. When she saw Maren, she settled, but only halfway. That was fair. Maren hadn't earned more than halfway from anyone in decades.

"The handwriting," Rosalind said, without greeting. "You recognized it yesterday. Before you said anything. I watched your face."

Maren poured tea from the pot she'd kept warm. Her hands were steadier than they had any right to be; age had at least given her that, a performer's calm over a collapsing stage. "Sit down, child."

"I've been sitting down my whole life. Sitting is how you stay unseen." But she sat.

Maren opened the codex to the final page and turned it toward her. "Read it aloud."

Rosalind read. Her voice was flat, controlled, a smuggler reading a manifest. *"On the eve of the third moon's darkening, the Covenant-Bearer comes down out of Thornfell bearing the ember unlit. She is narrow through the shoulder and quick of step. Her eyes are the grey of slate after rain. She carries nothing she has not stolen and loves nothing she has not lost. Daughter of the forge-mother. Last of the line. Let her be found."*

Silence. The brazier ticked as the coals shifted.

"Say the last part again."

"*Daughter of the forge-mother.*" Rosalind's voice cracked on the second word, just slightly, and she hated it—you could see her hating it—and Maren's heart broke a little more. "What is a forge-mother?"

"It's a title," Maren said. "One title. Held once." She folded her hands around her tea to keep them honest. "And before I tell you what it means, I'm going to tell you a story, and you're going to want to interrupt me, and I'd take it as a kindness if you didn't. I've been rehearsing this story for twenty-five years and I only get to tell it once."

Rosalind said nothing. Slate-after-rain eyes, watching.

"The world used to have dragons," Maren began. "Not the bones in the museum halls, not the wyverns the southern lords hunt for sport. Dragons. Fire that thought. And a thousand years ago—or near enough, the numbers stop mattering—the thing we call the Hollow pressed through from wherever it presses from. Between realms, the old texts say. A hunger with no body and no bottom. It doesn't conquer. Conquering implies wanting something. It *consumes*. Light, warmth, memory, name. It eats the world the way rot eats fruit—from inside, quietly, until the skin gives way."

"And the dragons fought it."

"The dragons burned it back. But fire that thinks can spend itself, and they were spending. So the last of them made a bargain with the mortal realms. Their fire, given freely into keeping—into hearths, into braziers, into the Spire at the top of the world—and in exchange, kept alive. Tended. Renewed. Because dragon-fire isn't fuel, child. It's a promise, and promises die if no one keeps saying them." Maren nodded at the brazier. "That is what we are. Not priests. Not soldiers. Keepers of a sentence spoken a thousand years ago, renewed at the Spire every hundred years, or the sentence ends and the Hollow hears the silence."

Rosalind was quiet a long moment. "How many renewals have happened?"

"Nine."

"And the tenth?"

Maren met her eyes. "Was due eleven years ago."

The silence that followed had weight. Rosalind looked at the brazier—at the little guttering flame the Emberwrights had tended through her whole lifetime—and Maren watched her do the math, watch her arrive at the same terrible sum Maren had been carrying since the year the renewal should have come and no one had gone.

"Eleven years," Rosalind said. "And the world hasn't ended."

"No. Because the wall doesn't fail all at once." Maren rose and went to the hall's eastern window, the one that faced the high passes, and pulled back the shutter. Grey morning light fell across the floorboards. "Look north. What do you see?"

"Mountains. Snow. The Thornfells."

"Now look at the village at their edge. Fenlow. Can you see the smoke?"

Rosalind leaned forward. Maren watched her scan the valley, professional and quick, the way she'd read terrain her whole life—and then watched her go very still.

"There's no smoke," Rosalind said. "Fenlow burns peat. Even in summer there's smoke off Fenlow. There's always—" She stopped. "When did you last see smoke?"

"Three days ago."

* * *

They took the low road because Rosalind refused the high one—*I know every path in these mountains and the low road's fastest to Fenlow*, and Maren didn't argue, because arguing with a smuggler about paths was like arguing with a fish about water. They walked through morning mist with the codex wrapped in oilcloth against Maren's chest, and Rosalind moved ahead the way she must have moved her entire life: silent, watchful, reading the world for threats the rest of them couldn't see.

Maren studied her back and grieved, quietly, in advance.

At midday the mist burned off and Fenlow came into view, and Maren heard Rosalind stop breathing.

The village sat in its fold of the hills exactly as it always had—thatched roofs, stone walls, the mill wheel turning lazily in the stream because no one had thought to stop it. Doors stood open. All of them. Every door on every house, swung wide to the grey afternoon like the village had opened its mouth and forgotten how to close it.

"No crows," Rosalind said softly. "No dogs. Where are the dogs?"

They went in through the western lane. The first house was the tanner's—Maren knew him slightly, a broad cheerful man named Hesk who'd bought candle-tallow from the guild for thirty years. His door stood open. Inside, bread sat on the table, cut, half a loaf with the knife still standing upright in the crumb. Two bowls of porridge, one spoon laid down mid-stroke, the other still resting in the bowl. A chair pushed back at an angle that suggested the person who'd left it had done so quickly, but not violently. No blood. No struggle. No sign of anything at all except absence, total and recent and wrong.

"Hesk?" Maren called, and hated how small her voice sounded. "Hesk, it's Maren of the Emberwrights!"

Nothing answered. Not even echo. Sound seemed to land in the village and die there.

Rosalind moved through the house like water, checking corners, the loft, the yard. She came back shaking her head. In the second house, a cradle. Empty, blankets thrown back, a wooden bird toy on the floor beside it—as if it had been dropped, or set down, or simply ceased to matter. In the third, a man's boots by the fire, still warm when Rosalind touched them.

"Warm," she said, and her voice had finally lost its flatness. "Maren. The boots are *warm*."

"I know."

"People don't leave warm boots. People don't leave bread cut. People don't—" She stopped in the middle of the lane, turned a slow circle, and Maren saw something she had never seen on the smuggler's face: naked incomprehension. "There's no pattern. No direction. They didn't flee anywhere—they're just *gone*, like someone lifted them out of—"

"Out of the page," Maren said.

Rosalind stared at her.

"That's what the Hollow does. It isn't a beast, child. It doesn't carry anyone off. It *unwrites*. It takes the warmth first, then the memory, then the name, and by the end there's nothing left to bury because there was never anything there to begin with." Maren's voice stayed level, which cost her everything she had. "These people aren't dead. Dead would be a mercy with edges on it. These people have been *edited*."

"How do you know? How could you possibly know that?"

"Because I've seen it before." Maren walked to the well at the center of the village square, and there she stopped, because this was the thing she'd been walking toward all day, the thing she'd known she'd find, the way you know a scar is still there under the sleeve. "Eleven years ago. A shepherd named Aldric, up past the shrine at Coldmere. He came to the guildhall raving about lights in the high snow and voices offering him things. We thought him mad. A week later his whole steading was like this—doors open, meals half-eaten, and not one soul in the valley remembered he'd ever existed except me, because I'd spoken with him and I wrote his name down." She touched the well's rim. "I wrote his name down, and that's the only reason anyone knows there was ever an Aldric at all."

Rosalind came and stood beside her. Below them, the well shaft dropped into darkness, and the darkness had a quality Maren didn't like—a depth that seemed deeper than the stones allowed, a blackness that wasn't empty but *attentive*.

"The codex says the renewal failed," Rosalind said slowly. "Eleven years ago. And now this. You think it's starting. The eating."

"I think the wall has cracks, and the cracks are spreading, and the only mason is a girl who reads prophecies about herself and doesn't believe in them yet." Maren turned to face her fully. "So believe this instead. Whatever you decide about the codex, about the forge-mother, about the daughter-of-the-line—that flame in our guildhall is the last lit coal of a fire that stands between everything you've ever known and that." She pointed down the well. "And it is going out. Faster every month. I've measured it. I'm the only one who measures it, because measuring it means admitting it, and admitting it means someone has to *do* something, and no one will, because doing something means walking into those mountains toward the Spire, and everyone who's ever talked about the Spire talks about it the way sailors talk about the deep water."

Rosalind looked down the well for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was almost gentle, which was somehow worse than anger.

"Maren. Look around you. This village was fine three days ago. If the Hollow is loose—if the fire is failing—then the Emberwrights have known for eleven years and done nothing. Four old people guarding a brazier and lying to themselves." She shook her head. "I've carried contraband past border wardens my whole life. I know what it looks like when an institution is rotten. It looks like excuses. It looks like *we meant to*."

"Yes," Maren said. "It does."

Something in her tone made Rosalind stop. Turn. Search her face.

"Maren. What aren't you telling me?"

And Maren—who had rehearsed this story for twenty-five years, who had told herself she'd confess everything the moment the right listener appeared, who had watched Corvane climb the stairs that morning warming his hands at the last fire in the world like a man warming himself at any ordinary flame—Maren opened her mouth to answer.

From up the valley, thin and high, came a sound.

Singing. A single voice, wordless, sweet, drifting down from the tree line above Fenlow where no living soul remained. It sang a melody Maren hadn't heard in twenty-five years, a lullaby, slow and simple, the kind a mother sings to a child at dusk—

—and beside her, Rosalind Lockridge went white as the snow on the high passes, and whispered:

"I know that song."
