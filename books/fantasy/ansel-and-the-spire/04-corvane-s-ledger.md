## 4. Corvane's Ledger

The guildhall kept its records the way a miser kept coins: hoarded, hidden, and counted twice. Rosalind had learned that in her first year, when she'd needed proof of delivery for a shipment of ember-glass and discovered the archive room behind the map closet, behind a door disguised as shelving, behind a lock so old it opened to a hairpin and patience.

She went there now, in the hour before dawn, with the Vellum Codex wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like something stolen.

Which, she supposed, it was.

Corvane had dismissed the book over supper with a wave of his hand—*a forgery, girl, a confection of ink and wishful thinking*—but Rosalind had watched his eyes while he said it. She'd spent fifteen years reading faces across smuggling tables, learning which smiles hid knives, which yawns masked fear. Corvane's dismissal had been too smooth. Too quick. A man who truly believed a book was worthless did not look at it the way he'd looked at it: not with scorn, but with *recognition*. The way a man looks at a debt he thought long buried.

And Maren—old Maren, half-blind Maren, who could no longer thread a needle but could still read a hand at forty paces—had gone white as tallow when she touched the codex's final page.

"That's Elira's script," she'd whispered. "Or I'm a merchant's wife."

Elira. The name meant nothing to Rosalind. But Maren had pressed her lips shut after that, glancing at Corvane, and said nothing more all evening.

So Rosalind did what she always did when answers wouldn't come to her. She went looking.

The archive room smelled of dust and lamp oil and the ghost of tallow candles burned down decades ago. Shelves climbed the walls into darkness, crammed with ledgers bound in cracked leather, route-maps rolled and tied with string, delivery manifests going back further than the guild's current membership could remember. A single high window let in the last grey of night. Rosalind lit the lamp on the reading desk, set the codex down beside it, and began.

If the codex was genuine—if its prophecies were real—then someone had written them. And if Maren recognized the hand, then whoever wrote them had been here, in this hall, close enough for an old scribe to know their letters by heart. Which meant the codex's history lived somewhere in these shelves. Guilds recorded everything. Who donated what, who bequeathed whom, which volumes passed through which hands. Paper remembered what people preferred to forget.

She started with the acquisition ledgers, working backward through the years. Most entries were dull as gravel: *Two crates pitch-resin, received from Lowmark, condition fair.* *Payment rendered to ferrymen, Thorn River crossing.* But guild bookkeeping had one virtue—it was obsessive about dates. Every entry anchored to a day, a season, a year.

And every hundred years, if Maren's trembling hands and hollow voice were to be believed, the Emberwrights carried dragon-fire up the mountain to the Spire and renewed the covenant that held back the Hollow.

The last renewal should have happened ninety-nine years ago.

Rosalind found the year easily enough once she knew to look for it. The ledgers from that era were thinner than the others, the entries sparser, the handwriting of the guild-scribes growing shakier toward the end of each volume—as though fewer people remained to hold the pen. She pulled the volume for the renewal year itself and set it under the lamp.

The pages smelled of mildew and old smoke. She turned them slowly, scanning. Deliveries of charcoal. Wages paid to hearth-tenders. A note about a roof repair. Ordinary life, recorded in ordinary ink, in the months before the covenant was supposed to be renewed.

Then, three-quarters through the volume, the entries stopped.

Not tapered. Not thinned. *Stopped*, mid-page, between a record of lamp-oil purchased and a blank space where the next entry should have been. The remaining pages of the ledger were empty. Someone had closed this book and never opened it again.

Rosalind sat back. Her heart was beating oddly, slow and heavy, like footsteps on stairs.

Guilds didn't stop keeping books. Even dying guilds—even guilds down to their last four members, huddled around a guttering brazier—kept books. Keeping books was how you proved you still existed. The Emberwrights' whole creed was continuity: fire passed hand to hand, record passed page to page. An abandoned ledger wasn't neglect.

It was surrender. Or silencing.

She checked the next volume, the year after. Entries resumed—but different. New handwriting. New format. And the first entry in the new book made her blood run cold:

*Received, from the estate of the late Guildmaster Halloway: one iron strongbox, contents unlisted.*

Halloway. The name surfaced from guild lore like a drowned thing. Every Emberwright learned the old stories eventually, usually from Maren, told in the low voice she saved for graves: how the guild had once numbered in the hundreds; how the renewal of ninety-nine years ago had never come—the bearer had died on the road, or turned back, or simply never existed, the accounts disagreed—and how the Hollow had crept a little closer into the world ever since, and the guild had shrunk with it, generation by generation, fire guttering as the pact starved.

Guildmaster Halloway had been the last master of the old guild. He had died the same winter the ledger stopped.

Rosalind turned back through the thinning pages, faster now, hunting. And there—two-thirds through the final active year, wedged between a delivery of candlewax and a wage payment—she found an entry that did not belong.

*Disbursement authorized by G. Halloway, Guildmaster. Sum: forty crowns gold. Recipient: [blank]. Purpose: [blank].*

Forty crowns was a fortune. Forty crowns was a year's keep for the entire hall. And both recipient and purpose had been struck through—not blotted, not faded, but deliberately scratched out with a blade or a sharp nib, the paper nearly torn with the force of it.

Someone had wanted this entry unread.

Rosalind's mouth had gone dry. She copied the date onto a scrap of paper with fingers that weren't quite steady, then reached for the following volume—the first of the new era, the one begun after Halloway's death, the one whose first entry mentioned his strongbox.

The new hand was neat, controlled, confident. It belonged, according to the flyleaf inscription, to *A. Corvane, Keeper of Records.*

Corvane. Her guildmaster. The man who had waved away the codex at supper and called it forgery before anyone else had even touched it.

Rosalind told herself it meant nothing. Names repeated. Hands changed. A young Corvane might have inherited a mess and done his best with it. She turned the pages of his early ledger with the deliberate calm of someone walking a plank, telling herself the plank would hold.

It held for six years of entries. Charcoal. Oil. Wages. Repairs. Then, in the seventh year of Corvane's record-keeping, another disbursement appeared:

*Sum: twenty crowns. Recipient: withheld per instruction of buyer. Purpose: continuation of arrangement previously established.*

*Continuation of arrangement.* As though the arrangement were a standing order for flour. As though whatever Halloway had bought with his forty crowns in his final desperate year had been picked up afterward, quietly, by the man who kept the books—and kept paying for it, year after year, decade after decade.

Rosalind flipped forward through Corvane's ledgers, years blurring past under her thumb, and found the payments again and again. Sometimes annually. Sometimes gaps of two or three years. Always small enough to hide inside ordinary expenses, always logged with that same chilling phrase or some cousin of it: *continuation of arrangement*, *renewal of prior understanding*, *per established terms*. The sums grew smaller as the guild grew poorer, but they never stopped. The most recent entry was eleven years old. After that, either the arrangement had concluded—or Corvane had stopped writing it down.

Eleven years ago. Rosalind did the arithmetic without wanting to. Eleven years ago she had been fourteen, newly sworn into the guild, running her first solo pass through the Thornfells. She remembered that winter. She remembered Corvane watching her come down off the mountain with a courier's satchel, and the strange, appraising look he'd given her—like a man checking a coin against a weight.

She pushed the memory aside. Coincidences were luxuries. Focus.

She spread the codex on the desk beside the ledgers and looked at the two objects together for the first time. The prophecy-book, written in a hand Maren knew. The ledgers, recording payments to an unnamed buyer for an unnamed purpose, beginning the very year the last renewal failed. Two threads, and somewhere in the dark between them, a shape.

Someone had paid to break the covenant. Someone inside the guild had arranged it, enabled it, *continued* it—for decades. And now a smuggler had stumbled across a book that named her as the one who could undo it all, and the guildmaster who had spent thirty years feeding that arrangement wanted the book called a forgery before anyone looked too closely.

Rosalind closed her eyes and breathed.

Betrayal, she discovered, did not feel the way the ballads described it—a knife-flash, a hot rage. It felt cold. It felt like standing on a frozen lake and hearing, beneath your boots, the long slow crack of ice giving way. Everything she had built her invisible life upon—the guildhall that sheltered her, the brazier she'd warmed her hands at, the old man who'd signed her induction papers—all of it sat on a foundation someone had been hollowing out for a century.

*The Hollow doesn't devour you,* Maren had said, the night they'd read the codex by candlelight. *It edits you. It takes the parts of your story that bind you to others, and it unpicks them, stitch by stitch, until you're alone in a room with nothing true left.*

Someone in this guild had been letting it do exactly that. To the covenant. To the fire. To everyone who would have stood against it.

Rosalind opened her eyes.

She was not a hero. She had never wanted to be one; heroes got names, and names got people killed. She was a smuggler, and smugglers survived by knowing three things: what was valuable, who wanted it, and how to move it unseen. Right now the valuable thing was truth, the person who wanted it buried was sleeping two floors above her, and the way to move it unseen was—

Footsteps in the corridor outside. Slow, measured, unhurried. Corvane walked like a man who owned every floorboard he crossed.

Rosalind moved without thinking, the way she'd moved a thousand times on night passes: lamp out, codex into the oilcloth, oilcloth into the gap behind the false shelving where she'd found the archive door years ago. She slid into the shadow between a shelf of route-maps and the cold stone wall, and went still, breathing through her mouth, becoming furniture.

The archive door creaked open.

Lamplight spilled across the floor. She watched it through the gap in the shelves—a long yellow wedge, trembling slightly, as though the hand holding the source were not entirely steady. Corvane stood in the doorway a long moment, saying nothing, doing nothing. Just listening. The silence stretched until Rosalind's own pulse seemed loud enough to betray her.

Then he crossed the room. She heard him stop at the reading desk—her desk, her lamp's scorch mark still warm on the wood—and heard the soft rasp of his fingers on paper. He was standing exactly where she had been sitting. Looking at exactly what she had been looking at.

A long exhale. When he spoke, his voice was low, addressed to the empty room or perhaps to himself, and it was not the voice of a guildmaster dismissing a superstition. It was tired. Almost gentle.

"You shouldn't have found it, girl."

Papers rustled. Then, softly, the sound of a ledger being lifted from the shelf—the volume for the renewal year, she was almost certain, though she couldn't see. His footsteps retreated to the door. Paused.

"Go home to your passes," Corvane murmured. "Some debts are better left unpaid."

The door clicked shut. The lamplight vanished under it.

Rosalind stayed in the dark a long time, counting her breaths the way she counted paces on a night climb—in, two, three, four; out, two, three, four—until her heart slowed and the shaking stopped.

Then she retrieved the codex from its hiding place, tucked it beneath her coat, and slipped out through the map closet into the grey beginning of morning.

She didn't go to her bunk. She went to Maren's cell at the end of the east corridor, where a light already burned—because Maren, in fifty years of keeping the guild's secrets, had never once slept well—and knocked twice, softly, the smuggler's knock.

The door opened before the second knock landed. Maren looked at her face, and whatever she saw there made the old woman step back and pull her inside without a word.

"I need to know," Rosalind said, before the door had even closed, "who Elira was."

Maren closed her eyes. For a moment she looked every one of her seventy-odd years, and older. When she spoke, her voice was barely above the whisper she used for graves.

"Sit down, child. And bar the door."

* * *

Outside, far below the guildhall, in the town where the mountain met the valley, a man with no face worth remembering paid a boy a copper to carry a message up the hill. The message contained no words. It contained only tomorrow's date, written in a smooth, careful hand—

the same hand, though neither of them knew it yet, that had signed the first entry in Aldous Corvane's ledger, ninety-two years before he was born.

By dawn, it would be in Corvane's possession. By dusk, Rosalind suspected, so would hunters.

She barred the door, and listened to Maren breathe, and waited for the story that would explain why her whole life had been a lie written in someone else's ink.
