## 5. The Scribe's Grandson

Tobin Ashvale had rehearsed this moment for eleven days, and in every rehearsal he had not been shaking.

The guildhall of the Emberwrights crouched at the mouth of the Thornfell passes like something that had given up on standing tall—a squat stone building with a slate roof furred in moss, its eaves carved with dragons so worn by weather they might have been pigs. Smoke rose from a single chimney. A sign above the door read EMBERWRIGHT GUILD — KEEPERS OF THE FLAME, and beneath it, in smaller letters someone had scratched long ago: *ask what we keep it from.*

He knew the answer. He'd spent his whole life knowing the answer, or believing he did. His grandfather had filled three notebooks with it before the tremors took his hands, and Tobin had grown up falling asleep to the sound of Aldous Ashvale muttering prophecies like a man arguing with an old friend who owed him money.

*She will come out of the low country with frost in her hair and no name worth writing down,* the old man had said, over and over, in the last year of his life. *But write her down anyway, boy. Write her down anyway.*

Tobin pushed open the door.

The warmth inside was wrong—not the generous heat of a hearth but the thin, rationed warmth of a fire being eked out, the way a miser counts coins. The main hall was larger than the building's exterior promised, all dark beams and older stone, and at its center burned a brazier no bigger than a washbasin. Four people sat around it. They looked up as one.

"Guild business?" asked a broad woman with burn-scarred forearms. Her tone made *guild business* sound like a door being closed.

"I'm looking for someone."

"We're all looking for someone," said a young man by the wall, without looking up from the knife he was sharpening. "Mostly ourselves, if you believe Maren."

An old woman in the corner—tiny, wrapped in shawls despite the season, her eyes quick as a wren's—lifted her head. "Who are you looking for?"

"A smuggler." Tobin's voice cracked on the word and he hated himself for it. He cleared his throat. "Rosalind Lockridge."

The effect was immediate and strange. The broad woman went still. The knife-sharpener stopped mid-stroke. And the old woman—the scribe, it had to be Maren, his grandfather had written about her too, *Maren of the guildhall, keeper of the codex when there was still a codex to keep*—set down her cup with exaggerated care, as though sudden movements might frighten the name away.

"And who," she said, "is asking?"

"Tobin Ashvale." He pulled the oilskin packet from inside his coat and held it against his chest like a shield. "Aldous Ashvale was my grandfather. He wrote the Vellum Codex."

* * *

They put him in a chair by the brazier, which he understood was either an honor or an interrogation technique, and possibly both. The broad woman—Brakka, she said, though Tobin suspected that wasn't her whole name—stood behind him where he couldn't see her. The knife-sharpener, whose name was Pell, resumed sharpening with pointed indifference. The fourth Emberwright, a hollow-cheeked man named Ostler, simply watched him with the flat patience of a fisherman watching a line.

Maren sat across from him with the Vellum Codex open on her knees.

Tobin had never seen the actual book. His grandfather had described it a hundred times—the calfskin cover gone soft as cloth, the pages the color of old teeth, the ink that never quite dried—and seeing it now was like meeting a person he'd only known through letters. His chest hurt in a way he didn't have language for.

"You say he wrote it," Maren said. "I say the same. But the guildmaster says forgery. So convince me, boy. How does a book written a hundred years ago carry your grandfather's hand?"

"Because my grandfather wrote it a hundred years ago." Tobin heard how it sounded and pressed on before they could laugh. "I know. I've done the arithmetic myself. He was born in the year the codex first appears in guild records. He died last spring, aged ninety-one. The handwriting matches his at every stage of his life—the early entries match his apprentice work, the later ones match his letters to me. I've compared them. I'm a copyist by trade; I don't make mistakes about hands." He swallowed. "Either my grandfather lived a century and hid it, or the book is exactly what it claims to be, and time works differently around prophecy than it does around everything else."

Silence. The brazier crackled, and the crackle sounded almost like speech.

"It's the second one," Maren said quietly. "I know it's the second one. I wanted to hear whether you knew it too." She turned a page with a fingertip, gentle as touching a sleeping animal. "Your grandfather wrote the final entry two nights ago. Tomorrow's date. A description of a woman coming off the high pass."

"Frost in her hair," Tobin said. "No name worth writing down. I know. It's why I'm here."

"Where is she now?" Brakka, from behind him.

"Gone to Corvane, I'd guess. She came back this morning—I saw her cross the yard while I was waiting in the trees. I didn't want to walk up to a stranger's door and start talking about destiny. People shoot you for less in the low country." He hesitated. "Is she here?"

"She's in the records room," Pell said, "going through ledgers she has no business going through, and Corvane let her because Corvane lets Rosalind do whatever Rosalind wants, which should tell you something about how much attention anyone pays to—" He stopped. Looked at the doorway.

She was standing in it.

Tobin had imagined this moment too, in the rehearsals: the Covenant-Bearer revealed, some figure of obvious significance, someone whose importance announced itself. What stood in the doorway instead was a lean woman in a road-worn coat, dark hair escaping its braid, a smudge of ink on one knuckle from the ledgers, and eyes that swept the room the way a smuggler's eyes must sweep every room—exits first, threats second, everyone else a distant third. Frost-burned cheeks. A face built to be forgotten.

Except the eyes. The eyes were doing something no forgettable face should do: they were afraid, and furious about being afraid, and pretending very hard to be neither.

"That's him?" she said to Maren. "The grandson?"

"This is Tobin Ashvale."

"Good." She crossed the room, took the oilskin packet out of his hands before he could react, and weighed it. "Then you can tell me why your dead grandfather wrote my tomorrow in his book, and you can do it quickly, because I've just spent four hours in Corvane's ledgers and I'm in no mood for mysticism."

* * *

They ended up in the records room because Rosalind refused to be anywhere Corvane could watch her talk, and because, she said, "if I'm going to be told I'm the center of some century-old prophecy, I want to be holding evidence of fraud at the time. It keeps things balanced."

So Tobin sat at a scarred table across from a woman who unrolled his grandfather's marginalia with the tenderness of someone defusing a trap, and began, for the third time in two days, to explain.

"The codex isn't a book of predictions. That's the mistake everyone makes, including the guild, including—" he gestured vaguely upward, toward wherever guildmasters lurked, "—him. Grandfather called it a ledger. Debts and payments across a boundary. The covenant is a contract, and contracts get renewed, and the renewal terms were written down once by someone who could see both sides of the border. The Hollow doesn't foresee the future. It remembers it. All of it, all at once, the way you remember your own name. Prophecy is just memory leaking backward through a bad seal."

Rosalind's eyes hadn't left him. "And the marginalia?"

"My grandfather spent forty years annotating the covenant terms. Cross-referencing. He found the pattern in the payments." Tobin turned to the last sheet, the one he'd handled least, the one whose edges had gone soft from his own handling. "Every hundred years, the covenant renews. Every renewal requires a bearer—someone to carry the ember from the dying dragon to the Spire. And every bearer, grandfather wrote, is chosen the same way. Not by lot. Not by merit." He made himself look at her. "By bloodline. There's only ever been one line."

Something moved behind Rosalind's face—quick, denied, buried. "That's superstition. Bloodlines. That's the kind of thing guilds invent to keep power in families."

"Yes," Tobin agreed. "Usually. But listen to the description. *She will come out of the low country with frost in her hair.* Your hair goes white at the temples—I saw it when you walked in. *No name worth writing down.* You're a smuggler. You live under other names; Maren told me. *She will carry the ember as she has carried everything else—alone, unpaid, and unthanked.*" He stopped. "Grandfather wrote that entry ninety-one years ago. Before you were born. Before your parents were born."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, the brazier popped.

Rosalind looked down at the marginalia, and Tobin watched her read the same line three times, her lips moving slightly, and he recognized the gesture because it was his grandfather's gesture, and something about that recognition frightened him more than anything else that had happened since the old man died.

"There's more," Tobin said. "There's a note about the bearer's parentage. Grandfather spent years trying to trace the line forward after the last renewal—it failed, or was skipped, twenty-five years ago, and the trail goes cold. But he wrote down what he recovered of the older generations. The bearers' names. And the name of the woman he believed was the last of the line before the trail breaks." He slid the final page across the table. "He called her the forge-mother. Elira."

Rosalind went white.

Not dramatically—not the swooning pallor of storybooks—but the specific bloodless gray of a person who has just heard a dead relative's name spoken aloud by a stranger. Her hand came down flat on the table, steadying herself, and Tobin saw the knuckles go taut.

"Where," she said, and her voice was level in the way ice is level, thin ice, ice over deep water, "did he get that name?"

"Guild baptismal records, mostly. Old ones. And a marriage entry from the Thornfell parish that lists her trade as forge-keeper and her husband as unnamed, which was unusual enough that grandfather flagged it." Tobin hesitated. "Why? Do you know her?"

"No." Too fast. Then, slower, with terrible care: "My mother's name was Elira. She died when I was six. My father was never named. I grew up telling people I didn't have one because it was easier than explaining." She laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "You're telling me my mother is in a hundred-year-old prophecy."

"I'm telling you my grandfather thought she might be. I'm telling you he died believing the next bearer would come from her line, and then I read yesterday's date in his book, and then I found you exist, and I—" Tobin ran out of certainty, which was his natural state, and finished honestly: "I don't know what I'm telling you. I know ink. That's all I know. But the ink is consistent, and I've checked it until my eyes bled, and I needed someone else to check it, because if I'm wrong I've wasted my inheritance chasing ghosts, and if I'm right—"

"If you're right, then someone's coming for me." Rosalind straightened. The fear was still there, Tobin could see it, but she'd done something with it—folded it small and put it somewhere load-bearing, the way you pack a pack. "The Hollow's servants. Maren says villages are emptying at the mountain's edge. And Corvane's ledgers show payments from a buyer he won't name, dated the exact year the renewal failed." She looked at the marginalia, at the codex, at Tobin, and her gaze had changed. It was the look of a woman recalculating a route. "You came here alone. Through the passes. Carrying the only annotated copy of a document the Hollow would burn the world to destroy."

"I took the long roads," Tobin said. "The ones that aren't on maps."

Something flickered across her face—surprise, then appraisal, then something almost like respect, filed away quickly as if respect were contraband. "You know those roads?"

"I grew up on them. Grandfather didn't trust the mapped ones. Said maps were how the Hollow finds people—everything written down is findable." He paused. "That's rather the point of me being here, actually. Everything written down is findable. Including you. Including the bearer. Grandfather's notes say the Hollow hunts the covenant's heirs by following records—baptismal rolls, ledgers, parish books. Paper trails. It eats memory, Maren says, but first it follows paper."

Rosalind was quiet for a long moment. Outside, wind worried at the shutters. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped into something flatter and harder, the voice of a professional assessing cargo.

"So the safest thing you could have done—the smartest thing—was burn your grandfather's notes and never come here at all."

"Yes."

"But you didn't."

"No."

"Why not?"

It was the question he'd been dreading, the one beneath all the others, and Tobin found he couldn't dress up the answer. He looked at his hands, ink-stained like hers, and told the truth.

"Because my grandfather spent his whole life writing about a woman who carried everything alone, unpaid, and unthanked, and he made me memorize the description when I was nine years old, and I have been waiting my entire adult life to meet her. And when I saw you cross the yard this morning, I thought—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Everyone in these mountains lives like they're already erased. Like it's safer to be no one. And maybe it is. But my grandfather thought being remembered mattered more than being safe, and he died poor and half-mad and certain, and I would rather be wrong with him than right without him."

The silence stretched. Rosalind looked at him for a long time, and Tobin understood, in a way he couldn't have explained, that he was the first person in years to look at her and see anything at all—that she had spent her whole life being glanced past, and that being seen, truly seen, was not the comfort he'd imagined offering her. It was a wound. Being known was dangerous. Being known was the thing she'd built her whole invisible life to avoid.

He was terrifying her, and he couldn't stop.

"You should be afraid of me," she said finally. "Everyone who gets close to me ends up in the path of whatever's coming. Ask Maren. Ask anyone."

"I'm a copyist," Tobin said. "Fear and I have an arrangement. It leaves me alone and I ignore it."

The ghost of something crossed her mouth—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile might someday decide to live. She gathered the marginalia, squared its edges, and rolled it tight.

"All right, Ashvale. Here's how this works. You travel with us, you do exactly what I say on the road, and if I tell you to run, you run and you don't look back and you don't argue. In exchange—" she tapped the rolled pages against her palm, "—you teach me everything in these notes. Every name, every date, every payment. If I'm going to be somebody's prophecy, I want to know the terms better than the people who wrote them."

"And if Corvane objects?"

"Corvane can object to the weather." She was already moving toward the door, and paused there, backlit, not turning around. "Get your coat. We leave within the hour. There are roads I need to show you that aren't on any map—including yours."

She was gone. Tobin sat alone in the records room amid the smell of dust and old ink, heart hammering, and realized his hands were trembling again.

Eleven days of rehearsal, and in none of them had she said *yes.*

In none of them had he imagined that being seen would cost her so much—or that she would pay it anyway.

From the hall below, faintly, he heard Maren's voice rise in alarm, and Brakka's boots on stone, and then Rosalind's voice, sharp and clear:

"Maren. The brazier. Something's happening to the fire."
