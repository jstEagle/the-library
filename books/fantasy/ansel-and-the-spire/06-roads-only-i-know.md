## 6. Roads Only I Know

They left the guildhall before dawn, while the fog still owned the streets and the brazier's ember sat in its iron cradle against Maren's chest like a sleeping bird.

Rosalind had dressed for the road the way she always did: dark wool, soft-soled boots, her hair bound back so nothing caught on branches or grasping hands. But this time she had packed differently. This time she carried a flint striker, a vial of lamp oil, a knife with a blade long enough to matter—and the Vellum Codex wrapped in oilcloth, strapped tight against her spine where no thief would think to look.

Tobin Ashvale met her at the stable door with a pack that clinked when he walked. He had brought books. Actual books. She watched him shift them from hand to hand, trying to make room for the bread Maren pressed on him, and felt something between pity and disbelief.

"You're bringing those."

"They're my grandfather's notes." He said it the way other men said *my children*. "The marginalia cross-references three different recensions of the covenant rites. If we reach the Spire and the codex is wrong about something—"

"It won't be wrong about the road." She swung up onto the mule she'd borrowed from a tanner who owed her more than he knew. "The codex doesn't know the Thornfells. I do."

Maren mounted behind her, arms circling to protect the ember-cradle, and Tobin scrambled onto the second mule with his books hugged to his chest like a shield. The fog swallowed them three streets from the guildhall, and by the time the sun came up thin and white over the rooftops, there was nothing left in Cindermarsh to suggest that three people had ever left it at all.

That was the point. That had always been the point.

* * *

The first day they took the low road, because the low road was what people expected, and expectation was a thing you could hide inside.

Rosalind kept them moving through the morning markets of the river towns, past carters and fishwives and the gray-faced men who lounged outside taverns doing no work she could name. Twice she saw someone watching them too long—once a woman with a shawl pulled high, once a man whose face seemed, in the corner of her eye, oddly smooth, like an egg, like wax. Each time she turned the party down an alley, through a courtyard, out a gate that opened onto a drainage path along the river, and each time the watcher did not follow.

Because the watchers didn't know the paths. That was the whole architecture of her life, laid bare now that she was finally looking at it: twenty years of learning every seam in the Thornfells, every collapsed mine adit and shepherd's stair and deer trail worn into the rock by hooves that predated the guild itself. The Emberwrights had thought her useful because she could move cargo without being seen. They had never understood that being unseen was not a trick. It was a country, and she was its only citizen.

"Are we lost?" Tobin asked for the fourth time since noon. They were climbing a slope of loose shale above the river gorge, following a line that looked, to anyone without her eyes, like the random wanderings of a drunk goat.

"No."

"There's no road here. There's barely a *slope* here."

"The road is for people who want to be found." She picked her way up through the scree, testing each stone with her boot before trusting it. "We are not those people."

Behind her, Maren made a sound that might have been a laugh. "She used to say that as a girl, you know," the old scribe offered, breathless from the climb. "When she'd disappear from the hall for days. Corvane would rage, and Elira would just smile and say, *she's not lost, she's practicing.*"

Rosalind's boot slipped half an inch on the shale. She recovered, kept climbing, and said nothing. But the word *Elira* sat in her chest the rest of the afternoon like a coal she couldn't spit out.

Her mother's name had been everywhere lately—in Maren's stories, in Tobin's grandfather's marginalia, in the codex's careful script. *Forge-mother.* A woman who had gone to the Spire and paid with everything she was, leaving behind a daughter who grew up learning how to vanish. Rosalind had spent one sleepless night already turning that over, and she suspected she would spend many more. But not today. Today the Hollow was behind them, and the mountain was ahead, and the ember needed her steady.

* * *

They camped that night in a place no map had ever named, because Rosalind had never named it either—a hollow beneath an overhang of granite where a spring seeped cold and clean from the rock and the wind couldn't find them. She built no fire. Instead she showed Tobin how to warm the ember-cradle with their bodies, taking turns holding it close, and how to eat hardtack softened in melted snow so the noise of chewing wouldn't carry.

"Why does it matter?" he whispered. "There's no one within miles."

"You don't know that." She nodded upward, toward the black shoulder of the pass. "The things chasing us don't leave tracks. They don't need light. What they have is patience, and ears, and a hunger for exactly the kind of warmth a fire puts off. So we give them nothing. No smoke, no smell, no sound. We become weather."

He looked at her for a long moment in the dark, and she realized he was seeing her—not the guild's quiet courier, not the woman who signed receipts and vanished, but whatever it was he'd found in his grandfather's notes. It made her skin prickle. Being seen was like walking on ice over deep water; you never knew which step would crack it.

"How do you know all this?" he asked. "Not the paths. The *rest*. How to move, how to camp, how to think about being hunted. Who taught you?"

"No one." She passed him the ember-cradle and settled back against the stone. "I taught myself. When you grow up with no name worth saying and no family worth claiming, you learn the country instead. The country doesn't care who your father was. It only cares whether you pay attention."

"That sounds lonely."

"It was safe." She closed her eyes. "Safe is better than lonely. That's what I told myself for twenty years, anyway."

She heard him turn that over, the way he turned everything over, like a man reading a difficult page. Then, quietly: "And now?"

"Now I'm carrying a dragon's last fire up a haunted mountain because a book told me to." She let the ghost of a smile touch her mouth, there where no one could see it. "Ask me again when we're alive."

* * *

On the third day, the watchers stopped pretending.

Rosalind spotted the first one at midmorning—a figure standing motionless on a ridge two valleys east, too far away to show detail, wrong in a way she couldn't articulate until she did: it wasn't moving *with* the landscape. Everything else on that ridge bent in the wind. It stood straight through the gusts like a fence post, like something that had read about wind but never felt it.

By noon there were three. By dusk, five, spread across the high ground in a pattern that was almost respectful—no, she corrected herself, studying their spacing as she led the party down into a spruce-choked ravine. Not respectful. *Methodical.* They weren't trying to catch them. They were herding them, closing the obvious routes one by one, funnelling the party toward the main pass where the road was wide and the ambush would be easy.

"They're pushing us north," she told the others, crouched in the blue shadow of the spruces while the light died. "Every route south is going to have teeth in it by tomorrow."

"So we go north?" Tobin's voice was carefully level. She was starting to recognize when he was frightened by how precisely he spoke.

"We go *nowhere* they've planned for." She traced lines in the needle-soft ground with a twig. "There's a route through the Karst Teeth—the limestone hills west of the pass. Caves, sinkholes, old smuggler tunnels from the tin wars. The maps call it impassable. The maps are wrong, but only if you know which holes drop you two hundred feet and which ones go through."

"And you know?"

"I've moved contraband through the Teeth eleven times." She snapped the twig and stood. "Once with a belly wound. Once carrying a man heavier than both of you put together. It can be done."

Maren had been silent, holding the ember-cradle, watching the ridge where the motionless figures stood against the last red light. Now she spoke, and her voice had the careful weight of a woman opening a door she'd rather keep shut. "Rosalind. Those things on the ridge. In the old records, when the Hollow's servants hunted a Covenant-Bearer, they didn't always attack directly. Sometimes they waited for the Bearer to take the dangerous road, and simply... let the mountain do the work."

"I know."

"If the Teeth kill us—"

"They won't." She met the old woman's eyes. "I've buried enough friends in these mountains to know exactly where the mountain kills people. I intend to introduce you to none of those places."

It was the most she'd said about herself in three days, and it hung in the cold air between them. Tobin was looking at her with that expression again—the one that made her feel like a specimen, like a page under glass. But Maren just nodded slowly, and there was something in the old scribe's face that Rosalind couldn't read, some grief or guilt folded small and hidden, and then the darkness came down and there was no time to ask.

* * *

The Karst Teeth were everything the maps promised and worse.

They entered through a sinkhole Rosalind called the Keyhole—a vertical shaft forty feet deep with iron rungs driven into the rock by hands a century dead, rusted to lace but still holding if you trusted them gently. She went first, testing each rung, calling softly up the shaft: *left foot, higher, there's a good one.* Tobin descended white-knuckled with his grandfather's books strapped to his back, and Maren came last with the ember-cradle lashed to her chest, refusing with surprising ferocity any suggestion that someone else carry it.

Below the shaft, the world changed. The tunnels of the Teeth were a cathedral of dripping stone, passages widening into chambers ribbed with flowstone, narrowing into crawls where the rock pressed close enough to taste. Rosalind moved through them the way other people moved through their own homes—left at the split where a fallen stalactite lay like a dead god's finger, right where the ceiling dipped, always counting paces, always noting the airflow against her cheek because moving air meant a way through and still air meant a tomb.

For the first time in her life, she talked while she traveled. She couldn't help it. The tunnels wanted explaining.

"This chamber—" her voice echoed soft and vast, "—was where the tin-war smugglers hid a whole season's shipment when the king's men sealed the valley. Forty barrels of ore, right here, for eight months. There's a hearth-scar on the east wall where they cooked. You can still smell the charcoal if the air's damp."

"You *know* that?" Tobin's voice came back to her out of the dark, threaded with wonder. "You know what happened in a cave nobody's mapped?"

"Nobody official." She ducked under a shelf of stone. "The people who actually live in these mountains keep their own records. They just don't write them down. They tell them. Over fires, over drinks, to whoever bothers to listen and remember." She paused at a junction, tasting the air—cold from the left, faintly mineral from the right—and chose right. "Turns out listening and remembering is most of smuggling. And most of surviving."

Behind her, she heard Tobin fumbling in the dark, and guessed he was writing. The thought should have annoyed her. Instead it warmed something in her chest she hadn't known was cold. Somewhere in the dark under the earth, in a place no map recorded, someone was writing down that she had been here—that she had known the way. It was such a small thing. It felt enormous.

* * *

The trouble came on the second day underground, at the crossing she privately called the Throat.

It was a flooded gallery—thigh-deep water over a gravel bed, sixty yards of passage where the ceiling came down to within a foot of the surface. She had crossed it a dozen times. But as they waded in, packs held high, the ember-cradle lifted overhead in Maren's trembling arms, Rosalind felt the water pull wrong against her legs. A current, where there had never been a current. Faint, but real, dragging downstream into the dark.

Someone—or something—had unsealed the lower galleries.

She stopped the party in the widest part of the passage, water swirling around her waist, and listened. Above them, through fifty feet of limestone, came a sound she had hoped never to hear in the Teeth: footsteps. Deliberate. Even. Unhurried. Walking the surface trail above the flooded gallery, pacing them, the way a wolf paces a frozen elk pond.

"They've found the upper entrance," she breathed. "They're waiting for us to come out."

"Can we go back?" Tobin whispered.

"Back is two days through ground they'll have closed behind us. These things don't chase. They *arrange*." Her mind ran the map of the Teeth, every passage, every fork, hunting for an option that didn't exist yet. The current tugged at her thighs, patient as the things above. Downstream. Downstream meant the lower galleries, the drowned levels, the deep sumps where the tin-war tunnels had flooded and stayed flooded—

Except.

Except there was one passage, one she'd found years ago by accident, following a draft that shouldn't have been. A crack behind a curtain of flowstone in the lower gallery, breathing cold dry air, too narrow for a grown man with a pack. Too narrow for anyone.

Almost.

"I need to look at something," she said. "Stay here. Keep the ember dry. If the water rises past your knees, go back to the last chamber and wait—I'll find you."

"Rosalind—" Maren started.

But she was already diving.

The cold hit her like a fist. She let the current take her, counting seconds, feeling the gravel scrape beneath her, until her fingers found the flowstone curtain by its texture—smooth as poured wax, exactly as she remembered. She wedged herself into the gap behind it, lungs burning, and forced her shoulders through the crack into the narrow throat beyond. Air. Cold, dry, *moving* air, streaming against her wet face from somewhere deeper in the mountain.

She surfaced in a pocket of blackness, gasping, and laughed—one short, disbelieving bark of sound that echoed off walls no human voice had touched in a hundred years. The draft was strong here. Steady. Coming from below and ahead, which meant open ground somewhere beyond, which meant—

Which meant the Hollow's servants had arranged their trap around every road that existed. And Rosalind Lockridge had just found one that didn't.

She swam back, broke the surface beside the others, and saw their faces swing toward her—Tobin's pale with fear, Maren's set and grim, the ember glowing faint gold even underwater-lit, alive, stubborn, *hers* to carry.

"Change of plan," she said, water streaming from her hair, grinning like a woman possessed. "Everyone under. There's a crack in the floor of this river, and on the other side of it, a road the Hollow has never heard of—because I'm the only person alive who knows it exists."

* * *

They came out of the mountain near midnight, through a fissure in a hillside bramble-choked and forgotten, into a valley of silver grass under a sky salted with stars. Behind them, the Karst Teeth stood black and silent. Whatever paced the ridgelines above the flooded gallery was pacing it still, arranging its trap around roads that no longer mattered.

Maren sank down onto the grass with the ember-cradle in her lap and laughed until it turned into something else, something wetter. Tobin simply sat down where he stood, soaked to the ribs, and stared at her.

"You found a tunnel," he said. "Underwater. That wasn't on any map. That *wasn't anywhere*."

"It was somewhere. It was in my head." She wrung out her hair, suddenly embarrassed by the grin still clinging to her face. "Twenty years of learning ways no one records. Turns out that's worth something after all."

"It's worth everything." He said it simply, without performance, the way he said true things, and she had to look away at the mountains rather than let him see what it did to her. All her life, invisibility had been a wound she'd learned to dress as a skill. Tonight, for the first time, it had been armor. It had been *power*. The faceless things that hunted them had infinite patience and perfect method, and they had still lost her, because they had studied every map in the world and the one map that mattered lived behind her eyes.

Then Maren went still.

The old scribe had risen halfway, one hand pressed flat against the ember-cradle, her head cocked toward the northern sky. Rosalind followed her gaze—and her stomach dropped.

Far off, above the shoulder of the high passes, the stars were going out. Not clouding. Going out, in a slow spreading stain, as though something vast were drinking the light itself, and at the center of the stain, faint but unmistakable, burned a single point of cold white fire, like an eye opening across the roof of the world.

"The Hollow knows where we're going," Maren whispered. "It's not herding us anymore, child. It's *waiting* at the Spire."

Rosalind looked at the dying stars, then down at the ember glowing in its cradle—small, stubborn, warm against all that cold—and tightened the straps on her pack.

"Then we'd better teach it," she said, "what happens when it underestimates the person holding the map."
