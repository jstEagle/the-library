## 8. The Faceless Pursuit

The river came out of the mountains the way grief comes out of a person—all at once, and louder than you expected.

Tobin heard it before he saw it, a white roar threading up through the pines, and then the trees broke apart and there it was: the Vell, running fast and gray-green with snowmelt, wide as a thrown rope and twice as knotted. The ford his grandfather's marginalia had marked was a shallow spine of stones maybe forty paces across, barely breaking the surface, water combing over them in long silver fingers.

"Three days since the shrine," Maren said, coming up beside him. Her voice had that scribe's flatness she used when she was reciting rather than feeling. "Three days, and the ember's still burning. That's longer than it's answered anyone in a century."

Tobin glanced back at Rosalind. She walked at the rear of their little column the way she walked everywhere—like a person perpetually listening for a door opening behind her. The ember rode against her chest in its iron lantern-cage, wrapped in wool and ash-cloth, and even through all that wrapping he could see its glow on the underside of her jaw, a warm orange stain that moved when she breathed. She hadn't let anyone else carry it. Not once. Not even to rest her arms.

She caught him looking and her eyes narrowed. "The ford," she said. "You're staring at me instead of the ford. Don't."

"I wasn't—"

"You were. Everyone does it now. Look at me like I'm a page you're trying to read." She pushed past him toward the water's edge, and the ember's light swung with her, painting the pines in brief copper. "It's a ford. We cross it. We don't stop, we don't camp on the near bank, we don't do anything that lets the far side feel like home. Understood?"

Maren made a small sound—not quite a laugh. "She's right. Water confuses them. Running water, anyway. The Hollow doesn't like things that won't hold still long enough to be written on." She was already unbuckling her satchel, lifting the codex out and wrapping it in oilskin with the practiced economy of a woman who had done it ten thousand times. "Tobin. You'll take the codex across. I'll take the ember-case's spare wrappings and the rites. Rosalind takes the ember itself."

"And if we get separated?" Tobin asked.

Maren's hands paused on the oilskin. Just for a moment. Just long enough that he noticed, and wished he hadn't.

"Then we walk to Thornfell and wait," she said lightly. "The Emberwrights have waited a hundred years. We can wait a few days more."

She was lying. He'd spent his whole life reading his grandfather's handwriting, learning to hear the man's voice in the loops and crossings-out, and he knew what a lie looked like when it was written down. Maren's lie looked exactly like that: neat, legible, and wrong in a way that only showed if you'd spent years learning the hand.

The near bank was shingle and dead reeds. Rosalind crouched at the waterline, testing the current with two fingers the way a cook tests a pot, and Tobin crouched beside her because there was nowhere else to be and because being near her had started to feel less like a choice and more like a fact of the world, the way north is a fact.

"The stones will be slick," she said. "Diagonally, downstream-lean. If you go in, don't fight the water. Fight *sideways*."

"I know how to cross a river."

"Do you?" She looked at him then, and there was something in her face that wasn't quite unkind but wasn't kind either—something measuring. "You froze at the shrine. When the ember flared. Everyone did, a little, but you—" she stopped herself, and he watched her decide not to finish the sentence, which was somehow worse. "Just stay behind me. That's all. Stay behind me and hold what you're carrying and don't be a hero. Heroes are just corpses with better stories."

"That's a terrible thing to say."

"It's a true thing to say. Terrible and true tend to travel together." She stood, and the ember-light moved up her throat. "Maren. On my word. Not before."

Tobin looked at the far bank. Pines again, darker, the shadows under them lying flat and absolute, like ink pooled in the woodcut of a forest. He remembered his grandfather's marginalia on the Vell crossing—a single line, cramped, the ink brown with age: *The river remembers what crosses it. Do not let it remember you.*

He had thought, when he was twelve and first transcribing that note into his own commonplace book, that it was a metaphor. Grandfather Ashvale had loved metaphors the way some men loved drink—indulgently, to excess, and always with consequences in the morning.

It was not a metaphor.

* * *

They were a third of the way across when Tobin saw the first one.

He would spend the rest of his life trying to explain the seeing of it, and he would never quite manage it, because the problem was not that the thing was hidden. The problem was that it was *plain*—standing in plain sight on the far bank, among the pines, and his eyes had simply declined to report it. It was the visual equivalent of a word you read four times without reading. His gaze had slid off it the way a boot slides off wet stone.

Then Rosalind stopped, mid-river, water to her knees, and said, "Don't."

One word. She said it the way you'd say *down* to a dog that has heard the whistle.

There were three of them.

They stood at the treeline on the far bank, spaced like the points of a triangle, and they were shaped like people—tall, narrow, dressed in traveling clothes that were almost right, a merchant's coat, a drover's cloak, a scribe's gray robe, garments that fit the way a memory of a garment fits. And their faces—

Their faces were paper.

Not white. Not blank, exactly. *Smooth*, the way the first page of a new ledger is smooth, the way vellum is smooth before the first stroke. There were the faintest suggestions of features, the way a page holds the ghost of the next page's writing pressed through from behind—dents where eyes should be, a shallow crease that might have been a mouth. And as Tobin watched, the crease moved. The center one's face *shifted*, subtly, the way paper shifts when a hand passes over it, and for one horrible instant he saw pressure rising beneath the surface, as if something on the other side of the face was trying to write its way through.

"Back," Maren said, from behind him. Her voice was steady. Her hands, on his shoulder, were not. "Slowly. Back to the near bank. They want us on the water."

"Why the water?" Tobin whispered.

"Because water can't be written on." A pause. "And because they can't cross running water carrying what they intend to carry. They need us to finish the crossing. They need us to bring it *to* them."

The center figure took a step forward. Its boot came down on the shingle without a sound, and Tobin understood, with a lurch, that the silence was the worst part—that the world had simply declined to record the footstep, the way a bad scribe skips a line. The other two moved too, flanking, unhurried, and their faces caught the light and gave nothing back. No shine. No shadow. Faces like unwritten pages, and Tobin felt a scream build somewhere behind his sternum, not because they were monstrous but because they were *patient*. They moved like clerks. Like men who had done this many times and would do it many more.

"Rosalind." Maren's voice, low and fast. "The rites. If they take the ember across the water themselves, it's over. It has to be carried by a willing hand or it gutters. You understand? They can't take it by force across the water. They have to make *us* bring it to them, or wait for us to cross, or—"

"Or take the carrier," Rosalind said.

Nobody answered. The river roared. The paper faces waited.

And then the center one spoke, and Tobin's whole body tried to leave through the back of his skull, because the voice was *Maren's* voice. Exactly. The same dry scribe's flatness, the same reciting cadence.

"*The codex,*" it said. "*And the one who reads it. Those are the debt. The fire can burn. We do not grudge the fire. Give us the book and the reader, and cross in peace.*"

"It's lying," Rosalind said.

"It's not lying," Maren whispered, and there was something in her whisper Tobin had never heard before, something that sounded horribly like recognition. "That's the trick of them. That's the whole trick. They tell you the truth in a voice you trust, and they let your trust do the carrying." She stepped forward, water swirling around her ankles, and lifted her chin at the paper-faced thing wearing her voice. "I am Maren Solvane, scribe of the Emberwright Guild, fourth of her name, and I have kept the rites for fifty years, and I know what a covenant's debt is. The debt is not the book. The debt is not the reader. The debt is *renewal*, and renewal is owed at the Spire, and you know it, and you know I know it."

The center figure's face rippled.

For a moment—just a moment—the pressure beneath the paper resolved, and Tobin saw features trying to form: the suggestion of an old woman's face, deep lines, a scribe's squint, and it was Maren's face, or a memory of Maren's face, worn like a garment worn inside out. It was wearing *her*. It had taken her voice and now it was trying on her face, and the horror of it was so precise and so intimate that Tobin's knees nearly went.

"*The reader, then,*" it said, in Maren's voice, and its blank gaze—those dents where eyes should be—fixed on Tobin. "*The grandson. The ink of the ink. Give us the one who writes and we will let the fire walk on.*"

"No," Tobin said. It came out as a squeak. He cleared his throat. "No."

"*You carry his book. You carry his hand. You are the last of his line and his line is the last of the record, and the record is what we eat first.*" The face rippled again, and now the features pressing through were Tobin's own—young, narrow, frightened—and he was looking at his own face rendered in smooth blank paper, a page waiting to be written on, a page that would be written on, a page that— 

"MOVE!"

Rosalind's arm hit his chest like a beam and drove him sideways into the current, and the world went white and roaring and freezing, and the last thing he saw before the water took his eyes was the three figures leaving the bank.

They walked onto the river.

They walked *out onto* the river, onto the moving water, and the water did not splash them, did not touch them—the current parted around their boots in smooth gray seams, and where they stepped, the river went silent. Not quiet. *Silent.* A spreading patch of nothing, a hole in the roar, and the silence moved with them, and Tobin understood that they were not crossing the river.

They were *editing* it.

* * *

He came up coughing downstream, twenty yards along the near bank, having lost his footing entirely when Rosalind shoved him. The codex was still in its oilskin, strapped to his chest—he had done one thing right, one instinctive right thing, his arms had wrapped around it the way a drowning man's arms wrap around a spar. The ember—

The ember.

He thrashed around. The river. The stones. Rosalind—

She was still mid-river, and she was *surrounded*.

The three of them had reached her. The silence had reached her—a circle of dead quiet in the middle of all that roar, a hole in the world's sound, and inside it Rosalind stood with the lantern-cage held above the water in both hands, and the paper faces had closed to arm's length around her, and their mouths—that crease, that shallow crease—were moving. Speaking. Tobin couldn't hear what. Inside the silence, nothing carried. But he could see her face, and her face was doing something he had never seen it do.

It was *listening*.

Her head tilted. Her eyes had gone somewhere else. Her lips parted, and the ember's glow through the cage threw her features into copper relief, and Tobin watched her listen to whatever they were saying in their stolen voices, and he saw the exact moment the listening became dangerous: her arms began to lower.

The lantern-cage came down. An inch. Two inches. Toward the water. Toward *them*.

"*Rosalind!*" He screamed it and the river ate it, and he was moving before he decided to move—up the bank, along the shingle, boots slipping on wet stone, toward the ford's spine of rocks, toward the silence, toward her. "ROSALIND!"

She didn't turn. Inside the circle of paper faces, she was being *spoken to*, and whatever they were saying, they were saying it in a voice she knew. He could see it working on her like water working on stone, patient, inevitable. The lantern came down another inch.

Tobin hit the first of the ford stones at a dead run.

He had crossed a hundred rivers in his life. He had never once done it at a sprint, in the dark, on stones furred with weed, with a book strapped to his chest and his heart trying to exit through his teeth. The first three stones held. The fourth didn't—it rolled, slick and treacherous, and his leg went out from under him and he went down onto one knee in the freezing current, and the cold closed around his leg like a fist, and the current *pulled*, and he did the thing Rosalind had told him not to do: he fought it. Straight on. And the river took his other leg.

He went under.

The world became roar and cold and tumbling gray, and somewhere in it his hands found the codex strap and held, held, held, because that was the one thing his body knew how to do without him. Then his boot struck stone and he surfaced, gasping, twenty feet downstream of the ford, and hauled himself onto the shingle on the *far* side—on the wrong side, the far side, the bank with the pines and the pooled shadows—coughing, shaking, alive.

And the ember was still in Rosalind's hands, above the water, because in the moment his scream and his splash and his flailing body came crashing through the ford, her head had turned. Just a fraction. Just enough. The listening broke.

The lantern came back up.

And then she *moved*, and Tobin, watching from the shingle with river-water running out of his hair, understood that everything he had ever believed about Rosalind Lockridge being a smuggler and nothing more was a polite fiction he had been permitted to maintain. She didn't fight the paper-faced things. She *denied* them. She stepped inside the reach of the nearest one—inside, where its stillness couldn't work on her—and drove her shoulder into its chest, and the thing came apart like a page in the rain. Not blood. Not flesh. It came apart into *sheets*, hundreds of them, blank and white, and the river took them and the current shredded them and the silence died with a sound like a book slamming shut.

The other two recoiled. The circle broke. And Rosalind came off the ford stones at a run, ember held high, water exploding around her knees, and she did not stop running until she was out of the river, up the far bank, into the pines, into the dark, and Tobin was on his feet and running after her because the far bank was *his* bank now, he had crossed it, and there was no version of this in which he went back.

* * *

They ran until the pines thickened and the river's roar faded to a mutter behind them, and then Rosalind stopped so suddenly that Tobin nearly went into her back. She set the lantern down on a root, unwrapped the top of the cage, and looked at the ember with an expression Tobin couldn't read.

It had guttered. Not out—never out—but low, the way a fire gets when the wind has been at it, sullen and small, and the light it threw was more memory than heat.

"The cold," Tobin said. "The river. It's—"

"It's not the river." She was quiet. She was very, very quiet, in the way of someone standing at the edge of something. "They were talking to me, Tobin. In the water. They had—" her jaw worked. "They had her voice. They had *her* voice and they were telling me things she would say. Things about—" She stopped. "Where's Maren."

The question landed in the clearing like a dropped stone.

Tobin turned. Looked back the way they'd come, down through the pines, toward the river he could no longer see. "She was right behind me. She was—she had the codex wrappings, she was going to follow, she—"

He stopped.

Because he remembered. He remembered it now with the terrible clarity of things that were always going to have been true: Maren's hand on his shoulder at the water's edge, the oilskin bundle pressed into his arms, her voice saying *you'll take the codex across*—and the moment the paper faces stepped onto the river, the moment the silence bloomed, Maren had done something. She had stepped *toward* them. He had seen it and not seen it, the way he had not seen them on the bank. She had walked into the circle of dead quiet with her chin up and her hands open, the way you'd approach a reader who wanted to check your work, and she had said something he couldn't hear, and then—

And then the ford, and the shove, and the running, and he had not seen her since.

"She went toward them," he said. His voice sounded like it belonged to someone else. "She went toward them on purpose. She—she put the codex on me, she made sure I had the book, and she walked *toward* them, she traded herself for the crossing, she—"

"Stop." Rosalind's hand closed on his collar. Not gently. "Think. Did they take her? Did you see them take her?"

"I saw—" His breath was coming wrong. "I saw the silence close. I saw her standing in it. And then I was in the river, and when I came up they were gone, all of them, and the river was just a river again, roaring, and she wasn't—"

He couldn't finish it. Rosalind let go of his collar and turned away, and for a long moment the only sounds were the pines and the distant water and Tobin's own ragged breathing, and the ember sat on its root throwing its small defeated light, and the codex hung against his chest, and his grandfather's line came back to him with the weight of a headstone: *the river remembers what crosses it. Do not let it remember you.*

Maren had crossed. Maren had stood in the silence. And the silence had closed over her like water over a stone.

"They didn't kill her," Rosalind said at last. Her voice was flat, controlled, the voice of a woman holding something enormous down with both hands. "If they'd killed her, they'd have left her where we'd find her. That's what they do. That's what the Hollow *is*—it doesn't kill, it *unwrites*. They took her somewhere, or they kept her, or—" She stopped. Started again. "We can't go back across tonight. The ford will be watched. We can't go forward without her rites, without her knowing the Spire's approach. And we can't stand here."

"So what do we do?"

She looked at the ember. Then at him. And what was in her face was not comfort, was not reassurance, was not any of the things a person is supposed to put in their face at a moment like this. It was arithmetic.

"We do what she bought us," Rosalind said. "We carry it forward. And we get her back." She picked up the lantern, and the ember's guttering light caught the underside of her chin. "Because I've been unwritten my whole life, Tobin. I know how it's done. And I know it can be *undone*."

She turned and walked into the dark, and Tobin stood a moment longer with the codex against his chest, feeling its weight, feeling his grandfather's handwriting in it, feeling fifty years of Maren's careful scribe's hands in every page she'd ever touched. His hands were shaking. They had shaken through the whole crossing. He had frozen on the bank—frozen while she walked into the silence, frozen while Rosalind fought, frozen until the only useful thing his body had done was hold a book and fall in a river.

The Hollow ate memory first. He understood that now. Not because they had told him—because he could feel it already beginning, the way the memory of Maren at the water's edge was already softening, her face already going slightly, horribly indistinct, like writing left out in the rain.

He knelt, unslung the codex, and opened it with numb fingers to the first blank page.

And wrote, by ember-light, while he could still see her face: *Maren Solvane, scribe, fourth of her name. She lied well and died—* 

His pen stopped.

*She lied well,* he wrote, *and walked into the silence so we wouldn't have to. Remember her.*

He closed the book and followed Rosalind into the dark.
