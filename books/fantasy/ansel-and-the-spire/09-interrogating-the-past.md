## 9. Interrogating the Past

The fire had burned down to coals by the time Rosalind could look at anything without seeing the river, the white hands, the way Maren's mouth had shaped one word—*go*—before the paper-faced thing folded her into the dark like a letter being put away.

They had made camp in the lee of a fallen watchtower, three hours past the ford, because Tobin's legs had given out and because Rosalind could not have walked another step without leaving the ember behind, and the ember was the only thing in the world she still trusted. It sat in its iron cradle between them, pulsing slow as a sleeping heart, throwing light that was somehow older than firelight ought to be.

Tobin sat with his back against the tower stones, the codex in his lap, his hands still trembling. He hadn't spoken since the ford. He had frozen there—she'd seen it, seen the boy go rigid as the faceless thing came out of the water, seen him stand there while Maren was taken, and only then, only after, found the courage to snatch the brazier and run. He had saved the ember. He had not saved Maren. Rosalind did not know yet which of those facts she was angrier about, and so she fed neither, and the silence between them grew thick enough to chew.

The ember cracked. A spark leapt, hung, and settled.

"Read it to me," Rosalind said.

Tobin flinched. "What?"

"The marginalia. Your grandfather's notes. All of it." She turned to face him fully, and the ember's light caught the angles of her face and made them hard. "You've been doling it out like a man paying a debt in installments. A line in the waystation. A hint at the shrine. *She's more than a smuggler, Rosalind. There's more you need to understand.*" Her voice dropped into a fair imitation of his careful, apologetic tone, and she saw him wince. "Maren is gone. Possibly dead. Possibly something worse than dead, because I've seen what the Hollow leaves behind and it isn't death, it's *subtraction*. And I am done being fed the truth a spoonful at a time by people who've known it longer than I've been alive."

"I don't know all of it," Tobin said quietly. "I swear that on my grandfather's grave. I've read his notes a hundred times and I still don't—"

"Then read them to me a hundred and one times. Out loud. Starting now."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked down at the codex, and his thumb found the edge of the pages the way a man's thumb finds a scar—automatically, tenderly, a wound he'd been touching all his life.

"She was taken because of me," he said. "Maren. If I hadn't—"

"Maren was taken because the Hollow wanted her, and the Hollow wanted her because she knew things about me that everyone in my life apparently decided I was too fragile to hear." Rosalind heard her own voice rise and did nothing to soften it. "So yes, Tobin. Sit with that if you need to. But you'll sit with it *while reading aloud*."

Something shifted in his face—not anger, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a boy who had spent his whole life in the shadow of a dead man's handwriting and had never once been spoken to like a person with a spine.

He opened the codex.

* * *

The Vellum Codex smelled, up close, like a church and a sickroom at once—incense and old glue and the faint sweet rot of vellum that had been handled too much by too few hands. Tobin turned past the prophecies, past the columns of dates and the careful script Rosalind had seen in the waystation, past the page with her own description that had started all of this, and into a thicket of marginalia that made the main text look tidy as a ledger.

Her grandfather—no, *his* grandfather, she corrected herself, and the correction stung—had written in every direction. Up the margins. Sideways in the gutters. On scraps pasted over older scraps, so that the book was less a book than a palimpsest of one man's obsession, decades of it, ink layered on ink like sediment.

"He stopped sleeping properly around the time he started this," Tobin said. His fingers hovered over a passage without touching it. "My grandmother used to say he was married to the book. She wasn't being cruel. She was being accurate."

"Read."

He read.

"*The Covenant-Bearer will not be found among the Emberwrights, though she will be mistaken for one. The fire knows its own. Watch for the year the passes run warm in winter.*" He turned the page. "*The bearer is not chosen. The bearer is made—no. No, that's not right either. The bearer is* owed. *The covenant keeps its own accounts.*"

"Owed," Rosalind repeated. "What does that mean?"

"I don't know. He wrote it and underlined it and then wrote *ask the keepers* next to it, and I think he did ask the keepers, because there's a letter from a guildmaster—this one—" Tobin fished a folded, yellowed paper from the codex's back binding, "—thanking him for his interest and telling him, in the politest possible language, to stop writing."

Rosalind took the letter. The seal was broken and brown with age, but she recognized the sig stamped into the wax: a flame within a wheel. The Emberwright mark. The guildmaster's signature was a sharp, impatient scrawl—*A. Corvane*.

Corvane had been guildmaster twenty-five years ago. Corvane had been guildmaster her whole life.

She set the letter down very carefully, the way you set down a thing you might later need to throw.

"Keep reading."

"*The bearer's line runs through the forge-mother,*" Tobin read, and Rosalind's breath stopped in her throat, though she could not have said why—only that the words landed in her chest like a hook finding meat. "*Elira of the Emberwrights, who went up to the Spire in the year of the thin snow and did not come back down as herself. The daughter of the forge-mother will carry what the forge-mother left. The fire will know her before she knows herself.*"

The wind moved through the broken watchtower and made a sound like a long, slow exhale.

"Elira," Rosalind said.

"Yes."

"That's my mother's name."

"I know," Tobin said. "I've known since the waystation. It's why I found you—no. It's why I *looked* for you. There's a difference, and I've had a lot of time to think about the difference, and I'm not sure it makes me any better." He rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand. "My grandfather wrote the name Elira forty times in this book. Crossed out and rewritten. He was trying to find her. Or find what happened to her. He traced the Emberwright records, the parish records, the Spire archives—he wrote to the Spire, Rosalind. Nobody writes to the Spire. He wrote anyway."

"And what did he find?"

"He found that Elira of the Emberwrights had a daughter. Born in the guildhall's lower infirmary, midwife's record, dated—" Tobin recited it without looking, the way you recite something you've read so many times it has worn a groove in you. "And the record lists the mother and no father. *Father: not named. Father: not asked.* His note in the margin says, *They didn't ask. In a guild that records everything, no one asked. That's not an oversight. That's a wall.*"

Rosalind stood up. She hadn't decided to stand; her body simply refused to sit any longer, the way a kettle refuses to hold its steam. She walked to the edge of the ruined tower and looked out at the dark shoulder of the Thornfells, where the passes she knew better than her own hands went up and up into country no map troubled itself to record.

Her mother had died when she was six. That was the story. That was the whole story, the entire architecture of it: *your mother was taken by fever, little wren, and your father—well. Some doors are better left shut.* Corvane's voice, in her memory, delivering it with the gentle, distant kindness of a man discussing weather in a country he'd never visit. She had built her life on top of that story the way smugglers build trails on top of avalanche fields—carefully, quietly, never trusting the ground.

And underneath it, all along, had been this. A mother with a name in a prophecy book. A mother who had gone up to the Spire. A guild full of people who had watched a six-year-old girl carry her mother's memory around like a stone in her chest and had said *fever*.

"Who else knew?" she said, without turning around. "In the guild. Who else knew my mother was the forge-mother?"

"Rosalind—"

"*Who else.*"

"Maren," Tobin said. "Almost certainly Maren. She was Elira's contemporary—my grandfather's notes mention a young scribe, M. of the Emberwrights, who corresponded with him for a few years before the letters stopped. And Corvane. Corvane signed the letter. Whether he knew what he was signing for—" Tobin spread his hands. "I've wondered about that for years. The polite ones are sometimes the ones who know the most. Politeness is a very efficient way of not answering questions."

The ember cracked behind her. Rosalind watched a nightbird cross the moon and thought, with a clarity that felt almost like fever, of every kindness Maren had ever shown her. The honeyed tea when she was small and feverish herself. The way Maren had taught her letters using smugglers' marks instead of scripture, because *you'll need the marks more, wren*. The way Maren had never once, in twenty years, asked her about her father—and she had thought, all this time, that it was tact. That it was mercy.

It was a wall. Maren had built it around her, brick by brick, out of silence.

She should have felt grateful. She felt flayed.

* * *

Tobin kept reading. She made him. That was the cruelty she allowed herself, and she knew it was cruelty, and she did it anyway, because if she stopped him now she would never start again, and the truth was a pass she had to walk through in one go or not at all.

"*The forge-mother's offering was not blood,*" he read, in the flat, careful voice he'd adopted to keep his own fear out of it. "*The old rites take blood from the lesser bearers. From her, the Spire took more. The Spire took* memory. *The Spire took* name. *This is why the records fail after the year of the thin snow. This is why no document names Elira's fate. It is not that the record was lost. The record was the payment.*"

"Stop." Rosalind's voice came out strange to her, sanded down. "Say that again."

"The record was the payment."

"She paid with—" Rosalind turned. The ember light moved across her face and she was distantly aware that her eyes were wet and did not care. "She paid with herself. With being remembered. That's what happened to her at the Spire. She didn't die of fever. She went up there and she was *erased*, and then she came back down, and she lived—what, two more years? Three?—and everyone who looked at her saw a woman they couldn't quite place, and no one told her daughter because her daughter was *six*—"

"Rosalind."

"—because a six-year-old can't be trusted with the truth, because the truth would *hurt* her, so instead they let her grow up believing her mother died of a fever in a season when there was no fever, let her grow up thinking she was nobody's daughter in particular, a foundling with a guild badge, a *ghost*—" She heard the word come out of her own mouth and it landed like a blade. "That's what I called myself. That's what I *built*. Twenty years of being invisible because I thought invisibility was all I was ever going to be given. And the whole time—"

"The whole time," Tobin said softly, "the book had you in it."

"The book had me in it before I was *born*." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged. "Do you understand what that means? My mother walked into that Spire knowing. Knowing there'd be a daughter. Knowing what the daughter would have to do. She traded her name to buy time, and the time she bought was *mine*, and everyone who knew—Maren, Corvane, whoever else sat around that guttering brazier passing the secret down like a dish of something bitter—they all looked at me and saw a debt walking around in smuggler's boots and said *nothing*."

"They were protecting you."

"They were *protecting themselves*." The words came out hot. "Protecting themselves from having to be the one who told me. From watching my face when I learned what my mother gave up. From being the messenger. You know what I did for a living, Tobin? I carried things. That was the whole trade—carry the thing through the pass, ask no questions, deliver it, leave. And every single person in my life treated me the same way the guild's clients did. Something valuable. Don't tell it what it's carrying. Don't let it look in the box."

The silence after that had weight. The ember pulsed. Somewhere up the mountain, wind moved through stone, and Rosalind thought she heard—she could not be sure, she would not be sure until later—something beneath the wind. A sound like pages turning. A sound like the Hollow, patient, reading.

Tobin closed the codex gently and stood. He was taller than she'd registered, she realized. When had that happened? He'd arrived at the guildhall a scholar's grandson, all elbows and apologies, and somewhere between the waystation and the ford and this broken tower he had become a person who could stand in front of an angry woman holding a book full of ruin and not step back.

"My grandfather spent forty years on this book," he said. "And you want to know what he wrote at the very end? The last marginal note, in shakier ink, the year before he died?" He opened the back cover and turned to a final pasted scrap, and read: "*I have spent my life asking who the bearer will be. Tonight I asked instead what it costs to be the one who knows and says nothing. The answer: it costs everything, and it buys nothing, and the silence grows in you like a second spine. Tell her. Whoever she is. Whoever finds this. Tell her early. Tell her badly. Just tell her.*"

Rosalind stared at him.

"He wrote that," Tobin said, "and then he never told a living soul, because he died before he found you. I found the note after the funeral. I've been carrying it around for six years, trying to figure out what *tell her* meant, and then I read a date in this book that hadn't happened yet, and a description of a woman with a smuggler's burn scar on her left hand, and I understood that *tell her* was my job now. And I have been terrible at it. I've been so careful and so slow and I told myself it was kindness." His voice cracked, just once, and he pushed through it. "It wasn't kindness. It was fear. The same fear that made your guild stay quiet for twenty years. The same fear that made Maren teach you letters and never your own name's worth."

The anger in Rosalind's chest did not go out. But it changed shape—banked, like the ember, from open flame to something that could be carried. She thought of Maren's hands on the brazier at the ford, pushing it toward Tobin even as the paper face closed over her. *Take it. Go.* Even being taken, the old scribe had spent her last free second on the covenant, on the ember, on the girl.

That was either love or penance. Rosalind did not yet know which, and suspected she would not know until she found Maren again—and she *would* find Maren again, because the Hollow took people whole, it did not kill them, it *kept* them, and what was kept could be reclaimed. She held onto that the way she held the ember's cradle: with both hands, refusing to drop it.

"Read me the rest tomorrow," she said at last. Her voice was rough and steady. "All of it. Every scrap, every crossed-out line. And then you're going to teach me to read his hand myself, because I'm done depending on other people's translations of my own life."

Tobin nodded. He sat back down and, after a moment's hesitation, set the codex down between them, open, so the ember's light fell across the pages—so that the fire, too, could read.

Rosalind did not sleep. She sat with the book in her lap while Tobin finally, exhaustedly, drifted off against the stones, and she traced her grandfather—*his* grandfather's—ink with one fingertip, and she thought about walls. About how the people who love you build them out of silence and call them shelter. About how the only way through a wall is to become the kind of thing that can pass unseen—and how she had spent twenty years becoming exactly that, and how, for the first time, it felt less like a wound and more like a tool.

Near dawn, she found it. A scrap pasted inside the codex's back cover, half-hidden under the letter from Corvane, in Elira's—no. In a hand she did not know, hurried, the ink blotched as if written in a place too cold for the pen to flow:

*If the daughter comes before the hundred years turn, tell her the guildmaster's ledger does not balance. Tell her to count what is missing. Tell her the buyer's mark is a closed eye.*

Rosalind went very still.

She thought of Corvane's study. Of the ledgers she had never been permitted to touch, the records she'd been told were guild business and none of hers. Of the guildmaster's face when she'd brought him the codex—too smooth, too quick, the dismissal coming before the question had finished being asked.

She looked at Tobin, asleep, and at the ember, pulsing, patient, *owed*.

Then she began, quietly, to pack.
