## 10. The Guildmaster's Bargain

The ledger did not lie, and that was the trouble with ledgers.

Corvane had learned that lesson twenty-five years ago, in a winter that smelled of tallow smoke and wet wool, when the Emberwright Guild still had forty members and a name people spoke without laughing. He had been a young guildmaster then—newly sworn, newly certain—and he had sat at this same desk with the same ledger open before him, watching columns of red ink bleed down the page like wounds that would not close.

* * *

The guildhall had been dying by inches. He could still see it if he let himself: the great hearth in the covenant hall burning low because they could no longer afford good coal; the roof over the east wing sagging under snow no one had the coin to clear; the roster book with its thinning ranks, names struck through one by one as smiths and wardens and fire-keepers gave up their vows for honest wages in the lowland foundries. Forty members. Thirty-one. Twenty-six.

And beneath it all, the debt. The debt like a stone tied to the guild's ankle, dragging it down year after year—the loans taken to rebuild after the pass floods, the interest compounding like frost creeping across glass, the creditors' letters arriving with polite, patient menace. The Ironharrow Consortium had been the politest of them. Their representative had come three times, each time sitting in Corvane's office with his gloved hands folded, each time offering the same solution with the same gentle smile.

"Sell the hall," the man had said. "Or sell the rites. Either way, Master Corvane, the guild cannot continue as it is."

"The rites are not for sale."

"Of course not." The smile had widened by a degree, patient as erosion. "Nothing is for sale until it must be."

Corvane had thrown him out. He remembered the small satisfaction of it, and how quickly it had curdled.

That winter, the ember in the great brazier had guttered so low that old Serra Vane, the last fire-keeper who remembered the full rite, had spent whole nights cupping her hands around it like a woman warming herself at a dying campfire. "It's not fed by coal," she told him once, her voice cracked as old glaze. "It's fed by belief. By keeping. Nobody keeps anymore, Corvane. They light candles and call it devotion."

He had nodded the way you nod at an old woman's stories. That was his sin, if he was honest about it now—not greed, not even desperation, but the ordinary arrogance of a practical man confronted with something impractical. He had inherited a guild built on a story: that dragon-fire held back the Hollow, that a bearer carried an ember to a Spire every hundred years, that the world's survival hung on ceremonies performed by men in ash-grey robes. A fine story. It had kept the guild fed for centuries. But stories did not pay creditors, and dragons were nursery tales, and the Hollow—

The Hollow was a word grandmothers used to frighten children toward the fire.

So when the third letter came from the Ironharrow Consortium, and then a fourth, and then a visitor who was not the polite man with the gloves but someone else entirely—someone who arrived on the night coach with no luggage and paid his fare in coin too old to be minted—Corvane had been tired enough, and frightened enough, and skeptical enough, to listen.

* * *

The man had waited for him in the covenant hall, standing before the great brazier where the last ember sat in its iron cradle, glowing like a coal of memory. He was unremarkable in every way except one: his face. Not wrong, exactly. Just smooth. Unlined. Like a face that had never been called upon to express anything, worn smooth the way a stair is worn smooth by feet—or the way paper stays smooth when nothing has ever been written on it.

"Guildmaster." His voice was pleasant. Reasonable. The voice of a man discussing grain prices. "Thank you for seeing me."

"I didn't."

"You came down anyway." The smooth face inclined toward the brazier. "A remarkable artifact. Centuries old, they say. And yet—" he leaned closer, studying the ember with the mild interest of a collector, "—it does very little, doesn't it? It warms a room. It lights a candle. For all that ceremony, for all that cost, for all those vows your people take—" he turned back, and his eyes were flat as river stones, "—what has it ever actually done?"

"It holds," Corvane said. "It holds back the Hollow."

"Does it?" The man smiled, and the smile reached every part of his face evenly, like water finding its level. "Where is this Hollow, Master Corvane? Point to it on a map. Show me the border it stands behind. Show me one soul it has devoured in your lifetime. One."

Corvane opened his mouth. Closed it. Because the answer was: nowhere. No one had seen the Hollow. No one had seen the dragons either. The covenant was a wall no one had ever seen an enemy attack.

"You keep a fire against a wolf," the man said gently, "that no one has ever heard howl. And the fire costs you everything. Your hall. Your members. Your future." He spread his hands. "I am not your enemy, Guildmaster. I am offering to buy a story. Stories can't be stolen. Only sold."

"What would you even do with it?"

"Preserve it." Another level smile. "There are collectors of such things. The rites, the words, the diagrams of the Spire's heart. Paper knowledge. Harmless as paper." He produced a purse and set it on the brazier's stone lip, and the sound of it—dense, heavy, final—was the loudest thing Corvane had ever heard. "Enough to clear your debts twice over. Enough to feed forty people through ten winters. Enough to save the guild, Master Corvane. Isn't that what a guildmaster is for?"

He remembered standing there a long time. Remembered the ember glowing in its cradle, doing nothing, saving no one he could see. Remembered the roster book upstairs with its struck-through names, and Serra Vane asleep in her chair beside the fire she believed the world depended on.

He remembered thinking: *if the story were true, the fire would show me a sign.*

The fire showed him nothing at all.

He took the purse.

* * *

Afterward—for years afterward—he had almost managed to forget it. The debts cleared. The guild stabilized. He raised roofs and paid wages and rebuilt the membership slowly, carefully, choosing pragmatists over mystics, men who tended the ember the way you tend any heirloom: respectfully, and without belief. Serra Vane died that spring, muttering about signs, and he appointed no one to replace her full duties, because full duties required full pay, and the fire had never asked for anything before.

The buyer never returned. The papers he purchased—the rite of renewal, the bearer's litany, the diagrams of the Spire's heart—sat in some collector's vault, harmless as paper.

Except.

Except that the codex went missing that same year. Except that the renewal, due at the century's turn, had quietly failed to happen—no bearer sent, no ember carried, no Spire approached—and the world had conspicuously declined to end. Except that lately, in the past months, there had been reports. A village emptied at the mountain's edge, doors open, meals half-eaten. Shepherds coming down from the high passes grey-faced, saying they'd met travelers on the trails who walked without leaving prints, whose faces—

Whose faces were smooth.

Corvane sat in his office now, twenty-five years older, and read the reports again by lamplight, and felt the bargain he had made finally, finally turning its face toward him.

Because there was more. There was the girl.

Lockridge. Rosalind Lockridge. The smuggler who had come staggering into his guildhall with the Vellum Codex in her pack and tomorrow's date written on its final page and her own description inked beside it in a hand a hundred years dead. Maren had recognized the handwriting. Maren, with her soft heart and her dangerous reverence, had looked at the girl like looking at scripture.

And Corvane had looked at her and seen a ledger entry. Because he knew—had known since the night the buyer left—that the codex had been copied, that somewhere out in the world existed a second account of the covenant's terms, and that whoever held it held proof. Proof of what the guild had sold. Proof of what the guild had failed to do. Proof that the guildmaster who had saved the Emberwrights from bankruptcy had done it by selling the only thing that mattered.

If the girl was right—if the prophecy was real—then the Hollow was real, and it was loose, and it was loose *because of him*. Twenty-five years of hunger between realms, unfed, unopposed, gathering. Every empty village, every smooth-faced traveler, every shepherd's terrified account traced back along a single line to a purse set on a brazier's lip by his own hand.

And if the girl was wrong—if it was all superstition after all—then she was still a threat, because she had the codex, and the codex had the dates, and dates made people believe. Belief was the one thing he could not afford now. Belief would bring questions. Questions would bring the ledger. His ledger, with its careful entries, the payments recorded in his own neat hand across the years—not just once, but *annually*, because the buyer had insisted on a retainer, a modest sum each year to "maintain the arrangement," and Corvane had told himself it was insurance, protection money, nothing more, while his pen scratched the entries into permanence:

*To the Ironharrow Consortium, for archival services rendered.*

Twenty-five years of entries. A confession written in his own handwriting, filed in his own cabinet, waiting for one curious apprentice or one ambitious rival to find.

He had sent hunters. That was the practical thing. Bren Dain and six of the best, with orders worded carefully—*bring her back, or bring back proof she's a fraud*—because Corvane was not a murderer, whatever else he was. He told himself that often enough now that the sentence had begun to feel load-bearing. He was not a murderer. He was a man protecting his guild from a fraud and a smuggler who'd stolen guild property. The codex belonged to the Emberwrights. Technically. In spirit. In the way that everything he had ever rationalized belonged to him.

But he had also sent a second message. Quietly. Through channels he had not used in twenty-five years. To the Consortium. To the smooth-faced man, or whatever answered in his place these days.

*The bearer has surfaced. The codex with her. Handle it.*

He had told himself that was practical too. Containment. If the Hollow was real, better it be occupied chasing the girl than walking into his guildhall. If it wasn't real, the message would vanish into whatever vault swallowed such messages, and no harm done.

Either way, he slept badly.

* * *

Tonight he slept worse.

He woke past midnight to a silence with a texture to it—the particular hush of a room that has been listened to. His candle had burned to a stub. The ledger lay open on his desk where he'd left it, and he had left it closed—he was nearly sure he had left it closed—and it lay open to the page dated twenty-five years ago, to the first entry, the first payment, the neat line of his own handwriting.

Beneath it, in a script that was not his, in ink that gleamed faintly wet, someone had added a single line:

*We accept delivery of the remainder.*

Remainder. He stood in his cold office reading those two words over and over, and his mind, practical to the last, laid out the arithmetic for him with terrible clarity. The rites had been the first payment. The retainer had been the second. But the buyer had always spoken of the purchase in the singular—a story, a whole story, complete—and Corvane had sold only the words. The rest of the covenant—the ember, the bearer, the Spire itself—had never been delivered.

Until now. Until the bearer surfaced, and he had sent word: *handle it.* And the thing on the other end of twenty-five years of polite correspondence had understood, in its smooth and patient way, exactly what was being handed over at last.

*We accept delivery of the remainder.*

"No," Corvane said aloud, to the empty room, to the ledger, to whatever read along with him. "No. I didn't—I never meant—"

The candle went out. Not guttered. Went out, all at once, like a word erased.

In the dark, he heard the front gate of the guildhall open below—slow, unhurried, the sound of a key turning in a lock by someone who had every right to use it. Then footsteps in the courtyard. More than two. Even. Patient. Coming up the path toward the hall where the last ember burned in its cradle, doing nothing, saving no one he could see.

Corvane snatched the ledger off the desk and held it against his chest like a shield, like a child's blanket, like the only evidence in the world of what he'd done and therefore the only thing that might save him—and ran for the covenant hall, because whatever was climbing his stairs, whatever had come to collect the remainder, it would have to go through the fire first.

Through the fire that had never shown him a sign.

Through the fire he had sold.

* * *

He reached the hall as the doors opened.

They did not burst open. They swung inward gently, the way doors open for expected guests, and the cold came in with them—not wind-cold but a different cold, a subtracting cold, a cold that took warmth away rather than bringing it. Four figures stood in the doorway, backlit by starlight, and even at that distance Corvane could see that their faces caught the ember-light and gave none of it back. Smooth. Blank. Patient as paper.

"We're closed," Corvane heard himself say, and his voice came out thin and absurd, a merchant's reflex, a shopkeeper's plea. "Whatever you've been told, we—we have nothing here for sale."

The foremost figure tilted its head, considering him with the mild interest of a collector.

"On the contrary," it said pleasantly, reasonably, in a voice like grain prices. "You have everything. You always did." It stepped forward, and its companions fanned out with terrible unhurried grace, moving to flank the brazier, cutting it off from the door. "You sold us the words, Guildmaster. Now you've sent us the bearer's trail. All that remains is the ember." The smooth face inclined toward the cradle, toward the coal of memory glowing there, and something in the gesture was almost courteous. "You've been holding our property for twenty-five years. We've simply come to balance the books."

Corvane looked at the ember—small, dim, faithful, the thing he had never believed in and had betrayed anyway—and for the first time in his life he understood what Serra Vane had tried to tell him on the nights she cupped her hands around it like a dying campfire.

It was not fed by coal.

It was fed by keeping.

And he had never kept anything in his life.

"No," he said again, but the figures were closing, and the ledger was heavy against his chest, and somewhere far to the north, up roads no map recorded, a smuggler with a codex in her pack was carrying the last ember of the old world toward a Spire of black glass—and Corvane, guildmaster of the Emberwrights, keeper of nothing, suddenly understood that the only way left to save himself was the one thing he had spent twenty-five years refusing to do.

Believe.

He lunged for the brazier.
