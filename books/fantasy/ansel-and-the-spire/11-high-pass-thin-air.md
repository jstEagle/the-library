## 11. High Pass, Thin Air

The treeline gave up on them two hours past dawn, surrendering in stages—first the pines thinned to wind-bent shrubs, then the shrubs to lichen, then even the lichen to bare stone furred with old snow. Above that there was nothing but the mountain's grey spine and a sky so wide and empty it felt less like weather than like exposure. Rosalind had crossed the Thornfells a hundred times, and she had never once thought of the high passes as anything but road.

Now they felt like a wound.

She felt it before she understood it: a wrongness in the satchel slung against her ribs, the way a mother feels a child go quiet in the next room. She stopped on the switchback, boots skidding on frost-slick rock, and pressed her palm flat against the leather.

The ember was dimming.

"Rosa?" Tobin was ten paces behind, bent into the wind, his grandfather's codex wrapped in oilcloth and strapped to his back like a hunch he'd grown accustomed to. Maren came after him, slower, her breath tearing white in the thin air. "What is it?"

"It's cold." Rosalind unslung the satchel with hands that had gone careful, the way they went careful around anything that mattered. "The ember's going cold."

She opened the satchel's double flap and looked down into the iron brazier-cage the Emberwrights had trusted to her—the last dragon-fire in the world, carried like a coal in a cradle of black metal and old prayers. Two nights ago it had burned steady, a bead of orange light that never guttered no matter how hard the wind screamed at it. Last night at the ford, after the faceless things came and took Maren's memories and very nearly took Maren herself, it had flared like a warning.

Now it was the size of a lentil. And it was the color of a dying coal—orange bleeding to red, red bleeding to that dull grey-black that meant a fire had already given up and the eye simply hadn't caught it yet.

"No," Maren said. She pushed past Tobin, and Rosalind stepped aside to let the old scribe look, because Maren had tended the guildhall brazier for forty years and knew fire the way Rosalind knew passes. Maren's face, when she finished looking, was the worst thing Rosalind had seen since the empty village. "It's feeding on her."

"On me?" Rosalind looked down at her own hands as if they might be bleeding. "I haven't—it's not taking anything. I'd know."

"It's not taking, child. It's asking." Maren pulled her scarf down so her voice would carry over the wind. "Dragon-fire doesn't burn wood or coal. It burns what's given. Willingly. That's the whole of the covenant—that's what the old rites *mean*. The Emberwrights didn't keep dragons. They kept a *conversation*. And the fire's been running on what your mother gave it for twenty-five years, and the account's come due."

The wind came down the pass in a long, flat shove, and Rosalind braced against it and watched the ember shrink a hair's width smaller, and felt something in her chest shrink with it.

"How do we feed it?" Tobin asked. He had the codex half-unwrapped already, snow gathering on the oilcloth. "There must be rites. My grandfather wrote—"

"He wrote what he was told." Maren's voice had gone flat, the flatness of someone standing at the edge of a thing they'd spent a lifetime avoiding. "The rites are words, boy. Words are the shape of the giving, not the giving itself. The fire takes what you offer and it takes it *truly*—not the way you give a coin to a beggar and keep the memory of having it. When you give to the fire, it's gone. Whatever it is. Whatever it was."

Rosalind looked at the ember. The ember, faintly, seemed to look back—or that was the thin air talking, the altitude pressing on her skull until everything up here felt animate and watching. She thought of the shrine two days south, where the brazier had flared at her presence like a dog hearing its name. She thought of the codex's final page. *The daughter of the forge-mother.*

"Then I'll give it something," she said.

* * *

They made camp in the lee of a shattered ridge, a half-circle of fallen stone that broke the wind just enough to keep the brazier-cage from being scoured. There was no wood up here, no fuel but what they carried, and the cold came up out of the rock itself like the mountain was breathing at them from below. Rosalind sat cross-legged with the open satchel in her lap and the ember's dull red glow painting her knuckles, and she thought about what a person owns, really owns, when their whole life has been built to be abandoned.

She'd learned it young. A smuggler's first lesson: never carry what you can't lose. No letters, no portraits, no keepsakes with names scratched into them. Her gear weighed exactly what it weighed and nothing in it pointed back at her. She had spent twenty years being a ghost, and a ghost's pockets are always nearly empty.

Except.

Her hand went to the cord at her throat before she'd decided anything, the way a tongue goes to a missing tooth. Beneath her shirt, against her skin, hung a small oilskin packet no bigger than a coin purse. She hadn't opened it in years. She wasn't sure she could have said, out loud, why she still carried it—only that on the one night a year she let herself think about her mother, she took it out, and looked, and put it away, and that was enough. That had always been enough.

She untied it now.

Inside, in a nest of waxed paper gone brittle with age, lay a lock of hair. Dark, near-black, with a single strand of deep red running through it like a vein of ore in stone. Her mother's. Cut—Maren had told her once, in one of the old scribe's rare unguarded moments—on the morning Elira Lockridge rode north to the Spire and never came back. A keepsake for the child. A promise that she'd been real. That someone with this hair had held Rosalind on her hip and sung to her and chosen, at the end, to walk into a tower and let a fire eat her name.

The ember stirred.

It was a small movement—a flicker, a lean, the way a candle leans toward an opening door—but Rosalind felt it in the satchel, in her sternum, in her teeth. The fire knew what was in her hand. Of course it did. It had been burning on Elira Lockridge's sacrifice for a quarter century. It knew its own.

"No," Tobin said quietly. He'd seen. He was sitting close enough to see, and his face had gone the particular grey of a man watching someone about to do something irreversible. "Rosa. That's—she gave you that. That's the only—"

"I know what it is."

"It's the only piece of her you *have*."

Rosalind closed her hand around the lock and the oilskin crinkled and the ember flared again, hungry, hopeful, and she understood with terrible clarity that this was the shape of the whole covenant. Not glory. Not prophecy. Just this: a woman on a cold mountain, holding the last of her mother, deciding.

"The alternative," she said, "is that it goes out. And then the Hollow comes down out of the between-places, and it doesn't take pieces of us, Tobin. It takes all of us. It took a whole village in a night and left the soup warm on the tables." She looked at him, and her voice came out steadier than she felt, smuggler-steady, the voice she used when the border guards were close. "My mother gave everything. Her name. Her memories. Herself. I'm being asked for a lock of hair. I can do the arithmetic."

"That's not arithmetic. That's grief wearing arithmetic's clothes."

She almost smiled. He was getting braver, the scribe's grandson. At the ford he'd frozen while the faceless things closed in, and she'd seen the shame sit on him like a stone ever since, and now here he was finding his voice at the worst possible moment, which was, she was learning, the only moment that mattered.

"Maren," she said. "The rites. Give me the words."

Maren had turned her back to the wind and was watching the horizon with the rigid stillness of a woman who had kept too many secrets and was watching the bill arrive. But she turned, and she came, and she knelt in the snow across the brazier-cage, and her voice dropped into the old cadence—the guildhall cadence, the one Rosalind had heard murmured over the last dragon-fire her whole life without ever knowing it was a grammar of surrender.

"*What is given is given,*" Maren said. "*What is burned is not lost, but changed. Fire of the first forges, fire of the last keep, take what is freely offered and remember for us what we cannot keep.*" Her eyes came up, wet and fierce. "That's all there is. The words don't bind it. The *meaning* binds it. You have to mean the giving, Rosalind. All of it. If you hold anything back, it takes the holding back too, and that's worse."

Rosalind unwrapped the oilskin the rest of the way.

The wind dropped. Not gentled—*dropped*, the way sound drops, all at once, so that the whole pass went silent as a held breath. Snow hung in the air without falling. The ember rose from its bed of ash and hung in the iron cage, a lentil of red light, and it turned toward her hand the way a face turns.

*Oh*, Rosalind thought. *You're alive. You've been alive this whole time, and everyone who tended you was talking to someone.*

"I don't remember her," she said aloud, to it, because the words needed somewhere to go and the fire was the only listener that mattered. "That's the truth. She died when I was six and everyone who remembered her either forgot on purpose or had it eaten out of them, and I've spent my whole life carrying a woman I can't picture. I don't know her voice. I don't know her hands. I know one song and I don't even know if I remember the song or just remember being told I used to know it." Her throat closed. She pushed through it. "But she was real. This proves she was real. And if I give it to you, no one will ever be able to prove it again."

The ember pulsed. Once. Soft. Not impatient. *Compassionate*, she would have said, if she'd had the word for it, and she did not, and the lack of the word felt like the whole problem with her life.

She meant the giving. She found the place in herself where the lock of hair lived—not as an object but as a weight, a *this is mine and it is my mother and it is the last of her*—and she opened that place the way you open a fist, finger by finger, against every instinct the body has.

She fed the fire her mother's hair.

It didn't burn. That was the terrible, beautiful thing. The lock simply left—passed from her fingers into the light the way a word passes from a mouth into air—and the ember drank it, and *blazed*. Color came back into it in a flood: orange, then gold, then a white so deep it had blue in it, dragon-fire's true color, the color Maren had described a hundred times and Rosalind had never once believed. Heat rolled off the brazier-cage and melted a circle in the snow around them and steam rose and the mountain, for one moment, was not indifferent.

Then the flare settled into a steady, healthy burn, and the wind returned, and Rosalind knelt in the meltwater with her empty hands open in her lap, and the packet at her throat was just a packet, just oilskin, just a shape where something used to be.

She had expected to cry. What came instead was worse and cleaner: a vast, ringing *lightness*, as if she'd set down a pack she'd forgotten she was wearing. And underneath the lightness, a small cold voice that said: *you can carry less now. You are easier to move. You are one step closer to nothing at all.*

"Rosalind." Maren took her hands. The old scribe's grip was iron wrapped in paper. "It will come back for more. You understand that. The hair was a down payment. It's asking because the account is deep—twenty-five years deep—and the higher we climb, the more it costs to keep it burning."

"Then it can keep asking," Rosalind said. "I'll keep deciding."

* * *

They climbed through the afternoon, and the ember burned steady, and Rosalind told herself that was the end of it.

She should have known better. Nothing in the covenant had ever been cheap. Her mother had paid a name. The Emberwrights had paid a guild, dwindling decade by decade to four old people huddled around a brazier, and then to three, and then to whatever they were now—a smuggler, a scribe, a half-hollowed woman, walking north with the world's last fire in a cage.

The pass narrowed as the sun went down, the ridge rearing up on their left like a black wave about to break, a sheer drop yawning on their right into blue glacier-shadow. The air went thin enough to taste of iron. Rosalind's lungs burned with every step, and the cold stopped being weather and became a presence, an intelligence, something leaning on them from all sides at once. This was the height where even smugglers turned back. This was the height where the passes stopped being roads and became ordeals, and the old maps simply stopped, and the cartographers wrote *here be the Spire's shadow* and left the rest blank.

The ember dimmed at moonrise.

She felt it the same way as before—the wrongness against her ribs, the child gone quiet—but this time she didn't stop walking, because she already knew what stopping would mean. She climbed and the ember shrank and the cold leaned harder, and by the time the ridge offered its one sheltered hollow she was moving on will alone, and the fire in the cage had shrunk back to a lentil, and then to a pinprick, and its light had gone that grey-black again, the color of a question already asked.

She opened the satchel and looked at it and it looked at her, and this time there was no mistaking the asking.

"It's not enough," she said. Her voice came out cracked by the altitude. "The hair. It wasn't enough."

"It was never going to be enough." Maren lowered herself onto a stone with the careful movements of a woman whose joints had opinions. "I told you the account was deep. Twenty-five years, Rosalind. Your mother bought a century with everything she had and it's still come up short, and the fire is running the last mile on fumes and faith." She met Rosalind's eyes, and there was something in the old scribe's face Rosalind had never seen there before—not guilt exactly, though there was plenty of that, but *recognition*, the look of someone watching a younger version of themselves walk a road they know to the end. "It will take what you treasure. That's how it works. That's how it's always worked. The covenant isn't fed with things. It's fed with *cost*."

"Then it can have my gold. My gear. My—"

"Those aren't treasure to you. You built your whole life so they wouldn't be." Maren's voice gentled, which was somehow more merciless. "You're a ghost, child. You made yourself a ghost. The fire can't live on a ghost's pockets."

Rosalind sat with the brazier-cage in her lap and the pinprick of dying light and the wind screaming over the ridge above them, and she did the inventory she'd spent twenty years refusing to do.

Her gear: replaceable. Her gold: replaceable. Her name: already for sale, if the codex was true, already promised to a flame at the end of this road. Her routes, her passes, her smuggler's knowledge: hers, but earned, and earned things can be re-earned. Her anger at Corvane: she could live without it, gladly. Her grief—

Her grief was not on the table. Her grief was load-bearing.

Which left one thing, and she had known it was the one thing since Maren first said the word *asking*, and she had been climbing all day toward it the way you climb toward a door you don't want to open.

"I remember a song," she said.

Tobin looked up sharply. Maren went very still.

"It's nothing. It's four lines. Maybe five. I don't even know the words right—I know the *shape* of the words, the way they went, the rise and fall of it. She used to sing it when I couldn't sleep, and I don't remember her singing it, I just remember—remember that there was a sound, and it was low, and it went up at the end like a question, like she was asking the dark to be kind to me, and the dark said yes." Rosalind's hands had closed around the iron cage, knuckles white, and she hadn't told them to. "It's the only thing I have left of her that isn't an object. It's not hair. It's not a page in a book. It's the last living piece of her, because it's not a *thing* she left, it's a thing she *did*. She was doing it *to* me. For me. And it's the last place she's still—" Her voice broke and she let it, because Maren had said the fire took the holding back too, and she was done holding back. "It's the last place she's still *happening*."

The ember flared at the word *happening*. Small. Attentive. Listening.

"If I give it," Rosalind said, "I won't lose the fact of her. I'll still know Elira Lockridge was my mother, still know she went to the Spire, still know she chose it. I'll lose—" she swallowed— "I'll lose the sound. I'll lose the one moment where she isn't a story I was told. And then she'll be a story I was told, all the way down, and I will never, for the rest of my life, hear my mother's voice again. Not even in my head. Especially not in my head."

The wind howled. The pinprick dimmed another shade, patient, terrible, giving her all the time in the world, which was the cruelest mercy she could imagine.

"Rosalind." Tobin's voice, rough. He'd pulled the codex out and was holding it against his chest like a shield. "There has to be another way. Write it down. If you sing it now, I'll write it down, every note, and then even if the fire takes it, the ink—"

"The fire takes what's *given*." Maren's voice was quiet and absolute. "But the giving has to be true, and a thing written down isn't given, it's *kept*. If she gives the song while a copy sits in that book, the fire will know. And the fire will take the book instead. Or take the words from every page and every eye that reads them." She looked at Tobin with something close to pity. "You can't out-clerk a dragon's fire, boy. Your grandfather learned that. It's why the codex is riddles and margins. It's why he never wrote the rites plain."

Tobin's mouth closed. He looked down at the codex, at his grandfather's careful, evasive hand, and Rosalind saw him understand, all at once, what kind of man his grandfather had had to be—someone who loved the truth too much to write it down.

Rosalind sang.

She sang it once, there in the hollow, low, under the wind—four lines, maybe five, the melody rising at the end like a question. Her voice was bad with cold and thin with altitude and it cracked on the third line and she didn't stop. Tobin's eyes went wet. Maren bowed her head as if in prayer, and maybe it was, because the melody was older than the guild, older than the Spire, a lullaby that had been sung to covenant-bearers since the first forge was lit, and Maren was hearing it for the first time in fifty years and the last time in her life.

Rosalind sang it a second time, slower, memorizing her own memorizing, fixing the shape of it in the air one final time so that the last thing she gave would be the whole of it.

Then she found the place inside where the song lived—not the words, the *sound*, the low warmth, the question rising, the dark saying yes—and she opened it the way she'd opened her fist over the lock of hair, finger by finger, against every instinct she had.

"*What is given is given,*" Maren whispered. "*What is burned is not lost, but changed.*"

Rosalind fed the fire her mother's lullaby.

It didn't hurt, and that was the horror of it. It was like exhaling a breath she'd been holding for twenty years. The song simply left, passed out of her into the light, and the ember *roared*—a soundless roar, a bloom, white-gold fire filling the iron cage and throwing their shadows huge and dancing up the black ridge wall—and the cold broke. Not eased. *Broke*, like a fever, like a siege lifting, and the air around the brazier went warm as a kitchen in summer, and Rosalind knelt in that small circle of impossible summer on the roof of the world and felt the silence where the song had been.

She reached for it, the way you reach for a dream on waking.

Nothing. There was a shape where it had been, a lullaby-shaped absence, and she knew that her mother had sung to her, knew it the way she knew her own name, and the knowing was a closed door, and behind the door was nothing she would ever reach again.

She had thought she'd cry. Instead she laughed—one short, cracked, astonished sound—and pressed her forehead against the warm iron of the cage.

"You could have warned me," she said to the fire, and the fire burned on, steady and gold and utterly without apology.

* * *

They slept in watches, huddled in the warm radius of the ember like travelers around a hearth, which was, Rosalind supposed, exactly what they were. She took the last watch, when the sky had gone the deep bruised purple that comes before dawn, and she sat with her back to the stone and the brazier-cage on her knees and she took the inventory again, because it was the only prayer she had left.

Gear: replaceable. Gold: replaceable. Mother's hair: gone. Mother's voice: gone. Name: promised. What remained was a woman who was lighter than she had ever been and colder in one specific place, and who was, she realized with a strange calm, no longer afraid of the Spire. The fire had been teaching her the whole climb, lesson by lesson, and the final lesson was this: everything the covenant would ask of her at the tower's heart, she had already practiced. She knew how to open her hand now. She knew what it cost. She knew she could pay it.

The east was just beginning to grey when she heard it.

Not the wind. The wind had a voice she knew, all its weathers and moods. This was under the wind—a rhythm, a crunch of boot on frozen scree, regular as a heartbeat, coming up the pass behind them. She killed the brazier's light with the cage's iron shutter, a trick Maren had taught her, and the gold vanished and the dark came in like water.

Two points of movement on the switchbacks below. Then four. Then six, fanned out across the face of the mountain with the unhurried competence of people who knew exactly where their quarry had to pass, closing off the road ahead and the road behind.

And on the wind, faint but unmistakable, carried up from the dark below: the ring of drawn steel, and a voice Rosalind knew, calling up the mountain in a tone that tried to be gentle and did not succeed.

"Rosalind. Rosalind Lockridge. We need to talk."
