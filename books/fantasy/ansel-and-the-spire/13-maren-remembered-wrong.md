## 13. Maren, Remembered Wrong

They found the trail on the third morning after the ford, and it was wrong in a way Rosalind couldn't name until she was standing in it.

Tobin spotted it first—a thread of grey wool snagged on hawthorn, the same grey as Maren's shawl. Then another, a hundred paces on. Then a boot print in the soft ground beside a stream, pressed deep, unhurried. The faceless ones did not run with their captives. They walked. What was there to flee from? Nothing behind them could hurt them, and nothing ahead of them wanted anything but to open.

"She's alive," Rosalind said, and heard how much she wanted it to be true, how much the wanting bent her voice. "They don't kill. They *take*."

"That's not comfort," Tobin said quietly. "You know that's not comfort."

She knew. She had spent the night at the ford not sleeping, listening to the river, turning over and over the image of Maren's face going slack as the paper-faced thing gripped her, the old scribe's mouth opening on a scream that the water swallowed whole. She had told herself *alive* like a charm. But Tobin was right. The Hollow didn't kill. Killing ended a thing. The Hollow *edited* it, and an edited thing went on walking, and talking, and smiling at you, and that was so much worse.

They followed the prints north along the river until the valley narrowed, then up a sheep-track Rosalind knew from her smuggling years—a path that switchbacked through bracken to a shepherds' bothy below a crag called the Old Man's Tooth. The faceless ones would not have chosen it. But they had not chosen it. The prints simply went there, because Maren's feet had carried her there, because some part of the old woman had known these hills for sixty years and her legs remembered even if nothing else was left to her.

Rosalind didn't know that yet. She only knew the bothy's low roof against the sky, smoke-thin and blue, and her heart slamming against her ribs like something trying to get out.

* * *

They approached the way smugglers approach everything: wide apart, downwind, quiet. Rosalind went first. The door of the bothy stood ajar, and through the gap came the smell of peat smoke and, absurdly, of porridge.

Maren sat on the stool by the hearth, stirring a pot.

She looked—Rosalind's breath caught—she looked *fine*. Better than fine. The bruises at her wrists had faded. Her hands were steady on the spoon. Her shawl was mended where the faceless ones had torn it, mended with neat, even stitches, and her hair had been rebraided. They had kept her like a housekeeper keeps a house. Tidied her. Put everything back in its place.

Except.

"Maren." Rosalind stepped through the door, and the old woman looked up with a polite, faintly puzzled smile, the smile you give a stranger who has entered your kitchen.

"Hello," Maren said. Her voice was Maren's voice, dry and warm as old paper. "I'm afraid you've caught me at breakfast. There's not a great deal, but you're welcome to share. Are you travelers? The road's been quiet lately. Too quiet, some say." She chuckled at her own joke, a soft rustling sound.

Rosalind stood in the doorway and felt the floor of the world tilt.

"Maren. It's me."

"Is it?" The old woman's eyes—sharp, brown, quick—moved over her face without a flicker of recognition. Not hostility. Not fear. Just the mild, open blankness of a stranger. "I'm sorry, dear. Have we met? You have an honest face. I'd remember it, I think." She smiled again. "I remember everything, you know. That's my curse and my pride both. Ask me anything. The names of every apprentice I ever trained. The price of lamp-oil in the year of the great frost. What my mother said to me the last morning of her life, word for word." She tapped her temple. "It's all in here. Perfect as the day it was written."

"Ask her about the codex," Tobin said from the doorway, very quietly.

Rosalind's mouth had gone dry. "Maren. The Vellum Codex. You recognized the handwriting. You told us—it was your master's hand, you said, the prophecy-scribe Ashvale—"

"Oh, yes." Maren nodded pleasantly. "The codex. I know it well. A remarkable book. I spent thirty years of my life with it, copying, collating, cross-referencing." She stirred the pot. "I couldn't tell you why it matters, mind. Some old superstition or other. Dragons and covenants and a bearer who carries the fire." She laughed. "Superstitions are like that. They get into your bones when you're young and never quite leave."

"You spent thirty years—" Rosalind's voice cracked. "Maren, you *believed*. You were the one who believed when Corvane called it forgery. You told me the covenant was real. You told me about the Spire. You—"

"I said all that?" Maren looked genuinely curious, the way a scholar looks at a footnote she can't source. "How strange. I don't recall it. And I recall *everything*, dear. That's the whole trouble." She set down the spoon and folded her hands in her lap, and for a moment something moved behind her eyes—something vast and smooth and patient, like water closing over a dropped stone—and then it was gone, and she was only a kind old woman offering breakfast to strangers.

"Sit," Maren said. "The porridge is getting cold."

* * *

Tobin made her leave the bothy. She didn't remember deciding to go; she remembered his hand on her arm and the door closing and then she was standing in the bracken with the wind coming off the crag and her whole body shaking.

"She's in there," Rosalind said. "She's *in there*, Tobin. Everything except—"

"Except you."

"Except me." She laughed, and it came out wrong, high and cracked. "Do you understand what that is? They didn't take her memories. They took *one* memory and all its roots. Every moment we ever shared—gone. And not just gone. She's filled the hole with *nothing*, with smooth blank certainty, and she doesn't even feel it as a gap. A gap you'd notice. This is—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "It's like a tooth pulled. The tongue goes to the socket and the socket's healed over and after a while you can't even remember which tooth it was. You just know your mouth is smaller than it used to be."

Tobin was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, he was looking at the crag, not at her, and his voice had the careful flatness of a man handling something sharp.

"My grandfather wrote about this. In the marginalia. I didn't— I read the passage and I didn't understand it, and I'm sorry, Rosalind, I'm sorry I didn't understand it sooner." He pulled the codex's loose sheaf of notes from his coat, found the page with fingers that weren't quite steady. "*The Hollow does not devour,*" he read. "*Devouring implies appetite, and appetite implies want. The Hollow is want. What it does to the taken is not consumption but correction. It removes what does not serve it, and smooths the edges of the wound until the wound is invisible. The taken are not destroyed. They are revised.*"

"Revised," Rosalind repeated.

"Revised toward silence." He lowered the page. "That's why they took Maren and let her go—or left her where we'd find her. It wasn't mercy. It was a message. *We can do this to anyone. We can do it to everyone who knows you.* Every person who remembers you is a door into you, and they went through the oldest door they could find and they shut it behind them."

The wind moved through the bracken, a long dry hiss, like pages turning.

"They're editing me out of the world," Rosalind said. "Before I ever get to the Spire. They don't need to kill the Covenant-Bearer. They just need everyone who might have helped her to wake up one morning and not know why they're standing in a doorway holding bread for someone who isn't there."

"And you," Tobin said. "What did they take from *you*? You were in the water too. You touched it."

She hadn't let herself think about it. Now, prompted, she reached inward the way you probe a sore tooth, and found—nothing missing. Her mother's lullaby, spent on the high pass, was a deliberate loss, a clean cut she had made herself, and it ached but it ached *honestly*. Everything else was there. The guildhall. The ledger page. Maren's face over the brazier, the first time the old scribe had looked at her and *known*.

"No," she said slowly. "They didn't touch me. They can't, can they? Not while I carry the ember. The fire remembers me. It's the one record of me they can't revise." She looked at her own hands, at the faint warmth that lived in the wrapped lantern at her back even now, even here, like a second heartbeat. "That's why they took Maren instead. They can't get at the source, so they're cutting the channels."

* * *

They went back in. Rosalind made herself. It was the hardest thing she had done since the ford—harder, because at the ford the enemy had at least been *strange*, smooth faces and wrong movements, a horror you could run from. This horror poured tea.

Maren had set out three bowls. She fussed over them, added a pinch of salt to each from a stoppered jar, and when Rosalind sat down across from her the old woman said, "You take it with honey, I think. No—" she frowned, a small crease of genuine puzzlement, "—I don't know why I think that. I don't know you at all. But my hands wanted to put out honey." There was a jar on the table. She had set it out without knowing why.

Rosalind's chest hurt.

"Maren. I'm going to ask you some things. Just—answer as best you can."

"Of course, dear. I do love questions. Questions are how you find out what you know."

"The guildhall. The Emberwrights. Tell me about them."

"Ah, the Emberwrights!" Maren's face lit with true fondness. "A dying order, I'm afraid, but a proud one. Four of us left, when last I counted. Corvane, who is careful the way a man is careful when he's standing on a rotten floor. Sella, who tends the brazier. Bren, who hunts. And—" she paused, and her smile flickered, just barely, a candle in a draft, "—and me. Maren. Scribe. Keeper of records."

"Four," Rosalind said. "You said four. Corvane, Sella, Bren, and you."

"Yes."

"There was a fifth."

"Was there?" Maren laughed. "Dear, I would know. I remember everyone. Every face I've ever seen is filed away in here, perfect as the day I saw it. If there had been a fifth, I'd—" She stopped. Her spoon had gone still in the bowl. For a moment, three seconds, five, the old woman sat looking at the middle distance with an expression Rosalind had no name for, an expression like a person standing in a room they've lived in for forty years and feeling, all at once, that one wall is the wrong color. Then it passed. "Well," Maren said briskly. "The porridge won't eat itself."

Tobin's hand found Rosalind's under the table and gripped it hard.

"Tell me about the Spire," Rosalind said.

"What spire?"

"The black tower. In the glacier. Where the covenant is renewed."

"Is there such a place?" Maren asked, and she sounded genuinely interested, the way she'd sound about any legend. "I've heard the name, I think, in the old songs. But songs are songs. No one living has seen it."

"You've seen it. You drew me a map. In charcoal, on the back of a guild requisition, because you said the maps that showed the way to the Spire had all been bought or burned, but you remembered the way your master walked it, and your memory was perfect—"

"I don't draw," Maren said pleasantly. "My hands shake."

Rosalind looked at the old woman's hands. They were steady as bedrock. They had been steady since the bothy door. Whatever had hold of her had even taken the tremor, sanded her down, made her smoother, *neater*, a corrected proof.

Something in Rosalind went very cold and very quiet, the way she went quiet before a fight, before a run through a pass with hunters behind her. Because she understood, all at once, standing in a warm kitchen with honey on the table, exactly what she was looking at. The faceless ones had smooth faces because they were *revised people*. Somewhere behind every blank face was a kitchen like this one, a life like this one, someone who had been loved and then lovingly, thoroughly, *corrected*. The Hollow didn't have an army. The Hollow had a back-catalogue of everyone it had ever gotten its hands on, and it was still adding.

And it had gotten its hands on the one person alive who knew everything—about the codex, about the covenant, about the Spire, about—

About Rosalind's mother.

The thought arrived like a blade between the ribs. She kept her face still. She poured the tea. She asked about the weather, about the sheep on the crag, about nothing, while inside her the thought turned and turned: *they didn't take Maren at random. They took the archive. And they will have gone through it, page by page, looking for me.*

* * *

They left at dusk. There was no argument to be had about it; Maren would not come. "Come where, dear?" she'd said, laughing, when Rosalind asked her to walk with them. "I live here. I've always lived here. My whole life is in these hills." It was even true, in the revised way. The Hollow had built her a small, complete, seamless world with a bothy and a crag and a pot of porridge, and Rosalind at the center of it like a hole in the shape of a person.

At the door, Maren caught Rosalind's hand.

The old woman's grip was strong. Her eyes were bright, and for one moment—not the water-closing thing, something else, something quick and desperate and *hers*—she leaned close.

"I don't know you," Maren whispered. "I want you to understand that I don't know you. But my hands have been wrong all day. They keep reaching for things that aren't there. A pen, when there's no ink. A map, when there's no paper." Her voice dropped further. "And this morning I salted three bowls before I remembered there are only two of us. I've lived alone for thirty years. I *know* I've lived alone for thirty years. But my hands set out a third bowl, and they were *sure*, and hands don't lie, girl. Hands remember what the head is not permitted to."

"Maren—"

"Go." The old woman's eyes were wet and fierce. "Whatever you are, whatever I've lost—I don't know, and I think I'm not allowed to know, and that frightens me more than anything in sixty years has frightened me. So go, and be quick about it, and don't come back here, because whatever left me like this will come back to check its work." She pressed something into Rosalind's palm, folded small, and pushed her gently out the door. "And girl. If you find the person my hands are missing—tell them my hands remember. Tell them the hands remember *everything*."

The door closed.

Rosalind stood in the bracken with her fist closed tight, and opened it. Inside was a scrap of paper, torn from the flyleaf of some old book, and on it, in a hand that shook—shook, the way Maren's hands were not permitted to shake anymore—was a single line of charcoal:

*Elira's daughter. The forge-mother's child. Ask me what I hid, and why, and who made me swear.*

The wind came off the crag and the bracken hissed like turning pages, and far to the north, where the glacier held the Spire the way a fist holds a knife, the light was already failing. Rosalind looked at the paper, and at the closed door, and at Tobin's face, white in the dusk.

"She knew," Tobin said. "Some part of her—"

"She knew my mother's name." Rosalind's voice came out low and steady, which surprised her, because inside she was anything but. "And she knew what she hid. And whatever she hid, the Hollow went looking for it in her head—and either it didn't find it, or it found it and left the rest of her standing there as a lock with the key torn out." She folded the paper into her coat, over her heart, where the ember's warmth pressed against it like a second pulse. "We're going to the Spire, Tobin. And on the way, you're going to tell me every word your grandfather ever wrote about the forge-mother. Because someone swore Maren to silence about my mother, and someone paid Corvane to break the covenant, and I am done being the last person in the world to know my own story."

Above them, on the crag, the Old Man's Tooth stood black against the last of the light—and for a moment Rosalind could have sworn she saw a figure standing on it, smooth-faced and still, watching them go.
