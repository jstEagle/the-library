## 14. Forge-Mother

The fire in the shrine had gone down to a red whisper, and Maren sat before it the way she had sat before fires for sixty years—hands open, palms toward the warmth, an old keeper's habit of warming herself on something older than herself.

She could not sleep. None of them could, but Rosalind and Tobin had at least the grace of exhaustion to hide behind, curled in opposite corners of the ruined shrine with the ember-casket between them like a sleeping animal they were all afraid to wake. Maren had only her thoughts, and her thoughts had become treacherous country.

Because there were holes in them now.

She had noticed it first on the mountain road, three days past: a smoothness where a memory should have been, the way a tongue finds the gap where a tooth was pulled. She knew she had lived sixty-one years. She knew the guildhall, the smell of ink and tallow, the weight of the Vellum Codex in her arms when she was young enough to carry it without ceremony. But when she reached for certain things—certain years, certain faces—her mind slid off them like rain off oiled leather.

And every one of those smooth places, she was beginning to understand, had one thing in common.

The girl.

Rosalind.

Maren looked across the dying fire at the shape of her, blanket-draped, dark hair loose over one shoulder, one hand resting near the casket even in sleep—as if some part of her kept watch even when the rest surrendered. And Maren felt again that terrible sliding sensation, that gap-toothed wrongness, and beneath it something else. Something pressing up from underneath the hole like water through cracked ice.

*You know her,* said the thing beneath. *You have always known her.*

"No," Maren whispered, so quietly it barely stirred the air. "I met her this spring. Corvane sent her word of a run. I saw her come up the guildhall steps with frost in her hair and I thought—"

Thought what?

The ice cracked wider.

*I thought: there she is. At last. After twenty-five years, there she is.*

Maren's breath went out of her in a thin white thread. The fire popped. In the corner, Rosalind did not stir.

The memories did not come back the way memories were supposed to come—whole, ordered, arriving when called. They came the way floodwater comes through a breached dike: in bursts, in pressure, in fragments that hurt going down.

* * *

A kitchen. Not the guildhall's kitchen—an older one, low-beamed, smelling of woodsmoke and wet wool. A woman stood at the hearth with her sleeves pushed up, and her hands were burned in the old way, the forge-way, shiny-scarred across the backs where sparks had kissed her a hundred times and she had never once flinched.

Elira.

*Maren knew that name the way you know your own heartbeat—not as information but as fact.* Elira Vane, who had come to the Emberwrights at nineteen with nothing but scarred hands and a voice like banked coals, who had tended the great brazier through two hard winters when no one else could keep it alive, whom the oldest members had already begun calling *forge-mother* though she'd never borne a child—

Except she had. Except she was bearing one *now*, heavy under her apron, seven months along and still standing at the hearth because the fire answered her better than it answered anyone, and Maren was younger then, thirty-six, holding a ledger against her chest and saying:

"You can't keep working the forge. Corvane will see."

"Corvane sees what he wants to see." Elira did not turn around. The firelight moved on her face like water. "The ember dims, Maren. You've seen the readings same as I have. It dims a little more every year, and the renewal is—" she paused, and Maren heard the arithmetic she refused to finish, "—soon. Whatever I carry, it will be born into a world that needs its fire. So I work while I can."

"The codex says the next bearer—"

"I know what the codex says." Now Elira turned, and her eyes were the color of the deep part of a flame, and Maren remembered—remembered with the force of a blow—that she had loved this woman the way you love a sister, the way you love the person who stands between you and the dark and pretends it costs nothing. "I've read my grandmother's hand a thousand times. *The daughter of the forge-mother shall carry the ember home.* I've had twenty-five years to decide whether to believe it." Her hand went to her belly, an unconscious gesture, protective and proud at once. "I've decided to make it true either way."

The fragment shattered. Maren gasped, gripping the edge of the stone bench, and the present rushed back—the shrine, the cold, the ember-casket's faint warmth, the girl asleep across the room.

Twenty-five years. Rosalind was twenty-five.

*No,* said the thing beneath the ice. *Not beginning. Middle. There is more. There is the worst of it. Keep looking.*

* * *

She didn't want to look. That was the truth of it; she sat before the guttering fire and fought her own mind like a woman fighting sleep in a snowdrift, because she knew—some animal part of her knew—what waited at the bottom of these returning waters.

But the Hollow had already been inside her once. It had taken what it wanted and left the rest unstable, and unstable things do not stay put. The memories came whether she willed them or not.

Snow. A night of snow, and a child screaming in a way that tore the night open—a newborn's fury at the cold of the world—and Maren's own hands, younger hands, wrapping the child in wool while Elira lay white-faced on a bed that smelled of herbs and blood.

"A girl," Maren had said, stupidly, because someone had to say something.

"A girl." Elira reached for her, and Maren put the bundle in her arms, and watched the forge-mother of the Emberwrights look at her daughter with an expression Maren had no words for then and still had no words for now. It was not joy. Joy was too small a word, too clean. It was joy braided through with grief so tightly you couldn't tell which strand was which.

"She has your eyes," Maren said.

"She has *fire* in her eyes," Elira corrected softly. "That's different. That's worse." And then, quieter, almost to the child: "I'm sorry for what I'm going to give you. And I'm sorry for what I'm going to take away."

Maren had asked—I had asked, *I*, the memory insisted, *I asked her what she meant*—and Elira had only shaken her head and said, "Not yet. When she's older. When the covenant turns."

*When the covenant turns.*

The year the codex named. The year that had just arrived, written in a dead scribe's careful hand: tomorrow's date, and a description of a woman with frost in her hair coming up the guildhall steps.

* * *

The third fragment came like a knife.

It was the worst one, and it came whole, mercilessly whole, every detail sharp as the night it happened.

Rain, that time, not snow. Spring rain hammering the guildhall roof, and Elira in the doorway of Maren's cell at an hour when no honest person was awake, holding a sleeping child of six against her shoulder. Rosalind—six years old, round-cheeked, one fist closed around a wooden dragon someone had carved for her.

"Maren." Elira's voice was steady. That was the horror of it; Maren would remember forever how steady it was. "I need you to help me do something terrible."

"What's happened?"

"The covenant. I've been reading the margins—Tobin's grandfather's margins, the ones Corvane won't let anyone copy. The renewal isn't a hundred years out. It's *now*. It failed to renew last cycle—someone interfered, someone sold the rites, and the Spire has been running on what my mother's line put into it a century ago, and it's nearly empty." She shifted the child on her hip. "There's a way to buy time. A payment the Spire will accept short of a full forging. But the price—"

"Name it," Maren said. "Whatever it is, we'll find another way."

"That's just it." Elira smiled, and it was the saddest thing Maren had ever seen, and she had buried two husbands and a son. "There isn't one. The Spire takes payment in identity. Memory and name. That's what a covenant-bearer gives—it's why the old names are half-erased on the Spire walls, why no one remembers who forged the first pact. The fire doesn't burn fuel. It burns *self*. And if I go now, pay partially, buy the world another twenty-five years—my daughter grows up. She has time. Time to be found, to be trained, to be ready when the covenant turns for true." She looked down at the sleeping child. "She'll be twenty-five. Old enough. Strong enough. If people help her."

"Elira." Maren's voice broke on the name. "You're talking about erasing yourself."

"I'm talking about *choosing*," Elira said, "which is more than the Hollow ever lets anyone do. Listen to me. The Hollow's servants will come for the codex, for the child, for anyone who knows. Corvane has already been approached—I've seen the letters, I know what he's been offered, the coward hasn't signed yet but he will. So here is what I need from you." She stepped close, and pressed something into Maren's hand: a small iron key, warm from her body. "My daughter goes to the Lowdens in Millbrook, tonight. Farm family, good people, paid to forget they were ever paid. She grows up as their niece. No records. No name in any ledger. *Especially* no name in any guild ledger—do you understand me? Not even yours. Especially not yours."

"And you?"

"I leave for the Spire in three days. Alone. I tell everyone I'm making a pilgrimage." Elira's jaw tightened. "Corvane will let me go. He'll be relieved. One less witness to his correspondence."

"Then let me come with you—"

"No." It cracked out of her like a whip, and the child stirred, and Elira lowered her voice to that banked-coal softness again. "No, Maren. Because you're the one who stays. You're the one who watches her grow up from a distance and makes sure the world doesn't notice her until she's ready. You're the one who keeps the margins safe, who keeps the key, who—if I fail—if the partial payment isn't enough—finds her at twenty-five and puts the codex in her hands." She pressed her forehead briefly against Maren's, an old forge-guild gesture, keeper to keeper. "You are the only person alive I trust with this. Do you understand? Everyone else, I erase from the plan deliberately. But you—you I'm trusting with everything."

"And what do I get to keep?" Maren had whispered. "Of you?"

Elira pulled back. Rain hammered the roof. The child slept on, wooden dragon clutched tight.

"The knowing," she said. "You get to keep the knowing. Someone should remember what I chose, even if I can't." She kissed her daughter's hair, once, the way you strike a match. "Even if she never can."

* * *

Maren came back to herself on the floor of the shrine. She did not remember falling. Her knees ached against stone; her cheeks were wet; the fire had burned down to blue-tongued coals.

Across the room, Rosalind was awake. Had perhaps been awake for some time. She sat wrapped in her blanket, watching Maren with those fire-colored eyes—*her mother's eyes, her mother's exact eyes, how had I never let myself see it*—and her voice, when it came, was very quiet.

"You knew her."

It wasn't a question. Maren realized she must have spoken aloud somewhere in the drowning—said the name, or parts of it, fragments leaking out of her like light through a broken lantern.

"Rosalind—"

"Don't." The girl rose, blanket falling, and crossed the room in four strides, and crouched before Maren, and took her hands—and her grip was fierce, and shaking. "Don't soften it. Don't manage me. I have spent three weeks learning that everyone around me has decided what I can bear. Tobin with his marginalia he parcels out a page at a time. Corvane with his lies. Even the *Hollow* tried to bargain with me like a child." Her voice cracked and she forced it straight. "Tell me. All of it. Now."

So Maren told her.

All of it. The kitchen and the snow and the rain-night, the key she still wore on a cord around her neck for twenty-five years without remembering why, the farm in Millbrook, the paid-to-forget family, the pilgrimage that was no pilgrimage. She told her about the margins Tobin carried—how Elira had read them, how Elira had known the prophecy named her daughter before her daughter was born, and had gone to the Spire anyway, *because* her daughter would be born. She told her about the price. Memory and name. Identity, burned as fuel.

And she told her the last of it, the part that made Rosalind's hands go still in hers:

"She wasn't taken, child. She wasn't lost, or killed, or stolen by the Hollow. She walked up to the Spire in the rain with her head high and she *paid*, willingly, gladly, and what she bought with herself was twenty-five years of your life. Every meal. Every winter you survived. Every smuggler's path you learned. She bought it all, and the coin was herself." Maren's voice frayed. "You asked me once, on the road, why I seemed to grieve someone I couldn't name. This is why. I have spent twenty-five years keeping a promise I couldn't remember making. She took even that from me—no. Forgive me. She asked nothing. The Hollow took it, later, when it cut you out of me. But the promise was hers, and it was mine, and it was *love*, Rosalind. It was love measured in exactly what she gave up. Everything."

Silence. The coals ticked. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the pass with a sound like slow breathing.

Rosalind did not cry. Maren had expected tears and understood, watching her, that she was wrong to expect them. What moved across the girl's face instead was stranger and harder: recognition. The expression of someone fitting a final piece into a picture they had been assembling blind since childhood—the orphan's arithmetic, the unnamed father, the mother who died vaguely, of vague causes, when she was six. It had never added up. Now it did, and the sum was unbearable, and she bore it the way her mother must have borne the walk to the Spire: upright, silent, eyes full of fire.

"She chose oblivion," Rosalind said at last. Each word placed like a stone. "For me."

"She chose *you*," Maren said. "Oblivion was just what it cost."

Rosalind bowed her head. For a long moment the only sound was the wind, and then—quietly, terribly—she began to laugh, a small broken sound with no humor in it at all.

"Do you see the joke, Maren? Do you see what the Spire is going to ask of me?" She lifted her head, and the firelight caught her eyes, and they were blazing now, truly blazing, coal-deep and gold-rimmed. "It will ask the same thing it asked her. Memory and name. And I was going to stand in that place and think I was being brave. Original. Some new kind of sacrifice no Lockridge ever made." Her jaw tightened. "I'm not brave. I'm *inheritance*. I'm the second verse of a song my mother started singing before I could walk."

"Or," Maren said slowly, because sixty years of keeping had taught her that prophecies were doors and not walls, "you are the first person in a hundred years to walk into that place knowing the full price before you pay it. Your mother went in blind with faith. You'll go in with *sight*. That's not repetition, child. That's mastery."

Rosalind stared at her. And something in her face shifted—not softened, nothing so gentle—but gathered, the way a storm gathers, the way a forge-fire gathers when the bellows start.

Outside, far down the valley, a sound rolled up through the pass. Not thunder. Thunder did not repeat itself in disciplined intervals, and thunder did not come with torchlight flickering between the pines below the shrine, many torches, moving in formation.

Tobin woke with a start, hand flying to the casket. "What is that? Hollow—"

"No." Maren was already on her feet, old bones protesting, mind clearing with the cold clarity of long practice. She knew that formation. She had drilled those men herself, twenty years ago, before age took her off the training yard. Guild hunters, moving in corral-and-close, the pattern used for one purpose only: bringing home a target alive, or proving to the guild that they hadn't needed to.

"They've found us," she said. "And those are Emberwright torches. Rosalind—child—your guildmaster isn't just hiding his ledger anymore."

Rosalind rose, and took up the casket, and held it against her chest the way a mother holds a child, and Maren thought: *there she is. There is Elira's daughter. There is the forge-mother's fire, come back around at last.*

"He sent them," Rosalind said. It was not a question either.

Below the shrine, the torches began to climb.
