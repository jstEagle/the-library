## 15. Corvane's Hunters

The gorge had a name on no map and a smell Rosalind knew better than her own heartbeat: cold stone, pine rot, and the mineral tang of snowmelt running over iron. The locals called it the Throat. Smugglers called it the last honest place in Thornfell, because nothing lived in it—no birds, no goats, not even the moss that clung to every other north-facing wall in the range—and so there was nothing to betray you.

She had told Tobin that once, three days ago, when they'd crossed its upper lip in failing light. "Nothing to betray you," she'd said, and he'd written it down in the margin of his grandfather's codex like it was scripture, because Tobin wrote everything down, because ink was his way of holding on to a world that kept trying to slip through his fingers.

Now she stood at the bottom of the Throat with Maren's hollowed silence behind her and the ember's warmth against her breastbone, and she understood she had been wrong about one thing. Nothing lived in the gorge. But men could be brought into it.

"They're above us," Maren said.

Rosalind hadn't heard anything. That was the point of Maren—seventy years of listening to mountains had worn her hearing into something closer to divination. She stood with her head tilted, one hand resting on the strap of the satchel that held the Vellum Codex, her face as smooth and unreadable as it had been since the ford. Since the thing wearing paper for a face had reached into her and cut Rosalind out of her memory like a rotten tooth from a jaw.

"Six," Maren said. "Maybe seven. They've been following since dawn. They let us come down here."

*They let us.* Rosalind closed her eyes. Of course they had. She knew this gorge the way she knew the inside of her own hands, and anyone who wanted to trap her would know that too—which meant whoever was hunting her knew her, or knew someone who did.

Tobin had gone pale. He was twenty-three and built like a stack of books someone had left out in the rain, and he had frozen at the ford while Maren was taken, and Rosalind had watched him carry that shame ever since like a stone sewn into his coat. His hand went to the codex strap. "How many ways out?"

"Two you can walk." Rosalind turned slowly, reading the walls. Grey granite streaked with quartz, scree fans spilling from the side ravines, the melt-swollen stream chattering over stones the color of old teeth. "One only I can walk."

"Then—"

"No." She said it before he finished, because she already knew what he was going to ask, and the answer was the same answer she'd been giving since the shrine, since the ford, since the night she'd pulled him out of a Hollow agent's grip by the collar and felt how light he was. "We don't split up. Not again."

Maren made a small sound—not agreement exactly, but the ghost of it. Whatever the faceless ones had taken from her, they had left her stubbornness intact, and her stubbornness had apparently decided Rosalind was worth following. Rosalind didn't know whether to be grateful or grieved. Probably both. Grief and gratitude had stopped being separate things somewhere back on the high pass, where she had fed a lock of her hair and then her mother's lullaby to a dying ember, and the ember had burned brighter for it, and she had learned that everything precious was also fuel.

The first crossbow bolt hit the scree fan six feet to her left.

It came down off the eastern rim, thudding into shale with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and then the gorge filled with voices—rough guild voices, Emberwright accents, the flat command cadence of men who had trained together for years. Torches kindled along both rims, orange points blooming in the grey light, and Rosalind counted them with the cold part of her mind, the part that had gotten her through eleven years of passes and checkpoints and bribes.

Eight torches. Eight hunters. Guild-issue oilcloth, guild-issue boots, and on the nearest man's chest, catching torchlight, the ember-and-anvil sigil of the Emberwrights—the same sigil stitched over her own heart.

"They're ours," Tobin whispered. "They're *ours*, why would—"

"Because Corvane sent them." Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of that. "Dead or discredited. Those were the orders, I'd wager. He doesn't care which."

A rope uncoiled down the western wall, and men came down it hand over hand, fast and practiced, dropping the last ten feet into the scree. Seven of them fanned out across the gorge mouth, cutting off the downstream route, and the eighth came down slower, alone, and landed apart from the others the way a man lands when he expects to be talking rather than fighting.

Rosalind knew the shape of him before the torchlight found his face.

"Bren," she said.

*

Bren Aldous had been her oldest friend for nineteen of her twenty-six years. He had taught her to throw a knife standing on the loading dock behind the guildhall, laughing when she hit the fence post instead of the target, laughing harder when she hit the target dead center and pretended she'd meant to all along. He had carried her on his shoulders through the Midwinter fires when she was nine. He had held her when her mother died—held her awkwardly, a boy of twelve who didn't know what to do with a crying girl, patting her shoulder like the shoulder might break—and he had said, in the fierce certain voice of a child defending a fortress: *"I'm your family now. You don't get a say in it."*

He stood in the scree now with a drawn blade and eight men at his back and an expression on his face like a man walking on river ice and hearing it groan.

"Rosie." Her childhood name, the one only three living people were allowed to use. His voice cracked on it. "Don't run."

"I wasn't going to run." She kept her hands loose at her sides, away from her knives, and put herself between the hunters and Tobin and Maren without seeming to. It was an old smuggler's trick—move your body, never your eyes—but Bren knew all her tricks. He'd helped invent half of them. "You brought eight men into my gorge, Bren. You knew I'd hear them coming a mile out. You knew I'd have the high ground if I wanted it. So either Corvane didn't tell you who you were hunting, or you wanted me to see your face first."

His jaw worked. Behind him, the seven fanned wider, boots grinding shale, and Rosalind marked each one: Dace, who owed her for the winter he couldn't pay his dues; Halloway, solid and incurious; the twins, Pell and Perrin, who moved like one animal; two she didn't know well; and young Osric at the far edge, sixteen and terrified, gripping his crossbow like a railing.

"You were seen," Bren said. "At the ford. Dead men walking out of the water, faces like blank paper, and you standing in the middle of it with fire in your hands. Half the valley saw. There are stories in every tavern between here and Lowmark, Rosie. Stories about *you*."

"The stories are true."

"That's the problem!" It burst out of him, loud enough that the twins flinched. "Corvane says you stole guild property—that ember, the codex, all of it—and that you killed the Hollow agents' victims yourself, that you—" He stopped. Swallowed. "He showed us the ledger page. Your name in the margin. Payments received."

Something went very cold and very still in Rosalind's chest. "Show me."

"Rosie—"

"*Show me the page, Bren.*"

He hesitated, and that hesitation told her more than the page would have. Then he pulled a folded sheet from inside his coat and held it out at arm's length, the way you offer meat to a dog you don't trust.

She took it. Unfolded it. Read it twice.

It was a ledger page, guild watermark, Corvane's cramped hand. And there in the margin, dated four days ago—a date before she had ever opened the Vellum Codex, before she had known the word Covenant-Bearer—was an entry in her handwriting. Or something very close to it. *Received of G.C., 40 crowns, account settled.*

Her handwriting. Her hand, which she had known for twenty-six years, which had signed a thousand smuggling manifests, which had a particular hitch in the descender of the R that no forger had ever caught.

Except that she had never written it. And the date—

The date was tomorrow.

The gorge seemed to tilt. The stream noise receded. Somewhere very far away, Tobin was saying her name, and Maren's hand had closed on her arm with surprising strength, and Rosalind stood in the bottom of the Throat holding proof that she had betrayed her own guild four days before she'd learned there was anything to betray.

"He forged it," she said. Her voice sounded strange to her. Flat. "Or someone forged it for him. Bren, I have never taken a crown from Corvane in my life. You know that. You've split every take with me since I was fifteen. When have I ever—"

"I know." Barely a whisper. "Gods help me, Rosie, I *know*. That's why I came down here instead of taking the shot from the rim." He lifted his chin slightly, and she saw it then—the thing under the guilt in his face, the thing he'd been carrying since the rim. "There's more. He gave the order two nights ago. *Bring her back dead or discredited.* Not captured. Not questioned. And yesterday he changed it. Yesterday he said the ember had to come back to the guildhall *unlit*. Broken, if necessary. He said—" Bren's throat moved. "He said the covenant is a debt, and debts can be cancelled, and the only thing standing in the way of cancelling it is a smuggler girl and a dying coal."

Behind her, Maren made a sound like a door closing.

"And you believed him?" Rosalind asked. "Nineteen years, Bren. Nineteen years, and you believed him?"

"I didn't." His voice broke clean through. "That's why we're talking instead of shooting. But the others—" He glanced back at his men, and Rosalind read it in a heartbeat: Dace believed, Halloway believed, the twins believed whatever paid their dues, and little Osric believed because believing was easier than thinking. "Half of them lost people to the Hollow stories. They want someone to blame. Corvane handed them you on a plate."

"So what happens now?" Tobin's voice, thin but steady, from behind her shoulder. "You cornered us. Orders are orders. What happens now?"

What happened now was Dace.

He'd been edging along the scree while they talked—she'd tracked him the whole time, the cold part of her mind never stopping its counting—and now he raised his crossbow, not at her, at Tobin. At the satchel. "Enough talk, Aldous. Take the coal. Corvane said the girl's to be discredited, not consulted."

"Dace—" Bren started.

Dace fired.

Rosalind had spent eleven years learning that fights were decided before they began—in the angles, in the footing, in the half-second of intention that preceded any action. She had watched Dace's weight shift onto his back foot. She was moving before the bolt left the string.

She didn't go for Dace. She went for the space between Dace and Tobin, knife out, and slapped the bolt out of the air was impossible—so instead she simply wasn't where it went, hauling Tobin sideways by the collar, the quarrel sparking off stone where his head had been. Then the world dissolved into motion and shouting.

She fought the way she traveled: by routes nobody else knew. Not strength—she had none to spare against seven armed men—but geometry. She put the twins in each other's line. She backed Halloway onto the slick stream stones and let the river do the work, one sweep of his footing, one splash, one man coughing in knee-deep meltwater. She took Dace's wrist against a boulder and heard the crossbow clatter away and left him gasping with a knife flat against his cheekbone, not cutting, just *there*, and looked past him at the rest.

"Anyone else," she said, breathing hard, "want to explain to the Hollow why they emptied a village of forty souls? Because I've seen it. Doors open. Meals half-eaten. A doll in the road. That's what your guildmaster's bargain buys. That's what you're helping him protect."

Silence, except the stream and the breathing. Seven hunters, disarmed or disheartened, and Bren standing among them with his sword still drawn and his eyes wet and furious—at himself, she thought, mostly at himself.

Osric, sixteen and shaking, still had his crossbow half-raised. "The ledger," he blurted. "The real ledger. My uncle keeps the strongroom. If she's lying about the forgery—"

"She's not lying." Bren said it quietly, and the words fell into the gorge like stones into a well. He sheathed his sword. Walked toward her through the scree, slow, hands open, and stopped an arm's length away—the closest they'd stood since before the ford, since before everything. Up close she could see he hadn't slept. Neither had she. It was strange, the things that made people kin.

"Show me," he said. "Not Corvane's copy. Yours. The payments. The buyer. All of it."

Rosalind reached into her coat and drew out the folded pages she had carried since the guildhall—Corvane's ledger, copied in her own careful hand, column after column of crowns flowing out to an unnamed buyer in the year the last renewal failed to happen, and again every year since, a tithe of silver feeding something that ate villages. She had shown them to Maren. She had shown them to Tobin. She had not shown them to anyone whose heart she cared about breaking.

She showed them to Bren.

He read for a long time. The torches guttered on the rims above. Somewhere in the middle pages his hands started to shake, and by the end he was holding the paper steady only by pressing it flat against his thigh.

"Twenty-five years," he said finally. "He's been selling us for twenty-five years. And I marched down here with a blade for *you*."

"You marched down here and talked first," Rosalind said. "That's the difference, Bren. That's the whole difference. Some betrayals you can't walk back. This one you can. If you choose to."

He looked up. Nineteen years looked back at her out of his face—the dock, the knives, the Midwinter fires, the boy who'd declared himself her family without asking permission.

"What do you need?" he said.

And it was Maren, silent until now, hollowed and edited and still somehow the sharpest mind in the gorge, who answered for all of them.

"The Spire," she said. "Before Corvane understands his hunters have turned. Before he does something desperate." Her blind-white eyes found Rosalind's. "Child, he sold the rites once. Do you understand what a man like that sells *next*, when he learns the covenant can still be renewed? He won't send eight men next time. He'll sell the road itself."

Bren turned to his men. Dace was rubbing his wrist, Halloway dripping, the twins watching their leader with the flat patience of animals waiting for a decision.

"You heard the keeper," Bren said. "We ride for the Spire. Anyone who'd rather report back to Corvane—" he paused, and something old and hard came into his voice, something Rosalind had never heard there before, something that sounded like the guild as it must have been a hundred years ago, when it was worth dying for— "can explain to the guild's founders why they chose a liar's coin over the last fire in the world."

Nobody moved toward the ropes.

Rosalind folded the ledger pages away, and her hand brushed the ember's warmth through her coat, steady as a second heartbeat, and she thought: *eight men, one turncoat, a hollowed keeper, a frightened scribe, and me.* It was not an army. Against what waited below the ice, it might not even be a beginning.

But it was more than she'd had at dawn.

They were cinching packs and dousing torches when Tobin appeared at her elbow, codex clutched to his chest, and said quietly: "Rosie. The date on that forged entry. You saw it too, didn't you."

She had hoped he wouldn't say it. She had hoped she could carry it alone a little longer, the way she carried everything.

"Tomorrow," she said. "Someone wrote my confession tomorrow."

Tobin nodded slowly, and his eyes went to the codex—to the book that had named her, dated her, described her before she existed in its pages—and Rosalind saw the same dread take shape in his face that was already coiled in her own.

The codex had never once been wrong about a date.

Which meant somewhere ahead, already written, already waiting, was the day it said she would fail.
