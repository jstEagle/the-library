## 17. The Hollow's Heart

The stairs went down farther than the Spire was tall.

Rosalind counted them at first—two hundred, three hundred—then lost the count when the walls began to change. The black glass of the tower had been warm and faintly luminous above, humming with old fire like a hearthstone hours after the coals were raked. Here, below the glacier's roots, the glass had gone cold and dark, and instead of names it held *absences*: stretches of wall where the surface was smooth as river ice, as if something had been written there once and then scraped away.

She ran her fingers along one such stretch. The glass was not merely blank. It was *hungry*.

"Stop touching things," Tobin said behind her. His voice echoed strangely, arriving before him and after him at once.

"I'm deciding whether to be afraid," she said. "It helps to have data."

Maren walked between them with the ember-brazier cradled against her chest, both arms wrapped around it the way a mother carries a sleeping child. The ember itself had dimmed again since the high pass—since Rosalind had fed it her mother's lullaby, note by note, until she could no longer remember how the song began—and it burned now no larger than a robin's egg, a bead of sullen orange light in a cage of black iron. It cast just enough glow to make their shadows lean and wrong on the curved walls.

"The heart-chamber should be close," Maren murmured. "Elira described it to me once. She said—" The old scribe stopped. Her breath caught audibly in the dark.

"What?"

"She said you'd know it when you felt it." Maren's knuckles were white on the brazier's handles. "I thought that was poetry. Scribes say things like that."

It wasn't poetry. Rosalind felt it now, rising through the soles of her boots like water through cloth: a pressure, a *pull*, gentle and enormous, the way the sea pulls at a swimmer who has stopped fighting. Something down here knew she was coming. Something down here had been waiting a very long time, patient as erosion.

The stairs ended.

The chamber beyond was round and vast, its ceiling lost in darkness overhead, its floor a single unbroken sweep of black glass polished to a mirror's depth. At its center stood a dais of the same material, and set into the dais was a socket—a hollow shaped exactly, unmistakably, like the iron cage of the ember-brazier. Around the socket, carved in letters older than any alphabet Rosalind knew, ran a ring of script that glowed faintly, fitfully, like embers breathing under ash.

And standing beside the dais, with her back to them, was a woman.

Rosalind's body reacted before her mind did—a lurch, a reaching, her hand half-raised toward a silhouette she had spent twenty years trying to hold onto and twenty years failing. Broad shoulders. The particular way of standing with weight on one hip, hands loose, as if perpetually about to laugh or lift something heavy. Dark hair bound back with a strip of red cloth.

Her mother had worn a strip of red cloth in her hair every day of Rosalind's remembered life.

"No," Tobin breathed. He had seen it too. His hand found Rosalind's sleeve. "That's not—that can't be—"

The woman turned.

It was her mother's face. Not an approximation, not a likeness, not a memory imperfectly rendered. It was Elira Lockridge as Rosalind had last seen her at six years old: the crooked smile, the small scar through the left eyebrow from a forge-spark, the eyes the color of strong tea with light behind them. Every detail exact. Every detail *loving*. That was the worst of it—the face looked at her with such naked, uncomplicated love that Rosalind's knees nearly buckled under the weight of it.

"Hello, little spark," the woman said.

*Little spark.* The name her mother had called her across a hundred mornings, over bread and forge-smoke, in the dark when the storms came down off the Thornfells and the whole world shook. A name no living person knew. A name that existed nowhere but in Rosalind's own head—in the sealed room of memory she had guarded like contraband for two decades.

"You're not her," Rosalind said. Her voice came out steady. She was almost proud of that.

"No," the thing wearing her mother agreed pleasantly. "I'm not. But I'm holding everything she was, and I'm offering it back."

* * *

They did not move from the stairway. Tobin's grip on her sleeve had become a fist; Maren had gone gray as tallow, and the ember in her arms had shrunk another fraction, cowering.

"How?" Rosalind asked. Keep it talking. Corvane's ledgers had taught her that much—every transaction has terms, and whoever states the terms first holds the advantage. "You eat memories. You take. That's what the Hollow does. Since when does it give anything back?"

"Since always." The Hollow-Elira spread her hands—an easy, forge-worker's gesture, callused knuckles, burn-scarred forearm. "You've had it backwards, all of you. We don't devour. We *keep*. Everything your kind forgets—the dead, the drowned, the burned, the broken pacts—we hold safe. Nothing is ever truly lost, Rosalind Lockridge. It only changes hands." The tea-colored eyes glittered. "Your mother understood that better than anyone. She came down these same stairs twenty-five years ago and made a payment. Do you know what she paid?"

Rosalind's throat closed. She thought of the unfinished line on the Spire's upper wall—*Elira*, cut off mid-stroke—and said nothing.

"She paid herself," the Hollow said gently. "Her name. Her history. Every memory anyone held of her, surrendered into our keeping, in exchange for time—for a hundred years' grace on a covenant that should have failed. And do you know what we received, in all that vastness? Do you know what it is to hold a soul entire?" Its voice softened into something almost tender. "We received *her*. The lullabies. The laughter. The way she danced badly on purpose to make her daughter groan. The smell of the forge at dawn. Twenty years of loving a child whose face she couldn't remember giving birth to. We keep it all, little spark. Pristine. Untouched. Waiting."

"Waiting for what?" Tobin asked hoarsely.

"For someone to want it back."

The chamber seemed to tilt. Rosalind locked her knees.

"And the price?" she managed.

"That's the beautiful part." The Hollow-Elira smiled her mother's crooked smile. "There isn't one. No trade. No covenant. No fine print in a dead language. You simply walk away. Turn around, climb the stairs, leave the ember in this room—or don't, it hardly matters, carry it home and watch it gutter out over the winter, the result is the same. Abandon the forging, and I return her to you. All of her. Into your mind, whole, permanent—you'll remember her lullaby again, every verse. You'll remember her voice. You'll fall asleep tomorrow night knowing, finally, finally *knowing* her, after twenty years of carrying a photograph of smoke." It tilted its head, red cloth swaying. "All you lose is a world you've never belonged to anyway. A guild that used you. A prophecy that conscripted you. A name that got you hunted across half a mountain range. Walk away, Rosalind. Be nobody again. Nobody was always safer."

The silence afterward was total. Even the ember seemed to hold its breath.

And Rosalind—she hated herself even as it happened—Rosalind *wanted*.

It rose in her like floodwater. Twenty years of it, all the grief she'd packed down beneath competence and anonymity and the careful craft of being unseen: the birthday she'd spent alone at fourteen inventing her mother's laugh because she'd forgotten the real one; the way she'd once paid a hedge-witch three months' smuggling earnings for a memory-charm that turned out to be worthless; the nights on the passes when she'd talked aloud to a woman who wasn't there, narrating her day, *look, Mama, snow, look, Mama, I didn't die.* She wanted it back with a hunger that made the Hollow's hunger look like a child's whim. She wanted it more than she had ever wanted anything, and the thing wearing her mother's face knew it, and waited, patient as erosion, letting the wanting do its work.

"Take the offer," Maren whispered. It took Rosalind a moment to understand she'd heard right—to realize the old scribe was crying, silent tears cutting lines through trail-grime. "Child, listen to me. I watched your mother walk up out of this place with her eyes already going empty. I carried her home. She looked at me the next morning like a stranger, and by week's end she looked at *you* like one, and she still chose it, she still said it was worth it—but you don't have to choose it. No one wrote this price into the covenant. There's no law. Take your mother back. Let the world keep its own damned fire."

"Maren—" Tobin started.

"Hush. Let her choose. For once in her cursed life, let someone let her *choose*."

And that was the knife of it, Rosalind thought distantly. That was the true blade hidden in the soft belly of the offer. Because they were right. Everyone had always decided for her—the guild, the codex, the prophecy, her mother most of all, choosing oblivion on her behalf before Rosalind was old enough to object. Her whole life had been other people's signatures on contracts she never saw. And here, at last, was a door marked with her own name, opening onto the single thing she had ever truly desired.

The Hollow-Elira took one step closer. Up close, in the ember's dying light, Rosalind could see that the face was perfect in every detail except one: it did not breathe. The chest never rose. The red cloth never stirred.

"Say yes," it said, "and I will show you the day you were born."

Rosalind closed her eyes.

* * *

In the dark behind her eyelids, she built the scene the way she built all her best routes: carefully, step by step, testing each footing before trusting it.

Her mother, returned. Sitting across a real table in the rebuilt guildhall, drinking real tea, telling her everything—the father's name, the reason for the flight to Thornfell, the story of the six-year-old girl carried over the passes in a flour sack while soldiers searched the road. Rosalind would finally be *finished*. The open wound would close. She would be whole, and warm, and known.

And her mother—

Her mother would be a lie.

That was the thought that stopped the floodwater. Not morality, not duty, not the fate of the mortal realm—those were abstractions, and abstractions had never once kept her alive on the ice. What stopped her was simpler and colder: she had spent her life learning to tell forged goods from true. She could feel a false seal through leather gloves. She could taste cut pepper mixed with ash. And the thing being offered to her, however perfect, however loving, was the finest counterfeit she had ever been shown—because it was made from stolen goods. Her mother's laughter, kept in a hungry dark for twenty-five years by a thing that ate worlds. However tenderly it was handed back, it would arrive *handled*. Tainted. Her mother's love, filtered through the Hollow's keeping, would be the Hollow's love wearing her mother's voice, and Rosalind would sit across from it for the rest of her life knowing that somewhere inside the warmth was a mouth.

Worse—oh, worse, the part that made her stomach turn over—worse was what the acceptance would cost *Elira*. Because the Hollow hadn't lied about that part. It was holding her mother. Holding her the way a spider holds a fly: preserved, displayed, *kept*. And every year Rosalind walked away, every year the covenant thinned and the Hollow crept closer to the world of the living, her mother stayed in that dark, a treasure in a thief's vault, smiling her stolen smile at nothing.

There was only one way to empty the vault.

"Here's my counteroffer," Rosalind said.

She opened her eyes and stepped forward—off the stair, onto the mirror-black floor, alone, leaving Tobin's fist clutching empty air. The Hollow-Elira's smile widened, certain of its victory.

"I'm not taking her back," Rosalind said. "I'm coming to get her."

For the first time, the thing wearing her mother's face went still.

"You keep everything," Rosalind continued, walking, each step ringing faintly on the glass like a hammer on an anvil. "Fine. Then you're holding a debt, and debts come due. My mother paid you fairly—her name, her memories, a clean trade. But the renewal she bought runs out this winter, and the codex says the account falls to me. So I'm not abandoning the forging, and I'm not trading with you either. I'm going to put that ember"—she nodded at the socket in the dais, at the ring of dying script around it—"into that hole, and light a fire in your vault, and burn every stolen thing you've hoarded back into the world where it belongs. Including her."

"You cannot," the Hollow said. The tenderness was gone from its voice now, and what lay underneath was vast and cold and old. "The forging requires a payment. Blood, name, or self. That is the covenant's law, older than your guild, older than your kind. You will stand in that fire and be unmade, or you will fail, and either way—"

"Yeah," Rosalind said. "About the payment."

She reached the dais. Up close, the socket yawned like a mouth, and the script around it pulsed faster, sensing the ember's nearness, sensing *her*. Behind her she heard Tobin shouting her name and Maren shouting something else—blessing or warning, impossible to tell—and she heard, beneath it all, the sound of the Hollow-Elira beginning to scream without moving its borrowed lips, a sound like paper tearing in a bottomless room.

Because the Hollow had made one mistake, in all its long patience. It had told her the truth about the terms. *Blood, name, or self.*

It had assumed she would bargain for her life. It had assumed everyone did.

Rosalind lifted the brazier from Maren's arms—the old scribe released it without a fight, weeping—and set it into the socket, where it fit with a click like a lock turning home. The dying ember flared once, weakly, questioning.

"It's all right," Rosalind told it, and meant it. "I know the way."

Then she raised her voice to fill the whole cold chamber, to fill the vault and everything hoarded inside it, and spoke the words she had rehearsed on a thousand empty miles of pass, alone, to no one:

"My name is Rosalind Lockridge. Daughter of Elira, daughter of no one, smuggler of the Thornfell passes, ghost of the Emberwright Guild. And I pay my mother's debt in full—with everything I am called, everything I am recorded, everything I am remembered. Take the name. Leave me the rest."

The ember rose out of its cage like a bird leaving a snare.

Far above, muffled by a hundred fathoms of ice and stone, came a sound Rosalind would not identify until later: the boom of the Spire's great doors being forced, and voices—many voices, some of them shouting her name.

Some of them shouting *Corvane's*.
