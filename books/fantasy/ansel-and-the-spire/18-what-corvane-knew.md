## 18. What Corvane Knew

The mountain had been waiting for him.

Corvane felt it in the way the wind moved against his climb—patient, deliberate, like a creditor's hand on the shoulder. Twenty-five years he had climbed mountains of a different kind: ledgers, debts, the slow arithmetic of a guild dying by inches. He had always believed that if you kept the numbers moving, if you never let a column sit empty long enough for anyone to read what was written there, you could outrun anything.

He had been wrong. The numbers had caught up. They were written in fire now, and they were climbing toward him at the pace of one woman and a dying ember.

"Guildmaster." Bren's voice came from behind him, flat as slate. "The pass narrows ahead. We'll reach the glacier by dusk."

Corvane did not turn. He watched the Spire instead—or the place where it would be, a black seam in the ice above them, visible even from here, even through the whiteout haze. It looked like a wound in the world's skin. It looked like a door someone had left open and forgotten to close, and everything cold and hungry beyond the world had been leaking through it ever since.

*End the debt forever.* That was what he had told the remnants who'd followed him out of Thornfell—eleven of them, hollow-eyed men and women who had lost their guildhall, their purpose, their certainty, all in the space of a fortnight. Eleven was not an army. Eleven was enough to carry fire-oil and hammers, and that was all Corvane needed. You did not need to fight a tower of black glass. You only needed to break it, the way you broke anything: with heat and force and no questions asked.

He flexed his gloved hands inside their fur lining. They ached. They had ached for twenty-five years, though he had never told anyone why—not the ache of cold, but the ache of a grip held too long around something that should have been let go.

The ledger page. He could still see it. Rosalind Lockridge's face when she'd found it—if she had found it; he had to believe she had, because she had run, hadn't she? Straight up the mountain, straight toward the thing he'd spent half his life pretending didn't matter. She had his handwriting in her hands now, or Maren's did, or Tobin Ashvale's did—that pale little scribe's grandson who smelled of ink and trouble. Somewhere on this mountain, the truth about Aldous Corvane was being passed from hand to hand like a lamp in a dark room, and every person who saw it would see him exactly as he was.

Not a keeper. Not a guildmaster. A man who had sold the light.

* * *

They made camp in the lee of a fallen serac, and Corvane sat apart from the eleven, as he had sat apart from everyone for twenty-five years, and let himself remember—because there was no use pretending anymore. Pretending was a currency, and he was done spending.

It had been winter then too. The guildhall had been colder than this mountain; poverty is colder than weather. Forty-three Emberwrights left in the rolls, and thirty-one of them gone within two years—dead, deserted, or simply vanished into the lowlands where a man could earn bread without kneeling before a brazier of guttering flame and calling it sacred. The dragon-fire had been the size of a fist by then. Some nights it was the size of a thumbnail. Corvane had sat before it with the guild's books spread across his knees and done the only arithmetic that mattered: *how many days.*

The buyer had come to him the way buyers always come—quietly, through intermediaries, with money that smelled of nothing at all. No name. A face he could never afterward describe, smooth and pleasant and forgettable, like a word on the tip of the tongue. The offer had been simple: the rites of renewal—the true ones, the ones Maren's order guarded with their lives—for a sum that would keep forty-three names fed for twenty years.

"They're just words," the buyer had said, and its voice had been kind, which should have been the first warning. Kindness from something with no name. "Old words. Words for a world that no longer exists. Your dragons are dead, guildmaster. Your covenant is a story old women tell to make the cold feel chosen. Sell me the story. Feed your people. That isn't betrayal. That's mercy."

And Corvane—young then, hungry then, with the ledger open on his knees and the brazier down to its last coal—had wanted so badly to believe that mercy was what it was.

He had copied the rites himself. That was the detail that lived in his hands, the one that made them ache. His own pen, his own ink, his own careful merchant's script transferring a thousand years of covenant onto fresh paper for a stranger whose face slid away whenever he tried to hold it. He had told himself the words were inert. He had told himself the Hollow, if it existed at all, could do nothing with mere instructions.

But the Hollow did not eat flesh first. Maren had said that once, years later, in one of her cryptic moods, staring into the little fire. *It eats the telling. It eats the memory of the telling. A rite removed from its keepers is a tooth pulled from a jaw—the hunger comes after.* And Corvane had understood, too late, in the slow horror of the following decade, what he had actually sold. Not paper. The *capacity*. Every Emberwright who died or deserted after that night took a piece of the rite with them, unwritten, untaught, and the covenant starved the way any living thing starves: gradually, then suddenly, then all at once.

The last renewal had failed because there was no one left alive who knew how to perform it. Because a man with cold hands and warm intentions had sold the knowing.

And the payments had continued. That was the part Rosalind would have found in the ledger, the part that made it unforgivable rather than merely tragic. Year after year, the same discreet sums from the same unnamed source—not payment for services rendered, but *retainer*. Insurance. The buyer paid to ensure the covenant stayed broken, and Corvane had taken the money, because by then taking it was the only thing he knew how to do. He had built the guild's survival on the interest of its own destruction. He had fed forty-three people with the wages of the dark.

*You told yourself it was already broken,* he thought, watching the firelight play on the faces of the eleven around their campfire. *You told yourself you were only collecting on a debt already owed.* But that was the architecture of ruin, wasn't it? You never built it all at once. You laid one stone, and then another, and each stone was reasonable, defensible, necessary—and one day you stood back and saw that you had built a wall between yourself and every honest thing you'd ever meant to be.

"Guildmaster."

Bren again. The big man crouched beside him, close enough that Corvane could see the frost in his beard and the thing in his eyes that had been growing there since the gorge—the thing Corvane refused to name.

"The men are asking," Bren said. "What happens to the girl. If we find her at the Spire."

"She won't be found," Corvane said. "She'll be stopped. There's a difference, and the difference is that we go home after."

"And if stopping her means—" Bren's jaw worked. "The orders you gave. Dead or discredited. I've been turning those words over for three days, guildmaster, and I can't make them sit right. She carried me out of the Whitefall slide with my leg broken. She was sixteen. She walked nine miles with me on her back because no one else could find the path."

"I know what she is," Corvane said quietly. "I've known since she was born."

Something flickered across Bren's face—too fast to catch, too slow to miss. "What does that mean?"

"It means," Corvane said, rising, brushing snow from his coat, "that some debts are older than yours, Bren, and mine is coming due tonight. Get the men moving. We climb while the light holds."

* * *

They reached the glacier at dusk, and the Spire rose out of the ice like a blade out of a wound.

Corvane had seen engravings of it in the oldest guild records—he had burned most of those records himself, over the years, quietly, a page here and a page there—but no engraving had prepared him for the reality of it. Black glass, seamless, taller than any tower men had built, thrusting up through the glacier as if the mountain were giving birth to it. And warm. He could feel the warmth from fifty yards off, radiating against the killing cold like a hearth in a ruined house, and something in his chest clenched at the wrongness of it, the *aliveness* of it.

The Hollow servants were there too. He had hoped—Ishad hoped, in the small dishonest chapel of his heart—that the faceless things would have cleared the way for him, that his old arrangement counted for something still. But the paper-faced figures stood scattered across the ice like abandoned mannequins, motionless, watching the Spire with the patience of things that had waited a hundred years and could wait a hundred more. They did not stop the eleven Emberwrights. They did not seem to care about them at all. Their attention was fixed upward, on the tower, on whatever was happening inside it.

On her.

"Fire-oil," Corvane said. "Set the charges at the base. Hammers for the glass. We crack it, we burn it, we go home. No one speaks to anything that comes out of that tower. Nothing in there is human anymore."

The eleven moved to obey—all but one.

"Bren," Corvane said. "Take the east face."

Bren did not move. He stood with his hammer still slung across his back and his eyes on the Spire, and then, slowly, terribly, he turned them on Corvane.

"I've been thinking about the ledger," Bren said.

The words dropped into Corvane's stomach like stones into a well.

"Maren kept the archive. Everyone knows that. When the hall emptied out, her books went into storage, and storage means the cellar, and the cellar means anyone with a key can get in." Bren's voice was steady, which was worse than shouting. "I went looking. Not for anything. Just—looking. After the gorge. After she showed me the page."

"You showed him—" Corvane began, and stopped, because there was no finishing it. There was no version of the sentence that saved him.

"A buyer," Bren said. "No name. Payments every year for twenty-five years. Starting the year the last renewal failed." He took a step closer, and the other ten Emberwrights had gone very still, their fire-oil cans hanging from their hands, their faces turning between the two men like spectators at a trial. "I've served this guild since I was fourteen, guildmaster. I gave it my whole life because I thought it was worth something. Keeping a fire lit. Holding a line. And the whole time, the man leading us was the reason the line was breaking."

"The guild would have died otherwise." The words came out of Corvane cracked, ragged, twenty-five years of rehearsal falling apart in his mouth. "Forty-three people, Bren. Children. Elders. You think I don't know what I did? I have known every single day. I did it anyway, because the alternative was watching them starve in a hall full of holy nonsense while the world went on not caring whether our fire burned!"

"And the Hollow came anyway," Bren said. "Whole villages, gone. Doors open. Meals half-eaten. That's what your bargain bought. That's what the interest looks like."

Corvane opened his mouth—and the Spire answered for him.

Light bloomed inside the black glass, deep in its heart, gold-red as a forge waking, and the warmth rolling off the tower doubled, tripled, pressed against them like a living hand. The paper-faced figures on the ice turned their smooth heads as one, and for the first time Corvane saw something like urgency in their posture, something like fear, and he understood with total clarity that it was happening inside: the reforging, the renewal, the thing he had come to prevent, being done by a smuggler's daughter with an ember in her hands.

"No," he heard himself say. "No—stop her—we have to stop her, if the covenant renews, the debt doesn't end, the payments, the *arrangement*—"

"Listen to yourself." Bren's voice was almost gentle now, and gentleness was the cruelest thing Corvane had faced all night. "Twenty-five years, and you're still protecting the arrangement. Even now. Even standing at the foot of the only thing that's ever going to save us."

The remaining Emberwrights were setting down their fire-oil. One by one. Deliberately. Ten small sounds of surrender, each one a verdict. Old Wenna, who had tended the guildhall brazier for thirty years, looked at Corvane with an expression he had spent a lifetime dreading and could not now avoid: not hatred. Pity.

"We stand with her," Wenna said simply. "Whoever she turns out to be. Whatever her blood is. She climbed the mountain when we wouldn't. That's all the proof I need."

Corvane stood alone on the ice, and the strangest thing happened: the weight lifted. All of it. The ledger, the secret, the aching hands, the twenty-five years of holding the wall up with his shoulders—it all came away at once, and what remained underneath was so small, so tired, so ordinary, that he nearly laughed.

An old man in expensive gloves, standing in the snow, having built nothing that lasted.

"I was trying to save them," he said, and heard how it sounded, and said it again anyway, because it was the only prayer he had left. "I was trying to *save them*."

"I know," Bren said. "That's the worst part. So was she."

Above them, the Spire blazed like a second dawn, and somewhere in its burning heart a woman was offering up her name to the fire, and the Hollow—the thing Corvane had fed, had served, had bargained with in a cold room half a lifetime ago—was screaming.

He felt it scream. He felt it pass through the ice beneath his boots and up through his bones, and in that scream he heard his own voice from twenty-five years ago, saying *yes, agreed, done*, shaking a nameless hand in the dark.

The bargain had come due. And the terrible, merciful truth was that it was not being collected from him.

It was being paid by someone better.

Corvane sank to his knees in the snow, and no one stopped him, and no one helped him up, and the light of the renewed covenant poured over him like a tide, washing the ice, washing the faceless watchers backward into the dark, washing everything clean except the one thing it could never wash: the knowledge of what he had known, and when, and what he had done with it anyway.

Behind him, far below on the mountain, the first sound of the Spire's renewal rolled down the slopes like a bell struck in the bones of the world—

and inside that sound, faint but unmistakable, he heard footsteps descending. Coming toward the place where he knelt.

Coming to see what the architect of ruin would do, now that the building had finally fallen.
