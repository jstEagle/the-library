## 21. Names We Choose

The guildhall smelled of new timber and old smoke.

They had rebuilt the east wing first—the wing that had burned when Corvane's hunters came through the pass road with fire arrows and bad orders—and the carpenters had left the beams pale and raw, smelling of sap and sawdust, so that walking beneath them felt like walking inside a living tree. Rosalind liked it. The old hall had always smelled of dust and tallow and the particular staleness of a place where people stopped believing in their own work. This one smelled like something still growing.

She sat at the long table in the morning light, a cup of barley tea going cold at her elbow, and looked at the page in front of her for the hundredth time since the Spire.

The Vellum Codex lay open to its final leaf. Tobin's handwriting marched down the margin in small, careful letters—his grandfather's habit of annotation passed down like an heirloom of ink:

*Rosalind Lockridge. Daughter of Elira, forge-mother. Bearer of the ember. Born in the year the ice sang.*

Her name. Written in a hand she now knew better than her own reflection, because it was the only hand in the world that could still write it.

Everyone else had forgotten. She had known they would—she had chosen it, after all, had stood in the burning heart of the Spire and offered up every record, every memory, every mention of *Rosalind Lockridge* to the flame, and the flame had taken its payment with a thoroughness that was almost gentle. Maren remembered a woman who had walked out of the glacier's shadow and helped carry Maren down the mountain, but asked her, twice a day, what her name was, and each time forgot the answer before the tea was poured. Bren remembered leading a hunt into the high passes and coming back with nothing but a ledger page he couldn't explain and a hollow feeling behind his ribs that he described, embarrassed, as *like losing a word you were about to say*. Even the codex itself had gone blank where her name had once been prophesied—the final page now bore only tomorrow's date and a description of a woman whose face the scribe had left unfinished, as if his grandfather's pen had simply refused.

Only Tobin remembered. Because three nights before the reforging, sitting by a fire in the shrine at Ash Hollow while she slept, he had dipped his pen and gambled on ink over memory, writing her name into the margin of a book that had already cost his family two generations of obscurity.

*Ink is older than the Hollow,* he'd said, when she'd found it and gone very quiet. *Older than dragons, maybe. My grandfather believed names written truly couldn't be unwritten. I just... decided to believe him too.*

She ran her thumb along the letters now. They hadn't faded. Whatever the flame had done to the world's memory, it had not touched this small act of stubborn record-keeping, and every morning when she woke in the narrow room above the kitchen, her first thought was not of the fire or the mountain or the screaming dark that had recoiled from the renewed covenant—it was of whether the page would still say her name.

It always did. And every morning, reading it, she felt the same strange doubled sensation: like hearing her own voice played back to her, familiar and foreign at once.

Because here was the thing no one warned you about getting your name back: it didn't come with instructions.

* * *

"You're doing the staring-at-the-book thing again," said Bren, dropping into the chair across from her hard enough to make the table jump. He set down a crate of nails, then a second crate, then himself more firmly, and helped himself to her tea without asking, which was how he had always announced that a conversation was happening whether she consented or not.

"It's my book."

"It's the guild's book, technically, since the guild's charter is being rewritten on the table next to it, and you're supposed to be helping." He nodded at the fresh sheet of vellum beside the codex, where Maren's careful draft of the new Emberwright charter lay half-finished. "Corvane's old one had clauses in it I'm not proud we enforced. 'Obedience to the Guildmaster in all matters touching the covenant.' Turns out that clause is how one man sells the world's safety to save his own ledger." He drank. "Maren wants the new one to say the covenant belongs to whoever keeps it. Not to any single keeper."

"That sounds like Maren."

"She also wants it signed by every member, past and present, who'll hold a pen. Which brings me to my actual point." Bren set down the cup and looked at her with the directness of a man who had known her since they were both thieves in the lower town, stealing apples off the guild's cart and blaming each other with total sincerity. "You have to sign it."

"I know."

"Do you?" He leaned forward. "Ros. You've been sitting at this table for four days reading your own name like it might bite you. You haven't signed anything. You haven't told anyone what you're going to call yourself. Half the guild thinks the woman from the Spire has no name at all, which, fair, but the other half thinks she's waiting for someone to give her one, and I've watched you go quiet every time Maren starts suggesting options. *Elira's daughter.* *The Bearer.* *Emberwright.*" He ticked them off on scarred fingers. "You hate all of them."

"I don't hate them."

"You flinched at 'the Bearer' so hard you knocked over your own cup."

That was true. She had. Because *Bearer* was what the prophecy had called her, and the prophecy had been wrong about nearly everything except the date, and she had spent twenty-five years building a self out of exactly nothing—no family name that meant anything, no history anyone could check, no face anyone remembered twice—and she had survived on that nothing, and part of her, the part that still walked the smuggler paths in her sleep, did not want to be handed a name like a yoke.

"I spent my whole life being nobody," she said finally. "And it turns out nobody was a fine thing to be. Nobody got fed. Nobody got hunted. Nobody died in gorges or lost friends to paper-faced things in rivers." She turned the codex page toward him. "Then I find out I was somebody all along—somebody's daughter, somebody's prophecy, somebody's *payment*—and every version of that person was written by someone else. My mother wrote me into hiding. Your guildmaster wrote me out of the records. Tobin's grandfather wrote me into a book a century before I was born. Everyone got a turn holding the pen except me."

Bren was quiet for a moment. Outside, the sound of hammers rose and fell; somewhere in the yard, one of the younger Emberwrights was laughing at something, the bright unguarded laugh of a person who had never watched a village stand empty with its doors open.

"So sign your own name," he said. "Not theirs. Yours."

"That's the problem, Bren. I don't know what mine is."

He grinned, and it was the old grin, the apple-thief grin, and it cracked something loose in her chest that had been tight since the gorge. "Good," he said. "Means you get to pick. Most people never get the chance. They just inherit whatever their parents slapped on them and spend their whole lives either living up to it or climbing out from under it." He stood, taking his crates of nails with him, and paused at the door. "For what it's worth—I knew you before you had a name worth keeping, and I'd follow you up that glacier again tomorrow. Whatever you write on the line, it won't change what I already know you are."

"And what's that?"

"Trouble," he said, and went out into the light.

* * *

Tobin found her at dusk on the rebuilt balcony, where the guildhall's eastern wall overlooked the Thornfell passes, and where the first hearth of the new season had been lit in the great iron basin that had once held nothing but ash.

He came quietly—he always did, a man trained by a lifetime of archives to move without disturbing anything—but she heard him anyway. She was learning the sound of him the way she'd once learned the creak of a specific stair, the rattle of a specific shutter: knowledge that arrived without permission and refused to leave.

"The charter's ready," he said, leaning on the rail beside her. Below, the valley lights were coming on one by one, and in each window where an Emberwright kept a hearth, the flame burned with that faint gold-green edge that said dragon-fire, steady and patient, holding back the dark the way it had held it back for a thousand years and would now hold it for a thousand more. "Maren finished the last clause. She wants signatures at dawn."

"Maren always wants signatures at dawn. She says ink dries truer in cold air."

"She says a lot of things she attributes to tradition that I suspect she invented last Tuesday." He smiled, then let it fade, and looked at her sideways. "You haven't signed yet."

"No."

"Can I ask why? You don't have to answer."

She watched the fire in the basin. It moved differently than ordinary flame—slower, denser, like something alive that had agreed to sit still. In the depths of it, if she looked long enough, she could almost see shapes: wings, maybe, or mountains, or a woman's hands folding a child's blanket in a house that no longer existed anywhere but in a dead woman's surrendered memories.

"When I was six," Rosalind said, "my mother braided my hair every morning before she left for the forge. Two braids, crossed at the back, pinned with a copper clasp shaped like a flame. I remember the weight of it. I remember the clasp clicking. I remember thinking, *this is what mothers do, this is what daughters are, this is the shape of my life.*" Her voice stayed level. She'd had practice. "I don't remember her face anymore. That went into the Spire with everything else. But I remember the braids. And I've been thinking—if she'd lived, if she'd never gone up that mountain, she'd have named me herself. Given me whatever name she wanted. And I'd have carried it my whole life without ever asking whether it fit, because children don't get to ask." She turned to look at him. "I got the choice instead. Do you understand what a strange inheritance that is? Everyone else gets a name the way they get a nose. I get to *write* mine."

Tobin was silent for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was careful, the way it was whenever the conversation edged near the margin of the codex. "You could reclaim it. Rosalind. It's yours. I only wrote it down—it was always yours."

"I know."

"Or—" He hesitated. "Or you could take a new one. And I'd write that down too. If you wanted."

"And if I chose no name at all? If I walked out of here tomorrow and let the whole world keep forgetting?"

"Then I'd keep the book," he said simply. "So that someday, if you ever wanted it back, there'd be a page with your name on it, waiting. That's all a scribe is, in the end. Someone who holds the door open."

Something in her chest went hot and tight, and she looked away, back to the fire, because some things were easier to feel when you weren't being seen feeling them—even by the one person in the world who could see you.

"My mother gave up her memories to buy time for the covenant," Rosalind said. "She gave up her *name*, Tobin. She walked into the Spire knowing she'd come out the other side a stranger to herself, and she did it so that a daughter she'd never see grow up would have a world to grow up in. Everything I am started with her giving everything away." She shook her head slowly. "And I keep thinking—that can't be the whole lesson. Love can't only be loss. Somebody in this story has to choose something and *keep* it."

"So choose," he said softly. "Keep something."

Below them, the dragon-fire breathed in its iron basin, gold-edged and patient, and far to the north, past the passes, the glacier that had birthed the Spire caught the last red light of sunset and held it, warm as a coal.

* * *

Dawn came gray and clean, with the smell of snowmelt in the air.

They gathered in the great hall—all of them, the remnant and the renewal of the Emberwrights together. Maren stood at the head of the table with the charter laid open before her, her hands steady even though her memory was still full of holes, still missing the shape of a girl she'd once hidden in a root cellar while her mother climbed a mountain to forget herself. She had told Rosalind, two nights ago, in the plain voice she used for hard truths: *I can't remember loving you. I know I did, because everyone tells me so, and because of how it feels when I look at you—like a room where furniture used to be. So I've decided to fill the room again. New pieces. Starting now.* And then she had asked Rosalind to teach her to play knucklebones, because apparently the old Maren had never learned, and the new Maren intended to do everything the old one hadn't.

Bren stood at Maren's right. The young Emberwrights—there were nine of them now, drawn by rumor and by the visible fact of dragon-fire burning in hearths that had been cold for a generation—stood along the walls. Tobin sat with the codex open beside the charter, pen ready, because Maren had ruled that the new covenant would be recorded twice: once in law, once in ink, so that no future guildmaster could burn one copy and claim the other was forgery.

One by one, they signed. Maren first, her letters tall and exact. Then Bren, scrawling his name like a man signing a confession. Then the nine, some bold, some hesitant, one boy pressing so hard he tore the vellum and had to be given a second chance under Maren's reproving eye.

Then it was Rosalind's turn.

The room went quiet. She felt their attention settle on her—not the hungry curiosity of people waiting for a spectacle, but something gentler: the attention of people watching someone decide something that belonged only to them.

She picked up the pen. Dipped it. Held it over the line.

*Rosalind Lockridge.* She could write it. Tobin's margin-page sat right there, offering it back to her, every letter intact. She could reclaim her mother's choice of name, her mother's legacy, the identity that prophecy and blood and sacrifice had all conspired to write for her. It would be true. It would be hers by every right that existed.

But rights weren't the same as choices. Her mother had taught her that, in the end—had chosen oblivion rather than let the covenant fail, because a choice freely made was worth more than an inheritance passively received. Elira Lockridge had not been defined by what she was given. She had been defined by what she gave.

Rosalind set the pen to the line and wrote.

She wrote it slowly, carefully, the way her mother must once have braided her hair: two strands, crossed, pinned with something bright. A name built from what she chose to keep and what she chose to become. *Rose*—for the woman who had walked out of the ice, the name the world half-remembered, the flower that grew wild in the Thornfell passes where nothing else dared take root. *Lynn*—for the lake below the Spire, black glass water that had reflected the renewed fire the night the covenant blazed, the first beautiful thing she had seen as the person she was becoming. And for the rest—a surname of her own devising, one no ledger had ever recorded, no prophecy had ever foretold, no guildmaster had ever sold: *Vale.* For the valleys she had smuggled through, the hidden roads only she had known, the low places between mountains where a ghost could survive and, eventually, stop needing to be one.

*Roslyn Vale.*

She signed it, and the ink took, and something in her chest settled—not with the finality of a door closing, but with the ease of a pack adjusted properly on the shoulders. Lighter. Fitted. Hers.

Maren read the name aloud, testing it, and nodded once. "Roslyn Vale. Charter-member of the refounded Emberwright Guild." Her eyes crinkled. "It suits you. Though I confess I'll probably call you *girl* anyway, out of habit."

"You called me worse," Roslyn said, and was rewarded by the flicker of Maren's smile—the smile of a woman building new furniture in an empty room.

Tobin bent over the codex and, beneath his grandfather's marginal note, added a line of his own in his small careful hand: *Roslyn Vale. Chosen of her own free will, in the year the fire returned.* He looked up at her as he blotted it, and neither of them said anything, because some records speak loudest unwritten.

Bren cleared his throat. "Well. Now that that's settled—" He spread a map across the table, weighted at the corners with nail-crates, and Roslyn saw that the southern reaches were marked in fresh charcoal. "Word came down the pass road yesterday. Three villages south of the Thornfells report their hearth-flames guttering. Green-edged fire, burning low. Same signs as before, they say." He looked up, and there was no fear in his face—only the flat readiness of a man who had learned exactly what was worth fearing and refused to waste the emotion elsewhere. "Could be nothing. Could be embers running thin in old hearths that never got properly relit. Or—" he tapped the map, "—could be something left over. Something the Hollow dropped when it ran."

Maren's jaw tightened. Tobin's hand drifted, unconsciously, to the codex.

Roslyn Vale looked at the map—at the roads only she knew threading through those southern hills like veins through a wrist—and felt the old familiar pull, the smuggler's compass in her blood, except it pointed at trouble now instead of away from it.

"Pack for cold," she said. "We leave at dawn."

Outside, the dragon-fire burned steady in every hearth, and spring was coming to the Thornfells at last—but spring, she was learning, was not an ending. It was just the season when things planted in darkness finally showed what they intended to become.
