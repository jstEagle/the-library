## 22. First Fire of Spring

The guildhall smelled different now.

Rosalind noticed it the moment she came down the stairs in the grey hour before dawn—the old smell of ash and cold stone still there underneath, but over it something warmer, something living. Woodsmoke that had been tended all night instead of let to die. Tallow and bread. The particular sweetness of a brazier fed with care rather than rationed like a dying man's last coin.

She stopped on the bottom stair and breathed it in the way other people might drink water after a long thirst.

Three months since the Spire. Three months since she had walked out of the black glass tower with no name anyone could hold onto, and found Tobin waiting at the glacier's edge with a scrap of vellum held out in both hands like an offering, his fingers blue with cold, ink smeared up his wrist where he'd written against his own knee for lack of a desk.

*Rosalind Lockridge,* the margin said, in his careful scribe's hand. *Covenant-Bearer. She chose this.*

He had gambled on ink over memory, and the ink had won. Barely. For the first weeks, when she woke in the night, she had to go to the codex and read her own name just to be sure the woman sleeping in the bed was someone. The letters were an anchor thrown into dark water; she hauled herself up them hand over hand until the name fit again, until it stopped feeling like a coat borrowed from a stranger and started feeling like skin.

Mostly, it fit now.

She crossed the hall. The great hearth burned steady—not the guttering, apologetic flame of the old days, but a true fire, gold at its heart with that faint impossible shimmer that said dragon. Emberwrights' fire. It had been burning without pause since the night she carried the ember home from the Spire, and every hearth under Emberwright care across the Thornfells burned the same way: in the waystation at Miller's Rest, in the shrine below the high pass, in the little chapel at Hollow's Edge where the village had been emptied and was, slowly, filling again with people who came back because the doors could be locked against nothing now. There was nothing left out there in the dark to lock against.

The Hollow had receded like a tide going out. Not gone—Maren was clear about that, as clear as a woman could be whose memory had been edited by the thing itself—but pushed back beyond the passes, beyond the far ice, back into the between-places where hunger waited but could not reach. The covenant held. The fire held.

Rosalind knelt at the hearth and added two splits of birch, arranging them the way the ember liked, the way Maren had taught her in another life—a life Maren no longer remembered teaching.

"Maren's up," said a voice behind her. Bren, wrapped in a blanket, hair flattened on one side, carrying the teapot like a weapon he'd decided to befriend. "Been up all night, knowing her. Says the new rites won't write themselves."

"The new rites are mostly her complaining about how the old ones were better," Rosalind said.

"Yes, but she writes down the complaints." Bren poured tea into three chipped mugs, handed her one. His face did something complicated for a moment—the thing it always did lately, the thing she'd stopped trying to wave away. He had come to the gorge to bring her back dead or discredited, and she had shown him a ledger page instead, and he had turned, and some betrayals could be walked back, but they left footprints. "I'm glad you're here," he said, which was what the complicated look always meant. "In case I haven't said it enough times."

"You've said it enough times."

"Good." He drank his tea. Outside, the sky over the eastern peaks had gone the color of a bruise healing—purple fading to rose along the ridgeline. "Sun'll be up soon. She'll want you on the wall for it. She's been saving something."

Rosalind raised an eyebrow. "Saving what?"

"Wouldn't say." Bren grinned, and for a moment he was fifteen again, both of them stealing apples off Corvane's windowsill before everything went wrong, before ledgers and bargains and a guildmaster who had sold the world's safety for solvency. Corvane himself was gone now—broken at the Spire's threshold, taken south by guild remnant and council both, to answer for twenty-five years of quiet ruin. Rosalind found she could not hate him anymore. That frightened her more than hatred would have. Hatred was simple. What she felt was closer to pity, and pity was heavier to carry.

She took her tea up to the wall.

* * *

Maren was waiting at the parapet with a ledger under her arm and frost in her white eyebrows, looking exactly like a woman who had argued with the dawn and lost but intended to file a complaint about it anyway.

"You're late," she said.

"I'm early. You're just earlier."

"Same thing." But there was no bite in it. There hadn't been for months—not since the day Maren had sat Rosalind down across a table, folded her hands, and said, in the voice she used for hard truths: *I don't remember you. I know that hurts you, and I want you to know it hurts me too, not remembering. So we're going to do it properly. I'm going to learn you the way I'd learn any text—line by line—and you're going to be patient with your old teacher.* They had been doing it properly ever since. Line by line.

Maren opened the ledger on the parapet stone. "New rite, third draft. I want you to hear it before the others do, because half of it is yours whether you like it or not."

"Mine?"

"You gave your name to the fire." Maren said it plainly now; the first time she'd tried, her voice had cracked like green wood. "That's not in any of the old texts. The old payment was memories, or a life, or a line unfinished mid-stroke like your mother's—" She paused, steadied. Rosalind had learned to let her pause. "The old payments were all subtraction. Yours was the first that was also a gift. That changes the shape of the words. Listen."

And she read. The new rite was shorter than the old one—that was the first surprise. Where the ancient covenant-words had wound through pages of invocation and fear, *keep the fire or the dark will keep you*, Maren's draft moved like clean water: tend the flame, share the warmth, write down what must not be forgotten, choose your name and mean it. Simple things. Hard things. Things a smuggler with no history and a scribe with ink-stained cuffs and a keeper with holes in her head could actually carry.

"It's good," Rosalind said, and meant it. "It sounds like people."

"It sounds like you," Maren corrected. "Which I'm told is the same thing." She looked out east, where the light was climbing. "There's the other matter. The missing pieces."

Rosalind went still. This was the conversation they circled every few weeks, approaching from different directions like wary animals.

Maren's hollowing had taken every memory that touched Rosalind—her mother Elira, the hiding of the child, the years of watching a girl grow up invisible in the guildhall's corners. The rest of Maren's long life remained intact, perfect, merciless in its detail. She remembered the famine winters and the codex's every page and the exact weight of every lie Corvane had ever told her. Only Rosalind was gone, cut out clean.

For a while Maren had tried to recover the pieces. She had read Tobin's grandfather's marginalia, interviewed old guild members, reconstructed Elira's story from documents like an archaeologist assembling a stranger's skeleton. And then, one evening in midwinter, she had closed the folder and pushed it across the table and said: *No. I'm done digging.*

"Why?" Rosalind had asked, stung despite herself.

*Because those aren't my memories,* Maren had said. *They're evidence. A keeper knows the difference. If I fill the hole with facts, I'll spend the rest of my life grieving a girl I never actually knew—and worse, so will you, because you'll see me grieving and think it's the real thing, and it isn't, and we'll both know it isn't.* She had tapped the table once, decisively. *So instead I'm going to make new ones. Starting now.*

And that was what she had done. She had asked Rosalind to teach her the smuggler paths—not the ones from before, but the ones Rosalind knew now, the ways only she knew. She had sat with her through the nights when the name still didn't fit, taking notes in her precise scribe's hand, building a record of the woman Rosalind was becoming. She had laughed, deliberately, extravagantly, at Rosalind's terrible jokes, on the stated principle that a memory made laughing was worth ten recovered.

"There," Maren said now, nodding at the horizon, where the sun was about to break the ridge above Thornfell. "That's what I saved you for. First sunrise after the last hard frost. In the old book it says the keepers watched for it alone, in silence, as a vigil." Her eyes crinkled. "I've amended that too."

Below them, doors began to open along the rebuilt row of cottages at the guildhall's foot. Bren came out with the teapot. Tobin emerged blinking, codex under his arm out of pure habit, followed by the two youngest Emberwrights, who had joined since the reforging—young people, actual young people, drawn back to a dying order that wasn't dying anymore. Even a couple of Miller's Rest farmers had walked up in the dark for it, because word had gone round that the Emberwrights watched the first fire of spring on the wall, and anyone who wanted could stand with them.

Nobody stood alone in silence. They stood together, passing tea, breath steaming, shoulders touching against the cold.

The sun broke the ridge.

Light poured down the Thornfells like something spilled from a generous hand—gold flooding the snowfields, catching the pines, setting the glacier above the Spire's hidden tower ablaze white and rose. Somewhere under all that ice, the black glass stood warm, holding its renewed heart. Somewhere beyond the far passes, the Hollow pressed against a door that no longer rattled on its hinges.

Rosalind watched it come, and did not hide.

That was the whole of it, really. That was the price paid and the payment made. She had given the fire her name and gotten it back written in someone else's hand—one line in a margin, *she chose this*—and in the space between losing it and reclaiming it, she had learned the difference between a name you inherit and a name you sign. Elira Lockridge had gone to the Spire to buy time with her memories, loving her daughter in the only currency oblivion accepted. Rosalind had gone to finish the sentence her mother left mid-stroke. Both of them had paid. Both of them had chosen. And this morning the sun came up on a world where the choosing had been worth it.

Tobin reached her side, breathless from the stairs, and without a word opened the codex to the final page. Tomorrow's date was there, as it had been that first night in the ruined waystation—but the page beneath it was blank now, fresh vellum, waiting. He'd shown her weeks ago: the prophecy was finished. Whatever came next, no book had written it yet.

"Any thoughts?" he asked. "For the next entry. Maren wants the codex kept current. Says a covenant nobody writes down is just a rumor."

Rosalind looked east, where the light was still spreading, where the passes lay open and safe and full of morning, where a woman with no recorded past stood in full view of everyone and felt, for the first time in her life, entirely seen.

"Put down that the fire holds," she said. "And put down that we're going to need a bigger guildhall."

Tobin laughed and uncapped his ink.

Behind them, Maren cleared her throat, opened her ledger to a fresh page, and wrote, in a hand as steady as the flame:

*First fire of spring. All present. All accounted for.*

*Nothing missing that we cannot make anew.*
