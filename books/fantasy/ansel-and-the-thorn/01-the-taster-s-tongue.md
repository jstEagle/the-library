## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The bells of Saint Verren's rang the sixth hour, and Quentin Blackwood was already on his knees in the king's antechamber with a silver spoon in his mouth.

This was the shape of his life: cold stone under his shins, the smell of beeswax and yesterday's woodsmoke, and the small, patient ritual of dying by degrees for a man he had never once spoken to. He swallowed. He waited. He counted to sixty the way Master Corvel had taught him — *one for the tongue, two for the throat, three for the belly* — and when nothing seized him, nothing burned, nothing turned his blood to vinegar, he nodded once to the steward and said the words that had kept him fed and housed and un-hanged for five years.

"It's clean."

The kitchen boys filed in behind him with the breakfast trays, moving with the careful choreography of people who had learned not to spill anything near royalty. Quentin rose, brushed the dust from his knee, and took his place at the sideboard where his real work began: standing slightly behind the king's left shoulder, tasting each dish before it touched the royal plate, watching the king eat, and waiting — always waiting — for the day the counting failed.

King Aldric III ate like a man who had forgotten food could be interesting. He was fifty-one years old and gray at the temples, broad through the chest still but softening at the middle, with the heavy-lidded eyes of someone who had spent thirty years signing things other people wrote. This morning he was dressed in hunting green though the hunt had been cancelled on account of rain, and he ate his way through smoked trout, brown bread with honey, a rasher of cured ham, and a dish of pears stewed in wine and winter spice, all without once looking up from the dispatches a nervous clerk read aloud beside him.

Quentin tasted everything first. That was the arrangement. The tongue of the taster was the crown's first wall against poisoners, and in the five years since Master Corvel had died — quietly, in his sleep, which everyone agreed was suspicious only after the fact — Quentin had been that wall. He was good at it. He was, if he was honest with himself, better than good. He had never once let the king so much as burp sideways.

What no one in the palace knew — what no one could know, because the city of Carreth hung mages from the Iron Arch on Wexford Street and left them there as a lesson in civic order — was that Quentin Blackwood was not merely immune to poison.

He was a charm-seller. A hedge-witch's bastard grandson with a witch's blood thinning in his veins, selling forbidden little workings out of a coat pocket in the night market for copper coins. And the reason he was better than any taster the crown had ever employed was not training, or caution, or some gift of the gods.

It was magic. Small, illegal, daily magic, worn down to habit like a coin rubbed smooth.

He did it now, automatically, as the pear dish came toward the king. While the clerk droned about grain tariffs in the eastern provinces, Quentin let his attention sink into his tongue — into the deadened, cotton-wrapped thing it had become — and found the familiar hum beneath it. Five years of swallowing every poison the kitchens could produce, in doses no honest man would survive, had done something to him that no physician could explain and no priest would bless. His body had learned to *answer*. When venom touched him, something old and quiet in his blood rose to meet it, neutralizing, dissolving, drinking the harm away. The court physicians called it constitution. Master Corvel, in the last year of his life, had called it by its right name over a cup of watered wine, and then looked at Quentin with an expression that was half envy and half pity, and said: *You're not a taster, boy. You're a sponge. God help you.*

So Quentin tasted, and his blood drank the danger, and the king lived, and Quentin collected his wage of eight silver marks a month plus lodging in the tasters' wing, and everyone was satisfied except the part of Quentin that lay awake at night wondering what five years of swallowed poison was doing to the rest of him — the parts that didn't get tested.

The spoon came back to him with a portion of the spiced pears.

They were beautiful, as the palace pears always were: poached until translucent, glazed dark amber with honeyed wine, dusted with cinnamon, clove, and a thread of orange peel curled on top like a question mark. The cook, Mistress Hetta, was proud of this dish. She made it perhaps twice a season, when the winter stores of pears were at their sweetest, and she watched from the doorway now with her arms folded, waiting for the king's pleasure as though his approval were a matter of personal honor.

Quentin put the spoon in his mouth.

Honey. Wine gone soft and round with slow heat. Cinnamon first, then clove blooming warm at the back of the throat, the ghost of orange peel, the pear itself collapsing into sweetness —

— and underneath it, faint as a whisper heard through a wall, *bitterness*.

Not the bitterness of clove or burnt sugar or wine turned. Something else. Something that didn't belong in a kitchen at all. It sat at the very root of his tongue, green and mineral and wrong, the way a snapped stem smells, the way crushed leaves of some plant he couldn't name taste — and it was gone almost before he registered it, drowned under the spice, so brief he might have imagined it.

His charms hummed.

That was the thing that stopped his breath. Not the taste — tastes lied, tastes were tricks of the morning and the mood — but the hum. Deep under his skin, in the place where his illegal little talent lived coiled and sleeping, something had stirred. The way a dog lifts its head before the earthquake comes. The way his grandmother's charms used to tick in their boxes on hot nights, she'd said, when storms were far off over the hills.

Quentin did not move. He had learned, in five years of court life, that a taster's face was a piece of state equipment, oiled and blank. He swallowed. He counted. One for the tongue. Two for the throat. Three for the belly. Ten. Twenty. Sixty.

Nothing seized him. Nothing burned. His blood rose up and drank whatever it was, the way it drank everything, and left him standing there whole and hollow and suddenly, quietly afraid.

"Clean," he said.

King Aldric took three bites of the pears, pronounced them adequate to no one in particular, and pushed the dish aside in favor of more trout. Mistress Hetta's face fell by a degree. The trays were cleared. The clerk moved on to a dispute over mill rights. The morning proceeded exactly as ten thousand mornings before it had proceeded, and Quentin stood at his post with his hands folded behind his back and his heart going like a trapped bird.

*It's nothing,* he told himself. *Spice gone off. A bad pear. The bottom of the wine cask.*

But he had tasted that bitterness before. Not in a dish — in a *person*, years ago, when he was nineteen and stupid and had bought a love-charm from a woman in the night market whose stall smelled of pond water and iron. The charm hadn't worked. Of course it hadn't; half the market's wares were sawdust and theater. But the woman had been ill — he'd seen it in the yellow of her eyes, the sweat of her — and when he'd shaken her hand to take his coin back, that same green, mineral wrongness had come off her skin like heat off a stove. Root-of-the-tongue bitter. Stem-snapped bitter.

She was dead within the month. Slow fever, the street said. Wasting sickness.

Quentin had thought about her maybe twice in six years. Now he could not stop thinking about her, standing in the warm morning light of the king's antechamber while a clerk read about grain.

* * *

The tasters' wing occupied a narrow slice of the palace's east face, above the sculleries and below the pigeon lofts, and it smelled permanently of both. Quentin's cell was twelve feet by nine, furnished with a bed, a chest, a washstand, and a window too narrow to climb through even if he'd had anywhere to go. He had lived in worse. He had lived in much worse, in the years between his grandmother's death and Master Corvel finding him half-starved in the river district, doing charm-work for dockhands who paid in fish heads and didn't ask questions.

He locked the door. Then he knelt, pried up the loose floorboard under the washstand, and lifted out the oilcloth bundle that was, in a very real sense, his entire life.

Inside were his charms. Not many — eleven, at last count, and he knew each one the way a father knows children. There was the hearing-coin, worn smooth, which let him listen through walls for as long as he held it under his tongue (he never wore it during tastings; a man needed one honest sense). There were three ward-knots of braided horsehair, good for keeping dreams clean of meddling. There was a finding-pin that pointed, weakly and grudgingly, toward whatever he'd most recently lost. The rest were smaller things: a knot that eased toothache, a scrap of lead that turned a knife-hand clumsy for the space of ten heartbeats, a dried rowan berry that — he was fairly sure — did absolutely nothing, but which his grandmother had carried and so he carried too, the way one carries a name.

None of it was grand. None of it would ever make him rich, or free, or safe. Charm-work at his level was candle-flame work: warmth enough to live by, light enough to see the next hour, and nothing that would ever throw back the dark. The great workings — weather-binding, soul-craft, the raising of wards over cities — those had belonged to the Thorn-mages, the old blood, and the old blood was ash. Everyone knew that. Every child in Carreth knew that. Two hundred years ago the crown had burned the Thorn-speakers out of the world in the war the histories called the Purging, and the land had been clean of their magic ever since, and the Iron Arch on Wexford Street existed to keep it that way.

Quentin's grandmother had been careful her whole life, and it had bought her seventy-one years and a peaceful death in her own bed, which was more than most of her kind got. Her last instruction to him, delivered in a voice already halfway to elsewhere, had been simple:

*Small workings, Quent. Small workings and a shut mouth. The moment they can see your fire, they'll come to put it out.*

He was turning the hearing-coin over in his fingers when the humming started again.

Faint. At the edge of perception. Coming — he went still, listening with more than his ears — coming from the direction of the palace kitchens. From below. From the trays, the pots, the stores. Or from one thing among them, one specific thing, singing a note he had never heard it sing in five years of service.

He closed his hand around the coin and the hum cut off, muffled, like a bird under a cloth.

Quentin sat back on his heels in the middle of his narrow cell and did the arithmetic of fear. The bitterness was in the pears. The pears came from the winter stores, dressed by Mistress Hetta, drawn from casks sealed in autumn. If the bitterness was poison — and his blood had drunk it easily, which meant it was mild, which meant it was meant to be *mild* — then it was either accident or design. Accidents happened; a bad cask, a contaminated spice shipment. Accidents did not make his charms hum like a struck bell.

And accidents, he thought, did not taste like that. Poison meant to kill announced itself. Arsenic was harsh, hemlock numbed, nightshade burned. What he'd tasted had been gentle. Patient. A flavor designed to hide inside another flavor, to pass unnoticed by every tongue in the kingdom except —

Except his. Except the one tongue in Carreth that answered poison with magic instead of death.

A cold thought walked up his spine on small feet: *if someone is dosing the king slowly, carefully, invisibly — then whoever they are, they've built their poison to defeat ordinary testing. And the only thing standing between their work and discovery is me.*

He was, he realized, not the wall between the king and his enemies.

He was the flaw in it.

* * *

Evening came down on Carreth like a lid. Quentin walked home the long way, as he always did, taking the covered arcade past the chandlers' row and the fountain square where the statue of King Aldric's grandfather held a sword nobody remembered the point of. Rain had swept through at midday and the cobbles still shone, lamp-lit, mirror-bright, doubling the city into the sky. Somewhere a fiddler was playing badly. Somewhere a watchman called the hour. Ordinary sounds. Ordinary city.

He did not feel ordinary. He felt like a man carrying a lit coal in his mouth.

At the corner of Tanner's Lane he stopped, as he stopped every evening, and let himself do the thing he did every evening: he listened to his own life and asked whether it was still holding. Wage: sufficient. Lodging: secure. Cover story: intact. Tongue: numb, reliable, compromised by one unexplained bitterness. Charms: hidden. Grandmother's rule: observed. Five years of small sales and a shut mouth, and every night the ledger balanced, and every night he told himself the same thing — *one more year. Save a little more. Buy the cottage in the hill country, the one with the south-facing garden, and disappear into a life where nobody tastes anything for anybody.*

The ledger balanced tonight. It balanced badly.

Because underneath the routine, under the wet shine of the streets and the fiddler's scraping, the dread had settled into him and made itself at home. He kept seeing the pear, translucent and glistening on the silver spoon. Kept tasting that green, mineral ghost at the root of his tongue. Kept hearing, faintly, absurdly, the hum.

He was so deep in it that he nearly walked past the notice board outside the Guildhall without looking. Nearly. But something — habit, or the animal instinct of a man who lives by noticing — pulled his eye to the fresh paper pinned there, ink still sharp, the seal of the Lord Chamberlain's office pressed fat and red at the bottom.

*A reward,* it said, *of fifty crowns, for information leading to the recovery of stolen property belonging to the Crown. Inquire at the Chamberlain's offices. Discretion assured.*

Stolen property. Vague. Unusual. Reward notices usually named the thing — a ring, a reliquary, a runaway servant. This one named nothing. And below the main text, in smaller print, almost an afterthought, a single line:

*Particular attention given to reports concerning persons seen near the city walls after dark.*

Quentin read it three times. Around him the evening crowd flowed past, hooded and hurried, none of them giving the board a glance. The lamps hissed. The fiddler scraped. Somewhere beyond the walls, out in the dark fields where no honest person had business walking, the rain was falling on whatever it was the Lord Chamberlain wanted found.

Persons. After dark. Near the walls.

Fifty crowns was a fortune. Fifty crowns was ten years of charm-sales, was the cottage in the hill country outright, was freedom. Whoever had posted it wanted something badly, and wanted it quietly, and had written the notice so broadly that it could mean anyone — beggars, smugglers, lovers meeting at the postern gate.

Or it could mean one person. One small person. One small person with green eyes, if the rumors that drifted through the night market were worth anything at all — and Quentin had heard those rumors just last week, laughed at them, dismissed them. Green-eyed children, a buyer had said, slipping him a copper for a toothache knot. Seen along the north wall. Wild-looking. Folk say they speak backwards.

He had dismissed it. He dismissed it again now, standing in the lamplight with the notice board dripping gently in front of him.

But his charms, deep in the oilcloth bundle under his coat, against all reason and all his grandmother's rules, had begun — very softly, very patiently — to hum.
