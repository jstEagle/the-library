## 2. Charms for Copper

The court emptied slowly, the way a wound closes — layer by layer, from the outer offices inward, until only the king and his shadows remained behind the great doors of the audience chamber. Quentin Blackwood slipped out with the lesser functionaries, a grey man in a grey coat, carrying an empty tasting tray and a tongue that still hummed faintly from the morning's work.

He had tasted eleven dishes today. The pheasant in aspic, the honeyed roots, the wine cut with river water because the good vintage was being saved for the Harvest Court. His mouth remembered each of them the way a ledger remembers debts: neutrally, precisely, without appetite. Five years of this had scraped the pleasure out of eating and left behind something more useful — an instrument. A forked reed that quivered at the wrong bitterness, the wrong sweetness, the wrong *anything*.

The palace guards at the east post nodded to him. One of them, Brakkus, a heavy man with a boxer's flattened nose, even smiled.

"Evening, Taster."

"Evening." Everyone here called him Taster as if it were his name. Perhaps it was. Quentin Blackwood had died five years ago in a debtor's alley in Lowmarsh; what walked out of it was the Taster, a man with a numb tongue and no history worth hanging.

The city took him like water takes a dropped coin.

* * *

Dusk over Corvane was a color he never tired of, though he'd never admit to tiring of anything. The sun went down behind the western terraces and the smoke of ten thousand hearths rose straight into the windless sky, turning amber, then bruise-purple, then the deep honest black that let the lamps take over. Lamplighters worked the Corn Exchange row with their long poles, and the smell of fried onions and tar and horse came off the streets in layers, and somewhere a fiddler was murdering a reel badly enough that people paid him to stop.

Quentin walked home the long way, which was to say he walked toward the night market.

His lodgings were three rooms above a chandler's shop on Saint Verre's Lane — one room, really, and two closets arguing over the remainder. He kept them spare. A bed, a stove, a table with a drawer that had no lock because locks invited attention. Under the third floorboard from the window, wrapped in oilcloth, lay everything else he owned: his stock in trade.

He waited until full dark before lifting the board.

Charms for copper. That was how the buyers said it, half-laughing, the way you name a thing small so it can't be prosecuted as anything large. *Give us a charm for copper, Taster.* As if love-charms and luck-knots and fever-wards grew on the same low bush as coppers, picked free by anyone with a coin and a complaint. The law had grander words for it. *Arcane trafficking. Unlicensed thaumaturgy.* The gallows outside the Marsh Gate had grander shapes for it too: a squat timber frame with room for four, repainted every spring because the crows and the weather were hard on white paint.

Five years. Fourteen hundred-odd sales. Not one of them large enough to hang him, if he was careful, and he was careful the way other men were religious.

He counted out tonight's stock on the table. Six fever-wards — knotted cords of red thread, each one holding a breath of warmth he'd coaxed out of a candle flame and taught to stay. Three sleep-knots for the insomnia trade; dockworkers bought those mostly, men whose hands shook from the powder they unloaded at the ash-quays. A single finding-charm shaped like a small tin eye, for a woman who'd lost her wedding ring down a gutter and could not be convinced her husband hadn't pawned it. And two luck-bells, cast from cheap pewter, their clappers replaced with splinters of rowan that he had whispered to for an hour apiece until the metal forgot it was metal.

Small magic. Tiny magic. Magic so modest it barely qualified, the kind the Purging statutes dismissed with a fine rather than a rope — though the magistrates of Corvane had lately taken a broader view of *lately*, and fines had become floggings, and floggings had become appointments at the Marsh Gate.

He wrapped each piece separately. He blew out his lamp. He went down the chandler's stairs on feet that made no sound, because silence was the first charm he had ever learned and the only one he never stopped casting.

* * *

The night market lived in Maudlin Square, under the ribs of the old aqueduct, where the daylight traders wouldn't set foot because the daylight guilds had claimed every inch of legal ground and left the darkness to everyone else. It began around the ninth bell and ended whenever the watch decided it had ended, and in between it was the most honest place in Corvane.

Honest, Quentin thought, threading between a fortune-teller's table and a man selling stolen prayer books, in the way that only illegal things are. Nobody here pretended. The woman selling eels that had fallen off no legitimate cart did not claim they'd swum to her. The boy fencing lockpicks did not call them *soul instruments*. And when a customer pressed three coppers into Quentin's palm and asked for a charm, nobody asked what the charm was *really* for, because everybody in the square was really for something else entirely.

He set up in his usual spot beneath the third arch of the aqueduct, where a slab of fallen masonry made a shelf and the stone above had been stained black by two centuries of pigeon argument. He didn't announce himself. He never did. He simply laid the wares out — the red thread, the pewter bells, the tin eye — and lit a stub of candle, and the market, which knew him, sent him his customers.

First came the laundress Marta, broad as a door, who bought a fever-ward for her grandson without a word about last month's grandson, for whom the previous ward had not, in the end, been enough. Quentin took her coins and met her eyes and did not say *I'm sorry the magic doesn't work like a promise*. What he said was: "Tie it at the bedpost. Left side. And if the fever comes back anyway—" he hesitated, because the next part cost him "—send for a physician first, and keep the ward on regardless. They work better together, medicine and thread. Like rope."

Marta looked at him a long moment. She was not a stupid woman. "You're a better liar than you are a merchant," she said, and pressed a fourth copper into his hand, and he hated himself efficiently and completely, and gave her the fourth copper back, and she pretended not to notice that he'd done it, and they both pretended the whole exchange had been about thread.

That was the guilt. That was the nightly tax. Every sale had its shadow-sale, the little dishonesty underneath: that a knot of thread and a borrowed breath of flame could stand between a child and the pox. He knew what his charms were. Warmth that remembered being warm. Comfort shaped like help. He sold comfort, and people bought hope, and sometimes — often enough to keep him doing it — the hope itself did half the work, and the fever broke, and the buyer came back weeping with gratitude, and Quentin took the coins with a smile that felt like theft.

A charm is a small true thing wearing a large false promise, his first teacher had told him, back when there had been a teacher, back when teaching it had already been a hanging offense. Sell the small truth. Let them imagine the rest.

Second came the ash-quay man for his sleep-knot. Third, a pale clerk-type who hovered at the edge of the candlelight and asked, too quietly, whether Quentin had anything for *nerves*, and accepted a fever-ward with the understanding that nerves and fevers were cousins. Fourth and fifth together: two young soldiers off the wall garrison, spending pay they couldn't drink fast enough, buying luck-bells for a dice game that would separate them from the bells within the hour. Quentin told them to wear the bells, not gamble them. They promised. They lied. Everyone was honest about something.

He was wrapping a finding-charm for a fishwife when the sixth customer arrived, and later — much later, in the bad hours, in the hours he reserved for reviewing his mistakes — Quentin would try to remember exactly what the man had looked like, and fail, and understand that this failure had been engineered.

The man was ordinary. That was the whole of him. Medium height, medium coat, a face built from the parts of faces that agree not to be remembered. He turned a luck-bell over in his hands with fingers that were very clean.

"For the wall?" Quentin asked. Standard patter. Luck against arrows, luck against fever, luck against the boredom that actually killed more garrison men than either.

"Something like that." The man set the bell down and did not pick it up again. "Actually, I'm told you hear things, under this aqueduct. Things the criers don't shout."

"I sell charms for copper," Quentin said pleasantly. "Gossip I give away free, and it's worth the price."

The clean-fingered man smiled, showing nothing. "There's talk at the gates. Shepherds coming in from the west downs, drovers, that lot. They say children have been seen near the walls at dusk. Alone." He let the word sit. "Little girls, mostly. Walking the ditch-line like they're looking for a door in it. And when a watchman hails them, they run — but here's the thing, merchant." He leaned in, conspiratorial, familiar, awful. "They don't leave footprints. Hard frost three nights running, and the ditch-line's clean as a table. Green-eyed little girls who don't print the frost."

The candle flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft.

Quentin laughed. He was proud of the laugh, afterward — it came out round and easy, a merchant's laugh, bored and amused at once. "Green-eyed children who don't dirty the snow. What are they paying gossip-sellers in these days? You've been at the gin, friend. Or someone wants you repeating nonsense in markets like this one, and you're earning your keep."

"Maybe." The man straightened. "Maybe it's nonsense. Only — word is, the palace is asking after it too. Quiet-like. Chamberlain's men, going gate to gate." He picked up the luck-bell after all, weighed it, set down four times its price in silver, and left it on the stone. "For the wall," he said. "Luck against whatever's walking it."

Then he was gone into the crowd, and the crowd closed over him like water, and Quentin stood under the arch with four silver pieces he suddenly did not want to touch.

* * *

He should have gone home. That was the review he gave himself later, in the bad hours: *you should have packed your thread and gone home.*

Instead he found Doss, the pie-man, whose stall occupied the corner of the square nearest the aqueduct's shadow and whose ears collected more of the city than any two criers' mouths put together.

"Pie," said Doss.

"Gossip," said Quentin.

"Those are the same price." Doss handed him a mutton pie in a napkin greasy enough to read by. "Ask."

"The green-eyed children."

Doss's eyebrows went up, which for Doss was a whole sermon. "So it's reached the palace, then. Aye. Gate-toll men won't talk official, but my cousin's wife sells chestnuts by the Marsh Gate, and she says the drovers can't stop mentioning it. Little lass on the west ditch, seen four nights now. Never begs, never speaks. Just walks the line, dawn and dusk, like she's measuring it." He wiped his hands on his apron, which needed it. "Chandler's boy swears he got close once. Says her eyes were green as new sap. Says when she saw him looking, she put her finger to her lips—"

"—like this?" Quentin said, and then stopped, because his own finger had risen to his own lips without consulting him, and Doss was staring at it, and the market noise seemed to have stepped back a pace, the way a room does when a glass breaks somewhere.

"Like that," Doss said slowly. "You seen her?"

"No," Quentin said. "No. Bad knee. It moves when I don't tell it to." He ate the pie without tasting it, which for him meant nothing; nothing had taste anymore except the wrong things. Wrong things tasted enormous.

*Green as new sap.* There was a phrase for that color. There were — his mind supplied, unasked, from a childhood spent half-asleep against his grandmother's hip while she hummed songs in a language she claimed she'd invented to soothe him — there were *words* for that color, old ones, soft ones, and his charms, wrapped in oilcloth three floors above Saint Verre's Lane, had gone quiet as held breath.

Superstition, he told himself. Market rot. Some child run away from a farm, orphaned by the summer fevers, surviving on ditch-water and charity and too quick to be caught. Children ran wild in every city. The frost business was a shepherd's tale; frost melted where things walked, and refroze, and liars embroidered. And the palace inquiry — well. The palace inquired after everything lately. Vane's new men went door to door asking questions about everything from grain prices to gravediggers' ledgers. It meant nothing. It meant nothing.

He sold the rest of his stock by midnight. Fever-wards, sleep-knots, both luck-bells — the soldiers had come back after all, sheepish, wanting more. Copper in his purse, silver besides. A good night's trade by any measure.

He walked home through streets gone quiet, past the Marsh Gate gallows standing pale and patient in the moonlight, freshly painted, empty, waiting with the particular courtesy of furniture. He climbed the chandler's stairs. He checked his floorboard, counted his remaining stock, touched each charm once the way a man touches a sleeping child's head.

Under his fingers, the red threads were warm. Warmer than he'd left them. Warmer than the room.

All six of them, humming faintly, in unison, like throats clearing to speak.

Quentin sat down slowly on the edge of his bed and listened to his own illegal magic sing to something it recognized, somewhere out in the frost-black dark beyond the western wall — and for the first time in five careful years, the Taster was afraid.

At the window, three floors up, with the latch thrown and the shutters barred, a small green light passed by outside. Slowly. Measuring the street.

It stopped directly below his rooms.

And stayed there.
