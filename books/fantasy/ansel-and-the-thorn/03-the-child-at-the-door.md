## 3. The Child at the Door

The knock came at the hour when Quentin Blackwood trusted nothing.

Not that he trusted much at any other hour. But the last bell of night had long since rung, and the oil in his lamp was burning low and brown, and he was sitting at his table with his boots off — a luxury he permitted himself only when the city outside had gone soft and blind — counting the night's copper into little stacks by candlelight. Eleven charms sold. Three lies told. One near-miss with a lamplighter who'd looked too long at his sleeve. A fair evening's work for a man who would hang if any of it went wrong.

So when someone knocked on his door — three small raps, spaced like a heartbeat — every stack of copper stopped mattering.

He didn't move. He listened. The tenement around him breathed its usual noises: the widow Hesk's cough through the wall, the settling groan of beams that had been settling for sixty years, rain starting against the shutters in fat, reluctant drops. No footsteps retreating. No heavy breathing of a drunk who'd mistaken his door. Just the rain, and then — again — three small raps, patient as arithmetic.

"Who's there?" He kept his voice flat, bored, the voice of a tired clerk. In five years of double living he had learned that the most dangerous thing a man could sound like was interesting.

Silence. Then, so quietly he almost missed it under the rain: a sound like breath drawn to speak, and held, and never released.

Quentin rose without a whisper of sound, which was itself a small dishonesty of the body he'd practiced until it became nature. He took the iron poker from the hearth — not because iron did anything, but because a man holding a poker made different choices than a man holding nothing — and put his eye to the gap in the shutters.

The landing outside was empty.

No. Not empty. His gaze dropped, dragged down as if by a hook behind the eyes, and there she was.

A girl. Eight years old, perhaps, though hunger aged children strangely and she had the look of one who had been hungry past the point where hunger becomes a country you live in. She stood in the center of the landing with her arms at her sides and her chin up, the way children stand when they have decided that whatever comes next, they will meet it standing. Her dress had once been fine — he could see the ghost of embroidery at the hem, thread-of-gold worn down to bare suggestion — and now it was road-dirt and rain to the knee. Her hair was dark and lank and plastered to her skull. She was soaked through. She must have been standing in the rain for some time, waiting for the last bell, waiting for the lamps to die, waiting for him.

And her eyes —

Her eyes were green. Not the green of river water or bottle glass or any green that belonged to the world Quentin knew. They were the green of the first sap-rise of spring, of the pale bright line where bark splits and something living looks out. They caught his lamplight and did not give it back the way eyes should. For one absurd moment he thought: *I have seen this color before.* And then the thought slid away from him like a fish, and he could not say where.

He should have closed the shutter. Every instinct he had honed over five years of survival said *close the shutter, douse the lamp, be no one.* A child at his door at this hour was not a child; it was a hook, and somewhere above it sat a fisherman. Vane's men had been asking questions in the market — he'd heard it secondhand, the way he heard everything dangerous, wrapped in gossip. Questions about strangers. About oddities.

But she didn't knock again. She simply looked up — directly up, unerringly, at the slit of shutter where his eye was, though no child should have known exactly where an eye would be — and waited.

Quentin opened the door. Later, examining that night the way a man examines a scar, he would understand that some part of him had already opened it, days before, when a buyer in the night market had mentioned green-eyed children seen near the walls and Quentin had laughed and pocketed the man's copper. Some part of him had been listening for that knock ever since, the way a tongue listens for bitterness.

"You've the wrong house," he said.

She stepped inside.

It was done so simply, so completely without aggression, that he found himself stepping back to let her pass, which was ridiculous, which was madness, which was exactly what a mark does. She crossed his single room as if she had crossed it before — pausing at the table, regarding the stacks of copper with no interest at all, then turning a slow circle, taking in the pallet, the cold hearth, the shelf where he kept his flour and salt and, behind a loose board, everything that could kill him.

Then she stopped. Her head tilted. And Quentin saw her nostrils flare slightly, the way a hound takes scent.

She was looking at the loose board.

His blood went cold and stayed there. "Out," he said, and put iron into it this time, the voice he used on drunks who wouldn't leave the market stall. "Whatever game someone paid you to play, I'm the wrong audience. Out, before I call the watch."

They both knew he would not call the watch. Calling the watch meant explaining why a royal food-taster kept a hidden shelf of forbidden charms. She looked at him with those impossible green eyes and he had the sick certainty that she knew he would not call the watch, that she had counted on it, that she had chosen this door among all the doors in Ashfall because of precisely what was behind the loose board.

The rain drummed. The widow Hesk coughed. The girl did not move.

* * *

He tried reason first, because reason was cheap. He told her there was a fever in this quarter. He told her he had nothing worth stealing. He told her the palace would send men by morning — which was true more often than he liked — and that whatever she wanted, he couldn't give it. She listened to all of it with the grave attention of a judge hearing a plea she has already decided against, and when he finished she sat down on the floor beside his cold hearth, folded her hands in her lap, and became, impossibly, even more immovable.

He tried the door next. Opened it wide, gestured at the night with the courtesy of an innkeeper. She looked at the open door, looked at him, and did not go through it. When he took her shoulder to turn her toward it — gently, only gently, he was not a man who handled children roughly — the moment his skin touched hers, every charm on his hidden shelf *sang.*

There is no better word for it. He felt it through the wall, through the floorboards, through the soles of his bare feet: a rising hum, thin and silver, like wet fingers on glass rims, like the note a struck bowl holds after the ear stops believing in it. The charms he had bought and stolen and copied and sold for five years — dead things, mostly, trinkets with a trickle of borrowed power, the magical equivalent of banked coals — all of them woke at once. He felt them the way he felt his own teeth.

The girl's eyes widened too. Not in fear. In *recognition.*

Quentin snatched his hand back as if her shoulder had burned him. The hum faded slowly, reluctantly, like a crowd falling back to sleep. Neither of them moved for a long moment. Rain. Lamp. The smell of wet wool and woodsmoke and, beneath it, faintly, something green — the smell of a forest floor in the first warm week of thaw, rising off a child in a ruined dress in the middle of a city built on stone and ash.

"What are you?" he whispered.

She opened her mouth.

He braced himself for crying, for pleading, for the practiced whine of a beggar-child worked by some gang-master. What came out instead was three words in a language he had never heard and yet — and this was the part that would keep him awake for the rest of his life — *recognized*, the way you recognize a face in a crowd years after you've forgotten whose it was. The words were low and resonant and shaped strangely, each syllable carrying weight beyond its size, and they fell into the room like stones into still water. The lampflame leaned toward her. The hum in the wall rose once, briefly, and settled.

He did not know what she said. He knew, with absolute and terrible certainty, that it was true.

Three words. Then her mouth closed, and she looked at him — not pleading now, but *waiting*, the way one waits after handing someone a letter, having done all that a sender can do.

"I don't understand you," he said hoarsely. It was the first fully honest thing he'd said aloud in months.

She considered this. Then she raised one small hand and pointed — at him. At his chest, precisely, at the place behind which his heart was doing something arrhythmic and ashamed. Then she pointed at the loose board in the shelf. Then she pointed at herself, and out the window, northward, through stone and slate and rain, in the direction of the mountains no one in Ashfall thought about except when they complained about the wind coming off them.

Him. The charms. Herself. North.

"No," said Quentin Blackwood, food-taster to the crown, seller of illegal trifles, keeper of a life balanced so finely that a strong sneeze could end it. "Absolutely not. Whatever this is, whoever sent you — no. I taste soup for a living. I am nobody. Do you understand? Nobody."

Something flickered across her face then — the first expression she'd shown that wasn't patience. It might have been pity. That was somehow worse than anything else she could have looked at him with.

* * *

In the end — because he could not put her out into the rain, because the watch would ask questions he could not answer, because the hum of his charms had not entirely died and kept murmuring at the edge of his hearing like a room full of people whispering about him — he gave her the blanket off his pallet and half his bread, and told himself it was temporary. One night. Until the rain stopped. Until he could make inquiries, discreetly, about missing children, about gangs, about whoever had aimed her at his door like an arrow.

She ate the bread in small, precise bites, wasting nothing, and drank the water he gave her as if it were wine at a coronation. Then she lay down on the floor beside the hearth — refused the pallet with a shake of her head so final he didn't argue — pulled the blanket to her chin, and watched him with open green eyes until sleep took her between one blink and the next, instantly and completely, the way children and soldiers sleep.

Quentin sat at his table and did not sleep at all.

He turned the evening over, piece by piece, the way he turned a spiced pear over on his tongue, hunting the bitter note. The buyer in the night market: *green-eyed children near the walls, they say, folk seeing things.* He'd dismissed it. Rumor was the currency of the market and mostly counterfeit. But she had come from the walls, hadn't she? Road-dirt to the knee. A dress that had once belonged to someone rich, or at least someone finer than any road. And the words — those three resonant, impossible words that had made his lamp lean and his charms sing.

Because here was the thing he had not let himself think while she was awake, the thing he circled now in the dark like a moth against a pane:

He *had* heard the language before. Not whole. Never whole. Fragments — syllables surfacing out of deep water. His grandmother, dead thirty years, singing over her loom in the evenings, songs in no tongue anyone in the village could name, songs she claimed were just "old nonsense, old nonsense, querido," waving away questions with flour-white hands. He had thought, as a boy, that they were gibberish she'd invented to soothe herself. He had forgotten them so thoroughly that forgetting had seemed complete.

Until tonight. Until a soaked child spoke three words in his room and thirty years fell away and he was five years old again, watching his grandmother's hands move on the wool, hearing her hum sounds that made the fire sit up straighter in the grate.

Old nonsense.

The charms on his shelf hummed their low silver note, and Quentin understood, with the slow dread of a man reading his own sentence, that nothing in his carefully balanced life had ever been nonsense. Not the grandmother. Not the charms that came too easily to his hand, that answered him a half-beat faster than they answered other sellers. Not the strange immunity of his own tongue, which tasted truths other tasters missed. He had spent five years thinking himself a clever criminal, and it had taken a mute-eyed child three words to show him the ledger underneath: that he had inherited something, that the something had been hunted hard enough to be driven underground and ground down to lullabies, and that it had just walked through his door out of the rain.

On the floor by the hearth, the girl stirred. Her eyes opened — not gradually, but all at once, awake the way a blade is out of a sheath — and fixed on him across the dark room.

She raised one finger to her lips. *Quiet.*

Then she pointed, slowly and deliberately, at the door.

Quentin listened. Under the rain, under the widow's cough, far below at the mouth of the alley: the muffled clop of a horse walking where horses shouldn't be, at the hour when honest riders were asleep. Hooves on cobblestone, unhurried, stopping.

A lantern flared faintly through the shutter-gap, painting a moving stripe across the ceiling.

The girl did not look afraid. She looked at Quentin with those spring-sap eyes, and pointed north again, and waited — patient as arithmetic — for him to understand how little time he had left to decide.
