## 4. What the Charms Remember

The girl had not left by morning.

Quentin had half-hoped she would dissolve with the dark, the way nightmares did — that he would wake on his narrow cot to gray light and rain against the shutters and no green-eyed child sitting cross-legged on his floor, watching him sleep. But when he opened his eyes, she was there, exactly where she'd been when he finally gave up trying to make her go and lay down fully clothed at midnight: small hands folded in her lap, spine straight as a candle, eyes fixed on him with the patience of something far older than eight years.

"Good morning," he said, because it seemed absurd to say nothing.

She tilted her head. She did not answer. Of course she did not answer; she hadn't spoken since those three words last night, the three words that had made every charm in his hidden cache sing like struck glass.

He got up, washed his face in the basin, went through the motions of a man who was still an ordinary man. He set out bread and hard cheese and the last of the honey. The girl ate neatly, without greed, pausing between bites to study the room — the chipped crockery, the dried herbs hanging from the beam, the loose floorboard under which his life's crimes slept in a cedar box.

When her gaze lingered there a moment too long, Quentin's stomach dropped through the floor.

"No," he said, pointing at the board. "That's nothing. That's nothing at all."

Her eyes came back to his face. And she smiled — a small, apologetic smile, the smile of someone who has already seen exactly what is under the floorboard and is sorry to have made him uncomfortable about it.

Quentin sat down across from her and put his head briefly in his hands.

* * *

By midmorning he had run out of ordinary explanations.

A lost child would cry. A mute child would gesture, would draw pictures in spilled flour, would tug his sleeve toward doors and windows. This girl did none of it. She waited, watchful, as though *he* were the one failing to communicate — as though the fault lay in his ears, not her tongue.

So he did what he always did when the world refused to make sense. He went to work.

He cleared the table. He fetched the cedar box from beneath the floorboard and set it down between them, and watched her nostrils flare slightly, the way a hound scents game. Inside, wrapped in wool and waxed linen, lay his inventory: eleven charms, each one a small crime, each one a small masterpiece. A luck-knot for a fisherman's nets. A fever-bead of willow and silver thread. Three whisper-shells for lovers who couldn't afford letters. A warding coin stamped with a sigil so old even Quentin only half understood it — inherited stock, from his grandmother's box, the kind of thing you sold to a frightened merchant's wife because it looked impressive and mostly did nothing.

He laid them out in a row.

"Now," he said, keeping his voice level, the voice he used in the night market when a buyer got curious. "You're going to tell me who sent you. You're going to point, or write, or draw it in the honey if you have to. Because I've been careful for five years, girl. Five years. And last night, everything in this box started humming when you opened your mouth, and humming charms get their owners hanged."

The word *hanged* landed. He saw it land — a flicker in her expression, not fear exactly. Recognition. As if hanging were a thing she understood the way he understood rent: an old fact of the world.

Then she reached out and touched the warding coin.

The hum started low, in the floorboards, in his back teeth. The other charms woke one after another down the row like lamps being lit — the fever-bead first, then the luck-knot, then the whisper-shells shivering against each other with a sound like distant bells heard through water. Quentin had handled these objects daily for years. They had never once made a sound. Charms weren't supposed to make sounds. Charms were supposed to be quiet little lies with just enough truth in them to sell.

The girl spoke.

Three syllables this time, soft, almost apologetic — and the hum resolved into something else. Not noise. *Response.* The charms were answering her, the way a dog answers its name, and Quentin felt the hair rise along his arms because he could suddenly hear what they were saying, or rather he could feel the shape of meaning pressing against the inside of his skull like a face against a window.

*Asking,* he thought wildly. *She's asking them something. And they know her.*

His hands were shaking. He pressed them flat against the table.

"That's not possible," he said aloud. "That language is dead."

And then he stopped, because he heard himself, and understood what he'd just admitted.

* * *

He knew the language was dead the way everyone knew it — the way you know the shape of a wound your city carries. The Purging was three hundred years gone, but Ashmark still wore its scars. Every child learned it in schoolroom chants: the Thorn-mages who spoke to root and briar and bone, whose magic needed no charm, no circle, no price of silver — only words in a tongue older than the crown itself. The War of Briars. The burning of the groves. King Aldric the Cleanser and his nine years of ash. *Root them out, burn them deep, let no seed of the Old Tongue sleep.* There were still laws on the books about it. There were still names carved into execution stones in the temple quarter, worn nearly smooth, that Quentin had walked past ten thousand times without reading.

No one spoke the Old Tongue. No one *could*. The last speakers had died screaming before Quentin's grandmother's grandmother was born. Scholars kept fragments in sealed archives, the way you keep teeth in a jar.

Except.

Except his grandmother had sung to him.

Quentin stood very still in the middle of his own kitchen while the memory rose up out of whatever buried place he'd kept it, whole and sudden, smelling of woodsmoke and dried lavender. He was four, maybe five, sick with a winter fever, and she'd sat beside him rubbing his chest with goose fat and singing under her breath — not the hymns, not the lullabies every nurse in Ashmark sang. Something older, lower, with strange rounded vowels and a rhythm that didn't match any song he'd learned since. When he'd asked her once what it meant, she had stopped mid-verse, gone very quiet, and said: *It means nothing, little wren. It's just sounds my mother taught me. Sounds and sounds.*

But she had never sung it again when anyone else could hear.

*Little wren.*

Quentin looked at the girl. The girl looked back.

"Wren," he said slowly. "Is that — is that your name? Did someone tell you—"

She shook her head. Then, deliberately, she pointed at herself, and said a single word in that rolling, rooted language — and the word, whatever it meant, sounded unmistakably like *wren*, the bird, the small brown singer, the name his grandmother had given him over a fever bed thirty years ago.

The floor seemed to tilt. He gripped the chair.

"You can't know that," he whispered. "There's no way you can know that."

But of course there was a way. There was exactly one way, and it was standing in front of him wearing a patched dress and an expression of infinite patience: she was speaking the language itself, and somewhere in its buried architecture, the word for a small brown songbird and the word for a small brown boy had always been neighbors. His grandmother hadn't given him a nickname. She'd given him a translation.

Every fragment she'd ever sung him — the nonsense syllables, the "sounds and sounds" — was the Old Tongue, worn smooth by generations of women who no longer knew what they were carrying. His family had been a jar of buried seeds for three hundred years, passed hand to hand, sung to feverish children in the dark, and no one had known. No one had *known.*

And now the seed had walked up to his door with green eyes and knocked.

* * *

He tested her all afternoon. He couldn't help himself; testing things was the trade he'd been born to, the taster's instinct — take a little, wait, watch what it does to you.

He said the syllables back to her, clumsily, mangling them, and watched her wince and correct him with gentle gestures — lips pursed here, throat open there — until one word came out of him shaped properly, and the nearest charm, the fever-bead, glowed faintly warm for the space of a heartbeat. Just from a word. No circle drawn, no blood, no price. The thing that had gotten a civilization exterminated, and it worked like breathing.

He wrote out the fragments of his grandmother's lullaby from memory, phonetic, ugly, on the back of a market list, and sang them to her haltingly. The girl went absolutely still. Her eyes filled — not spilling over, just filling, bright and shocked, the way eyes fill when a stranger says the name of your dead mother — and she reached across the table and took his hand, both of hers around his one, and squeezed.

They sat like that for a long moment, a food-taster and a foundling, holding hands over a scrap of paper covered in a dead language, while outside the window Ashmark went about its morning: carts rattling, fishmongers crying, the palace bells ringing the third hour over a city built on ashes it preferred not to think about.

"How," he said finally, uselessly. "How are you here? Where did you come from? Who—"

She released his hand and answered with the only eloquence she had: she pointed at herself, then northward, out beyond the walls, beyond the fields, toward the hills where the maps went blank and the ink just said *old forest.* Then she pointed at the floor, deep, deeper, as though the direction she'd come from was down as much as north. Then she pressed her palm flat against her own chest, and her face did something complicated — grief and urgency braided together — and she spoke six or seven words in a rush, low and urgent, and this time the charms didn't hum. This time they *shook*, rattling against the tabletop like teeth, and the temperature in the little room dropped, and Quentin felt a pressure behind his eyes like a storm building on a horizon he couldn't see.

He didn't understand a single word.

He understood, with absolute clarity, that it was a warning.

"All right," he said. "All right. Slowly. We'll — we'll do this slowly."

* * *

Night came, and with it the question he'd been circling all day like a man pacing the edge of a well.

He should take her to the temple orphanage. That was the sane move, the safe move, the move that kept his neck out of rope. Hand her to the sisters, walk away, tell himself the humming he'd heard was nerves, that the glow was a trick of candlelight, that his grandmother's songs were only songs. By week's end the whole impossible episode would be filed away with the rest of the small cowardices that made up a careful life.

Instead he found himself laying a second plate on the table, and a blanket over the cot, and — after a long internal argument he lost — moving the cedar box to the space behind the loose brick by the hearth, which was harder to find and which he'd sworn he'd never need.

The girl watched him do it. When he turned around, she was smiling again, that same small apologetic smile, and she patted the cot twice, decisively, the way you invite someone to sit.

"It's my cot," he muttered. "I'm letting you have it. That's different from you taking it."

She patted it again, gracious in victory.

He blew out the lamp and lay down on the floor in his coat, staring at the ceiling beams, listening to the small sounds of her breathing, and tried to assemble the day into something that made sense. A dead language living in a child. His grandmother's buried inheritance surfacing after thirty years. Charms answering her like old dogs hearing a whistle they'd forgotten existed. And underneath all of it, threaded through everything, that bitter note he'd caught in the king's spiced pear two days ago — the taste that wouldn't leave him, that sat on the back of his tongue even now like a question he'd been avoiding asking.

He had assumed it was a new spice. A court intrigue, maybe — slow poison, palace politics, the ordinary rot of ambitious men. He'd told himself he'd keep his head down and his mouth shut, the way he always had.

But the girl had come from the north, from below, from somewhere old. And the bitterness in the pear had tasted — he was only admitting this now, in the dark, alone with it — the bitterness had tasted *green*. Rooty. Like sap, like bark, like the underside of a stone in a forest.

Like something that belonged to the same world as the words she spoke.

The girl stirred on the cot. In her sleep, she murmured — a thread of that ancient language, soft and looping, a child dreaming in a tongue three hundred years dead — and in the brick behind the hearth, muffled and patient, eleven forbidden charms began, very quietly, to sing along.

Quentin lay in the dark and listened to them, and understood that his careful life had ended two nights ago at his own door, and that whatever came next had already chosen him.

In the morning, he decided, he would go back to the palace. He would taste the king's breakfast, and this time he would not pretend not to know what he was tasting.

The girl murmured again in her sleep. One of the words, he was almost certain, was the one that meant *hurry.*
