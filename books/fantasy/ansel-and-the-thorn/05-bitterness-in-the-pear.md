## 5. Bitterness in the Pear

The pear arrived at the king's table the way all treachery arrives: beautifully.

It sat on a plate of white Vellorine porcelain, glazed and spiced, its skin lacquered with honey until it shone like something pulled from a saint's reliquary. The court chef, Master Odo, had outdone himself — a winter pear poached in mulled wine, split and refilled with almond cream, dusted with crushed candied violets. Around it, the breakfast table glittered: smoked river trout, warm bread, a compote of quince, small cups of black tea from the southern trade roads. King Aldric sat at the head of it all in a morning robe of dove-grey wool, his hands wrapped around his cup as though the warmth were the only honest thing left in the room.

Quentin stood at his shoulder, as he had stood every morning for five years, and waited for the silver fork to be pressed into his hand.

"Blackwood." The king's voice was rougher than it had been a month ago. Rougher than it had been a week ago. "You look tired."

"I sleep badly, Majesty."

"You and half my court." Aldric smiled, but it was a thin thing, stretched over something weary. He was fifty-one years old and had been hale as an ox until the autumn; now there was a greyish cast beneath his skin that no amount of royal lighting could disguise, and his fingers trembled faintly against the porcelain. The physicians called it a wasting humor. They bled him twice a week and prescribed rest and bitter tonics, and the king grew thinner by the day while the kingdom's factions circled like gulls over a dying whale.

Quentin took up the fork and cut a sliver from the pear.

He had learned long ago that tasting was not one act but three. First the tongue — the tongue was the least of it, the numb tongue, the tongue that had been burned dull by five years of swallowing things meant to kill. Second the nose, which caught what the tongue missed: almonds, bitter bark, the copper-sweet ghost of certain roots. Third — and this was the part no master of tasters had ever taught him, because no legitimate taster would ever admit to possessing it — the charms. The little woven things sewn into his left cuff, humming their low, illegal hum against his wrist, reading the food the way a blind man reads a face.

He put the sliver of pear in his mouth.

Honey. Mulled wine. Almond cream, sweet and fine. Candied violet, a whisper of perfume.

And underneath it, so faint that a lesser tongue would have called it nothing at all — bitterness. Not the bitterness of the wine or the violet. A green bitterness. A bitterness like sap, like crushed stems, like the inside of a living branch.

Like the lullaby his grandmother used to sing, rendered in flavor.

Quentin chewed. Swallowed. Kept his face smooth as pond ice, because a taster's face was the last wall between a king and a knife, and because his own pulse had just gone loud enough to hear.

*That note.* He knew it. He had tasted it four days ago in a dish of spiced pears — the same dish, he realized now, the same preparation, Odo's winter pear — and he had told himself it was the rind, the season, the mulled wine gone slightly off. He had told himself a great many comfortable things in the past four days, and every one of them had been a lie he'd paid for with a night of staring at his ceiling while Wren slept on his floor pallet, her green eyes closed, her small hands curled like ferns before they unfurl.

He cut a second sliver. Tasted again. The bitterness was there, patient as a spider, sitting just beneath the honey where only someone looking for it would ever find it.

"Majesty," Quentin said, and set down the fork. "I'd counsel against the pear today. The wine in the poaching has turned."

Odo made a sound of wounded protest from the sideboard. "Turned? I'll have you know—"

"The mulled wine," Quentin said pleasantly, without looking at him. "Perhaps a bad cask. It happens even in your kitchens, Master Odo."

"It does not happen in my kitchens."

"Then perhaps it happened in the merchant's cellar before it reached yours." Quentin turned and gave the chef the flat, mild smile of a man who had spent five years being blamed for things. "I taste what I taste. Would you prefer I lie about it?"

The king waved a hand, tired. "Leave the pear. Bring the trout." And then, quieter, as Odo swept the plate away with the expression of a man planning a vendetta: "Blackwood. You hesitated."

"A heartbeat, Majesty."

"You never hesitate." Aldric's eyes came up, and for a moment the grey sickness in his face seemed to recede behind something sharper — the old king, the one who had held a border for twenty years. "Five years you've eaten poison for me and never once flinched. Today you flinched. Why?"

Because the poison is the same green bitterness that lives in a child who speaks a language dead two hundred years, Quentin did not say. Because I think someone in this palace is feeding you something older than poison, and I do not yet know if it is meant to kill you or *change* you. Because yesterday I found a charm in my own collection humming a note I have never heard it hum, tuned toward the palace like a dog pointing at a door.

"Because I value my reputation," he said. "A taster who misses a turned cask is a taster looking for new work."

The king studied him a moment longer, then let it go, which was its own small mercy. Quentin served the trout, the bread, the quince. Nothing else carried the note. He drank the tea first, as always, and felt the familiar nothing — his body had stopped registering most toxins years ago, a slow education written into his blood one swallowed death at a time. Arsenic tasted like breakfast now. Nightshade like a headache that never quite arrived.

But the green bitterness — the green bitterness he could still feel, faintly, the way a man who has gone deaf can still feel thunder in his chest. Which meant it wasn't working on the body the way poisons worked on the body at all.

Which meant it was working on something else.

* * *

He should have gone straight back to his lodgings after the morning levee. Instead he went to the cellar records.

Every dish served to the king was logged: source, date, preparer. Quentin had learned the logging system in his first year, ostensibly to do his job better, actually because a taster who understood supply chains lived longer than one who didn't. Poison rarely walked through the front gate wearing a skull mask. Poison arrived in a shipment of almonds from a farm whose owner owed someone money. Poison arrived in a new spice merchant's third delivery, when everyone had stopped checking.

He stood in the cold archive beneath the steward's wing, lamplight swinging as he flipped pages, and traced the pear backward through the ledger.

Winter pears: orchard stock, the royal demesne at Callowmere, unremarkable. Mulled wine: cellared claret, three years old, unremarkable. Almond cream: ground fresh daily, unremarkable. Candied violets—

Candied violets: delivered fortnightly by Hessel & Sons, confectioners, Copperlane. New supplier as of eight weeks ago. Previous supplier: Marrow's Sweetings, dissolved — the proprietor deceased, autumn fever.

Eight weeks. The king's decline had begun nine weeks ago.

Quentin stood very still in the cold and let the arithmetic finish itself. Then he pulled the delivery ledgers for the violets themselves, and found the receiving signature, and read the name twice to be sure of it.

*A. Vane, Lord Chamberlain's office.*

Well. That proved nothing. The Chamberlain's office signed for half the deliveries in the palace; it was a bureaucratic formality, a stamp, a clerk's pen doing a clerk's work. Any of six under-secretaries might have—

Except that Quentin had spent five years learning to notice the note beneath the honey, and there was a note beneath this too. The signature was correct. The hand was correct. But the ink was fresher than the entries around it — touched up, gone over, the way a forger goes over a line to give it age's confidence. And the entry above it, the one recording the *previous* violet delivery under old Marrow's contract, had been scraped. Parchment doesn't forget. Hold it to the light and the ghost of the old writing rose up like a drowned man.

He held it to the light.

Under the scrape, in the old hand: *received and inspected, R. Marrow, 14th of Frostmonth.* And beside it, in a margin scrawl he recognized because he'd seen it on a hundred routine documents — the Lord Chamberlain's own secretary, a soft-spoken man named Pell — *sample retained per L.C. instruction.*

Sample retained. Per the Lord Chamberlain's instruction. Eight weeks ago, the week before the king began to fade.

Quentin set the ledger down with hands that were almost steady. Almost. His heart was going like a fist on a door.

Think. Think like a taster, not like a frightened man. There were innocent explanations. There were always innocent explanations; that was the architecture of a good poisoning, layers of innocence stacked like plates. Perhaps Vane had retained a sample for quality control. Perhaps the scrape was water damage. Perhaps the bitter note in the pear was the rind, the season, the wine.

And perhaps a mute child had not appeared at his door speaking the language of a magic the crown had burned out of the world two hundred years ago.

He thought of Wren this morning, standing at his window with her palms flat against the glass, watching the palace across the city — not the way a child watches a castle, with wonder. The way a bird watches a snare. She had looked at the palace and then she had looked at him, and she had said nothing, because she could say nothing, and somehow the nothing had been the loudest warning he had ever received.

*The Thorn,* she had drawn for him last night, in the ash of the hearth. A tree, or the memory of one. Roots going down and down. And beneath the roots, a shape like a heart, and beneath the heart, a shape like a mouth.

He hadn't understood it. He was beginning to.

* * *

The trouble with knowing was that knowing changed how a room felt.

Quentin had walked the palace corridors ten thousand times and they had always been simply corridors — marble and tapestry and the smell of beeswax, servants flowing around him like water around a stone. Now every corridor was a throat. Every courteous face was a question he couldn't afford to answer wrong. When the under-steward nodded to him, Quentin heard *does he know*. When two guards paused their conversation as he passed, he heard *him, the taster, watch him*. Paranoia, he told himself. You're a man who sells illegal charms out of a night market stall; paranoia is occupational. But paranoia had kept him alive five years, and he trusted it the way sailors trust weather.

He was crossing the Fountain Court at midday when the feeling sharpened from weather into fact.

Three men stood near the colonnade. Not guards — guards wore the king's blue and gold. These wore the undyed grey of the Chamberlain's household retinue, and they were not talking. They were watching the doors of the steward's wing. Watching, specifically, the archive entrance Quentin had come out of forty minutes earlier.

One of them saw him looking. He didn't look away, which was the whole message. He held Quentin's gaze for a count of five, pleasant as a shopkeeper, and then the three of them moved off together toward the east range.

Where the Lord Chamberlain's offices were.

Quentin finished crossing the courtyard at an unhurried walk, because running was confession, and climbed to the gallery above the great hall where the afternoon petitioners would soon be queuing. From there he could see the flow of the palace the way he'd taught himself to see it years ago — not as rooms but as currents. And the current was wrong today. Grey-liveried men where there were usually none. A clerk from the Chamberlain's office taking notes outside the kitchens, notes about what, about whom. Two of the king's personal guard reassigned — he overheard the rotation change — replaced by men whose commissions he didn't recognize, which meant commissions signed somewhere other than the king's hand.

The king was ill. The king was guarded by strangers. The king's food carried a bitterness that predated kingdoms, and the man who administered the king's household — the man who controlled every plate, every cup, every physician's tonic that crossed the royal threshold — had begun quietly repositioning men around the sickroom like a player moving pieces toward a cornered king.

Not killing him. That was the thing that turned Quentin's stomach, turning it over and over as he stood in the gallery noise. If Vane wanted Aldric dead, there were a dozen clean ways and the man was competent enough to use one. This was slower. This was a wasting illness, a fading king, a regency by increments — and a faction that needed the king pliable, or absent, or both, and needed it done in a way that no blade could be traced to.

Or — and here the thought arrived with the cold patience of the bitterness itself — needed the king *fed*. Fed slowly, steadily, tithe by tithe, to something that liked its meals the way the Thorn had liked them. Old stories said the Thorn-magic had never taken life outright. It had taken *surrender*. It had fed on wills bent green, on minds softened like fruit in honey, until the person remained and the person was no longer theirs.

The king gripped his cup every morning like it was the only honest thing left in the room.

*Oh,* Quentin thought, standing in the gallery with the petitioners' murmur rising below him like the sound of a distant sea. *Oh, you poor stubborn old man. Something is drinking you.*

* * *

He made it out of the palace by late afternoon, through the west postern, past the checkpoint where the guard checked his taster's seal and waved him through — *him, the taster, watch him*, or maybe just wind in the archway, impossible now to separate the signal from the fear. The city took him in the way it always did, smoke and bells and the smell of roasting chestnuts from the Cornermarket, ordinary life grinding on above the machinery of treason.

His lodgings were three streets into the Tanner's Quarter, up two flights, behind a door he locked with a charm disguised as a cheap iron key. He climbed the stairs fast, unlocked, slipped inside —

Wren was standing in the middle of the room, facing the door, and she had clearly been standing there a while. Waiting. On the floor at her feet lay his charm-roll, unrolled, every woven piece laid out in a careful line — and every single one of them was glowing.

Not brightly. Not the showy gold of market-trick light. A low green luminescence, sap-colored, pulsing gently, all of them together, in rhythm. In time with each other. In time, when he went still and listened with the skin instead of the ears, with something far beneath the floor, beneath the street, beneath the whole quarter — a slow vast pulse like a second heart under the world.

And in the middle of the line, the oldest charm he owned — his grandmother's knot, the one he'd never sold, the one that had hummed the first night Wren spoke — was glowing brightest of all. And it was pointed. Not physically. But he could feel it the way you feel someone looking at the back of your neck. Pointed south and east, across the city, toward the palace rock.

Toward whatever lay beneath it.

Wren looked up at him. Her eyes were spring-sap green and wide and afraid, and she raised one small hand, and pointed — not at the charms, not at the window, not at the palace.

At him. At his mouth. At his tongue.

Then she pressed her palm flat against her own chest, and drew, in the air, with one finger, the shape he had seen in the hearth-ash: roots, going down. A heart. A mouth.

And she shook her head, slowly, and pointed at the king's palace again, and this time she made a sound — the first sound he had ever heard her make besides breath — a thin, cracked, wordless keen, like a hinge that had rusted shut trying to open.

They know, he thought. Or they suspect. Either way, the taster has tasted something he shouldn't have, and the man who signs for violets has grey men in the courtyards, and there is a child in my room who is the last speaker of a dead tongue and every charm I own is singing to something under the palace like a dog answering a whistle.

Outside, far off across the rooftops, the evening bell began to ring. Underneath it — so faint that later he would spend hours convincing himself he'd imagined it — Quentin heard the pulse answer. One slow beat, deep beneath the city, patient as a spider.

Someone was dosing the king.

And now, he understood, with a cold settling certainty that felt exactly like the bitterness under the honey — someone had begun to wonder about the taster.
