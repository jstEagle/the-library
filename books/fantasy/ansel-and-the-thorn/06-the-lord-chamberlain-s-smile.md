## 6. The Lord Chamberlain's Smile

Aldous Vane had learned long ago that the secret to power was not the taking of it but the tending of it, the way a gardener tends a hedge: patiently, daily, with shears kept sharp and out of sight.

He rose before the dawn bells, as he always did, and dressed in the dark while the palace slept around him like a sick animal. His chamberlain's keys hung at his belt — forty-one of them, each oiled monthly, each opening a door somewhere in this sprawling stone body of the kingdom. He knew every lock. He knew which doors groaned and which swung silent. He knew, better than any man alive, that a palace was nothing but a throat, and whoever held the keys decided what went down it.

Including, these past three months, the king.

Vane took his morning meal alone in his office above the treasury stair, a boiled egg, brown bread, watered wine. He ate sparingly. A man who controlled his appetites could not be controlled by them. On the desk before him lay the day's first correspondence — inventories, petitions, a report from the eastern granaries that he read twice without expression — and beneath those papers, wrapped in oilcloth no larger than a prayer book, sat the vial.

It held perhaps six measures of liquid so pale it might have been water, if water carried a faint green shimmer when tilted toward candlelight, like the sheen on a beetle's back. It had cost the faction a fortune and two lives to acquire, and Vane handled it the way he handled everything: precisely, without sentiment.

Three drops in the king's evening wine. Not enough to kill. Never enough to kill. Enough to make a strong man tired, then forgetful, then vague, then — in another month or two, if the physicians' tonics continued to fail as they were meant to fail — gentle. Suggestible. A king who asked his council what they thought, and meant it.

The realm needed a regent's hand whether it admitted so or not. The king had sired no heir; the queen had died a decade ago of childbed fever along with the child; and the factions had spent ten years circling one another like dogs deciding which throat to open first. Vane's masters — the old marshal, the Lord Treasurer, the Archbishop who was neither pious nor old but understood the value of appearing both — had chosen patience over the knife. Poison that looked like illness. Illness that looked like grief. Grief that excused withdrawal from rule. And when the moment ripened, a regency council with Aldous Vane's fingerprints on every document and his men in every corridor.

He did not think of himself as a poisoner. He thought of himself as an administrator. History, he had observed, was written by whichever side kept its ledgers cleanest.

At the seventh bell he made his rounds, as he always did, beginning with the kitchens.

The kitchen was the true heart of any palace, whatever the priests said about chapels. Two hundred souls worked the ovens and spits of Blackmere's undercrofts, and Vane walked among them nodding, remembering names, asking after a scullery boy's fevered mother, complimenting a pastry cook on her honeyed quince. They loved him for it. Or feared him. In Vane's experience the two were indistinguishable at close range, and either would serve.

The king's breakfast was being finished on the far table — spiced pears in wine syrup, soft cheese, a custard flavored with meadowsweet, small loaves still steaming. The royal food-taster stood at his station by the door, as he always did: Blackwood, the quiet one, five years in service, never late, never ill, never memorable. Vane watched him work with the mild attention of a man checking a clock he already trusted.

Blackwood tasted the pears. Something crossed his face — a flicker, gone almost before it arrived, like a fish turning under ice.

Vane filed it away. He filed everything away. That was the whole of his genius, if he had one: other men threw things out, and he kept them.

* * *

The tithe came to him at midday through the usual channel — a sealed letter left inside the confessional of the palace chapel, unsigned, written in the Treasurer's cramped accountant's hand. *Increase the measure. His resistance grows. The work beneath must be fed.*

Vane read it twice, then fed it to the candle flame and watched it curl into black lace.

*His resistance grows.* Of course it grew. Any body fed a slow poison learned to bear it; that was simple physic, the same principle by which a physician built tolerance to a fever draught. But the letter's phrasing troubled him more than its instruction. *The work beneath.* He had served the faction nine years and had never once been told plainly what the tithe was *for*. Poison a king into pliancy — that he understood. Kings were livestock; you fed them, you bred them or didn't, you culled them when their meat turned sour. But the instructions had always come wrapped in language Vane found distasteful in its vagueness. *Feed the root. Keep the debt current. The old creditor wakes hungry.*

Superstition, he'd assumed. Old men's theater, the kind of incense-and-cipher nonsense the Treasurer used to feel important. The drops themselves were merely a drug — he'd had it analyzed, discreetly, by an apothecary who now resided at the bottom of a well — and drugs worked on blood, not on *creditors*.

But lately there were things he could not file away so neatly.

The tremors, for one. Three in the past month, small ones, rattling the glass in the west windows at hours when no quarry blasted and no army drilled. The engineers blamed the river. The river, Vane noted, ran nowhere near the west wing.

The blight, for another. The kitchen gardens had begun producing vegetables that rotted from the inside out, sound-skinned and hollow-hearted, and the royal orchard's oldest pear tree — planted, the gardeners claimed, before the kingdom itself — had flowered black this spring. Black blossoms, like frost given form. The gardeners burned the branches and whispered about it for a week.

And the dreams. Vane did not dream, as a rule. He slept four hours, deeply, on schedule. But twice this month he had woken before his own alarm with the sensation of something vast turning over beneath him — not hearing it, exactly, but feeling it the way one feels a ship's engines through the deck plates, a vibration older and larger than oneself. Both times he had lain awake until dawn doing arithmetic in his head, because arithmetic kept out everything else.

Now there was the matter of the child.

The reports had begun arriving a fortnight ago, and at first he had dismissed them as he dismissed most things: market gossip, sailors' tales, the natural folklore of a city that had too many mouths and not enough bread. Green-eyed children seen near the walls at dusk. A beggar girl on Tanner's Row whose eyes glowed like foxfire. Children speaking in tongues. The city watch logged such nonsense weekly and buried it in the same drawer as sightings of ghosts and mermaids.

But the reports had not stopped. They had *converged*. Seven separate sightings in twelve days, all within a half-mile radius, all describing a single child — small, eight years or so, ragged, silent, eyes green as new leaves. And one report, from a gate sergeant with no imagination whatsoever, contained a detail that had lifted the hair on Vane's forearms: the child had stood at the locked postern gate and spoken to it, and the gate had opened.

Old locks do not open for children. Vane had personally inspected the mechanism. It required a key, leverage, and a grown man's weight.

He had ordered the sergeant flogged for drunkenness, because a Chamberlain could not be seen crediting magic, and then he had doubled the watch on the walls and set three of his private men to finding the child quietly, before the story spread to ears less disciplined than his own.

Because here was the thing Vane understood that his superstitious masters perhaps did not: stories were load-bearing. A kingdom was a wall of them, mortared with habit, and you could pull out a great many stones before anyone noticed — but a green-eyed child who opened locked gates with her voice was not a stone. She was a crowbar.

The Purging had happened two hundred years ago for exactly this reason. Every schoolchild learned the catechism: the Thorn-mages had trafficked with the world's roots, had burned the old war across three continents, and the first king had cut the gift out of humanity like a surgeon cutting out rot. Whatever survived of it survived in gutter rumor and grandmother's lullabies, harmless as moths. Harmless — unless it wasn't. Unless somewhere in the dark between the walls, the old fire had banked itself instead of dying, and was waiting for a mouth to speak it awake.

Vane did not believe in magic. He believed in *leverage*, and a living relic of the Thorn was leverage beyond any treasurer's dreaming — or a threat beyond any hangman's rope. Either way, it could not be allowed to wander loose while he was mid-course in the delicate business of unseating a king.

He summoned his three hunters to the map room at dusk and gave them their instructions in a voice like cool water: find the child, follow the child, and above all learn who sheltered her. Do not take her. Not yet. A child alone was a stray; a child with a protector was a thread, and threads pulled.

"And if she speaks to you," Vane added, as they turned to go, "do not answer."

They smiled at that, thinking it a joke. Vane did not smile. He had read the confiscated archives once, early in his career, alone at night in the records vault — the interrogation transcripts from the Purging, the accounts of what Thorn-speakers' voices did to doors and dogs and men's hearts. He had told himself afterward that the transcripts were exaggerations, the hysteria of a frightened age.

He had also, that same night, checked the locks on his own chambers twice.

* * *

Evening brought him where it always brought him: the king's apartments, with the evening tray and the decanter.

King Edric IV sat by the window in a dressing gown, watching the sun go down over the city he no longer governed. He had been a strong man once — broad, loud, famous for wrestling his guardsmen in the yard and laughing while he lost. Now his hands lay in his lap like borrowed gloves, and his eyes had the soft unfocused quality of a portrait of himself.

"Chamberlain," the king said, with the ghost of warmth. "You keep good hours, Aldous. Better than my physicians. Better than my council." A pause, and then, mildly, terribly: "Better than me, I think."

"The realm keeps your hours, Majesty. You need only rest."

"I rest." The king looked at his hands. "I rest and rest. There is a taste in the wine lately, do you know it? A bitterness, like pear skin, like the white part of the peel. My father used to say a wise man tastes everything twice." He looked up, and for one clear moment the fog lifted and Vane saw the wrestler, the king, the man — saw him *see*. "Do you taste everything twice, Aldous?"

Vane poured the wine with a steady wrist. Three drops had gone in upstairs, in the antechamber, from the vial against his heart. "I taste everything once, Majesty. Thoroughly. It is my trade."

"Yes," the king said softly. "Yes, I believe it is." And the fog rolled back in, and he drank.

Vane stood by while the king drank, as protocol demanded, though no taster's duty bound a Chamberlain to witness. He watched the throat move. He calculated, as he did every evening, the remaining weeks of useful lucidity — six, perhaps seven, if the Treasurer's escalation held to schedule — and felt nothing whatsoever about it, the way a steward feels nothing about the calendar.

Then, as he gathered the tray, the king spoke again, and the words went through Vane like a nail through wet wood.

"*Scead thornes wacath under stanum,*" the king murmured, to the window, to the darkening garden, to no one. "*Scead thornes wacath.*"

Vane froze with the tray in his hands.

He knew the shape of the words if not their meaning — he had heard that cadence once, two hundred years dead, in a vault transcript. The shadow-tongue. The pre-kingdom speech. The language the crown had burned out of the world so thoroughly that even its name was contraband.

"Majesty?" he said carefully. "What was that?"

The king blinked at him, mild and vacant again, a tired old man in a dressing gown. "A nursery rhyme," he said. "My nurse sang it. I never knew what it meant." He smiled, sweet and slack. "Isn't it strange, the things we carry?"

Vane bowed and withdrew, walking at a measured pace down the corridor, past the guards, past the tapestries, until he reached the privacy of the stairwell — where he stopped, set his back against cold stone, and permitted himself, for the duration of eleven breaths, to be afraid.

Then he mastered it, as he mastered everything, and began to think.

The king knew fragments. Nurses' rhymes, carried down like heirlooms nobody could appraise. Harmless — probably. But the child on the walls spoke the tongue *whole*, and the ground beneath the palace shivered, and the Treasurer's letters spoke of debts and creditors as though the kingdom owed rent to something in the cellar.

Threads. Everywhere threads now, and Vane pulled at them in the dark of his own mind: the bitter taste the taster Blackwood had flinched from at breakfast — *did the man know something? Did he suspect the wine?* — and the green-eyed child moving through the city like a spark along a fuse, and the black-blossomed pear tree, and the tremors coming up through the foundations like a heartbeat gathering pace.

Somewhere below his feet, in the deep dark under the oldest stones, something waited. Somewhere in the sprawl of the city, a child was walking toward it — or away from it, or toward whoever would help her reach it. And somewhere in the palace, a quiet little food-taster had noticed a bitterness in the pears that only a guilty man should have been able to taste.

Vane straightened his cuffs, climbed the stairs, and returned to his office, where he lit a fresh candle, drew a clean sheet of paper, and wrote three orders in his small exact hand.

The first increased the dose.

The second put a watcher on Quentin Blackwood — day and night, patient, thorough, a thread to be followed home.

The third concerned the child, and it was only one line long:

*Find where she sleeps.*

He sealed all three, rang for his courier, and sat for a while after the man had gone, listening to the palace settle around him — the groan of old beams, the tick of cooling stone, and beneath it all, faint as a pulse through a floorboard, something else.

Something turning over in its sleep.

Vane smiled at the candle, the way he smiled at everyone, and reached for the next day's correspondence.
