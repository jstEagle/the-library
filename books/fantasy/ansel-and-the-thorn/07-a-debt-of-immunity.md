## 7. A Debt of Immunity

The poison came for him on a Tuesday, in a cup of elderflower wine, and Quentin Blackwood barely noticed it at all.

That was the trouble. That was the whole rotten trouble.

He stood at the king's elbow in the morning chamber, the silver tasting spoon still warm from his mouth, and waited for the world to tilt. It didn't. A faint bitterness ghosted across his tongue — that same green, rooty note he'd found in the spiced pears three days ago — and then it was gone, swallowed by honey and cold well water, leaving nothing behind but a numbness so old he'd stopped thinking of it as an affliction and started calling it a talent.

Five years. Five years of swallowing death in measured spoonfuls until his blood learned its alphabet. The physicians called it constitution. The charm-sellers in the night market, before they'd stopped selling to him out of fear, had called it something less polite: *a man pickled in poison*. Whatever it was, it had saved his life this morning, and it had done it so quietly that no one in the gilded room had any idea a murder had just been attempted.

"Is it good today?" King Aldric asked. His voice had gone thin over the winter, papery as old vellum. He looked smaller in his nightrobe than he did in state robes, his hands spotted, his eyes watery — but then, everyone looked smaller at breakfast.

"Excellent, Majesty," Quentin said. "The elderflower is from the south terraces."

It was not excellent. It was dosed. And the king drank three cups of it while Quentin stood with his hands folded and his heart going like a trapped bird against his ribs, smiling the small, forgettable smile of a man whose only job is to be unremarkable.

*Someone inside the palace,* he thought, watching the wine disappear down the royal throat. *Someone with access to the cellars, the kitchens, the cup. Someone patient.*

Patience was the part that frightened him most. This wasn't rage or ambition striking once and hoping. This was a slow tide, rising a finger's width a day, designed to look like age, like illness, like the natural dimming of a lamp. Whoever poured it wanted no corpse, no scandal, no succession crisis. They wanted a king who faded gently into a regency — and a regent they could hold by the collar.

Quentin bowed, withdrew, and walked back through the corridors with his face arranged into pleasant blankness while every step screamed *run*.

* * *

His rooms above the chandler's shop on Candlemaker's Row were exactly as he'd left them: narrow, tidy, smelling of tallow and dried lavender. Wren sat cross-legged on his bed in the last of the afternoon light, sorting through his stock of charms with the grave concentration of a jeweler appraising stones.

She'd laid them out in rows on the coverlet. The luck-knots. The fever-charms braided from red thread. The little wax seals that kept milk sweet and nightmares shallow. She held one up — a knotted hazel twig he sold to sailors for two coppers — and turned it toward him, and her green eyes asked the question her voice never would.

"It's nothing," Quentin said, hanging up his court cloak. "A parlor trick. Knots and words and a wish. Real magic—" he touched his own tongue, where the numbness lived like a second tenant "—real magic costs more than two coppers."

Wren set the twig down carefully, in line with the others, and looked at him with an expression he was beginning to dread, because it was the expression of someone who understood more than she said. Which, given that she said nothing, meant she understood everything.

Three days she'd been here. Three days since she'd appeared at his door at dusk, silent, sap-eyed, speaking those three words in a language that had made every charm in his hidden box hum like struck glass. Three days of feeding her bread and honey, of watching her sleep curled tight as a fern, of lying awake cataloguing everything he didn't know.

What he knew was this: her tongue was the Old Tongue. The Thorn-tongue. The language the crown had burned out of the world four generations ago, so thoroughly that scholars argued about whether it had ever existed at all. He knew because his grandmother had sung him fragments of it over the wash-tub — nonsense syllables, she'd claimed, songs her own grandmother left her — and when Wren spoke, those syllables snapped into place like bones finding their joints.

And he knew the bitter note in the pear, and now in the wine, was the same note that clung to Wren's skin. Thorn-derived. Old magic rendered into poison. Which meant someone in the palace had a source, a supply, a purpose — and the last speaker of the language that source came from was sleeping in his bed eating his bread.

"You can't stay," he told her, and heard how useless his voice sounded, how much it wanted to be overruled.

Wren tilted her head. Then, deliberately, she picked up the hazel twig, closed her fist around it, and pressed her other hand flat against her chest. Her eyes didn't leave his.

"I know what you're saying." He sat down heavily on the stool by the window. "You're saying I'm the same as you. Small magic, hidden magic. A knot and a word and a wish." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "Girl, if they find what I am, they hang me from the Saltgate with my charms around my neck so the crowd can spit at them. If they find what *you* are—"

He stopped. He didn't know what they'd do to her. That was worse.

Outside, the bells of Vespermark rang the fourth hour. Somewhere below, the chandler's cart rattled home. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a city that hung mages on market days and called it justice and went to supper afterward.

Wren climbed off the bed, gathered the charms back into their box with careful fingers, and pushed it across the coverlet toward him. Then she pointed at the box, and at herself, and shook her head slowly.

"No," Quentin said. "They're not yours either. They're mine, and they're evidence, and we're both—"

A knock at the street door. Two floors down.

Quentin went absolutely still. Wren's head came up like a deer's.

Nobody knocked at the chandler's door for him. He had no friends who visited; friendship was a liability in his trade. His charm-buyers met him in the night market, in crowds, in motion. The landlady took his coin on the first of the month through a slot in the door and had never once, in five years, climbed the stairs.

The knock came again. Patient. Unhurried. The knock of someone being paid by the hour.

He crossed to the window without a sound. The street below lay in long amber shadow. A man stood by the chandler's door — a broad, comfortable-looking man in a porter's coat, hands clasped behind his back, studying the doorframe with the mild interest of someone confirming an address. As Quentin watched, the man raised his hand and rapped again, and this time called out, pleasantly, in a voice pitched to carry:

"Master Blackwood? Message from the palace. Master Blackwood?"

*A message.* Palace messengers wore livery. They rode horses. They did not stand in the street wearing porters' coats, knocking on doors like debt collectors.

Unless they weren't messengers. Unless they were informants, and the knock was theater, and the real work had already been done — because Quentin saw it then, saw it with a clarity that turned his stomach to ice water. Across the lane, half-hidden in the doorway of the shuttered dyer's shop, a boy of perhaps twelve lounged against the wall. Not lounging. Watching. Watching the windows of the chandler's house with the fixed patience of a cat at a mousehole.

The boy had seen Wren. Had to have. Three days of fetching bread, of her standing at the window in the light with her unmistakable green eyes — eyes the whole city was beginning to whisper about, if the night-market rumor was anything to go by. *Green-eyed children near the walls.* He'd dismissed it as tavern talk. Tavern talk was always true somewhere.

"They know," he breathed. "Oh, gods. They already know."

Wren was beside him at the window. She looked down at the watcher, and something moved through her face — not fear. Recognition, almost. Sorrow, maybe. As if she'd seen this particular kind of waiting before, in some older story.

The knock came a third time. "Master Blackwood? They say you're home evenings."

Quentin's mind, trained by five years of tasting to separate flavors under pressure, separated his options the same way. Go down, open the door, be charming, claim no knowledge of any child — possible, if the informant had only rumor and no sighting. But the boy across the lane would keep watching, and tonight or tomorrow there'd be a second visit with livery and authority behind it, and by then the trail would be warm.

Or: the back stair. The chandler's house had one, built for smuggling kegs past the excise men a generation ago — a narrow iron ladder down the rear wall into the yard, and from the yard, the alley that ran behind Candlemaker's Row like a spine. He'd noted it his first week here, the way he noted every exit in every building he ever slept in. Caution was the whole of his religion.

But the yard was overlooked by three windows, dusk was failing fast, and Wren—

Wren was already moving. She'd pulled his spare cloak off its peg — the brown one, the anonymous one — and wrapped it around her shoulders, hood up. She stood by the door to the back landing with the charm-box hugged to her chest, and she looked at him the way she'd looked at him the first night: *come, then. Hurry.*

"Not yet," he whispered. "We wait for full dark. If I vanish the moment they knock, that's a confession."

Below, the porter tried the handle, found it locked, and strolled away down the Row — too casually, pausing to examine a lamppost, a shutter, a stray cat. The boy across the lane shifted his weight and settled in. He could wait hours. They were paid to wait hours.

So Quentin waited too. He lit no lamps. He sat in the darkening room with Wren's small hand gripping two of his fingers — when had she started doing that? — and watched the light die out of the sky one color at a time, and understood, with a grief that surprised him with its size, that his life was over.

Not his body. His life. Five years of careful arithmetic — sell small, stay small, taste clean, save coin, buy a cottage someday in some gray town where nobody knew his name — all of it balanced on a single principle: *be no one*. And he had broken it. He'd let a mute girl with the oldest eyes in the world sleep under his roof for three nights, and somewhere between the third bowl of honey-bread and the fourth hour of Vespermark's bells, he had stopped being no one. Now he was a name on an informant's slate. A thread the palace had hold of.

And the terrible thing — the thing he kept turning over like a stone with something alive under it — was that some buried part of him wasn't sorry.

* * *

Full dark came at last, and with it a thin rain that smelled of coal smoke and autumn. Perfect weather. Rain thinned the watchers; even paid ones hated standing in it.

Quentin dressed like a man going out on ordinary errands: plain coat, plain hat, satchel of charm-stock that wouldn't raise an eyebrow if searched — half his inventory was innocuous, and he blessed his own paranoia for keeping it that way. Wren wore the brown cloak, hood deep. To anyone glancing twice she was a small apprentice, a nephew, a shadow.

At the back landing he paused, one hand on the cold iron rail, and made himself look at the room one last time. Bed, stool, window, the lavender bundle over the lintel. Five years of safety distilled into four objects. He'd walk out of it and not come back, and the strangest part was how little it hurt, and how much that lack of hurt frightened him.

"Stay close," he murmured. "If I say *walk*, you walk. If I say *run*, you run and don't stop for me. Do you understand?"

Wren squeezed his fingers once. Then she released them, and in the dark her eyes caught the last glow from the street-lamp below and held it, green as spring sap, green as the first leaf of the world, and she spoke — softly, clearly, three words in the Old Tongue that made the charm-box in his satchel shiver against his hip like a living thing.

He didn't know what they meant. He suspected he would learn. But they sounded, in the rain-dark, like a promise: *I will not run from you.*

Down the iron ladder, quiet as breath. Across the yard, where the rain ticked on overturned barrels. Into the alley, which ran black and dripping between the backs of the shops, and which they followed south, away from the palace, away from the gates, away from every road a sensible fugitive would take.

Because a sensible fugitive would take a road. And roads had riders on them, and riders had orders, and orders these days, Quentin was increasingly certain, came from Lord Chamberlain Aldous Vane — whose smile Quentin had been returning at court for five years without ever once seeing it reach his eyes.

Where do you hide a child the crown wants, in a city that hangs mages? You don't. You get her out. And to get her out unseen, you don't use the streets or the gates.

You use the old temple quarter. The drowned quarter. The places beneath the city that the city preferred to forget — because Quentin had a client, a rag-and-bone man named Doss who bought fever-charms by the dozen and paid in rumors as often as coin, and Doss had told him once, drunk and grateful, about the catacombs under the ruined temple of Saint Verity, and about the woman who lived down there. A court archivist once. Disgraced. Mad, some said. Knew more about the kingdom's buried history than the royal library did.

*If anyone knows what's in that poison,* Doss had slurred, *it's Mother Ilsa.*

Rain, alleys, a culvert, a grate lifted on rusted hinges that groaned once and were still. Quentin lowered Wren into the dark first, then swung down after her, drawing the grate shut above them, sealing out the lamplight and the rain and the whole bright poisonous surface of the city.

The dark below smelled of stone and old water and — faintly, unmistakably, impossibly — of thorns.

Wren's hand found his in the blackness. She was trembling. So, he discovered, was he.

Somewhere far above, muffled by earth and brick, a bell rang the midnight hour, and Quentin Blackwood, royal food-taster, illegal charm-seller, began to walk into the dark with the last speaker of a murdered language, carrying everything he owned in a satchel on his hip — and knowing, with the certainty of a man who has finally tasted the truth in his own cup, that by morning the hunt would have a name, and the name would be his.
