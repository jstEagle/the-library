## 8. Running the Rooftops

The man's door opens and the night pours in, and Wren runs into it because the other door — the one the hard-shoed men will come through — is already full of footsteps.

She does not think in words. Words live in the old place inside her, the green place, and they are not for this. She thinks in warmth and in wound, and the city is a wound tonight, torn open from wall to wall, and the man — the warm man, the taster, the one whose charms hummed like bees when she spoke to them — grabs her hand and says a word she doesn't know but understands anyway.

*Run.*

So they run.

* * *

His hand is hot. That is the first true thing she knows about him, truer than his face, truer than the small room with its hidden drawers and its smell of copper and dried herbs. Every person she has ever touched has been a kind of weather. The matron at the foundling house was cold the way river stones are cold, a cold that never left. The hard-shoed men were cold like the underside of a coffin lid. But this man's hand is a banked fire, a hearth with something still living in the ash, and when his fingers close around hers she feels his magic the way you feel sun through a window — small, domestic, stubborn. A candle-magic. A keep-the-frost-off magic.

It is nothing. It is less than nothing, next to what sleeps under her ribs.

But it is warm, and she is eight years old and has been cold for a very long time, and so she runs with him.

The rooftops of the city rise around them like the backs of sleeping animals. He hauls her up a ladder that groans, across a slope of slate slick with the day's rain, and she learns his balance before she learns his name — how he hesitates half a heartbeat at every edge, how his weight rolls to the balls of his feet, how he always, always looks back for her. She catalogues him the way she catalogued the swallows that nested in the temple eaves: by motion, by habit, by the small honest things a body does when it thinks no one is watching.

He looks back for her four times before they cross the first street.

* * *

Below, the city is a wound, and she sees it the way she sees everything — not with her eyes first.

The city has a skin of stone and a body underneath, and the body is hurt. She felt it the day she arrived at the walls, felt it like a hand pressed against a bruise: something under the foundations, deep, deep, turning over in its sleep the way a burned man turns in his blankets. The streets are scar tissue laid over the hurt. The palace at the city's heart is the hottest place of all, a knot of heat like an infection, and she has been walking toward it for weeks without meaning to, pulled by the thing below the way a dropped apple is pulled by the ground.

Tonight the wound is bleeding light. Torches in the streets below them, moving in patterns. She cannot hear the shouting over the wind and the slap of their own footsteps, but she can feel it — the hard-shoed men, four of them, then six, their coldness spreading through the warm body of the city like ink through water.

The warm man pulls her down flat behind a chimney. His chest is heaving. He presses one finger to his lips, and she copies him, and for a moment they are both just two shapes in the dark breathing the same cold air, and below them a lantern swings, and a voice says something in the flat language of this kingdom — *child, green eyes, the Chamberlain's seal* — and the lantern passes.

The warm man exhales. His relief comes off him like steam.

She reaches out and touches his wrist. Not for comfort. To listen.

Under his skin, his little magic is frightened. She can feel it fluttering like a bird in a closed room. But underneath the fright there is something else, something the man himself may not know is there: a thread. Old. Thin as spider silk. It runs from him backward through the city, backward through years, and it tastes — she tastes it the way you taste rain coming — it tastes of the green place. Of her grandmother's hands. Of a language that was drowned but did not die.

His grandmother sang to him. She is suddenly, absolutely certain of it. Songs in a tongue he thought was nothing, nonsense syllables, a lullaby with no meaning — and the meaning went into him anyway, the way water goes into stone, slow, invisible, until one day the stone is hollowed and holds.

She looks at him in the dark and he looks back, and neither of them has a word to give the other. But she thinks he feels it too, because his breath catches, and his hand comes up — slowly, the way you move near a wild bird — and tucks a wet strand of hair behind her ear.

* * *

They run again.

The rooftops here are older, sagging, tiled in moss-slick clay, and the gaps between buildings widen. He leaps first and lands, and turns, and holds out both arms, and she leaps, and for one moment she is flying — truly flying, the wind roaring past her ears, the whole wounded city spread out beneath her like a dark sea — and then his arms close around her and she is not flying, she is held, and something in her chest that has been clenched since the night the hard-shoed men came to the foundling house loosens by one degree.

Not all the way. Not even halfway. But one degree.

He sets her down. She points, ahead and left, because she has been feeling it for three streets now: a cold place, a dead place, a place where the city's hurt goes underground. He frowns at her pointing. She points again, insistent, and adds the only thing she has to add — she opens her mouth and lets the old word out, the one for *under*, for *beneath-the-roots*, and his charms hum in his pockets, she hears them, a faint insectile shiver, and his frown changes.

It changes from *I don't understand you* to *I don't understand you, but I believe you.*

That is enough. That is more than she has had from anyone in her whole life.

They go left.

* * *

Twice more the cold men come close.

The first time is on the long ridge of a dye-house roof, and the men are in the street below with a dog, and the dog's nose is a terrible thing, a wet black compass pointing at warmth. The warm man does something she will remember for the rest of her life, however long that is. He takes a small clay bead from his pocket — one of his charms, she can feel its tiny candle-heat even through the fabric — and he crushes it in his fist and whispers to it, and the warmth of him, their warmth, the whole warm smudge of two fleeing bodies, *shifts*. It slides sideways across the street, ten feet, twenty, pooling around a stack of rain barrels, and the dog's nose swings after it and the men kick the barrels and swear and move on.

Illusion. Small, cheap, gutter-market illusion, the kind of magic this kingdom hangs people for.

He did it without hesitating. For her.

He looks at his own empty fist afterward like it belongs to a stranger. She takes his hand again. The banked fire is lower now — the charm cost him, she can feel that, a hollowness in his warmth like a room with the hearth swept out — but it is still warm. Still burning.

The second time is worse. A crossbow bolt comes out of the dark and takes a chip out of the chimney beside her head, and the crack of it is so loud that the world goes white and silent, and then the warm man has her by the collar and the back of her shirt and is hauling her across the ridge of the roof at a speed that tears a sound out of her — her first sound of the night, a thin animal yelp — and she hates herself for it, hates that she gave them a sound to follow, and he feels her shame through their joined hands, somehow, the way she feels everything of his, and he squeezes her fingers once, hard, deliberate.

*Not your fault,* the squeeze says, in the language that has no words.

She squeezes back.

* * *

The temple quarter comes up out of the dark like a row of broken teeth.

She has been here before — weeks ago, in the first cold days, sleeping in the shell of a burned sanctuary with the other nameless ones — and she knows what the warm man does not: that the temples here were not burned the way buildings burn. They were *unmade*. She can feel it under her feet even through the rooftops, the old absence, the place where the green place was cut out of the world and the world healed over the cut wrong, lumpy and aching, like flesh over a missing bone.

The kingdom did this. The kingdom with its lanterns and its hard-shoed men and its seal-stamped papers did this, burned it out root and branch, and hung the speakers from the temple gates, and called the ash *peace*.

And now the kingdom's men are hunting her, and the kingdom's poison is being fed to something under its own floor, and the wound the kingdom made is opening again, and she is the last tongue that knows its name.

She is eight years old.

She runs anyway.

* * *

He finds the entrance because she shows him — a collapsed colonnade behind a ruined bell tower, a mouth of dark stone breathing cold air, and the cold here is different from the hard-shoed men's cold. This cold is old and clean and patient, the cold of deep water and deep earth, and when she steps into it the thing under her ribs turns toward it like a plant toward light.

The warm man hesitates at the mouth of the dark. She feels his fear — real fear, the honest kind, the fear of a man who has spent five years being small and careful being asked, all at once, to be neither. His candle-magic gutters. His hand tightens on hers.

She looks up at him. In the last grey light from the street she studies his face the way she studied his balance on the rooftops: the tired eyes, the mouth that wants to be a smirk and keeps failing, the jaw set like a man walking into a door he has decided to walk into.

She does not have words for him. So she gives him what she has.

She presses his hand flat against her own sternum, over the place where the old thing sleeps, and lets him feel it — just for a moment, just a crack in the door — the green heat of it, the vast slow pulse, older than the kingdom, older than the burning, patient as roots under winter stone.

He makes a small sound. His knees nearly go. She feels him understand, in the body if not the mind, that the child he is holding is not a child the way other children are children; that he has not rescued a stray; that he has tied his small warm candle to a forest fire and the fire is *grateful*, and the gratitude is the most frightening thing of all.

Then he steps down into the dark with her, and the city closes over their heads like water.

* * *

The sewers beneath the temple quarter are not sewers. She knows this and he is beginning to know it. The channels are too old, the stone too fine, cut with lines that might be decoration and might be writing — and when her bare toes touch one of the carved lines in the dark, the line *warms*, faint as a dying ember, and the warm man's breath stops, and his charms hum, and somewhere far above and far below, in the wound at the city's heart, something turns over in its sleep and settles.

They walk. The dark is total but she does not need light; the old stone speaks to her in temperature and texture, and she guides him with tugs of the hand — left, down, careful, here — and he follows. He follows without arguing, without stopping, without once trying to take his hand back and lead himself. She counts this. She puts it away in the green place with the other treasures: the swallow nests, the taste of stolen plums, the memory of her grandmother's voice.

A man who follows a mute child into the dark because she points.

The sound of pursuit fades above them, thins, becomes a rumor, becomes nothing. In its place comes the sound of water moving in the dark, patient and old, and the small twin sounds of their own breathing, and once — so far below that it might be a dream — a long, slow, grinding sigh, like a mountain shifting its weight, like a root growing through rock, like something that has waited a hundred years deciding to wait a hundred more.

The warm man stops. She feels the question go through him, up his spine, out through his scalp: *what was that.*

She does not answer. She cannot. Instead she turns in the blackness, finds his face with her hands — his cheeks, rough; his jaw, tight — and holds it, and pours one word into the dark between them, the oldest word she has, the first word her grandmother ever gave her, the word that means *root* and *anchor* and *the thing that holds when everything else is burning*:

"*Ansel.*"

Not his name. Not yet. A word she does not know she is saving for him.

The dark swallows it. Far below, the old stone warms beneath their feet, like something listening.

And then, ahead of them in the tunnel, where no light should be and no person could live — a light. Small, yellow, swinging gently. The sound of a woman's voice, cracked with age, singing a lullaby in a language that makes Wren's blood sing and the warm man's charms hum like a hive waking.

He goes rigid. His hand crushes hers.

He knows the song. She can feel that he knows the song.
