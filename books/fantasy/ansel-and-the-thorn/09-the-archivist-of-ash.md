## 9. The Archivist of Ash

The water in the old temple quarter ran knee-deep and smelled of a hundred years of secrets. Quentin carried Wren on his hip through the flooded passage, her thin arms looped around his neck, her breath warm against his ear. Every few steps the torchlight caught something pale drifting past — a fish, he told himself, or a scrap of cloth — and every few steps he chose not to look closer.

She had not spoken since the rooftops. She didn't need to. When he paused at a branching tunnel, she would tug his collar left or right with the certainty of someone reading a map written on the inside of his eyelids. Twice she pulled him back from passages that looked perfectly fine, and twice, waiting at the junction, they heard things moving in the dark that had no business having so many feet.

"You know where we're going," he murmured. It wasn't really a question.

Wren pressed her palm flat against his chest, over his heart, then pointed down and ahead. *Down and ahead.* The same gesture she'd made at his door, three nights ago, when his charms had hummed like struck glass and his life had quietly come apart at the seams.

*Down and ahead,* then, into the catacombs beneath the temple quarter, toward the only person in Corvane who might tell him what the bitterness in the king's wine meant — a disgraced archivist named Ilsa, whom Quentin had never met but whose name lived in the margins of every forbidden text he'd ever sold. Buyers whispered it the way men whispered about ghosts: *if you want to know what the crown burned, ask the woman under the ash.*

He found the entrance by accident, which he suspected was the only way anyone found it. A section of tunnel wall that should have been brick was instead a curtain of root — pale, fibrous, wrong for this far underground — and when Wren touched it, the roots drew apart like a held breath released.

Beyond lay a corridor of candle stubs, thousands of them, melted into stalagmites of wax along both walls. The air changed. Warmer. Dry. It smelled of dust and tallow and, faintly, of pears.

Quentin's stomach turned over.

* * *

Mother Ilsa was smaller than her reputation. She sat at a table buried under ledgers in a vaulted chamber lined floor-to-ceiling with shelves, and the shelves were lined with books, and the books were wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, row after row after row — a library's worth of contraband, more illegal paper than Quentin had moved in five years of careful, guilty sales, all of it gathered here like driftwood after a flood.

She did not look up from her writing. "Close the roots behind you," she said. Her voice was dry as the air. "They don't like drafts."

He did. When he turned back, she was looking at Wren.

The old woman had gone very still. Her pen hovered above the page, dripping a small black bloom of ink. Her eyes — pale, watery things that should have belonged to a harmless grandmother — were fixed on the girl with an expression Quentin had no name for. Grief, maybe. Or recognition. Or the particular horror of a scholar who has just watched a dead language stand up and walk into her study.

"Where," Ilsa said quietly, "did you find her?"

"She found me." He set Wren down. The girl stayed close to his leg, watching the archivist with those green eyes — spring-sap green, sap-green, the color of the bitterness in the king's wine. "She doesn't speak. Not our tongue. She speaks—"

"I know what she speaks." Ilsa rose, joints cracking like green wood in a fire. She came around the table slowly, bent low, and did not touch Wren, though her hands trembled with wanting to. "Child. May I?" she asked, and waited, and waited, until Wren gave the smallest nod.

Ilsa took the girl's hand and turned it palm-up. In the candlelight Quentin saw what he hadn't noticed before: a mark on Wren's palm, faint as a watermark, a branching line like a winter tree seen through skin. His own charms, tucked against his ribs, warmed suddenly — a low, sweet hum, like a hive waking.

Ilsa let go as if the hand had burned her. She sat down hard on her stool.

"The Purging was two hundred and eleven years ago," she said, to no one, to the shelves, to the ash of everything. "We were taught it was total. We were taught there were no speakers left." She laughed — one dry, terrible note. "And now one is standing in my library eating my bread."

Wren had, indeed, found the bread. Quentin hadn't even seen where.

"Mother Ilsa." He stepped forward. "My name is Quentin Blackwood. I'm the king's taster."

"I know who you are." She didn't look at him. "You sell charms out of the night market. Bad ones, mostly. Kind ones. You've been in my margins for years, boy; half my buyers learned the trade from your handwriting." Now she looked up, and her eyes were flint. "Which means you're either the bravest fool in Corvane or you've run out of choices. Which is it?"

"The king is being poisoned."

Something shifted behind her face. "Go on."

"There's a bitterness. In a spiced pear dish served three days ago, and again last night in his red wine. No one else tastes it. My tongue's been dead five years — that's the work, we can't flinch, we can't react — but underneath the numbness I taste it anyway, and it isn't any poison I know. Arsenic burns. Wolfsbane numbs the lips. This is—" He struggled. How did you describe a flavor that felt like being remembered? "It's green. That's the only word. It tastes green, and cold, like the first frost on a garden, and when I taste it my charms sing."

Ilsa had gone white. "Say the last part again."

"When I taste it, my charms sing."

The old woman closed her eyes. For a long moment the only sound was Wren chewing, and the soft drip of the ink from Ilsa's abandoned pen.

Then she stood, crossed to a shelf near the ceiling, and began pulling down oilcloth bundles with the speed of a much younger woman. "Two hundred years ago," she said, talking as she worked, "the crown didn't just burn the Thorn-speakers. Burning people is fast and cheap and leaves nothing to tax. No — the Purging was an accounting. They catalogued. They confiscated. Everything of the Thorn that couldn't be destroyed was brought *here*, under the palace district, into vaults dug by the first kings, and sealed." She dropped a bundle on the table. Another. "Because you cannot destroy a living thing that remembers how to be a forest. You can only bury it and starve it."

"What are you saying?"

She unwrapped the first bundle: a book bound in bark. The second: a clay tablet cracked down the middle. The third: a stoppered vial of something dark and vegetal that made Wren stop chewing.

"I am saying," Ilsa said, "that the thing you tasted in the king's wine is called a tithe. And a tithe is not poison. Poison kills. A tithe *feeds*."

* * *

She laid it out the way scholars do — flatly, precisely, each fact placed like a stone in a wall — and with every stone Quentin felt the ground shift further out from under everything he believed.

The Thorn, she said, was not a school of magic. It was older than magic-as-the-crown-defined-it, older than the kingdom, older than the word *kingdom*. It was a network — roots under the world, a mind in the deep places, and the speakers were its voice. Where the Thorn grew, the land was rich and strange and obedient to its speakers, and the first kings had been petty warlords in its shadow, paying tribute in grain and blood for leave to build their walls.

"And then one dynasty decided tribute was humiliation," Ilsa said. "King Aldric the Clever. He couldn't kill the Thorn, so he did arithmetic instead. He worked out what it needed to live. Water. Soil. Blood — because everything that lives needs blood, given willingly or taken. And he cut off the supply. Salted the fields where it surfaced. Burned the groves. Killed the speakers so no one could feed it by rite. Then he built his palace directly over the largest surviving fragment of the root-system, on the principle that it's easier to guard a prisoner if you sit on the cell."

Wren had stopped eating. She was looking at Ilsa with an intensity that made the hair rise on Quentin's arms.

"For two centuries the Thorn has slept under the palace. Starving. Dreaming, maybe. And the crown's dirtiest secret — the one passed from archivist to archivist, never written where a king could read it — is that Aldric's successors didn't trust the seal alone. Once a generation, in secret, they paid a tithe. A little blood. A little death. Just enough to keep the thing asleep rather than desperate. A starving beast breaks its chains. A fed one dreams on."

Quentin's mouth had gone dry. "And the pear dish. The wine."

"Green frost," Ilsa said. "Distilled from blighted growth — wood that the Thorn has touched and rejected. To an ordinary body it's a slow wasting illness. But it carries the Thorn's own signature, boy. It's a message written in the only language the thing below understands." She picked up the cracked tablet and turned it toward him. Carved spirals covered its face, and around the spirals, in a newer hand, someone had written figures — dates, quantities, intervals. "This is a tithe ledger. Two hundred years old. Look at the interval."

He looked. "Once a year."

"Now think," Ilsa said softly. "When did the spiced pears first appear on the king's table?"

Quentin counted back without meaning to — he counted everything, it was the curse of a careful man — and arrived at the answer, and hated it. "Midwinter. Eleven months ago."

"And there hasn't been a tithe in sixty years. The last archivists who knew the rite died out under old Queen Sarelle, and the faction that inherited their papers sat on them, because feeding a monster is expensive and embarrassing and no one truly believed the stories." Ilsa's hands flattened on the table. "Until someone found my predecessors' ledgers. Until someone decided the sleeping thing under the throne room was not a prisoner but an *asset*. And they started dosing your king eleven months ago — not to kill him, Quentin. To open him. A body steeped in tithe becomes a vessel the Thorn can pour itself into. They are not assassinating King Edmond. They are *seasoning* him."

The chamber seemed to tilt. Quentin gripped the edge of the table.

"They want the Thorn to wake," he said. "Inside the king."

"I want to say they're mad." Ilsa's laugh was ash. "But look at the tremors in the city these past months. Look at the crops rotting from the root along the river. The thing below is stirring whether they intend it or not, and every dose they pour down your king's throat is another log on a fire they think they can sit beside." She shook her head. "Men like that always think they'll be the exception. The Thorn does not negotiate. It grows. Through soil, through bodies, through crowns. Two hundred years ago it ate a third of the world before Aldric starved it, and the world still hasn't grown that third back."

Silence. The candles guttered. Somewhere far above, muffled by earth and stone, the city went on with its evening — cart wheels, a drunk singing, the ordinary music of people who did not know what was under their feet.

Wren tugged Quentin's sleeve.

She pointed at the tablet. At the spirals. Then at herself. Then she opened her mouth and said three words in the Old Tongue, and every book on Ilsa's shelves rustled at once, like a forest in wind, and every candle flame leaned toward her as if bowing.

Isla made a sound like a prayer breaking off halfway.

"What did she say?" Quentin asked hoarsely.

Ilsa didn't answer at once. She was crying, he realized — silently, without changing expression, tears running into the seams of her face as though this happened often and meant nothing anymore.

"She said, '*I came to bind it.*'" The old woman wiped her face with a sleeve, briskly, like closing a ledger. "That's the prophecy my predecessors died protecting. The last speaker comes in the hour of the waking — to waken it, or to bind it. The choice was always going to arrive wearing green eyes." She looked at Wren, then at Quentin, and her flint eyes had gone soft and terrible. "Binding requires a rite, boy. A blooded speaker to speak it, and a vessel soaked in the Thorn's own signature to carry it. Do you understand what I'm telling you? The faction has spent eleven months preparing exactly one such vessel in all the world."

The bitterness rose in Quentin's memory — green, cold, patient — every pear, every cup of wine, every morning he had swallowed the king's breakfast and kept his face smooth and his tongue dead.

Five years of tasting. Five years of swallowing.

"No," he said.

"His blood is full of it," Ilsa said gently. "The king is the most seasoned vessel in Corvane. And the faction knows it. And somewhere between this room and the throne room, they will have noticed that their taster keeps surviving doses that should be killing him — and asking themselves what else that tongue of yours is good for."

Above them, faint through fathoms of stone, a bell began to ring. Not the temple bell. The palace bell — the one that rang for fire, for invasion, for a king in danger.

Three strokes. A pause. Three more.

Ilsa went rigid. "That's the summons pattern," she breathed. "Every sworn servant of the crown is recalled to the palace. Tonight. Immediately." Her eyes found Quentin's. "Including the royal taster."

Wren's small hand slid into his.

Somewhere across the dark city, Lord Chamberlain Aldous Vane was already smiling.
