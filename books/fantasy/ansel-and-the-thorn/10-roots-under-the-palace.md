## 10. Roots Under the Palace

The candle guttered, and Ilsa did not relight it.

She had learned, in forty years beneath the earth, to read by the light that leaked through the catacomb grates at odd hours — the pale wash of dawn through a vent meant for drainage, the orange flicker of a watchman's lantern passing overhead like a slow fish. Her eyes had adjusted the way all buried things adjust. She could read a page by the memory of a page.

Tonight she did not need to read. Tonight she told.

The girl sat cross-legged on the stone floor, hands folded in her lap with a patience no eight-year-old should own, and Quentin Blackwood stood behind her with his arms crossed like a man guarding a door he could not name. The taster. Ilsa had known him the moment he ducked beneath the low arch — she had catalogued his face years ago, in the ledgers of the court she was never meant to keep: *Quentin Blackwood, appointed under seal, tongue certified numb, loyalty unexamined.* The crown wrote everything down. It simply never read what it wrote.

"You're certain," Quentin said. Not a question. A man hoping to be contradicted.

"I am certain the way stone is certain," Ilsa said. "Sit down, taster. This will take the length of a candle, and you will want to be sitting when it ends."

He sat. The girl — Wren, he had named her, or she had named herself in a language he could not parse — leaned slightly until her shoulder touched his arm. Ilsa watched that small gravity and felt something old and cold shift in her chest, the way a foundation shifts before a wall cracks.

"Before the kingdoms," she began, "before the first wall was laid in this city, the world was rooted."

* * *

She had been young when they brought her down. Twenty-three, a junior archivist with ink-stained cuffs and a commendation for cataloguing the seized libraries of the Purging — the great burning, the great digging-out, the century the crown spent killing a language.

They had told her it was an honor. Archivists were the memory of the realm, and the realm had decided what to remember. Her first assignment was the Ash Vault: the sub-cellar beneath the palace foundations where the confiscated texts were kept, not destroyed — *kept* — because kings burn books and then, quietly, kings keep them. Power hoards even what it fears. Especially what it fears.

Her master was an old man named Corvus Hale, who smelled of tallow and vinegar and who had not been above ground in eleven years. On her first night he set a lamp before her, opened a ledger bound in gray hide, and said:

"Before you file a single page, girl, you will hear the shape of what we keep. Not the contents. The shape. Contents can be survived. Shape cannot."

She had thought him mad. She had been twenty-three.

"The world was rooted," he said, and his voice changed — dropped into a cadence she would later recognize as quotation, as liturgy, as the fossil of a spoken thing. "Not a figure. A fact. The old texts describe a lattice — a living web under all the earth, the way a man has blood under all his skin. The Thorn was not a plant. It was the world's own circulation. Every spring, every river, every green thing that broke soil was the lattice breathing out. And it could be *spoken to.*"

"Spoken to," she repeated.

"There was a tongue for it. Older than any crown. The speakers didn't command it — that's the lie the histories tell, the lie that made the Purging righteous. They *conversed.* They asked the lattice to lean, and it leaned. They asked it to hold back a flood, and it held. They asked it to open the earth under an invading army, and—" Corvus's mouth went dry and thin. "And it opened."

"Then the war was justified."

"The war was a *harvest*," he said. "But we will come to that."

* * *

The Purging had not begun as a purge. It began as a famine.

Ilsa told it plainly, in the candlelight, and watched the girl's green eyes — green as spring sap, green as the one color that did not belong in a catacomb — track her mouth as though the words themselves were being tasted. Quentin shifted, restless, and Ilsa raised one finger without looking at him, and he stilled.

Three hundred years ago, the grain had failed across the whole of the known world. Blight, the chronicles called it, though the old texts named it more honestly: a *sundering*. A faction of speakers — the texts call them the Severed, though whether that was their name or their sentence, no one knew — had cut a wound in the lattice deliberately. Why, the records never say. Ambition. Grief. The texts are human documents; they record human motives and go silent before the vast ones. What is recorded is the consequence: the Thorn withdrew from the wounded region, and everything the lattice had fed — crops, herds, children in their cradles, the very soil — sickened and grayed.

The kings of that age did what kings do. They found someone to blame.

The speakers had not caused the sundering — most of them had died trying to mend it, spending their lives into the lattice like coins into a wound. But they were few, and strange, and their tongue could not be learned by ordinary means — it had to be *inherited*, sung into children before they could walk, a mother-tongue of the blood — and so they could not be replaced, could not be reasoned with en masse, could not be absorbed. A power that cannot be absorbed can only be destroyed. Every court in the world arrived at that arithmetic within a generation, and the Purging was not one war but a hundred, a coordinated extermination dressed in a hundred different flags.

"They were not soldiers," Ilsa said. "That is what the ledgers do not say loudly enough. The wardens who died defending the sanctuaries were farmers and midwives and singers. The crown's own records — the ones I catalogued, the ones no minister has read in two hundred years — list the dead by trade. Beekeeper. Tanner. A woman whose occupation is given only as *she sang at births.* Eleven thousand pages of it. I read every page. It took me four years, and when I finished I went up into the daylight and could not remember what it was for."

Quentin's voice came low. "And the sanctuaries?"

"Nine great ones. The texts speak of a tenth — the First Root — but its location was never written. The nine were burned or salted or drowned over sixty years. The lattice, wounded and unmourned, pulled inward. The world's blood drew back from its skin. That is why the north is moor now, and the deep south is glass-sand, and the rivers run thin. We live on a body that has been bleeding for three centuries and has learned to live on less."

The candle flame leaned. In the draft, for a moment, Ilsa smelled green — actual green, crushed leaf, spring — in a room forty feet under the palace, and she watched the girl's nostrils flare, watched her turn her head toward the eastern wall with the unerring motion of a compass needle.

"Yes," Ilsa said softly. "You feel it. Good. Then hear the last part."

* * *

Corvus Hale had shown her the shape of it in her first year, and she had not understood until her tenth.

"The Purging failed," he told her, on a night when the lamps burned low and his voice had the rasp of a man reading his own eulogy. "Not entirely. Not everywhere. One heart of the lattice survived. The largest. The oldest. And do you know where the victors built their capital, girl? On the ruins of the greatest sanctuary, because holy ground is well-drained and well-watered and the foundations of the old world were sound. They built the palace on top of it. They built their throne room on top of it. And the heart went to sleep under the flagstones, and for three hundred years the kings have been sleeping on the neck of a thing they never killed."

She had laughed. She remembered that — the ugly, reflexive laugh of a young woman refusing a fact. Corvus had waited out her laughter with the patience of stone.

"The tithe," he said. "You have heard the phrase in the oldest ledgers. *Thorn-tithe.* The Purging's architects were not fools. They knew they could not kill the heart — they tried for sixty years, and the ground itself refused them, and the men who dug too deep came back wrong or did not come back. So they bound it instead. A binding is not a killing. A binding is a diet. The heart was fed — a measured, rationed feeding, blood and old magic and the ash of burned speakers, poured into the deep vaults on a schedule, enough to keep it dreaming and no more. Every generation of kings renewed the tithe without knowing what it was. It was in the coronation oaths. It was in the harvest rites. It was in the palace's very bones — the reason the palace has never cracked in three hundred years of tremors that leveled every other building in the old quarter. The heart holds the foundations up. The tithe keeps it from noticing what stands on it."

"And the schedule," Ilsa said now, to the girl and the taster, in the candlelight, "was broken."

Quentin went very still. She watched him arrive at it — watched the taster's trained tongue arrive at the truth the way his tongue arrived at poison, by bitterness, by the wrongness in a familiar flavor.

"The tithe was administered by the Office of the High Steward through a chain of under-officials," Ilsa said. "I was one of them. The last one. When I found the true ledgers — not the ritual ledgers, the *real* ones, the feeding schedules, the quantities — I did what archivists do. I wrote a report. I addressed it up the chain. And I learned that the chain had been cut from the other end. The tithe had been redirected. Someone above me, someone with a seal I was not cleared to see, had been *increasing* the feed. Slowly. Over years. Not enough to wake the heart — not yet — but enough to end its dreaming. Enough to make it stir."

"Vane," Quentin said.

"I don't know the name," Ilsa said, which was true, and watched him not believe her, which was also true. "I know the seal: a thorned circle, inverted. I know that when I asked my question in the wrong room, I was relieved of my post within the month, my access revoked, my name struck from the rolls. I was permitted to live because a dead archivist raises questions and a disgraced one does not. So I came down here, where the old records live, and I have spent nineteen years reading the shape of what was done."

* * *

There was one text Corvus had never let her file. He kept it in a lead-lined case under his cot, and he took it out only once a year, on the anniversary of the Purging's end, and read from it aloud in the dark.

It was a warden's testament — one of the last speakers, a woman who had survived the burning of the Sixth Sanctuary and lived long enough, hidden, to write down what the wardens had always known. Corvus translated it haltingly; the testament was written partly in the Old Tongue, and the Old Tongue could not be fully translated, only *gestured at*, the way a shadow gestures at the thing that casts it.

"The heart will sleep as long as it is starved and fed in measure," Corvus read. "But the lattice remembers its speakers as a mother remembers a voice. When the last speaker comes — and one will come, because the tongue is in the blood, and blood finds its way back — the heart will know her. It will reach for her. And she will stand at the place where it sleeps, and she will choose.

"If she speaks the words of waking, the lattice rises, and the debt of three hundred years is collected, and every kingdom built on the wound is unmade root and branch. The world will be green again. The world will also be over.

"If she speaks the words of binding — the old binding, the true one, not the crown's starvation — the heart is not fed and not killed but *answered.* Made whole by consent. A binding of that kind requires two things the world has not held in one place since the Purging: a speaker of the blood to give the words, and a vessel to carry the answering — a body steeped in the old magic, saturated, marinated in tithe and charm and the heart's own flavor, so the lattice has something of itself to hold when it wakes enough to be held.

"The vessel dies," Corvus read, and his voice did not waver, because he had read it every year for thirty years and had worn the grief smooth as a river stone. "The speaker lives, and the world lives, and no one will ever know what was paid, because that is the nature of roots. Everything rests on them. No one sees them."

He had closed the case. He had looked at her — twenty-three, ink-stained, laughing a year ago at a mad old man — and said: "Now you know why we keep the shape and not the contents. Because someday a girl will come down here with green eyes, and she will need someone who knows which words are which."

* * *

Ilsa finished, and the catacomb was silent in the way of places that have been silent a long time — not empty silence but *attentive* silence, the silence of something listening through forty feet of stone.

The girl had not moved. Her hands were still folded. But tears ran down her face, steady and unchildlike, and her lips were moving — shaping, silently, sounds that Ilsa's ear, trained on two hundred dead dialects, could not follow and her bones, she realized with a cold that started in her spine and spread outward, *could.*

Quentin had his hand on the girl's shoulder. "The vessel," he said. His voice was careful, the voice of a man tasting something and refusing to name the flavor. "Steeped in the old magic. Saturated. Someone who's been—" He stopped.

"Someone who has been taking the tithe into his body for years," Ilsa said, "in small measured doses, building what the old texts call *deep familiarity.* Yes. The texts are more specific than either of us would like. They describe a taster."

He looked at her then, and Ilsa saw the whole architecture of the man's double life stand revealed in his face — not the guilt, not the fear, but the terrible arithmetic underneath, the same arithmetic every court in the world had once performed: *a thing that cannot be absorbed can only be spent.*

"No," he said.

"It is not a sentence," Ilsa said. "It is a shape. The texts give the shape; the choice remains the choice. The speaker chooses to speak. The vessel chooses to hold. Nothing in the old binding is compelled — that is the entire point of it, that is why it *works* where the crown's starvation failed. Consent is the mechanism. Which means, taster, that no one — not I, not the heart, not fate itself — can make you the vessel. It can only be offered."

"I said no."

"I heard you." She rose, joints cracking, and took the lamp to the eastern wall — the wall the girl had turned toward, the wall behind which, forty feet up and two hundred yards east, the throne room's flagstones lay like a lid on a pot. "But before you say it a second time, you should know the rest. The feed has been increasing for six years. The tremors in the low quarter, the blight in the river farms, the wells that have gone bitter — that is not a heart stirring in its sleep. That is a heart *turning over.* The ledgers I escaped with give the schedule. By my count, by the old measures, the dreaming ends within the year. Perhaps within the season."

Behind her, the girl spoke.

One word. Aloud. The first sound Ilsa had heard her make, and it was not a child's sound — it was a root-sound, a word that seemed to come up through the floor rather than out of the mouth, and every charm on Quentin Blackwood's hidden person sang at once, a faint chord of humming like a struck bowl, and the candle flame stood up straight and tall as if saluting.

Ilsa did not know the word. She knew its shape. She had read its shape a hundred times in a warden's testament in a lead-lined case.

It was the Old Tongue's word for *hurry.*

Quentin was on his feet. "What did she say?"

Ilsa turned back to them, and forty years of buried reading settled onto her shoulders like a mantle she had been fitted for at twenty-three and had spent her whole life refusing to wear.

"She said," Ilsa told him, "that we have less time than I thought — and that the king is asking for you by name."
