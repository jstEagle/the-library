## 11. The King's Table

The summons came at dawn, and it did not come the way summons came.

For five years Quentin had been fetched to court by a liveried page with a bored mouth and a slip of paper bearing the seal of the Master of Household — a routine thing, dry as biscuit, *the king breaks his fast at the seventh bell; attend*. This morning there was no page. There were two men in the grey-and-silver of the Lord Chamberlain's own retinue, standing in the fog outside his lodgings with the particular stillness of men who had been told to wait as long as it took.

"His lordship requests the taster," said the taller one. Not *the king requests*. Not *the household requests*. The Lord Chamberlain. By name, by office, by weight.

Quentin's tongue — that numb, scarred, well-practiced tongue — pressed against the back of his teeth where he kept the little lie of calm. "I'll want an hour. I keep a fast before tasting. Clears the palate."

"His lordship said you'd say that." The man's face did not change. "He said to tell you the kitchen will accommodate your fast. You're wanted within the hour."

*He knows my habits.* Of course he knew them. Vane knew everything that happened inside those walls; Quentin had always assumed that was a courtier's exaggeration, the kind of legend servants built around powerful men because it was easier than understanding them. Now, walking between the two greys through streets still blue with morning, he understood it was simply inventory. Vane counted things. Quentin had spent five years being counted without noticing the tally marks.

Wren was safe. That was the litany he carried under his ribs like a second heartbeat. Wren was beneath the temple quarter with Mother Ilsa, behind three locked doors and a door that was not a door but a collapse dressed as one, in a room full of ash-preserved books that no one living had reason to open. He had left her eating honeyed bread with the solemn concentration of a child who had learned, recently and permanently, that food could be a weapon. She had not spoken since the word *hurry*. She had only looked at him with those sap-green eyes and touched two fingers to his wrist, once, deliberately, the way you press a seal into wax.

Ilsa had translated nothing else for him. There had been no time. There had been only the walk up out of the catacombs, the fog, the two grey men, and the arithmetic of survival: go to the palace, taste what they put in front of him, be exactly the careful hollow man they believed him to be, and come back down before the day turned.

Simple. It had never once been simple.

* * *

The royal apartments smelled wrong.

Quentin noticed it before he noticed anything else — before the doubled guard at the stairhead, before the way the chambermaids moved through the antechamber like mice crossing open floor, before the King's Table itself, laid for a breakfast that would not be eaten so much as endured. Underneath the beeswax and the rosewater the servants used to mask sickness, there was a green smell. Faint. Rooty. The smell of a cellar where something has been growing in the dark for a long time.

He had smelled it yesterday in the wine. He had told himself it was a bad cask, a tainted herb, some vintner's carelessness. He had told himself a great many things on the walk from the catacombs, and now, standing in the king's presence chamber with his hands folded and his eyes lowered, he stopped telling himself anything at all and simply *looked*.

King Aldric sat propped in the great chair at the head of the table, and he was dying by inches in front of forty people who had all agreed, silently and mutually, not to see it. He was fifty-one years old and looked carved from wet wood. His hands lay on the tablecloth like something set down and forgotten. His physicians hovered at his shoulder with their phials and their grave faces, prescribing rest, prescribing broth, prescribing prayers — everything except the truth, which was that their king was being fed, spoonful by spoonful, to something none of them had words for.

And beside the chair, attentive as a gardener, stood Lord Aldous Vane.

"Master Blackwood." Vane's voice was warm. That was the horror of the man; Quentin had heard him be genuinely warm with stableboys and scullery maids, and it was never performance, or if it was, it was performance so deep it had become flesh. "You honor us. Though I'm told you've been keeping irregular hours of late."

"The night market has its own hours, my lord." A half-truth, offered lightly, the way you offer a coin to a beggar so he won't follow you. "A taster's palate is worth more after dark."

"I imagine it is." Vane smiled and gestured to the table. "His majesty's appetite has been poor. The physicians want plain foods, but a king cannot dine on gruel before his council — it unsettles people. So we've prepared a modest service. I'd value your opinion on each dish before it reaches him."

There it was. Not *taste for the king*, which was Quentin's office, his duty, the sentence written into his indenture five years ago. *Give me your opinion.* As though Vane needed a palate. As though the Lord Chamberlain, who controlled every kitchen, every cellar, every delivery that passed the palace gates, required assistance knowing what was in the food.

What he wanted was a witness who didn't know he was one. If the king sickened visibly at table, there would need to be a record: *the taster approved each dish.* Quentin's signature, written in advance, on whatever came next.

"Of course, my lord."

The first course was brought. Quail in grape sauce, and Quentin took his spoonful, held it against his palate, and let the numbness do its work while his mind read the flavor like a ledger. Grape, verjuice, cinnamon, marrow-stock, salt. Clean. He nodded. The server bore it to the king.

The second course. Whitefish with fennel. Clean.

The third was pears — spiced pears, baked soft, the same dish that had started all of this weeks ago, the bitterness no one else had ever detected. Quentin took his portion and found the bitterness waiting for him like an old acquaintance. Stronger now. Beneath the honey and the clove, that green root-note, that cellar-darkness, threaded through the fruit's sweetness like a vein of ore through marble.

He swallowed it. Five years of swallowing had taught his body to file such things away, to metabolize the unmetabolizable, and he felt the tithe settle into him with barely a whisper — a cold thread behind his breastbone, present, accounted for, ignored. He thought of Ilsa's voice in the dark of the archive: *a thorn-tithe is not meant to kill quickly. It is meant to accumulate. Every dose is a brick.*

They were building something out of their king.

"He takes the pear," Vane said softly, watching Aldric's trembling hand lift the fork. "It's the one thing he still enjoys. Isn't that so, your majesty?"

The king did not answer. His eyes were fixed on the middle distance, on some country only he could see, and his jaw worked slowly at the fruit as though chewing were a task delegated to him by someone else.

But then — Quentin would replay this moment for the rest of his life, however long that proved to be — the king's gaze drifted. Wandered along the table. Caught on Quentin's face the way a drunk man catches a doorframe.

And focused.

Not the flat glaze of the tithe-drunk. Something underneath it, swimming up from far below, like a face seen through deep water. The king's brow creased. His lips moved, shaping a word that had no sound, and then his hand came off the tablecloth and shot out — faster than it had any right to be, faster than the physicians could react — and closed around Quentin's wrist.

The grip was iron. Dying men should not have iron in them.

"Your majesty—" one of the physicians began.

"*Quiet.*" It came out cracked, rusted, but it was a king's voice, the voice that had commanded armies at Marrow Ford, and the whole room went still as a held breath. Aldric pulled Quentin closer. Close enough that Quentin could see the veins in the king's temples, dark and branching, darker than veins should be. Close enough to hear the wet rattle of his breathing. Close enough that when the king spoke again, it was a thread of sound meant for one ear alone.

And the words were Old Tongue.

Broken, slurred, half-dissolved — the accent of a man speaking a language he had learned as a child and buried for forty years, dredged up through poison and fog. But Quentin knew them. Three nights in a catacomb with Ilsa's lexicons and Wren's patient green-eyed repetition had planted enough of the language in him that the meaning arrived whole, arriving the way the Thorn's magic arrived — not through the ear but somewhere deeper, behind the breastbone, next to the cold thread of the tithe.

*The roots know your name,* the king whispered. *Do not let them finish the meal.*

Quentin's blood went to ice water.

The king knew. Not suspected — *knew*. Knew what was in the pears, or at least what the pears were for. Knew about the roots beneath the palace, the thing Ilsa had named in her ash-smelling archive, the heart of the Thorn that the Purging had never truly killed. And he knew Quentin — knew him somehow, knew him enough to warn him, in a dead language, at a breakfast table surrounded by the very men feeding him to the dark.

How long? Quentin's mind raced down corridors and found every door locked. How long had Aldric known? Had he tasted it himself, years ago, in some earlier dish, with a younger tongue? Had someone taught him fragments of the Old Tongue the way Quentin's grandmother had sung him fragments — lullabies that were spells wearing nightclothes? Was the whole kingdom seeded with buried speakers, buried knowers, a generation of grandparents humming extinct magic to children who didn't know what they were hearing?

The king's grip tightened. His eyes, inches away, were wet and burning and terribly lucid, and in them Quentin saw something that cracked his loyalty open like a struck geode: fear. Not for himself. For Quentin.

"They watch," Aldric breathed, and this time it was Common Tongue, ruined and ragged. "Smile, taster. Smile, or we are both—"

The strength went out of him mid-word, like a snuffed candle. His hand slid from Quentin's wrist and dropped to the cloth. The glaze came down over his eyes again, the tithe closing over the brief surface of his waking like water over a drowned lamp, and he sagged back into the great chair and resumed his slow, distant chewing.

* * *

The chamber erupted into motion — physicians surging forward, cushions adjusted, water offered, Vane's voice rising in smooth, concerned command: *"Gently, gently, his majesty has overexerted — Master Blackwood, you may withdraw, you've done admirably—"*

Quentin withdrew. He did it the way he did everything: smoothly, correctly, with his eyes lowered and his pulse hammering against the walls of his throat. He bowed to the king who had just spoken a dead language into his ear. He bowed to the Lord Chamberlain whose smile had not wavered by a hair's width through any of it. He walked past the doubled guards, down the stair, through the antechamber where the chambermaids had already resumed their dusting as though kings whispered prophecies at breakfast every day.

Vane caught him at the outer door.

Not physically. Worse. With presence — the man simply materialized at his elbow, hands clasped behind his back, gazing out at the inner courtyard where a fountain played over stones gone black with old water.

"A remarkable scene," Vane said pleasantly.

"My lord?"

"The king, seizing your arm. Speaking to you." Vane turned his head, and his eyes were pale grey and perfectly calm, and Quentin understood suddenly why the faction had chosen this man, this smiling, patient, inventory-keeping man. "Privately. In a language our scholars assure me is nonsense — the babble of the poisoned mind. You'd agree it was nonsense, Master Blackwood?"

Every instinct Quentin owned screamed at once, a chorus of five years' survival. *Say yes. Say it was gibberish. Be the hollow man.*

But he had spent last night in a catacomb learning that language. He had spent three nights with a child who spoke it natively. And Aldous Vane did not ask questions he did not already have answers to — that was the first law of men like him. The question was a trap either way, and the only variable was which trap.

So Quentin did the bravest cowardly thing available. He frowned, honestly, and said:

"It sounded like fever, my lord. Though—" he hesitated, let the hesitation show, a craftsman offering a detail he shouldn't have, "—there was a word or two I almost recognized. My grandmother sang old songs. Northern counting songs. Some of the sounds were alike."

Vane studied him for a long moment. The fountain played. Somewhere across the courtyard, a guard coughed.

"Your grandmother," Vane said at last, warmly. "How interesting. You must tell me more about her sometime." He clapped Quentin once on the shoulder — a light, companionable blow that Quentin felt all the way down his spine. "Enjoy your evening, Master Blackwood. Rest well. A taster's gift is precious, and I should hate to see it wasted."

*I should hate to see it wasted.* Said the way a man says *I know where she sleeps.*

Quentin walked out through the palace gates into the morning crowd, and the city received him, loud and ordinary and oblivious, fishwives crying herring, cart wheels grinding, children running underfoot. He walked four blocks. Five. Made sure of his tail — there was a tail, a lean man in a dyer's apron who had no dye on his hands — lost him in the cloth market, doubled back through the tannery stink, and ran.

He ran the rest of the way to the temple quarter with the king's whisper lodged behind his breastbone like a splinter, right beside the cold thread of the tithe, two foreign objects in his flesh that were beginning, he realized with a clarity that felt like falling, to resemble each other.

*The roots know your name.*

At the false collapse behind the third locked door, he knocked the old pattern — twice, pause, three times — and Ilsa opened it before the third knock fell, as though she had been standing there all morning. Behind her, in the lamplight, Wren sat cross-legged among the ash-books, and she was looking at him already, green eyes wide, and her small hands were moving in urgent shapes before he had crossed the threshold.

Ilsa read them and went white.

"She says," the old woman said slowly, "that a man came asking at the market stalls last night. Showing a drawing." Her eyes lifted to Quentin's face. "She says the drawing was of her. And that the man holding it was asking after a charm-seller with a numb tongue."

Quentin stood in the doorway with the palace still on his clothes and the king's warning still in his bones, and understood that the hourglass had been turned over while he was bowing at the king's table.

"We leave tonight," he said.
