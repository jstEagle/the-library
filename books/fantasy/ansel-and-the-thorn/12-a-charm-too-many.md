## 12. A Charm Too Many

The summons came at dusk, folded inside a summons that was not a summons.

Quentin had been in his rooms for less than an hour — long enough to bank the fire, long enough to check the loose brick behind the wardrobe where Wren was hidden with bread and a cup of watered wine and strict instructions to be silent as a stone — when the knock came. Two knocks, a pause, a third. Palace cadence. His stomach dropped through the floorboards before he'd even crossed the room.

The page in the corridor wouldn't meet his eyes. That was the first bad sign. Pages were trained to look at you, to be pleasant, to be furniture with manners. This one stared at Quentin's boots as if they might bite and held out a slip of cream-colored paper sealed with black wax.

*His Lordship the Lord Chamberlain requests Master Blackwood's presence at his convenience. The Vane townhouse, Candlewick Row. Tonight.*

*At his convenience. Tonight.*

The second bad sign was the wax. Lord Chamberlains sent business through the chancellery, through clerks, through channels that left records. Black wax, hand-delivered, meant a conversation that would never appear in any ledger. Quentin had sold enough charms in five years to know what it meant when a powerful man wanted no paper trail.

He dressed carefully. Good coat, the grey one, the one that made him look respectable rather than prosperous. Prosperous was a word that got tasters robbed in alleys and questioned in offices. He left the fire banked, left the kettle on the hook — a small domestic theater for anyone who came looking — and touched the wardrobe door once with two fingers before he went out.

Wren's green eyes watched him through the gap. He couldn't speak to her, couldn't risk even a whisper, so he did what he'd done every night since the rooftops: he held up one hand, palm flat, and then closed it slowly into a fist. *Wait. Stay. Be small.* She pressed her hand against the inside of the door, and he felt the faint warmth of it through the wood, the way he'd felt her warmth since the night she'd first made his charms hum. Then the gap closed, and he was alone on the stair with his own heartbeat.

Candlewick Row was twenty minutes' walk through streets gone soft with evening rain. Quentin used every one of those minutes. He turned the situation over and over like a coin he was checking for clippings. Vane knew *something*. If Vane knew everything, there would be no invitation — there would be soldiers at his door and a cell below the White Court and a trial that lasted exactly as long as it took to pronounce *mage*. So: Vane knew something. The question was how much, and what he wanted, and whether Quentin was walking into a negotiation or a trap dressed as one.

He had lied to powerful men before. It was, in its way, the family trade. A taster lied with every swallow — lied to the king's face with a calm tongue and a steady hand, *this is safe, sire, eat*, when the truth was only *I am still alive, so it is probably safe, probably*. He had made a career of standing between a crown and its death and smiling while he did it.

But Aldous Vane was not the king. The king was a fading warmth, a man half-dissolved into his own illness, and Quentin had spent the last weeks telling himself the lie that the illness was illness. He had tasted the thorn-tithe in the wine. He had felt it on his tongue like a buried thing, like a root, like something patient. And the only person in the palace with the authority to put a cup in the king's hand night after night after night was the Lord Chamberlain.

* * *

The Vane townhouse was the kind of building that apologized for its own wealth. Grey stone, narrow windows, a door of black oak with a knocker shaped like a closed hand. Inside, everything was dark wood and darker velvet, and the candles were beeswax — expensive, faintly honeyed — burning in sconces spaced so precisely that the light never quite reached the corners. Quentin noticed, because noticing was how he'd stayed alive, that there were guards. Not in the room. In the house. He counted three by their boots on the stairs above and one by his breathing somewhere behind the paneled wall.

Vane received him in a study lined with books that had never been read. The Lord Chamberlain sat behind a desk of pale birch with a decanter of amber wine and two glasses, and he looked exactly as he always looked at court: silver at the temples, a face like a well-governed city, and the smile — that famous smile, the one the young clerks imitated and the old clerks feared — already in place, warm as a hearth and about as dangerous as one, if you didn't know what hearths did to houses that caught.

"Master Blackwood." Vane rose and poured with his own hands, which was itself a performance; a man like Vane had servants to pour. "Thank you for coming. Sit. You'll take a little of the Amber Reach? It's a good vintage. I had it tasted, of course." The smile widened by a degree. "By someone whose palate I trust."

Quentin sat. He did not touch the wine. "Your Lordship honors me."

"I do." Vane settled back, glass cradled in long fingers, and for a moment simply looked at Quentin with the mild, cataloguing attention of a man appraising a horse. "Five years in His Majesty's service. Five years of tasting, and never once a tremor, never once a refusal. Do you know how rare that is? Most tasters last three. The good ones burn out — the fear, you understand. The constant arithmetic of *is this the cup*. But you—" he gestured with the glass, an easy, inclusive motion, "—you never seem afraid. I've wondered about that. For a long time, I've wondered about that."

"The work agrees with me," Quentin said.

"Does it." Vane set the glass down without drinking. That was the third bad sign, and by now Quentin had stopped counting because the number no longer mattered. "I'll be plain with you, Blackwood, because I find plainness saves everyone a great deal of wear. You have a second trade. You've had it for years. Charms. Small ones — warming charms, luck charms, charms against toothache and unfaithful husbands. Sold in the night market, in the tannery rows, in the fish quarter, for copper, mostly, sometimes silver. You've been careful. Genuinely, remarkably careful. But careful is not the same as invisible."

The fire in the grate made a small sound, a settling of coals, and in that sound Quentin heard five years collapse.

He had run the arithmetic a hundred times in the walk over, and here it was, arriving anyway. He thought of the fishwife with the charm against drowning sewn into her husband's cap. The lamplighter's girl and her fever-knot. The old soldier who paid him in stories and wanted only a charm to dream of his wife without weeping. All of it, every small kindness, every copper earned in the dark — laid out now on a birch desk in front of the most dangerous man in the kingdom.

Magic was death. Not metaphorically. The statute was specific: *the practice of the forbidden arts, being witchcraft, sorcery, or the old ways of the Thorn, is punishable by hanging, and the body shall not be buried.* He had recited it to himself the night of his first sale, standing in an alley with three coppers in his fist, and he had recited it every night since, the way other men said their prayers.

"I don't know what you've been told, my lord," Quentin began, "but—"

"Please." Vane raised one hand, and the smile did not waver, which was the most frightening thing about it. "Don't insult us both. I have a sworn statement from a man who bought a charm against rheumatism from you eleven days ago. I have the charm. It works, Blackwood. I had it tested." A pause, delicately weighted. "It works."

*Tested.* Quentin's mouth went dry. Somewhere in this city there was a man — or what was left of a man — who had been made to demonstrate a warming charm for the Lord Chamberlain's men, and the demonstration had cost him. Quentin made himself breathe. Made himself be the taster: calm hands, level eyes, a man whose whole art was not showing what the cup contained.

"Then you know," Quentin said quietly, "that everything I've ever sold was small. Warming. Healing. Luck. I've never raised so much as a cantrip against another living soul. Five years, and the sum total of my crimes is that a laundress's hands stopped aching in winter."

"Crimes," Vane repeated, tasting the word. "You see, that's the interesting part. By the statute, it doesn't matter. Small, large, kind, cruel — the old ways are the old ways, and the kingdom burned them out of the world for reasons I don't pretend to fully understand. If I took that statement downstairs to my guards and did my duty, you'd be dead by Thursday, and no one at court would even ask why. Your king would taste his breakfast on Friday and never know the tongue that kept him was gone." He leaned forward, and the firelight caught the silver at his temples and turned it cold. "But I'm not going to do my duty, Master Blackwood. I'm going to offer you a trade."

And there it was. Quentin had known it was coming since the black wax, had felt it waiting in the room like a fourth guard, and still his heart stumbled when it arrived.

"The child," Vane said.

The word landed in Quentin's chest like a dropped stone. He kept his face still. He had spent five years keeping his face still while poison sat on his tongue; he could keep it still now.

"I don't know of any child."

"A girl. Eight years old, perhaps. Green eyes — not green the way eyes are green, but green the way spring sap is green, the way new leaves are green. Seen near the walls by the tanners, near the fish market, near your street." Vane's voice never rose, never sharpened; it was the voice of a man reading a menu. "My people have been asking after her for some weeks. And my people are thorough. They've asked after her near your street *particularly*, because three separate witnesses place a man of your description — grey coat, careful walk, the taster's stoop — speaking with her. Once by the fountain. Once at the corner of Bellows Lane. And once—" the smile deepened, and Quentin felt the floor tilt under his stillness, "—once outside your own door, Master Blackwood. Where she stood for some time. Where she was *let in*."

The room was very quiet. The beeswax candles burned with their faint honey smell, and somewhere above them a guard shifted his weight, and Quentin sat in the warmth of a stranger's fire and felt the cold come up through the floor of the world.

*She stood for some time. Where she was let in.* He had been careful. He had been so careful — checked the street, watched for watchers, moved her at night, kept the brick loose only when the building slept. But careful was not invisible. Vane had said it himself. A man who spent his life watching for poison in cups was, perhaps, a man who had forgotten to watch for poison in the shape of a question asked of a neighbor.

"She's a beggar child," Quentin said. "She followed me. Children do. I gave her bread because I'm not in the habit of letting them starve on my step. If that's a crime in this kingdom, my lord, hang me for it."

"Perhaps you did." Vane spread his hands, magnanimous, terrible. "Perhaps that's all it was. But here is what troubles me, Blackwood, and I'll share it with you as one careful man to another. That child is not a beggar. That child is the last speaker of a language that has been dead for three hundred years, and there are people — serious people, patient people — who believe she is the most dangerous thing to enter this city since the Purging. And you—" he picked up his glass again, finally, and drank, a small deliberate sip, "—you, a taster with a hidden second trade and a tongue that never trembles, took her in. Either you are the most charitable man in the capital, or you know exactly what she is. And I have read your service record, Master Blackwood. Charity is not among your documented virtues."

*The last speaker.* Quentin kept his breathing slow. So Vane knew that much — which meant Vane had sources Quentin couldn't imagine, or Vane was one of the *serious people, patient people* himself, the ones who put thorn-tithe in a king's wine and called it medicine. Mother Ilsa's voice in his memory, low and grim in the catacomb dark: *They're not killing him. They're feeding something. And a feeding needs a keeper.*

"What do you want?" Quentin asked. His voice came out flat and even, the taster's voice, the voice that said *this cup is safe* while standing on the edge of the only cliff he had.

"Simple things." Vane set the glass down and turned it once with his fingertips, a small precise rotation, a man aligning the world. "The girl. Delivered to me, alive and unharmed, within three days. In exchange: the statement downstairs goes into my fire. Your five years of copper charms become a story I once heard and never believed. You keep your post, your rooms, your head, and your king's breakfast continues to arrive at your door every morning with you standing between it and the dark." He paused. "You may even keep your little trade, if you're discreet. I'm not a zealot, Blackwood. I'm a practical man. The kingdom needs its small magics more than it admits. What it does not need—" and here, for the first time, something moved underneath the smile, something old and hungry and afraid, "—is what's sleeping under it, waking up."

*Under it.* Quentin's skin went cold. He thought of the bitter root-note in the king's wine, night after night, patient as a season. He thought of Wren's hand pressed flat against the inside of the wardrobe door, her warmth coming through the wood like a candle through a wall.

"And if I refuse?" Quentin asked.

"Then Thursday," Vane said pleasantly. "And then my people find the girl anyway, because they *will* find her, Blackwood — she's a child, and children leave traces — and they will find her frightened, and alone, and they will not be gentle. You've seen what my people can do to a grown man with a rheumatism charm. Imagine what the statute's provisions for *the old ways of the Thorn* look like when applied to a child who *is* the old ways." He rose, smoothing his coat, and the audience was clearly over. "Three days. A note left with the bootmaker on Candlewick Row, the shop with the green awning. You'll say only: *the errand is done.* No name. Nothing written down. And Master Blackwood—" he extended his hand, and Quentin took it, and Vane's grip was dry and cool and strong, "—I want you to understand that I bear you no ill will. In your place, I would do exactly what you're about to do. Everyone does. That's rather the point of offers like this one."

* * *

The rain had stopped. The streets shone. Quentin walked home through the wet dark at a careful, unhurried pace — a taster going home from a social call, nothing more — while inside his skull the whole night turned over and over like a coin being checked for clippings.

Three days. Deliver the child. Keep his head.

It was, he had to admit, a good offer. That was what made it monstrous. Vane had built it the way a cook built a sauce: every element balanced, nothing wasted, the exit made comfortable so the guest wouldn't look too closely at the kitchen. And Quentin had stood in that warm room and shaken the man's hand and let his face do what it was made to do — the taster's face, the safe face, the face that had lied to a king five mornings a week for five years.

*In your place, I would do exactly what you're about to do.*

Yes. Vane believed he'd won. Vane believed that by Thursday's end the last speaker of the Thorn would be in a cellar under Candlewick Row, and the taster would be back at his post with his little fire and his little crimes, grateful, obedient, bought.

Quentin climbed the stairs to his rooms and stood a long moment outside his own door, listening. No boots above. No breathing behind the wall. The city dripped and settled around him, and somewhere far beneath it all — under the palace, under the foundations, under the whole bright lie of the kingdom — something patient turned in its sleep, fed.

He let himself in. Banked coals. The kettle, still faintly warm. He crossed the room, knelt, and pulled the loose brick.

Wren looked up at him from the dark, green eyes catching the firelight like water catching a lamp. She read his face the way she read everything — whole, at once, without words — and her small hand came out and closed around his wrist, right over the pulse, the way she'd learned to check him the way he checked cups.

He held up one finger. *Wait.* Then he pointed at the window, at the north dark beyond it, where the road ran out of the city toward the hills and the river and the last sanctuary of the Thorn. He held up three fingers, then folded them down one by one. *Three days. Then we're gone.*

Wren studied him. Then, slowly, she smiled — and it was the first time he had ever seen her do it, and it went through him like a blade through bread, clean and warm and ruinous. She raised her own hand and pressed it flat against her chest, over her heart, and then pressed it flat against his.

*Together,* said the gesture, in the language they had built between them out of nothing.

Quentin began, quietly, to pack.

He had until the bootmaker's green awning, until the third dawn, until the lie he'd told with his eyes curdled into the truth Vane would hunt him for. Three days was nothing. Three days was a river crossing, a border, a lifetime.

Outside, far off toward the palace, a bell rang the hour, and Quentin Blackwood — royal food-taster, illegal charm-seller, and as of tonight, the most dangerous liar in the kingdom — counted the days he had left, and started spending them.
