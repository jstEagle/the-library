## 13. Sanctuary Road

The road north began as a rumor and ended as a scar.

Quentin had planned their leaving the way he planned everything — in small, deniable pieces. A sack of dried plums bought from three different vendors so no one seller could name him. A change of clothes for Wren that made her look like a boy, hood pulled low over those impossible green eyes. Two days' head start purchased with a forged writ of passage bearing the seal of a customs officer who owed Quentin more than his life; the man had once eaten at a table where Quentin's tongue had stood between him and death, though he didn't know it.

The writ would buy them two days. Perhaps three. Then Vane's hunters would come, and they would come fast, because Lord Chamberlain Aldous Vane did not forgive debts and did not forget faces.

"Keep your eyes down," Quentin murmured as they passed through the north gate in the gray hour before dawn, when the watch was changing and every soldier's mind was on his bed. "If anyone speaks to you, cough. Children are allowed to be sick."

Wren looked up at him from beneath the hood. She didn't cough. She pressed her small hand against his sleeve — she had begun doing that, these last weeks, a touch instead of an answer — and walked on.

They were four miles past the walls when she stopped in the middle of the road, tilted her head like a bird hearing something underground, and tugged his hand twice.

"What is it?"

She pointed back the way they'd come. Quentin saw nothing: hedgerows, wet grass, the smudge of the city behind them. But then he felt it too — not with his ears but with the place behind his sternum where his charms lived, the little woven knots of magic he'd sold for copper and kept for survival. They hummed. All of them at once, faintly, like struck glass.

Riders. Far off. Coming.

He took her hand and left the road.

* * *

Wren knew the world by its warmth.

That was the first thing she understood about herself, or the first thing she let herself understand, standing in the high grass while the man-who-fed-her crouched beside her and listened to horses he couldn't yet hear. The world was full of fires. Small ones: rabbits in their burrows, a hawk turning above the field, worms turning the soil into gold-threaded dark. Large ones: the city behind them, a wound of cold iron and hot fear, ten thousand little flames shuttered behind stone. And the riders — the riders burned wrong. Their fire was banked and hard, like coals wrapped in leather, and one of them carried something else, something old and hungry that made the ground remember being burned.

She pulled the man's hand. *This way.* She didn't have the words for it in his language — she barely had words in any language that lived — but she had the Thorn, and the Thorn had ways of saying things that needed no voice. She pushed the knowing through her palm into his skin: *left, along the water, under the willows.*

He flinched. Not from pain. From surprise. He looked at his own hand as if it had spoken.

"You felt that," she thought at him, and saw him feel it again, the shape of her meaning arriving in his head like a memory he hadn't lived. His face did something complicated — fear and wonder fighting for the same ground — and then he nodded once and turned left, toward the water, and she loved him a little for not asking questions.

The man-who-fed-her. She had no other name for him yet. Names were dangerous things in her mouth; the last time she had spoken a true word aloud, whole hillsides had leaned toward her, and men with iron nets had come out of the smoke. So she held her names close, all of them, even his, especially his, warm as a coal inside her chest.

But she was tired of holding them.

That night, in the hollow beneath a fallen oak where he built a fire no larger than a cupped hand, she sat across from him and studied his face in the light. He had lines around his eyes like a man who spent years watching doors. His hands were careful hands. When he gave her food he always gave her the larger share and pretended not to notice doing it.

She reached across the fire and touched his wrist, where the pulse beat.

"Teach me your words," she said.

It came out broken, half in the old speech, half in his, and the fire flared green for a heartbeat — the Thorn leaking through her, delighted — but she said it. Her first sentence in the living tongue. His eyes went wide and wet in the firelight.

"That's my line," he whispered, and laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle, and she understood without understanding why that no one had taught him things in a very long time either.

So they began.

* * *

They traded languages the way starving people trade bread — carefully, gratefully, a piece at a time.

By day they moved: along the river first, then up into the hill country where the farms thinned and the roads went to rutted track and the track went to nothing. By night, in whatever shelter they found — hay barns, ruined shrines, the lee side of drystone walls — they sat knee to knee and taught each other.

Quentin learned fast, faster than he expected, and understood why on the third night when Wren corrected his pronunciation of the word for *water* and the stream beside them gurgled, briefly, in answer.

"The words aren't just words," he said slowly. "When I say them right, something... listens."

Wren nodded, solemn as a judge. She tapped his chest, then the ground, then the dark sky above the trees, and said a long soft phrase in the Old Tongue. He caught only fragments: *root*, *under*, *all-one-root*. Everything grew from the same deep place. The language wasn't a tool; it was a taproot. Speak truly, and you touched the thing all growing things shared.

"My grandmother sang fragments of this," he told her, one night, surprising himself. "Lullabies. I always thought they were just nonsense sounds from her village." He hummed four notes, halting, ashamed of how badly he remembered. Wren went absolutely still. Then she sang them back completed — a whole line, a melody like water finding its way downhill — and watched him with an expression he couldn't name until he could: recognition. The look of someone handing you back a piece of yourself you hadn't known was missing.

After that, he stopped thinking of her as a burden he was carrying north. She was carrying something too. Between them, they might actually arrive.

* * *

On the sixth day the hunters found their trail.

Quentin smelled the trouble before he saw it: woodsmoke where there should have been no camp, and under it the bitter-green stink he now knew too well, the tithe-scent, the poison-note. Someone ahead of them on the track was burning charm-work — hunter's charms, tracking charms, the kind he himself had once sold to men with hard eyes and never asked what they hunted.

He pulled Wren off the track into a stand of birches and knelt, thinking. Behind them, the road south. Ahead, at least one tracker working the same path they needed. Above them, open hillside. His charms hummed their low warning chorus.

"We can't go around," he breathed. "Not with daylight left. And we can't go back."

Wren tugged his sleeve. She pointed at the birches, then at themselves, then made a gesture he didn't understand — hands closing, opening, like something blooming.

"No," he said. "Whatever you're thinking, no. We stay hidden. We are quiet people. Quiet is our whole profession."

She stared at him with the patience of the very young and the very certain, and then she spoke — one word, low, in the Old Tongue, aimed at the birch trees.

The birches answered.

It was subtle, thank all the buried gods. The white trunks didn't move, exactly. But the light among them changed, deepened, greened, and the stand of trees seemed to fold itself a half-step closer around them, and the sound of the wind through the leaves rearranged itself into something that was almost — almost — speech. *Here,* the trees said, in the way trees say anything, which is to say in the way water says *down*: simply, inevitably, without argument. *Nothing here but trees.*

Quentin's skin crawled and sang at once. He had spent five years selling magic in thimbles, magic small enough to hide in a palm, and here was a child of eight pouring it out like spring flood, casually, the way other children whistled.

The tracker passed within thirty feet of them. Quentin saw him clearly through the altered light: a lean man in riding leathers, a hound straining at his fist, the hound's nose working the air and finding — nothing. Nothing but trees. The man cursed softly and moved on down the track.

Wren exhaled, swayed, and Quentin caught her before she hit the ground. The cost, then. Magic like that drew from somewhere, and the somewhere was her.

"You don't spend yourself like that," he said fiercely, holding her up, terrified in a way he hadn't been even at the gate, even with riders behind them. "You hear me? Not for me. Never for me."

She looked up at him, pale, exhausted, and smiled — the first true smile he'd seen on her — and said, in careful, accented, newly-learned trading-speech:

"You spent *yourself* for king. Five year. I know." She tapped his chest, right over the hum of his charms, right over the slow poison his body had learned to carry. "Now I spend for you. Fair."

He had no answer to that. He carried her to shelter and sat awake long after she slept, listening to the birches settle back into being merely trees, and understood that whatever this was between them — rescuer and rescued, teacher and student — it had quietly become something else. Something with a name he was afraid to say even inside his own head, because everything he had ever named had been taken from him.

*Family*, said the word anyway, stubborn as a root. *You're building family, you fool.*

* * *

They fell into a rhythm, and the rhythm became a home they carried with them.

Mornings: he woke first, made tea from pine needles and whatever berries Wren approved with a nod or vetoed with a wrinkle of her nose — she was infallible about plants, and he learned quickly never to argue. Middays: movement, always movement, the sanctuary lying somewhere north beyond the hill country, past the river country, a place Mother Ilsa had described only as *"where the bramble remembers."* Evenings: lessons.

The lessons were his favorite part, though he'd have died before admitting it.

Wren taught the way rain teaches stone — patiently, endlessly, without any expectation of gratitude. She'd say a word and make him find it in the world. *Aelith* — she'd point at the moss on the north side of the wall. *Sorren* — the fox that watched them from the ridge each dusk. *Vael* — and she'd press her hand flat over his heart, because there was no English word for vael, only the feeling of it: the warmth of a thing tended. A kept flame. What you built around another person to keep the cold out.

"Vael," he repeated, hand over hers.

"Vael," she agreed, satisfied, and stole his tea.

He taught her his language in return, and discovered she hoarded words like coins. She liked the unnecessary ones best — *petrichor*, *gloaming*, *lambent* — rolling them around her mouth in the dark like sweets. One night she asked him, gravely, what his mother had called him when he was small, and he realized with a lurch that he couldn't remember ever telling her his name, and that she must have known it all along regardless, the way she seemed to know everything, and that no one had asked him that question in twenty years.

"Quentin," he said. "Just Quentin. My mother died when I was six."

Wren considered this with terrible eight-year-old thoroughness. Then she said a word in the Old Tongue he hadn't learned yet, soft and round, and when he raised his eyebrows she translated with great care:

"It means: *the ones gone are not gone. They go down to the root. In spring, they come up green in other mouths.*"

The fire popped. Quentin looked away and busied himself with the teapot and did not trust his voice for some time.

* * *

On the eleventh night, the dreams started — both of them, the same dream, at the same hour.

Quentin dreamed of a garden under a palace. Black bramble grown through marble halls, vast and patient, its heart a knot of green fire pulsing like a second sun buried in the earth. And the bramble was *hungry* — fed, lately, fed on purpose, tithe by tithe — and it was dreaming too, dreaming of a war old enough that kingdoms were the ash it had left behind. In the dream, something spoke his name in the Old Tongue, and the name it used was not Quentin.

He woke gasping. Across the dead fire, Wren was already sitting up, eyes wide and shining green in the starlight, her lips moving around words he couldn't hear.

"You felt it," he whispered.

She nodded. Then she held up one finger — *wait* — pressed her palm to the earth, and closed her eyes. Long moments. Her brow furrowed. When she opened her eyes again, she was frightened, and Wren frightened was the most frightening thing Quentin had yet seen in this long flight.

She spoke slowly, translating as she went, choosing his words like stepping stones:

"It wakes faster now. They feed it more." Her jaw tightened. "And it knows I'm coming. It—" she stopped, searched, tried again. "It isn't angry that I'm coming. It's *glad*."

"Why glad?"

She looked at him for a long moment, and he watched her decide how much truth he could carry.

"Because it thinks," she said finally, "that I'm bringing it a door."

Far to the south, faint as a memory, his charms began to hum — and this time they did not stop.
