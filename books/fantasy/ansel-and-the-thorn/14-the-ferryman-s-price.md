## 14. The Ferryman's Price

The river announced itself before we saw it — a low gray hush beneath the birdsong, the smell of wet stone and silt that had been moving since before the first king drew his first border. Wren felt it too. She stopped on the rutted road, tilted her head like a bird catching a far-off sound, and tugged my sleeve twice. Twice meant *hurry*. Once meant *wait*. Three times, pressed hard and fast, meant *danger*, and I had learned to dread her small hand more than any horn or hound.

She tugged twice.

"Hungry?" I asked, though I knew better. She shook her head and pointed down the slope, through the alders, to where the Serein ran wide and slow and silver in the late-afternoon light. The ferry crossing at Marlow's Bend was nothing but a rope strung between two leaning posts, a flat barge scarred with forty years of hooves and cartwheels, and a hut where the ferryman lived with his dogs and his debts. Smoke rose from its chimney. A good sign. A living man would take us across for coin, and coin I still had, sewn into the lining of a coat that had once tasted court dinners and now smelled of woodsmoke and fear.

We had come north for nine days. Nine days of stolen bread and sleeping in hay barns, of Wren teaching me words under her breath while I watched the road behind us — words that sat strangely in my mouth, old as grave-dirt and warm as embers. *Saelith*, she'd say, touching a green leaf. *Voren*, pressing her palm to my chest, over the heart. I did not know what most of them meant. I only knew that when she spoke them, the little charms stitched into my coat lining hummed like struck glass, and something in me that had been numb for five years — numbed by design, numbed by duty, numbed by the king's own poisoned table — stirred and ached like a limb waking.

I had sold charms for copper in the night markets of Corvale for five years. Small things. A charm to keep milk sweet. A charm to turn aside a drunkard's fist. Nothing that would hang me, if I was careful, and I had been so very careful. Careful men do not flee north with the last speaker of an exterminated tongue tucked under their arm. Careful men do not feel their whole life narrowing to a single river crossing.

"Stay behind me," I told Wren, and she gave me one of her looks — the ones that said, without any language at all, *you are small and I am not afraid of the same things you are*.

The ferryman was a broad, gray-bearded man mending a net on his porch, and he looked up as we came down the bank with the flat appraisal of a man who has ferried every kind of trouble and been paid by most of it.

"Two for the crossing," I said. "And quiet about it."

"Quiet costs double."

I paid it. He counted the coins with thick fingers, then jerked his chin toward the barge. "Wind's against you till dusk. We wait." He went back to his net, and I sat Wren on a bollard and bought smoked fish from him because hunger is the one enemy you can actually feed.

That was when the dogs started.

Not barking — that low, rising whine that dogs give when they smell something they cannot name. The ferryman's three hounds came off their chain-line and stood at the tree line, hackles up, facing south along the road we had not yet finished leaving.

Wren's hand found mine. Three tugs, hard and fast.

I turned. Two riders had come out of the alder shadow, walking their horses, unhurried, which was worse than a gallop. Men who gallop are chasing. Men who walk already know they have you. The nearer one wore no livery, but his horse wore palace shoes — I had spent enough years around the royal stables to know the particular wide-set stamp of a king's farrier — and the sword at his hip was palace steel, plain and well-kept. The other rider was younger, and he held a crossbow laid casually across his saddlebow, casual as a man holding a conversation.

"Taster," the first rider called, and my name died in my throat, because it wasn't my name. It was my trade. "Quentin Blackwood. Lord Vane sends his regards."

Every plan I had made in nine days of flight — every route, every fallback, every lie rehearsed in the dark — emptied out of me at once. What remained was Wren's hand in mine, and the river at our backs, and the strange new warmth under my breastbone that had been growing since the night she first spoke at my door.

"You've made a mistake," I said. "I'm traveling alone."

"We saw two sets of prints at the mill ford." He dismounted, easy, a man talking to a neighbor over a fence. "Small feet. And there's a bounty posted in every garrison from here to the capital. Green-eyed child, eight years, doesn't speak. Doesn't need to, does she? Not for what she is." His eyes moved past me to Wren, and there was something in them I recognized from the night market — the look of a buyer appraising goods. "Lord Vane wants her alive. You, he says, we can discuss."

"Discuss," I repeated.

"He's a fair man. For a traitor who sells filth to peasants, he'll let you keep your head if you hand her over. That's fairer than you'd get from the magistrates." He took another step. Behind him, the crossbowman leveled his weapon, not at me — at Wren, and my blood went cold in a way it never had at the king's table, tasting death a spoonful at a time. "Come now. You're a sensible man. Five years selling charms and never caught — that's sense, that is. Be sensible one more time."

And here is the thing I have never told anyone, the thing I barely admitted to myself: some part of me wanted to be sensible. Some tired, cowardly, five-years-numb part of me wanted to hand over the burden, keep my head, go back to the city and the night stalls and the small safe crimes. I had built my whole life out of half-measures. Half-truths. Half-magic, hidden in coat linings, deniable, survivable.

Then Wren stepped out from behind me.

She didn't hide. She planted herself at my side, small and straight-backed, and looked up at the hunter with those eyes like spring sap, and she spoke — three words in the Old Tongue, clear as a bell over the water, and every charm in my coat sang aloud, audible, unmistakable, a chord of pure ringing light-noise that made the horses scream and rear.

The crossbow loosed. Reflex, panic — the bolt flew wild, high, splintering against a post.

And the warmth under my breastbone became a fire.

I did not decide it. That is the truth I will carry, whatever I am afterward: I did not choose it the way a man chooses to lie or steal. It chose the moment, and I only opened the door. Five years of the tithe-poison sat in my blood like seed in winter soil, and Wren's voice was the rain, and everything the crown had fed me to make me a tool — everything that should have killed me slowly — stood up inside me and answered her.

I raised my hand, and the river heard me.

I said a word I did not know I knew — *saelith*, water, leaf, the green going-things — and the Serein rose.

Not a wave. Not a wall. The river simply stood up along the bank, all along the bend, a curtain of brown silver water hanging in the air like a held breath, and through it the afternoon sun broke into a thousand trembling rainbows. The hunters' horses bolted. The crossbowman fired again and the bolt stopped — stopped, quivering, caught in the water's skin as if the river had decided it was done being crossed by such things.

"Get to the barge," I said, and my voice came out doubled, layered, mine and something older underneath, and it frightened me more than the riders did.

The ferryman was on his knees with his hat off, muttering prayers to gods whose names were banned in three kingdoms. I hauled the barge free of its mooring with a gesture — *voren*, home, heart, hold — and the rope unknotted itself like a patient serpent, and Wren scrambled aboard and pulled me after her by the sleeve, twice, hard: *hurry*.

The first rider found his courage and his blade and charged the waterline, shouting something about the king and treason, and I brought the river down.

It fell across the bank like a judgment, not drowning him — I felt, distantly, the magic asking me whether I wanted it to drown him, and I said no, no, not that, never that — but taking his horse's legs out and pinning him in the shallows, gasping, his sword floating away beside him. The crossbowman fled. I heard his horse crash away south through the alders, and I understood, even as I pushed us out into the current, that this was the part that mattered. Not the pinned man. The runner. The witness. The palace rider who had seen a royal food-taster stand up on a riverbank and speak the forbidden tongue and raise the Serein like a banner.

There was no coming back from that. No clever lie, no half-measure, no careful life to return to. Somewhere behind us a man was riding south with a story that would empty the garrisons, and every bridge and ford and ferry from here to the sanctuary would grow teeth.

I stood at the stern as the barge swung into midstream, and I waited to feel ruined.

Instead — and this shames me even now, even after everything — I felt *light*. The numbness was gone. All five years of it, gone, as if the river had carried it off along with the bolt and the bounty and the name Quentin Blackwood, food-taster, seller of small charms, keeper of half-measures. My hands were shaking, but they were *mine*, fully mine, for the first time since I'd knelt and sworn the taster's oath. I had used real magic, openly, against the crown's own men, and the sky had not fallen. The sky had simply gone on being blue above the standing water, and I had gone on being alive inside it.

Wren tugged my sleeve. Once. *Wait.* Then she pointed at my chest, and said a word I hadn't learned yet, soft and wondering, and pressed her small palm flat over my heart where the fire still smoldered.

"What does that mean?" I asked.

She considered the question with the gravity of an archivist, then reached down, scooped a handful of river water, and let it pour through her fingers back into the Serein. Then she touched my chest again. Same word.

*What flows out cannot be taken back.*

"That's not comforting," I said, and she smiled — the first true smile she'd given anyone, I think, since her village — and behind us the pinned hunter crawled out of the shallows onto the mud, alive, and knelt there watching us go, and I knew exactly what he was watching us for. Not to stop us. To remember us. To carry the description south: height, coat, the color of the light around his hands.

The ferryman recovered himself enough to pole us across, hands trembling on the shaft, refusing his coins back when I offered. "Keep it," he whispered. "Keep it and don't tell the river I saw you." As if the river were mine. As if I could command it to forget him. Perhaps, now, I could. That thought frightened me more than the riders had.

Dusk came down purple on the far bank. We climbed into the hills north of Marlow's Bend, Wren riding my shoulders, her chin on top of my head, both of us watching the road below. An hour past full dark, we saw it: a single rider coming down to the crossing at a hard gallop, torchlight bobbing, the palace stamp flashing on the horse's shoes as it wheeled toward the ferryman's hut.

The witness, reporting. Or worse — the beginning of the answer, riders pouring north along every road, and somewhere behind them, in a lamplit office in the capital, Lord Aldous Vane setting down his wine and smiling his thin smile and saying, *so the taster has finally shown us what he is.*

Wren's arms tightened around my neck. She said one word against my hair, in the Old Tongue, and this one I knew, because she had taught it to me on the third night, pointing north at the stars:

*Sael-voren.* Home-going. Onward.

"Yes," I said, and my breath steamed in the cold, and it smelled faintly of green things, of sap, of spring. "Onward."

We walked into the hills, and behind us the torches multiplied along the southern roads like sparks blown from a fire — and ahead of us, days north, the last sanctuary of the Thorn waited with a price I could not yet imagine.
