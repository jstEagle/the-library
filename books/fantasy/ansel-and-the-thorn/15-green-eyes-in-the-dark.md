## 15. Green Eyes in the Dark

The tremor came at the third hour past midnight, and Lord Chamberlain Aldous Vane felt it in his teeth before he felt it in the floor.

He was awake because he was always awake now — sleep had become a country he could no longer afford to visit — standing at the window of his chambers in the east wing with a cup of untouched wine going bitter in his hand. The palace slept around him in its usual architecture of snores and shifting bedclothes, and beneath it, beneath all of it, something turned over.

Not an earthquake. Earthquakes did not come as a single slow exhalation, like a sleeper rolling toward the edge of a dream. The wine in his cup shivered into concentric rings. Somewhere below, in the deep cellars where even the under-stewards did not go, he heard — or imagined he heard — a sound like roots growing through stone. A creak of immense patience.

Vane set the cup down on the sill and pressed his palm flat against the cold glass until his heartbeat steadied.

*This is what you were promised,* he told himself. *Power has a sound. Power has a weight. You are hearing the sound of your own elevation.*

The words had worked for months. They worked less well each night.

* * *

In the morning, the reports began.

They arrived the way bad news always arrived at court: dressed as trivia, carried by men who would not meet his eye. The royal gardener's complaint that the kitchen-garden lettuces had blackened overnight, every head in three rows, as though scalded from below. The well-master's note that the water in the eastern wells tasted of iron and old leaves. A mason's petition about hairline cracks spidering up the foundation wall of the Chapel Royal — cracks that ran, the man had marked with charcoal in a fit of professional horror, not randomly, but in patterns. Branching patterns. Like frost. Like veins.

Like roots.

Vane read each report twice, burned them in the brazier one by one, and watched the ash curl upward instead of down.

"You look tired, my lord."

Ser Roderick Hale stood in the doorway of his study, captain of the chamber guard, a broad honest face that Vane had spent two years learning to use and was only now beginning to distrust. Hale carried a leather folio under one arm and the particular stiffness of a man delivering something he'd rather not.

"The king slept poorly," Vane said. It was not a question; it never was anymore. The king slept poorly every night, waking pale and hollow-eyed, eating less, speaking less, his lucid hours shrinking like a tide going out. The physicians called it a wasting humor. The tithe called it nothing at all, because the tithe did not care to be named.

"He asked for you," Hale said. "By name. That's the third time this week."

"Did he say why?"

"No, my lord. But he said it strange." Hale hesitated, and Vane watched the honesty war behind the man's eyes and win. "He said it in the old way, my lord. Words nobody speaks. I wrote them down as best I could."

He passed over a scrap of paper. On it, in Hale's careful soldier's hand, a string of syllables that meant nothing to the captain and everything — too much — to Vane, who had heard them once before, whispered by the faction's oldest member across a candlelit table while the thing beneath them had stirred for the first time: *the heart knows its feeder.*

Vane burned that paper too.

"Double the patrols," he said. "Walls, gates, the river stairs. And the bounty stands. Green eyes, eight years or thereabouts, traveling with a man of middle years. Fifty crowns for word of them. Two hundred for the child alive." He paused. "Unharmed. I want her unharmed, Hale. If she comes back with so much as a scratch, the man who scratched her will wish the Thorn had taken him instead."

"And the taster? Blackwood?"

"What of him?"

"He hasn't reported to the kitchens in six days. His lodgings stand empty. The neighbors say he left in the night with a bundle wrapped in his cloak."

Vane looked out the window at the garden where the lettuces had died, at the gardeners digging them out with the grim efficiency of men burying something. Six days. He had given Blackwood six days' grace because he had liked the man's hands — quick, deft, honest-seeming — and because a bargain made aloud was a bargain half-kept, and he had wanted to believe the taster would keep it.

"He's fled," Vane said. "Which means he was never ours, which means everything he sold me with those soft brown eyes was a lie wearing a lie." He heard his own voice come out flat and cold and was grateful for it. "Put his description in every garrison from here to the border. And find me Mother Ilsa's old catacomb keys. If they've gone to ground anywhere in this city, it will be beneath it."

Hale saluted and went. Vane remained at the window a long moment, watching a gardener shake dead lettuce roots free of the soil — roots that came up black and fibrous and far, far too long, trailing from the earth like drowned hair.

* * *

The faction met where it always met, in the sealed room beneath the Treasury annex, where the walls were granite older than the kingdom and the single table had been carved, generations ago, from wood no living forester could name. Seven chairs. Five occupied. The candlelight did strange things down here; it always had. Shadows pooled against the walls like standing water, and the air tasted faintly of iron and old leaves, and Vane had told himself for two years that this was simply how old rooms smelled.

Dowager Countess Maren sat at the head, as she always sat at the head, a small dry woman with rings on every finger and eyes like chips of slate. To her right, Chancellor Ossory, fat and pleasant and utterly without mercy. To her left, the one Vane thought of privately as the Priest, though he wore no vestments — a narrow grey man whose name Vane had never learned and had stopped asking after, because the Priest answered questions with questions, and the last time Vane had pressed him, the Priest had smiled and said, *"You'll learn my name when you need it, Aldous. Pray you never need it."*

"The tremors," Maren said, without preamble. "Report."

"Contained," Vane said. "Minor structural damage in the lower levels. The masons have been paid and sworn. The city believes we sit on old mine workings."

"The crops."

"Handled. Blight, we're calling it. A wet spring." He laid out the fiction piece by piece, smooth as dealing cards, and watched none of them blink, because none of them blinked; that was the first thing he had noticed about these people, years ago, before he understood what he had joined. They did not blink. "The king declines visibly. By month's end he will be unable to hold audience. The regency council will have no choice but to convene, and the council is ours."

"Good," said Ossory, dabbing his lips with a napkin he had brought for no reason, since no food was served down here and never had been. "Then we may speak of acceleration."

Vane kept his face still. "Acceleration."

"The tithe doubles," Maren said. "Beginning tonight. The doses are prepared."

Something in the room shifted when she said it — not the air, not the light. Something deeper. Vane felt it in the soles of his feet, a faint subsonic hum, and told himself again that old granite settled, that old buildings breathed.

"My lords," he said carefully, "the dosage was calibrated. The archivists' texts specify—"

"The texts specify what the texts specify." Maren's voice did not rise; it never rose. "The heart grows restless, Aldous. You've felt it. We've all felt it. It stirs because it starves, and a starving thing cannot be bound to our purpose. Feed it, and it sleeps deeper into our palm. That was always the design."

*Was it?* The question rose in him unbidden, treacherous, and he strangled it before it reached his face. Because here was the thing Vane had never said aloud, not to himself, not in the dark, not once in two years of service: he had never actually read the design. He had read excerpts. Summaries. Passages selected for him by the Priest's grey hands, in translations the Priest himself had vetted. He knew the tithe weakened the king. He knew the regency followed. He knew that the faction's power — *his* power, his elevation, his name carved eventually into the annals beside the great chamberlains of history — depended on the thing beneath them staying asleep and obedient.

He did not know what the thing was. Not truly. The texts spoke of a Thorn heart, a remnant of the pre-kingdom world, buried and bound by the founders. He had assumed a relic. A battery. Old magic banked like coin, waiting to be drawn upon by men clever enough to hold the ledger.

But the tremors were getting worse. And the crops had not merely blighted — the gardener's dead lettuces had come up with roots ten feet long, and roots grew *toward* things, and Vane had begun to dream, every night now, the same dream: green light far below, and a voice that did not speak in words but in hunger, patient as winter, saying *soon, soon, soon.*

"Lord Chamberlain." Maren's slate eyes rested on him. "You hesitate."

"I calculate," Vane said. "Doubling the tithe risks killing the king outright. A dead king gives us a succession crisis, not a regency. The northern dukes would—"

"The northern dukes are being handled." Ossory waved a ringed hand. "You worry about the wrong end of the knife, Aldous. Your task is the child."

"The child," Vane repeated.

"The speaker. The last of them." For the first time, the Priest spoke, and the room seemed to lean in around his voice the way shadows lean toward flame. "You understand what she is, don't you? You've read the passages we gave you. She is the key we do not possess. With her, the heart can be woken fully — bound to *our* bloodline, our command, generation upon generation. No more hiding in cellars. No more dosing kings drop by drop like apothecaries. A kingdom with a god under its floorboards answers very quickly when knocked upon."

*A god.* Vane had thought: a relic. A battery. The word *god* sat in his chest like swallowed ice.

"And if she binds it instead?" he heard himself ask. "The prophecy runs both ways. Your own texts say so. A speaker may wake the heart or bind it forever. If Blackwood reaches the sanctuary—"

"Blackwood," said Maren, "is one frightened tradesman with parlor tricks. The sanctuary is a ruin guarded by three old women. And you, Aldous, are letting a child outrun the entire apparatus of the crown." She folded her small dry hands. "I chose you because you do not flinch. Prove the choice sound. Double the hunt. Every road, every river, every hedge-witch and hedge-priest between here and the Thornlands. I want the girl in this room within the fortnight."

"And if the doubled tithe kills the king first?"

Maren smiled. It was the first time Vane had ever seen her do it, and it was worse than anything the thing below had shown him in his dreams.

"Then we accelerate further," she said. "Grief makes excellent mortar, my lord. Now go. You have a kingdom to search."

* * *

He walked back through the palace alone, past midnight, torches guttering in brackets, his footsteps ringing on stone that had begun, he was almost certain, to feel warm.

That was the detail he could not put down. Warmth. The flagstones of the lower corridors, which had been cold as a crypt for four hundred years, held a faint heat now, like ground above a deep fire. The servants had started leaving offerings — he'd found a saucer of milk and a crust of honey-bread on a cellar step two nights ago, arranged with the furtive devotion of people who would deny it under torture. Superstition, he'd told himself. Servants' nonsense.

But the servants had been here longer than he had. The servants remembered things the archives had been ordered to forget.

In his chambers he poured wine he did not drink and stood again at the window, and below him the city lay quiet under a sickly moon, and beyond the walls the fields stretched north — the fields where the wheat had come up grey-headed in a single week, where farmers were already muttering about cursed ground, where somewhere on some road a frightened man and a green-eyed girl were running toward the one place in the world that might undo everything the faction had built.

Two hundred crowns. Every garrison. Every road. He had given the order and meant it, and yet.

And yet he found his mind returning, with the persistence of a tongue finding a broken tooth, to the moment in the sealed room when Maren had smiled. To the word *god*, sitting in his chest like ice. To the warmth rising through the floors, gentle as a hearth, patient as a snare — the warmth of a thing being fed, and fed, and fed, by men who believed themselves its masters.

He had wanted a name in the annals. He had wanted power with a floor beneath it, solid stone, permanent. Instead he was beginning to suspect he had spent two years climbing a staircase built inside the throat of something vast, and that every rung had been a rib, and that the door at the top opened inward.

Far below, so faint he might have dreamed it, the palace sighed — a long, slow, root-deep exhalation — and in the fields beyond the walls, every crow rose off the battlements at once, all together, silent, wheeling north.

Toward the sanctuary. Toward the bramble. Toward the taster and the child.

Vane watched them go until the sky was empty, then rang for his secretary and said, in a voice he did not recognize as his own:

"Wake the stable master. I'm riding out myself at dawn."
