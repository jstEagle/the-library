## 16. The Sanctuary of Thorns

The road stopped being a road three days before we found the sanctuary.

It narrowed to a cart track, then to a footpath, then to a suggestion — a seam of worn earth threading through pine forest so old and dark that noon felt like dusk. Wren walked ahead of me the whole way, and I noticed she never once hesitated at a fork. She couldn't have known this country. No one could have; the maps ended a hundred miles south with a warning in faded ink: *Here be the Purging's edge.* But her feet knew. When the path bent, she was already bending. When it crossed a stream on stones slick with moss, she stepped where the stones wanted to be stepped on.

"You've been here before," I said, though I knew she hadn't.

She looked back at me and touched her chest, then pointed north, then made a motion like water flowing downhill. *It pulls.* That was all her hands could say. Her voice still lived somewhere behind her silence, surfacing only in that language that made my charms ache against my skin like old scars in rain.

By the fourth day the forest itself began to change. The pines thinned. The undergrowth went strange — ferns the size of cartwheels, moss that glowed faintly green after sundown, trees whose bark had split and healed in patterns too regular to be accident. And everywhere, threading through it all like veins under skin, bramble. Black canes as thick as my wrist, arcing overhead, diving into earth, rising again. Thorned, but not viciously so. They grew *deliberately*, the way a wall is built rather than the way weeds happen.

Wren stopped walking so suddenly I nearly ran into her back.

Ahead, the forest ended. Not gradually — it simply stopped, cut off along a clean curve perhaps two hundred yards wide, and beyond it rose the sanctuary.

I had imagined ruins. I think we both had: broken towers, a chapel roof fallen in, the usual bones of the old world that the Purging had left scattered across the north. What stood before us instead was alive. A fortress of bramble, if you could call it that — walls of woven black cane forty feet high, dense as ironwood, crowned with thorns longer than my forearm and gleaming dully like oiled steel. It curved away on both sides until the forest swallowed it. There was no gate. There was no door. There was only the wall, breathing — because it *was* breathing. I could see it now that I looked properly: the whole vast structure swelled and eased, slow as a sleeping animal, and a smell came off it that I will remember until I die, warm and green and bitter, like crushed stems and summer storms.

My charms were screaming. Not audibly — they were cheap things, copper and bone and knotted thread — but every one of them pulled toward the wall at once, hard enough to drag the cord at my neck taut. Five years I'd worn them without feeling anything but their small dishonest warmth. Now they strained like dogs on leashes.

"They remember this place," I said stupidly. "They're *drawn* to it."

Wren wasn't listening. She had gone very still, her green eyes enormous, fixed on the wall. And then she did something that turned my blood to ice water: she smiled. Wide and unguarded, the first smile I'd seen on her since the night market, since before any of it. She took my hand — hers small and calloused and cold — and walked straight at the bramble.

"Wren—"

The wall opened.

There was no gate swinging, no canes parting with a crack. The opening was simply *there* when we reached it, a corridor through the living blackness, walled smooth as polished ebony, lit from within by a faint green luminescence seeping out of the canes themselves. Warm air breathed out over us, carrying that stem-and-storm smell, and beneath it something older — dust and honey and, very faintly, smoke. As if a fire had happened here once, long ago, and the place had never quite finished grieving it.

We walked in. Behind us, the way closed like water closing over a stone.

* * *

Inside, the sanctuary was a grove pretending to be a building.

Corridors of bramble branched off the entry passage, some narrow as alleys, some opening into vaulted chambers where the canes had grown together into ceilings dripped with pale flowers that hadn't yet opened. Light came from everywhere and nowhere, that same green glow, soft enough not to cast shadows. We passed rooms furnished in living wood — benches grown from the floor, shelves, a table still wearing its bark — and I understood that people had lived here, had shaped this place the way you'd train an orchard, and that they had done it with a patience no kingdom I'd ever served possessed.

And then we met the wardens.

I heard them before I saw them: a slow triple rhythm of footsteps, uneven, approaching down a side passage. Three figures emerged into the green light, and my hand went to the knife I no longer had any business reaching for.

They were old. Not elderly — *old* the way the forest was old, the way the language Wren spoke was old. The first was a woman tall as a door, brown-skinned and white-haired, dressed in robes that might have been woven from the bramble itself, its thorns turned outward like a crown of nails. The second was a man so bent he seemed folded in half, his beard trailing near the ground, his eyes milky and blind and somehow seeing everything. The third — the third barely registered as human at first. It was small, child-sized, and gray as ash, with a face smooth and featureless except for two closed eyes, and it moved with a gliding wrongness that made my skin crawl before my mind caught up and understood: this one was not flesh. Or had not been, for a very long time.

"Two come walking," said the tall woman. Her voice was deep and unhurried, like something said over a grave. "One who tastes. One who speaks."

"You know why we're here," I managed.

"We know why the *child* is here." The blind man tilted his head toward Wren, and his ruined eyes glistened. "The question is why you are, taster. The wall does not open for the curious. It opened for her. You came through on her coat-tails, like a burr."

"I came through because she'd walk in alone otherwise."

Something moved across the tall woman's face — not quite approval, but the ghost of it. "Then you are already more use than most men who reach this door." She turned. "Come. The heart will want to see her."

"The heart," I repeated. "You mean it's real. The thing under the palace — the thing they're feeding—"

"All in time." She raised one long finger. "First, the testing. No speaker enters the inner grove unproven. Not even the last one."

*The last one.* The words landed in my stomach like stones. Ilsa had told us what Wren might be, on that night in the catacombs among the ash-eaten books: the final speaker of the Old Tongue, the last voice the Thorn would ever hear. But hearing it from Ilsa, half-mad and whispering, had been theory. Hearing it from these three, in this place, made it weight.

Wren, for her part, showed no fear. She walked between the wardens as if she'd been expected all her life — which, I suppose, she had.

* * *

The testing took place in a chamber deep in the sanctuary's core, circular, its ceiling lost in flowering darkness. The floor was bare earth. At its center grew a single bramble cane, no thicker than my thumb, rising out of the soil and stopping at the height of a child's head. It looked, absurdly, like a candle waiting to be lit.

"Stand there," the tall woman said to Wren, indicating the cane. Then, to me: "You. Against the wall. Whatever happens, do not touch her, do not speak your gutter-charms, and do not run. Running is worse than useless."

"What happens?"

"The past asks her a question," said the blind man, settling himself onto the earth with the groaning care of extreme age. "If her answer is true, the past will know its own."

The gray one said nothing. It never did. It simply positioned itself at the chamber's far side, folded its small hands, and opened its eyes — which were white, and depthless, and fixed on Wren with an attention that made the air feel thinner.

Wren looked at me. One glance, quick, checking that I was where I'd promised to be. I nodded. She turned to the little cane and waited.

The tall woman began to speak.

It was the Old Tongue — I knew that much now, after weeks of Wren's hands and Ilsa's fragments and the king's broken whispering at his own table. But where Wren's speaking sounded like birdsong and breaking ice, the warden's voice made the words feel like weather. The syllables rolled out of her low and rhythmic, a chant or a summons, and as she spoke the chamber changed. The green light dimmed. The earthen floor darkened as if shadow poured up out of it. And images began to move in that darkness — not quite visions, not quite reflections, but memories with nowhere else to live.

I saw fire. Vast, horizon-wide fire, and above it shapes like torn banners wheeling in a smoke-black sky. I saw armies whose banners bore the thorn sigil meeting armies whose banners bore the sun-and-crown, and between them the land itself rising up in revolt — roots splitting roads, forests marching, rivers running backward. I saw a city burn that I recognized with a sick lurch: the capital, younger by centuries, its palace spires snapping like kindling. I saw the Purging not as history books told it — a necessary cleansing, a triumph of order — but as what it actually was. A slaughter. Mages dragged from their homes. Children with green eyes put to the sword for the crime of speaking to plants. And beneath it all, underneath everything, a pulse: slow, immense, patient. A heartbeat in the deep places of the world, wounded and furious and waiting.

The heartbeat filled the chamber. I felt it in my teeth. In the charms against my chest, which had gone hot enough to scorch.

And Wren answered.

She lifted her chin and spoke back into the darkness — fluently, effortlessly, in a voice that *worked*, that carried, that made the air shiver. I didn't understand a word. I understood all of it. The images flared brighter wherever her voice touched them: the burning city steadied, the wheeling shapes paused, and that terrible pulse below the world slowed — slowed — and seemed, impossibly, to listen.

She spoke for a long time. About what, I couldn't say. Perhaps about herself. Perhaps about the dead. Perhaps, knowing her, about the taste of pears and the sound of rain on rooftops in a city that hung people like me. When she finished, the darkness drained back into the floor, the light returned, and the single cane at the chamber's center did something none of us had asked of it.

It flowered. One white blossom, unfolding at its tip, perfect as a held breath.

The blind man began, very quietly, to weep.

"She is worthy," the tall woman said. Her voice had roughened. "More than worthy. The heart has been asleep for four hundred years, and it just turned over in its sleep to hear her." She looked at me then, and everything in her face had changed — the gravity of it, the pity. "Come outside, taster. You and I need to talk about what happens next."

"Why do I need to hear it?"

"Because," she said, "it concerns you."

* * *

We stood in an open court where the bramble walls fell away and the sky showed through, gray and cold. The other two wardens remained inside with Wren. I could hear her voice faintly through the living walls, still speaking, softer now — telling the old ones, maybe, everything that had happened since the world forgot them. The tall woman, whose name I finally learned was Sera, watched me with those grave dark eyes.

"There are two parts to the binding rite," she said. "You should understand them fully before we go further, because Ilsa's books would have given you only half."

"A blooded speaker," I said. "That's Wren. The heart has to be spoken to in the old language, bound in the old words. Ilsa said as much."

"Yes. The speaker opens the door. But a door means nothing, taster, if nothing stands in it." Sera clasped her hands behind her back. "The Thorn heart cannot be bound from outside. It must be bound *through* a vessel — a living body placed between the speaker's voice and the heart's fury, so that when the binding comes, it passes through flesh and is anchored there. The vessel holds the door shut from within. Forever."

The word *forever* sat in the cold air between us.

"That sounds fatal," I said.

"It is fatal." She said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "The vessel doesn't die quickly. They become the lock. The heart's rage pours into them and the binding turns it to root and stone, and the vessel stands — or lies — at the threshold until the end of things. Some vessels lasted years. One, in the first age, lasted a century. But they never leave. They never wake. From the moment the rite completes, they belong to the door."

I looked at my hands. Taster's hands, scarred faintly at the fingertips from five years of needles and pricks, steady even now. "So find someone else. There must be others willing. Pilgrims. Believers. Anyone."

"There is no one else." For the first time, Sera's composure cracked — not into weakness, but into something like anger, ancient and tired. "Do you understand what saturation means? The heart is poison, boy. Tithe-poison, distilled and concentrated across centuries. Any ordinary body that touches the rite burns away in minutes — the vessel must be able to *hold* the poison while the binding works, must have been steeped in it, seasoned by it, until their blood knows its taste." She stepped closer. "Five years, Quentin Blackwood. Five years you have swallowed the tithe with the king's breakfast and walked away untouched. Your tongue is numb because your whole body has learned to carry what would kill any other man. Do you truly believe that was chance? That the crown's cruelty, the faction's poison, the slow murder they fed you daily — do you believe it wasn't, in some older accounting than theirs, *preparation*?"

The world tilted. I gripped the living wall beside me and the bramble canes shifted slightly, almost gently, under my palm — as if the sanctuary itself had been waiting to catch me.

All those mornings. All those bitter notes I alone could taste, swallowing them down with a professional's blank face while the king watched me for the flicker that meant death. I had thought myself lucky. Clever. Immune. I had built a life on the fact that the poison didn't take me.

It hadn't failed to take me. It had been *making* me.

"There has to be another way," I heard myself say.

"If there were, I would tell you. I have buried too many vessels to lie about this." Sera's voice gentled. "But hear the rest, because the rest matters. The rite requires two things, and only two. A blooded speaker — and Wren is that, proven today beyond any doubt. And a *willing* vessel. The binding cannot be forced. The heart would reject a chained man, a deceived man, a man brought to the door in terror. It opens only for someone who chooses it. Freely. With full knowledge." She held my gaze. "No one can make you do this, taster. Not the wardens, not the prophecy, not the girl who loves you. That is the mercy in it, and the horror. The choice is entirely yours."

Behind us, through the flowering dark of the walls, Wren's voice rose and fell, telling the old ones her story. And I stood in the cold gray light with my poisoned, seasoned, prepared blood, understanding at last what all my careful years had been building toward — and hearing, distantly, from the direction of the outer forest, the first horn of Lord Vane's hunters.

Sera heard it too. Her head turned toward the wall like a deer's.

"They are three days behind us," she said quietly. "Perhaps less. So whatever you choose, taster — choose soon."
