## 17. Riders at the Bramble

The thorns woke before the riders did.

Quentin felt it in his teeth first — a low hum, like a tuning fork struck against bone — and then in the soles of his feet where he stood on the sanctuary's living floor. The black bramble that walled the sanctuary had been still for three days, a sleeping cathedral of woven branches, and now every spine on it stood erect, quivering, aimed outward like ten thousand needles finding north.

"They're coming," Wren said.

She didn't say it in words. She never did, not fully. She pressed her palm flat against the bramble's inner face and the whole wall shivered, and Quentin understood the way one understands a dream: *horses. Iron. Fire in little clay pots. Many men who do not want to be here.*

"How many?" Ilsa asked from the doorway of the warden's hall, her gray braid whipping in a wind that had no source.

Wren held up both hands, fingers spread twice over. Twenty. Thirty. More behind them.

Quentin looked at the three wardens. They had not moved since dawn — the tall one called Sorrel with her bark-gray skin, the hunched one named Fen who had not spoken since their arrival, and the eldest, Mother Ashen, whose eyes were white as boiled eggs and who saw, everyone agreed, considerably more than anyone else present. Ashen stood at the center of the grove now, beside the shallow bowl of dark soil that marked the place where the Thorn heart slept beneath the world.

"The outer ring will hold for an hour," Sorrel said. Her voice was dry leaves. "Perhaps two if they are fools."

"And if they're not?"

"Then less." She turned her blind-seeming gaze on Quentin. "Taster. Come. There is something you must hear while there is still time to hate it."

* * *

Aldous Vane had commanded men through sieges, tax revolts, and one brief and ugly succession crisis, and he had learned long ago that fear was a currency best spent early. So he rode at the head of the column himself, resplendent in the Chamberlain's silver-and-black, and let his soldiers see him do it.

He regretted it immediately.

The road north had been wrong for two days. Crops in the fields they passed grew in spirals. Birds flew in circles until they dropped. And now, as the column wound up the last valley toward the sanctuary, the horses were screaming — not whinnying, *screaming*, a sound Vane had heard only once before, at the pit beneath the palace, the night the tithe had been increased and something far below had rolled over in its sleep.

"Steady the line," he said, and hated how his voice cracked on *steady*.

Captain Brann drew alongside. The man's face was the color of tallow. "My lord, the men are saying—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "They're saying the thorns are watching us."

Vane looked. He wished he hadn't. The bramble wall rose out of the valley floor like a wave frozen mid-break, forty feet of black wood and longer black spines, and though it had no eyes, no face, no anything a reasonable man could name, it *did* watch. He felt its attention pass over the column the way one feels sunlight through a window — warmth without comfort.

His masters had told him this would be simple. Ride north, burn a fortress of hedge-witches, collect a child. His masters, Vane was beginning to understand, had never actually come here themselves. His masters sat in a marble room above a buried thing and fed it king's blood through a golden straw and called it policy.

*And you carried the straw,* he reminded himself. *You measured the doses. You smiled at the king while his mind curdled.*

"My lord?" Brann again.

"Fire arrows on my mark," Vane said. "Burn the wall. Then we go through whatever's left."

It was, he reflected, exactly what a man without doubts would order.

* * *

Sorrel took Quentin to the eastern edge of the grove, where the bramble grew thin enough to see the valley through, and there she told him.

"You know what a vessel is," she said.

"A cup. Something that holds." He kept his eyes on the riders massing below. It was easier than looking at her.

"The binding rite needs two things. A speaker to say the words — Wren, if she is what we believe. And a vessel to hold the Thorn heart while it is bound. Not a cup, taster. A *womb*. The heart must live inside something living while the old tongue closes around it, or it simply pours back into the earth and waits another thousand years to be stupid again." She paused. "The vessel cannot be just any body. It must already be soaked in the heart's own substance. Old magic, in the blood, in the marrow, in the tongue itself. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

Below, the first fire arrows arced up against the morning sky, beautiful and obscene.

"I've been tasting for five years," Quentin said quietly.

"For five years someone has been feeding the king tithe — distilled essence of the Thorn heart, cut with wine. And for five years, every dish you did not die from, you absorbed instead. You have more of the old magic in your body than any living soul, taster. The wardens scryed you the night you arrived. You glow to our sight like a lamp in a cellar." Sorrel's leaf-dry voice softened by a degree. "You are not merely *a* vessel. You are the only vessel in the world that fits."

Quentin watched the arrows strike the bramble. They burned like orange stars against the black wood, and the wood drank them — he saw it happen, saw the flames gutter and sink inward, swallowed, hoarded. The wall did not even smoke.

"There's another way," he said.

"No."

"There's always—"

"No." Sorrel turned to face him fully. Up close her skin really was bark, fissured and gray, and her eyes were the green of deep water. "There is a child in that hall who crossed half a kingdom alone, mute with grief or with purpose or with both, to find people she'd never met because a lullaby told her they existed. There is a kingdom south of here sitting on top of a god's wounded heart, feeding it poison and calling it governance. And there is you — a man who has spent his whole life standing between other people and their deaths, taking small sips of ruin so that kings could eat safely." She laid a hand on his arm; it weighed like a branch. "I am very old, and I have watched many brave people find clever alternatives, and I am telling you: there is no other way. There is only whether you choose it or whether it is chosen for you when the wall comes down."

Through the thinning bramble, Quentin saw Wren in the grove behind them, kneeling by the bowl of dark soil, her small hands pressed to the earth, her lips moving silently. Learning. Memorizing. Preparing to speak a language that had been dead longer than kingdoms.

*Destiny,* he thought, *is just a trap with good manners.*

"When?" he asked.

"Soon," said Sorrel. "Sooner than either of us wants."

* * *

Fen died at the second hour.

He had gone out through a gate in the bramble that only opened for wardens — a seam in the thorns that sealed behind him like water closing over a stone — and he had walked, alone and unarmed, toward the burning line of Vane's soldiers, and Quentin watched from the wall as the old warden raised his arms and the ground itself rose to meet the cavalry.

For one full minute it worked. Horses screamed and threw their riders. A rank of spearmen went down in a tangle of earth and iron. Fen's hunched silhouette stood unmoving amid the chaos, and the battlefield bent around him like river water around a post.

Then the fire pots came. Six of them, hurled together, drenching him in burning pitch that did not sink into the ground because it was already burning too fast. Fen burned standing up. He burned without screaming, which was worse, and at the last he turned — a column of flame shaped like an old man — and faced the sanctuary, and raised one blackened hand, and Quentin understood it was farewell.

The bramble wall shuddered along its entire length. Somewhere deep in the wood, something grieved.

"Two remain," Ashen said, when Sorrel came back inside with ash on her sleeves and nothing else of her brother-warden. The eldest warden's white eyes were wet. "We knew the price when we planted this place. Grief later. Work now."

* * *

By midday the outer ring was breached.

It happened all at once, the way walls die. Vane's sappers had spent the morning not attacking but digging — three tunnels at the roots, packed with pitch and powder — and when they fired them the great wall did not fall so much as *flinch*, a convulsion running through forty feet of thorn, and in the convulsion a gap tore open on the southern side wide enough for twenty horsemen abreast.

Quentin felt the breach in his own chest, a cold tearing, and knew — with the strange new sense the tithe had given him — that the sanctuary itself had felt pain.

"To the inner grove!" Ashen's voice cracked across the grounds like a whip. "All of you, to the grove! Sorrel, the inner ring!"

The inner ring was smaller, denser, older — a labyrinth of bramble grown around the sleeping heart, passages that shifted, spines that moved. Wren grabbed Quentin's hand as they ran, and her grip was fierce, and she was speaking — aloud, in words, in the trade-tongue they had built between them across a hundred miles of flight — "*No. No. No.*" Over and over, the one word she had ever taught herself to say plainly, the word she'd used the night she refused to leave his doorway, the word that meant, in her private grammar, *you are mine and I am yours and this is not finished.*

"Not yet," Quentin said. "Not yet, little bird."

The first riders came through the breach behind them. He heard the hoofbeats change on the soft ground, heard shouting, heard a scream that ended wetly — the thorns exacting tolls — and then they were inside the labyrinth and the green dark closed over them like a mouth.

* * *

Vane entered the sanctuary on foot, because his horse had refused.

That was the thing he would remember afterward, in the years when he remembered anything at all: not the battle, not the burning warden, but his horse — a warhorse, a killer, an animal that had charged pike lines without flinching — planting all four legs and screaming at the bramble wall as though it were looking into hell.

He walked through the breach with his sword drawn and his doubts walking beside him like a second man.

Inside, the sanctuary was worse. The outer grounds were a battlefield; the inner labyrinth was a judgment. Bramble corridors shifted as his men advanced, sealing behind them, splitting columns, and twice he heard soldiers cry out and then stop crying out in a way that made the hair on his neck stand. The light was green and sourceless. The air tasted of sap and iron and, faintly, horribly, of the pit beneath the palace — the same smell, the same *presence*, as though the thing under the marble room and the thing under this grove were two ends of one long body.

*One heart,* he thought. *They told us we were destroying the last of it. They lied about everything.*

"My lord!" Brann, ahead, at a fork in the green maze. "Movement — there's a clearing, some kind of — there are people—"

Vane pushed past him.

The inner grove opened like a secret. A circle of level earth, maybe fifty feet across, ringed by the densest bramble he had ever seen — a wall within the wall, unbroken, humming. At its center stood a bowl of dark soil, and beside it an ancient woman with white eyes, and a girl.

Just a girl. Eight years old, mud to her knees, green eyes enormous in a small exhausted face. She stood in front of the old woman the way a door stands in a frame.

And behind them, at the edge of the grove, a man stepped out of the bramble shadow with his hands slightly raised and his face pale and familiar.

Vane knew him. Of course he knew him. Every officer in the hunt had memorized the description: *Blackwood, Quentin. Royal taster. Charm-seller. Traitor.* Vane had offered him a bargain in a palace corridor six weeks ago — *the child for your head* — and Blackwood had agreed with his mouth and lied with his eyes, and Vane had respected the lie even as he prepared to punish it.

"Taster," Vane said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "Step away from the child."

"No," Quentin said.

It was such a small word for such an enormous moment. Vane almost laughed.

"You've seen what my men did to the outer wall. You've seen what we did to the old one who walked out alone. This ends one way, Blackwood. The faction wants the girl alive. What condition she arrives in is, I'm told, negotiable." He raised his sword a few degrees. "Walk away. Last kindness I'll offer."

Behind the girl, the ancient woman with white eyes spoke for the first time, and her voice was the strangest thing Vane had heard all day — stranger than the screaming horse, stranger than the shifting thorns — because it was addressed not to him but to the ground.

"He is here," Mother Ashen said softly. "The saturated one. The vessel. Heart of the tithe, tongue of the old magic." Her white eyes found Quentin. "Child of Wren, speaker — the rite can begin whenever you are ready. But know this: once begun, it cannot pause, and the vessel does not walk away from it. The vessel *ends* in it."

The words landed in the grove like stones in still water.

Wren turned. Looked at Quentin. And Quentin watched her understand — watched it arrive in her face the way winter arrives, gradually and then all at once — what *vessel* meant, what *ends* meant, why the wardens had been so gentle with him for three days, why Sorrel had taken him aside that morning while the arrows flew.

"*No,*" Wren said. Loudly. In words. In both languages at once, somehow, the trade-word and the old word braided together, and the bramble wall rang like a struck bell. "*NO.*"

"Wren—" Quentin started.

But it was Vane's moment, and Vane ruined it, because Vane was a practical man and practical men do not wait through other people's revelations. "Take them," he said. "All of them. Now."

And the riders came into the grove.

* * *

Later — in whatever space later existed — Quentin would try to reconstruct those minutes and fail. Memory broke the assault into shards:

Sorrel meeting the first three horsemen alone at the grove's edge, arms spread, and the bramble rising around them like a green wave, and none of it — horse, rider, warden — emerging.

Brann dragging Vane backward by the shoulder, shouting about the ground, the ground is *moving*, my lord—

Ashen standing unmoved at the bowl of soil while chaos circled her, murmuring, always murmuring, keeping time like a drummer before a door.

Wren on her knees in the dark earth, both hands buried to the wrist, her whole small body shaking, her voice climbing out of silence for the first time in her life into full, furious speech — the Old Tongue pouring out of her in a flood, and every word landing in Quentin's chest like a hook, because the language recognized him, the language had been living in his blood for five years and was now, syllable by syllable, *coming home*.

And under it all, beneath the grove, beneath the mountain, beneath everything: a heartbeat. Slow. Enormous. Waking.

The Thorn heart had heard its name spoken at last, and it was turning over in the dark, and it was looking for a door.

Quentin knelt down opposite Wren, in the torn soil, as riders wheeled and wardens burned and Lord Aldous Vane shouted orders that no one obeyed anymore, and he took her hands out of the earth and held them, dirt and all.

"Little bird," he said. "Listen to me. Just once, listen all the way."

Her eyes were wild, streaming, green as spring sap, green as the first leaf after a fire.

"It has to be me," he said. "You know it does. Five years of the king's table. Five years of sips. I'm not the man who happens to be here, Wren — I'm the man this was always going to need. All those years I thought I was surviving. I was being *made*." He smiled, and found, to his astonishment, that the smile was real. "Every bitter pear. Every dosed wine. Every charm I sold to keep myself alive. It was all a road, and it ends here, and here is the first place I've ever chosen to stand."

"*No,*" she whispered. "*No, no, no—*"

"Yes," he said gently. "Yes, yes, yes."

Above them the sky went dark with smoke. The bramble wall began, at last, to bloom — white flowers opening along every black spine, hundreds of them, thousands, a snowfall of blossoms in the burning air — and the heartbeat beneath the world quickened, and the Old Tongue rose in Wren's throat like water finding its level, and Quentin Blackwood, royal taster, charm-seller, traitor, vessel, bowed his head between the child's small dirty hands and waited for the door to open.

Across the grove, Lord Vane stopped shouting.

He was looking at the blooming wall. He was looking at the thing the flowers were making light for. And on his face, plain as sunrise, was the expression of a man seeing, for the first time, the true shape of the masters he had served —

— and his sword arm began, very slowly, to turn.
