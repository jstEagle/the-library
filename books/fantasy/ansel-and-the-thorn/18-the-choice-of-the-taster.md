## 18. The Choice of the Taster

The wardens had told him what the rite required, and Quentin had done what he always did with unbearable information: he held it on his tongue and said nothing.

He stood in the inner grove while the sanctuary shuddered around him, black bramble walls groaning like ship's timbers in a storm. The heart of the Thorn lay beneath a dome of woven branches at the grove's center — a knot of wood the size of a sleeping child, veined with sap that glowed a slow, sullen green. It pulsed. He could feel it pulsing through the soles of his boots, through his teeth, through the five years of poison sitting patient in his blood like sediment in an old bottle.

*A willing vessel,* Mother Ilsa had said. *Saturated with the tithe. The heart will pour itself into such a vessel, and the vessel will hold it until it cools.*

And vessels did not survive the pouring. Vessels were not meant to.

"Quentin."

Wren's voice. Not her hands or her eyes this time — her voice, the one she'd been building word by word across three hundred miles of road, syllables of the Old Tongue traded back and forth over campfires like contraband. She had learned to say his name in the space between *Quen* and *tin*, and she used it now like a blade laid flat against his chest.

He turned. She was running toward him through the falling ash of the outer wall, green eyes wide, hair full of thorn-leaves, and behind her the third warden — old Sorrel, the last of them standing — was screaming words that made the bramble writhe.

"You understood," Wren said. She grabbed his wrist, the same wrist the king had once gripped, and her fingers were shaking. "You understood what they said. I saw your face."

"Wren—"

"Don't." Her voice cracked. "Don't do the thing where you taste something terrible and swallow it and smile. I know all your faces now. Every single one. Don't you dare use them on me."

Above them, the eastern wall of the sanctuary came apart. Not burned — *unmade*, bramble unraveling into threads of light and rot, and through the gap came torchlight and horses and the sound of Vane's hunters, dozens of them, their war-horns cutting through the smoke. The first wave had breached at dusk. The wardens had bought them an hour. The hour was spent.

"They're coming," Quentin said quietly. "And there's only one way to stop what's under the palace from ever waking fully. One way to unmake the war before it starts. You know that too. Ilsa told us both."

"I don't care." Wren's chin was trembling but her voice didn't. Eight years old, last speaker of a murdered language, and she looked at him with the fury of someone three times her age. "I don't care about the war. I don't care about the kingdom. They killed everyone who spoke my language and now they want to kill you too and I'm supposed to just — just *use* you—"

"It isn't using if I give it."

"It is!" She struck his chest with both fists, once, twice, small blows that wouldn't have bruised a sparrow. "It is, it is, it *is*—"

He caught her hands. Held them. Knelt, because he wanted her face level with his for whatever he said next, and because his knees had gone strange ever since Sorrel spoke the word *vessel*.

* * *

Quentin Blackwood had never belonged anywhere. That was the plain arithmetic of his life, and he had made his peace with it the way he made peace with bitterness: by learning to taste around it.

His mother had died when he was four. His grandmother, who sang lullabies in a tongue no one else knew, when he was nine. After that: a workhouse, a kitchen, a master taster who discovered the boy's tongue went numb instead of dead and thought, *there's coin in that*. Five years in the king's palace eating poison in measured doses, growing immune the way a tree grows rings — invisibly, internally, each season adding a layer he couldn't see and couldn't show anyone. Five years selling charms to desperate people in a city that hung mages from the Salt Gate. Five years of being useful to everyone and known by no one.

He had told himself it was safety. Caution. Sense. But kneeling in the burning grove with Wren's hands trapped in his, he finally admitted what the truth had tasted like all along: loneliness. Loneliness disguised as competence, served up daily, and he had swallowed every bite without complaint because complaining was a luxury belonging people could afford.

Then a mute child had knocked on his door speaking a dead language, and everything had changed — not because of prophecy or destiny, but because for the first time in his life, someone needed him and he *wanted* to be needed. Because teaching her words over a guttering fire had felt more like home than the palace ever had. Because when she slept curled against his side on the sanctuary road, he had lain awake thinking: *this. This is the thing I would burn down kingdoms to keep.*

And the kingdom was burning down around him now, and keeping her meant leaving her.

"Listen to me," he said. "I've tasted everything. Poisons, curses, things I can't name. That was my whole life — taking the bitter mouthful so someone else didn't have to. And I hated it, most days. I hated being the one who swallowed." He smiled, and it wasn't one of his masks; it was the real one, crooked and tired and true. "But this isn't that. This is the first mouthful I've ever chosen. Do you understand? Nobody's ordering me. Nobody's paying me. I'm choosing this the way I never got to choose anything."

"No." Wren was crying now, silent tears tracking through the ash on her cheeks. "There has to be another way. I'll speak the rite differently, I'll find another vessel, I'll—"

"There is no other vessel. There's only me. Five years of tithe in my blood, Wren. The wardens said it themselves. I'm not the sacrifice the rite demands." His voice caught, and he let it. "I'm the one it's been waiting for. All those years I thought I was just surviving. Turns out I was being *made*. For this."

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

She pulled her hands free and hit him again, and then stopped hitting and simply pressed her forehead against his chest, and he wrapped his arms around her and held on while the sanctuary screamed and the horns drew closer and somewhere beyond the walls the thing beneath the palace turned in its sleep and dreamed of waking.

"I found you," she whispered into his coat. "I walked all that way and I found *you*. It's not fair that I only get to keep you for one spring."

Quentin closed his eyes. He thought of his grandmother's lullabies, fragments of the Old Tongue sung over a motherless boy, and understood suddenly that she hadn't been singing to comfort him. She'd been keeping a door open. A door he'd walked through the night a green-eyed child spoke three words and his charms hummed like struck bells.

"One spring," he said into her hair. "Better than none. Better than anything I had before you."

* * *

Sorrel found them like that. The last warden was bleeding from a dozen cuts, her robes scorched, her ancient face calm as still water.

"The riders are at the second ring," she said. "We have minutes, taster. Perhaps fewer. When you're ready, bring the child to the heart."

"She doesn't want—"

"None of us want." Sorrel's eyes, milky-white, seemed to look straight through him. "Wanting is for peacetime. Come."

They crossed the grove together, Wren's hand locked in his hard enough to hurt, and Quentin took inventory of the world the way he took inventory of a dish — every flavor noted, nothing wasted. The smell of burning bramble, sharp and almost sweet, like caramel left too long. The pulse of the heart beneath its dome, faster now, answering the violence at the walls. The weight of Wren beside him, small and furious and alive. The strange lightness in his own chest, which he recognized with surprise as fear, and beneath the fear, quieter, unmistakable:

Peace.

Vane burst into the grove as they reached the dome.

The Lord Chamberlain looked wrecked — fine clothes torn, one eye swollen shut, soot to the elbows — and his sword was drawn but hanging low, and his remaining eye went from Wren to Quentin to the glowing heart and back again, and what crossed his face was not triumph. It was horror wearing triumph's clothing.

"Stop," Vane said hoarsely. "Blackwood. Stop. I've seen what they're feeding under the palace. I've *seen* it. Whatever you're about to do—"

"Then help us or get out of the way," Quentin said.

For one suspended moment, the man who had hunted them across half the kingdom stood with his blade trembling in his fist — and then, behind him, the ground itself split along the sanctuary's southern edge, and something vast exhaled from the crack, a breath of cold and hunger that bent the flames sideways, and Aldous Vane made his choice too, though his was simpler: he turned his sword toward the darkness and started shouting orders to men who no longer answered to anyone.

Wren stepped up to the dome. The bramble opened for her like hands unfolding.

"Say it," Quentin told her. "All of it. Every word your people ever kept. Make them proud."

She looked up at him, green eyes streaming, and said — in the Old Tongue, in the language of the Thorn, in the voice of everyone the Purging had silenced — *"Ansel."*

He blinked. "What?"

"It means *the one who stays*." Her chin lifted. "If you're going to do this, you do it with a name I choose. Not Blackwood. Not the taster. Ansel."

Something in his chest cracked open, and out of the crack came not pain but warmth, spreading, filling, and he laughed — actually laughed, here at the end of everything — and said, "Then I stay, Wren. Right to the last drop."

She began to speak.

The Old Tongue poured out of her like water finding an old channel, words layered on words, and the heart of the Thorn rose from its bed of branches and hovered, blazing, and Quentin stepped forward and opened his arms and let it come home.

* * *

It did not hurt, at first. It was like drinking — like the deepest draught he'd ever taken, green fire flooding down his throat and into the poisoned sediment of his blood, and the five years of tithe rose to meet it, king's pears and chamberlain's wine, every bitter mouthful dissolving into light. He heard Wren's voice spiral higher. He heard the riders scream and flee. He heard the thing beneath the palace howl once, far away, as the war it carried inside it was unmade thread by thread, unwritten, returned to the silence before kingdoms.

Then it began to hurt.

He held anyway. He was a taster; holding was what he did. He held the heart of the Thorn in his mortal body while it cooled, and the cooling was the dying, and he watched the sanctuary burn white around him — not destroyed now, *blessed*, every black bramble blossoming at once, ten thousand flowers opening like a held breath released — and the last thing he saw was Wren, still speaking, tears cutting silver lines through the ash on her face, her voice never wavering even as her knees gave and she knelt in the flowers beside him.

He tried to tell her it was sweet. That after everything, all of it, every bitter year — this, right now, was the sweetest thing he had ever tasted.

His mouth wouldn't make the words.

The white blooms drifted down like snow, and Quentin Blackwood — Ansel, the one who stayed — closed his eyes in the arms of the girl who had spoken, and the long night of the world rolled gently on without him.

Far to the south, beneath a silent palace, something ancient sighed and did not wake again.

And in Wren's arms, in a grove of impossible flowers, a man who had belonged nowhere at all was, at last, exactly where he chose to be.
