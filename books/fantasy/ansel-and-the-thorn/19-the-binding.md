## 19. The Binding

The sanctuary was burning when Wren began to speak.

She heard it before she saw it — the crackle of the outer bramble catching flame, the screams of the wardens, the iron ring of Vane's soldiers breaking through the living walls. The black thorns that had stood for a thousand years were dying around her in a halo of orange light, and inside that dying ring, in a grove no larger than a chapel, Quentin knelt in the center of the binding circle with his sleeves rolled back and his hands open, waiting.

*You can still run,* he had told her an hour ago, when the first horns sounded in the dark. *I can hold them off. I've been lying to dangerous men for five years; I'm good at it.*

*No,* she had said. It was the clearest word she had ever spoken in any language. *You're the vessel. I'm the voice. That's what the wardens said. That's what the Thorn wants.*

*The Thorn,* Quentin had said, with the ghost of his market-stall smile, *can want things it doesn't get.*

But he had knelt down in the circle anyway. He always did that — argued with her and then did what she needed, as if protecting her and obeying her were the same act, as if he had decided somewhere on the sanctuary road that his life was a coin he had been carrying for years, waiting for something worth buying.

The Thorn heart rose from the earth at the center of the grove.

Wren had seen it in her dreams her whole life, before she knew what dreams were, before she knew the words for what she saw: a knot of black wood the size of a sleeping child, veined with slow green light, breathing. Not beating. *Breathing.* It had been buried here since the Purging, since the kings of the new world burned the speaking people and salted their graves, and it had waited under the roots of the sanctuary with a patience that was older than hunger.

Around it, the wardens' circle still glowed — three rings of carved stone, two of them dark now, their wardens dead or dying in the smoke. Only Mother Bracken's ring still burned white, and her light was guttering.

*Now,* Bracken's voice said, not in Wren's ears but in her blood, in the place where the old words lived. *Speak, last daughter. Speak us whole or speak us ended. There is no third way.*

Wren stepped into the circle.

The heat of the burning bramble washed over her back. Somewhere beyond the fire, boots on stone, the barked orders of men who had ridden for nine days to kill an eight-year-old girl. She did not look at them. She looked at Quentin.

He looked frightened. Not of the Thorn, and not of the soldiers. Frightened for her, the way he had been frightened the first night at his door, when she had spoken three words and his charms had sung in answer. He had spent five years selling little lies of magic in a city that hung people like him, and he had never once, in all that time, spent a single charm on himself. She understood that about him now. It was the whole of him, really. He was a man who had made himself small so that other things could be safe.

She was going to unmake him, and he had chosen it, and she did not know if that made it better or worse.

*Begin,* said the Thorn.

Wren opened her mouth, and for the first time in her life, she did not hold back.

The Old Tongue did not come out the way speech comes out. It came out the way a river comes out of a broken dam. The words she had been carrying since before memory — the lullaby-fragments, the warning she had carried down from the ruined north, the whole buried language of a people who had been ash for three hundred years — rose through her chest and throat and poured into the grove, and every word was true, and every word had weight, and the stones of the warden-rings drank the sound and blazed.

She spoke the opening of the rite: *Root that remembers, heart that hungers, sleeper under the floor of the world — hear the last voice that loved you.*

The Thorn heart shuddered. The green veins in its wood brightened. And Quentin cried out — a short, sharp sound, bitten off fast, because Quentin bit everything off fast — as the first thread of the binding entered him.

Wren felt it happen through the words. The rite needed a vessel, something saturated with the old magic, something the Thorn would recognize as kin. Five years of tasting tithe-poisoned food had done it — the slow poison the court faction had fed to the king, the same bitter note she had warned him of, the same bitterness that no one else's tongue could find. Quentin's body was full of it. Full of the Thorn's own substance, drunk in drop by drop across a thousand breakfasts, a thousand cups of wine. He was the most Thorn-touched living thing in the world, and he had become it by accident, by loyalty, by doing his small job well.

The Thorn reached for him the way a drowning man reaches for a rope.

*Speak the binding,* Bracken gasped. *Quickly, child — it will try to take all of him—*

Wren spoke faster.

The words were enormous. She felt them moving through her like great animals through tall grass. *You were wronged,* she said to the Thorn, in the language of the people it had loved. *You were burned and buried and named a monster by men who feared your voice. We do not forgive it. We will never forgive it. But the war you remember is three hundred years dead, and the ones who burned you are dust, and if you wake now you will burn the innocent and the guilty together, and the world will end the same way it ended before — in fire, in ash, in silence.*

The Thorn's light turned red.

It did not want to hear it. Of course it did not want to hear it. Wren felt its rage pour up through the ground, through her feet, into her teeth — three hundred years of burial, of listening to the kingdoms build their walls on its grave, of hearing its language called dead. The bramble walls of the sanctuary, burning, turned their thorns inward. The stones of the circle cracked. Somewhere behind her, very close now, a man's voice — Vane's voice, smooth and cold even through smoke — shouting orders.

*The vessel,* the Thorn said, in a voice like roots splitting stone. *Give me the vessel, and I will sleep. Give me the one who carries me in his blood, and I will let the world keep its little life.*

*No,* Wren said.

*Then I take him.*

The thread between the Thorn and Quentin went taut like a wire, and Quentin screamed, and Wren felt the rite tearing — the binding becoming a devouring, the vessel becoming a meal — and she did the thing the wardens had told her not to do, the thing no speaker had ever done, because it had never been necessary.

She stopped directing the rite and stepped into it.

She spoke the binding words *through herself.* She made her own small body the second channel, the bridge, so that the power flowing into Quentin flowed through her first, and she could feel all of it — the whole weight of the ancient thing, its grief like a glacier, its rage like a forest fire, and underneath both, so far down it was almost invisible, its terrible, unbearable *loneliness.* It had been the last of its kind for three hundred years. It had been waiting for someone to speak to it. That was all it had ever wanted. That was all it had ever needed. And no one had come.

*I came,* Wren said, in the Old Tongue, with the whole burning sanctuary shaking around her. *I am here. I am the last voice of the people who planted you. And I am telling you: the war is over. Let it be over.*

The red light held for one heartbeat. Two.

Then, through the channel, through her, through Quentin, Wren felt the Thorn *look* at Quentin — truly look, the way she had looked at him that first night through a cracked door, deciding whether this tired man with his hidden charms was safe. It saw the five years of tithe in his blood. It saw the small magics, the warmth he carried for her, the way he had knelt in the circle with his hands open. It saw that he was afraid and had stayed anyway.

*He tastes of bitterness,* the Thorn said. *And of kindness. The old ones tasted of kindness too.*

*Yes,* Wren said.

*Bind me, then,* the Thorn said. *But let him carry the binding. Let him remember me. I do not want to be forgotten again.*

And Wren spoke the final words.

They were not loud. That was the strange thing, the thing she would remember all her life: at the end, the greatest words she would ever speak came out of her almost as a whisper, a lullaby, the same melody her grandmother's grandmother might have sung over a cradle three hundred years ago. *Sleep, root. Rest, heart. Be held. Be held. Be held.*

The light went from red to green to white.

* * *

Quentin felt the Thorn enter him the way a river enters a dry bed.

There was no pain, at first. There was only fullness — the five years of numbness in his tongue, the five years of small careful lies, the five years of being a hollow man in a dangerous city, all of it suddenly *filled*, flooded, every empty space in him occupied by something vast and old and grieving. He could feel the Thorn's memories as if they were his own: forests before kingdoms, a world where the speaking people and the rooted people had tended each other, and then the smoke, the salt, the burning, the long dark under the stones.

*It's all right,* he tried to say, though he no longer had a mouth in any way that mattered. *I know what it is to wait. I know what it is to be no one's.*

The Thorn settled into him like a bird into a nest.

The binding was not a cage. He understood that as it happened — the wardens' word had been wrong, or the old translations were wrong. A binding, done rightly, was not a prison. It was a *promise.* The Thorn would sleep, but not buried and forgotten. It would sleep in him, held, tended, spoken to. It would be carried the way you carry a memory of someone you loved. The ancient war would never begin because the ancient rage had finally been given what it needed, and what it needed was so small and so enormous that Quentin wanted to laugh, if he had still had a throat: it needed a family.

The white light poured out of him and across the grove.

He heard, dimly, through the roaring of the rite, the sound of the inner gate breaking — Vane's men, the last of them, coming through the smoke with steel drawn. He heard Vane's voice, closer than the rest, and then Vane's voice *stopping*, the orders dying in his throat, because the Lord Chamberlain had come around the burning bramble into the grove and seen it: the light pouring out of the kneeling taster, and through the light, for one unguarded instant, the thing itself — the true shape of what slept beneath the palace, what his masters had been feeding with the king's slow poison, what they had promised him a regency in exchange for. Not a weapon. Not a throne. A buried, burning, betrayed *god of grief*, and Vane had served it for years without knowing, and now he knew.

Quentin heard the sound of a sword being drawn — but not turned toward him.

He heard Vane say, in a voice scraped down to nothing: *You never told me what it was.* And then, to his own soldiers, or to his masters far away in the capital, or to no one: *You never told me.*

The sound that followed was short, and terrible, and Quentin was too full of the Thorn to look, and perhaps that was a mercy the universe granted him at the end.

The light crested. The Thorn, held, held, held in the vessel of his body, in the voice of the last speaker, in the promise they had made together — the Thorn *let go.* The rage went out of the ground like a tide going out. Quentin felt the tithe in his blood, five years of it, turn from poison to simply *presence*, and then from presence to nothing at all. The bramble walls of the sanctuary stopped burning. The fires died mid-flame, and where they had been, white flowers opened along every black branch, thousands of them, tens of thousands, the whole burning fortress blooming at once in the dark.

*Thank you,* the Thorn said, from somewhere very deep inside him, in the Old Tongue, in Wren's language. *Brother. Keeper. Stay.*

*Stay,* Quentin agreed.

And then, because a vessel that has given everything does not keep standing, the light went out of him, and the grove, and the world.

* * *

Wren caught him.

She did not remember crossing the circle. She was simply there, on her knees in the flower-strewn ash, with Quentin's head in her lap and her hands pressed against his chest, and he was lighter than a man should be, as if the binding had taken not only his life but his weight, his warmth, everything except the shape of him.

His eyes were open. They found her face the way they always did — quickly, surely, the way a man finds the one safe thing in a burning room.

"Hey, sprout," he said. His voice was a thread. "Told you... I was good at dangerous jobs."

"You didn't tell me anything," Wren said, and her voice broke on the words, and she did not care, because she was speaking the Old Tongue and the human tongue and both of them at once, and it did not matter, because he was leaving. "You just knelt down. You just always knelt down."

"Someone has to," he said. "Be the... the floor. Under everybody's feet."

The white flowers were falling from the bramble like snow. Mother Bracken stood at the edge of the grove, the last warden, leaning on her staff, and she was singing — low, wordless, the mourning-song, the one that had not been sung in three hundred years. Far away, the soldiers were fleeing through the blooming thorns, and the thorns let them go, parting like a crowd, like water, because the war was over and the Thorn no longer wished to hold anyone.

Quentin's hand came up — so slowly — and touched her hair.

"You'll speak," he said. "You'll speak all the time now. Whole sentences. Don't... don't waste it on people who don't listen."

"You listened," Wren said.

"Yeah," Quentin Blackwood, royal food-taster, charm-seller, vessel, said, and smiled his market-stall smile one last time, small and crooked and true. "I always... listened to you."

The white light went out of his eyes, gently, the way a lamp goes out at the end of a long day, and the Thorn heart in Wren's dreams went quiet for the first time in three hundred years, and the sanctuary stood blooming and silent under the stars.

Wren held him for a long time. The wardens' song rose around her. The ash settled like a blessing.

And far to the south, beneath a palace built on a sleeping grave, the earth stopped trembling — and in the king's chamber, a poison that no longer had a purpose began, at last, to fade from an old man's blood.

The soldiers who fled the blooming sanctuary would carry the story to the capital, and the story would not fit in any report, and no one would know what to write.

And the girl who spoke would carry something heavier, and stranger, and full of a patience older than grief — because the Thorn had made her one last promise, in the last words of the rite, and she had not yet told anyone what it was.

*He is not ended,* the Thorn had said. *Only planted.*

Wren looked down at Quentin's still face, at the single white flower that had come to rest on his chest like an answer, and she began, softly, in the old language, to sing him the lullaby her grandmother's grandmother had sung — the one that meant, in a tongue three hundred years dead: *sleep now, and grow.*
