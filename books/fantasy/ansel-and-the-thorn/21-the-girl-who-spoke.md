## 21. The Girl Who Spoke

The garden had been a refuse heap when they first came to it — a sunken yard behind the archivist's cottage, choked with broken roof tiles and the grey bones of a collapsed shed. Ilsa had said, in her dry way, that everything worth keeping was buried under something someone else had thrown away. Wren believed her. She had spent the whole winter digging.

Now it was spring again, nearly summer, and the garden was a country of small green order: rows of beans climbing twine, a bed of onions like a regiment of pale spears, lettuces soft as folded cloth. And at the center, where no vegetable would grow, the bramble.

It stood barely taller than Wren herself, its canes black as cooled iron, arched and thorned like the ribs of some sleeping animal. It had not spread. It did not need to. From every cane tip hung white flowers, five-petaled, faintly luminous even at noon, and they never fell. In all the months since the sanctuary burned, not one petal had touched the ground.

Wren knelt before it with the watering can and spoke to it the way she spoke to it every morning, in the old language, the language that had lived in her throat before she ever had words anyone else could use.

*Thal-en-vara. Sel i doren. I am here. The day is new.*

The bramble did not answer. It never answered. But the flowers turned, very slightly, toward her voice, the way sunflowers turn toward light, and that was enough. That had to be enough.

She watered it last, always last, after everything else, because Ilsa said you saved the best for the end of the work so the work would have somewhere to go. Then she sat back on her heels in the warm dirt and looked at it, and did the thing she did every morning and could not stop doing, which was to wait.

*Come back,* she did not say. *You promised nothing. You stayed anyway. Come back.*

The bramble stood in the sun and held its white flowers, and said nothing, and Wren got up and went inside to help Ilsa with the bread.

* * *

The city had learned new stories in the months since midsummer. Wren heard them at the market, where she went now with Ilsa's list clutched in her fist and coins counted twice, because Ilsa trusted her with money but not with arithmetic.

They said the king had risen from his sickbed thin as a winter branch and strange-eyed, and that he had begun speaking sentences no one understood, and that his physicians wrote them down now instead of bleeding him. They said the Lord Chamberlain Aldous Vane had been found dead in his own chambers on the night the sky over the north went white, his sword through his heart and a ledger open beside him — pages of names, payments, tithe-schedules, the whole machinery of the poison laid out in his own neat hand, as if he had known he would need to leave an accounting. They said the faction behind him had scattered like rats from a fired granary; some were taken, some fled over the borders, and two lords had hanged themselves rather than face the questioning.

They said other things too, quieter things, the kind of things people say only when they are sure no soldier is near. They said the ground under the palace still trembled sometimes, softly, like a dog dreaming. They said crops came up wrong in the river valleys, stalks blackened at the root, and then came up right again overnight, as if something had changed its mind. They said there were soldiers who had marched north to burn a nest of witches and come back silent, unwilling or unable to say what they had seen, except that one of them, a sergeant with a burned face, had been heard weeping in his cups and saying over and over: *it bloomed, gods help us, it bloomed white.*

And they said — this was the newest story, still wet, still dangerous — that the crown was looking for a girl. A girl with green eyes who spoke the old tongue. Not to hang her. To honor her. Or so the criers said. But Wren had seen what honoring looked like from the inside of a kingdom's hands, and she kept her hood up and her eyes down and told no one anything.

Ilsa had dyed her hair brown with walnut husks in the autumn. "Grief is private," the old woman had said, working the dye through Wren's hair with fingers that smelled of ink and lye. "But survival is public. Learn the difference."

Wren had learned it. She carried Quentin's charms in a pouch at her belt — the ones he hadn't sold, the little warming-stones and quieting-charms, his first clumsy work — and she wore them openly, because who would suspect a child's trinkets? Only she knew they hummed when she passed the bramble each morning, a sound below hearing, like a struck glass remembered rather than heard.

She paid for the onions. She kept her eyes down. She walked home through streets that still smelled, faintly, everywhere, of ash.

* * *

Ilsa was kneading bread when Wren returned, her sleeves rolled, flour to the elbows, her grey braid pinned up in a style forty years out of fashion. The cottage smelled of yeast and woodsmoke and old paper. There was always old paper. Ilsa had rescued three crates of court archives during the purges that followed Vane's death — documents marked for burning — and they filled half the cottage now, stacked in towers that leaned like drunks.

"You're late," Ilsa said, without looking up.

"The market was full."

"The market is always full. That's what makes it useful." She punched the dough down with more force than the dough deserved. "There was a man asking after cottages on this lane. He wasn't buying."

Wren set down the basket. "A finder?"

"Or worse. Polite boots, patient eyes. I gave him bread and directions to the tannery." Ilsa finally looked at her, and her gaze was the old archivist's gaze, the one that read people like marginalia. "It's time you heard the rest of it, child. You're eight years old and you've died once already, in a manner of speaking, and you deserve the whole ledger."

Wren climbed onto the stool by the hearth, the one that was hers, and waited.

Ilsa talked while she worked, which was how she said true things — her hands busy so her mouth wouldn't have to be brave. She talked about the weeks after the sanctuary, when the soldiers came south carrying reports no minister could file. About the king's recovery, and how the first fully lucid thing he had done was summon the archive and ask, in broken Old Tongue, whether any speakers survived. About how Ilsa had gone to the palace herself, an old disgraced woman with nothing left to lose, and shown him the truth she'd spent thirty years cataloguing: the thorn-tithe schedules, the thing beneath the foundations, the prophecy of the speaker who would waken or bind.

"And do you know what he said?" Ilsa asked. "Half-dead man, poisoned for a year, king of a kingdom built on a grave. He said: *the taster knew.*" Her hands stilled in the dough. "He remembers your friend, child. Remembers gripping his wrist. Remembers the warning he tried to give. The king has been asking for you both since autumn."

Wren looked at her hands. There was dirt in the creases, and a scratch from the bramble that had already healed, though the mark of it lingered faintly silver, the way Thorn-marks did.

"They want to give me a house," she said. It wasn't a question. The criers' story had reached even here.

"They want to give you a title. Keeper of the Old Speech, or some such silk-wrapped thing. A place at court. Guards. Scholars who will want to pick apart your mouth like a rare moth." Ilsa resumed kneading, harder. "And if you refuse, they may decide honoring is less convenient than containing. Kingdoms are like that. I've read their diaries."

"What did you tell them?"

"That my granddaughter was ill. That she was grieving. That grief is private." Ilsa's voice gentled, which it almost never did. "I bought you a season, maybe two. I'm telling you because you should choose, not be chosen for. It's the only inheritance I have to give you."

Wren sat with that. Outside, through the window, she could see the bramble's white flowers catching the afternoon light, and the sight loosened something in her chest and tightened something else, because everything about the bramble did both those things at once, always, forever.

"I don't want a title," she said at last. "I want—" She stopped. The word she wanted was *him*, and she had learned that saying it aloud made rooms go quiet and made Ilsa's eyes go wet in a way the old woman hated. "I want to stay here. With the garden. Until—"

"Until?" Ilsa prompted, when she didn't finish.

"Until it blooms into something," Wren said. Which was not what she meant either, but Ilsa nodded as if it were, and floured a cloth over the dough, and let it be.

* * *

That night Wren dreamed the way she always dreamed now: in the old language, whole and fluent, a tongue that belonged to her even in sleep.

She dreamed of a grove of black bramble under a burning sky, and of a man standing in the middle of it with his arms open, and the fire going through him like light through a window, and his face — she could never hold his face in dreams, it slid away like water — his face turning toward her with an expression she knew in her body if not her eye: the look of someone who has finally, at the very end, stopped running.

In the dream she spoke the binding words, all of them, the full rite, and the words poured out of her like a river uncorked, and the burning man smiled, and the bramble bloomed white, and then—

Then the dream always broke in the same place. The man became the bramble. The bramble became a man. And between those two things, in the space where the change happened, there was a door, and the door was ajar, and every night she woke just before she could see what stood behind it.

She woke now, in the small hours, heart hammering, and lay listening to the cottage settle. Ilsa snored gently in the next room. Somewhere far off, so far it might have been imagination, the ground gave one soft shudder — the dream-dog under the world, rolling over — and was still.

Wren got up. She always got up. She padded barefoot through the kitchen, out the back door into the cold blue dark, and knelt by the bramble among the moths.

The flowers glowed faintly at night, she had discovered. Faintly, and more strongly when she spoke.

"*Sel i doren,*" she whispered. "*Ansel.*"

She had begun using the name. Not aloud to anyone — Ilsa thought she imagined it, or pitied her, or both — but to the bramble, at night, when no one could hear. Ansel. The one who stayed. She had found the word in one of Ilsa's rescued fragments, a glossary page in a dead archivist's hand, and the moment she read it she had known it was his, the way you know your own name written in someone else's book.

The flowers turned toward her voice.

And then — Wren's breath stopped — they turned *past* her. All of them together, smoothly, deliberately, swiveling on their stems to face the eastern road, the road that led down out of the hills toward the city, toward the palace, toward the deep places where something old slept bound and dreaming.

The bramble had never moved like that. The bramble had never pointed anywhere.

Wren knelt in the cold dirt with her heart going like a bird in a box, and watched the white flowers hold their vigil toward the east, and felt, for the first time in all these months of mourning, not grief.

Anticipation.

Something was coming down that road. Or waking beneath the city. Or both. The door in her dream stood ajar, and tonight, for the first time, she had the feeling — sudden, total, certain as spring — that it was about to open.

Behind her, in the cottage, Ilsa's voice drifted sleepily through the window: "Child? What are you doing out there?"

Wren didn't answer. She was watching the bramble watch the road, and learning, petal by white petal, how to hope.
