## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The widow's grief was the color of wet slate.

Lena Zhou had learned to see memories as colors long before she'd learned to see them as anything else—before she understood that other people carried their pasts inside them like stones in a riverbed, worn smooth by time, while she could reach into that river and lift a stone out whole. Grief was slate. Joy ran gold, though gold was rare and usually counterfeit by the time it reached market. Love came in every color at once, which made it nearly impossible to extract cleanly, and Lena had stopped taking love-commissions years ago for exactly that reason.

This grief, though. This grief was clean.

She sat across from Widow Maren Hale in the widow's own kitchen, hands folded on a table scarred by forty years of knives, and let her gift sink down through the woman's mind like a hand lowering into dark water. The kitchen smelled of bread gone stale and lavender someone had hung too late to matter. Outside the window, the Veil went about its evening: smoke rising straight from chimneys because there was no wind worth mentioning, goats complaining in their pens, the mountains standing around the valley like patient jailers wearing crowns of snow.

*Nothing leaves,* Lena reminded herself, as she always did at this point in the work. *Not magic. Not memory. Not you.*

The grief lay near the surface, mercifully. Some clients wanted things buried deep dug out—a first betrayal, a dead child's face, the sound a husband made when the roof beam came down—and those extractions took hours, took sweat, sometimes took blood from Lena's nose. But Maren Hale had come to her three days after the funeral and said, in the flat voice of someone who has already decided, "Take it out. All of it. I can't carry him anymore."

So Lena carried him instead.

She found the grief where it pooled, heavy and dark, wrapped around everything the widow thought about her dead husband. Carefully—always carefully—Lena began to sever the threads that bound the stone to the riverbed. This was the part no one taught you, because no one else could do it. The trick was not to pull. Pulling tore. You had to convince the memory it wanted to leave. You had to make forgetting feel like rest.

"You'll still know he died," Lena murmured, the way she always spoke during extraction, half to the client and half to herself. "You'll know you were married. You'll know his name, if someone says it. You just won't—" She hesitated over the word. "*Feel* it. It'll be a fact, not a wound."

Maren Hale nodded. Her eyes were dry. They had been dry for three days, which was its own kind of terrible.

The last thread parted. Lena lifted the grief out of the widow's mind and into her own cupped hands, and there it was: a stone of wet slate, cold against her palms, humming faintly with forty-one years of a man named Aldous Hale laughing at his own jokes before he finished telling them.

Lena felt all of it in the moment of transfer—the way you always did, one full taste before the vial sealed. She felt Aldous's hand on Maren's shoulder in a barley field thirty years ago. She felt the argument they'd never resolved, about a door he kept meaning to fix. She felt the morning he didn't wake up, and the terrible ordinary silence of the kitchen where Lena now sat.

Then she breathed out, and the memory slid down into the lead-lined vial in her pocket, and it wasn't hers anymore. It belonged to whoever had paid.

"Is it done?" Maren asked.

"It's done."

The widow looked around her kitchen slowly, like a woman checking whether anything had been stolen. Nothing had been stolen. That was the horror of it. Everything was exactly where she'd left it, including herself.

"I don't remember what I paid you to take," Maren said.

"No. You won't."

"Will I want it back?"

Lena stood, tucking the vial away. "Some people do. Come find me in a year if you do. I keep a record." She didn't say that the record was mostly useless—that a memory returned after a year apart grew back wrong, like a bone set badly. She didn't say that some clients who came back weeping had to be turned away at the door, because you couldn't unring a bell twice.

Maren pressed coins into her hand—more than the going rate, Lena noticed, though she said nothing about it—and walked her to the door. At the threshold the widow paused.

"He fixed the door," she said suddenly. "The week before. I remember that he fixed it, I just don't remember why it mattered to me."

"That's how it works," Lena said gently. "You keep the shape. The weight goes."

Maren nodded and closed the door, and through the wood Lena heard her begin, very quietly, to hum.

The walk home took twenty minutes along the valley road, past fields that never failed and orchards that never blighted, because the Veil was generous in the way that prisons are generous—with routine, with safety, with the slow suffocation of anything wild. Magic could not enter the valley. That was the whole of its founding, four hundred years back, when refugees fleeing the Mage Wars had climbed over the shoulder of the mountain and found a place where spells guttered out like candles in rain. The founders had built walls of law instead of stone: no charms, no hexes, no scrying, no bargains with things that lived outside. And in exchange for that surrender, the Veil offered peace. Perfect, airless, eternal peace.

It also offered Lena her living, because the founders had missed something—or perhaps hadn't wanted to look too closely at what they'd found. Magic could not enter the Veil. But human minds were not magic, exactly. They were something older and stranger, and in the valley's soil-less silence, certain people born here discovered they could do with their bare hands what sorcerers needed circles and blood to accomplish elsewhere.

They could take a memory out of a head and hold it.

They couldn't put it back—not cleanly, not in the valley, where whatever force silenced magic also mended the hole left behind, sealing it shut like water closing over a dropped coin. So the memories accumulated. And since nothing left the Veil—not letters, not goods beyond a handful of sanctioned traders, not people who ever intended to return—the memories piled up in cellars and strongboxes, growing musty, until someone clever realized that the sanctioned traders could carry them out, and that outside the valley, in the world where magic flowed free, a bottled grief or a bottled joy was worth more than its weight in silver.

Lena had been taking memories since she was nine years old. First small ones, from volunteers: the embarrassment of a fall, the boredom of a sermon. Then, at fourteen, real work, apprenticed to old Sefa Corvane who had taught her the river-and-stone meditation, the breathing, the discipline of not flinching when the memory touched your tongue on its way out. Sefa was dead now. Most of her clients were dead now. Lena was twenty-six and the best thief of intangibles in a valley that pretended it had no thieves, and she went home each night to a small stone house at the edge of town with one lamp, one chair, and shelves of empty vials waiting to be filled.

She passed the wardens' station as the light went amber. Two of them stood in the doorway, bored, armored in the gray of the Veil's founding families. One of them, Toma, raised a hand.

"Lena. Working?"

"Widow's grief. Routine."

"Anyone ever take yours?" He grinned. He asked it every time, the same joke, the same grin, and every time she gave the same answer.

"Nothing worth stealing, Toma."

He laughed and waved her on, and Lena walked the last stretch of road with the lie sitting comfortably in her mouth, the way old shoes sit comfortably on feet. Because that was the thing about her profession that no client ever asked and no warden ever wondered: every memory she lifted passed through her. Every grief, every joy, every shame she extracted spent one full breath inside her skull, tasting itself, tasting her. Forty-one years of Aldous Hale had been hers for a single heartbeat, and she would carry the shape of that heartbeat forever, even though the weight had moved on to a buyer in some city she'd never see.

Her own memories, meanwhile, stayed where they were. Untouched. Unexamined. A shelf of stones she refused to look under, because she had learned early—Sefa had told her once, drunk, in the voice people use for truths—that a memory-taker who starts stealing from herself doesn't stop until there's nothing left to steal from. Lena had decided at fourteen that she would rather be hollow than empty, and she had kept that bargain for twelve years, and she told herself every night that the bargain was holding.

Most nights she almost believed it.

---

Her house was cold in the way houses get when only one person's body ever warms them. She lit the lamp, counted the widow's coins onto the table—enough for two months, maybe three, if she was careful, and she was always careful—and set the vial in the delivery box by the door, ready for the trader's cart on Thursday. Then she ate standing up, bread and hard cheese, looking out the window at the mountains going blue with dusk.

Somewhere beyond those peaks was the world. Kingdoms, capitals, a king on a throne whose name changed depending on which trader told the story. Magic everywhere, thick as weather. People who could throw fire and speak across distances and, presumably, put memories back where they'd been taken from, mend the bones they broke. Lena had never seen it. No one born in the Veil had, unless they left for good, and leaving for good meant exile—meant the wardens striking your name from the rolls, meant your family mourning you as dead, meant never again feeling the valley's strange silence settle over your skin like a coat that fit.

She had thought about leaving exactly once, at nineteen, standing on the high pass with her pack already strapped. She had gotten as far as the boundary stones, the twin pillars of black rock that marked where the Veil ended and the world began. She remembered putting her hand out toward the open air beyond them, and remembering—suddenly, vividly, with her whole body—Sefa's voice: *the valley keeps us, child, but it also keeps us safe*, and she had turned around and walked home and never spoken of it, not to anyone, not even to Maro.

Maro. She should go see Maro. He'd want to hear about the Hale job, or rather he'd want to hear about the coins, because Maro measured all human experience in currency and had once tried to convince her that joy was undervalued at market. They had grown up together in the foundling house off Tanner's Lane, two orphans the valley had absorbed without comment, and he was the closest thing she had to—

To what? Family? Friend? She turned the words over like coins she suspected were clipped.

The knock came at that moment. Three strikes, soft, uneven—the rhythm of a small fist.

Lena frowned. She had no appointments tonight. Clients came by day, through the front gate, announced. Nobody knocked on her door after dark except Maro, and Maro knocked in fours, a stupid childhood code, *four for a friend.*

Three strikes. Soft. Uneven.

She took the poker from beside the hearth—it was habit, not fear; the Veil was the safest place in the world, everyone said so, the wardens said so, the silence said so—and opened the door.

Rain. That was the first thing. Rain, falling in sheets, though the sky had been clear at dusk, though the Veil's weather was famously gentle, famously predictable. The storm stood over her little house like something that had traveled a long way to arrive.

And in the storm, on her doorstep, stood a child.

Small—six, seven years old, hard to tell. Drowned-gray cloak plastered to a thin frame. Bare feet blue with cold on her stone step. Hair so pale it was nearly white, plastered down like riverweed. The child looked up at her, and Lena saw eyes the color of deep water, old water, water that had been dark a very long time—and the child opened his mouth and spoke.

The sounds meant nothing. They were nothing like the trader-tongues Lena had picked up scraps of over the years, nothing like the formal speech of the wardens' records. The syllables were round and ringing, older-feeling than any language she knew, a language that sounded less invented than *remembered*, as if it had been waiting somewhere beneath all other languages for someone to need it again.

"I don't understand you," Lena said, and the child's face crumpled, and he tried again, faster, desperate, tears cutting channels through the grime on his cheeks, and still it was that ringing, ancient nothing.

Then she saw what he held clutched against his chest with both hands, the way children hold things they've been told never to let go of.

A shard of gold. Jagged, broken-edged, longer than her hand. It caught the lamplight spilling through the doorway and threw it back wrong—not a reflection but a gleam, a light that seemed to come from inside the metal, from underneath it, like a face behind frosted glass.

Every rule Lena had ever learned stood up inside her at once. *Magic cannot enter the Veil.* The shard was glowing. Therefore the shard was not magic, or the rules were lies, or the world had just quietly come to an end on her doorstep. The child was soaked, freezing, alone, and no one crossed the mountains—not traders, not pilgrims, not armies, not lost children. The pass was closed eight months of the year and watched the other four. There was no path by which this boy could exist.

He held the shard up toward her. An offering. Or an appeal.

And Lena—who had spent her whole life touching other people's pasts, who knew better than anyone alive what it cost to hold a memory that wasn't yours—reached out and took it.

The world went white.

Not light. *White.* A throne room assembled itself around her out of nothing: pillars of white stone, banners she didn't recognize, a crowd on its feet and roaring. On the throne sat a man in a crown of gold, young and tired and smiling, smiling at his people, smiling at his—

At his son. A small boy with white hair running up the steps of the dais, arms out, and the king's smile widened, widened, widened past what a face could hold, and behind the king's eyes something else smiled wider still, something vast and patient and hungry, and the little boy reached up for his father's hand and the hand did not move, and the crown was breaking, cracking, a piece shearing away, spinning, falling—

Lena came back to herself on her knees on her own doorstep, rain hammering her shoulders, the shard lying in her lap and burning cold, and the child crouched over her saying her name.

Not a name. *A name.* In that ringing dead tongue, over and over, urgent—but the shape of it, the cadence, was unmistakably the cadence of a name. Her name. A name she had never told this child, a name that no one in the valley used, a name she had not heard spoken aloud in twelve years.

The rain fell. The shard glowed. Somewhere far above the storm, thirteen moons' worth of darkness waited behind the clouds, and the first of them was already turning.
