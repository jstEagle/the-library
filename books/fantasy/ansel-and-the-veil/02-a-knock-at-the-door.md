## 2. A Knock at the Door

The storm came up the valley like something with intent.

Lena heard it before she saw it—a low grinding in the west, where the mountains should have been white and were instead a bruised purple wall of cloud. She was sitting on her roof patching the thatch with one hand and counting coins with the other, which was the only way she knew how to do anything: two tasks at once, so neither had room to hurt.

Forty-one coppers. Three silvers. And the widow's grief, folded up somewhere behind her ribs like a letter she hadn't decided whether to open.

That was the trouble with the work nobody told you about. Taking a memory out of a head was easy—easier than picking a lock, easier than lifting a purse. You found the seam of it, the place where the feeling met the remembering, and you slid your attention in like a thin blade, and you cut. The client wept or laughed or stared at nothing for an hour, and then they woke lighter, and Lena walked home heavier, and that was the whole trade. Nobody asked what happened to what she took. Not even her. Especially not her.

She'd been carrying the widow's grief for six days now. It sat in her chest like wet wool. Grief always did—it was the worst cargo, worse than rage, worse than love, because grief didn't want anything. It just stayed. It made itself at home. Somewhere in Bellharrow or Grayfall or whatever city paid best this season, some merchant's wife would drink it down and feel, for one borrowed hour, the full weight of having lost a husband of thirty years, and then she'd pay, and Lena would be rid of it, and the widow would wake tomorrow not knowing why her hands kept reaching for a second cup.

The Veil did not allow magic. That was its whole reason for existing, if valleys could be said to have reasons. Whatever ran through the world outside—the bright terrible current that let kings level mountains and priests raise their dead halfway back—stopped dead at the ridgeline, as though the valley were a hole in the world's attention. The founders had built here on purpose. Wards older than any kingdom still living hummed in the standing stones along the passes, and no spell had ever crossed them, and no spell ever would.

But memories weren't magic. That was the loophole the thieves had found, generations back. A memory was just meat and time, weight and salt. It could be carried anywhere. So the Veil became what it became: a vault with people inside it. The desperate came down from the outside—or sent brokers, or paid smugglers—and they left their worst years in the heads of people like Lena, and the valley grew rich on other people's pain, and forgot to ask questions, because forgetting was the local industry.

Lena was very good at her job. She tried not to think about what that meant.

The first rain hit the thatch like thrown gravel. She swore, jammed the last wad of straw into the gap, and swung down off the roof onto the mud-slick ground, coins jingling in her pocket. Her house stood at the ragged edge of Hollowmere, the last street before the terraces gave up and became orchard and then wild slope. Small, stone, one door, one window with real glass because she could afford exactly one luxury and had chosen light over warmth. A thief's house: nothing valuable, everything hidden.

The storm swallowed the valley whole. Rain came sideways. Lightning turned the world briefly into a woodcut of itself—black hills, white sky, the single crooked line of the road—and in that flash Lena saw something on the road.

A small shape. Moving against the wind.

She told herself it was a dog. She told herself it was a blown branch. She stood in her doorway with the rain hammering her shoulders and watched the shape come on anyway, stubborn as a debt, and by the third lightning stroke it was close enough to have legs and arms and a face turned up toward hers, and by the fourth it was at her gate.

It was a child. Six, maybe seven years old. Barefoot. Soaked through clothes that were wrong in every particular—not patched, not homespun, not any weave Lena recognized, a fabric that seemed to drink the lightning rather than reflect it. The child stood in the flooded lane with water rushing past its ankles and looked up at her with an expression she had never seen on anyone, adult or child: not fear, not pleading. *Recognition.* As though Lena were the expected thing and the storm merely weather.

Then the child opened its mouth and spoke.

Not common-tongue. Not the trader's pidgin that came up from the lowlands, not the high-flown liturgy the priestesses mumbled at funerals, not anything. The words came out round and ringing, each one shaped like a struck bell, and they landed in Lena's ears with a weight language shouldn't have. She understood none of it and felt all of it. The child wasn't asking to come in. She couldn't have said how she knew, but she knew. The child was *announcing* something. Reporting. The way a soldier reports.

"Inside," Lena said, because the alternative was watching a child freeze in her lane, and whatever else she was, she wasn't that. "Come on, then."

The child crossed her threshold and stopped there, dripping, and looked around the single room with those same recognizing eyes—at the cold hearth, the shelf of jars, the loose floorboard under which forty-four coins now slept—and Lena felt, absurdly, judged.

"Right," she said. "Towel's behind you. Don't touch the board by the hearth, it bites."

The child did not take the towel. The child raised one small fist, which Lena now saw had been clenched this entire time, knuckles white, and opened it.

Light filled the room.

Not firelight, not lightning-echo. Gold—the deep old gold of a wedding ring buried a century in river silt and then brought up clean. It came from a jagged shard the size of a man's thumb, irregular as broken flint, and it threw shapes on the walls that had no business existing: towers, thrones, a mountain crowned with something too bright to look at. Lena's skin prickled from wrist to shoulder. Every hair on her arms lifted and pointed at the shard like iron filings finding north.

And underneath the prickle, underneath the wonder, a colder thing woke up and started screaming.

*Magic.* In the Veil. In her house. Where magic could not go, had never gone, where the wards on the passes had held since before the kingdom had a name. Either the wards had failed—and if they'd failed, everything the valley was, everything keeping the kings and their wars and their hungry bright power out, had failed—or—

Or the thing in the child's hand hadn't come *through* the wards. It had come *around* them somehow, or under them, or it was older than they were.

"Put that down," Lena said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "Put it on the table. Slowly."

The child looked at her. Looked at the table. Did not move.

Rain drummed. Lightning walked the ridge. And Lena Zhou, who had spent her whole life taking things from other people's heads without ever letting anything into her own, heard herself say the stupidest sentence of her career:

"Can I see it?"

* * *

Later—much later, when she had time to be afraid properly—she would try to reconstruct the decision. There wasn't one. That was the honest answer and she hated it. There was no moment where she weighed the risk and chose. There was only the shard lying on her scarred oak table throwing its impossible shadows, and the child watching her with the patience of stone, and her own hand moving across the space between them as though someone else were steering it.

Her fingertips touched the gold.

The world inverted.

She was standing in a hall so tall its ceiling was a rumor—pillars of white stone going up and up into gloom, banners hanging like sleeping bats. Incense. Cold air moving at her ankles. She was looking at a king.

He sat on a throne of the same white stone, and he was young—younger than her, surely, though crowns age people strangely—and he was smiling. It was a good smile. It reached his eyes. He was leaning forward to listen to someone Lena couldn't see, and his whole face was open, and Lena thought, with a strange domestic pang, *he looks like a man who fixes things*, a man who mended fences and remembered birthdays.

Then the smile widened.

It kept widening past the point where a face could hold it. The eyes stayed kind and the mouth went on stretching, gently, terribly, like a door opening in a house where no one is home, and behind the king's eyes—behind them, deeper in, in the dark where a person keeps the self that watches itself—something else looked out. Something patient. Something that had been smiling for a very long time and intended to go on.

The hall flickered. Now the king was older, grey at the temples, signing a document while the smile behind his eyes watched his own hands move. Now he was younger again, kneeling in the white hall, alone, speaking words in that bell-toned language—the child's language—and the words had the cadence of a vow. Now he was in a garden, laughing, holding a small boy upside down by the ankles while the boy shrieked with joy, and the smile on the king's face was real, was entirely his own, was the truest thing Lena had seen in the whole flood of images—and the thing behind his eyes leaned closer to that laugh the way a starving man leans toward a window full of cooking smells.

Now—

Now she was somewhere else entirely. Wet stone. Dark. A woman's voice saying names, a list of names, reciting them like a rosary, and every name made Lena's chest ache with a grief she recognized because she was *carrying* it, she had been carrying it for six days, the widow's grief, the wet-wool weight of it—but this wasn't the widow's voice, this was someone else mourning someone else, except the shape of the sorrow was identical, sorrow was sorrow, and—

More. A market under colored awnings. A battlefield seen from very high up, so the dead looked like spilled ink. A kitchen, bread, a dog. A coronation. A funeral. A hundred lives braided together, none of them hers, all of them passing through her like water through a net, and she understood, in the last instant before it spat her out, that these were stolen memories—that she was holding memories that had been *taken*, the way she took them, from someone, for someone, and they had been waiting, all this time, in a piece of broken gold, for a pair of hands like hers.

Lena came back to herself on the floor.

She was on her back beside the table. The storm was still going; the window glass shivered in its frame. Her mouth tasted of copper and incense. The child crouched over her, close enough that she could see the rainwater still drying in its hair, and its expression had changed at last—from recognition to something more careful. Waiting. The way you watch a lock you've just picked, to see if the mechanism forgives you.

"What," Lena said. It came out as a rasp. She tried again. "What *was* that."

The child tilted its head. Then it pressed the shard briefly against its own sternum, pointed at Lena, and pointed at her own chest, and made a gesture she knew from the markets of Grayfall: the universal sign for *carried goods*. Cargo. Freight.

*You took it,* the gesture said. *You're carrying it.*

"I don't know what you—" She stopped. Sat up. The room swayed and settled. The widow's grief was still there behind her ribs, wet wool, familiar—but around it now, faint as a watermark, were the others. The woman reciting names. The garden, the boy, the laugh. They sat in her like coins in a pocket she hadn't known she owned.

Nothing left the Veil. Nothing entered it. Those were the two facts her whole life stood on, the walls of the world, and one of them had just broken in her doorway soaked in rainwater, and the other had broken when she touched the gold.

Because that was the thing she couldn't stop circling, the thing making her hands shake now as she pushed herself up onto the bench: the shard had shown her those memories. Had *pushed* them into her. That was magic—she didn't know the theory but she knew the taste of it now, that bell-weight, that current—and magic did not work in the Veil. Couldn't. Unless the rules had a seam in them too, a place where a thin blade could slide in.

Unless the rules had been waiting, all this time, for a thief.

Outside, the storm was turning itself inside out over the mountains, and somewhere in it, she knew with a certainty that had nothing to do with sense, more things were coming. Things that wanted the shard. Things that had perhaps been trying to get into the valley for a very long time and had finally found a crack, and the crack was a mute child on her doorstep.

The child climbed onto the bench beside her, uninvited, and set the shard down between them with great care, the way you'd set down a sleeping animal. Then it looked at her and pointed, once, east—toward the far side of the valley, toward the capital she'd only ever seen in the memories of clients who'd fled it.

Then it spoke again, that bell-language, three words this time, and though she understood nothing, the shape of them went through her like a needle through cloth:

Urgent. Counting. Almost finished.

Thirteen, said the cadence of it, though there was no word she could name. Thirteen, and nearly done.

Lena looked at the shard, and the shard threw its golden shadows across her walls—towers, thrones, a mountain wearing a crown of light—and behind her ribs, among the freight of strangers' sorrows, something that was not hers turned over in its sleep and began, softly, to dream.

She should burn it. She should carry it to the elders at dawn and let them do what the valley had always done, which was bury whatever wouldn't fit and call the burial peace.

Instead, Lena pulled the blanket off her bed and put it around the child's shoulders, and sat down to wait out the storm.
