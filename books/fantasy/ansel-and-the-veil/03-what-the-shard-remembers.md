## 3. What the Shard Remembers

The shard did not let go of her.

Lena had set it on the table after the child fell asleep—set it down the way you'd put down a snake you weren't sure was dead—and still she could feel it against her palm, a warmth like a coin held too long in a fist. It sat there in the lamplight, jagged and gold and utterly wrong for the Veil, where nothing gleamed unless someone had polished it by hand.

She should have buried it in the ash-pit behind the smokehouse. That was the sensible thing. The Veil had one law older than the wardens, older than the elders' long grey robes: nothing from outside comes in, and if it does, it goes back out or into the ground. Magic could not cross the boundary stones. Everyone said so. Everyone had always said so.

And yet the shard had touched her mind through two layers of skin and a lifetime of practiced walls, and what it had poured into her was still settling in her chest like silt in a flooded cellar.

She picked it up again. Of course she did. Curiosity was the only hunger that had never left her.

The moment her fingers closed around it, the room went away.

* * *

She stood in a hall so tall its ceiling vanished into gold haze. Pillars marched away on either side of her, each one carved with faces—kings, she thought, generations of them, their stone eyes worn smooth by centuries of upturned gazes. Banners hung between the pillars, deep blue shot with silver, though the silver had faded to the color of old teeth.

On a throne at the far end sat a man.

He was not what she expected. She hadn't known she'd expected anything, but whatever it was, it wasn't this: a broad-shouldered man in his middle years, dark hair going grey at the temples, dressed simply—a soldier's plain tunic under an unadorned mantle of state. He looked tired in the way Lena recognized instantly, the way you look when you have been carrying something heavy for so long you've forgotten it isn't part of your body.

He was smiling.

That was the first wrong thing. Not that he smiled—he looked like a man who had smiled often, whose face knew the shape of it—but that the smile didn't move. It sat on his mouth like paint on a mask, fixed and pleasant, while his eyes stared out at some middle distance where no one else seemed able to look.

Lena drifted closer. In the vision—or the memory, or whatever this was—no one saw her. Courtiers moved around her like fish around a stone. A chamberlain read from a scroll. A delegation of farmers knelt with bowed heads, presenting something she couldn't see. And through all of it the king sat smiling his painted smile, and his hands rested on the arms of the throne with the perfect stillness of hands that had forgotten they were hands.

*Something is wrong with him,* she thought, and the thought itself felt like a bell rung underwater.

She moved around the throne's side, close enough to see the fine grain of the wood, the king's jaw, the pulse ticking slow in his throat. Slow. Too slow. She counted without meaning to—one beat for every five of hers.

Then the king turned his head and looked directly at her, and Lena's whole body went cold.

Because the smile widened.

Not the mouth—the mouth stayed exactly where it was, that same fixed pleasant curve—but behind the eyes, deep in the wet dark of them, something *smiled*. Something leaned forward behind the glass of the king's face the way a child presses its face to a window, and Lena felt it looking at her with an attention so vast and so patient that her knees nearly went.

*There you are,* it did not say, because it had no need of words. The feeling arrived whole, the way memories arrived when she took them: *There you are. I know your work. I have tasted your work.*

The hall shattered into gold light.

* * *

Lena came back to herself sitting on the floor with her back against the bedframe, the shard lying in her lap, her heart slamming. Grey dawn light stood in the window. Her tea, made hours ago, had gone cold and skin-filmed beside her.

In the bed, the child was awake, watching her with those dark, unblinking eyes.

"Don't do that," Lena rasped. Her voice sounded scraped. "Don't—whatever that was, don't share it."

The child tilted his head. Then he slid off the bed, crossed the floor, and sat down across from her, cross-legged, solemn as a small monk. He pointed at the shard. Then at her. Then he pressed both hands flat over his own heart, and his face did something complicated—fear and urgency braided together—and he pointed at the shard again.

"I don't understand you," Lena said. "I don't understand any of this."

But she was beginning to, in the way you begin to understand a fever—as a rising heat you can't argue with. She thought about the king on his throne. She thought about the thing behind his eyes, and how it had recognized her. *I have tasted your work.* She thought about the crates that came down from the capital every season, the ones Old Sef never let her count, the ones she took her stolen goods from without asking questions because asking questions was how thieves ended up in the ash-pit themselves.

Thirteen years of thefts. Thirteen years of memories passing through her hands, out of the Veil, up the mountain road, into the capital.

Into him.

"No," she said aloud, to the room, to the shard, to the cold tea. "No, I'm a thief, not a—I take things *from* people, I don't feed—" She stopped. Pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "I'm talking to a child who doesn't speak common and a piece of jewelry. This is what madness looks like. Good to know."

The child reached out and touched her wrist. Gently. The way you'd touch a frightened animal.

And then he got up, went to the cold hearth, crouched, and began to draw in the grey ash with one deliberate finger.

* * *

First he drew a mountain range—a long, careful line of peaks. Below it, a valley, and in the valley a cluster of marks that Lena realized, with a strange lurch, were meant to be the Veil: the smokehouse, the wardens' hall, her own crooked little house rendered in six strokes of ash.

"You've been here three days," she said. "How do you know the layout?"

He didn't answer. He drew a road winding up out of the valley, over the mountains, down into lowlands where he scratched small squares—villages, towns—each one placed with the confidence of someone who had walked the road himself or studied it until it lived in his bones. The road ended at a river, and on the river a city, and above the city a shape Lena knew only from the memory the shard had shown her: a great hall, pillars, banners.

The palace.

The child tapped the city twice. Then he tapped his own chest. Then, slowly, watching her face the whole time, he drew a circle above the palace—a crown, or a moon, or both—and broke it into pieces with quick strokes. Four fragments, scattered outward like petals torn from a flower. One fragment he drew falling toward the mountains. Toward the Veil.

Toward them.

"That's a story," Lena said. "Children draw stories."

He shook his head—slow, emphatic, frustrated—and grabbed her hand. He pressed her palm flat against the ash drawing, over the broken circle, and held it there, and looked up at her with an expression that was not a child's expression at all. It was the expression of someone asking her to carry something, and knowing the weight of it, and asking anyway.

Lena looked at his hand on hers. Small. Chapped from the storm. There was a scar across the back of it, old and white, the kind that came from a burn.

"Someone hurt you," she said quietly. "A long time ago. Before you came here."

His chin trembled, just once. He mastered it. He was, Lena thought, the most controlled person she had ever met, and he was perhaps seven years old.

He let go of her hand and added one more mark to the map: a small figure standing outside the city walls. Then, beside it, another figure—smaller, drawn holding the first one's hand.

Lena stared at it for a long time.

"Is that you?" she asked. "Is that—who? Who's holding your hand?"

The child looked at the two figures in the ash, and his face closed like a door. He wiped them away with the side of his fist, hard, smearing the whole corner of the map, and sat back on his heels and would not look at her.

"All right," Lena said softly. "All right. We'll come back to that."

She rose, went to the window, and looked out at the Veil doing what the Veil always did at dawn: smoke rising from chimneys, wardens walking their rounds along the boundary stones, the mill wheel turning, everything gray and quiet and safe. Somewhere out there, Widow Anselm was waking up lighter than she'd been in thirty years, missing a grief she couldn't name, and someone in the capital was wearing it like a coat.

*I have tasted your work.*

Her hands had fed that thing for thirteen years. Whatever sat behind the king's eyes had grown fat and patient on memories she'd pried loose and shipped up the mountain, crate by crate, coin by coin. She had told herself she was a midwife of pain—that she lifted suffering out of people and that was a mercy, whatever happened to it afterward. She had never once followed the crates past the boundary stones.

The child tugged her sleeve. He pointed at the map, then at her, then made a walking motion with two fingers across his palm.

"I can't," she said. "You don't understand what you're asking. Nobody leaves the Veil. Nobody leaves and comes back, and nobody leaves and *stays* gone either—the wardens keep records, they have people in every town on the lowland roads. And even if we got out—even if—" She gestured at the shard on the table. "That thing showed me a king with a demon behind his face. You want us to walk a hundred leagues to fight it? With what? I steal memories for coin. I'm good at it. That's the whole of what I am."

The child considered her for a long moment. Then he did something he had never done in three days under her roof.

He smiled.

It was a small smile, crooked, one-sided, gone almost as soon as it came—but it transformed his whole face, and Lena felt it land somewhere beneath her ribs like a dropped stone. Because she had seen that smile before. Not on him. On the king, in the vision—the shape of the mouth, the tilt of it, the way one corner rose before the other.

Family resemblance. Impossible, absurd, and written as plainly as the ash map.

"Oh," Lena said. Very quietly. "Oh, you're *his*, aren't you. Whoever he is now—whatever's wearing him—you're his blood."

The child's smile faded. He nodded once, a single grave dip of the head, and put his finger on the map again, on the road, on the city, and then on his own heart.

*Come with me. Help me. Please.*

Lena turned away from him and pressed her forehead against the cold window glass.

* * *

They came for the shard at noon.

She heard them coming—three sets of formal footsteps on her street, the particular cadence of elders trying to walk like ordinary neighbors—and she had just enough time to drop the shard into the flour jar and shove the child behind her before the knock came.

"Lena Zhou." Elder Cassia's voice through the door, dry as August grass. "Open, please."

Cassia stood on her step flanked by Elder Rho and young Elder Tam, who still had the decency to look uncomfortable. All three wore their grey hoods back, which meant formality, which meant judgment already delivered and merely awaiting ceremony.

"Elders." Lena kept her voice bored. "To what do I owe—"

"The warden on night rounds saw light in your window past the lawful hour. Two nights running." Cassia's eyes went past Lena's shoulder, found the child, and narrowed. "And a stranger has been seen entering and leaving this house. A stranger not entered in any register."

"He's a foundling. Half-drowned in the storm. I was going to bring him to the hall once he could walk straight."

"You were going to bring him *now*." Cassia held out her hand. "And the light, Lena. What burned in your window two nights past?"

"A candle. Elders forgive me, I lost track of the hour. It happens."

"It happens," Cassia agreed mildly. "Once. Tam, search the house."

Tam hesitated. Lena watched the hesitation and hated herself for calculating, automatically, whether she could take the shard and run—not out of the house, out of the *valley*—before they caught her. Three days ago that thought would have been unthinkable. Now it sat in her chest like a coal.

"Wait," she said.

Everyone stopped.

She went to the flour jar. For one last heartbeat she stood with her hand inside it, fingers curled around the warm gold edge of the shard, feeling it hum against her skin like a living thing, like a heart that wasn't hers but knew her name anyway. Behind the king's eyes, something had smiled at her. *I have tasted your work.* Every crate she'd ever packed had fed it. Every coin she'd earned was minted from its appetite.

She could give them the shard. They would bury it in the ash-pit, or throw it in the deep pool below the falls, and the Veil would go back to being gray and safe and forgetting, and she would go back to lifting griefs out of widows for clients she never saw, and in twenty years she would be Old Sef, hollow and precise, counting other people's losses in other people's ledgers.

Or.

She pulled her hand out empty and stepped aside. "Search, then."

They found it in four minutes. Of course they did; Tam was thorough and the flour jar was the third place anyone would look. Cassia held the shard up to the daylight with two fingers, like a dead rat, and her face did not change, but Lena saw her thumb flinch away from the metal as if it had bitten her.

"Where did this come from?"

"The child had it when I found him."

"Where did the child come from?"

"Out of the storm. I told you."

"Nothing crosses the boundary stones, Lena." Cassia's voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "Nothing enters. Nothing leaves. That is not a rule we enforce; it is a law the world enforces, and we are merely its custodians. So either this object did not cross the stones—which is impossible—or the law has changed, and if the law has changed, then everything the Veil is built upon is already breaking, and the only sane response is to destroy this thing before the break spreads." She turned to Tam. "The ash-pit. Melt it, sink it, salt the ground after. Go."

"Wait." Lena heard her own voice and was surprised by how steady it came out. "Before you destroy it. Ask it one question."

Cassia paused. "Objects don't answer questions, girl."

"This one does. Touch it. Really touch it, the way I touch a memory. Just once. Tell me what you see." Lena held the elder's gaze. "If you see nothing—if it's just pretty metal—bury it and I'll help you dig. But if you see what I saw, you'll want to know what's sitting on the throne of this kingdom before you decide the safest thing to do is look away."

Silence on the step. Rho shifted. Tam looked at Cassia, and Cassia looked at the shard, and for one suspended moment Lena thought—hoped—the old woman's curiosity would win. Elders were made of curiosity; that was the terrible secret of them. It was why they'd taken the grey hood in the first place, back when they were young and hungry for the world's edges.

Then Cassia's face shut like a ledger closing.

"We are custodians, not adventurers. The Veil survives because we do not ask what glitters beyond the stones." She handed the shard to Tam. "Destroy it. And Lena Zhou—you will come before the hall tonight, and you will account for the stranger, and you will account for yourself."

They turned to go. And Lena, standing in her doorway with the child's small hand finding hers and gripping it hard, heard herself say:

"No."

Cassia stopped. Did not turn around. "No?"

"The shard stays. The boy stays. And I won't be accounting for anything tonight, because I won't be here." Her heart was hammering so hard she could feel it in her teeth, and underneath the fear ran something she hadn't felt in thirteen years of careful, competent, hollowing work: *purpose*, bright and terrifying as a struck match. "You taught me the rules, Elder. Nothing enters, nothing leaves, ask nothing, want nothing. I followed them my whole life. And something out there is eating a king, and it's been eating him with my help, and I am done following rules that exist so we don't have to know that."

Cassia turned then. She looked old, suddenly, and tired, and for a flicker of a second—there and gone—she looked almost envious.

"You leave the Veil," she said quietly, "and you cannot come back. Not unchanged. The wardens will hunt you, and worse than the wardens, the valley itself will forget you were ever in it. Is that what you choose?"

Lena looked down at the child. He was watching her with those dark unblinking eyes, and he was not begging, and he was not afraid. He was simply waiting, the way you wait for weather.

"Somebody has to remember him," Lena said. "Somebody has to remember all of it. If not me, then who?"

She closed the door.

Through the window she watched the three grey figures stand a moment on her step, then turn, unhurried, toward the wardens' hall. She had until nightfall, maybe less. She needed rope, food, a route past the eastern stones where the mist thinned, and one ally foolish enough to come with her—and she knew exactly one person in the whole gray valley foolish enough.

The child tugged her sleeve and pointed at the flour jar, at the smear of white on the table where the shard had lain.

"Yeah," Lena said, reaching for her coat. "I know. We're going to need a better hiding place. Pack light, kid—we leave before the lamps are lit."

Outside, far up the valley wall, the first of the thirteen moons was rising pale over the boundary stones, earlier than it should have been, and neither of them saw it yet.
