## 4. Thirteen Turns

Ash dreams in pictures because pictures are the only language the world has not taken from him.

In the dream he is small again, smaller than he is now, and his father's hand is a warm country around his own. The throne room stretches out like a hall carved from the inside of a shell, all pearl and shadow, and the light comes down in ropes through high windows and lies across the floor in golden bars that they step over, one, two, three, like children playing a game whose only rule is laughter.

His father is laughing.

That is where Ash goes when sleep takes him: back to the sound of his father laughing, before the laughing changed.

* * *

Here is what Ash remembers, and remember is a cruel word for it, because remembering is all he has left, and every day there is less:

He remembers the first moon. It rose the night the war ended, fat and white as a peeled egg, hanging over the capital like an eye that would not blink. The people sang in the streets. His father stood on the balcony with tears on his face and told the crowd that peace had come at last, that the price had been paid, that no one should ever ask what the price was.

*Never ask,* his father said, and his voice cracked like ice underfoot, *and you will be happy.*

The crowd was happy. That is how it works here. Ash learned that early. In this kingdom happiness is not something you feel; it is something that is done to you, gently, from above, like having your hair combed by someone who loves you or like being smothered with a pillow by someone who does not know their own strength.

He remembers the second moon rising beside the first, and the third, each one arriving without season or reason, and no one finding this strange, because asking why the moons had multiplied was a kind of asking, and asking had gone out of fashion the way lamps go out one by one down a street until the whole road is dark and no one remembers needing light to walk.

He remembers his mother's hands. He will not describe them yet. Some pictures are too heavy to lift; they must be carried a little while longer before they can be set down.

And he remembers—the way you remember a fire, the way you remember falling—the twelfth moon.

* * *

The twelfth turn came in autumn, though autumn had grown only a rumor by then, thin as a coin rubbed smooth between two thumbs.

By the twelfth turn the palace had already begun its slow change, which no one named aloud. The servants smiled more than servants should. The guards' eyes had gone flat and pleasant, like the eyes painted on shutters to frighten birds. Meals tasted of nothing and everyone said they were delicious. Once, in a corridor, Ash watched a chambermaid stop mid-stride and stand perfectly still for the length of sixty heartbeats, her face slack as a bed left unmade, and then resume walking with a smile so wide it looked like a wound turned inside out, and when he asked her if she was well she said, *Oh yes, little lord, I have never been better, I cannot remember ever being better, I cannot remember anything at all*, and she laughed, and the laugh had nothing inside it, and she went on down the corridor smiling at the walls.

Little lord. She still called him that. That was before.

On the night of the twelfth turn, Ash climbed to the observatory because his father had stopped coming to say goodnight, and Ash wanted to find him the way children find things: by going where they are not supposed to go.

The observatory smelled of dust and old brass and, underneath, faintly, of rot—sweet rot, the kind that comes from fruit left too long in a silver bowl, beautiful and wrong. The great lens of the telescope lay open to the sky, and through it the twelve moons hung in their impossible ring, twelve pale coins, twelve closed mouths.

His father stood at the rail below the lens, alone, in his nightshirt, which he had never done before; kings do not wander halls in their nightshirts. His hair was loose. His shoulders were shaking.

And he was talking to himself—but Ash, flattened against the doorframe with his heart going like a trapped bird, understood within three breaths that his father was not talking to himself.

*I have kept my word,* his father said to the empty air, and the empty air seemed to lean in around him, attentive, the way a crowd leans in. *Twelve turns. Twelve. You have fed as we agreed. The kingdom is quiet. No one fights. No one grieves. I have given you grief enough to drown in—*

The air answered.

It did not answer in words. That is important. Even now, even in memory, Ash can render it only as image, because the thing that spoke did not use language; it used absence. What Ash saw, standing in the dark of the observatory, was this: a room after the people have left it. A table still laid for supper. A name half-written on a letter and then scratched out. It was the feeling of reaching for a word and finding the place where the word used to be, and the thing that lived in that place opened what might have been a smile and said, without speaking:

*TWO MORE.*

His father's head drooped like a flower cut at the stem. *One,* he whispered. *You said one. One turn and it ends. One turn and you release what you took. You swore it by the old tongue, and the old tongue holds—*

The air rippled with something that was almost laughter. Ash felt it pass through him like cold water through cloth, and in its wake it left a picture, planted in his mind the way a thief plants a stolen ring in an innocent pocket:

A garden. Rows and rows of names growing in it like flowers, and a figure moving down the rows, picking them, one by one, and eating them, and the figure wore his father's crown, and the figure's face—

The figure's face was his father's face, but wider behind the eyes. Wider, and stiller, and patient the way stones are patient.

*TWO MORE,* the absence said again, and then, with a cruelty so casual it took Ash years to understand it was deliberate:

*AND THE SMALL ONE FIRST. THE SMALL ONE IS ALMOST RIPE. YOU WILL NOT REMEMBER HIM PAST THE THIRTEENTH TURN, AND THEN HE WILL BE MINE TO PICK LIKE ALL THE OTHERS.*

His father came up off the rail with a roar that Ash had never heard from him, not in war stories, not in grief, and he seized the air where the voice had been with both hands, and of course his hands closed on nothing, and he stood there clutching emptiness in front of the telescope like a man trying to strangle the dark between stars.

*Not him,* his father said, and his voice broke into pieces, and the pieces were the worst thing Ash had ever heard, because each piece was full of love. *Take me. Take everything. Take my mother's face, take my father's voice, take the smell of rain on the field where I was crowned—take it all, I gave you leave to take it all—but leave the boy. Leave my son. He is the only—*

He stopped.

He stopped mid-word, the way a clock stops.

For a long moment he stood very still, hands still raised, mouth still open around the shape of a word that had been taken out of it. Then his arms lowered slowly, slowly, and his face smoothed over like water after a stone has sunk, and when he turned toward the door Ash flung himself back against the wall and made himself small and silent as a held breath.

The king walked past the doorway without seeing him.

Three steps beyond it, the king stopped. Turned. Frowned down at Ash with the polite, distant puzzlement of a man looking at a stranger who seems somehow familiar—a man met once at a fair, perhaps, or a face seen in a crowd.

Then the frown cleared. The smile came up under it, smooth as oil rising through water.

*Can I help you, little one?* his father said kindly, in the voice he used for petitioners.

And Ash, who could not answer—who had never been able to answer, whose voice had been taken long before this night, so that the thing wearing his father's smile could never be accused of stealing a child's words along with everything else—Ash said nothing, because there was nothing he could say.

His father patted his head, the way you pat a dog, and walked on down the corridor toward his rooms, humming.

Ash stayed against the wall for a long time. He did not cry. Crying is loud, and loudness had become dangerous; the smiling ones heard loudness and came to see what was wrong and helped you, always helpfully, helpfully, helpfully, until you smiled too.

Instead he did the only thing left to do. He took out the picture of his father laughing in the throne room, stepping over bars of gold light, and he looked at it, and folded it small, and hid it in the deepest room of himself, behind everything else, in a place so far back that even the thing that ate memories might grow tired of searching before it found it.

*Come find me when it is over,* he told the picture. *Wait for me here.*

Then he went to pack.

* * *

Here is what Ash knows that Lena does not yet know, though he has tried, oh how he has tried, pressing the knowledge at her through the shard, through drawings, through the fierce dumb pressure of his hands:

There are thirteen moons. Twelve have turned.

On the thirteenth turn, the binding completes. The garden of names will be harvested down to the roots. The king will not merely forget his son; the king will forget that there was ever a son to forget, and the small erased boy will become what all the picked flowers become—not dead, never dead, just eaten, kept, worn, part of the wide still patience behind his father's eyes.

And it is soon. It is sooner than soon. He can feel it in the shard he carries, the jagged piece of his father's crown that he tore free on the worst night of his life and ran with, barefoot, through corridors of smiling people, out gates that opened for him because no one could quite remember whether they were supposed to stop him. The shard hums against his chest like a struck bell that will not finish ringing. Each day the hum climbs a note. He has counted. There are not many notes left.

He knows the way to the capital. Of course he knows it; he was born in the palace at the end of that road, before the road forgot him. But his feet are small and the wardens watch the valley passes and the elders have noses like hunting dogs for anything that should not be in the Veil.

Which means he needs her. The tall one with the careful hands and the hungry eyes, the one who touched his shard and went white as flour and saw what he sees.

The woman who steals memories. Who does not know yet what she is, what her gift is *for*, what it has been sharpening itself against all these years in the dark of the valley.

She thinks she is a thief.

Ash closes his eyes and sees her clearly: Lena, standing in her small stone house with the storm behind her, holding the shard at arm's length as if it were a snake that might also be a key. Around her, in the picture he makes of her, he draws—for this is how he thinks, in lines and colors, in the grammar of images—he draws a door. Because that is what she is. A door that does not know it is a door. And doors, if you are patient, if you wait out the storm, open.

Through the wall of the little house he can hear her moving, packing, muttering to herself about hiding places and lamp-lighting and better plans. Alive and ordinary and gloriously unaware.

Above the valley, earlier than any moon has a right to rise, the thirteenth moon is climbing the dark, pale as a peeled egg, patient as a stone, opening its white mouth over the world.

Two more, it had promised his father.

But promises, Ash knows better than anyone alive, are only words.

And words can be stolen back.

* * *

He sits up in the dark on his pallet by the hearth and takes out the shard and holds it cupped in both hands, and it shows him what it always shows him when the night gets long: the king on the throne, smiling, and behind the king's eyes something else smiling wider, and beneath the king's feet, so small you could miss it, so small it could fit in a thief's cupped hands—a boy, erased, waiting to be remembered.

*Hold on, Father,* he thinks, in the old tongue, the tongue the Quiet cannot eat because it lives in pictures, not words. *I am bringing someone.*

Down the valley, in the village of the careful-handed woman, a lamp gutters and relights. Packing sounds. A door.

Soon, then.

Ash puts the shard away, folds his arms around the folded picture of his father laughing, and lets himself, for one more hour, be only a child in the dark, listening to the future walk toward him through the storm.
