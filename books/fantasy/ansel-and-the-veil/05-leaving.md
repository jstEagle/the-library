## 5. Leaving

The wardens changed shifts at the third bell after midnight, and for eleven minutes the eastern palisade was watched by a man named Doss who owed Lena two favors and had never once asked what either of them cost.

She counted those minutes now from the shadow of the tannery wall, Ash pressed against her side beneath a wool cloak three sizes too big, his breath fogging in the cold like a small engine of fear. The shard was wrapped in linen and bound against her ribs with a strip of leather, and even through the cloth she could feel it—warm, patient, faintly humming, like a sleeping animal that knew it was going somewhere.

"You're sure about Doss," Maro whispered at her shoulder.

"I'm sure about his debts."

"Debts rot." Maro shifted, and the buckles on his coat clicked until he stilled them with a hand. He'd dressed for travel the way he did everything: too much, too loud, prepared for a journey that would require rope, three knives, and apparently a coil of waxed cord he kept touching like a rosary. "People don't."

"People pay." She didn't look at him. Looking at Maro when he was worried was like looking directly into a lamp; you came away seeing spots for an hour. "He'll leave the gate bar loose and forget to mention it to anyone. That's all I need him to do. Forgetting is the one thing this valley does well."

Ash made a sound against her hip—not language, not quite. A note of protest. He'd been making it since sundown, every time her hand drifted toward the shard or her eyes toward the dark line of the palisade. It took her two days to understand it wasn't fear. It was *urgency*. The child had a clock inside him that none of them could see, and it was ticking somewhere behind his sternum, and every hour they spent planning was an hour he counted against them with those enormous, unblinking eyes.

"We go on your signal," Maro said. "And if it goes wrong—"

"It won't go wrong."

"Everything goes wrong eventually. My mother used to say it. Then she said it again when it did." He exhaled through his teeth. "I'm coming with you."

Lena turned then, because that deserved looking at. "No."

"Yes."

"Maro."

"Lena." He said her name the way he said everything, lightly, as though it were a joke only he had heard yet. But his eyes weren't light. They hadn't been since she'd told him, three nights ago in the back room of the Gilded Tooth, what the shard had shown her—the king on his throne, the thing behind his eyes smiling wider than the face could hold. "You're my oldest friend. You're also the only person in this valley who's ever beaten me at cards, which means you cheat better than I do, which means you need supervision. Those are my reasons. I have others but they're embarrassing."

"This isn't a job."

"No," he agreed. "It's worse. Jobs end."

"The Veil doesn't let people back in unchanged. You know what crossing does. You've seen the ones who tried."

"I've seen the ones who tried alone, drunk, in winter, carrying everything they owned." He tapped his chest. "I'm sober, it's almost spring, and I'm carrying nothing I own worth keeping. Different animal entirely."

She wanted to argue. She had arguments banked like coins—danger, futility, the simple arithmetic that two people moving through the dark made twice the noise. But underneath the arguments was the thing she couldn't say out loud: that the boundary was real, that magic pooled against it like water against glass, and that whatever waited outside the valley had already touched her once, through a scrap of gold, and left fingerprints on the inside of her skull that hadn't washed off.

If she crossed, she would change. Everyone knew it. The wardens' whole purpose was built on it. And she did not know into what.

Ash tugged her cloak. When she looked down he was holding up his hands, cupped together, and in them he'd arranged three pebbles and a button in a pattern she recognized from two nights ago: the map he'd drawn in spilled tea on her table. Three shapes for three travelers. He looked up at her with the terrible directness of children and the very old, and he nodded once at Maro.

"Traitor," Lena told him softly.

Ash smiled. It was the first time she'd seen him do it, and it rearranged his whole face, and she understood suddenly why the elders wanted the shard destroyed and the child forgotten: because some things, once seen smiling, could not be unseen, and the Veil ran on not-seeing.

* * *

Doss kept his eleven minutes and lost maybe ninety seconds of them to nerves, pacing the length of the east gate with a lantern he kept forgetting to raise. Through the gap between planks Lena watched him stop, look at the bar, look away, look at the bar again—a man rehearsing innocence so hard he was nearly guilty of something.

The bar was pine, old, swollen with forty years of rain. It lifted easier than she'd expected, and the sound it made was small, a wooden sigh, swallowed by wind.

Beyond the gate the world simply stopped being the Veil.

That was the strange part, later, the part she'd try and fail to describe: there was no wall, no shimmer, no line drawn in the dirt. The road continued. The pines continued. The moon hung where moons hang. But the air itself was different—thinner, somehow, or thicker, as though the valley had been a room and she had just stepped out of it and discovered the room had been holding its breath around her all her life.

They walked. Maro insisted on rearguard, checking their backtrail every hundred paces with a competence that annoyed her because it was genuine. Ash held her hand and did not let go, and his grip tightened by degrees as they went, as though he could feel the distance opening behind them the way you feel a fever breaking.

The boundary found them a mile out, where the road dipped through a hollow full of ferns and the pines grew older, taller, wrong for the valley's soil.

Lena felt it before she saw anything: a coolness sliding over her skin like wading into a river in early spring. Not cold exactly. *Alive*-cold. The difference between still water and moving. It poured over her shoulders, down her arms, pooled in the hollows of her throat, and every hair on her body rose to meet it, and somewhere below thought, below memory, some animal part of her that had lived its whole life in a place where magic could not follow recognized what was touching it and sang.

"Oh," she said. Just that. A single syllable wrung out of her.

"What?" Maro's voice cracked upward. "What is it? Is it happening? Do I look different?"

"You look stupid."

"That's normal, then." But his voice shook, and when she glanced back his eyes were wide and wet and fixed on his own hands, turning them over as though expecting them to glow.

It passed through them and kept going, downhill, toward the valley, and Lena stood in the hollow and felt it recede and felt something else rise in its place—something that started in her chest as exhilaration, bright and total, the feeling of a lock opening, of a debt forgiven, of a door she'd been told was painted on actually swinging wide—

—and curdled, slowly, as the moon climbed, into homesickness.

Not for a house. She'd never had one worth the word. For the *shape* of things. For the widow whose grief she'd carried home last month in a jar of silence. For the smell of the tannery and the Gilded Tooth's bad wine and the elders' voices droning rules she'd spent thirty years pretending to obey. For a life that had been small and hollowing and entirely, safely hers. She stood in a foreign hollow under foreign stars and grieved for a prison she'd chosen, and hated herself for it, and kept grieving anyway, because that was the truth of leaving: you did not get to pick what you mourned.

Ash pulled her hand. He pointed back the way they'd come, at the darkness where the valley lay, and then he pointed forward, down the road, and his expression was gentle about it, which was somehow worse.

"I know," she said. "I'm coming."

* * *

They walked until the sky went grey at the edges, and made camp in the lee of a fallen tree that had been dead longer than Lena had been alive, its trunk soft with moss, hosting a civilization of beetles. Maro built a fire with the efficiency of long habit and produced, from his absurd pack, dried fish, hard cheese, a bag of candied ginger, and a tin of tea leaves he guarded with his life.

"You planned this," Lena said.

"I plan everything. Planning is how I stay charming instead of desperate."

"How long?"

He fed the fire a twig. "Since you came to me with the shard story and lied about half of it."

"I didn't lie."

"You said you'd handle it. That's a lie people tell with their whole chest." He handed her tea without asking how she took it, because he knew, because thirty years. "You had the look. The same look you had when we were nine and you decided we were going to see the wardens' hall from the inside. I got whipped for that. Worth it. Best view in the valley."

Ash sat between them, eating fish with the focused solemnity of someone refueling rather than dining. Watching him, Lena felt the shard shift against her ribs, warm through the linen, and remembered the flood of it—the king's smile, the wider smile behind it—and the question she'd been circling for days finally landed in her mouth.

"Maro. Why are you really coming?"

He was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice.

"When I was fourteen," he said, "my mother sold a memory. One. To pay a debt. She sold the day my father died, because it hurt too much to keep, and afterward she was lighter, happier, everyone said how well she was doing." He turned the tin of tea in his hands. "Except she'd look at me sometimes like she was trying to place me. Like grief had been load-bearing and she'd knocked out the wall and the rest of the house was settling. She died thinking she'd had a good life. I've never decided whether that was mercy or theft." He looked up. "Someone's selling memories out there, Lena. Whole villages' worth, from the sound of your shard. Someone's knocking out walls everywhere. I want to see the mason's face."

Lena held his gaze across the fire, and past him the dark road unspooled southward, toward Bellharrow, toward the capital, toward a map a mute child had drawn in tea.

Ash set down his fish. He was looking at the fire, but not at it—at something through it—and his lips moved, shaping silent syllables in the dead tongue, counting something. His fingers ticked against his knee, one, two, three, and stopped, and started again, and stopped.

Lena leaned over and stilled his hands with hers.

His eyes came up to hers, and in the firelight she saw it plain at last, the thing behind the urgency: not fear for himself. Fear *on schedule*. As if he knew precisely how much time the world had left, and had known it since the storm, and had been waiting, patiently, for the adults to finish packing.

"How long?" she asked him quietly. "How long do we have?"

Ash held up his fingers. One. Two. Three.

Then, slowly, watching her face the whole time, he folded the third finger down.

Two.

Maro stopped chewing. "Two what?"

Lena stared at the child's raised hand, at the fire eating through its fuel, at the road going south into the dark, and felt the last warm weight of homesickness drain out of her, replaced by something colder and cleaner and far more useful.

"Two of something," she said, standing, shaking out her cloak. "We find out walking."
