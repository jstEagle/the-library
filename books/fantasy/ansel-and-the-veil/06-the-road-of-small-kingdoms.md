## 6. The Road of Small Kingdoms

Maro had stolen a lot of things in his life—coins, letters, a bishop's ring off his finger while the man slept, once, memorably, an entire goat—but he had never stolen a feeling, and watching Lena do it had always seemed to him like watching someone reach into a fire and pull out a coal with their bare hands. She made it look easy. That was the trick of her. Everything about Lena looked easy until you thought about it too long.

Three days out of the Veil, he was thinking about it too long.

They walked the pilgrim road east through country Maro half-remembered from a job years ago, when he'd been young enough to think distance was freedom. The land itself hadn't changed. Terraced barley going gold at the edges, stone walls stitched across hillsides like old scars, crows arguing over something dead in a ditch. What had changed was the people.

The first village they came to was called Hobb's Rest, or so said a sign whose letters had been repainted recently and badly, as if someone had done it out of sheer enthusiasm rather than need. A woman stood at the well in the square giving away bread.

Not selling. Giving.

"Take it," she said, pressing a loaf into Maro's hands before he could refuse. Her smile went all the way up to her eyes, which should have been a good sign and instead made the back of his neck prickle. "Take two. There's plenty."

There wasn't plenty. Maro had grown up hungry in a way that taught you to read a household the way Lena read faces, and he could see the arithmetic behind this woman's generosity: the flour bin by her door sat low, the children playing in the lane were thin at the wrists, and the bread itself was the dark, dense loaf of a family stretching its stores toward harvest. She was giving away food she needed.

"Kind of you," Maro said carefully.

"It's nothing." She beamed. "It's really nothing."

And there it was—the phrase snagging on something, the way a fishhook snags on silk. *It's really nothing.* He'd heard that cadence before. Not from peasants on roads. From clients. From the hollow-eyed rich who came to the Veil's back doors wanting to unburden themselves of grief, of guilt, of love that had curdled into obligation, and who walked out afterward light as air, smiling their wide smiles, saying *I feel wonderful, I feel like myself again,* not knowing that what they'd bought was a hole shaped like whatever they'd cut out.

The woman moved on to Ash, holding out a third loaf, and Maro watched her face change. Just for a heartbeat. The smile stayed where it was but everything behind it flickered, like a lamp guttering—a look of confusion, almost of pain, as if the child's silence were a word she couldn't quite read. Then the smile reasserted itself, smooth as poured cream, and she patted Ash's head and wandered off humming.

Ash stared after her. Then at the bread in his hands. Then up at Maro, and the look in those too-old eyes said, plainly, in the language without words they were all three of them becoming fluent in: *What is wrong with these people?*

"I don't know yet," Maro muttered, "but I'm going to find out."

Lena caught up to them at the edge of the square, a bundle of apples in her arms that she had absolutely not been given. "Don't look at me like that. He offered them to everyone who passed. It would've been rude."

"You stole from a man having a nervous breakdown of generosity."

"He'll never miss them." She said it lightly, then heard herself, and stopped. They stood there a moment in the road with the apples between them, both thinking the same thing neither wanted to say: *that's how it starts.* That's the sentence every thief lives inside. He'll never miss it. She'll never know. The whole trade ran on the certainty that what was taken was weightless to begin with.

"Come on," Lena said finally. "We should make the next village by dark."

* * *

The next village was worse.

It was called Merrow's Ford, and it sat on a brown river with a mill and a shrine and forty-odd houses, and every single person in it was happy. Not content—happy, aggressively, uniformly happy, the way a room is uniformly lit when there are no shadows anywhere because there are no walls. An old man with a hacking cough insisted on carrying their packs to the inn and laughed the whole way, wetly, delightedly. The innkeeper refused payment for three beds—"we don't take coin here anymore, coin makes people grasp"—and gave them stew with meat in it that Maro knew cost more than the inn earned in a week. A girl of maybe ten played a fiddle in the corner, and she was good, genuinely good, and when she finished a tune the whole room sighed with pleasure like one animal, and she started another immediately, still smiling, though Maro noticed her fingers were bleeding slightly at the tips and nobody, including her, seemed to mind.

He watched the room over the rim of his bowl. It was a habit older than his friendship with Lena, older than the Veil: count the exits, mark the hands near weapons, note who watches whom. But tonight the thing to watch was the happiness itself, and once he started looking he couldn't stop.

Because it wasn't real joy. He knew real joy; he'd seen it rarely and always briefly, at weddings and homecomings and once in a rainstorm after a drought, a farmer standing in his field laughing at the sky. Real joy has texture. It comes and goes. It sits alongside tiredness and irritation and boredom and lets them all share the bench. This was different. This was joy with the seams showing, joy worn like a coat that fit nobody, identical on every face. And underneath it—

Underneath it, gaps.

He started cataloguing them the way he'd catalogue a house before a burglary. The miller told a story about his wedding day and got to the part where the bride appears and simply... skipped, mid-sentence, to the feast, smiling harder, as if the middle of the story were a stair with a step missing. The innkeeper mentioned her son twice and both times said *my boy* with a small hesitation, the way you say a word in a language you learned late. The blacksmith had a scar down his cheek and when Maro asked about it the man smiled his wide smile and said, "Funny, I can't recall," and sounded pleased about it. Pleased. Delighted, even. *I can't recall!* As if forgetting were a prize he'd won.

Lena had gone quiet beside him. She'd gone quiet the moment they crossed the threshold, and she ate her stew in small mechanical bites, staring at nothing, and Maro recognized the look because he'd seen it on her a hundred times over fifteen years: the look of a woman hearing an echo of her own work coming back to her from somewhere she didn't put it.

After the fiddle girl's ninth tune, Maro leaned close. "Tell me what you're seeing."

"You first."

"Giving away things they need. Forgetting things they shouldn't. Happy like it's a uniform." He kept his voice under the music. "In the Veil we called this the shine. Clients fresh from a taking. You know it better than anyone."

"I know it," Lena said. Her voice was flat, careful, the voice she used when feeling anything too much might crack something open. "But these aren't clients. These are farmers and millers. Nobody paid for a taking here. Nobody even knows what a taking is." She turned her bowl slowly on the table. "Unless somebody took from them anyway."

"Who'd bother? They're peasants. There's no coin in peasant grief."

"No," Lena agreed. "There's no coin in it at all."

She let that sit. Maro felt the shape of it arrive in his chest before his head caught up, cold and heavy, like a stone dropped down a well.

"Then what are they selling it for?"

"Who says they're selling?" Lena's eyes came up. "Who says they're getting anything? Maybe it's just—harvest. Somebody harvesting cheap. Somebody sweeping the countryside."

* * *

They left Merrow's Ford at dawn, and the whole village turned out to wave goodbye.

That was the part Maro couldn't shake. Forty people standing in the misty street waving at three strangers, smiling their identical smiles, calling blessings after them, pressing more gifts into their hands—a wheel of cheese, a scarf, a carved wooden bird—and the fiddle girl playing them out of town like a funeral procession that had gotten its directions wrong. Ash walked backward for a hundred yards, staring, until the village dropped behind a hill.

For six miles nobody spoke. Then Maro did.

"When I was nine," he said, "my mother sold my sister's laughter."

Lena glanced at him. Ash, walking between them, looked up.

"Not all of it. That was the horror of it, when I understood later—she sold the *middle*. The memory of the worst year, the year our father died and we nearly starved, the year Sela stopped laughing. Some broker came through offering bread and a clean start, and my mother—she wasn't weak, Lena, she was drowning, and it was that or sell us. So she sold the year. Kept the edges. And Sela woke up lighter, happier, and neither of them ever knew why the happy felt like wearing someone else's shoes." He kicked a stone off the road, harder than necessary. "I used to think that was the bottom of what the trade could do. Sell a poor family's worst year so they can afford to keep living. But this—" he gestured back down the road, at the invisible village, the invisible forty smiles, "—this isn't a sale. Nobody here chose. Somebody came through and skimmed them like cream. And they're *grateful*. They think they feel better."

"The widow," Lena said quietly.

"What?"

"The woman I stole from. In the Veil. Her grief—I took it, delivered it, collected my fee. Standard job." Lena's jaw worked. "But I never asked where it went after the client. I never asked what happened to it. Memories have to go somewhere, Maro. We always assumed they just... faded. Spoiled, like milk."

"And now?"

"Now I'm standing in a kingdom where entire villages are smiling with holes in their heads, and the child carrying a piece of the king's crown flinches every time someone smiles at him." She looked at Ash, who was watching the road ahead with his fists clenched around the shard hidden beneath his shirt. "Something is eating. Something big enough to eat a countryside and leave everyone saying thank you."

Maro chewed on that for a mile. The road climbed through barley fields where laborers worked singing—not working songs, but lullabies, sweet and slow, men with scythes crooning to the wheat—and the singing carried farther than it should have, and none of the singers looked at each other while they did it.

"Thirteen moons," he said at last. "That's what the elders kept muttering before we left. Thirteen turns of peace since the king's coronation-feast, since whatever happened in the capital that nobody talks about. Thirteen years of no war. Everyone calls it the Long Blessing." He laughed, without any humor in it at all. "You know what I called it growing up? I called it the years my mother smiled too easily. I thought peace just did that to people. Made them soft in the head. Turns out softness in the head can be *bought*."

"Or harvested," Lena said.

"Or harvested. Cheap. At scale." He spat. "Gods below, Lena. If someone's been skimming the whole kingdom for thirteen years—if every village on every road is a little bit hollowed—then how far does it go? How many people?"

Neither of them answered. Ahead, the road bent around a hillside, and where it bent they could see the next valley, and the next village in it, smoke rising from its chimneys in fat contented columns, and from this distance, in this light, it looked exactly like every picture of home and happiness that had ever been painted on any wall in any tavern in the world.

Ash stopped walking.

He stood very still in the middle of the road, head tilted, listening to something none of them could hear. Then he raised one small hand and pointed—not at the village, but past it, beyond it, toward the horizon where the land rose toward hills and hills rose toward mountains and mountains, somewhere past seeing, held the capital and its thirteen moons of false peace.

His finger trembled.

And then, deliberately, he turned the pointing hand around and pointed back the way they'd come—at Hobb's Rest, at Merrow's Ford, at every smiling village strung along the road behind them like lanterns—and drew a slow line through the air from there to there. *All of it. All of them.*

"Yeah," Maro said softly. "That's what I was afraid of."

It was Lena who said what all three of them were thinking, in the flat careful voice she saved for truths too heavy to carry any other way:

"It's not the crown that's broken. It's the whole kingdom. And nobody knows. Because nobody can remember anything being different."

They walked on. Behind them, faint on the wind, the lullabies followed for another mile, sweet and slow and wrong, sung by men who could not remember why they only knew the verses and never the ends of songs.

And far ahead, at the edge of sight where the road dropped toward the river city of Bellharrow, Maro saw something that made him stop a second time—market banners, dozens of them, snapping in the wind above the rooftops, and even at this distance he could read the device stitched on the nearest: a closed eye, gold on white, and beneath it words he'd heard whispered of but never seen written:

*THE MEMORY MARKET — ALL GRIEF BOUGHT FAIRLY.*

"All grief bought fairly," he repeated, and found his mouth had gone dry. "Lena. That's where your widow's grief went. That's where all of it goes."

Lena stared at the banners a long moment. Then she pulled her hood up against the wind, and Maro saw her do the thing she did when she'd stopped being afraid and started being dangerous: she smiled.

"Then we're going shopping," she said.
