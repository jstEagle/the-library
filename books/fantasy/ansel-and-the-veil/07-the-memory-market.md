## 7. The Memory Market

Bellharrow smelled of rain and copper and, beneath it all, something sweeter—like fruit left too long in the sun. Lena caught it the moment they passed through the western gate, that cloying undertone threading through the smoke of cookfires and the wet wool of the crowd. She had smelled it once before, years ago, on the hands of a client who paid her in river-pearls and never gave his name.

She knew what it was now. It was the smell of memories going stale.

"Keep your hood up," she murmured to Ash, who walked pressed against her side like a burr. "And Maro—if anyone asks, we're brokers from the eastern valleys. We've come to sell."

Maro snorted. "We *have* come to sell. That's the problem." He adjusted his pack straps and flashed his market grin at a passing guard, easy and empty, the kind of smile that made people look away before they looked too close. "I still say we should have gone around."

"The road around adds nine days." Lena kept her eyes forward. Nine days they did not have, though she could not have said why she was so certain of that. Only that Ash had stopped sleeping properly three nights ago, staring at the moon through whatever crack or window they could find, and that the child's silence had grown heavier each night, like water rising in a locked room. "We go through. We keep our heads down. We find out where these memories are coming from, and then we're gone."

"And if someone recognizes your work?"

"Then I'll recognize theirs first."

The street opened before them, and Lena forgot, for one long breath, how to breathe.

* * *

They called it the Lantern Row, though there were no lanterns—or rather, there were nothing *but* lanterns, thousands of them, glass globes strung on wires between the rooftops like a sky brought down low. Each globe held a curl of pale light that moved. Not flickered. *Moved*, slow and searching, the way a moth moves inside a jar, the way grief moves behind a sleeping face.

Memories. Bottled, labeled, lit from within by their own living weight.

The stalls ran the length of the street on both sides, merchants calling out their wares the way fishwives call out the morning catch. *First kisses, gentle-used, no bitterness! A soldier's courage, single owner, barely spent! The taste of your mother's bread—you'll swear you never lost it!* Buyers drifted between them with the soft, satisfied smiles Lena had been seeing since the border villages, and she understood those smiles differently now. They weren't happiness. They were patchwork. People stitched together out of other people's best hours, walking around warm and hollow, full of light that wasn't theirs.

"It's a graveyard," Ash said suddenly.

Lena stopped. Maro stopped. Three days on the road and the child had spoken perhaps ten words, all of them in that old tongue, all of them apparently nonsense—and now this, in perfect common, in a voice like a small cracked bell.

"What did you say?"

Ash was looking up at the lanterns, at the lights pressing against their glass. "It's a graveyard," he repeated, "where the dead walk in circles." Then he closed his mouth again, as if the words had cost him something dear, and would not say more.

Maro's grin had gone stiff. "Kid's got a poet's heart," he said, too loudly. "Come on. Broker's row's this way. If we're looking for supply chains, we start with the biggest stall."

* * *

The biggest stall belonged to a woman named Odile Ferrance, though the brass plate said only FERRANCE FINE MEMORIES, EST. BY APPOINTMENT. Her shop front was less a stall than a salon: velvet drapes against the drizzle, a carpet actually swept clean, and shelves upon shelves of lanterns arranged not by price but by feeling—Lena could see the logic of it immediately, the griefs clustered together for customers who wanted to wallow, the joys segregated so no stray sorrow might dilute them.

Odile herself was silver-haired and soft-handed, with the unlined face of a woman who had purchased her own peace decades ago. She looked up when they entered, took in their travel-worn clothes, and smiled the way a jeweler smiles at gravel.

"Eastern valleys," she said. "You'll be selling, then. Everyone from the east sells. What have you brought me?"

"Grief," Lena said. "A widow's. Fresh-harvested, if you know what fresh means."

Something sharpened behind Odile's pleasant eyes. "Do you indeed. And do *you* know what fresh means, girl? Because half the product that comes through my door has been handled so roughly it arrives bruised. Grief especially. Amateurs tear it getting it out, and then I'm selling torn goods at whole prices, and my reputation bleeds."

"I don't tear anything." Lena set her hand flat on the counter, let her voice drop into the register she used across card tables and kitchen fires, the one that made people lean in. "I take a memory the way you'd take an egg from under a hen. Warm. Whole. Ask anyone in the Veil."

The word landed. Lena watched it land—the smallest hitch in Odile's breathing, the fractional stillness of her hands. *The Veil.* Even here, even in a city that traded in stolen lives like ribbon, the valley's name carried weight. Magic could not enter the Veil; everyone knew the stories. But its thieves were legend among the trade, because a thief who worked without magic worked by craft alone, and craft left no scars.

"A Veil thief," Odile said slowly. "In my shop. Wearing road dust." Her gaze slid past Lena to Ash, hunched in the corner shadow, then to Maro lounging by the door with studied boredom. "And traveling with company. How modern. The Veil doesn't let its thieves wander, as I understand it. Nothing leaves."

"Nothing leaves," Lena agreed. "So maybe I never came from there. Maybe you're imagining things. You deal in memories, madam—I'd think you'd know how unreliable they are."

For a moment the two women regarded each other across the counter, and Lena felt the strange vertigo of her own profession turned back on her. This was a negotiation, and negotiations were just theft done politely. She needed information. Odile had it. The only question was the currency.

Odile broke first—not in defeat but in appetite. "Show me the grief," she said, "and I'll tell you what you want to know. Yes, I can see it in your face, girl, you didn't come here to sell. You came here to dig. Fine. Digging is expensive, and I accept payment in kind." She gestured at the shelves. "One good harvest, freely given, and I'll show you the back room."

* * *

The back room was cold. That was the first thing—a damp, cellar chill utterly wrong for a place that stored light. Lanterns hung in rows here too, dozens of them, but these bore no labels, no price tags. Their lights moved faster than the ones out front, pressing at the glass like fingers, like faces.

"These aren't for sale," Lena said.

"No." Odile's voice had changed, stripped of its merchant warmth. "These are inventory. Special orders. There are clients who don't buy joy, girl. There are clients who buy *specifics*. A particular summer. A particular face. A particular sin, witnessed from the right angle." She drifted down the rows, trailing one finger near the glass without touching. "You'd be amazed what the great houses pay for the private hours of lesser people. Jealousy keeps better than wine."

Lena's mouth had gone dry. "Who orders?"

"You know who orders." Odile stopped before a lantern near the back, one whose light burned a deep, bruised violet, and lifted it from its hook with both hands, tenderly, the way one lifts a sleeping child. "Everyone in this trade knows who sits at the top of the chain. We just don't say it above a whisper, because the last broker who said it loudly was found very peaceful and very empty, smiling at a wall." She held the lantern out. "Here. Since you claim to be such a craftsman. Tell me whose work this is."

Lena took the lantern.

The moment her fingers closed on the glass, she knew.

Not because of the light—though the light was familiar, a violet-gray like dusk over a wheat field. Not because of the smell—that sweet rot of aging memory, the same undertone she'd caught at the gate. She knew because of the *shape* of it. Every thief knows their own work the way a mother knows a cry in a crowded room. There was a seam to it, invisible to any buyer, a place where the memory had been eased loose from the mind that grew it, clean and whole and warm.

She had made that seam. Two years ago. A farmhouse outside the Veil's southern rim. A woman named Brigit Callow, who had lost her husband to a fever and her son to the king's war, and who had come to Lena weeping, saying *I can't carry it anymore, please, I'll pay anything.*

Lena had taken the grief. All of it. And Brigit Callow had smiled at her afterward with that terrible lightness, thanking her, and Lena had ridden home with a widow's sorrow folded into her chest like contraband, and sold it to a courier, and never asked—because she never asked—where the courier took it.

"This is mine," Lena heard herself say. Her voice sounded far away. "I harvested this. Two years ago. From a woman named—"

"Brigit Callow." Odile nodded, unsurprised. "Yes. It's come through my hands twice since. Repackaged, of course. Rebranded. Out front it's listed as *Widow's Devotion, gently aged, ideal for mourning reenactments.* There's a collector in the capital who pays obscene sums for authentic peasant grief. Says the poor feel everything more purely. He likes to wear it during festivals." She said it lightly, professionally, and then paused, tilting her head. "You look ill, girl. Most sellers are proud to see their work displayed."

"Where does it go after the collector?" Lena asked. "After he's finished wearing it. Where does it *end up?*"

"Why do you care? Your part of the transaction ended the day you handed it to your courier."

"*Where does it end up?*"

The lantern light trembled between them. In the corner, Ash had risen to his feet without a sound, and Maro had pushed off the doorframe, and neither of them spoke, and the cold of the room seemed to gather closer, listening.

Odile sighed, and for a moment the silver-haired broker looked older, tired, like a woman carrying inventory no shelf could hold. "Back to the palace," she said quietly. "Everything goes back to the palace. That's the part none of you Veil thieves ever ask about, and it's the only part that matters. Do you know what happens to a memory after it's worn? It comes back changed. Used. Marked by whoever wore it. No buyer wants secondhand goods, so the worn ones get returned—shipped upriver in iron crates, sealed with the king's own wax." Her eyes met Lena's. "Every month. For eleven years. And lately—" she hesitated, and Lena watched genuine fear move across that unlined face, the first honest expression she'd shown all day—"lately the crates come back *empty*. Whatever's up there eating them has gotten hungry."

"Eating them," Lena repeated. "What's up there?"

"I sell memories, girl. I don't ask questions I can't afford the answers to." Odile reached out and took the lantern back, cradling it. "But I'll tell you what I told the last thief who stood where you're standing, three years ago, before she went upriver and vanished. The crown buys grief the way other men buy grain. Not to use. To *feed*. And a man who feeds something that eats memories is either its prisoner or its keeper, and either way, you don't want to be standing in front of him when it finishes the meal."

* * *

Outside, the rain had thickened, drumming on the lantern glass until the whole street seemed to shiver. Lena walked without seeing, Maro at her shoulder, Ash's small hand gripping hers with a strength that bordered on painful.

"Lena." Maro's voice was low, careful—the voice he used on spooked horses and spooked friends. "Talk to me."

"She's still in there." Lena's breath fogged. "Brigit Callow. I took her grief and I told myself I was helping her, that she couldn't carry it, that I was a knife doing surgery. And some fat collector wears it at festivals. And then it gets shipped to the palace and *eaten*." She laughed, and hated the sound of it. "Eleven years. Eleven years of harvests going upriver. Do you understand what that means? Everything I ever took—every job, every coin—it's all in crates. It's all in that palace. Being devoured by whatever wears the king."

"So we turn around," Maro said. "We go east, we go anywhere, we forget Bellharrow and palaces and—"

"We can't." It was Ash who said it. Both words, clear as struck glass, and both of them froze. The child was staring up through the rain at the lanterns swaying overhead, at hundreds of small imprisoned lights, and his eyes were wet—not with rain. "They remember being taken," he whispered. "All of them. Even after they're eaten, the *eating* remembers. And it's almost finished turning, and when it finishes, no one will remember anything at all."

He squeezed Lena's hand, hard, and pointed—not at the lanterns, not at the palace road, but across the street, where a noblewoman was stepping down from a lacquered carriage under an umbrella held by a servant. Rich silks. A bored, beautiful face. And on her right hand, catching the lantern-light, a heavy gold ring set with a jagged shard of metal that gleamed the exact color of the piece hidden against Lena's own skin.

As the woman passed, the shard in her ring *hummed*—a note low and longing, like a string plucked in another room—and every lantern on the street flickered at once, and Ash made a sound Lena had never heard from him before, small and wounded and unmistakably a name.
