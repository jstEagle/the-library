## 8. The Second Shard

The ring sings before Ash ever sees it.

He feels it first as a toothache in his chest — a thin, bright ache that starts behind his sternum and climbs his throat like a swallowed bee. He is sitting on the floor of their rented room above the dyer's shop in Bellharrow, sorting buttons into a spiral because Lena said *stay where I can see you* and Maro said *don't touch anything that hums*, and the buttons are good for this: small, silent, obedient things that ask nothing of him.

Then the ache comes, and the buttons scatter under his hands without him meaning to move them.

*Gold*, he thinks, in the way he thinks everything now — not words, not quite, but shapes and heat and weight. *Old gold. Broken gold.*

He has felt one shard before. The one he carried out of the palace in his fists, wrapped in his mother's shawl, running through corridors that were forgetting him even as he ran down them. That shard had sung too, but quietly, like a person humming to keep from crying. This one does not hum. This one *calls*. It pours itself up through the floorboards, through the mortar of Bellharrow's crooked streets, through the soles of his feet, and it says — not in language, never in language, but in the old pressure that lives underneath all language —

*Here. Here. Here.*

Ash presses both palms flat against the floorboards. Through the wood he tastes the city: dye-water and tallow smoke, ten thousand small memories seeping down between the stones like rain. And beneath all of it, threading through the streets toward the river district, a single bright wire of gold-song, moving. Walking. Someone is wearing it, walking through the market with it on their hand, and they do not know what they carry, and the shard is calling to him the way a drowning thing calls to the shore.

He goes to the window. Below, the street is doing what Bellharrow always does at midday — too many people, too many colors, everyone smiling at everyone else with those wide, wet smiles that Maro says are wrong, that Maro says mean the memories have been thinned out of them like broth stretched with water. Ash does not understand all of what Maro means. But he understands the smiles. In the palace, before, the courtiers smiled like that. The Hollowed smile like that. Smiles with nothing behind them except the shape of smiling.

He finds her in the crowd almost at once, because the song pulls his eyes the way a hook pulls a fish.

A woman in dove-grey silk, older, silver hair pinned high, walking with two servants a half-step behind. She moves like the palace people move — like the ground owes her money. And on her left hand, on the third finger, a ring of heavy gold set with no stone at all, just a jagged seam of raw metal that catches the light wrong, that catches the light the way broken things do.

The shard.

His shard. His father's crown, worn on a stranger's finger like a trinket, like a button, like nothing.

Something rises in Ash that he has no shape for. It is bigger than anger. It fills him from the heels up, hot as fever, and for one long moment he stands at the window with his breath fogging the glass and his hands shaking against the sill, and the whole street below seems to dim slightly, as if a cloud has crossed a sun that isn't there.

*Lena will say wait,* he thinks. *Lena will say we do this carefully, we make a plan, we watch her house, we learn her habits.* Lena is careful the way spiders are careful. She would want to steal the ring while the woman slept, lift it off her finger soft as a dream, leave a coin or a lie in its place. That is how Lena loves things: quietly, from the side, without being seen.

But the shard is singing *here, here, here*, and the woman in dove-grey silk is turning down the river road, away from him, carrying a piece of his father further from his father with every step, and Ash is tired — he is so tired — of waiting in rooms, of being carried, of being small and mute and safe.

He is down the stairs before he decides to move.

* * *

Bellharrow swallows him immediately. This is the thing about cities that frightens him most: not the noise, but the *memories*. In the Veil there was silence, clean and total, magic unable to follow him even there. But Bellharrow leaks. Every street corner drips with somebody's yesterday — a baker's pride in his loaves, a drunkard's shame, a child's terror of the bridge grates. It washes over Ash in waves, uninvited, and he has learned to walk through it the way you walk through rain: head down, eyes shut if you must, letting it strike you without drinking.

Today he cannot keep his mouth closed against it. Today the gold-song is louder than everything, and he follows it through the crowd like a thread through cloth.

The market by the river is all awnings and shouting, fish-ice and spice dust, and the woman in grey is stopped at a stall of silks, holding a bolt of blue up against her shoulder while a merchant bows and flatters. Her servants stand behind her. Her bare neck gleams above her collar. The ring flashes when she gestures — *this blue, no, the other blue* — and each flash sends the ache spiking through Ash's teeth, his bones, the soft places behind his eyes.

*Just take it off her hand,* he thinks. *It's only a hand. Hands are slow.*

He gets within six feet of her. Five. Close enough to smell the orange oil she wears in her hair, close enough to see that the ring's jagged seam is not decoration — it is a fracture line, a wound in the metal, and inside the wound something glimmers that is not light. He reaches out with the reaching-feeling, the one he used once in the Veil to find Lena's thoughts when she was dreaming, gentle as touching the surface of water.

The shard *slams* back against him.

Not gently. Not like water. Like a door thrown open in a storm — and through it pours everything the shard remembers, everything it has been drinking in from the woman's finger these thirty years: a wedding, a funeral, a daughter's first steps, a son who died at sea, forty years of a life soaked into gold like wine into bread. And beneath the woman's memories, deeper, older, pressed down like flowers in a book:

*A hall of white pillars. A man laughing. A man kneeling. A crown coming down onto a brow, and thirteen voices singing, and then —*

*— then the breaking. Metal screaming. Gold flying apart like startled birds. A hand closing around one burning piece. Running. His own small legs running, though he was not small then, he was —*

Ash staggers. Bumps a fruit cart. An apple rolls off and splits on the cobbles and the vendor shouts at him and he doesn't hear it, doesn't hear any of it, because the memory is still pouring through him and it is *his*, it is the day the world ended, seen from the inside, and he had forgotten — he had let himself forget — how loud the breaking was. How the king had screamed. How the king had screamed *his name* as the gold came apart, and the name had been torn out of the air before it finished leaving the man's mouth, unwritten, unsaid, unremembered by every soul in that hall except the boy who was already becoming no one.

The woman in grey turns. Looks directly at him.

For a moment her face is only a face — lined, imperious, faintly annoyed at the boy jostling her market morning. Then her eyes flick to his, and something in her shifts, some animal caution, the look of a woman who has lived through courts and knows what it means when a stranger stares at your jewelry like a starving man stares at bread.

"Boy," she says. "Are you lost?"

Her voice comes to him warped, underwater. The shard is singing so loudly now that the whole market has gone distant, the awnings and the shouting and the fish-ice all pressed behind glass, and there is only the ring, only the gold, only the piece of his father's crown sitting on a stranger's hand while his father forgets his own son on a throne in the capital.

*Come home,* the shard says. *Come home come home come home.*

And Ash opens his mouth to say — to try to say, in the wordless way he says everything, *please, lady, please give it back* —

and what comes out instead is a Word.

* * *

He does not choose it. That is the truth he will carry afterward, the truth that will keep him awake in whatever beds they sleep in next: he did not choose it. The old tongue is not a language you speak. It is a language that speaks *through* you, when the thing you need is larger than your mouth, and what he needed was so large that it broke him open on its way out.

"*Soverhan.*"

One word. *Return.*

The sound is not loud. That is the horror of it — it is not loud at all. It is low and clear and perfectly pitched, a single bell-note in a room full of noise, and it passes through the market the way cold passes through a wall, and everywhere it touches, glass *remembers that it was once sand*.

The silk-stall's mirror cracks into a spiderweb. The apothecary's front window bursts outward in a glittering fan. Down the lane, three shopfronts, a lantern-globe, a pair of spectacles on a fat man's nose — gone, all gone, a thousand small explosions of crystal raining into the street, and the crowd does not scream at first because the sound is so beautiful, because the note hangs in the air like a held breath, and every person in the market feels, for one instant, the exact same thing:

They feel *remembered*.

Every one of them. The baker and the drunkard and the child afraid of bridge grates — for one heartbeat each of them feels seen all the way down, known completely, every stolen and sold and forgotten piece of themselves suddenly called by name. Some of them weep. Some of them laugh. One old man drops to his knees in the fish-ice with his arms spread wide like a man greeting rain after drought.

Then the feeling passes, the way a wave passes, and leaves them hollow where it touched, and the screaming starts.

The woman in dove-grey silk is staring at her hand. The ring has split along its fracture line like a pod opening, and the shard inside is floating — actually floating — a finger's width above her palm, turning slowly, shedding forty years of wedding-funeral-daughter memories like water off a duck's back. Her face is white as the silk. Her servants have run. Everyone is running.

Everyone except Ash, who stands in the center of the ruin with blood on his lip — he has bitten through it without noticing — and his hands outstretched, and the shard drifting toward him across the broken air like a spark drifting toward a flame.

*No,* he thinks, dazed, watching his own fingers tremble. *No, I didn't mean — I only wanted —*

The shard touches his palm.

The world goes white and vast and old. For a moment he is everywhere the crown has ever been: the throne room, the war tents, the thirteen towers where the binding was sung, the flooded dark beneath the palace where the Quiet first learned to smile with a borrowed mouth. He sees the king whole — young, laughing, terrible and kind — and he sees the king now, and the distance between those two men is the distance Ash has been running his entire life.

Then it settles into his skin, cool as well-water, and the market comes back: the shattered glass, the weeping strangers, the ringing silence where the bell-note used to be.

The noblewoman has not run. She is backed against her own abandoned silks, one hand pressed to her mouth, staring at him with an expression he knows. He saw it once, years ago, on the face of a warden in the Veil who found a wolf den and realized the cub inside was not a wolf.

"What," she whispers, "what *are* you?"

Ash wants to answer. He opens his mouth and nothing comes — the Word has spent whatever was in him; his tongue lies in his mouth like a dead fish. So he does the only thing he can do. He closes his fist around the shard, warm now, quiet now, singing a small satisfied song against his palm like a cat settling, and he runs.

* * *

Lena finds him at the edge of the river district, sitting on an overturned boat hull, holding his bleeding lip and shaking.

She skids to a stop in front of him, breathing hard, eyes wild, and grabs his shoulders and turns him around checking him for wounds, and he lets her, because her hands are quick and frightened and real. Behind her, Maro arrives at a dead sprint with a stolen fishmonger's apron still tied around his waist and an expression that promises a very long lecture.

"Ash." Lena crouches to his eye level. Her voice is the controlled kind of calm that costs her something. "The whole quarter heard it. Windows blew out on Cordwainer Street. They're saying a god walked through the fish market." Her grip tightens. "Was that you? Look at me. Was that *you*?"

He looks at her. He opens his hand.

The shard sits in his palm, gold and fractured and glowing faintly, like a coal remembering fire. Lena goes very still. He watches her face do the thing it does when she is deciding whether to be afraid — the small flicker, the swallow, the jaw setting. He has learned to read her the way she reads locks.

"That's two," she says softly. Almost to herself. "Two shards." Then, to him, sharper: "You spoke. Maro said — people are saying a word, one word, in a language nobody—" She stops. Starts again. "Ash. What did you say?"

He shakes his head slowly. Because he doesn't know. Because the word wasn't his. Because somewhere in the white place the Word took him, he heard other words stacked behind it, thousands of them, a whole drowned language waiting in his chest like water behind a dam, and he is afraid — truly afraid, for the first time since the storm night — of what happens to the world if the dam breaks.

Maro crouches beside Lena, looking at the shard, then at Ash, and his usual grin is nowhere to be found.

"Lena," he says quietly. "There were Hollowed at the market. Two of them. Standing by the fountain. They didn't move when the glass went — didn't blink, didn't run, just stood there facing the boy like compass needles." He swallows. "And then they turned around and walked west. Toward the palace. Fast."

Lena looks at Ash for a long moment. The river slaps against the pilings. Somewhere behind them, in the wounded quarter, glass is being swept up and people are telling each other stories about gods.

"Can you do it again?" she asks him.

Ash closes his fist over the shard. And in the old pressure that lives underneath all language, honest as he knows how to be, he answers her the only way he can:

*I don't know. And I'm afraid to find out.*
