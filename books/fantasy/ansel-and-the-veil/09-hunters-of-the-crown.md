## 9. Hunters of the Crown

Maro had stolen a lot of things in his life—coins, letters, a bishop's ring off his finger while the man slept, once, memorably, an entire barge of salted eels—but he had never before tried to steal three people across a kingdom. It was, he decided somewhere on the fourth day out of the Veil, much harder than eels.

Eels didn't ask questions.

"Are we being followed?" Ash asked, for the ninth time that morning. The boy had learned Common with terrifying speed, the way dry ground learns rain, and now he used it constantly, hungrily, as if every word were a coin he'd found in the street.

"No," Maro said.

"Are you sure?"

"I'm never sure. That's what makes me good at this." He shifted the pack on his shoulders and squinted back down the road they'd walked since dawn: a brown ribbon of mud stitched between hedgerows, empty in both directions except for a cart of turnips and its driver, who was humming to himself with the serene, glassy contentment Maro had come to dread. Everyone hummed these days. Everyone smiled. Three villages back, a woman had given them her only loaf of bread and then stood in the road waving until they were out of sight, beaming like the sun itself owed her money.

Lena said it was grief-harvesting. Maro said it was worse than that. You could harvest grief from one widow; you couldn't make a whole valley smile.

He kept that opinion to himself. Lena carried enough weight.

* * *

They made camp that night in the shell of a waystation shrine, some old god's name worn off the lintel, the little alcove where offerings used to sit now home to a family of mice. Maro got a fire going small and smokeless, the way you learn when your whole trade is not being seen, and Lena sat with the crown shard wrapped in flannel against her chest, turning it over in her hands without looking at it, the way you touch a bruise to check it still hurts.

Ash was asleep almost instantly, curled against her side like a burr. Children could do that—sleep anywhere, trust anyone. Maro envied it the way he envied people who could whistle.

"Veyra," Lena said quietly.

Maro looked up. "What about her?"

"The archivist's letter. The one we bought in Bellharrow before—" She stopped. Before Maro had gone missing for two days and come back with frostbite on his conscience and a story he hadn't finished telling. "She signed the harvest orders. All of them. Thirteen years of them."

"Regent-Counsels sign things. That's the job."

"These aren't tax ledgers, Maro. These are people." Lena turned the shard over again. In the firelight it threw a color that gold shouldn't throw—a deep, patient red, like something breathing under cloth. "A courtier named Dellemont. A seamstress from the palace town. Two soldiers. Their memories, pulled out whole, sold down the river to pay for... whatever she's paying for. And the people left behind—" She shook her head. "You saw the woman in Harrowfen. The one who smiled at us."

Maro had seen her. He'd been trying not to see her ever since. She'd had a face like a house with all the lamps lit and nobody home, warm and bright and utterly vacant, and when she'd pressed a silver piece into Ash's hand she'd said, *be happy, little one, it's so easy to be happy,* in a voice like a music box winding down.

"Hollowed," Maro said. "That's what the archivist called them."

"That's what I'm afraid of. Not Veyra with an army. Armies you can see coming." Lena finally looked at him, and in the firelight her eyes had the flat shine they got when she was frightened and refusing to call it that. "I'm afraid of her sending people who don't know they're people anymore."

The fire popped. Ash stirred but didn't wake.

"Well," Maro said, because someone had to say something, "on the bright side, people who don't know they're people probably aren't very good at tracking."

He would think about that sentence later, with considerable bitterness.

* * *

It happened on the sixth day, in a market town called Wetherell that smelled of wet wool and frying onions, and it happened so gently that Maro didn't understand it until it was nearly done.

They'd stopped to buy rope. Ordinary errand, ordinary day, Ash riding Maro's shoulders and pointing at everything—the chickens, the bell tower, a dog with one ear—and Lena haggling with a rope-seller who was asking criminal prices and smiling about it, because everyone smiled about everything now.

And then a man stepped into their path and said, "Good morning," and Maro's spine went cold before his brain caught up, because there was nothing wrong with the man at all, and that was the problem.

He was perhaps fifty, well-dressed in the muted style of a palace clerk, with kind eyes and a pleasant open face. He stood the way people stand when they are completely unafraid—not brave, just empty of fear the way a cup is empty of water. Behind him, two more figures drifted closer through the crowd, unhurried, a woman in a gray traveling cloak and a young man with flour still dusted on his sleeves as though he'd been pulled from a bakery mid-loaf.

"You'll forgive the intrusion," the first man said, smiling warmly. "We're looking for travelers. A woman, dark-haired, carries a relic of the old reign. And a child."

"Lots of those on the road," Maro said easily. His hand found Lena's elbow behind him, a squeeze that meant *walk, don't run.*

"Oh, certainly. Lots." The man's smile didn't move at all. It was a painted smile, a door painted on a wall. "But this child speaks the old tongue. And the relic sings, if you hold it near. May I?" He extended his hand toward Ash with perfect courtesy, and Ash, who trusted everyone, began to lean down toward him—

Lena caught the boy's ankle and swung him off Maro's shoulders in one motion, and Maro saw her face change, saw her go thief-cold, and she said, "He's shy. Who's asking?"

"We serve the Crown," the man said. "In its long illness. We are the Counsel's instruments." He said it the way other men say *we serve the gods,* with total peace. "You have nothing to fear from us. We only want what is owed."

"And what's owed?"

"Everything," the man said pleasantly. "Everything is owed. Everything was taken, and everything returns to the Crown." He took a step forward, and the crowd flowed around the three of them without noticing, without caring, and Maro realized with a lurch that no one in the market was looking at them at all—that somehow, impossibly, these three occupied a pocket of attention the town simply declined to enter. "You may give it freely. Most do. It doesn't hurt. I remember that it doesn't hurt."

The words landed strangely. *I remember.* The man paused after saying them, as if surprised by their taste.

"You remember nothing," Lena said softly. "Do you. You've been told you remember."

Something flickered across the man's face—there and gone, a fish under ice. For half a heartbeat the smile wavered and underneath it was something lost and enormous, a man standing in a burned house trying to recall which room had been his. Then the paint smoothed over again.

"We should go," Maro murmured.

"We should," Lena agreed.

They went. Nobody ran—running was a confession—but they walked out of Wetherell at a pace just short of rude, and Maro did not let himself look back until the town was a smudge behind them, and even then he wished he hadn't, because the three of them were standing at the edge of the market watching them go. Not following. Just watching. Smiling.

"They'll report our direction," Lena said.

"Yes."

"How many do you think there are?"

Maro thought about the bakery boy with flour on his sleeves, pulled out of his life mid-morning, mid-loaf, and filed away in obedience like a ledger entry. He thought about the music-box woman in Harrowfen. He thought about how many smiling people they'd passed in six days, and how many of those smiles had been real.

"More than three," he said.

* * *

They stopped sleeping in inns. They stopped sleeping in towns at all. Maro taught Ash to read a road backward—to know who had passed by the way the mud held bootprints—and Lena taught him to sleep sitting up against a tree, which he took to with the same unnerving aptitude he took to everything, as if forgetting were a skill he'd been born with and remembering were the hard part.

At night Maro took the last watch, because he didn't trust himself asleep anymore. He kept seeing the burned-house flicker on the clerk's face. Somewhere inside that pleasant hollow was a man who had liked things, once—who had hated things, feared things, loved someone badly or well. Somebody had reached into him and taken the whole interior of his life out, and left the manners.

And the worst part—the part Maro turned over night after night like a stone with something alive under it—was that the Hollowed weren't cruel. That was the horror of it. Cruelty you could fight. But they came at you with perfect kindness, offering to take your burden, promising it wouldn't hurt, and they meant it, because there was nothing left in them that knew what hurt was for.

On the ninth night, camped in a quarry long abandoned, Ash woke screaming.

Not crying—screaming, a raw animal sound, and when Lena gathered him up he was staring past the fire into the dark with his whole body rigid, and the shard against her chest had begun to glow through the flannel, a slow red pulse like a heartbeat.

"What is it? What do you see?"

Ash's mouth worked. He'd been speaking Common for days now, but terror stripped him back down to image, and what came out was broken and terrible:

"Many. Walking. No faces—faces like—" He pressed both palms flat against his own cheeks, pressing hard, as if to keep his own face from sliding off. "Empty houses. Windows lit. Nobody home. They walk in a line. They walk *toward.*"

"Toward us?"

"Toward the shard." Ash's eyes found hers, huge in the firelight. "All roads bend toward it. They don't choose. They *bend.*"

Maro stood up slowly and looked out over the rim of the quarry. The moon was three nights from full, hanging fat and white above the broken stone, and the land beyond lay quiet—no torches, no movement, nothing at all.

Which was exactly what he'd expect, if the thing hunting them didn't need torches to see.

"We move tonight," he said. "Off the roads. Lena—how far to the next shard?"

"Too far." She was already packing, movements quick and precise, but her voice cracked on the second word. "Maro, they bent toward us in Wetherell before we ever touched anything. They're not tracking the shard. They're tracking *us.*"

"Then we stop being us for a while." He grinned at her, the old grin, the one from the Veil, from a hundred jobs where the plan was thin and the nerve was thinner. "Split up? No—don't make that face, I'm joking. Mostly. Look: they follow the child and the gold. So the child and the gold travel quiet, and one of us travels loud."

"Loud how?"

"Loud like me." He spread his arms. "Taverns. Card games. A man matching your description buying rounds in every town between here and the capital, telling a very funny story about a mute boy and a golden trinket. Let them chase a rumor while you two cut cross-country."

"No," Lena said immediately.

"It's a decoy, not a sacrifice. There's a difference. I've been both."

"Maro—"

"Lena." He said it gently, and that stopped her, because he almost never said anything gently. "Six days ago I sold you out once already. My sister's memories, Bellharrow, all of it—you know the story, I won't retell it. I owe a debt I can't pay in coin, and I am *very* good at running loud. Let me be useful the one way I actually am."

For a long moment she just looked at him, and the firelight moved on her face, and Maro understood that she was doing arithmetic—the cold kind, the kind the Veil teaches you—weighing his life against the mission and finding the numbers ugly either way.

"One week," she said finally. "Bellharrow bridge, the ferryman's landing. If you're not there—"

"I'll be there. I'm always there. It's my most annoying quality."

Ash crossed the fire and took Maro's hand, small and fierce, and said something in the old tongue—soft, formal, unmistakably a blessing or a promise—and then, in careful Common: "Come back. Houses should not be empty."

Maro laughed, and it came out rougher than he intended. "Kid, you're going to be insufferable when you grow up."

* * *

He ran loud for four days, and it worked beautifully, right up until it didn't.

He drank in taverns from Corleigh to Millbrook, told the story bigger each night—by the third telling the boy spoke seven languages and the trinket sang hymns—and watched, each time, for the tell. It came on the fourth night, in a coaching inn called the Gilded Hart, in the form of a gray-cloaked woman who laughed at all his jokes and asked no questions and whose eyes, when the candle guttered, reflected nothing at all.

He was out the window before his tankard hit the floor, and he led them a merry chase—rooftops, a drainage culvert, a frozen millrace he crossed on sheer spite—and he might have made it, he genuinely might have, except that when he doubled back through the inn's stable yard there were six of them waiting, standing in a loose crescent in the snow, unhurried, smiling, patient as furniture.

The clerk from Wetherell stood among them.

"There you are," the clerk said warmly. "We've been bending toward you all week."

Maro put his back against the stable wall and did fast, ugly math. Six of them, no weapons drawn—they didn't seem to need any—and the yard's single gate blocked by two broad bodies. Above, the sky was going the color of dirty wool, and somewhere far north, Lena and Ash were cutting cross-country toward a bridge, toward a shard, toward a king who was being eaten alive inside his own delight.

*Some debts are worth paying twice,* he thought, absurdly, and didn't know yet why the words came, only that they settled in his chest like a coin dropped into a well.

"Well," Maro said aloud, raising his empty hands and grinning his best grin at six empty faces, "this is going to be a fascinating conversation."

Behind the clerk, at the crescent's edge, the gray-cloaked woman tilted her head—and for one flickering instant, one human, wounded instant, her smile broke apart entirely, and she whispered, in a voice like someone calling from the bottom of a stairwell:

"*Run.*"
