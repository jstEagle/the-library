## 10. The Friend Who Sold Them

Maro was gone by morning.

Not gone the way travelers were gone—boots missing from the doorway, coat lifted off its hook, a note scrawled in his terrible handwriting. Gone the way a tooth is gone: leaving behind a socket-shaped absence that the tongue keeps returning to, probing for what should be there.

Lena knew it first as sound. The inn room above the tannery in Bellharrow's lower ward had its own music—the drip of the eaves, Ash breathing in the slow tide-rhythm of a child deep asleep, the groan of old wood settling toward dawn. She had learned to sleep inside that music the way she'd once learned to sleep inside the Veil's silence. When she woke and the music had changed—when there was one fewer breath in the room, when the space where Maro's snoring had been was just space—she lay still for a long moment with her eyes open in the dark, listening to the shape of his absence.

His pack was still under the window. His knife was on the table.

Maro did not go anywhere without his knife. He'd once told her, drunk on cheap plum wine in the Veil's only tavern, that he'd been born holding it, that the midwife had to pry it out of his fist, that he'd cried until they gave it back. It was a lie, obviously. Maro told beautiful lies the way other men told jokes—as gifts, freely given, asking nothing back but laughter. But he never lied about the knife.

Lena sat up. Ash stirred beside her, the crown shard tucked against his chest beneath his shirt, glowing faintly through the fabric like a coal remembering fire. In the three days since Bellharrow, since the memory market and the broker with the palace seal stitched inside his collar, the shard had grown warmer. More awake. It hummed now when Ash slept, a frequency Lena felt in her teeth rather than heard, and twice she had dreamed of a throne room full of smiling people whose smiles went up too high at the corners.

She dressed without lighting a lamp. Old habit. Thieves moved best in the dark they trusted.

The street outside was grey and wet, smelling of tannin and river mud. Bellharrow at dawn was a city pretending to be honest—bakers hauling their shutters open, a fishwife arguing cheerfully with a cat, laundry strung between buildings like bunting for a festival no one had announced. Somewhere north of here, past the flooded archive where the archivist Wren had hidden them, past the Road of Small Kingdoms with its villages of people who smiled too widely and gave away things they needed, the capital waited under thirteen moons of false peace. And somewhere in this city, in a vault beneath the memory market, sat a small tin box labeled *SERRA* containing everything Maro's sister had ever been.

Lena stood in the doorway and let herself think the thought she'd been refusing for two days.

He went for her. Of course he went for her.

They had argued about it in Wren's archive, knee-deep in cold water among shelves of drowned books, while Ash traced the map on the wall with one finger and the archivist pretended not to listen. Maro had wanted to go to Bellharrow first—to buy Serra back before the brokers realized what they had, before the price went up, before the Hollowed came. Lena had said no. Lena had said the words *we don't have time* and watched them land on Maro's face like slaps, because she knew what they meant. They meant *your sister is not the mission.* They meant *some things have to be spent.*

She had said those things to a man who had crossed the boundary of the Veil for her, who had walked into villages full of harvested strangers and held her hand in the night so she wouldn't dream about the widow's grief she'd stolen, who had never once—not once in nineteen years—asked her to be anything other than exactly what she was.

And now he was gone, and his knife was on the table, and Lena understood with terrible clarity that a man leaves his knife behind when he doesn't trust himself to use it.

"Ash," she said softly, back inside. "Get up. We're leaving."

Ash sat up already awake—he was always already awake, in that unnerving way of children who had learned that sleep was when bad things came—and looked at the empty bedroll, then at Lena's face, and something passed over his expression that was too old for him. He touched the shard through his shirt. Then he pointed at the table, at the knife, and made a gesture Lena had come to know: fingers walking, two fingers, a journey. *Where did he go?*

"I don't know," Lena said. "But I know where we're going."

She took the knife. It felt wrong in her hand, too heavy, too much like holding a piece of him. She wrapped it in cloth and put it in her pack anyway. If Maro had sold them—if that was what this was—she wanted to look him in the eye when she gave it back.

* * *

They didn't make it out of the lower ward.

Lena had planned it carefully, the way she planned everything: the tanner's back stairs, the drainage culvert that ran under Cooper's Row, the fish market at opening hour when the crowd was thickest and a woman and a child could become smoke. She had done a hundred extractions in cities less friendly than this. She knew how to be nobody.

But the Hollowed did not hunt the way men hunted.

She saw the first one at the mouth of the culvert—a courtier's body in a plain grey traveling coat, standing perfectly still in the drizzle, facing the culvert entrance as though he had been told *stand here* and had interpreted the instruction as eternal. His face was the problem. It wasn't blank; blankness she could work with, blankness was just another mask. It was *smooth*. Every feature present, every feature arranged, and nothing behind any of it. No hunger, no fear, no boredom, no weather. A face like a room after the furniture has been taken out.

Lena had seen the Hollowed described in Wren's drowned books. She had thought she understood. Understanding arrived now, all at once, in the gut: these were people. Someone had stood where this man stood and taken everything out of him, carefully, the way she took grief from widows—but she left the walls standing. Whatever had emptied him had taken the walls too.

There were others. She counted four as she pulled Ash back from the culvert mouth and pressed them both into the shadow of a drying shed: one at the corner by the chandlery, one walking the length of Cooper's Row with the unhurried precision of a clock hand, two more stationed at the far end of the fish market, flanking the only other exit. Not searching. *Positioned.* They had known where to stand before the sun came up.

"They're not looking for us," Lena breathed. "They're waiting for us."

Ash's hand found hers. His grip was small and fierce. Through his shirt the shard had begun to pulse, quick and hot, and Lena realized it was reacting to the Hollowed the way a compass reacts to iron—or the way a wound reacts to salt. These empty ones belonged to the thing that wore the king. The shard knew its own.

Which meant there was nowhere to run that they hadn't already measured.

"Roof," she whispered, and Ash nodded, and they were moving—up the tanner's stair, across the drying frames, boots finding the old familiar grammar of rooftops, tile and timber and gap. For six heartbeats Lena allowed herself the oldest comfort of her trade: height, motion, the city unspooling below like a solved problem.

Then she reached the ridge of the chandlery roof and saw the fifth one standing on the bell tower opposite, and the sixth on the granary, and understood that the roofs were measured too, and that every route out of the lower ward ended in a grey coat and a smooth face, and that whoever had drawn this net had known exactly how a thief would think.

That was when the voice came from below, warm and wry and horribly familiar.

"You can come down, Len. Or you can stay up there getting rained on. They'll wait either way. Waiting's the one thing they're good at."

Lena looked down into the yard of the drying shed.

Maro stood in the rain with his hands visible at his sides and his face turned up to her, and around him, at respectful distances, stood eight of the Hollowed like grey fence posts. He looked tired. He looked like he hadn't slept, which meant he hadn't, which meant he'd walked straight from their room to wherever the bargain was struck. He looked, most of all, like himself—which was the cruelest part, because she had braced for a stranger, for a smooth face, for the furniture taken out. Instead there was all of him, every crooked piece, and he had done this with all of him awake.

"Maro." Her voice came out flat. Good. Flat was armor. "You brought them to a child."

"I brought them to the person they were going to find anyway," he said. "Len, please. Come down. I need you to hear the whole thing before you decide what I am."

"There's not a version of this where you're anything good."

"There is," he said quietly. "It's the version where you remember what's in the box under the market."

The rain ticked on the tiles. Below, the Hollowed did not shift, did not breathe visibly, did not do anything so human as wait impatiently. Ash pressed close against her side, and Lena felt the shard's heat through both their clothes, and thought: *he could speak. He spoke once and shattered windows. If they try to take him—*

"Touch him and I'll show you what else the Veil taught me," she called down.

"No one's touching anyone." Maro spread his hands wider. "Those are my terms. I wrote them myself. Nobody lays a finger on you or the boy until you've heard me out, and if you say no—" he swallowed, and she saw it cost him— "if you say no, they take me instead. That was the deal. My location for his safety. My cooperation for your walk-away."

"Walk away where?"

"That's the part you have to hear."

Lena stood on the ridge a moment longer, doing the arithmetic thieves do: odds, exits, the weight of a sleeping child versus the speed of her own feet. There was no exit. There was only the choice of dignity, and dignity was a currency she'd always found overrated but never worthless.

She climbed down.

* * *

They put her in the tannery's counting room, which the Hollowed had cleared of tanners sometime in the night with the same bloodless efficiency they did everything. Rain streaked the one window. Maro sat across the table from her, and Ash sat pressed against Lena's side, and between them on the table lay the wrapped knife, which Lena set down like a verdict.

Maro looked at it and laughed—one short, broken sound, nothing like his real laugh, which was loud enough to embarrass taverns.

"You kept it."

"You left it."

"I left it so I couldn't change my mind halfway there." He rubbed his face with both hands. When he lowered them, he looked older than nineteen years of friendship should allow. "Ask me. Go on. You've been composing the question since the roof. Ask it so I can answer."

"What did they give you?"

"Serra." The word came out of him like a splinter coming out, fast and bloody and relieving. "All of her. The whole box. Her wedding day, her son's name, the year of the fever, the way our mother sang—all of it, every scrap the brokers catalogued, returned to her tonight. She's with her boy right now. She's remembering him right now." His voice cracked clean through. "Do you understand? She's been a stranger to her own child for six years, and as of midnight she isn't anymore. Because of you. Because you said *we don't have time*, and I couldn't—I could not—"

"So you sold us."

"I sold your *location*." He leaned forward, urgent, palms flat on the table. "Listen to me. Listen. Veyra's people have known roughly where you are since Bellharrow. The broker you shook down reported within the hour—you didn't think a man with a palace seal in his collar would just swallow losing his inventory? The net was closing regardless. All I chose was the *shape* of it. I chose terms. I chose that the Hollowed would not touch Ash. I chose that you'd get a door."

"What door?"

"The Regent-Counsel wants to meet you." Maro's eyes flicked to Ash, then back, and Lena read the fear in them and hated how much it frightened her. "Not the Hollowed. Not soldiers. Veyra herself. Tomorrow, at the Moonfeast, in the capital. She sent an invitation. An actual invitation, written in her own hand, with terms." He drew a folded paper from his coat and laid it on the table between them, and did not push it closer. "I haven't opened it. It's addressed to you."

Lena stared at the paper. Heavy stock, cream-colored, sealed with grey wax stamped with a sigil she recognized from Wren's books: a circle of thirteen moons around an eye.

"She knows about the shards," Maro said. "She knows about the boy. She knows more than we do, Len—that's the thing that kept me up all night, walking here in the rain. She's not hunting you like a criminal. She's *inviting* you like a guest. And I keep asking myself what kind of enemy does that, and the only answer I find is the kind who thinks she's not your enemy. The kind who thinks she's offering you something."

"Or the kind who lies beautifully," Lena said. "Like someone else I know."

Maro took that hit without flinching. He deserved it and he knew it, and something in his posture accepted it the way ground accepts a grave.

"I'm not asking you to forgive me," he said. "I'm not sure I would. I'm asking you to believe that I did the sums the way you taught me. You always said every job has a price and the trick is choosing which price. Their price was a child. Mine was you hating me." He managed half a smile, ruined at one corner. "Cheap, honestly. I've paid worse for less."

Ash shifted against Lena's side. Then, slowly, he leaned forward and looked at Maro—really looked, the way he looked at things, with that bottomless attention—and reached out and touched the folded paper.

The shard flared. Just once, a heartbeat of gold light through his shirt, and the paper's wax seal softened and split along its seam, opening itself like a flower deciding to bloom. Neither of them had touched the fold.

Inside, in a hand so elegant it seemed printed:

*Lena Zhou. Thief of Bellharrow, daughter of the valley that forgets.*

*You have carried what I have sought. I do not want the child harmed. I do not want you harmed. I want only to show you what sits upon the throne of this kingdom, and let you judge for yourself whether I am its servant or its jailer.*

*Come tomorrow, at the turn of the twelfth moon. Bring the boy. Bring your gift. Leave your knives.*

*One night remains in which the king still remembers how to smile. After that, neither of us will recognize him—and neither, I think, will you recognize what you have been carrying all this way.*

*—V. Regent-Counsel*

The rain filled the silence. Lena read it twice, three times, hunting the trap in it the way her fingers hunted a lock's tumblers, and found none, and distrusted it more for that.

"'What you have been carrying,'" she said slowly. "She knows what the shards are."

"She knows what *you* are," Maro said quietly. "That's what scares me. Read it again. *Bring your gift.* Len—she didn't write that like a threat. She wrote it like a request. Like a woman who needs a thief."

Outside the window, the Hollowed stood in the rain without getting wet somehow, or getting wet without minding, twelve grey figures in the courtyard and the lane, patient as furniture. Above the eastern hills, faint through the cloud, the twelfth moon was setting pale as bone, and Lena thought of the drowned books, of the line Wren had made her read three times: *on the thirteenth turn, the binding completes, and the host is consumed.*

One night.

Ash tugged her sleeve. When she looked down, he was pointing—at the letter, at Maro, at his own chest where the shard burned—and then he made the gesture again, the two-fingered walk, but this time pointing north, toward the capital, and his eyes were not afraid. They were certain. Certain the way the shard was warm. Certain the way a compass is certain.

"He wants to go," Maro said, reading it as easily as she did. "Of course he does. It's his father."

"We don't know that."

"We know enough." Maro stood, slow, like a man rising from a sickbed. "Whatever you decide, I'm walking in front of you tomorrow. That's not a request and it's not forgiveness-seeking. It's just where I stand now. Between you and whatever comes out of that palace." He picked up his knife from the table, weighed it, and slid it home in his belt. "Some debts you pay twice, Len. This one I'm paying in advance."

He walked to the door, and paused with his hand on it, and did not turn around.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the box was already open when I got there. Serra's memories. Someone had bought them yesterday and returned them anonymously. Paid triple and left no name." His voice went strange. "The brokers said it was a woman. Grey coat. Said she smiled the whole time, and that the smile was the worst part, like it had been borrowed from somebody else."

The door closed behind him.

Lena sat very still in the rain-lit room with the letter in her hands and the child warm against her side, and somewhere above the clouds the twelfth moon touched the hills, and the last night began.
