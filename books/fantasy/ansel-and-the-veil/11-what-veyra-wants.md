## 11. What Veyra Wants

The Regent-Counsel's chambers had no mirrors. Veyra had them removed the day she took office, all eleven of them, and the servants whispered about it for a season before they forgot—forgetting being the palace's native language now, its mother tongue, spoken fluently by everyone who smiled.

She kept one mirror anyway. It lived in a locked drawer beneath her correspondence, wrapped in black cloth, and once a month she unwrapped it and looked at herself and made certain she was still looking back.

Tonight was not that night. Tonight she stood at the window of the high tower and watched the twelfth moon climb over the capital like a coin someone had polished too many times, wearing thin at the center. Thirteen turns, the old arithmetic said. Twelve were done. The thirteenth would come with the Moonfeast, when the whole city would light its lanterns and give thanks for peace, and no one giving thanks would remember there had ever been anything to be thankful *for*.

They were happy. That was the thing no one understood, the thing she could never explain to the archivists she'd burned or the wardens she'd bought or the Hollowed standing silent behind her in their gray court dress. The kingdom was happy. Genuinely, softly, incurably happy. She had walked the Moonway that morning and seen a baker giving bread to beggars, a moneylender forgiving debts, a woman laughing in the rain without any reason to laugh, and every one of them had been *happy*, and every one of them had been hollowed down to the shape of that happiness like a bell cast around its own ringing.

The Quiet did not devour people. That was the lie the rebels told, the lie the drowned archive preserved in its flooded dark. The Quiet *simplified* them. It took the grief out of the baker and the greed out of the moneylender and the fear out of the woman in the rain, and what remained was smooth and pleasant and empty as river stone.

Veyra pressed her hand flat against the cold glass.

*And I am going to save them,* she thought, as she thought every night. *All of them. Even from themselves.*

* * *

The Hollowed did not knock. They simply arrived, the way weather arrives, and so when three of them filled her doorway at midnight she felt no surprise—only a small, professional assessment of which one carried the report.

It was Serrin. He had been her husband's squire once, before. Now his face was a beautiful blank page and his eyes held nothing but the task written on them, and she found this restful in a way she refused to examine too closely.

"Report," she said.

"The thieves crossed the boundary of the Veil nine days ago," Serrin said. His voice was even, unhurried, a man reading from a ledger inside himself. "A woman, Lena Zhou, memory-thief by trade. A boy of perhaps eight years. And a third—the thief Maro Dann, known to the woman."

"Known to her how?"

"Childhood. Shared trade. The records are incomplete."

Records were always incomplete. Records were just memories that had learned to hold still. "And the shards?"

"Two confirmed. One in the boy's keeping. One recovered from the noblewoman Dela Marre after the incident in Corrow—" he paused, and the pause was the closest a Hollowed came to emotion, a hitch in the machinery, "—where the boy spoke."

"In the old tongue."

"Yes, Regent-Counsel. Three windows shattered. Two guards deafened for an hour. The shard answered him." Serrin's head tilted, very slightly, the way a dog tilts its head at a sound it cannot name. "The ring sang first. Then he spoke. Then it came to him across the table and cut the lady's hand getting free."

Veyra turned from the window. "You find this remarkable."

"I find it—" Serrin stopped. The machinery worked. "I do not know what I find it. There is a space where the finding should be."

That was the trouble with the Hollowed. You could empty a person of memory, of want, of grief, but you could not empty them of *shape*, and sometimes the shape of a thing they'd been would press through the emptiness like a knee through wet paper. Her husband's squire had been curious. Curiosity had no memory to erase; it lived deeper than memory, in the architecture itself.

"You find it remarkable," Veyra said gently. "That is permitted. Remark is not loyalty or disloyalty. Remark is weather."

"Yes, Regent-Counsel."

"Where are they now?"

"The road toward Bellharrow. We have watchers in the city. The broker Halven has been instructed to make an offer to the woman Zhou—a large purchase, her own specialty, a memory of her choosing from our vaults. If she takes payment, we will know where she sleeps within a day."

"And if she doesn't?"

Serrin said nothing, because there was nothing to say. If the thief didn't sell, then the Hollowed would do what the Hollowed did, and the city of Bellharrow would learn what its happiness cost.

"Leave me," Veyra said. "Send no one else tonight. I have letters to write."

They withdrew without bowing—they did not bow; ceremony required remembering why one bowed—and the door closed behind them with the soft finality of snowfall.

Veyra sat down at her desk and did not write letters.

She opened the drawer instead, and took out the mirror, and unwrapped it.

* * *

Her family had died on a Tuesday.

That was the detail that survived everything, the splinter in the wood of her that no amount of quiet could wear smooth. Not the fire, not the fever—there were stories she told officials, versions where plague took them, versions where bandits did—but the truth was simpler and stupider. A Tuesday in autumn, a ferry crossing the Sorrel, a spring flood two seasons late weakening the pilings, and the river taking eleven souls in under a minute. Her husband Tomas. Her daughters Wren and Issa, aged six and four. Her mother. All of it gone between one wave and the next while Veyra stood on the far bank holding a basket of pears that no one would ever eat.

She had screamed until her voice broke. She had waded in until the current nearly took her too. And then, in the way of people who survive what should not be survivable, she had gone home and buried her grief so deep that when the king's men came asking for a new counselor—someone unattached, someone with nothing left to lose—she had seemed, from the outside, remarkably composed.

She remembered the first audience. She remembered it better than her wedding, better than her daughters' faces, because it was the last memory she owned that still had weight.

The king had sat on the throne and smiled at her, and it was a kind smile, a warm smile, and behind his eyes something else smiled wider, and she had not known then what she was looking at. She knew only that when the king spoke, her grief went quiet for the first time in a year—not healed, not eased, *quiet*, like a room where someone had finally stopped crying.

"You have lost everything," the king had said, in a voice like honey poured over a blade. "But everything can be returned. Nothing that was taken by time is beyond reach of the crown. Serve the binding, Veyra of the Sorrel. Complete the thirteen turns. And on the thirteenth night, when the last shard is set and the last memory given, your family will walk out of the past and into your arms. Whole. Warm. Remembering you."

"And the kingdom?" she had asked. She had been a counselor for exactly one hour and already asking the right questions. "What happens to the kingdom?"

The smile behind the eyes had widened.

"The kingdom will finally be at peace."

She had believed it. That was the thing she could never say aloud, the confession she kept locked tighter than the mirror: she had believed it *completely*, and she believed it still, and if that made her a monster then the monster had excellent reasons.

Because here was what the drowned archive got wrong, what the defectors and the rebels and the sentimental historians never understood: the binding was not cruelty. The binding was *mercy*. A war had ended because of it—a war that had burned the eastern provinces for a generation, a war she had read about in the true histories, where children ate bark and mothers sold daughters and the fields grew more dead than grain. The king had ended it by paying the only price that could end it. And the price was not his alone. The price was everyone's grief, everyone's hate, everyone's sharp edges, ground down year by year, moon by moon, until the thirteenth turn made it permanent and no one in the kingdom could ever again pick up a sword against their neighbor because no one would remember why anyone had ever picked up a sword at all.

Peace. Real peace. Not the paper peace of treaties but the peace of the grave, spread wide enough to hold a nation.

And the cost—

The cost was that her daughters were dead and would stay dead unless she served. The cost was that the Hollowed in her hallway had once been people with names. The cost was that somewhere in the vaults below the palace, in lead-lined boxes, sat the memories of her husband's laugh and Wren's terrible singing and Issa's habit of eating pears from the bottom up, harvested by the Quiet's agents over years, kept safe, kept *warm*, waiting for the thirteenth night the way seeds wait for rain.

She had checked. Once, three years ago, she had gone down into the vault with a lantern and found the box marked with her family's sigil, the sorrel leaf, and she had opened it and touched the memories inside—cold little shapes, like frost flowers—and one of them had flared against her fingers and shown her a kitchen, morning light, Tomas burning the porridge again, Wren singing, Issa laughing, and Veyra had knelt on the vault floor and wept for the first time since the river.

They were real. They were *real*. The Quiet had not lied to her. Whatever else it was, whatever it wanted the kingdom for, it had kept her family's selves intact and promised them back, and in twelve years it had never once broken a promise to her.

Could she say the same of any living man?

* * *

There was a knock. An actual knock—which meant it was not the Hollowed, and not a servant, and therefore either very good news or very bad.

"Enter."

The spymaster Ondrell came in wet from the rain, hat in hand, and Veyra noted the rain because Ondrell was a man who noticed things and being noticed noticing was his way of speaking. He laid a folded paper on her desk.

"Bellharrow," he said. "The broker Halven is dead."

Veyra went very still. "How?"

"His shop burned with him inside it. Before he died, he sent a message—half-burned, mostly cipher, but the readable part says the thieves came to him. The woman Zhou recognized merchandise in his inventory. Merchandise from the Veil." Ondrell's mouth twitched. "One of her own thefts, apparently. A widow's grief. She was not pleased."

"She destroyed the stock?"

"She burned the shop. Whether deliberately or in anger, the watchers can't agree. But here is the part that matters." He leaned in, and lowered his voice as though the walls might be listening, and perhaps they were; the palace listened the way the Quiet listened, patiently, everywhere. "Halven's ledger listed a buyer for the Veil goods. Every shipment, same buyer, same seal. The seal of the private chamber, Veyra. *Your* seal. Someone has been running a supply line out of the Veil under your authority, and the thief Zhou has now seen it."

Veyra looked at the paper without touching it. Somewhere in the palace, someone was forging her hand—or someone was following orders she had never given, which amounted to the same thing and was worse. The Quiet spoke to others besides her. She had always suspected it. She had never let herself confirm it, because confirming it meant admitting that her place in the design was not as secure as her place in the promise.

"Who else knows of the ledger?"

"Halven's apprentice escaped the fire. He's in custody. He remembers nothing useful—he's been drinking away the details in a dockside tavern, but he'll talk eventually."

"No. He won't." She rose. "Have the Hollowed collect him quietly. Not emptied—just held. If the thief Zhou comes looking for witnesses, I want a witness to give her."

Ondrell raised an eyebrow. "You want to feed her?"

"I want to *aim* her." Veyra walked to the window. Below, the city glowed with early festival lanterns, thousands of small warm lights, each one a household that slept soundly because something beneath the throne was eating its nightmares. "Think, Ondrell. The woman steals memories for coin. She has discovered her work feeds the palace. Shame will move her faster than soldiers. Let her follow the supply chain. Let her come to us angry and righteous, with the boy and the shards close at hand, believing she is uncovering a conspiracy."

"And isn't she?"

"Of course she is." Veyra smiled, and it was not a kind smile, though it was not unkind either; it was the smile of a woman doing arithmetic with lives. "She is uncovering exactly what I intend her to uncover. A conspiracy. Mine. She will bring the shards to the palace to confront it, and on the thirteenth night, when the binding completes, she will be standing in the one place in the world where the Quiet can take them from her gently, without blood, without breaking the boy."

"And if she doesn't come?"

Veyra watched the lanterns. In her mind, superimposed over them, she saw a kitchen with morning light and burning porridge and a song sung terribly, beautifully, off-key.

"Then the Hollowed will bring her," she said. "But they won't need to. People always come for what they've lost. It's the one law older than the crown." She turned back to Ondrell, and her voice softened into something almost tender. "She's carrying a child who speaks the dead tongue and a piece of a broken king. Do you know what that makes her?"

"A threat, Regent-Counsel."

"No." Veyra looked out at the happy, sleeping, simplifying city, and loved it, and mourned it, and would have burned it to ash to keep the promise that kept her upright. "It makes her a courier. And couriers," she said, "always deliver."

When Ondrell had gone, she locked the door, and sat alone with the twelfth moon climbing, and allowed herself—for exactly one hundred heartbeats, her nightly ration—to imagine the thirteenth morning. Tomas's arms. Wren's song. Issa with a pear, eating it wrong, laughing at her.

Then the hundred beats passed, and she put the future away like a letter she could not yet open, and began to plan how to catch a thief.
