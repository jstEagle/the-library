## 13. Ash Speaks

The drowned archive breathes.

That is the first thing I understood about this place, before I understood anything else: the water above us moves like a lung. It rises against the glass ceiling of the flooded library in slow swells, and when the moonlight comes through it, the whole room fills with shifting silver veins, as if we are standing inside a great sleeping animal and its blood is made of light.

I have been counting the swells. I count everything now. Twelve hundred and forty since Lena fell asleep among the shelves with a book open on her chest, her fingers still curled around the hilt of the knife Maro gave her. Twelve hundred and forty breaths of the drowned world above us, and in every one of them I have been practicing.

Practicing words.

Not the old tongue. The old tongue is easy—it lives in my chest like a second heartbeat, and when I use it, things break. Windows. Locks. The silence that keeps me safe. No. I have been practicing *her* language. Common, the archivist called it, before he went up to keep watch at the flooded stair. Common, which is the language of everyone who forgot me.

My mouth does not want to make the shapes. It has been closed for so long that my lips feel like something borrowed, something I am only wearing. But tonight the moon through the water is almost full, and the thirteenth turn is coming, and I can feel it in my teeth the way you feel a storm in an old scar. There is no more time to be a child who cannot speak. There is no more time to be safe.

Lena stirs. Her book slides. She catches it without waking, the way she catches everything—without seeming to try—and settles deeper into the nest of dry cloaks she has built between two shelves of drowned history.

I cross the room to her. My feet know how to be quiet; they learned that before they learned anything else. I kneel where she can see me if she opens her eyes, because I have decided that she should see me when I say it. All of it. She should not have to hear my voice out of the dark like a ghost's.

"Lena," I say.

The word comes out cracked. Rusty. Like a door in a house no one has entered in years. But it is *common*, and it is *her name*, and her eyes open at once—not all the way, not frightened, just awake the way she is always half-awake, the way people are who have spent their lives waiting for someone to come take something from them.

She looks at me for a long moment. Then, very carefully, she sits up.

"Ash?" she whispers. "Was that—you?"

I nod. My hands are shaking. I press them flat against my knees the way she taught me to do when the shard-songs get too loud, when the gold in my pocket starts remembering wars.

"You can talk." Her voice is strange—wonder stretched over something that might be fear, or might be grief. "Ash, you've been able to talk this whole time?"

"Not this," I manage. Each word costs me. I have to reach down inside myself and pull them up one at a time, like drawing water from a very deep well with a very small bucket. "This is new. Practiced. Since Bellharrow. Since the ring."

"The word that broke the windows."

"Yes."

She runs both hands through her hair and stares at me, and I watch her decide—watch her choose, right there in front of me, the way she chose in the Veil when the elders wanted the shard destroyed—to trust me anyway. I have watched her make that choice so many times now. I think it is the thing I love most about her, though I did not have the word for love until recently. In the old tongue there were many words for it. In common there is only one, and it is too small, and I will spend the rest of my life trying to fit what I mean into it.

"Okay," she says softly. "Okay. You're talking. Why now? What changed?"

"Time changed." I swallow. My throat aches around the shapes. "Thirteen turns. Almost done. And there are things you need to know before the last moon, and I could not—" I stop. Start again. "I could not draw them. Some things cannot be drawn."

"Then tell me." She shifts over, making space beside her in the cloak-nest, and pats it once—an invitation, casual as breathing, as if making room for me is the most natural act in the world. "Come here. Tell me slowly. We've got until the water rises or doesn't."

I climb in beside her. She smells of river-damp and lamp oil and the particular cold-metal scent that clings to her after she touches the shards—the thief's smell, the smell of someone who handles other people's pasts. Under the glass ceiling, the moonlight veins shift and slide across her face.

"I was born in the palace," I say.

She goes still.

"In the capital. Before the war. Before the binding. My father was—" The well is deep here; I haul hard. "My father was the king."

* * *

She does not interrupt me. That is the first mercy. She just listens, her whole body turned toward mine, her eyes never leaving my face, as if I am a text she is reading in a language she is learning fast.

"There were two sons," I say. "Everyone remembers one. The crown prince, who died in the last year of the war. His death broke something in my father. That part of the story is true. That part is in the archives upstairs, under the water, written by men who believed they were writing truth."

"And you," Lena says quietly.

"I was the younger son. Born three years after him. Small. Quiet. My mother died bringing me into the world, and I think—I think my father looked at me and saw the cost of me before he ever saw me at all. But he loved me. I remember that he loved me. He used to carry me on his shoulders through the palace gardens and tell me the names of all the trees in the old tongue, because the old tongue was ours, from my mother's side, and he said it kept her alive to speak it." My voice cracks on the word *alive*, and I press on before the crack becomes a break. "He named me in the old tongue too. A name that means ember. Something small that holds fire."

"Ash," Lena breathes.

"It is not a name I chose," I say. "It is the only piece of myself I had left."

The water above us swells. Silver light crawls across the shelves, across the spines of books that hold the true history of the kingdom—the binding, the war, the price. I have read them all in the days we have hidden here. I have read my own erasure in a dead archivist's careful hand.

"When the Quiet came," I say, "when my father went to the mountain to bargain with it, the bargain was not only his memory. That is the lie the histories tell—that he traded himself alone, nobly, cleanly. It was not clean. Nothing about it was clean. The Quiet wanted a host, yes. But it also wanted—" I search for the shape of the thought in common, and it will not come easily, so I build it brick by brick. "It wanted to be certain. Certain that no one would ever undo the binding. And a binding can be undone by love. By someone who remembers what was lost fighting to get it back. So the Quiet took the memory of me from everyone. Not killed me. *Erased* me. From my father. From the court. From the records. From the nurses who fed me and the guards who knew my face. Every mind that held me was emptied of me, and the spaces where I had been were smoothed over like water closing after a stone."

Lena's hand finds mine in the dark between us. She does not grab. She just rests hers there, open, and lets me decide whether to take it.

I take it.

"How did you survive?" she asks. "If everyone forgot you—if even your father—"

"Because forgetting is not the same as unmaking." I look up at the veined silver ceiling. "The Quiet took me from minds. But minds are not the only places memories live. I was forgotten by everyone who ever knew me—but I was still *made* of their remembering. Do you understand? A child is a thing his mother's songs built, his father's shoulders, the cook who slipped him honey. All those moments happened. They happened to *me*. When the Quiet pulled the memory of me out of every head in the kingdom, the moments themselves had nowhere to go. So they went into me. I became the house of my own forgotten life. I have spent eleven years walking around inside it."

"That's why the old tongue breaks things," Lena says slowly. "Why the shard answered you. Why Veyra wants you so badly."

"The Quiet cannot erase me twice. Whatever it wears my father's face and says *I have no son*, the fact of me is still true somewhere—in me. I am the loose thread. I am the one proof that the binding was theft and not sacrifice." I turn to look at her, and the moonlight is in my eyes, and I let her see everything in them. "And a thread, pulled, unravels the whole garment. That is what I am for. That is what I have *always* been for. The thirteenth turn completes the binding unless the binding is broken, and I am the breaking of it. The shards know me. They were cut from the crown my father wore the day he gave himself away, and gold remembers what minds forget."

For a long time neither of us speaks. Above us the drowned library breathes. Somewhere up the flooded stair, the archivist's boots creak on stone, keeping his lonely watch.

"My father," I say at last, and my voice is smaller now, and I hate that, and I say it anyway. "In the throne room. When you touched him. You saw him behind the glass."

"I saw him," Lena says. "He was screaming. And the thing wearing him was smiling."

"He mouths a word. You told Maro. You thought it was nonsense."

Her breath catches. "It wasn't nonsense."

"It was my name." The well is bottomless now; the words come up dark and cold and endless. "He has been mouthing it for eleven years. Through the Quiet's smile. Through thirteen feasts and thirteen false springs. He does not know what the word means anymore—he cannot know, the knowing was taken—but his mouth remembers. The body keeps what the mind loses, Lena. Did you know that? Your trade taught you that. Every widow's grief you lifted, every soldier's terror—they were gone from the head but living in the hands, the throat, the dreams. My father's mouth has been saying my name to an empty throne room for eleven years, and the Quiet lets him, because a word without a meaning is harmless. Because it thinks a name is nothing."

"But it isn't," Lena says.

"It is everything." I am crying now. I did not decide to cry; the crying decided itself, the way storms decide. "You asked me once—on the road, outside Bellharrow, the night the Hollowed nearly found us—you asked me what I was afraid of. I drew you a picture of a door. You thought I meant the hunters. I meant this. I meant telling you. Because once I say it, it is real, and once it is real, it can hurt, and I have been hurt in every way except this one. I have been erased and hunted and starved and mute. But I have never yet been *believed* and then left."

Her hand tightens around mine.

"I'm not leaving," she says.

"You should understand what you are staying for." I wipe my face with my sleeve, furious at the weakness of it, and force the last of it up from the well. "You have been unmade too. Do you know that? Everyone I love has been unmade by someone else's choice. My father chose to bind himself and it erased me. The Quiet chose hunger and it hollowed the courtiers. Veyra's masters chose her grief and turned it into a leash. And you—" I look at her, at the sharp tired beautiful wreck of her, the woman who steals other people's pasts because no one ever gave her a present worth keeping. "Someone chose for you, Lena. Someone brought you to the Veil as a child and let it hollow you politely, legally, one stolen memory at a time, and called it a living. The Veil is just the Quiet with better manners. It takes and takes and calls the taking safety. You have been being erased your whole life, and you were so good at surviving it that you mistook survival for a self."

She flinches. I see the flinch land, and I do not soften it, because softening it would be lying, and I have decided that whatever else I owe her, I owe her the truth with its edges on.

"So when you stay," I finish, "you are not staying for a mission. You are staying for the first choice anyone has ever offered you. Stay or go—but *choose*, and know that you chose. That is all I wanted to give you tonight. Not a burden. A door, unlocked, with me standing on this side of it."

* * *

The moon climbs. The silver veins in the water overhead lengthen and thin, like hair unbraiding. We sit shoulder to shoulder in the cloak-nest, and Lena does not speak for a long time, and I have learned that her silences are not empty—they are full, packed, like a warehouse before an auction.

Finally she says, "Your father carried you on his shoulders."

"Yes."

"And told you the names of trees."

"In the old tongue. The oak is *halath*. The birch is *sivenna*. The willow by the eastern wall was *moraeth*, which also means grief, because my mother planted it, and my father said the tree and the word had grown to fit each other." I smile despite everything. "I was four. I thought every tree in the world was a word for missing someone."

Lena laughs—a short, wet, startled sound—and then presses the back of her hand to her mouth, and then gives up and lets herself laugh properly, helplessly, until she is wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand. "Gods. Eleven years old and you're already doing this. Saying things that crack people open. You really are his son."

"I am going to save him," I say. "And it is going to cost you. I need to say that plainly, because Maro is gone and you are all I have and I will not buy your courage with a lie. To pull the thread, someone must gather the shards and stand before the Quiet on the thirteenth night and *take my father back*—all of him, every memory, every year. And the only person alive who knows how to take a whole life out of a man without killing him is a memory-thief. The best one. The one who has been carrying pieces of him through her own head without knowing it."

She is quiet again. Outside our glass ceiling, far above the water, I can feel the moon turning—feel it in the shards in my pocket, three of them now, humming against each other like struck bells that never quite finish ringing.

"The Veil," she says at last. "When this is done. If I survive it. Will I be able to go back?"

I consider lying to her. I decide, again, against it.

"No," I say. "Not unchanged. Doors don't close again once they've opened. But Lena—" I take her hand back, both of mine around her one, the way she held mine in the storm the night we met, when I was a shivering nothing with a piece of gold and no name anyone could pronounce. "You were never theirs either. The Veil raised you, but it never *kept* you. It kept a girl-shaped absence and called it Lena. Whoever you are underneath all the taking and the being-taken-from—that person hasn't been born yet. Maybe the thirteenth night is where she gets born."

Above us, the water goes suddenly, utterly still.

Both of us feel it. Both of us look up. The silver veins hang motionless, and in the silence I hear what the archivist heard a moment ago at the top of the stair—boots. Many boots. Wet stone ringing with a step that is perfectly, terribly even, the step of people whose hearts have been emptied and refilled with obedience.

The Hollowed have found the drowned archive.

Lena is already moving, snuffing the lamp, pressing the third shard into my hand. "Tell me the rest later," she whispers—and then, softer, with a fierce broken tenderness that I will carry through everything that comes after: "*Son of the king.*"

The boots begin to descend.
