## 14. The Third Turn of the Knife

They took him at the ford.

Maro had counted on the water to hide their trail—cold, fast, running brown with snowmelt—and it might have worked if Ash hadn't stopped in the middle of the crossing and turned his face upstream like a compass needle finding north. The boy had gone rigid, gripping Lena's sleeve, and Maro had known before he saw them: the Hollowed, standing on the far bank in their gray traveling clothes, arranged not like soldiers but like guests at a dinner party, patient and pleasant and wrong.

There were six of them. They didn't draw weapons. One of them smiled, and it was the smile that made Maro's stomach drop—the too-wide contentment of the villages they'd passed, the smile of people who had nothing left inside to be unhappy about.

"Run," Maro said quietly. "Both of you. Upstream, into the rocks."

Lena's head snapped toward him. "Maro—"

"The rocks slow horses. You're faster alone." He was already pulling his knife from his belt, already turning to face the bank, and he kept his voice light because that was the trick of it, that had always been the trick of it—make the lie sound like a joke and people believe you're joking. "Go on. I'll catch up. I always catch up."

Ash made a sound then, small and cracked, a word in the old tongue that Maro didn't know but understood anyway. *No.*

"Boy," Maro said, kneeling so they were eye to eye, water rushing around both their legs, "you remember what I told you about debts? About how some of them are worth paying twice?" He tapped the boy's chest, right over the heart. "This one's mine. Been mine since Bellharrow. Let me settle it."

Lena grabbed his arm. Her fingers were shaking, and her face was doing the thing it did when she refused to feel something—the flat competence-mask, the thief's blankness—but her eyes gave her away, the way they always had since they were children stealing apples off Sera Voss's cart. "You come back," she said. "You hear me? You come back or I'll steal every memory you have left and sell them for coppers."

"That's the spirit." He kissed her forehead, quick, the way you'd touch a hot stove. "Now go."

And she went. She took Ash's hand and plunged upstream into the boulder field, and Maro watched them go for exactly one heartbeat—two silhouettes against the white water, his whole life in the valley and everything after it—before he turned and waded toward the bank and the smiling dead-eyed courtiers waiting there.

* * *

They tied his hands with silk.

That was the detail that frightened him most, afterward, when he had time to be frightened. Not rope, not iron—silk, soft as a lover's ribbon, knotted by hands that never hurried. The Hollowed did not need restraints really; they needed him only to walk where they walked. But they bound him anyway, gently, the way you'd bandage a wound, and one of them—a woman with a face like a painted egg, smooth and pretty and utterly vacant—kept offering him dried apricots from a pouch at her belt.

"You should eat," she said, for the fourth time. "It's a long way to the road."

"I'm not hungry."

"You should eat," she said again, with the same warm inflection, as though he hadn't spoken. "It's a long way to the road."

The camp they brought him to sat in a fold of hills above the river, all gray canvas and banked fires, and at its center stood a carriage that cost more than everything Maro had ever stolen in his life put together. Black lacquer, silver fittings, curtains drawn. He'd heard about Veyra, the Regent-Counsel. Everyone had. The king's shadow, the king's voice, the woman who ran thirteen years of false peace without ever once being seen to smile.

The carriage door opened. She stepped down herself, which surprised him—he'd expected her to summon him inside, make him wait, make him sweat. Instead she came to him in the firelight, a tall woman in dove-gray with her hair pinned back severe and simple, and she looked at him the way an appraiser looks at a piece that might be counterfeit.

"Maro Kesh," she said. "Of the Veil."

"You have me at a disadvantage. Everyone knows your name. Nobody knows mine."

"I know a great deal about you." She circled him slowly. "Born outside the valley, smuggled in at age six. Trained as a memory-thief under old Halloway. Expelled twice for selling outside the guild's rates. No family. No holdings. One sister."

The word landed like a hook set behind his eyes. He kept his face still. Twenty years of card games and crooked dice and lying to wardens' faces had taught him how to keep a face still.

"No family," he agreed pleasantly. "You said that already."

"Rhea Kesh." Veyra stopped in front of him. "Sold three memories to a broker in Bellharrow four years ago to pay a debt she couldn't cover. Grief, mostly. Your mother's death, taken out of her so she could stop weeping. The broker's ledger shows the memories were purchased by a palace buyer." She tilted her head. "She works in a dye-house now. She is, by all accounts, quite cheerful. She hums while she works. She has no idea why she can't remember her own mother's face."

The fire crackled. Somewhere in the dark, one of the Hollowed laughed softly at nothing.

"What do you want," Maro said, and his voice wasn't pleasant anymore.

"The shards. The child. And the thief who carries them." Veyra folded her hands. "Give me those, and Rhea gets back everything she sold. Every memory, restored by morning, along with enough coin that she never sells another piece of herself. She'll wake up whole. She'll remember your mother's funeral. She'll remember you."

"And if I don't?"

"Then she stays as she is. Cheerful. Hollowed out one sale at a time until there's nothing left but the humming." Veyra's voice never rose, never fell. It was the voice of someone reading terms aloud. "I'm not threatening you, Maro. I'm showing you a ledger. You strike me as a man who understands ledgers."

He did. That was the hell of it. He understood them better than anyone alive, and the ledger here balanced clean and cruel: two lives he'd known for a season against one life he'd known forever. A child he loved, sure, in the fierce sudden way you love things that arrive broken at your door—but a child nonetheless, a stranger's child, a mission out of a drowned archive and a dead archivist's fever dreams. Against Rhea. Against Rhea humming in the dye-house with a hole where her mother used to be.

"Where are they now?" he asked.

Veyra smiled, and it was the first time he'd seen her do it, and it was worse than the Hollowed's smiles because hers had something living behind it. "Upstream. In the rocks. Cold, hungry, and moving slowly because the boy keeps looking over his shoulder for you." She let that sit. "My riders will find them by dawn either way, Maro. The bargain only changes what happens after. Tell me where they'll be, and I call the riders off the kill order. They come in alive. All of you come in alive. Rhea comes out of this whole."

*All of you come in alive.* His mind snagged on it, turned it over, found the seam. She hadn't said *free*. She hadn't said anything about what happened after they arrived. And beneath the words, faint as a smell, he felt the thing he'd felt in the villages—the wide contentment, the humming—radiating off this whole operation like heat off a forge.

But that was tomorrow's problem. That was the problem you solved with a knife and a lie and a bit of luck, and Maro Kesh had built a life out of exactly those materials.

"All right," he said. "Here's where they'll be."

* * *

He lied beautifully.

That was the truth of it, and he clung to it in the hours after, when the silk cut lines into his wrists and the apricot woman watched him with her empty pleasant eyes. He lied the way he'd never lied in his life, because every other lie had been for coin and this one was for blood. He gave Veyra a route through the rocks—detailed, confident, studded with true details lifted from real terrain so it would survive scrutiny. He described a cave above the second falls where they'd sheltered two nights ago, said Lena's ankle was turned, said they'd move slow. He even wept a little, letting his voice break on the boy's name, and Veyra watched him cry with her appraiser's eyes and believed him, or half-believed him, which was enough.

At midnight she sent twelve riders upstream along the route he'd given them, toward a cave that stood empty, toward a trail that went cold at the falls and split three ways into stone country where horses died and men starved. By the time they sorted it out, Lena and Ash would be a day ahead and climbing into the high passes, and Maro knew his girl—knew she'd take the cruelest route, the one no tracker would follow, because cruelty was safety and she'd never once in her life chosen the easy path when the hard one was available.

Which left him.

He'd known it would leave him. He'd known it in the water at the ford, when he'd pulled the knife and told her he'd catch up. There was no version of this ledger where he walked away. Veyra didn't leave loose threads, and a man who'd lied to her face with tears in his eyes was the loosest thread in the kingdom.

So when the riders came back at dawn, mud-spattered and furious, and Veyra emerged from her carriage with her face gone quiet and terrible, Maro was sitting up against the wheel, waiting, almost calm.

"They were never there," she said.

"No," Maro agreed. "They weren't."

"Why?"

He looked past her, east, where the sky was going pale over the hills. Somewhere out there, Lena was hauling a silent boy up a mountainside, cursing his name and mine, he thought, and it made him grin, actually grin, blood in his mouth and silk biting his wrists.

"Because my sister wouldn't want me to," he said. "Because she sold her grief to feed us when we were kids, and she never once complained, and if I traded her brother for her memories she'd wake up whole and find out what I was worth, and she'd rather stay hollow." He shrugged. "Some debts are worth paying twice, lady. I paid this one at the ford. Consider this the interest."

Veyra studied him for a long moment. Something moved behind her eyes—not doubt, exactly. Recognition. The look of a woman meeting a strange mirror of herself, someone else who had also priced a soul against a person and found the arithmetic wanting.

"You understand what happens now," she said.

"I've got a general idea."

"The Hollowed will question you. You will tell them everything you know, because that is what they do—they take, and what remains obeys. And then whatever is left of you will serve the crown, smiling, and you will forget that you ever chose this." She paused. "For what it is worth, I am sorry. I meant the offer sincerely. I wanted to give you the choice while it still existed."

"Yeah," Maro said. "That's the worst part. I believe you."

He thought about asking her for something—water, a prayer, a message to Rhea—but asking felt like begging and begging felt like losing, and besides, there was only one message that mattered and it couldn't be carried. It had to be remembered. So instead he looked at the apricot woman, the hollow one with the kind empty face, and he said:

"When you take me. When I'm—" he waved his bound hands, "—after. If some piece of me is still floating around in there somewhere, tell it this: tell it I caught up. Tell it I always catch up."

The woman offered him a dried apricot.

He ate it. It was the sweetest thing he'd ever tasted, and he was crying when he swallowed it, and the Hollowed gathered around him in the gray dawn light like guests arriving at a dinner party, gentle and patient and terribly polite, and the last free thought Maro Kesh had was not of fear at all.

It was of a girl of nine years old, laughing at him across Sera Voss's apple cart, juice running down her chin, saying *you're the worst liar I've ever met, Maro Kesh,* and him thinking: *just wait.*

Then they touched his temples, and the taking began.

* * *

Far upstream, in the rocks above the second falls, Lena Zhou stopped climbing so suddenly that Ash stumbled into her back.

"What?" he signed, urgent, scanning the slope below.

"Nothing." She pressed her hand flat against her sternum, frowning, as though something had brushed her there—cold, brief, like a door closing very far away. "Nothing. Keep moving."

Behind them, a day's ride south, the sun rose on an empty cave, twelve furious riders, and a man in silk bindings who was learning, smile by smile, how to forget.

But some debts are worth paying twice. And Maro Kesh had never once, in his entire crooked life, failed to catch up.
