## 15. Palace of Smiles

The capital announced itself by smell before it appeared over the hills: honey and roasting chestnuts and crushed moonpetals, the sweet rot of a city mid-celebration. Lena smelled it from the crest of the last ridge and felt her stomach tighten around nothing.

"Thirteen years of false peace," she said. "And they throw it a party."

"They don't know it's false," Maro said. He walked with his hood back despite everything—despite Bellharrow, despite the Hollowed who had hunted them for three hundred miles, despite his own face being known to at least one broker in every market town between here and the coast. Maro had never learned to be afraid properly. It was one of the things she loved about him and one of the reasons she'd spent half the journey wanting to shake him. "To them it's just peace. The best they've ever had."

"That's what frightens me."

Ash said nothing. Ash hadn't spoken since the noblewoman's drawing room, since the word that had shattered forty panes of glass and left the woman kneeling on her carpet with her hands over her ears, weeping without knowing why. But he walked closer to Lena now than he used to, close enough that his shoulder brushed her hip when the road narrowed, and he kept the shard-wrapped bundle pressed against his chest like a second heart.

They crested the ridge, and there it was: Aurelmar, the City of Lanterns, spread along its river in a spill of gold light. Even from this distance Lena could see the festival—the Moonfeast, thirteenth of its name, the greatest in living memory. Lanterns rose from every quarter in slow luminous flocks. Banners hung from bridges. The river itself seemed lit from beneath, as if the water had been persuaded to glow.

"It's beautiful," Ash signed, one-handed, not slowing his stride.

"Yes," Lena said. "That's the problem."

* * *

The disguise had been Maro's idea, because the disguise was always Maro's idea; he thought in costumes the way other people thought in sentences. Memory-brokers traveled to the capital for the Moonfeast the way pilgrims traveled to shrines—it was the single night of the year when the palace opened its ledgers, when licensed brokers could sell directly to the Crown's agents. All they needed was a license, a cart, and the particular air of bored prosperity that brokers wore like armor.

The license was real. It had belonged to a broker named Hessel Vane, out of Bellharrow, who had made the mistake three nights ago of trying to sell a memory that turned out to be one of Lena's own thefts—a miller's first love, sold twice—and who had made the further mistake of recognizing her face while doing it. They'd traded licenses in an alley behind his stall. He was alive. She'd checked. He was also tied up in a wine cellar with a very honest account of his evening ahead of him, and by the time anyone found him, the Moonfeast would be over and so, one way or another, would their business in Aurelmar.

The cart was borrowed. The clothes were bought with the last of the drowned archivist's coin: gray wool, good boots, silver pins shaped like closed eyes—the guild sigil. Lena had pinned hers on in front of a cracked mirror and stared at herself for a long moment. A broker's pin. She'd spent her whole life taking memories apart; now she wore the emblem of the trade on her collar like a medal.

"You look respectable," Maro said. "It's unsettling."

"Say that again and I'll take your memory of ever meeting me."

"You wouldn't. You'd miss me too much." He grinned, then didn't, then looked away down the road toward the glowing city. "Lena. If it goes wrong in there—"

"It won't go wrong."

"If it does. You take Ash and you go. Don't be clever. Clever is my job."

She looked at him. Three hundred miles of road sat between them and everything they'd been to each other—the Veil, the wardens' whistles, the night he'd come back leading Hollowed soldiers and then turned those same soldiers' ledger against them at the gate, buying her four minutes with a lie so beautiful she almost applauded. Some debts are worth paying twice, he'd whispered, and she still didn't know which debt he'd meant.

"We all walk out," she said. "All three."

"All three," he agreed, in the voice people use when they're letting someone win.

* * *

The gates of Aurelmar were open. That was the first wrong thing.

A capital city on feast night should have been a chokepoint—queues, tolls, wardens checking papers, pickpockets working the crowd. Instead the great southern gate stood wide as a barn door, unguarded except by two soldiers who were handing out small honeyed cakes to arriving travelers. Free. With both hands. One of them had flour on his cheek and a smile so uncomplicated it looked painted on.

"Cakes for the feast!" the soldier called to them. "Welcome home, travelers! Welcome home!"

Lena took a cake because refusing would have been stranger than accepting. It was delicious. It was, in fact, the best thing she had eaten in months, warm and spiced and dissolving on her tongue, and she stood there chewing it and feeling the wrongness crawl up her spine like cold water.

"He gave away his cakes," she murmured to Maro as they rolled through the gate into the press of the crowd. "Wardens hoard. I've bribed a hundred wardens. They never give anything away."

"Maybe he's just happy."

"Nobody is just happy."

Ash tugged her sleeve. When she glanced down, he was looking at the soldier—at his eyes—and making the sign she'd learned to dread, the flat hand drawn across the brow. *Empty shelves.* Not Hollowed. Hollowed were vacant, obedient, blank as fresh paper. This man was full. He was full of something, and it wasn't entirely him.

The streets swallowed them. And Lena began, street by street, to understand what she was walking into.

It was the happiest city she had ever seen. That was the only way to describe it, and the description curdled even as she formed it. Every face smiled. Not the strained festival grin of people performing joy for a holiday—a deep, settled, unhurried contentment, as though each person had just remembered something wonderful and was still holding it. A fishwife gave away her whole catch to children who hadn't paid. A moneylender tore up a debtor's note in the middle of the street, laughing, while the debtor stared at him in open alarm. An old man sat on his stoop giving away buttons—one button to each passerby, from a jar, solemn as a priest distributing sacrament—and everyone took one, and everyone thanked him, and everyone smiled.

"They've been harvested," Maro said quietly. He'd gone pale under his festival lantern-light. "Not emptied. *Trimmed.* Look—" He nodded at a young couple dancing in a square with no musicians, swaying to nothing, foreheads together. "You can take the grief out and leave the shape of the life. Take the fear, the resentment, the want. Leave the love and the habit. What's left is—" he gestured at the square, the lanterns, the button man, the whole glowing, gentle city—"this."

"Contentment," Lena said.

"Contentment with the teeth pulled out." His jaw worked. "In the villages it was thinner. Patches. Here it's—" He stopped. Lena watched him do the arithmetic she was already doing, the terrible multiplication: how many memories, how many thefts, how many thousands of quiet extractions it would take to sand the fear out of an entire capital city. Thirteen years' worth. Thirteen turns of the moon.

"The crown," Ash signed. He was staring at the palace hill, where the royal keep burned white with light above the city like a second moon anchored to the earth. "It drinks through the brokers. All the stolen things flow uphill. To the throne."

Lena's pin weighed suddenly heavy on her collar. She thought of Bellharrow, of the widow's grief she'd found repackaged and priced at nine silver marks, of the broker who'd told her, sweating, that the palace bought grief by the barrel—that grief especially, that grief was *prized*. She had told herself it was for archives. For study. For the king's melancholy, poor man, they'd said, the king suffers so.

The king suffers so. She looked up at the lit keep and understood, finally, why that phrase had snagged in her ear like a hook.

* * *

They stabled the cart at a feast-rate livery where the stableman refused payment twice before Maro pressed the coins into his apron pocket with the firmness of a man handling a drunk. Then they joined the current flowing uphill.

The Moonfeast proper filled the grand avenue: a river of people moving toward the palace square, carrying lanterns, carrying each other's children, carrying trays of food set down at intervals on long public tables—*take some, take some, please, we made too much.* Musicians played on corners, and the music was lovely, and it was all in a major key, every song, and after the fourth corner Lena realized no one was playing anything sad. Not one lament. Not one work-song. In thirteen years of false peace, in a kingdom that had lost a war and forgotten it had, no one in Aurelmar was singing anything but gladness.

A woman pressed a cup of mulled wine into Lena's hand without breaking stride. "For the feast! Drink, drink—everyone drinks tonight!" Her smile was enormous. Her eyes, when they met Lena's, were wet. "Isn't it wonderful? Isn't everything wonderful?"

"Wonderful," Lena said.

"I lost my son this spring," the woman said, still smiling, still walking, the words coming out smooth and light as if reporting weather. "But I don't mind anymore. Isn't that a mercy? I know I *should* mind. Everyone says I should mind. But the palace doctors were so kind, and now it's just—like a story about someone else. A sad story. With a happy ending somewhere later." She patted Lena's arm with fingers that trembled, just slightly, at the edge of the smile. "You should see the palace doctors. They fix everything."

Then she was gone into the crowd, smiling, wet-eyed, carrying her lantern, carrying the hole where her son had been, patched over with borrowed peace.

Lena stood very still in the moving river of people until Maro's hand found her shoulder.

"Lena."

"She's still in there," Lena said. Her voice came out flat and strange. "Underneath. She knows she should grieve. She's *asking* permission to grieve. They didn't take the love out—they took the mourning, and the love has nowhere to go, so it just—" She stopped. Swallowed. Somewhere behind her ribs, in the place where she kept the widow's grief she had stolen and carried and sold, something turned over.

"You couldn't have known," Maro said. "What the supply chain fed."

"I knew memories went to the palace. I asked once, years ago, and they said the king's household. I *asked*, Maro, and then I stopped asking, because asking doesn't pay." She looked down at Ash, who was watching her with those old, old eyes. "I helped build this. Every theft I ever ran. I fed the thing on the throne one mouthful at a time and called it a living."

Ash reached up and took her hand. He didn't sign anything. He just held on, the way he had in the storm the night they met, and after a moment she could breathe again, and they walked on.

* * *

The palace square opened before them like a held breath.

Ten thousand people, maybe more, packed shoulder to shoulder around a fountain the size of a village pond, all facing the keep. On a balcony high above, draped in white and gold, stood rows of courtiers—beautiful, serene, arranged like fruit. And at the center, at the rail, a figure in a crown of gold filigree, waving.

Even at this distance, even across ten thousand heads and a hundred feet of air, Lena felt it.

The shard against Ash's chest flared hot enough that he gasped. Lena's own stolen senses—the thief's gift that let her feel the seams in a mind—opened involuntarily, the way an eye opens when a bright light swings past. And what she felt rolling off the balcony, off the crowned figure, was *welcome*. Warmth like a hearth. Love like a tide. It washed across the square and every face below tilted up toward it, sunflowers finding the sun, and the cheering redoubled, and Lena stood in the middle of it gripping Ash's hand and Maro's sleeve and fighting not to lean into it herself.

Because it felt *good*. That was the obscenity of it. Whatever wore the king poured out affection the way a broken dam pours water, indiscriminate, bottomless, and every hungry, grieving, frightened scrap of humanity in that square drank and drank and loved it.

*He's feeding them,* Lena thought. *Scraps. Trimmed memories, distilled comfort, poured back over the city like sauce over a meal.* And somewhere in the arithmetic of it, in the balance of what flowed up through the brokers and what flowed back down through the balcony, she knew—with a thief's certainty, the same certainty that told her when a mark's mind had a seam—there had to be a profit. Something was being fattened.

The king raised both arms. The square went silent so completely Lena could hear the fountain.

"People of Aurelmar!" The voice carried impossibly far, warm as honeyed wine. "Thirteen moons of peace! Thirteen moons of plenty! And I love you—I love every one of you—as a father loves his children!"

The cheer that followed shook lanterns loose from their hooks. Beside Lena, Ash had gone rigid. He was staring up at the balcony, and his free hand had risen halfway to his mouth, and he was shaking—not with cold, not with fear exactly, with something older and more specific, the shaking of a body remembering something the mind has been forbidden to keep.

"Ash?" Lena crouched. "Ash, what is it?"

His lips moved. No sound. His eyes never left the figure in the crown.

And the figure—Lena saw it, she would swear for the rest of whatever life she had that she saw it—the figure on the balcony stopped waving. Just for a heartbeat. Just for the length of a missed breath. The crowned head turned, precise as a compass needle, and looked down through ten thousand upturned faces directly at the gray-clad woman crouched beside a mute child.

Across the whole roaring distance, Lena felt the warmth bend toward her like a searchlight. Felt it *recognize* her. Not her face—her craft. The seams in her. The thief's hollow places, the empty rooms where other people's memories had passed through and been sold. The thing on the balcony knew what she was the way a spider knows a fly by the tremor in the web.

Its smile widened. From this far, it shouldn't have been visible at all.

It was.

"Maro," Lena said quietly, rising, keeping her face angled toward the stage like any delighted festival-goer, keeping her grip on Ash's hand iron-tight. "The palace doctors. The ones who 'fix everything.' Where do you suppose a broker goes to sell directly to the household?"

Maro followed her gaze to the keep's flank, where a smaller door glowed beneath a lantern marked with the closed-eye sigil of the guild—the brokers' entrance, open one night a year, guarded by two smiling soldiers handing out honeyed cakes.

"There," he said.

"There," Lena agreed.

Above them, the king resumed waving. Below them, the crowd surged toward the feast tables, gently, terribly happy. And Lena Zhou, wearing another man's license and a dead trade's pin, turned her family toward the door in the mountain's side, where the thing that wore her kingdom's king was waiting to buy.
