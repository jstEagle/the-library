## 16. The King in the Mirror

The Moonfeast made the palace look like a lie told beautifully.

Lena had spent eleven years learning to move through rooms as if she belonged to them—a thief's first and truest craft, older than memory-taking itself—and even so, the Hall of Turning Light nearly undid her. It was built of pale stone that caught the thirteen moons through clerestory windows and scattered them across the floor in slow-moving silver pools, so that walking through the hall felt like wading through moonlight that had been poured out and left standing. Courtiers drifted from pool to pool in silk the colors of dawn things: rose, cream, the soft gold of new honey. Every one of them smiled.

That was the part that made her hands sweat inside her gloves. Not the guards, though there were guards—Hollowed ones, their eyes smooth as river stones, standing at intervals like furniture that had learned to breathe. Not the arithmetic of the thing, though she'd done that arithmetic all night: thirteen moons, twelve turns gone, and somewhere in this palace a binding that would finish at moonrise tomorrow. It was the smiling. Everyone in Bellharrow smiled too widely, but here the smiles had been refined, distilled, aged like wine until they were indistinguishable from happiness. A duchess laughed at something her husband said, and the laugh was warm and genuine and completely empty, a bell with no clapper, ringing because the wind moved it.

*They're happy,* Lena reminded herself, following Ash's small hand-tugs through the crowd. *That's the horror of it. They're actually happy.*

Ash walked beside her dressed as a page, his hair powdered to match the court's fashion, his silence indistinguishable from a servant's trained muteness. He had not spoken since the ring. Since the word that had shattered every window in a Bellharrow townhouse and left the noblewoman kneeling in the glittering wreckage, weeping without knowing why. Lena had asked him about it on the road, gently, and Ash had only pressed his hand flat against his own chest and then, slowly, against hers, and looked at her with an expression she had no name for.

She thought about it now, that expression, because she needed something true to hold onto. The palace was designed to make you forget there was anything outside it. Warmth everywhere, light everywhere, sweetness in the air—honeyed wine and roasted pears and woodsmoke from a hundred candles—and underneath it all, so faint most people would call it imagination, a hum. Like the Veil's silence, but inverted. The Veil pressed silence against your skin. This place pressed *sound*, a sound below hearing, a sound like being told everything was fine, everything had always been fine, there was nothing to worry about, nothing to remember, nothing to—

*Stop.* She fixed her eyes on the dais at the far end of the hall and made herself walk toward it.

The king sat on a throne that was less a chair than an arrangement of light, all filigree and moonstone, elevated on seven steps. He was not what she had expected. She had expected a monster, or a corpse, or something in between—a hollow king with a thing's face stretched over him like a mask. Instead he was beautiful in a tired way, a man in his fifties with grey coming in at his temples, wearing his crown loosely, the way a man wears a hat he's grown tired of. He was listening to a petition. A merchant, by his clothes, bowing and stammering about grain tariffs in the river provinces, and the king listened with his chin on his hand and smiled, and the smile was kind, and the smile was *wrong*, the way a painting of water is wrong if you try to drink it.

Lena had seen that wrongness before. She had spent eleven years taking memories out of people's heads, and she knew what a mind looked like from the inside when something was wearing it.

*Twelve steps to the dais. Two Hollowed at the base, four along the rail. Veyra not visible. Good. Veyra visible would be a different plan entirely, and the different plan is: run.*

She and Ash were brokered tonight—papers Maro had forged before Bellharrow, before the betrayal, before the cell and the sacrifice and the long grey morning of grieving him. Memory-brokers of the Silver Guild, summoned to the capital for the Moonfeast, because on the thirteenth turn, so the invitation said in its warm, curling script, *the crown wished to honor those who kept the kingdom's past.* Lena had read that line forty times. She still didn't know if it was a trap, a joke, or simply the Quiet's idea of humor.

The merchant finished. The king raised one hand, and the whole hall leaned toward him like grass toward sun.

"Grain will be plentiful," the king said, and his voice was warm and low and reached every corner of the hall without effort. "The river provinces have nothing to fear. The kingdom has nothing to fear. There has been peace for twelve years, and there will be peace for twelve hundred."

The hall sighed with pleasure. Lena felt it move through her own chest, that sigh, felt her own mouth wanting to curve, and she bit the inside of her cheek hard enough to taste copper. *That's the hum. That's what it does. Don't listen with your skin.*

Then the king's gaze moved across the hall, the way a host's gaze moves, acknowledging, and it passed over her—

—and stopped.

Not on Ash. On her. For one heartbeat, two, the king of the thirteen moons looked at a forged broker in a borrowed gown, and something happened to his face. The smile stayed. The smile was perfect. But behind it, deep, in the place where a face stops being a face and becomes a window, something *moved*.

Lena had a gift. It wasn't magic—magic couldn't cross the Veil, and she'd been born inside it, which meant whatever lived in her was older than the distinction, a thing of taking and holding, of reaching into the architecture of another mind and walking through its rooms. She had never used it on a king. She had never used it on anything like *this*. But the king was looking at her, and the something behind his eyes was looking at her, and the two looks were so different that the gap between them was a door, and doors, in Lena's long experience, existed to be walked through.

She reached.

* * *

Inside a mind was a house. That was how she'd always experienced it, ever since she was small enough not to know it was strange. The widow's grief had been a room with a flooded floor. Maro's fear had been a house with all the doors nailed shut. You walked in, you found the room, you took what you were paid to take, and you left the rest standing, because a thief who burned the house down had nothing to come back for.

The king's mind was a house too. But the house was *glass*.

Lena stood—there was no other word for it, though she had no body here—in a hall of clear walls that stretched up into darkness, and every wall was a window, and behind every window was a room, and in every room was a memory. She could see them the way you see lit windows from a street at night: a boy on a riverbank with a fishing pole, a young man kneeling before his father, a wedding, a war tent, a woman laughing in a garden, a child—

She moved toward the child and the window went dark. All the windows went dark, all at once, like a city pulling its shutters, and in the sudden black she heard it.

Screaming.

Not from outside the glass. From *inside* a room, one room, a small room she hadn't seen, and she turned toward the sound and there he was: the king. The real one. Pressed against the inside of the glass with both hands, his mouth open, screaming words that made no sound, his face not beautiful and not tired but *ruined*, the face of a man who has been awake for twelve years inside his own skull, watching. Watching his hands sign treaties he didn't choose. Watching his mouth smile at his councilors. Watching, Lena understood with a lurch, watching *everything*, every hour of every day, from behind a wall of glass while something else wore his delight out into the world.

The Quiet. The archivists under the drowned library had named it, in ink that bled: *the Quiet, that which was the price.* It had no shape here. It was the darkness between the windows. It was the reason the windows went dark. It was the hum, the peace, the thirteen moons of contentment, and it was vast, and it was patient, and it was *hungry* in a way that made the word feel inadequate, the way calling the ocean wet feels inadequate.

The king saw her.

She watched it happen—watched hope hit him like a physical blow, watched him slam both palms against the glass, and this time when he screamed she heard one word through it, faint, warped, a voice that had screamed itself nearly to nothing:

*Thief.*

Not an accusation. She understood that instantly, the way you understand things in dreams. It was a *title*. It was the only word he had left that meant hope. *You take things. Take me. Take me, take me, take me—*

The darkness gathered. She felt it notice her the way a sleeping man notices a draft—not waking, not yet, but turning toward it. The windows began to relight one by one, and the lit ones showed the Quiet's version: the king smiling on his throne, the king dancing with his queen, the king holding his son—

The window with the son flared, and in it, for a half-second, Lena saw a small boy with dark hair and eyes like a storm deciding, and the boy turned toward her through the glass, and the boy's face was *Ash's face*, older by ten years, but Ash's, unmistakably, the way a melody is unmistakable in a different key—

The king's fist struck the glass from inside. Once. Twice. And then he stopped screaming and did something worse. He went still. He looked at her through the glass with the terrible calm of a man who has one thing left to say and must spend it well, and he raised one hand, and pressed it flat against the inside of the window, over the place where her face would be if she had one here.

And his mouth moved. Slowly. Deliberately. Shaping one word, one name, in the silence where his voice couldn't reach:

*Lena.*

* * *

She came back to herself in the hall of Turning Light with her knees half-bent and Ash's grip hard on her wrist, hard enough to bruise, his eyes wide and fixed on her face.

The king was smiling at her. The whole hall was watching the king smile at her, and warmth poured off the dais like heat off a forge, and the hum said *everything is fine, you are fine, you are welcome here, stay, stay, stay.*

"Brokers of the Silver Guild," the king said, and his voice was honey and river-stones and nothing underneath. "The crown is grateful. On the morrow, when the thirteenth moon completes her turn, the kingdom's past will be gathered in and kept, forever, safe, and those who kept it will be honored." The smile widened by a degree. "You will stay. You will all stay. No one leaves the palace before the turn."

Applause, soft as snowfall. The courtiers beamed. Somewhere beyond the hall, a bell began to ring the feast-hour, and Lena counted the strikes with the part of her mind that was still a thief: *one, two, three—moonrise tomorrow, less than a day, less than a day—*

The king's gaze held hers one moment longer. The smile never moved. But behind it, behind the glass, faint as a candle in a cellar, something screamed her name again.

*Lena. You take things. Take me.*

She bowed, as brokers bow, low and smooth, and took Ash's hand, and walked out of the hall of Turning Light on legs that belonged to someone else.

Only in the corridor, in the shadow between two sconces, did Ash stop her. He turned her to face him, and his hands were shaking, and he pressed one palm flat against his chest and then against hers, the way he did, and then—slowly, watching her eyes—he raised one finger and pointed back toward the hall. Toward the throne. And then he pointed at himself.

*The boy in the window,* Lena thought. *The boy in the garden. The son the king smiled at with someone else's mouth.*

"Ash," she whispered. "Who are you?"

He opened his mouth. For the first time since the shattered windows of Bellharrow, he tried to speak—and what came out was not the old tongue, not the dead language of the storm, but one cracked, rusted, almost-forgotten word of common, a word he must have carried alone through ten dark years:

"Remember," Ash said. "He said—*remember me*."

And down the corridor, soft as snowfall, the Hollowed began to walk toward them.
