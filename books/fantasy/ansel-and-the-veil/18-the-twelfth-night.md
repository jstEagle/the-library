## 18. The Twelfth Night

The shards sang when she laid them together.

Not with sound—Lena had learned by now that the crown did not bother with anything so honest as sound—but with a pressure behind the ears, a hum like the moment before lightning finds the ground. Three pieces of gold on the archivist's table in the drowned dark beneath the flooded library: the jagged shard Ash had carried out of the Veil, the crescent torn from Lady Vessane's ring, and the long splinter Veyra's own blade had once guarded, pried from the hilt of a ceremonial sword that had never been meant for ceremony at all.

They fit. Of course they fit. They had been one thing once, before men made them three, before grief and war and bargains broke what should never have been broken.

"Stop touching it," Sef said from across the room.

Sef Callowin, court archivist, defector, a man who had spent thirty years cataloguing lies and then burned his own name off the rolls to escape them. He stood hip-deep in shadow among the shelves that rose out of the black water like drowned trees, holding a lamp he didn't need because he'd read by worse light than this. His hands were steady. His voice was not.

"I'm not touching it," Lena said. She was touching it. Her fingertips hovered a hair's breadth above the gold, close enough to feel the warmth coming off it, close enough to feel it *notice* her.

"You're hovering."

"I'm thinking."

"That's worse." He set the lamp down on a shelf above the waterline and came toward her, boots slapping through ankle-deep flood. "Every archivist who ever wrote about the binding used the same word for the person who performs it, do you know that? Not *hero*. Not *sacrifice*. *Thief.*" He stopped at the edge of the table, where the lamplight ended and the shards' own glow began. "I thought it was poetry. Archivists love poetry; it's how we smuggle truth past censors. But it isn't. It's a job description."

Lena looked at him.

"The binding doesn't break," Sef said quietly. "It transfers. That's what the old tongue means when it says *unbinding*—not release. *Re-holding.* Someone has to take everything the Quiet has been wearing. Every year of the king's life it swallowed. Every memory it fed on. All of it, into one mind, held whole, so the thing itself has nothing left to grip." He turned his palm up toward the shards. "And there is exactly one kind of person alive who can hold that much without breaking."

The water dripped somewhere far off in the stacks. Drip, and drip, and drip—the library keeping its own slow time, counting down to nothing.

"A thief," Lena said.

"A thief," Sef agreed. "Someone whose whole life is taking what isn't theirs and carrying it without letting it change their shape. Except—" and here his voice did something terrible and gentle, "—it will change your shape. That's the price. You take all of it, you give all of yourself. The texts are very clear. The vessel empties as it fills. Name first. Then face. Then everything else, down to the last stolen coin of memory, until there is nothing left but the holding."

Above them, muffled through stone and water and forty feet of palace foundation, the Moonfeast went on. Bells. Singing. Ten thousand people gently, terribly happy, celebrating the thirteenth turn of the false moon, the night the binding would complete and the king would smile forever and the kingdom would be at peace until the sun burned out.

Lena pulled her hand back from the shards.

* * *

She found Ash on the archive's high gallery, sitting cross-legged on a dry shelf with his knees drawn up, watching the water below. He always watched water. She'd never asked why. Some part of her suspected she already knew—that a boy erased from every memory in the world would find something honest in a thing that only ever moved forward and never pretended otherwise.

He looked up when her boots creaked on the boards. In the lamplight his eyes were the color of the shard's gold, and they were older than any child's eyes had a right to be, and they were frightened.

"You heard," she said.

Ash nodded. Then he lifted his hand—not to speak, not yet; speaking cost him something she could see, some deep well inside him that drained a little more each time—and drew in the air, the way he drew everything, quick and certain, a language older than letters.

*A way around,* the gesture said. *There is always a way around.*

"No," Lena said. "There isn't. Not this time."

He drew again, sharper. *You. Me. We run. North. Past the Veil. Past everything. We hide.*

"And the king stays screaming behind glass forever. And Maro's sacrifice buys us nothing. And everyone in that palace up there keeps smiling until the world ends politely." She sat down beside him on the shelf, close enough that their shoulders touched. He leaned into her the way he always did, small and warm and trusting, and it was the trust that hurt worst, because she knew now what she was going to do with it.

"When I found you in the storm," she said, "you were holding a piece of your father's crown and you couldn't tell me your own name. Do you know why I took you in? Not kindness. I want to be honest with you, Ash, because after tomorrow honesty is the only currency I'll have left. I took you in because you were a door I hadn't picked yet. A mystery with coin hidden somewhere behind it. That's who I was. That's who I've been my whole life—a woman who takes things and tells herself the taking doesn't count if she doesn't ask where they came from."

Ash shook his head slowly. *No,* the gesture said. *That's not true.*

"It is true."

*You fed me first,* he signed. *Before you asked anything. Before you even knew what I was carrying. Your hands decided before your head did.* His mouth quirked, almost a smile. *Thieves don't feed people first.*

Lena laughed, and it cracked in the middle, and she let it. "Maybe I was a bad thief."

He signed something then that she had to make him repeat twice, because the first time she refused to understand it. *You're not empty. Whatever you give tomorrow—you won't be empty. I'll carry the rest.*

"No," she said. "No, Ash. That's not how it works. When it's done, you won't remember me either. That's the mercy of it. You'll walk out of that tower with your father whole and free, and you won't know why your chest hurts when you pass a stranger in the street, and you'll live your life, and it will be a good life, and I will be no one. A gap. A draft through an open door."

His hand closed around her wrist. Small fingers, fierce grip. And then he spoke—one word, in the old tongue, the language that shattered windows and unmade wards and had been dead longer than kingdoms. It was soft this time. It was just for her.

She didn't know the word. But she felt it land somewhere under her ribs and take root, and she understood it anyway, the way you understand a bell is beautiful without knowing its note.

*Stay,* it meant. Or possibly *remember.* In that language, maybe those had always been the same word.

"I can't," she whispered.

He let go of her wrist. He folded his hands in his lap and looked at the black water below, and he nodded once, the way soldiers nod, the way people nod who have lost arguments before and learned that losing is sometimes the same as loving.

* * *

Maro's knife was still in her pocket.

She'd taken it from the Hollowed who'd carried it—the one who had been Maro, or worn Maro's face, or been emptied so thoroughly that the difference no longer mattered. They'd escaped the ambush on the Bellharrow road because Maro had bought their escape with himself, whispering *some debts are worth paying twice* as the Hollowed closed around him, and Lena had fought her way to his body afterward and found it breathing but vacant, smiling the wide palace smile, and she had taken the knife from his belt because leaving it there would have meant admitting he wasn't coming back for it.

She sat alone in the map room now, the room Sef called the Ledger, where the true history of the binding lay open in books worth more than the palace above them. The knife lay on the table beside the third shard. Maro's knife. Maro, who had sold them once to save his sister and died—no, worse than died—to save them twice.

*Some debts are worth paying twice.*

She'd thought he meant his sister. She'd thought he meant the betrayal, the debt he owed them, paid first in treachery and then in blood. Sitting in the lamplight with the shards humming their lightning hum, Lena finally understood he'd meant her. He'd known. Maro, who grew up three doors down from her in the Veil, who knew better than anyone alive what she was and what she could carry—he'd looked at the shape of the problem, the binding that needed a thief, and he'd seen the answer before she had, and he'd paid his debt in advance so she wouldn't have anyone left to refuse.

"You absolute bastard," she said softly, to the knife, to the man, to the dark. "You knew."

The door opened. Sef came in with the lamp and a face like a man delivering a verdict he'd argued against for years.

"The rite is in the old tongue," he said. "Twelve lines. I've transcribed them phonetically; Ash can teach you the sounds. It has to be performed at the turning of the thirteenth moon, at the crown's height, with all the shards assembled and the king—" he hesitated, "—the king present. Willing or not. The Quiet will fight. It has worn him for thirteen turns; it will not step out of its coat politely."

"I know."

"There's a second requirement." Sef set the lamp down. He wouldn't look at her. "The texts call it the *counter-gift*. The thief must bring something of equal weight to what she takes. A life's worth of memory, given freely, to balance the scale. Otherwise the transfer tears both minds apart and the Quiet simply moves into the wreckage." He finally met her eyes. "You've been stealing since you were nine years old, Lena. I've read your file. The Veil kept records too, whatever else it kept. You have twenty years of other people's lives stored in that head. Griefs. First loves. Dead children's voices. It balances. Barely. If you give all of it—all of it, every stolen memory, emptied out to pay the king's ransom—"

"Then I'm the counter-gift," Lena said. "All of it. Even mine."

"Even yours," Sef said. "Name last. The texts are specific. The name goes last because the name is the keystone. Take the name and the rest has nowhere to hang." He rubbed his face with both hands, and for a moment he was not an archivist but just a tired man in a wet basement who had liked someone. "There may be another way. There's always another way in the stories. I haven't found it. I've been looking since the Drowned Archive opened to us, and I haven't found it, and I'm sorry."

"Don't apologize." Lena stood. She picked up Maro's knife and slid it through her belt, where it belonged, where it would stay until the end of things. "Apologizing is for people who had choices. We're just deciding which price to pay."

* * *

The last hours of the twelfth night, she spent gathering.

Not the shards—they were gathered, wrapped in wool in a satchel at her feet, singing their low storm-song. She gathered other things. She sat in the corner of the Ledger with a scrap of charcoal and a sheet of archivist's vellum, and she wrote, because writing was the closest a thief ever came to giving back.

*To whoever finds this:* she wrote. *Her name was Lena Zhou. She was born in the Veil, in the warden's quarter, though she'd tell you she built herself from scratch and mostly believed it. She stole memories for coin for nineteen years and told herself it was a trade like any other. It wasn't. She knew it wasn't. She was very good at it anyway, and being good at a bad thing was the only talent anyone ever gave her, so she kept it.*

*She loved a boy named Maro the way you love a wall you lean on—not beautifully, but constantly. He is not dead. Check the palace cells. Check everywhere. Don't stop checking.*

*She found a child in a storm and fed him before she asked him anything, and that was the best decision of her life, and she made it by accident, which she would want noted, because she hated being sentimental.*

*If the king wakes—if any of this works—tell him the memories he gets back were carried by a thief, and that she handled them carefully, and that she's sorry for the ones she sold. Especially the widow's grief. Especially that one.*

She stopped. The charcoal hovered.

Then, underneath, smaller:

*Ash. If some part of you reads this, someday, in a language you don't remember learning—don't come looking. Be happy. That's the whole message. Be so happy it makes up for the rest of us.*

She folded the page and sealed it with wax from Sef's lamp and gave it to him without a word, and he took it without a word, and that was the whole funeral.

* * *

Ash found her at the water's edge of the archive, where the flooded stacks met the stone stair that led up toward the palace and the feast and the end of everything. The false moon was rising. Through some flaw in the ancient foundations, its light reached even here, thin and silver on the black water, and the drowned books cast shadows like drowned forests.

He pressed something into her hand.

A pebble. River-smoothed, gray, ordinary. From the stream outside the Veil, she realized—from home, from the boundary stones, from the last ordinary thing either of them had touched before the world turned over.

"So I don't forget it," she said. "Even after."

Ash nodded. Then he took her other hand, the empty one, and held it, and together they watched the false moon climb toward the place where the thirteenth turn would begin.

Above them, faint through stone and water, the bells of the Moonfeast began to toll midnight.

Lena Zhou, who had spent her whole life taking things, stood in the dark with everything she was about to lose gathered close—three shards, one knife, one pebble, one child's warm hand—and found, to her surprise, that she was not afraid.

She was ready.

The twelfth night ended. The thirteenth moon began to rise.
