## 19. Unbinding

The tower stairs did not end so much as surrender. Lena climbed them with Ash's hand in hers, past landings where courtiers stood smiling at the walls, past a window where the thirteenth moon hung swollen and wrong above the capital—too close, too gold, like a coin someone had bitten and bent. The shards in the satchel against her ribs hummed with every step, five pieces of a broken crown singing to each other in a key she felt in her teeth.

*One turn left*, she thought. *Tonight is the twelfth night. At moonrise tomorrow, he is gone forever.*

Unless tonight, someone stole him first.

The door at the top was gold, because of course it was, and it stood open, because of course it did. The Quiet had never needed to lock anything. It simply made people want to stay.

Lena stopped on the threshold. Ash tugged her hand.

She looked down at him. In the light spilling from the king's chamber his face was very pale and very steady, and when he opened his mouth, the old tongue came out of it like water from a cracked jar—too much, too fast, words she still mostly couldn't understand but had learned, in these weeks, to feel. This one she felt in her sternum.

*I know*, she said, in the gesture-language they'd built between them, hands speaking what voices couldn't. *I know what it costs.*

He shook his head. His fingers moved: *Not that. Not you.*

"Me especially," Lena said aloud, and her voice didn't shake, which surprised her. "That's the whole trick of it, Ash. Nobody else can carry this. Believe me, I've asked."

Because she had. She'd spent the day asking—in the drowned archive, in the archivist's trembling notes, in the shard-memory of a king who had bound himself willingly two decades ago. The binding required a thief. The throne required a hollow, and the Quiet would pour itself into any hollow offered, but only one person in all the world could make a hollow exactly the shape of a king: a memory-thief, taking everything, leaving nothing for the thing that wore him to hold onto. Not his wars. Not his son's name. Not even, in the end, her own.

The archivist had written it plainly, in ink that had run with the flood: *The price is total. No thief has ever come back from a whole theft. There is nothing left to come back with.*

Ash signed again, slower, the way he did when he was frightened: *Who will remember you?*

"You will," Lena said. "That's not nothing. That's everything, actually. That's the entire point."

Then she kissed the top of his head—he smelled of rain and old gold, he had always smelled of that, since the storm—and pushed open the golden door.

* * *

The king sat by a window the size of a barn door, dressed in white, watching the thirteenth moon climb.

He looked smaller than the coins. Lena had seen his face stamped on currency her whole life, stern and bearded and enormous, and here he was—a man in his fifties with grey coming into his temples, sitting very still, the way people sit when something else is doing the sitting for them.

When he turned, he smiled, and the smile was beautiful, and it was the worst thing Lena had ever seen. She had seen it before through her gift, behind glass, underneath: a man screaming while something over his features arranged delight like flowers on a coffin.

"Lena Zhou," the king said warmly. "Of the Veil. We've been expecting you since you were nine years old."

Her skin went cold. "I never told anyone my name on the road."

"You don't understand yet," the king said gently, rising, spreading his hands. "Nothing you stole ever left you. Memory doesn't move, girl. It *echoes*. Every grief you lifted passed through your hands, and my crown heard its own name in every one." He came closer, and the moonlight followed him like a dog. "You are not a courier of stolen goods. You are a bell, and every time you rang, we listened. You have been carrying a message to us for twenty years. You just finished writing it tonight."

Behind his eyes, something smiled wider.

Lena made herself breathe. She thought of the widow's grief, the first theft, how it had tasted like copper and rain. She thought of Bellharrow, of her own theft repackaged and sold. She thought of Maro going down in the archive stairwell with six Hollowed on his back, whispering *some debts are worth paying twice*, and she let the anger settle into her hands where it belonged.

"That's funny," she said. "Because I've been carrying a message too. Want to hear it?"

She reached out and touched the king's temple, and opened herself all the way.

* * *

Stealing a memory was like lifting a coin from a pocket—you found the seam, you slid along it, you took the small bright thing and left the lining intact. Lena had done it ten thousand times. She knew the geography of a mind better than the geography of any kingdom.

The king's mind was a country on fire.

She went in through the newest memories first, because that was technique, because that was craft, because if she started anywhere else she would never start at all. She took the Moonfeast preparations, thirteen years of them. She took the taste of coronation wine. She took the weight of a scepter and the particular ache of a throne carved for a taller man.

The Quiet noticed immediately. It turned toward her through the burning country of the king's mind like weather turning, vast and patient and amused.

*Little bell*, it said, in a voice that was every gentle thing that had ever lied to her. *You cannot carry me. I am larger than your hands.*

"I know," Lena said, and kept stealing.

She took his twentieth year, the year his daughter learned to ride. She took his fifteenth, the war, all of it—the mud, the orders he gave, the men who died carrying them out, the nights he vomited from shame and rose at dawn to give more orders anyway. Every crime. She took it because the Quiet lived on the sweet things and starved on the bitter ones, and she meant to leave it nothing sweet.

She took his wedding. She took his wife's laugh, which sounded like a hinge that wanted oiling, which he had loved precisely because it was imperfect. She took the smell of the nursery.

And there—at the nursery, in the deepest room of the deepest year—the fire went cold, and Lena found the vault.

The Quiet screamed.

It was not a sound. It was an absence shaped like a sound, a hole torn across the whole burning country, and through the hole Lena saw what the entity had spent twenty years burying under peace and plenty and gently terrible happiness: a small boy's name, carved in a father's handwriting, walled up alive.

*Do not*, said the Quiet, and for the first time it was not amused. *Bell, thief, little gutter-girl from the valley that forgets—if you take that, there will be nothing holding me and I will fall and I will fall on EVERYTHING—*

"Good," Lena whispered, inside the mind, inside the fire. "Fall somewhere else."

She took the name.

* * *

Outside, in the tower room, the king convulsed once and slid from her hands to the floor, and the thirteenth moon flickered.

Lena felt it go—the whole theft pouring into her like a river reversing, years and names and crimes and kindnesses, the daughter's riding lesson and the war and the oil-less laugh and the nursery and the buried boy, all of it flooding into a hollow that opened in her as fast as it filled, because that was the final mechanic of the craft no one taught: to take everything, the thief must become large enough to hold everything, and the only way to become that large is to empty yourself first.

So she emptied. She fed herself into the theft as it came—her mother's face, which she had carried from before the Veil; her first theft; her first lie; Maro's laugh on the wall behind the wardens' barracks; the storm; the knock; the child; her own name, *Lena*, peeling off her like paint in rain—

—and the Quiet, starved of its host mid-meal, unraveled.

She felt it happen more than saw it. The entity was nothing but grip—it had held the king the way ivy holds a wall, by refusing to let go—and with nothing left to grip, twenty years of patience came apart in seconds. It shrieked without a mouth. It scrabbled at the inside of her skull with fingers made of borrowed comfort. And then, finding not one familiar thing—not the moon, not the crown, not a single sweet memory to stand on—it did what the drowned archive had promised all desperate things do.

It let go of the world.

The thirteenth moon went dark like a snuffed candle. Across the city below, Lena knew, thousands of people were blinking awake from a twenty-year dream of contentment, and some of them were starting to scream, and some of them were starting to weep, and all of them were, for the first time in thirteen turns, actually feeling whatever they felt.

The last of the Quiet drained out through the floor like smoke finding a draft.

Lena knelt beside the king in the wreckage of the moonlight, holding a whole man inside her chest, and discovered she could no longer remember why the satchel mattered or what a satchel was or who had put it against her ribs, only that it was warm, only that someone was crying, only that the crying was small and fierce and hers—or was it the child's? Was there a child? There was gold. There was a great deal of gold, broken into pieces, and a woman standing over her with a drawn blade.

* * *

Veyra's face was ruined.

Not marked—ruined in the older sense, the way a statue is ruined, the way something made to be one thing is caught becoming another. The Regent-Counsel of thirteen peaceful turns stood over Lena with her sword leveled at the last unbroken shard, which she had pried from the satchel with her free hand, and her eyes were running with tears she did not seem to notice.

"They promised me my family," she said. Her voice was perfectly level, which was somehow worse than screaming. "My husband. My daughters. The Quiet showed them to me. Thirteen years of service for thirteen years restored, when the binding completed. Do you understand what you have done?"

Lena opened her mouth to answer and found she did not know what answering was. Words were happening nearby. They seemed to belong to the tall weeping woman.

"You have murdered them a second time," Veyra said. "You have murdered everyone's dead a second time. There were no more deaths in my kingdom, thief. No widows. No funerals. I built a country where nobody ever had to lose anything, and you—" Her hand shook. The shard glittered. "You gave it back to them. All of it. Grief, and loss, and the war, and the dying. You gave them the whole world back, and the whole world is a wound."

Somewhere far away, a child was shouting. The words were in the old tongue and they hit the air like thrown stones, and the windows of the tower sang, and Veyra did not turn around. She was looking at the shard in her fist, and her tears were dropping off her jaw onto the floor, and slowly, terribly, understanding arrived in her face—the specific understanding of a woman doing arithmetic about a bargain struck with a thing that no longer existed.

"It's gone," she said quietly. "There's no one left to pay me."

She raised the blade.

Lena watched it come and felt nothing, because feeling required a self and she had spent hers, and the last thing that floated up through the emptiness was not fear and not regret but a single image: a door in a storm, and a small wet child standing behind it, holding out a piece of gold as if it were the most important thing in the world.

*Someone should remember that*, she thought, with what remained of her. *Someone should keep—*

The sword came down—but not at her, not at the king, not at anything living. Veyra drove the blade through the last shard of the king's crown with both hands and all her strength, and the shard screamed gold light and split, and split again, and crumbled, and the fragments of it turned to dust the color of dawn and blew away through the great window toward the dead moon.

Veyra sank down beside the ruins of it. She did not weep now. She laughed instead, low and awful, a sound with no contentment in it at all—the first honest laugh, perhaps, in thirteen years of the falsest peace the world had ever known.

"Well," she said, to no one, to the dust, to the empty sky. "Now we are all poor together."

* * *

Small hands took Lena's face.

She could not have said whose. A child—she believed it was a child; the shape was right, the hands were small and rain-smelling—knelt in front of her and pressed their forehead to hers, and began to speak in the old tongue, fast and fierce and endless, the way water speaks to a dry field.

And things began to come back.

Not stolen things. Given things. A name arrived first—*Lena*, landing soft as a bird—and then the storm, the door, the knock, the shard humming against her palms; then Maro laughing on a wall, Maro falling in a stairwell, *some debts are worth paying twice*; then the widow's grief, copper and rain, laid back into her heart so gently she understood for the first time that it had never been a burden, only a sorrow someone had trusted her with; then the nursery, the buried name, a small boy erased from every book and every mind, and the man who had traded his own memory to end a war and lost his son in the bargain without ever knowing he'd had one—

The king was awake. He was sitting up against the window frame, and he was weeping the way Lena imagined only kings and children can weep, entirely, from the root, and his mouth was moving around sounds that wouldn't come, because the last thing Lena's theft had taken and the first thing it had sealed away was language—every word he owned, held now in her hollow chest like birds in a closed hand.

He looked at the child. He looked at the child for a long time.

And though he could not speak—though she had taken every syllable he had—his face did something that no entity had permitted it to do in twenty years. It broke. It broke open with recognition, and his arms reached, and the boy went into them, and the three of them knelt together on the tower floor under a sky with twelve honest moons while somewhere below a whole kingdom woke up weeping and rejoicing and grieving, all at once, all truly.

Lena held them both. She could not have said, yet, which of them she was.

But in her chest, in the hollow that had held a king, something stirred that was hers alone—new-made, unnamed, wide open—

and the valley, far to the south, felt her open like a door, and softly, softly, began to let the magic in.
