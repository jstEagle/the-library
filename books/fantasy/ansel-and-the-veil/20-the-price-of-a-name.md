## 20. The Price of a Name

The thirteenth moon does not set.

It hangs above the broken tower like an eye that has finished weeping and cannot close, and beneath it the world is very quiet—the kind of quiet that comes after a scream has gone on so long it forgets how to stop.

I am holding Lena's hand.

I want to write that down first, before anything else, because everything else is going to be hard to say, even inside my own head where words come out in pictures instead of sounds. I am holding her hand, and her hand is cold, and her hand is *empty*.

I did not know a hand could feel empty until now.

* * *

Here is what happened, told the way I see things:

The tower was full of light like a room full of water. Lena stood at the center of it with the crown's shards floating around her in a ring—seven pieces of gold, each one singing a different note, and all of them singing toward my father.

My father was on his knees.

The thing that had worn him—the Quiet, the wide smile behind his eyes—was coming apart like smoke when a door opens. It had nothing left to hold onto. For thirteen turns of the false moon it had lived inside my father's memories, wearing them like clothes, feeding on them slowly, and Lena had taken every last piece. She stood there with her arms open and her eyes wide and she *drank the king whole*.

I saw it happen. I will always have seen it happen.

She took his childhood first—I could tell because the air went gold and small, the color of a kitchen, the smell of bread I have never smelled but somehow know. Then his mother's voice. Then his first war. Then the war he ended. Then the bargain he made alone at midnight with something older than kingdoms, trading his own mind for peace, and the price that came through the door behind him smiling.

Then his son.

Then me.

That was the moment I understood what she was doing, and I tried to run to her, and Maro—Maro, who was supposed to be dead, who limped into the tower an hour before dawn with blood on his teeth and a debt paid twice—held me back with both arms and said, "Let her finish. If you love her, let her finish."

So I let her finish.

She took his name. His face. The shape of his hands. The memory of rain on the palace roof. She took everything a man is made of, and she held it, all of it, in a body built for carrying stolen things, and then—

And then the Quiet unraveled.

It did not scream. That was the worst part. It simply became less and less until it was a rumor of itself, and then not even a rumor, and the thirteenth moon flickered like a candle in wind, and somewhere far below us the whole city of Bellharrow gasped awake from its gentle, terrible happiness.

And Lena fell.

* * *

She did not fall down. That would have been easier. She fell *inward*.

I reached her before her knees touched stone, and I looked into her face, and her face was—

Her face was no one's.

I do not mean she was disfigured. I mean that the face I knew—the sharp chin, the crooked brow, the scar over her left ear from a warden's chain—was *gone*. Not hidden. Gone, the way a word goes when you have said it too many times and suddenly it is only sounds. What was left was a face, technically. Eyes, mouth, skin. But it was like a house after everyone has moved out. You can see where the furniture was. You can see the paler squares on the walls where pictures hung.

Every memory she ever stole, she gave back. Every widow's grief, every soldier's courage, every child's first snow—all of it poured upward into the ring of gold and scattered to its owners across the kingdom, and the owners woke in their beds weeping with returned joy or returned sorrow, and none of them would ever know the name of the girl who carried their burdens for coin.

But that was not the price.

The price was this: to steal a king whole, she had to become a vessel, and a vessel must be empty first. So the magic took her payment in the oldest currency there is. It took her own memories. Her childhood in the Veil. Maro. The storm. The door. Her trade, her craft, her cleverness, her loneliness, her name.

Lena Zhou is standing in front of me, and Lena Zhou does not know that she is Lena Zhou.

"Hello," she says.

Her voice works. Voices are not memories; they are bodies. But the voice has no one behind it. It is a bell that has forgotten what it was rung for.

"Hello," she says again, politely, to a stranger—a boy, wet-haired, mud-streaked, gripping her hand too tightly. "Were you—were we—"

She looks past me at my father, kneeling in the ruin of the throne-tower with tears running down a face that is finally, only, his own.

"Oh," she says softly. "Was that mine? Was that—" She frowns, reaching for something that isn't there. "I think I did something important."

"You did everything," I say.

But of course she cannot understand me. To her ears my words are the old tongue, the dead language, the sound of wind through a drowned archive. Her face makes the small apologetic movement people make when they cannot follow a foreigner's speech.

I am used to not being understood. I have been un-understood since before I was born, erased from my father's mind before I drew breath, a prince with no name in any living memory but my own. I thought I knew everything about being empty.

I knew nothing. I was never empty. I was *full* of the one thing no one else could see.

Lena is truly empty. And she saved my father anyway. She saved him without knowing why, without knowing him, without knowing herself—because whatever Lena is, underneath the memories, underneath the name, is a person who walks toward the screaming and opens the door.

That part the magic could not take. That part was never a memory.

* * *

My father rises.

It takes him a long time. He comes up the way a tree comes up—not quickly, not all at once, but with a kind of terrible patience, as if the earth itself is helping him. He is an old man now. Thirteen years were eaten out of him while he screamed behind glass, and the years show. His hair is white. His hands shake.

But his eyes are his eyes. I would know them anywhere. I have spent my whole life knowing them from the outside, from paintings, from the one portrait in the drowned archive where a painter caught him looking slightly off to the side—as if listening for someone.

He was listening for me. He just didn't know it.

He crosses the ruined chamber to where Lena stands swaying, and he takes her other hand, and he bows his head over it, and great silent sobs move through him like weather.

He cannot speak either.

This is the strange arithmetic of the night: the Quiet ate his words last, or perhaps Lena's theft took them along with everything else, or perhaps a man who has screamed behind glass for thirteen years simply wears the voice out. Whatever the reason, the restored king of a freed kingdom opens his mouth and produces nothing but breath. He remembers everything—every year, every crime, every kindness, every face at every feast—but the words will not come. They stand behind his teeth like prisoners at a locked gate.

He points at himself. He points at the sky, at the moons—twelve setting now, the thirteenth finally, mercifully, beginning its long descent. He points at Lena. He mouths shapes. Nothing comes.

"It's all right," Maro says from the doorway, and his voice cracks in the middle of the sentence, which I have never heard happen, not once, not even when they led him away in chains. "It's all right, Your Majesty. We know who you are."

My father shakes his head. He taps his own throat, fierce, frustrated. He looks at Lena—at the stranger's face where his rescuer used to be—and something breaks in him all over again, because he knows, the way kings know things, exactly what she paid. He mouths the word *name* at her. He points at her, then presses his fist to his heart.

She doesn't understand. She smiles the polite smile of the lost.

And I make my decision.

* * *

Here is what I know about giving:

For thirteen turns I watched the kingdom trade itself away piece by piece. Grief sold cheap in Bellharrow. Courage harvested from soldiers. Widows walking around with holes where their husbands used to be, smiling anyway, because someone had sold the sorrow and left the shape. I learned that a memory removed is not destroyed—it is only *elsewhere*, waiting, homesick. My father proved it tonight by coming back whole. Everything stolen can be carried home.

I am the keeper of the old tongue. The language before kingdoms. The language the crown was sung in, the language the binding was spoken in, the language that names things so completely that the naming becomes a kind of holding.

And I am the only person in this tower who still remembers Lena.

Not the facts of her—I barely know those. I remember *her*. The door in the storm. The way she knelt down to my height before she asked a single question. The way she hid me under her floorboards from the wardens and lied to the elders with a steady voice and shaking hands. The way she looked at the shard's visions and chose the mystery over safety, every single time, even when safety was begging her. The way she said my name—Ash—like she was handing me something.

Those memories live in me. And memories, I have learned, can travel.

I take her cold hand in both of mine.

She looks down at me with the blank kindness of a stranger, and I look up at her, and I begin to speak.

* * *

The old tongue does not describe. It *gives*.

When I speak the word for *door*, there is a door. When I speak the word for *rain*, the rain falls somewhere, even here, even in a ruined tower under a dying moon—one thin silver thread of it, finding its way through the broken roof, landing on stone that has been dry for thirteen years.

I start small.

"*Warmth,*" I say, and press her palm flat against my chest, and she feels my heartbeat, and something in her face shifts—a flicker, like a lamp relit in a far window.

"*Storm,*" I say, and the rain thickens, drumming on the tower stones, and her eyes widen. Not recognition. Not yet. But the body remembers what the mind has lost, and her shoulders loosen the way they loosened once, in another life, when a knock came at her door and she opened it instead of barring it.

"*Boy,*" I say, and touch my own chest.

"*Yours,*" I say, and point at her.

Tears start down her face. She doesn't know why. That is the cruelest and most beautiful part—she cries without any reason she can name, the way my father wept without words. The body keeps its own accounts. Somewhere in her emptied self, a debt is stirring.

"*Door,*" I say. "*Night. Knocking. You opened it.*"

A picture forms between us—I can feel it forming, the old tongue building it out of sound and rain: a small room, a storm, a child soaked through, a shard of gold clutched in a fist. Her hand comes up slowly and touches her own cheek, as if checking whether she is the woman in the picture.

"*You were not afraid,*" I tell her. "*Or you were afraid, and you opened it anyway.*"

Her lips move. No words yet—words will take longer; words are heavy freight—but her grip on my hand tightens, and her fingers know how to tighten, they know *me*, some animal part of her has catalogued the exact pressure that means *I've got you, kid*.

"*Friend,*" I say. "*Named Maro. Loud. Loyal. Owes you everything.*"

Maro makes a sound behind us that is definitely not crying, because Maro does not cry, and if anyone asks, it was the rain.

"*Thief,*" I say next, gently, because I will not give her back a lie. "*You stole memories for coin. You were good at it. It hollowed you. And you stopped. You chose.*"

Her breath catches. Good. Let her have all of it—the skill and the shame, the competence and the cost. A person rebuilt from only her virtues would be a statue, not a woman. I am not making a statue. I am bringing someone home.

"*Lena,*" I say.

The name lands on her like a coat settling onto shoulders.

She says it back—hoarsely, questioningly—"Lena." And then again, firmer, tasting it, testing whether it fits. "Lena." It fits. Of course it fits. It was hers. I am only returning what was taken.

We work through the night.

I give her the Veil in pieces: the wardens' lanterns, the elders' long faces, the smell of the valley where magic cannot follow. I give her Bellharrow and the broker's smug face and the widow's grief repackaged for sale, and I watch shame cross her returned face and turn, slowly, into purpose—the same transformation I watched the first time, and it comforts me to know that some things in a person are not memories at all but *habits of the soul*, and hers bend always toward mending what is broken.

I give her the road. The small kingdoms of the too-content. The noblewoman's shattering windows. The Hollowed, pale and perfect. Veyra's blade turning aside at the last instant, Veyra choosing the shard over the promise, Veyra weeping as the last fragment of her family's restoration burned in her hand—because Lena needs to know that even the enemy chose rightly in the end, or nearly.

I give her the tower. The light like water. The drinking of a king.

And all the while, my father kneels beside us, listening. He cannot understand the old tongue any more than she can—he was born three hundred years after it died—but he watches my mouth and her face, and he learns the shape of the story anyway, the way you learn a song in a language you don't speak. When I give her the memory of the storm, he closes his eyes. When I give her her own name, he bows his head.

Near dawn, Lena speaks.

Not the old tongue. Common speech, rough and rusty, like a gate opening after a long winter.

"Ash," she says. My name. The one she gave me. "Ash. You're—you gave me—" She stops. Starts again. Her voice steadies. "How much?"

"All of it," I say. In her language now. I find I can do that, lately. The thirteenth moon broke something open in me too, or maybe loving people in two languages teaches you both. "Everything you stole. Everything you were."

She looks at her hands. Turns them over. Studies them like maps of a country she's hearing about for the first time.

"And my name?" she asks. "Is that all of it? Is there—is there something older? Underneath?"

I go still.

Because yes. There is. Deep under Lena, deeper than the valley, deeper than the trade, there is a buried stratum I felt when I first touched her mind with the old tongue—a name below the name, sealed so long ago that even she never knew it was there. The magic took it with the rest. It sits in me now, in the place where I keep what I am given, glowing faintly, patient.

Ansel.

I could give it back right now. One word. One word in the language that makes things true.

But her face—her face is asking a different question than her mouth. Her mouth asks *what is underneath*. Her face asks *who decided I should be Lena?* And I remember what it is to have your identity chosen by someone else—to be named by another's need. I will not do that to her. Whatever is buried in her belongs to her, and she should dig for it herself, or choose to leave it sleeping.

"That," I say carefully, "is yours to ask for. When you're ready. Not before."

She studies me for a long moment. Rain drips through the broken roof. Below us, the city is waking without its gentle happiness, confused and grieving and free, and somewhere in the palace a king without a voice is about to learn how to rule with gestures and patience and a mute son to interpret his heart.

"Lena is enough," she says at last. "For now. Lena is—who I built. Whoever's underneath can wait their turn."

She squeezes my hand. Then she looks at my father, and at Maro, red-eyed and grinning in the doorway, and at the dying thirteenth moon, and she says the thing that turns my blood to ice and my heart to hope in the same instant:

"The valley," she says. "The Veil. It raised me. It kept magic out to keep us safe—and now magic is loose in the world, and the wardens, the elders—they'll seal it tighter than ever. Or worse." Her jaw sets, and I know that set jaw; I have crossed a kingdom behind it. "Someone has to go back. Someone has to tell them the truth."

She stands. Empty no longer—or empty differently, the way a cup is empty before the pouring.

"Get some sleep," Lena says to us, to all of us, in the voice of a woman remembering how to be a woman. "Tomorrow we go home."

And far below, in the west, where the valley lies, I feel it—faint as a heartbeat through a wall—the wards of the Veil trembling, softening, letting something in.

They felt her coming.
