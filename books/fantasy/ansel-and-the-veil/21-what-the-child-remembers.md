## 21. What the Child Remembers

Ash holds her hand in the tower room, and the hand is empty the way a cup is empty after a long thirst—still shaped, still whole, but hollowed through. He can feel the hollowness through her skin. It is like holding a door that opens onto nothing.

The king is weeping on the floor. Ash does not look at him yet. He cannot. Looking at his father is a wound he has carried since before he had words for wounds, and there is work to do first, and the work is her.

*Lena,* he thinks, though the name is not hers. He knows that now. He has known it since the tower, since the thirteenth moon rose fat and white over the capital and she reached into a god's mouth and pulled a man out of it. The name *Lena* is a coat she sewed for herself out of stolen cloth. Underneath it is a person with no name at all, because she gave it away with all the rest, and the giving-away was the spell, and the spell worked, and the Quiet starved in the space where a king used to be.

She saved everyone. That is the shape of what happened. Ash holds it in his mind like a stone held against the belly in winter.

And the cost of the shape is this: she does not know it. She does not know anything. She sits on the cold stones of the tower room with her hand in a child's hand, and behind her eyes there is a country with no borders, no roads, no houses, no people. He looked into it once, just after the unbinding, just to see. It was the loneliest thing he had ever seen, lonelier than the years he spent unwritten, lonelier than his father's eyes sliding over him like water over glass.

So he begins.

The old tongue does not have a word for *give back*. It has a word that means *un-swallow*, and a word that means *carry home*, and a word that means the sound a bell makes after it has stopped ringing, which is the closest his people ever came to talking about memory. He uses all three. He takes her hand in both of his, the way she took his in the storm, and he presses his forehead to her knuckles, and he un-swallows.

* * *

The first thing he gives her is small, because you do not hand a starving person a feast.

A smell: bread, dark and dense, baked on a stone hearth in a valley where magic cannot follow. A kitchen with a crooked window. Rain on the roof of a world that keeps things out. He feels the memory leave him the way a splinter leaves skin—a small relief, a small ache—and he watches it land in her.

Her fingers twitch.

He gives her the kitchen whole. The woman who ran it, broad and loud, flour to the elbows, calling her *girl* in a voice that meant *daughter* without saying it. The burn scar on the girl's forearm from the oven door, earned at seven years old, cried over for one hour and worn proudly ever after, because in the Veil even scars were proof you had a life worth scarring.

Lena's breathing changes. It goes from the flat evenness of the emptied to something ragged, something reaching.

More. He gives her the alley behind the baker's where she practiced her first theft, lifting an apple from a barrel with fingers that shook so badly she dropped it twice and ate the bruised thing crouched in the dark, laughing at herself, nine years old and already knowing that hunger was a teacher and she was its best student. He gives her the first coin she ever stole for pay, and the strange cold feeling of it, and the stranger colder feeling of the widow's grief she took that same week, and how she told herself it was a kindness, the taking, and how she almost believed it, and how the almost was the crack down the middle of her whole life.

Her lips move. No sound. But they shape something, and he leans close, and it is a word in the valley tongue: *Maro.*

Yes. He gives her Maro.

Maro at twelve, all elbows and schemes, teaching her the warden's patrol routes by drawing them in spilled flour. Maro laughing so hard at her terrible forgery of a market pass that he fell into the horse trough. Maro older, quieter, carrying a grief of his own in a locked room behind his eyes—his sister, her name scraped out of him by brokers, sold to someone who wanted to know what it felt like to lose a sibling without the inconvenience of losing one. Maro on the road, Maro in Bellharrow, Maro's face when he led the Hollowed to their door, and the truth under the betrayal like a coin under a cup: *some debts are worth paying twice.* Maro in the square of the capital, buying them their escape with everything he had, the Hollowed closing over him like dark water, his last look not at the danger but at her, at Ash, at the two of them running, his mouth shaping a word Ash could not hear and did not need to.

Lena makes a sound then. It is not a word. It is the sound a person makes when a wound they did not know they had is finally named, and it goes on a long time, and Ash holds her hand through all of it and gives her nothing more until it stops, because grief must be allowed to finish or it will start over.

* * *

He gives her the road. All of it, in pieces, like a mosaic assembled from broken tile.

The boundary of the Veil and the cold-water touch of magic on her skin for the first time. The villages of small kingdoms where everyone smiled too widely. Bellharrow and the memory market, the stall where her own theft sat repackaged in a velvet case, a widow's grief priced at three silver, and the broker's soft voice explaining that the supply ran from the palace, everything ran from the palace, the whole kingdom drank from the same cupped hands. The noblewoman's ring. The window shattering when Ash spoke his first word aloud in the old tongue—the word for *mine*—and the look on Lena's face, terror and wonder braided so tightly he could not tell which strand was which.

He gives her the Hollowed, their beautiful empty faces. He gives her Veyra, and the flooded archive, and the archivist with her ink-stained fingers saying *the king bound himself, do you understand, no one took anything from him, he gave it, and the thing that came in the door he opened—*

He gives her the palace of smiles. The Moonfeast. The king in the mirror, a man screaming behind glass, and the moment the man's mouth shaped her name—a name he should not have known, a name that passed through her hands like all the others, because every stolen memory in thirteen years had flowed through the Veil and the Veil's best thief, and some of them had stuck to her like burrs, and one of them had been the king's memory of a word, and the word was hers.

And then he gives her the tower. The thirteenth moon. The choice.

He gives it to her slowly, because it is the heaviest thing, heavier than the king, heavier than the Quiet, and he is afraid—though the old tongue has no word for his fear, only a word that means *the ground before a leap*—he is afraid of what she will do when she understands what she paid.

*You must steal him whole,* the shards told her, singing through Ash's own mouth. *Every year. Every name. Every crime and every kindness. And what you take, you carry. And what you carry, you become. And the thief who carries a whole man has no room left to be anyone.*

She did it anyway. He gives her the doing of it. The reaching. The taking. The king pouring into her like a river into a cup that should have shattered and did not, because she had been hollowing herself out for thirteen years without knowing she was making room.

And then he gives her the last part, the part he has been carrying in the deepest pocket of himself, guarded like a coal in cupped hands:

The moment after. The Quiet unraveling like smoke remembering it was never anything. Veyra turning her blade on the last shard rather than take the family the Quiet offered her, choosing truth over comfort at the very end, and Ash does not understand Veyra yet, but he understands that choice, because he is watching someone live inside it. And the king on the floor, waking, weeping, whole.

And Lena standing over him, empty.

* * *

She opens her eyes.

They are brown. He had not known that. He has known her since the storm, and he never knew the color of her eyes, because knowing things was not something the Veil allowed and he was too busy being afraid. They are brown, and they are looking at him, and there is a person behind them now, a whole crowded city of a person, and she is trying to speak.

"Maro," she says first. It comes out cracked. "Maro is—"

"Alive." The word is not his; it comes from the doorway, where the king stands, leaning on the frame, gray-faced and upright and present in a way he has not been present in thirteen years. "Pardoned this morning. He is downstairs eating everything in the kitchens and threatening the cooks with flattery. Your Maro," the king says, and there is wonder in his voice, the wonder of a man tasting his own voice after years of someone else wearing it, "is apparently impossible to kill. He informed me of this himself. At length."

Lena makes a sound that is half laugh and half sob, and it is the best sound Ash has ever heard, and he gives her nothing then, because some things must be lived and not remembered.

But there is more. There is so much more, and his hands are still full.

* * *

He gives her the Veil next.

The valley whole: the crooked streets, the wardens with their lanterns, the elders in their gray robes, the rule that every child learns before they learn their letters—*magic cannot enter, nothing leaves*—and under the rule, the truth she always half-sensed and never said aloud: the Veil was not a shelter. It was a lockbox. The Quiet's first theft, its oldest one, a whole valley of people sealed away from the world so that their memories could be farmed in peace, generation after generation, thieves raised in the dark who never asked where the dark came from.

She takes that one standing up. He feels her go rigid, feels the fury move through her like weather, and then—this is the thing he will keep, this is the thing he does not know yet that he will keep—he feels the fury turn, gently, toward herself, and he feels her choose, in the space of one breath, to put it down.

"Not my fault," she says quietly, testing the words like ice on a pond. "I didn't build the valley. I didn't—" She stops. Looks at her own hands. "I stole from people. That part was mine. That part I did."

"Yes," Ash says, in her own tongue, clumsy around the edges. It is only his fourth day of speaking it and the words still feel like borrowed shoes. "And you carried them home. Every one. I watched you carry a king."

She looks at him for a long moment. Then she sits back down on the cold stones, because apparently a person can only stand up to so much truth in one morning, and she says, "What else do you have in there?"

And he laughs, and the laugh surprises him, because he did not know he could, and then he gives her the rest.

* * *

It takes hours. The sun crosses the tower window and starts down the other side. He gives her the widow whose grief she stole, and then—this is the delicate work, the work that makes his hands shake—he gives her the widow's grief itself, which has been his to carry since the tower, since everything she took became his to hold while she held the king. The grief goes into her like water into a dry bed, and she weeps for a woman whose name she finally knows, and Ash understands that this is what restoration is: not the absence of loss but the presence of everything, loss included, loss especially.

He gives her the child she was. He gives her the thief she became. He gives her the woman on the road, the woman in the archive, the woman at the top of the tower.

And then there is one thing left, and he stops.

She feels him stop. Of course she does. She is a memory-thief; she can feel the shape of what someone holds the way a blind woman feels the shape of a room. "Ash," she says. "What's the last one?"

He looks at his hands.

It is the storm. It is the night the world ended and began, the night he came to her door with the shard in his fist and the old tongue in his throat and nothing in the world but the memory of a father who had forgotten him. He remembers it from the outside, too, because he was there for both halves: the child drowning in the rain, and the door opening, and the woman with the lamp looking down at him with no reason in the world to help him and no hesitation in her face at all.

*Come in out of the wet,* she said. *Whatever you are, come in.*

He could give it back. It is hers, by every right. She found him; the memory is hers the way the scar from the oven is hers. And if he gives it back she will be whole, entirely whole, nothing missing, nothing held back, and he will be—empty again. The way he was before her. A boy unwritten, carrying other people's lives in his pockets because no one remembered his.

The old tongue has a word for what he wants to do. It means *to keep one coal from the fire so the fire can be lit again.* It is not a selfish word. It is the word his mother used for the stories she saved to tell at night, the word for the seed kept back from the harvest, the word for the one memory you hold so that remembering itself does not die.

He keeps it.

"I'm keeping one," he says, in her tongue, and his voice does not shake, because he learned steadiness from her.

Lena frowns. "Which one?"

"The storm." He looks up at her. "The night you opened the door."

"Ash—"

"Everyone in this kingdom was unmade by someone else's choice." He says it carefully; he has been turning the sentence over for days, and it is the truest thing he owns. "You. Me. My father. Veyra. Maro's sister. Everyone. Everyone was erased by somebody." He presses his hand flat against his own chest, where the shard used to sit warm as a heartbeat. "So I am choosing one thing to remember. One. You, opening the door. You, choosing me before you knew what I was. Someone has to remember who you chose to be, Lena. And I choose it to be me."

She is crying. He did not know she could; the woman he met in the storm never cried, or if she did, she did it in rooms with the doors shut. She pulls him against her and holds on the way people hold things they have already lost once, and over her shoulder he sees the king watching them, and the king's face is doing something complicated, something that is grief and gratitude and a father's terrible arithmetic all at once.

"Then keep it," Lena says into his hair. "Keep it forever. And Ash—when you tell it, tell it right. I wasn't kind. I was curious, and you were wet, and I figured whatever you were, you'd be worth coin."

He laughs again into her shoulder. "I will tell it right," he says. "You were curious. And you opened the door."

"That's the same thing," she says.

"In the old tongue," he says, "it is the same word."

* * *

The king abolishes the memory trade nine days later, from the steps of the restored palace, before a crowd that has come to stare at the man who was a mask for thirteen years and is now, impossibly, a man. Ash stands beside him on the steps. Lena stands on his other side, and she has refused a title twice and been overruled once, and the argument is ongoing.

"Nothing that lives in a person may be bought," the king says, and his voice carries to the back of the square without magic, without the crown, which lies in a velvet case at his feet, whole again and never to be worn. "Nothing that lives in a person may be sold. Every memory held in every vault and market from here to Bellharrow will be returned to the head that first held it, and if the head is gone, then the memory will be read aloud in the public square, once, and then let go." He pauses, and Ash feels him gather the hardest sentence like a man gathering a heavy stone. "Including mine. All of it. The crimes the Quiet committed in my name will be read aloud, and I will stand and hear every one."

The crowd is silent. Then a woman near the front—the widow from the Veil's ledgers, Ash realizes, the first grief Lena ever stole, returned to her only last week—begins to weep, not with grief this time but with the strange relief of a debt forgiven that no one thought would ever be forgiven, and the sound spreads through the square like fire through dry grass.

Maro, in the front row, in a borrowed coat two sizes too small because he arrived with nothing and refuses everything offered, leans over to Lena and says, too loudly, "He's going to be insufferable now. Kings who've suffered always are."

Lena elbows him. Ash watches them, and files it, and keeps it—not in the old tongue's word for keeping, but in the newer one, the one he is inventing himself, the one that means *this happened and I was there and it was good.*

* * *

That night, on the palace balcony, Lena asks him the question he has been waiting for.

"The Veil," she says. The moons are up—twelve of them, and the thirteenth now only a moon like the others, its long corruption ended, its light just light. "The elders. The wardens. The people who raised me inside a lockbox and called it a blessing." She turns a cup of wine in her hands without drinking it. "The trade is dead. The supply is dead. But the valley is still there, and it's still sealed, and everyone in it still thinks the dark is a shelter." She looks at him. "Can it be opened?"

And Ash, who has spent his whole unwritten life being the thing that other people's choices left behind, smiles and gives her the only answer he has ever been certain of.

"You opened a door for me," he says. "Doors remember."

Below them, in the dark, the road runs west, toward the mountains, toward the valley that has begun—so softly, so quietly that only a child of the old tongue could feel it—to let something in.

The next morning, they pack for the journey home.
