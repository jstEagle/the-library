## 1. The Thief of Small Hours

There are three rules to stealing a memory, and I break two of them regularly.

Rule one: never take from someone who's watching. Rule two: never take more than you can carry — grief is heavier than it looks, and joy can be worse; joy has edges. Rule three, the one that keeps me honest and alive in roughly equal measure: *never steal anything that matters.*

I was breaking rule one at the moment the widow sat down across from me, because she was watching, and she had asked me to.

"Madame Corvane," I said, "you understand what you're buying."

The memory-market of Hollowmere opens at dusk, when the lamps along the Tallow Row come up amber and the traders unfold their little velvet trays like men selling teeth. Which, in a way, they are. Teeth, hair, baby shoes — all the small physical debris of a life — except here the goods are lighter than any of that and cost ten times more. A first kiss, bottled. A mother's voice, decanted. The smell of bread from a bakery that burned down twenty years ago, sold by the pinch.

I don't keep a stall. Stalls invite scrutiny, and scrutiny invites questions about where a woman with no family name and no history learned to handle memories without gloves. I work the benches at the edge of the square, where the lamplight is bad and the company is worse, and I let people come to me.

Madame Corvane came to me three nights ago with her dead husband's coat still hanging on her shoulders like an accusation.

"I want the night he died," she said now, folding her hands on the table between us. Her knuckles were the size of walnuts. A working woman's hands, once. "All of it. The fever, the doctor shaking his head, the — the sound he made at the end. I want it gone."

"You'll lose other things with it," I told her, because I always say this, because it's true, and because saying it makes me feel like less of a thief. "Memories tangle. Pull one thread and the whole night comes away — the good parts too. His laugh, if he laughed that night. Whatever he said to you, if he said anything."

"He said my name." Her voice didn't waver. That was the frightening thing about her. "He said *Liese*, and then he made the sound, and I have heard nothing else for eleven months. Every time I close my eyes. Every time." She leaned forward. Lamplight caught the wet in her eyes but nothing fell. Eleven months of crying will empty a person out. "Take it. Take all of it. I'll buy his laugh back later, from someone kinder than you."

"There's no one kinder than me," I said. "That's the problem with this trade."

She almost smiled. It was the closest either of us would come to warmth that evening, so we took it and moved on.

Here is how the taking works, since you're wondering, and everyone wonders: Hollowmere sits in the one valley in the world where magic cannot cross the border. The wards hold it out — have held it out for as long as anyone remembers, longer than names, longer than the village at the valley's mouth that lost its own name to the wards' hunger generations back. Nothing magical gets in. But magic doesn't need to get in to exist, not entirely. There are traces. Residue. The way a room holds heat after the fire dies, the valley holds just enough of the old art for certain people — born with the knack, or cursed with it, depending on your theology — to do small, precise, deniable things.

I can reach into a person's remembering the way you'd reach into a pocket. Gently, if I like them. Less gently if they deserve it. And what I draw out comes loose — a grey-silver thread only I can see, cool against the fingers, tasting faintly of whatever it holds. Madame Corvane's worst night tasted of iron and candle smoke and, underneath, absurdly, of oranges. He must have been peeling one when the fever turned. The mind files everything. The mind is a hoarder with no taste whatsoever.

I took it slow. Ten minutes, no more, while she sat perfectly still and watched me with those emptied-out eyes, and around us the market went on haggling — a man selling his childhood summer by the week, a woman pricing out somebody else's wedding because her own had been annulled and she wanted the memory of having had one. Nobody looked twice at us. In Hollowmere, grief changing hands is just another Tuesday.

When it was done, I coiled the thread into a vial of smoked glass, sealed it with wax, and pressed it into her palm — the empty half of the transaction, the receipt, the proof of what was no longer hers.

"It's done," I said. "You won't miss it. That's the cruelty of it. You'll just find the eleventh month of last year has a hole in it shaped like a man."

"And the coin?"

"Half now. Half when I've sold it, because I'm sentimental, and sentimentality is the only luxury I afford myself."

She counted out the silver without looking at it. Then she did something I hadn't expected: she reached across the table and gripped my wrist, hard, walnut knuckles digging in.

"You've done this before," she said. "Taken things. For yourself, I mean. I can see it — there's a look people get, when they've cut something out of themselves and the wound's healed crooked." Her eyes were terrible and clear. "What did you take, girl?"

*Nothing,* I nearly said. *I've never stolen from myself. My past is intact and boring and entirely accounted for.*

But here's the truth, which I tell no one, which I barely tell myself at four in the morning when the lamps are out and the Tallow Row is quiet: I don't know. I have memories enough — a childhood in fragments, an apprenticeship with a hedge-witch whose name I've misplaced, nine years in this valley learning a trade that shouldn't exist inside its borders. But the fragments don't fit together. There are gaps with smooth edges, like stones worn by water, and when I go feeling along the seam of my own history the way I'd feel along a mark's pocket, my fingers slide off. Something in me has been *handled*. Something in me has been taken, or buried, or traded away by a version of me I cannot find.

So I said, "A bad habit or two," and smiled my market smile, and pried her hand off my wrist with as much gentleness as I could fake.

She left. I sat in the bad lamplight with a vial of a dead man's dying warm against my palm, and I felt — as I always feel after a job — that particular vertigo, that sense of standing on a floor that might be a trapdoor.

The collector found me within the hour, because collectors always do. Word travels fast on the Tallow Row, faster than it should, and Master Ellery Voss has ears in every wine-shop and a purse deep enough to make the wine-shops sing.

He was wearing green tonight. Voss always wears green — the particular green of old money trying to look young — and he sat down across from me uninvited, arranged his rings, and said, "Xanthe Marchetti. They say you've lifted something exquisite."

"They say a lot of things about me. Most of them are flattery and the rest are lies."

"And this?"

"This is a widow's worst night," I said, setting the vial on the table between us. "Eleven months old. Iron, candle smoke, oranges. High quality — she kept it fresh by replaying it nightly, which I don't recommend as a preservation technique but does wonders for the color. One owner. No previous damage."

Voss picked up the vial and held it to the lamp, and I watched his face do the thing collector's faces do — that softening, that hunger dressed up as connoisseurship. He was quiet a long moment.

"The sound he made," he said at last. "At the end."

"That's the centerpiece, yes."

"People pay extraordinary sums for endings, Marchetti. Beginnings, no one wants — beginnings are common, everyone has them. But an ending. A real one." He set the vial down with the care of a man handling a relic. "Two hundred crowns."

"Four hundred."

"Two hundred and fifty, and I'll throw in the information that the council wardens have started asking after you."

My stomach performed a small, professional maneuver. "Asking after me why?"

"Asking after you *how*, is the better question. Quietly. Not official channels — a man asking questions in wine-shops, wearing a warden's boots under a merchant's cloak." Voss smiled with all the sincerity of a bank note. "Someone in this city thinks you're interesting, Xanthe Marchetti. Interesting is dangerous. Three hundred, and I forget to mention it again."

Wardens. The word landed wrong, the way a step lands wrong in a dark stairwell. Wardens patrol the border, the rim of the valley where the wards stand oldest and thickest. Wardens have no business in Hollowmere's markets, no business knowing my name — and yet this stranger in borrowed boots was asking after me by name, which meant someone had given it to him, which meant somewhere in the last nine years I had stepped on a thread I didn't know was attached to anything.

"Three hundred," I said, "and you also forget my face."

"I never saw your face. I never see it. That's why I deal with you." He counted the coins out onto the table, stacked them with two fingers, and slid the vial into his coat. Then he paused, and for one unguarded moment the connoisseur fell away and something almost human looked out. "You know what I can't figure out about you? You're good. Better than good. You could take *anything* — rich men's fortunes, kings' secrets, if any king were fool enough to come here. Instead you take widows' nightmares and sell them to me. Small hours. Small coin." He shook his head. "It's like watching a master locksmith open garden sheds."

"Garden sheds have locks too," I said. "And their owners sleep better afterward."

He laughed, paid, and melted into the crowd, green coat swallowed by the amber dark. Three hundred crowns. Enough to eat for a year, enough to keep my rooms above the chandler's, enough to keep being nobody in particular in the one place in the world where nobody in particular is safe.

I gathered the coins and told myself the familiar story: *You have a rule. Never steal anything that matters. You follow the rule. Therefore you are a good person, or near enough, or at minimum a profitable one.* The story usually worked. Tonight it sat in my chest like a stone, because Madame Corvane's question was still lodged in me like a splinter:

*What did you take?*

Because there was something else. I hadn't mentioned it to Voss, wouldn't have sold it to him at any price, wouldn't have admitted it existed under torture. When I drew the widow's nightmare out of her — when my fingers closed on that cold iron thread — something in the deep vault of me, the locked room at the bottom of my own head, had *shifted*. Turned over. Like a sleeper disturbed. Like something enormous rolling toward the edge of a dream.

And for half a heartbeat, reaching into a grieving woman's worst night, I had smelled — not iron, not candle smoke, not oranges —

Ash. Old ash, and beneath it something burning still, far off, patient, going out.

I sat alone at the market's edge while the traders packed their velvet trays, and I pressed my palm flat against my sternum, over the locked room, and felt nothing but my own stubborn heart.

"Small hours," I said aloud, to no one. "Small coin. Small life."

The lamps guttered. Somewhere above the valley's rim, beyond the wards that had held for longer than names, the first star of the evening should have been coming up over the mountains.

I could have sworn the sky stayed dark a moment too long.

But then, I thought, standing and counting my coins like the careful, dishonest, rule-abiding woman I was — I'd always had a bad feeling about the stars.

They'd never once told me the truth about myself.
