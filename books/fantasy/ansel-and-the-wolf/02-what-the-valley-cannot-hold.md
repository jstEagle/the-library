## 2. What the Valley Cannot Hold

I have three rules in the memory trade, and I've broken two of them before breakfast on more than one occasion. Rule one: never steal from anyone who can afford to have you killed. Rule two: never steal the same client twice — grief, like debt, compounds. But rule three is the load-bearing wall of my whole crooked little life: *never take anything that matters.*

Mattering is a matter of scale, of course. The widow Vessa's recipe for plum brandy mattered to her, and when she sold it to me last spring to pay a gambling debt, it mattered to me too, briefly, until I traded it to a baker for six months of bread and the recipe turned out to be useless because Vessa had never once written down an amount. That's the thing about memories. They don't come with instructions. You get the whole of a moment or nothing at all, and most people's moments are mostly nothing.

The woman who came to my stall that morning was not most people.

She arrived early, before the market had properly woken, when Hollowmere's memory-quarter still smelled like yesterday's lamp oil and the tanners' runoff from the canal. She stood at the edge of my awning with her hands folded like a woman at a funeral, which, in a sense, she was attending her own.

"You're the thief," she said. Not a question. Word travels in this city the way water travels through a cracked jug — slowly, messily, and always finding the lowest places.

"I'm a purveyor of secondhand recollections," I said. "Thief implies I lack charm."

"My son is dead."

I put down the memory-vial I'd been polishing. There are certain sentences that end conversations before they begin, and that's one of them. "I'm sorry," I said, and meant it, which was already inconvenient.

"He died in the flood. Two years ago. The gorge took the north road out and he was on it." Her hands hadn't moved. Folded, knuckles white, like she was holding something shut. "Everyone says time will soften it. Time has done nothing. Every morning I wake up and for half a breath he's alive, and then I remember, and it's like drowning him all over again. Twice a day. Sometimes more."

"Madam—"

"Tolla," she said. "My name is Tolla Ferren."

"Tolla Ferren. What you're asking for isn't a plum brandy recipe. It's the worst night of your life, and possibly every night since. I don't do wholesale amputation. It doesn't heal clean."

"I'll pay."

"Everything costs more than money." I leaned back in my chair and studied her, because studying clients is cheaper than therapy and nearly as effective. She was maybe fifty, gray coming in at the temples like frost claiming a windowpane. Hands of a woman who worked — rope calluses, a fisherman's widow or a net-mender's, something with water in it, which was its own kind of cruelty given how her boy had gone. "You'd be walking around with a hole where your son used to be. You'd see his face on strangers and not know why your chest hurts. Grief without a name is worse than grief, Tolla. Believe me. I'm something of an expert in holes."

Something moved behind her eyes then — not anger, exactly. Recognition. As if I'd said a password I didn't know I knew.

"You have holes," she said quietly. "Don't you."

"That's slander."

"It's true. I've seen you at the market. You look at people like you're checking them against a list you lost." She unfolded her hands at last and set them flat on my counter, and I noticed they were trembling. "I'm not asking you to make me happy. I'm asking you to make me functional. There's a difference, and I suspect you know it better than anyone in this valley."

Here is what I should have said: *no.* Here is what I always say: *no.* Rule three exists precisely for women like Tolla Ferren, because rule three was written by a younger me who understood that some things, once lifted, cannot be put back, and that the thief always ends up carrying what the owner couldn't.

Instead I heard myself say: "It would hurt. The taking. Like pulling a tooth with the root attached."

"Good," she said.

And that was that. I've often thought since that I could have saved us both a great deal of trouble by being a better liar, but there you have it — even thieves have their moments of catastrophic honesty.

* * *

Taking a memory is delicate work when you want the donor to survive intact. I use a focus — usually a small object tied to the memory itself, something the mind can pour through like sand through a funnel. For Tolla, I asked if she had anything of her son's.

She brought out a wooden boat. Hand-carved, crude, the kind of thing a father makes for a boy of five, except the knife-work was fine, careful, a grown man's patience in it. "He made this himself. Last winter. He was twenty-two and he carved his mother a toy boat." Her voice did something complicated on the word *mother*. "He said he'd take me down the gorge in a real one someday. He never got the chance."

The gorge. The flood. The north road.

I held the little boat in my palm and felt it hum, faintly, the way objects hum when they've been loved hard enough to soak it up. Memory clings to things. That's the dirty secret of my trade — I don't really steal from people. I steal from the world they've touched, and the world keeps excellent books.

"Sit down," I said. "This will feel like cold water going the wrong way through you. Don't fight it. Fighting makes it ragged."

She sat. I set the boat between us, laid two fingers on it, and opened myself the way I open for every extraction — a door in the chest, a held breath, a small deliberate act of self-erasure that I've gotten so good at I barely notice it anymore.

Tolla's memory came through the funnel of that little wooden boat, and it was wrong from the first taste.

Memories have temperatures. Most run warm — love, fear, shame, all the human weather runs blood-warm, and a good thief learns to read heat like a vintner reads a cork. This ran *cold*. Not the cool of sadness. Cold like a well in winter. Cold like something that had been kept somewhere dark and deep and was not entirely pleased to be disturbed.

And underneath the cold, something else. A flavor I had no name for. I've tasted ten thousand memories in nine years in Hollowmere — grief, joy, the specific embarrassment of falling down stairs at your own wedding — and none of them tasted like this. This tasted like *ash and starlight*, if you'll forgive the poetry, and I don't offer forgiveness cheaply.

The memory itself was ordinary, horribly so. Rain. The gorge road. A young man with his mother's stubborn chin waving from a cart, turning back to shout something cheerful that the rain ate. Then the sound — the crack of the hillside letting go, the brown wall of water — and then nothing, because that was the end of him, and the end of the memory, and Tolla had lived two years in the nothing after.

I drew it out slow, the way you're supposed to, and packed it into a lead-lined vial, and the vial went cold in my hand so fast it burned. Frost feathered across the glass. In nine years, no memory had ever frosted its own container.

Tolla sagged in the chair, breathing hard, tears running freely but her face — gods, her face. It was the face of a woman setting down a weight she'd forgotten wasn't part of her body. "Is it gone?" she whispered.

"It's gone."

"Do I—" She pressed a hand to her sternum. "There's a shape. Where it was. Is that normal?"

"The shape fades." Usually. "Give it a week. You'll remember that you had a son the way you remember a story someone told you. It'll hurt less each year. That's the mercy of it."

"And the rest? The mornings? Waking up and—"

"Waking up whole," I said. "For the first breath, anyway."

She paid me triple what I asked, which I hated, and hugged me, which I hated more, and left with the lightness of the amputated, and I sat alone in my stall holding a vial of frost and feeling like I'd just done something with consequences I couldn't see yet.

Rule three, I reminded myself. Never steal anything that matters.

A dead son matters. I had just stolen the only thing Tolla Ferren had left of him, and whatever cold thing lived inside that memory now lived in my keeping, humming its ash-and-starlight song against the glass.

I told myself it was grief. Grief runs strange in some people; I'd seen memories go murky, go bitter, go quiet as tombs. Cold was just another flavor. I was a professional. Professionals don't spook at frost.

I told myself all of it, and none of it took, and I carried the vial home in my pocket like a coal that refused to die.

* * *

That evening I did what I always do after a bad extraction: I drank. Odile's archive stays open late — the blind archivist keeps hours that would embarrass an owl — and her back room pours a plum brandy that tastes suspiciously like the actual memory I once stole from Vessa, though Odile swears she developed the recipe independently, which is archivist for *yes, from you*.

"You look terrible," Odile said, setting the bottle down without measuring. She measures for customers. For me she just watches my face and pours accordingly, and tonight she poured like a woman bailing a boat.

"Thank you. I cultivated it."

"You took something today that you shouldn't have."

I stopped with the cup halfway to my mouth. Odile is blind in the way that stones are blind, which is to say she sees everything worth seeing and misses all the furniture. "Word travels fast."

"No word travels at all. Your face traveled. You walked in here wearing guilt like a borrowed coat, two sizes too big." She settled across from me, her white eyes fixed slightly past my left ear, which is where she keeps everyone. "What was it?"

"A grief-taking. A woman wanted her dead son out of her head. Standard work. Except—" I turned the cup. "The memory ran cold. Frosted the vial. And it tasted like—" I hunted for it. "Ash. Starlight. Something old."

Odile went very still. Odile is many things — cryptic, infuriating, the closest thing I have to a friend, which says something damning about either of us — but she is never still. Stillness on Odile is like fire on water. It means something has fallen into the pond.

"Show me the vial," she said.

I almost did. My hand was actually moving toward my pocket when some animal instinct in me — the same instinct that's kept a thief alive in a city full of collectors for nine years — closed around the impulse and squeezed. "It's in my vault," I lied smoothly. "Sealed. Why? What do you know about cold memories?"

"Nothing," said Odile, in the tone of a woman who knows everything about cold memories. "Drink your brandy. Go home. Sleep."

"Odile."

"Sleep, Xanthe." And then, softer, in a voice I'd never heard her use, a voice with cracks in it: "Whatever you took today — keep it close. Don't sell it. Don't trade it. And if anyone comes asking — anyone at all, no matter what banner they carry or what face they wear — you come find me first."

"Anyone asking about what?"

"About anything." She refilled my cup with a hand that was not quite steady. "The valley is older than its rules, girl. Older than the wards. Older than the forgetting. Some nights I think the whole of Hollowmere is a lid on a pot, and everyone's forgotten there's a stove underneath."

"That's comforting. Thank you. I'll sleep wonderfully."

"You're welcome," she said serenely, and poured herself a cup, and we drank in silence while outside the market quarter settled into its nightly hush.

* * *

I climbed to my roof after, because roofs are where I think. My garret sits high enough in the Tanner's Row tenement that I can see over the wardline walls to the valley rim, and on clear nights — which is every night, because the covenant keeps Hollowmere's skies scrubbed and tame — I count stars. It's a habit. I don't remember starting it. That's the thing about my own past: it's full of habits with no origins, skills with no teachers, reflexes with no first time. I know how to pick a lock the way I know how to breathe, and if you asked me who taught me, I'd have to shrug. Somewhere in the missing rooms of my own house, someone taught me everything I am, and I live in the furnished parts and try not to rattle the locked doors.

Nine years in the valley. Nine years, and I've never once tried to leave. Everyone talks about the world beyond the wards like a story — magic out there, real magic, dragons and wonders, the whole impossible catalog — and I've always felt about it the way I feel about other people's memories: curious, distant, glad to be shut of it.

I counted stars that night the way I always do. There's a cluster over the northern rim I think of as mine, seven bright ones in a crooked line, and I found six.

I blinked. Counted again. Six.

Now, stars don't go out. Stars are the one thing in this valley that never changes — the covenant holds the sky steady the way it holds everything else, and in nine years I have never seen so much as a shooting star, let alone a vacancy. But there it was: a hole in the crooked line, black and exact, like a tooth knocked from a familiar jaw.

While I watched, a second star guttered. Not vanished — *guttered*, the way a candle gutters, flaring weak, dimming, flaring, and then it too went dark, and the dark where it had been seemed darker than mere absence. Darker than it had any right to be.

I stood up on the roof. My heart was doing something unprofessional.

Across the valley, along the southern rim, a third star failed. Then a fourth, further east. They were going out in sequence, I realized — in a line, a slow procession of darkness walking the rim of Hollowmere like someone snuffing lamps along a corridor. And beneath my feet, beneath the whole drowsy city, I felt — or imagined I felt, or told myself I imagined — a deep, subterranean shudder, the way a house settles, the way a great beast shifts its weight in its sleep.

In my pocket, the vial I'd sworn was in my vault — because apparently I lie to myself as fluently as I lie to clients — went cold enough to ache through the cloth.

I took it out. Frost crawled the glass in patterns that weren't frost-patterns, that were almost *writing*, almost language, and inside the leaded dark the memory of Tolla's drowned son pulsed with a light that had no business existing — ember-red, banked, breathing. Breathing slow. Breathing like something enormous asleep.

"Oh," I said, to the starless patch of sky, to the frozen vial, to the nine years of safe small life cracking neatly down the middle like river ice in spring. "Oh, that's not grief."

The seventh star of my crooked constellation held on longest. I watched it burn, and burn, and thin, and I swear on every rule I've ever broken that just before it failed, it flickered in a rhythm — long, long-short, short — that felt less like astronomy and more like a signal. Like something far away trying very hard to say a word.

Then it went out, and the valley had no stars at all, and somewhere beyond the northern rim, in the dark where the wards had begun to fail, something began to walk.

I stood on my roof with a dying god in my pocket and did the only sensible thing available to a thief at midnight.

I started planning how to run.
