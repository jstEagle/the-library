## 3. The Warden at the Gate

The wards fell on a Tuesday, which felt insulting. Catastrophes ought to happen on significant days—solstices, coronations, the anniversary of some great betrayal—so that afterward people could point to the calendar and say *there, that's when the world ended*. Instead mine ended on an overcast Tuesday in late autumn, while I was eating a meat pie of genuinely criminal quality at a waystation called the Drowned Ox, and I have resented that pie ever since.

I was three bites in when the oath-cord went cold against my sternum.

Wardens wear their oaths as braided cord, wolf-grey wool wound through with silver thread, and it sits against your heart like a second pulse. Mine had been warm for nine years—the low, steady warmth of a border holding, of magic kept out, of a job well done by a thousand wardens who would never be thanked because nobody remembered there was anything to thank them for. Then, between bite two and bite three, it went cold as a riverbed in January, and every hair on my arms stood up, and somewhere far to the north something old and vast exhaled its last breath.

I knew what it meant before I let myself know what it meant. That's a warden's trick: you learn to hold knowledge at arm's length, examine it sideways, postpone the moment it becomes real. I held it at arm's length for exactly as long as it took me to pay for the pie—which, given its quality, was too long—and walk out into the yard, and look north, where the sky above the valley's rim had begun, very quietly, to go wrong.

* * *

The Hollowmere border runs two hundred miles from the Salt Teeth in the west to the Greyfen in the east, and I had walked every foot of it in my time, most of it twice. The wards weren't walls. Strangers always expected walls. They were more like a held note—a chord sustained so long that everyone born inside it mistook the silence of magic for silence itself. The dragon-fire under the Ember Altar sang the note; the standing stones along the border carried it; and we wardens were the tuning forks, walking our circuits, listening for flat spots.

There are no flat spots anymore. There is no note at all.

I reached the nearest stone by nightfall of the second day. It stood where it had stood for longer than the valley had a name, a grey finger taller than three men, and I had touched it ten thousand times and felt nothing but cool granite, because that was the point—you only felt the ward when it failed.

I put my palm against it and felt *everything*.

Magic came off that stone like heat off a forge. Not the valley's magic—it didn't have any—but the outer realm's, pressing in from the other side like water finding a crack in a dam. The air around the stone tasted of iron and lightning and things I had names for and things I very much did not. My oath-cord, dead cold since Tuesday, gave one weak twitch against my chest, like a man trying to sit up after bad news.

"Well," I said, to the stone, because talking to objects is a habit you pick up on long patrols and never lose, "you've been a good wall."

And then I saw the tracks.

Wolf-sign, pressed deep into the soft ground beside the stone. Four prints, large, the toes splayed with the weight of a big animal moving fast. No wolf has crossed this border in a generation—the wards saw to that, and before the wards, the hunting parties saw to that, and before the hunting parties, honest fear saw to it. I crouched and put my hand in one print and my whole forearm fit inside it with room to spare.

Whatever made these was not a wolf. It was wearing a wolf the way a man wears a coat.

I am not ashamed to say I sat down in the mud for a moment. A warden learns to ration his fear—spend a little now, save the rest for when it's useful—but the account was overdrawn. Things from the outer dark, crossing a border that had held for centuries, leaving prints the size of dinner plates, heading south. Heading inland. Heading, if you drew a line from those prints through the hills and the flooded country and the pine dark, toward Hollowmere itself.

I followed them for six miles before I lost them at a stream, and it was losing them that saved my sanity, because it was there, kneeling in the shallows with my fingers in the silt, that I saw the other thing.

A thread of magic.

Not the brute pressure of the outer dark—that came off everything now, a low wrong hum underneath the world. This was different. This was *fine*, a single bright filament laid across the stream-bank like a strand of gold wire, running south, and when I touched it, it sang.

I don't mean that as poetry. Magic sings, if you're a warden and you've spent nine years listening to a border hum one endless note. Every worker of it has a voice. The archivists' spells sound like dry paper turning. The council's binding-wards sound like a courtroom clearing its throat. And this thread—

This thread sounded like laughter in a market crowd, quick and light and a little bit cruel, the laugh of someone who has just lifted your purse and your dignity in the same motion and will be gone before you notice either. It sounded like a woman's voice saying *don't take anything that matters* in a tone that suggested she'd already decided what mattered and it wasn't going to be yours.

I knew that voice.

Nine years is a long time. Nine years is enough to forget the color of a room you loved, the taste of bread from a particular oven, the exact words of a promise made in wolf-light on a border much like this one. But you do not forget a voice. You do not forget the specific grain of a person's magic, the way it sits in your ear like a key cut for a lock you didn't know you had. I had spent nine years telling myself I'd forgotten, and the forgetting had held up fine right up until the moment the actual thing arrived and showed me what a counterfeit my memory had been all along.

"Xanthe," I said, out loud, to a stream, in the middle of nowhere, like an idiot.

Because here is the arithmetic I had been refusing to do since Tuesday: the wards fall, and within hours, a thread of magic crosses the fallen border from the outside in—no, wait. I knelt there longer, making myself look at it properly, the way you make yourself read a letter you already know is bad news. The thread ran *south*, toward the city, yes. But it had come from the north. From beyond the border. From outside.

Which was impossible, because nothing living could cross the wards, and nothing inside the valley had magic to cross *with*.

Unless it wasn't alive. Unless it was memory. Unless something had been carried out across a border that shouldn't have let it pass, wrapped in a thief's clever hands, and was now coming home again, trailing its own bright thread behind it like a scarf.

I stood up. My knees objected. Somewhere in the dark to the east, something howled—not a wolf's howl, not anything's howl, a sound with too many joints in it—and I noted it, filed it, and did not run toward it, because I am a warden and not a hero, and wardens know which fires to fight first.

The thread ran south. So did I.

* * *

Three days to Hollowmere if you push, four if you're honest about your horse. I pushed. Her name is Bramble and she is the ugliest mare in the valley and I love her the way you love anything that has carried you away from disaster more than once. We came down out of the high pines on the fourth morning with the city spread below us in its bowl of hills, red roofs and chimney smoke and the memory-market's quarter already stirring, and I reined in and looked at it for a long time.

Hollowmere looked the same. That was the terrible part. The stars had gone out—I'd seen it each night, the sky above the valley emptying itself constellation by constellation, until last night there was nothing overhead but black, a ceiling instead of a heaven—and the city below went about its business as though the night were merely overcast. Carts in the gate-road. A fishwife arguing with a cooper. Smoke rising straight and domestic from a thousand chimneys. Nine years of held breath, and they hadn't noticed they were holding it.

They wouldn't. That was the covenant's whole design. The valley forgot magic the way a body forgets pain once the wound closes. Safe, small, sealed—and blind, all of them, to the fire guttering under everything.

I thought about riding to the warden-post and reporting. It's what the oath-cord said to do, even cold and dead as it was. Report the breach. Summon the council. Let the machinery grind. And the machinery would grind, and it would arrive eventually at the question of *who*, and the answer to *who* was a woman in the memory-market who had crossed my path exactly once, nine years ago, on a night I had promised myself I'd forgotten and had instead memorized down to the smallest detail, the way you memorize a room the last time you see it.

Report her, and the council would do what councils do. I had watched them do it to lesser crimes. She'd be in the wardhouse by nightfall and tried by week's end, and whatever she carried—whatever had punched a hole through a century-old covenant—would be confiscated, studied, argued over, and quite possibly destroyed, by men who understood none of it, while the last fire in the world went out unattended.

Or.

Or I could go into the city under a truce banner, find her myself, and ask her a question I had rehearsed for nine years and still did not know the opening line of.

Bramble shifted under me, blowing out a breath that steamed in the cold morning air. Below us, the gate-wardens were waving through a cart of turnips. One of them glanced up at the empty sky, frowned faintly, and went back to his ledger.

"You understand," I told her, "that this is a terrible idea."

She flicked an ear. Bramble's counsel has always been economical.

I turned her down the hill toward the South Gate, and as we rode, I became aware of the thread again—her thread, Xanthe's, the bright singing filament of it—no longer out in the wilds somewhere but woven into the city's noise ahead of us, unmistakable, pulling at me like a hook set behind the breastbone. Nine years I had kept that hook from catching. One touch of the true thing and here I was, a warden of the realm riding toward it at a trot, truce banner furled in my saddlebag, oath-cord cold as a stone on my chest.

The gate-warden at South Gate looked up at my grey cloak and stepped aside without a word. Wardens recognize each other the way dogs do. He didn't ask my business. If he had, I'm not sure what I'd have said. *Official warden business* was technically true. *Chasing a thread of magic that sounds like a woman laughing* was truer.

Inside the walls, the city closed around me, warm and oblivious, and the thread sang louder with every street. It led down, toward the river quarter, toward the market district—toward, I realized with a lurch that had nothing to do with Bramble's gait, the memory-market itself. Of course. Where else would a thief go home to?

I left Bramble at a stable whose owner charged me double and apologized for it in the same breath, and I walked the last stretch on foot, past stalls waking and shutters opening, following a sound only I could hear, until I stood at the mouth of Gildmarket Row with the morning crowd flowing around me like water around a stone.

And there—at the far end, at a stall hung with little brass instruments and bottles of nothing, where a woman with quick hands was leaning close to a client, murmuring, smiling a smile that didn't reach anywhere near her eyes—

There she was.

Older. Sharper at the edges. A stranger doing a stranger's work, in a city that had forgotten what waited outside it.

But the thread rose off her like heat-shimmer, and it sang, and every rehearsed word I had carried for nine years evaporated at once, leaving me standing in a crowd with nothing but the truth:

The wards were down, the stars were gone, the last fire in the world was dying—and the woman who might have killed it, or might be the only one alive who could save it, was haggling over the price of a stranger's worst memory twenty feet away, and she did not know my face.

I started toward her anyway.
