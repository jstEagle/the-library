## 4. A Debt in Wolf's Clothing

The truce banner was a lie stitched in white wool, and I carried it anyway.

That's the thing about oaths: they don't break all at once. They fray. You start by bending a rule for mercy's sake—*the wolf-warden may not enter Hollowmere unarmed*, so I went armed with nothing but a flag—and before you know it you're standing at the gate of the one city your order swore never to breach, wearing your grandmother's funeral shroud as a peace offering and telling yourself it's duty.

It wasn't duty. Duty had been satisfied three days ago when I reported the fallen wards to Warden-Captain Hale and watched him go pale around the mouth. Duty would have been waiting for the council to convene, for the hunters to muster, for the slow machinery of the realm to grind into motion. What I did instead was steal a horse, borrow a banner from a shrine that hadn't been visited since my grandfather's time, and ride north through the rain like a man with something to prove to himself.

I told myself it was because I knew the valley. Because I knew how wards fail and what comes through them first. All true. None of it the reason.

The reason had a name, and the name tasted like nine years of not saying it.

* * *

Hollowmere announced itself the way it always had—not with walls or towers but with a smell. Bread, tallow, wet stone, and under it all a faint mineral tang like a struck flint, which is what a city smells like when magic has been wrung out of it so thoroughly that even its ghosts have forgotten how to haunt. The covenant made this place. A whole valley scrubbed clean of wonder, safe as a locked pantry, and every soul inside it fattened on the safety without ever asking what paid for the lock.

The gate wardens—mortal men with pikes and boredom—stopped me at the archway. They looked at the banner, then at me, then at the banner again, doing arithmetic.

"Truce delegation," I said. "Warden business."

"Wardens don't come here," said the older one.

"The wards fell," I said. "Everything that used to be true is currently taking applications."

They let me through, which told me two things. First, that word of the darkened sky hadn't reached the gate yet, or hadn't been believed. Second, that Hollowmere's defenses were exactly as rotten as the covenant they leaned on. I filed both facts away and walked into the city I'd sworn an oath never to enter, feeling the absence of magic like a missing tooth my tongue kept finding.

Finding her took less time than I expected. That was the insult of it. Nine years, and I still knew where she'd be.

* * *

The memory-market opened at dusk, because grief keeps evening hours. Stalls of gray canvas lined the Tanner's Row, and behind each one sat a thief with soft hands and softer eyes, selling other people's yesterdays in little glass vials that caught the lamplight like trapped weather. I moved through the crowd with my hood up and my banner left rolled at a pawnshop—the pawnbroker had looked at the white wool, looked at me, and decided not to ask, which is the national pastime of Hollowmere.

And there she was.

Xanthe Marchetti, at the corner stall with the crooked awning, haggling with a collector over a vial of somebody's wedding day. She wore a coat the color of wet slate and a smile I recognized down to the exact degree of its insincerity. Her hands were the same—quick, expressive, always half a gesture ahead of whatever she was saying. Her hair was shorter. There was a new scar on her jaw, thin as a thread.

She laughed at something the collector said—a real laugh, surprised out of her—and the sound went through me like a hook through soft meat.

Nine years. I'd rehearsed this. In the watch-fires and the long patrols I'd built whole speeches, brick by brick, the way lonely men do. *You don't remember me, but I remember everything.* I'd imagined anger. I'd imagined her running. I'd imagined, in my weaker hours, her face when she understood.

What I had not imagined was walking up to her and having her look at me the way she looked at strangers—with polite appraisal and a merchant's quick calculation of what I might be worth.

"You're staring," she said. "Either buy something or move along. Grief waits for no man, but my patience does."

"Xanthe Marchetti."

Something flickered across her face—not recognition. Caution. "Do I owe you money?"

"No."

"Then you have me at a disadvantage, which I assume is deliberate, because men who open conversations with my full name are never selling anything." She set down the vial she'd been holding and gave me her full attention, and gods help me, even her suspicion was familiar. She used to look at me exactly like that when I came home late from patrol, deciding whether to be furious or amused. Usually she managed both.

"I need to ask you about a theft," I said.

"Bold opening. Most people warm up first."

"A large theft. From somewhere very far away."

Her eyes did the thing they always did when she lied—nothing at all. That was her genius. Everyone else's guilt lived in their faces; hers had been evicted years ago. "I sell forgettings, warden. I don't do burglaries. Whatever you've heard—"

"Three nights ago the border wards fell. The stars above this valley went out. Something stole a memory that was never meant to leave the dragon's keeping, and every road I can read leads here, to you." I kept my voice low. Around us the market hummed on, buyers and sellers trading away their worst nights, oblivious. "I'm not here for the council. Not yet. I'm here because if that memory is in this city, we have maybe days before things worse than me come looking for it."

For just a moment—one heartbeat, maybe two—something moved behind her eyes. Fear, old and well-practiced, the fear of someone who has been hunted before and learned to keep it folded small. Then it was gone, packed away, and she was only a shopkeeper again.

"Dragon," she repeated. "There hasn't been a dragon in living memory."

"There doesn't need to be. Only its memory."

"And you think I have it." She crossed her arms. "Because I'm a thief. Because I'm *the* thief. Because it's easier to blame the woman with the vials than to ask what else might be failing out there in the dark."

"Because it's you," I said, and heard too much truth land in the words, and watched her hear it too.

She stepped back. Just a half-step, barely a hand's width, but I felt it like a door closing. "I don't know you," she said quietly. "Whatever you think we are, whoever you think I am—you're wrong. I've never seen you before tonight."

And there it was. The sentence I'd been carrying toward for nine years, delivered flat and final, and it still knocked the wind out of me like a fall from height. I'd known. Of course I'd known—she'd sold her past piece by piece; Odile had told me enough before I left, though not nearly enough—but knowing a wound is coming and standing still for it are different animals entirely.

She didn't know me. The woman who used to fall asleep against my shoulder on the border stones, who knew the names of every star I couldn't name, who once told me she could pick my heartbeat out of a hundred heartbeats blindfolded—that woman was gone, and in her place stood a stranger with her hands and her laugh and none of her history.

"Say your name again," she said suddenly. Frowning. Not at me—at herself, at some itch behind her eyes she couldn't scratch. "Ansel. Why do I—" She stopped. Shook her head hard, like a swimmer surfacing. "Nothing. It's nothing. Common name."

"It's not," I said. "But it doesn't matter."

"Then why does it feel like it matters?" Sharp now, almost angry, and I could have kissed her for it, because anger was a language we'd both spoken fluently once, and for one absurd moment the gap between us seemed crossable. Then she caught herself, smoothed it over, became the merchant again. "I haven't stolen anything from any dragon. I don't traffic in things that matter—it's the first rule of my trade. So unless you have proof, warden, you're wasting both our evenings."

"I'll find the proof."

"People say that." She turned back to her stall, dismissing me with the efficiency of long practice. "They usually don't."

* * *

I should have left. Every instinct trained into me by twenty years of wardenship said: report, withdraw, return with force. Instead I bought a room at the Drowned Bell—an inn whose name I chose not to examine too closely—and spent the night walking streets I'd walked once before, in another life, with her hand in mine.

Here is what nobody tells you about loving someone who has forgotten you: the city does it too. Hollowmere remembered nothing. The bench outside the chandler's where she'd eaten plums and dripped juice on my cloak—just a bench. The alley behind the archive where she'd kissed me the night before she ran—just an alley, smelling of cabbage and rain. The world had quietly digested our history and passed it on as scenery. I was the only monument either of us had left, and I was starting to crack.

Near midnight I found myself at the market again, closed now, stalls shuttered, and I stood in the empty street beneath her crooked awning like a fool at a grave.

Because here was the arithmetic I couldn't escape. If she had the dragon-memory—if she'd taken it, however unknowingly—then she was carrying the seed of the covenant in her vault while the sky above us went dark. And if she didn't have it, then something else had taken it, something that had slipped through wards that had held for a generation, and it was already inside these walls.

Either way, the woman who didn't know my name was in danger. Either way, I wasn't leaving.

I was turning back toward the inn when I saw the light in her stall window. Late—even for her. Through the gap in the shutters I watched her kneel before a floorboard, pry it up, and lift out a strongbox I recognized with a sick lurch, because I'd helped her choose it, years ago, iron-banded, wolf-head hinges—

She opened it. Held something up to the candlelight. A vial, yes, but wrong—too large, and the light inside it didn't catch like glass catches. It *burned*. Even across the street, even through shutter-slats, I felt it: a warmth against my skin like a door opening onto summer, the first true heat I'd sensed since crossing the border. Dragon-fire, banked and dying, sleeping in a thief's strongbox in a city that had outlawed wonder.

And Xanthe stared at it with an expression I knew better than my own reflection. Not greed. Not triumph.

Recognition dawning, terrible and slow, on a face that had just realized it had been robbed by its own hand.

She looked up—straight at the window, straight at me, though she couldn't possibly have seen me in the dark. Thieves' instincts. The oldest magic she had left.

I stepped back into the shadow of the doorway, heart hammering, and made my decision the way all my worst decisions get made: instantly, and with my whole heart.

Tomorrow I'd tell her everything. About the wards. About the flame. About the night on the border and the promise and the nine years between.

Whether she believed a word of it from a stranger's mouth—that was tomorrow's problem.
