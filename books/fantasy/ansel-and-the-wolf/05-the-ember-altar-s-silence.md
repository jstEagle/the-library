## 5. The Ember Altar's Silence

The stars were still gone in the morning, which meant it hadn't been a trick of the light, or of wine, or of the particular quality of exhaustion that comes from selling a woman her own grief and then drinking until you can't remember whose face was crying.

I stood in my doorway with a cup of chicory going cold in my hand and counted the sky twice. Hollowmere's sky is never much to look at—the valley walls crowd out half of it, and the wards give everything above a faint greasy sheen, like the inside of a lamp—but there had always been stars past the sheen. Little stubborn ones. The kind you stop noticing until they're not there.

Now the dark above the valley was smooth as river stone. Not one star. Not even the cheap ones.

"Right," I said to the empty street, because saying things out loud to no one is a habit of women who live alone and like it that way, mostly. "That's fine. That's someone else's problem."

It was not someone else's problem. I knew it wasn't, the way you know a knock at the door at midnight isn't a neighbor come to borrow sugar. But knowing a thing and admitting it are two different trades, and I've always been better at the first.

There was exactly one person in Hollowmere who would know what an empty sky meant, and she lived at the top of the Archive steps, which was either a pilgrimage or a punishment depending on your knees.

* * *

Odile Marchetti—no relation, though she claims otherwise when it suits her—has been blind since before I was born, which means she has been listening to this valley for longer than most people have been alive. The Archive keeps her the way you keep a smoke detector: nobody remembers installing her, everyone panics when she goes off.

The stairs up to her reading room are worn into shallow bowls by centuries of feet. I climbed them slowly, telling myself I was catching my breath and not stalling. At the top, the door stood open on a room that smelled of dust, tallow, and old paper—the smell of every place where somebody once decided the truth was worth writing down.

"You're late," Odile said from her chair by the window she can't see out of. "You've been late since Tuesday."

"It's Thursday."

"I know." She turned her face toward me. Her eyes are pale as skimmed milk and fixed on nothing, but being looked at by Odile has never felt like being looked *past*. It feels like being opened. "Sit down, thief. Tell me about the cold thing in your vault."

My spine went to ice water. "I haven't told anyone about—"

"You didn't have to. You've been carrying it around under your skin for three days like a coal wrapped in wet cloth. You flinch when people touch your left side. You've stopped sleeping facing the wall." She folded her hands. "And last night the stars went out, and this morning you climbed seventy-two steps to see a blind woman. So. Tell me."

I sat. There didn't seem to be anywhere else to put myself.

"The client," I said. "The one who wanted her son forgotten. When I lifted the memory, it burned cold. Not like a memory at all. Memories are warm—they're body-heat, they're *someone's*. This was—" I hunted for it. "This was like holding a key that fits a lock you've never seen. Like it wanted to be somewhere."

"And you took it anyway."

"He was paying double. And she was drowning, Odile. You should have heard her. Some nights aren't worth keeping. I've built a whole life on that sentence."

"Mm." Odile's fingers found the arm of her chair and tapped once, twice—a rhythm I'd seen her use when consulting something, though the room held no books within reach. Blind archivists don't read the way we do. They read the way rivers read valleys: slowly, over a very long time, wearing the shape of the truth into themselves. "Describe the theft. Exactly. Don't dress it up for me; I've known you since you were stealing plums."

So I told her. The mother's house smelling of lye and burnt porridge. The son's name—I won't write it here, some things stay between the living and their dead. The way the memory had come loose too easily, like a tooth already rotten, and the way it had *changed* in my keeping. How I'd sealed it in the vault with the rest of my stock, and how the vault had been humming ever since, low, below hearing, the way a struck bell hums after you've clapped your hand over it.

"When did you take it?" Odile asked quietly.

"Three days ago. Why?"

"Because three days ago, the border wards fell."

The words landed in the room and just sat there. I made myself say the next part. "Fell how? Wards don't fall. Wards are older than the valley. Wards are—"

"Wards are a flame," Odile said, "and flames gutter."

She rose, unhurried, and crossed to the great cabinet against the far wall. She didn't need to see to find what she wanted; her hands knew the Archive the way my hands know a lock. From a drawer that I would have sworn contained tax ledgers she drew a scroll, sealed with wax so old it had gone the color of dried blood.

"Every archivist learns this in her first year, and then spends the rest of her life hoping she'll never need it," she said. "The Founding Record. Sit still, Xanthe. This is a story about your valley, and you're going to want to be sitting down for all of it."

"I'm sitting."

"For once in your life, do as you're told."

* * *

This is what Odile read to me, in a voice like dry leaves, while the starless morning crawled past the window:

When magic was young and greedy, it ate whatever it touched. It ate borders. It ate names—it still does; ask any village in the deep valley why it has none. And when the outer powers came hunting across the world, eating whole kingdoms the way fire eats a field, the last dragon of the northern range flew south with the powers behind it like wolves behind a wounded stag.

It could not outrun them. Nothing outruns them. So instead it chose the only trade left to it.

It came to this valley, where ordinary folk huddled in the shadow of the mountains, and it lay down in the high pass at the valley's head, and it let its great heart slow, and slow, and slow—and at the very last, it gave its dying fire away. All of it. Every spark of the oldest magic in the world, poured out along the valley's rim, drawn up into a covenant: *no magic crosses here.* Not the outer powers'. Not the wanderers'. Not the gifted children born inside who wept because the valley was silent to them. Nothing in, nothing out. A ring of dying dragon-fire, burning down so slowly that a lifetime is less than a breath of it.

"That's why Hollowmere is hollow," Odile said. "Not the mines. The word is older than the mines. We live inside a shell, girl. Inside the last kindness of the last dragon."

"And the wards falling?"

"A fire only dies twice. Once when it gutters, once when it's forgotten." She set the scroll down. "The flame is fed by belief, by boundary, by the covenant renewed generation to generation—or it was, until the archivists stopped teaching it and the wardens started patrolling a border nobody remembered was a *hearth*. Nine years ago something was taken from the flame. Something small. Something warm. A single ember, carried out of the pass by hands that didn't know what they held."

The room tilted. Or I did.

"Nine years ago," I said. My voice sounded like it belonged to someone standing very far away. "Nine years ago I was nineteen, and I—"

I stopped. Because here is the thing, the ugly little thing I have kept locked even from myself: I remember crossing into the valley. I remember the rain, the checkpoint, the warden's lantern. I remember deciding to build a life here, small and safe and mine. What I do not remember—not truly, not with weight—is *why*. Everyone has a reason. Mine is a door in my own house that opens onto blank wall. I told myself for nine years that some pasts aren't worth keeping.

"Some thieves steal purses," Odile said softly. "Some steal grief. And once in a century, a thief with a gift for finding the thing that matters most finds the thing that matters most in the *world*, and carries it through a dying ward like a lit coal through a dry field." She turned her blind eyes on me. "What did you take from the pass, Xanthe? Think. Reach back past the wall in your memory. What was in your hands?"

And I reached, the way I reach into other people's heads every day, gentle and greedy—and hit the blank wall, and hit it again, harder—

—and the wall *cracked*.

Just a hairline. Just enough. One image slid through, thin as wire: night, snow, the high pass, my own young hands cupped around something that glowed like a coal wrapped in gauze, and a voice behind me—young, furious, breaking—saying my name like a wound.

Then the wall closed, and I was on my knees on Odile's floor with my ears ringing, and I understood.

The client's son. The memory that burned cold. The vault that hummed.

They weren't separate thefts. They were the same theft, wearing different faces. Whatever I had taken from the dragon's dying mind nine years ago had been *moving*—passing from keeper to keeper, grief to grief, hiding itself inside ordinary sorrow the way a spark hides in ash. The widow's worst night. The mother's drowned boy. Every cold memory I'd ever handled and sold and forgotten, a chain of them leading back through nine years to the pass, to the dragon, to me.

The last recollection of the last dragon had been sleeping in my vault for three days. Maybe longer. Maybe it had been passing through my hands my whole career, patient as a seed, waiting for me to be stupid enough or desperate enough to gather the pieces.

"The seed," I whispered. "You said the flame needs feeding. The memory is—"

"The dragon's final recollection is the pattern of its own fire," Odile said. "The only complete copy left in creation. Burned at the right hearth, in the right hour, it would grow the flame anew. The Ember Altar in the old chapel at the valley's head—the dragon lay down upon it. That is the hearth." Her voice dropped. "Or the memory can sit in a thief's vault until the last of the fire dies, and then the outer powers walk into this valley like lords coming home, and everything we are ends quietly, without witnesses, because the sky will already be dark."

I got up off the floor because staying down felt too much like agreeing to die.

"My vault," I said. "I have to go. Now. If the council finds out what I'm carrying—"

"The council will vote to destroy it," Odile said flatly. "They'll call it prudence. Ordinary men always burn the seed and call it saving the harvest. Go, girl. And Xanthe—" I paused at the door. "There is a warden coming up those stairs behind you. He has been climbing since dawn. Wolf-blood, oath-cord, and a thread of your own magic caught on his coat like burrs. He knows what you carry. He has known since the wards fell."

"How could he possibly—"

"Because he was there," Odile said, "nine years ago, in the pass. And I think, child, that he has been looking for you ever since."

I heard them then—boots on the worn steps, steady, unhurried, climbing toward me through the silence of a valley whose sky had gone dark.

Seventy-two steps. I counted every one.
