## 6. Everything She Stole

There is a particular kind of dread that comes from opening a door you have opened a thousand times before and finding that the room behind it has changed while you weren't looking. Not rearranged. Not tidied. *Changed* — as if the contents had been having conversations with each other in the dark, making arrangements, fusing themselves together like two lonely people at a party who discover they're wearing matching socks.

I stood in front of my vault door for the better part of an hour before I could bring myself to turn the key.

The vault was mine in every way that mattered. I'd built it myself, back when I still trusted myself to know what mattered. A room beneath the tannery, walled in cold stone, lined with lead, sealed with salt and silence. Memory-keepers are supposed to keep memories safe for other people — hold them in trust, return them on request, charge interest in coin or kindness. But every thief keeps one drawer she never shows anyone, and mine held the things I couldn't sell, wouldn't sell, had sworn on my own name never to touch again.

I had told Odile the truth of it, more or less, two nights ago: that I kept a private collection, that some of it was old, that none of it was dangerous. She had sat in her chair with her eyes closed — she always kept them closed; I'd stopped wondering years ago whether there was anything behind the lids worth seeing — and she had listened to me lie with the patient attention of a woman who has heard every lie Hollowmere has to offer and catalogued most of them.

"You said," she reminded me, when I finally confessed where the dragon-memory had come from, "that your vault held nothing that mattered."

"I said it held nothing that mattered *to anyone else*." I had said it with a straight face. I had even believed it, in the way you can believe a thing right up until the moment it kills you.

Because here is what I understood now, standing in the dark under the tannery with a lantern lit and my hands shaking worse than they had shaken since the night the stars began going out: nothing in that vault belonged only to me. It never had. Every memory I had ever stolen came out of someone's head, and heads do not come with receipts. When you take a memory from a person, you take a piece of their architecture. You take the beam that held up the roof of who they were, and you hope — you *pray*, if you're the praying kind, which I am not, having seen what prayer does to people in this valley, which is nothing, slowly, for generations — you pray the roof holds without it.

And when you take a memory from a dragon, you take a beam from something that is holding up considerably more than a roof.

* * *

Let me tell you how the vault worked, because the mechanics matter, and because I have learned in these last few days that everything matters whether you want it to or not.

Memories, once lifted, don't sit inert in their jars like pickled eels. They breathe. They dream. A memory is not a fact; it's a living thing made of feeling and time, and feelings want things. They want to be felt. They want to go home. Put enough of them in one small dark room and they start reaching for each other across the shelves — grief finding grief, joy finding joy, the memory of a funeral leaning toward the memory of the wedding that preceded it, whispering through the glass like neighbors passing notes over a fence.

I had always found this charming, in a faintly unsettling way. My vault hummed at night. If you pressed your ear to the wall you could hear it — a sound like wind through wheat, like a hundred small voices murmuring in languages that no longer existed anywhere but in there. I used to say the vault dreamed. I thought it was a quirk of the trade. A curiosity. Something to mention at dinner parties, if Hollowmere had dinner parties, which it did not, because Hollowmere had markets and funerals and a council that met on Thursdays to discuss drainage.

What I had never done — not once in nine years — was go down there and simply *listen*.

You don't listen to your own inventory. That's rule number three, actually, though I never wrote it down: never listen too closely to what you keep, because listening is how keeping turns into remembering, and remembering is how a thief becomes just another person with a past, and a person with a past has to answer for it.

Tonight I listened.

* * *

The dragon-memory lived in the deepest alcove, in a jar of smoked glass that I had wrapped in three layers of black silk nine years ago and not unwrapped since. I remembered wrapping it. I remembered deciding it was too dangerous to look at, too strange, too heavy — a memory that came from something so far above the human scale that holding it felt like holding weather. I remembered telling myself I would study it properly someday, when I was braver, when the valley was calmer, when there wasn't a war on.

There was never a someday. There was never a brave. The valley stayed exactly as calm as a valley full of stolen memories can be, which is to say: not.

So I pulled the jar off its shelf, and the silk came away in my hands like shed skin, and the smoked glass showed me nothing, because that was the point of smoked glass — and then I did the thing I had trained myself never to do with anything in my keeping.

I opened it.

A memory, released from its jar, doesn't pour out like water. It rises like heat-shimmer off a summer road, invisible until it decides to be seen. And this one rose, and hung in the air of the alcove like a ghost deciding whether to stay, and then — because memories are honest even when thieves are not — it showed itself to me.

It was not what I expected.

I had expected fire. Scales. Wind. The view from very high up, the world laid flat beneath wings, the last sunset of the last dragon rendered in colors no human eye could parse. What I got instead was — well. Fire was in it. Somewhere in it, deep in the frame, a mountain burning orange against a sky that was already half-dark, the flame of the dying beast reflected in water below like a second sun drowning.

But the center of the memory — the part the dying mind had chosen, in its final moments, to hold onto — the part it had spent its last breath polishing smooth —

was a night on a border. A cold one. Stars overhead, real stars, thick as spilled salt. A young man standing in wolf-light at the edge of a ward-line, his breath steaming, his oath-cord glinting silver at his wrist. And beside him, close enough that their shadows touched, a woman whose face I knew.

Mine.

* * *

I want to be careful here, because what happened next is the sort of thing that sounds, when described calmly, like madness, and I need you to understand it was not madness. It was bookkeeping. Terrible, catastrophic, nine-years-overdue bookkeeping.

The dragon-memory had fused with another memory. Mine. One of mine. The borders between them had dissolved somewhere in those long silent years underground — the vault's murmurings, its dreaming, its little community of displaced recollections pressing against each other shelf by shelf, year by year, until two memories that should never have touched had grown together like trees planted too close, roots braided, trunks sharing grain, impossible now to saw apart without killing both.

And the memory mine had fused with was the worst possible candidate. Because it wasn't just any memory of mine.

It was the memory of the night I crossed the border.

I stood in that alcove and watched it play out in the shimmering air, and I felt my whole body go cold, colder than the vault, colder than the valley, cold all the way down to whatever place in a person keeps the things they can't afford to feel yet.

There I was. Twenty-three years old, maybe twenty-four — young enough to think I was clever, old enough to know better and do it anyway. Standing at the ward-line after dark, when the patrols thinned and the wolf-light made everything soft and blue and forgiving. And next to me, a warden. Young too. Broad-shouldered, quiet, with a way of looking at you that made you feel like the most important thing in a valley full of unimportant things. He was off-duty, or pretending to be. We had been talking for hours, I could see it in the memory's texture — hours, in the cold, about nothing, about everything, about the wards and what they cost and who paid.

And then he had said something. I couldn't hear the words — the fusion had blurred them, melted the audio into the dragon's dying roar the way two songs played at once melt into a third song that belongs to neither — but I could see what the words did. I could see my own face change. I could see the exact moment I decided.

Because here is the thing the memory showed me, the thing I had sold away piece by piece until nothing remained but the shape of the hole: he hadn't caught me crossing. He hadn't stopped me. He had *watched me go*. He had stood at the line and let me pass, and the promise — whatever was said in that wolf-light, whatever passed between us in words the fusion had eaten — the promise was that he would let me go. That he would carry the secret. That he would carry *me*, carried inside him like a coal, for as long as it took.

Nine years. He had carried it nine years.

And I had spent those same nine years selling myself off in parcels.

* * *

This is the part where I have to be honest with you, and honesty, for a thief, is a muscle you build specifically so you can lift heavy things with it.

I stole from myself first.

That's the order of operations nobody tells you about. Before you steal from a widow, from a collector, from a grieving mother begging you to take her son's last smile out of her head — before any of it — you steal from yourself. You decide which parts of you are too heavy to carry, and you put them on the market, and you call it survival, and you call it strategy, and eventually you call it nothing at all because you've forgotten there was ever anything to name.

I sold my past the way other people sell their hair or their blood — incrementally, without ceremony, telling myself each piece was small, each piece was redundant, each piece was a duplicate of something I'd surely remember anyway. Memories don't work like that. There are no duplicates. There is no redundancy. There is only the one night, the one conversation, the one moment when someone looked at you and meant it, and when it's gone it is gone in a way that no amount of money reconstructs.

I had sold the years before the border. I had sold the years after. I had sold, in a dozen transactions over eighteen months, nearly everything that connected me to the person I had been before I became the Thief of Small Hours. And the buyers — collectors, archivists, one unsettling gentleman who paid in foreign coin and asked unsettling questions — they had gotten fragments. Shards. Pieces of a puzzle missing its center.

The center, I had kept. Or thought I kept. The center was the border-crossing itself, the crime, the hinge my whole new life swung on. I kept it locked in the deepest alcove, wrapped in silk, because a thief needs to remember her crimes even if she remembers nothing else — otherwise how do you know what you're atoning for? How do you know why you flinch at certain names, why certain streets make your chest tighten, why you wake some nights with your heart hammering at nothing?

Except I didn't flinch at those names anymore. Hadn't in years. And I had told myself that was healing. That time had sanded me smooth. That the tightening in my chest was just the weather, just my heart being forty years old in a thirty-two-year-old body.

It wasn't the weather.

The center was gone too. Sold, or traded, or — and this is what the fused memory finally made clear — *spent*. Because you cannot fuse two memories by accident. Fusion takes pressure, and pressure takes time, and time takes absence. The dragon-memory had reached for something to hold onto in the dark, some human warmth to wrap around itself against the cold of the vault, and what it had found was the gap where my border-night used to be. It had grown into the gap. Filled it. Worn its shape. The way water wears a channel, the way ivy takes the form of the wall it climbs.

The dragon's dying recollection had become, in every way that mattered, my missing night. It had put on my memory like a borrowed coat, and the coat fit, because the coat had been tailored from the same cloth — the same border, the same stars, the same wolf-light, the same young warden standing at the line.

Because the dragon had been there.

That was the realization, when it finally landed, and it landed like a stone through a window. The dragon-memory wasn't just a record of a creature dying. It was a record of what the creature *saw* as it died. And from up there, from the height of it, from the last vantage point its failing eyes would ever command — it had seen the border. It had seen the wards. It had seen, on the night its flame first guttered, two figures standing in wolf-light at the edge of the covenant it had spent its life sustaining: a thief preparing to slip through, and a warden choosing, impossibly, tenderly, catastrophically, to let her.

The dragon had watched us. Its last living act of attention — the final thing its ancient mind chose to spend itself on, before the fire went down — had been *us*. Two nobodies at a fence line. A girl with stolen goods in her pack and a boy with an oath-cord and a decision he'd regret for the rest of his mortal life.

Why? Why would it — I turned the question over and over in the dark, pacing the alcove like a woman interrogating a corpse. Why would a dying god spend its final sight on us?

And then I understood, and understanding made it worse.

Because we were the crack. The dragon had felt it — had felt, through the covenant it anchored, the exact moment the seal weakened. Magic cannot cross into Hollowmere; that was the whole design, the whole sacrifice, the whole point of a creature giving its death to a wall. But the covenant ran on the dragon's flame, and the flame ran on *continuity*, on the unbroken wholeness of the seal — and a thief slipping through the wards with contraband in her pack was a needle in the weave. A single pulled thread. And the warden who let her pull it — who chose love over duty at the precise seam where the covenant was thinnest — he hadn't just broken a regulation. He had loosened the knot. The dragon had felt the slack enter the line the way a sailor feels a rope go soft, and it had looked, with the last of its strength, to see where the weakness lived.

It had looked at us. At me. At the thread.

And then it had died, and its last recollection — the seed of the fire, the ember that could regrow everything — had recorded the flaw along with the beauty, the betrayal braided through the tenderness, inseparable, the way they always are.

* * *

I sat down on the floor of the vault. Not gracefully. Not with any of the composure I perform for clients. I sat down the way you sit down when your legs stop agreeing to hold you, which is to say abruptly, heavily, with a sound like a sack of grain hitting stone.

And I did the arithmetic. Nine years of arithmetic, done in ten minutes, and the total was ruin.

I had stolen from the dragon. That was the crime underneath the crime. Everyone in Hollowmere knew me as a lifter of small sorrows — a widow's bad night, a merchant's humiliation, a drunkard's shame, taken for coin, sold to collectors who wanted suffering they could display like porcelain. Never anything that mattered. That was my brand. That was my rule. That was the story I told about myself at every market stall, to every client, to my own reflection, until the story wore grooves in my skull deep enough to walk in.

But nine years ago I had gone over the border and come back carrying something that mattered more than anything had ever mattered, and I hadn't known it. Couldn't have known it — the jar was sealed, the silk was black, the memory inside was silent as a banked fire. I had smuggled the single most dangerous object in two realms into the safest valley in the world, and buried it under a tannery, and built my respectable little criminal life directly on top of it, the way you build a house on a fault line because the view is good.

And to forget the danger of it — to forget the weight, the wrongness, the way the jar had burned cold in my hands the whole journey home, cold in a way no memory should burn — I had sold myself. Piece by piece. Night by night. Year by year. I had dismantled my own history and hawked it off in fragments, and every coin I earned had gone into the walls of this vault, and the vault had held the thing I was trying to forget, and the thing I was trying to forget had been quietly eating the last scraps of me the whole time.

Self-betrayal, laid bare. That's the phrase Odile would use. She'd say it gently, the way she says everything, her closed eyes aimed at some middle distance only she can navigate. *You sold yourself to forget what you took, and what you took needed you to remember.*

She'd be right. She's always right. It's insufferable.

Because here is the ledger, balanced at last, and every line of it is red:

I stole the dragon's death. To hide it, I erased my own life. To live with the erasure, I built a trade out of other people's erasures. And the whole rotten structure — the market stall, the reputation, the rules, the wry competence I wear like armor — all of it stands on a foundation I poured myself, over a chamber I dug myself, around a jar I carried home myself, containing the dying sight of a creature whose death holds up the sky.

And the stars are going out. One by one. And the flame is guttering. And the seed of the new fire is sitting in my vault, fused to the one memory I can never give up willingly, because giving it up means admitting that Ansel Yarrow — the warden at the gate, the man who does not know I remember him because I do not remember him, the man whose name I sold along with everything else — giving it up means handing over the night he let me go, the promise made in wolf-light, the only proof left anywhere in the world that I was once loved by someone who had every reason not to be.

The flame needs the whole memory. Both halves. The dragon's death and my night, braided together now, inseparable, one object, one offering.

Which means the Ember Altar will ask me for the one thing I have spent nine years making sure I could live without knowing I'd lost.

I climbed the stairs out of the vault slowly, one hand on the cold stone, the lantern held high, and at the top I paused and looked back down into the dark — at the humming, dreaming, murmuring room where I had stored my crimes and my heart in the same led-lined box — and I made myself a promise, the second promise I'd ever made at that threshold, and I intended, this time, to keep it.

Tomorrow, I would go to Odile. All of it. Everything. The theft, the fusion, the warden, the name I'd sold.

Because the stars were going out over Hollowmere, and there was a man at the gate with a truce banner and my face written somewhere behind his eyes, and sooner or later — probably sooner, probably tomorrow, probably at sword-point if I made him wait — he was going to ask me what I carried.

And this time, I was going to have an answer.
