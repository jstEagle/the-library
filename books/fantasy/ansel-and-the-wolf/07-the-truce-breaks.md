## 7. The Truce Breaks

The council of Hollowmere met in a room with no windows, which told you everything you needed to know about them.

I stood at the door of the council chamber with my truce banner folded over my arm like a dead bird and listened to nine people argue about the end of the world in the tone of merchants haggling over a shipment of wool.

"The wards fell," said Magister Corven, who had a voice like a hinge that needed oiling. "The wards *fell*, and this is what we discuss? Jurisdiction?"

"We discuss jurisdiction because jurisdiction is all we have left," said a thin woman whose name I never learned. "If we panic, we are nothing. If we deliberate—"

"If we deliberate much longer," Corven said, "we will be nothing anyway."

I had been in the city two days. Two days of truce — the old law, older than the covenant itself: a warden under banner may enter Hollowmere, may speak, may petition, but may not compel. Two days of being fed soup by suspicious innkeepers and watched by children who had clearly been instructed to watch me and were terrible at pretending otherwise. Two days of standing outside Xanthe Marchetti's shuttered house and not knocking, because I had knocked once already and been told through the door, in a voice like cool water over stone, that she did not know me, had never known me, and would thank me to take my wolf-smell back to whatever gutter I'd crawled out of.

She didn't remember me. I had prepared for her fear, for her anger, for her lies. I had not prepared for the particular horror of being looked at by someone who used to fall asleep against my shoulder and seeing only a stranger deciding whether I was worth robbing.

And now the council knew what she carried. Word travels fast in a valley where everyone has sold everyone else's secrets at least twice. Odile's archivists talked. The collector I'd tracked down talked. By morning, every chair in that windowless room knew that Xanthe Marchetti held the last recollection of the last dragon, and that the stars going dark overhead were not weather.

"—destroy it," the thin woman was saying. "That is the deliberation. That has always been the deliberation. Burn the memory, seal the thief, and pray the flame holds on its own."

"Pray," Corven repeated. "With respect, Mistress Hale, prayer is what people do when they've run out of options. We haven't run out yet. There is an option sitting right there." He nodded at me without looking at me, the way you'd indicate furniture. "Warden Yarrow. Sworn to the realm beyond. Sworn, I'd imagine, to see the covenant reforged. He wants the memory carried north to the Ember Altar. Let him carry it. Let him take our problem with him."

"And when magic returns to the valley?" Hale asked. "When the things his realm keeps at bay come sniffing at our borders again? When our grandchildren are born with fire in their hands and no idea how to hold it? You'd trade our safety for his duty?"

"I'd trade a dying thing for a living one," Corven said. "But then, I've always been reckless that way."

They voted. Of course they voted. Nine chairs, and the vote went five to four for destruction — burn the memory, arrest the thief, send the warden home under escort with his tail between his legs and his banner confiscated as a courtesy.

Magistrate Oren Vail, who chaired the chamber and had the face of a man perpetually disappointed in his own reflection, turned to me at last and gave me the courtesy of eye contact.

"Warden Yarrow. The council thanks you for your service and your patience. You will be escorted to the gate at first light. As for the woman Marchetti—" he lifted a slip of paper, already sealed, already signed, "—she will be taken into custody tonight and held in the wardhouse until the rite of unmaking can be prepared. Three days. Perhaps four."

Three days. Perhaps four. Said the way you'd say the bread needs another hour in the oven.

"She stole from a dragon," I said. My voice came out flatter than I intended. "She stole from something so far above her she couldn't even understand what she was taking, and she did it for coin, and now the sky over your city is a hole, and your answer is to *unmake* the only seed of fire left in either realm."

"Our answer," Vail said mildly, "is to survive. You are welcome to disagree, Warden. Under the truce, you're welcome to do almost anything except interfere. I trust you'll remember that."

I remembered it. I remembered it very carefully, the way you memorize the exact weight of a thing you intend to throw away.

* * *

The wardhouse sat at the bottom of Lantern Hill like a fist at the end of a long arm, and I walked past it twice in the hour after the vote, learning it.

Hollowmere had built its jail the way it built everything — small, tight, and certain that the world outside was someone else's problem. One entrance, iron-banded oak. A single guard room with a brazier and a bottle between the two men on duty. Cells below ground, six of them, ventilated through a grate system that opened onto the hillside behind the building, because even jailers got tired of breathing the same air as their prisoners, and convenience always beats paranoia eventually.

Xanthe would be in cell three or four. The deep ones. The ones for thieves who steal things that matter.

I found a doorway across the lane with a good view and settled in to wait for dark, and that was where the guilt caught up with me. It had been following me since the gate, patient as weather, and it finally laid its cold hand on the back of my neck.

Because here was the arithmetic of my life, laid out plain: I had sworn an oath at nineteen years old with my hand on the oath-cord and the wolf asleep in my blood, and that oath said *hold the border, keep the covenant, let nothing cross*. And for nine years I had held it. I had dragged smugglers out of the marshes. I had burned false wards off the stones of the old road. I had watched the valley from the ridge every season and told myself the forgetting inside it was mercy, was peace, was the price paid gladly by people who wanted to be safe more than they wanted to be whole.

And then a thread of magic had come walking down out of the dead border with her handwriting on it, and I had followed it, and somewhere between the gate and this doorway I had stopped being a warden investigating a crime and started being a man standing outside a woman's window in the rain, and there was no honest way to pretend those were the same job.

The oath-cord lay against my chest under my shirt, warm as a pulse. It had been warm for two days. I chose not to think about what that meant.

* * *

Dark came, and with it came the first of them.

I heard it before I understood it — a sound from the ridge above the city, thin and wrong, like a violin bow drawn across bone. The guards in the wardhouse heard it too; I saw one of them set down his bottle and cock his head like a dog. Then the second sound came, from the east wall this time, and then a third from somewhere near the granaries, and the sounds began to talk to each other, call and answer, call and answer, and every hair on my arms stood up because I knew that pattern. Every warden knows that pattern. It's how the long-dark things hunt. They sing to each other so they don't lose count of each other, and they only need to count when there are many.

The wards were dead, and the outer dark had noticed.

I moved. There wasn't a plan anymore — there was just the narrowing space between *tonight* and *too late*, and I stepped into it.

The front door of the wardhouse opened to a knock and a lie about orders from Magistrate Vail, and it closed around my forearm and a knee before the younger guard finished saying "sir?" His partner came up off his bench with a knife and I gave him the flat of my blade across the wrist, not the edge — I was still, in that moment, technically trying to be honorable — and put both of them down among the bottles and the coals and barred the guard-room door behind me from the inside, which bought me perhaps four minutes before someone thought to come around through a window.

The stairs down were narrow and smelled of wet stone and old despair. Six doors. I pressed my ear to each. Behind the fourth door: breathing, controlled, too even to be natural, the breathing of someone listening at her own door the same way I was.

"Marchetti," I said quietly.

Silence. Then, dry as dust: "Warden. Come to gloat? Or is the truce over early?"

"The truce is over." I drew the warding-knife from my belt — the one tool of my office that worked on locks as well as flesh — and set its edge into the seam of the door. Old iron, old magic, and the lock sighed open like it had been waiting years for permission. "The council voted to destroy what you carry. They voted to arrest you tonight. And something is singing on the ridge above your city, and it isn't alone, and none of us have three days."

The door swung. She stood in the cell with her back against the wall and her chin up, wrists bare, no weapons, wearing defiance like a coat two sizes too big. She looked terrible. She looked like she hadn't slept since the stars went out, which matched my own reflection, and some traitor part of me noted that we matched and filed it away where I wouldn't have to look at it.

"You're breaking me out," she said. Not a question. Testing the shape of it.

"I'm committing treason," I corrected. "Breaking you out implies I expect to get away with it."

"Why?"

The word hung there. Why. Nine years of why, and she spent it on the smallest version.

"Because the memory doesn't belong to you or to them," I said. "It belongs to the fire. And the fire is dying, and I am the last person in either realm still sworn to keep it alive. That's the answer I'll give the tribunal, if we live to have one." I held out my hand. "There's another answer. You're not getting it tonight. Move."

For a heartbeat she just looked at my hand — at the oath-cord visible at my cuff, at the knuckles, at whatever ghosts she could see that I couldn't — and then she took it, and her grip was a thief's grip, quick and hard and gone, and we went up the stairs together into a city that had started screaming.

* * *

The grate behind the wardhouse was exactly where the architecture promised, and exactly as convenient as I'd hoped. I went through first, into the hillside dark, and reached back, and she took my hand a second time — slower this time, deliberately, like she was collecting evidence — and came out into the night air of Hollowmere, free, hunted, and standing next to the man she didn't remember loving.

Above us, the sky was wrong.

I want to be clear: I grew up under the outer sky. I know what the dark looks like when it's merely empty. This was not empty. The places where the stars had gone out weren't black holes in the heavens; they were *openings*, and through them the night moved with intention, slow shapes unfolding against the deeper dark, joints bending in numbers joints shouldn't have. One of them detached from the ridge and poured down the slope toward the city like smoke learning to walk.

"They're coming through," Xanthe breathed beside me. "Your realm's monsters. They're coming into *my* valley."

"Your valley's walls are down," I said. "This is what they were for. All of it — the forgetting, the covenant, the price your people paid for nine generations. It was buying this." I turned to look at her, really look, and made myself say the rest. "You should know what you're running with, Marchetti. The council will call me a traitor by dawn. My own order will call me worse. I'm giving up everything I am to carry your stolen goods north, and I'd do it if I'd never seen your face before tonight. But I have seen it. A long time ago. And I need you to hear that I know exactly what I'm choosing, even if you don't."

She stared at me. Down the hill, the first screams started, and the bells of Hollowmere began to ring in the pattern that meant *not fire, not flood, the other thing, the old thing, the thing we built the wall to forget* — a pattern, I realized, that no one alive in this city had ever actually heard rung in earnest, and yet every hand pulling those ropes seemed to know it, the way the body remembers a wound.

"That story you keep not telling me," she said at last, and her voice had changed — smaller, and harder, both at once. "How long is it?"

"Nine years," I said. "Give or take a betrayal."

Something crossed her face that was almost recognition and almost grief and mostly fear. Then she pulled the hood of her stolen cloak up over her hair and became, in the space of a breath, the Thief of Small Hours again — competent, wry, armored.

"Well then, Warden," she said. "North is that way, the roads will be watched within the hour, and something with too many elbows is currently eating the granary district. I suggest we run now and hate each other properly later."

From the ridge above us, the singing changed key — and I had spent my whole life learning the songs of the long dark, and I knew this one. It was the song they sang when the hunting stopped being casual.

It was the song they sang when they'd found what they wanted.

And every voice in it was aimed, like an arrow, at the woman standing beside me.

"*Run,*" I said, and let the wolf rise.
