## 8. Running the Valley's Spine

**Xanthe**

Here is something they don't tell you about fleeing for your life: it's mostly walking.

I had imagined flight as a kind of glorious panic — hair streaming, lungs burning, some heroic sprint toward the horizon. What I got instead was Ansel Yarrow in front of me setting a pace up the sheep-tracks above Hollowmere that was fast enough to hurt and slow enough to talk, which I suspected was deliberate, because the man clearly had opinions and no one left to inflict them on.

"Keep off the ridge line," he said, not turning around. "Hunters watch ridges."

"You said we were going north along the ridge."

"I said along the valley's spine. The spine is under the ridge. There's a difference, and the difference is staying alive." He glanced back at me, and moonlight caught his eyes — that pale wolf-gold that had unsettled me from the first moment he'd put a truce banner on my table like an accusation. "You wanted to be a fugitive. This is what fugitive looks like. Mostly mud."

"I didn't *want* —"

"You broke out of a wardhouse with me at midnight carrying the last memory of a dragon. That's wanting, Xanthe. That's wanting with commitment."

I shut my mouth. Partly because he was right, partly because arguing uphill is a fool's sport, and partly — though I'd have bitten my own tongue before admitting it — because there was something almost comfortable about being needled by him. Which was absurd. I had known the man for two days, most of which had involved him threatening me, then saving me, in that order.

The valley fell away below us, Hollowmere's lamps scattered across the dark like coins dropped on black cloth. Somewhere down there, the council was convening emergency sessions and drafting arrest warrants with my name on them, and Sera Vance was sharpening whatever she sharpened. Above us, where stars should have been, there was nothing. Just a ceiling of velvet absence. Nine years I'd lived under that absence without once looking up properly. Now I couldn't stop looking.

"It's worse the higher you go," Ansel said quietly, following my gaze. "The dark. From the lowlands you can pretend it's cloud. Up here you can see the shape of it. Where the sky used to be full."

"How do you know what a full sky looks like? You're what, thirty?"

"Thirty-one. And I was born outside the valley, thief. Wardens aren't recruited from your lot of respectable amnesiacs." He said it lightly, but something moved behind the words. "I saw stars until I was nine. Then I took the oath-cord and came inside, and the sky closed like a book."

"That's a terrible bargain. Stars for a job."

"The job comes with a wolf." He patted his own chest, where the oath-cord hung beneath his shirt, coiled like a sleeping thing. "Most people would call that a fair trade."

*Most people,* I thought, watching him move through the dark — sure-footed, silent, more animal than man even in his human shape — *most people haven't seen what you look like when you run.*

Because I had. Twice now. Once crossing the flooded gorge at Cindermill, when the hunters' horns had sounded behind us and he'd simply — stopped being a man. It hadn't been the horror story the valley tells its children. No snapping jaws, no mindless hunger. He'd knelt, and the change had come over him like water pouring upward, and then there had been a wolf the size of a pony standing in the reeds, looking at me with those same gold eyes, waiting. Asking, somehow, whether I trusted him.

I'd climbed onto his back because the alternative was drowning, and told myself that was the only reason.

It was only later, wrapped in his cloak on the far bank while he shook the water off like any dog and pretended not to notice me staring, that the strange thing happened. He'd said something — I don't even remember what, some dry remark about my grip — and I had laughed. Not politely. Really laughed, the kind that surprises you coming out. And in the half-second of that laugh, the gorge was gone, the cold was gone, and I was somewhere else: summer, long grass gone gold, a boy with the same eyes lying beside me pointing at a hawk and saying —

Saying what?

Gone. Like smoke through fingers. But the shape of the feeling stayed, warm and specific and utterly unplaceable, and I had sat there in the wet grass feeling like a woman who'd found a key in her pocket for a door she'd never seen.

"You went somewhere," Ansel said now, and I realized I'd stopped walking. He'd turned back, head tilted, studying me with that unnerving attention. "Just now. Where did you go?"

"Nowhere. A cramp."

"Liar," he said, without heat. "You're a professional liar, so believe me when I say I know the difference between a lie and a cramp. Your face does something. When you remember."

"My face does nothing."

"Your face," he said, "does everything. It always did."

He said it simply, the way you'd say *the sun rises*, and then turned and kept climbing, leaving me standing in the dark with my heart doing something complicated against my ribs.

*Always did.* As if he'd known my face across years instead of days. As if there were a whole archive of me somewhere behind his eyes, catalogued and cross-referenced, while I stood here holding nothing but a stolen dragon-memory and a growing suspicion that I had sold off more than I knew.

We walked on. Behind us, far down the valley, something screamed — a high, jointed, wrong sound that no throat in Hollowmere could make. Ansel didn't break stride, but his shoulders changed.

"They're following the wards' corpse," he said. "The old ward-lines still hold a trace of power, like warmth in a dead hearth. The things from outside can read it. And the ward-line runs north." He looked back at me, and for the first time since the wardhouse, I saw fear on him — not for himself. "They're not hunting us, Xanthe. They're herding us. Everything that moves tonight is moving along the spine, and everything moving along the spine is moving toward the same place."

"Which is?"

"The border." He said it the way you'd say a diagnosis. "Where the flame is dying. So we walk faster."

**Ansel**

She was limping by the second hour and hiding it by the third, which told me more about her than any confession could have. Pride like hers doesn't come from nowhere. It's load-bearing. You build it over a person the way you shore up a failing wall, and I knew — because I had watched her build it, nine years ago, brick by stubborn brick — exactly which cracks ran underneath.

"We rest at the shepherds' bothy past the burn," I said.

"I'm fine."

"You're favoring your left leg and pretending the slope is why."

"I'm *adjusting* for the slope."

"Xanthe." I stopped and let her catch up, close enough to see the sweat on her brow despite the cold. "In three hours we may be running again. If your leg goes then, I carry you, and you'll hate every minute of it, and I'll enjoy it more than is dignified. Rest now and spare us both."

She glared at me. It was, absurdly, good to be glared at. Nine years I had carried the memory of that glare — aimed at me across a campfire on the border, aimed at me the night she left, aimed at me through the bars of every regret I owned — and having the original article in front of me, live and furious and breathing, was worth the blisters.

"There's a bothy?" she said finally.

"There was. Stone walls, a hearth, a roof if the roof has held. Shepherds used it before the covenant made the high pasture useless."

"Before the covenant." Her voice shifted, careful. "You say things like that. *Before the covenant. Outside the valley. Always did.* Little windows into a past I'm not in." She crossed her arms. "So ask me, warden. Whatever it is you keep not asking. Ask it."

The wind came down the valley and pushed between us, smelling of snow and, faintly, of ash — the outer dark's signature, seeping through the dead wards like damp through a wall. I thought about all the questions I'd carried for nine years, stacked like cordwood. *Why did you cross alone. Why didn't you wait. Why did you sell your own past, piece by piece, until there was nothing left of us to find.*

What I asked instead was: "When you laughed today. On the far bank of the gorge. What did you see?"

Something flickered across her face — the something, the remembering-something, quick as a fish under ice.

"Nothing," she said.

"Try again."

"A field," she said slowly, reluctantly, like each word cost coin. "Long grass. Gold. Someone was pointing at a hawk. I don't know who. I don't know where. It lasted less than a breath and then it was gone, and I want to know—" Her voice cracked, just slightly, and she hated that it cracked; I watched her hate it. "I want to know why a stranger's laugh tastes like grief."

The honest answer was *because you loved me and you cut it out of yourself and sold it to strangers, and love leaves scar tissue that remembers*. I had rehearsed that answer for nine years. I had composed versions of it by firelight, eloquent ones, forgiving ones, devastating ones.

What I said was: "Come on. Bothy's ahead. You can grieve after you've eaten."

She stared at me. "That's it? I hand you a mystery like that and you say *come on*?"

"The mystery keeps. Legs don't." I started walking, and after a moment, footsteps followed mine. Then, quieter, almost to herself:

"You're doing it again. Knowing things about me you shouldn't know."

"Yes," I said, and didn't elaborate, and felt her glaring at my back the whole way to the bothy, and thought: *let her glare. Let her wonder. Let the questions pile up until she digs.* Because I had finally understood something on this long cold climb, something Odile's books had never taught me: I couldn't give Xanthe her past back. It wasn't mine to give. But I could stand next to her while she found it herself, and that — that might be worth more. Memory taken is loot. Memory earned is hers.

* * *

**Xanthe**

The bothy had a roof, a hearth, and a floor of packed earth that had hosted a century of shepherds and their fleas. By fugitive standards it was a palace. Ansel built a fire small enough to hide — dry turf, no flame higher than a cupped hand — and I ate hard bread and harder cheese and tried not to look at the way he sat: back to the wall, door in view, eating in quick efficient bites like a man who expected the meal to be interrupted.

Outside, the jointed things screamed again, closer. My hand went to the satchel at my chest without my permission — the vault-box, and inside it, the memory that burned cold. The dragon's last recollection. The seed of the world's fire, riding against my heartbeat like a stone.

"It knows you're frightened," Ansel said.

"What, the box?"

"The memory. Cold-burning memories are alive, in their way. Mine —" He stopped. Adjusted. "Memories that matter have weight. Yours has been awake since the wards fell. It's been pulling you north all night. Haven't you felt it? Like a hook behind your sternum."

I had felt it. I'd been telling myself it was fear. Fear pulls you home, though, backward, toward beds and bolted doors. This pulled forward, into the dark, into the dying country beyond the wards, and it pulled with a patience that scared me more than the screaming things did.

"When we get there," I said, "to the Ember Altar. What happens to the memory? To me? Odile said the altar feeds on memory, and I've been doing arithmetic ever since, and every sum ends with *Xanthe, minus*."

Ansel was quiet for a long moment. Firelight moved on his face and made him look younger, and older, and familiar in a way that had nothing to do with the two days I'd known him.

"The arithmetic isn't finished," he said at last. "Don't pay debts early."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't." And then, softer, looking into the fire rather than at me: "Ask me again when we're closer. Some answers weigh too much to carry this far uphill."

I should have argued. Arguing was my native language; I'd made a living on misdirection and clever retreats. Instead I heard myself say, "Tell me something true instead. One true thing. Since we're apparently spending the apocalypse together, I'd like to know who with."

He looked at me then, fully, and the firelight did something to his eyes — turned the gold deep, banked-coal gold, ember gold — and for one disorienting instant I was somewhere else again. Not the field this time. A night. Rain. A border-stone with carvings worn smooth, and hands — his hands, younger — wrapping a cord around my wrist, and a voice saying *whatever you become, come back as something I can find—*

The vision snapped. I gasped like a surfacing swimmer.

"Xanthe?" His hand was on my arm. When had he moved? "What did you see?"

"A border-stone," I whispered. "Rain. You were — someone was tying a cord around my wrist. Saying —" I pressed my palms against my eyes. "Saying *come back as something I can find.*"

Silence. When I lowered my hands, Ansel had gone very still, the stillness of a man standing on ice and listening to it speak.

"That's a warden's oath-binding," he said finally, hoarsely. "It's what we say when we tie the oath-cord. *Whatever you become, come back as something I can find.*" A breath. "You weren't there for mine. You couldn't have been. It's spoken alone, at the border, at night."

"Well, someone said it to me in the rain." My heart was slamming. "And I have never in my life been to any border, warden, except the one we're running toward, and I have certainly never stood in rain tying cords with —"

"With someone whose face you can't see," he said quietly. "Whose voice you know anyway."

The fire popped. Outside, the jointed screamers had gone quiet, which was somehow worse.

"Why do I know your voice?" I asked. It came out smaller than I meant it to. "Why does my body know things my head doesn't? Why do I laugh at your jokes like I've laughed at them before? Why, when you say my name like *that* —" I stopped, because saying *like that* required describing *that*, and I wasn't ready to describe the way he said my name, low and certain, like a word he'd kept in his mouth for years.

Ansel reached up, slow, giving me every chance to pull away, and touched the hollow of my throat where the pulse jumped. Not romantic. Diagnostic. A warden checking a heartbeat.

"Because," he said, "some things don't live in the head."

"And where do they live?"

"Deeper down. Where you keep the things you'd die before trading." His hand fell away. He fed a knot of turf to the fire, and when he spoke again his voice was level, wry, carefully rebuilt. "Get some sleep, thief. I'll take first watch. Second watch, you wake me, and third watch nobody sleeps because the things with too many joints will be in the gorge by then, and we'll want a head start."

"You're assuming I can sleep."

"I'm assuming you'll try, fail, and lie there listening to me not-answer your questions, which is nearly as restful." He settled against the wall, blade across his knees, and the gold of his eyes caught the firelight one last time. "Sleep, Xanthe. Tomorrow we cross the Spine's shoulder, and after that there's no more valley to hide in. Only the border, and the dark, and whatever you decide to be when you get there."

I lay down on the shepherd's earth with the cold-burning box against my chest and stared at the smoky rafters, and just before sleep took me — real sleep, the first in two days — I heard myself murmur, half-dreaming, words I had no reason to know:

"*Wolf-light. You promised me wolf-light.*"

Across the bothy, very softly, Ansel Yarrow said: "I remember."

And far to the north, past the drowned gorge, past the shoulder of the Spine, something answered from beyond the dead wards — not a scream this time, but a voice, vast and patient and pleased, that said, in the tongue of the outer dark, a single word that Odile would later translate for me with shaking hands:

*Coming.*
