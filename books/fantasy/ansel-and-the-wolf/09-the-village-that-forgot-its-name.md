## 9. The Village That Forgot Its Name

The village had no name.

I don't mean it had lost its name the way a glove loses its pair—somewhere in a drawer, waiting to be reunited. I mean the name was gone the way a tooth is gone: a smooth place in the jaw where something used to sit, and everyone's tongue kept finding the absence without knowing what to call it.

We came down off the ridge at dusk, Ansel limping in his human shape and me limping in mine, which was less dramatic but equally sincere. The gorge crossing had cost us most of our dry clothes, half my dignity, and one of my boots to the river gods, who apparently accept offerings whether you intend them or not. My remaining boot squelched with every step like a small wet animal complaining about its employment.

"There," Ansel said, pointing.

Smoke rose from chimneys tucked into a fold of the hills—a scatter of slate roofs, a well, a shrine with no god in it that I could see from this distance, just an empty alcove facing east. Fields lay fallow around the houses, though it was planting season. I noted that and filed it away, because a thief notices what a town doesn't do before she notices what it does.

"No name," I said. "Look at the signpost."

He looked. The post stood at the crossroads with three arms, each carved for lettering, each sanded blank. Not weathered—sanded. Someone had taken a plane to every word on it, carefully, recently enough that the wood still showed pale scars where the letters had been.

"That's not rot," he said quietly.

"No," I said. "That's policy."

* * *

Here is what I knew about the covenant, before all of this began, which is to say here is what everyone in Hollowmere knew, which is to say almost nothing, delivered with great confidence:

Magic cannot cross into the valley. The wards hold. We are safe. We are lucky. Be grateful.

That was the whole catechism. Four lines, recited at festivals, stitched onto samplers, taught to children alongside their letters. *Be grateful.* It never occurred to me—not once in thirty years of living behind those wards—to ask what the wards cost, or who paid, or why the price was never listed anywhere I could find it. You don't audit a miracle. You just live inside it and complain about the weather.

But a nameless village with freshly sanded signposts and unplanted fields in planting season—that was an invoice coming due in plain sight.

"We shouldn't stop," Ansel said. He said it while already walking toward the nearest house, because warden logic operates on the principle that the correct tactical decision and the decision actually made are distant cousins who meet only at funerals. "Hunters will check the settlements."

"Hunters will also check the ridge," I said, "and I can't run another step in one boot. Unless your wolf-shape offers rides now. Does it? Because I have questions about the ethics of that arrangement."

"It doesn't offer rides."

"Then we're stopping."

He gave me a look—the look of a man cataloguing grievances for later—and knocked on the door of the nearest cottage, which had a wreath of dried rowan berries on it. Rowan for warding. Old habit, in a valley where nothing needed warding anymore. Or so we'd been told.

The door opened on a small woman, old the way valley women get old early, bent like a question mark over her own doorstep. Her eyes went to Ansel first, and I watched her face do something extraordinary. It didn't light up. It *broke*—gently, the way bread breaks, along lines that were already there.

"Tomas," she said.

"I'm sorry," Ansel began. "My name is—"

"You've been gone so long." She reached up and touched his cheek with fingers like dry leaves. "Your father said you'd come back when the roads were safe, and I told him, Tomas doesn't wait for safe, Tomas never—" She stopped. She was looking at his eyes. Something flickered there, some old arithmetic failing her, and then she blinked and the arithmetic started over and arrived at the same wrong answer. "Come in. Both of you. There's stew."

There was stew. There is always stew, in these moments. The world's universal apology for having broken something.

* * *

Her name was Maren. Or she was called Maren; I suspected even she wasn't certain anymore, because when I asked about the village—its history, its founding, anything at all—she smiled the way people smile when a child asks where the wind lives.

"The village has always been here," she said. "It's always been called..." She paused. Her mouth worked. I watched her reach for it, genuinely reach, the way you reach for a word on the tip of your tongue, and I watched the reaching fail, and I watched her decide, with the smooth efficiency of long practice, not to notice that it had failed. "Well. It's called what it's called. Sit, sit, the stew's getting cold."

The cottage was warm and close and full of small wrongnesses, the kind you only see if you've spent your life casing other people's homes. A loom with thread still wound on it, gray with dust—abandoned mid-work, years ago. A child's height-marks notched on the doorframe, stopping abruptly at four feet, the last one dated nine years back. Photographs—or rather, frames. The valley didn't have photography, but it had painted miniatures, and every miniature in Maren's front room had been turned to face the wall.

Every single one.

"Maren," I said, as gently as I know how, which is gently the way a lockpick is gentle—precisely, with intent. "Who are these?"

She glanced at the frames and her brow creased, a little furrow of honest puzzlement. "Keepsakes," she said. "From... someone. They're nice frames, aren't they? Good oak."

She'd turned them around herself. That was the horror of it. No warden had come through with orders; no council clerk had confiscated anything. Somewhere in the generations since the covenant, the forgetting had become self-administered. When a memory snagged against the wards' great silence—when you tried to think of your village's name, or your son's face, or whatever the flame had eaten—you didn't fight it. You turned the picture to the wall. You sanded the signpost. You planted less each year, because the fields had been worked by hands whose names you couldn't hold onto, and working land that remembers people you can't remember is its own quiet grief.

And I sat there in that warm kitchen eating stew that tasted like salt and thyme and shame, and I thought: *I did this.*

Not me personally. Not these exact hands. But my people. The covenant was bought and paid for, and the currency was this—names, faces, harvests, sons. Hollowmere got safety and called it luck. Villages like this one got the bill and were never shown the itemized charges.

Xanthe Marchetti, thief of small hours, who had built a whole moral architecture around the principle *never steal anything that matters*—I had lived my entire life inside the largest theft in the valley's history, and I'd never once noticed the missing weight.

* * *

Maren set Ansel a place at the table with the particular ceremony of a woman setting a place for someone she has set places for ten thousand times. She put the chipped blue bowl in front of him. Not the good bowls—the chipped blue one, and I understood, watching her do it, that the chipped blue bowl had been *his*, whoever Tomas had been, and that her hands remembered what her mind could not.

Ansel ate the stew. All of it. I watched him do that too, and there was something in the set of his shoulders that I couldn't read—something that wasn't patience exactly, and wasn't pity. Whatever it was, it looked expensive.

"You'll stay the night," Maren said. It wasn't a question. "Tomas's bed is made. I keep it made."

"Maren—" Ansel started.

"He'd want you to have it." She was already clearing dishes, moving with the brisk authority of someone heading off an argument before it can form. "It's just sitting there. What's the sense of a bed nobody sleeps in?"

And there it was. Nine years, by the marks on the doorframe. Nine years of a made bed. Nine years of turning miniatures to the wall and sanding signposts and calling the ache by no name at all, because the wards had taken the name along with everything else.

I caught Ansel's eye across the table. *We can't,* I tried to say with my eyebrows. *This is monstrous. We're deceiving a grieving woman for a roof.*

His eyebrows answered: *What would you have me tell her?*

Good point. The truth was that her son was dead or worse, gone beyond the wards into a realm that had stopped existing for her the moment the covenant closed—because you cannot grieve someone you cannot remember, and the wards had seen to it that she couldn't. Maybe Tomas had died at the border. Maybe he'd walked out one day and simply been subtracted from the valley's ledger, and his mother had woken to a made bed and a chipped blue bowl and no idea whose they were, only that they must belong to someone, because things always do.

Maybe that was mercy. The archivists would say it was mercy. Odile would say it in that blind, level voice of hers, and mean it.

Sitting in that kitchen, I decided that if anyone ever called it mercy to my face again, I was going to take something of theirs. Something that mattered. Rule-breaking as a public service.

* * *

Later, while Maren slept and Ansel took first watch at the window—I'd argued for second watch and lost, on the grounds that I'd "nearly drowned in a gorge today," which seemed like a low blow—I wandered the cottage with a candle, doing what thieves do in other people's homes even when there's nothing worth taking.

The back room was Tomas's. Of course it was. Made bed, folded blanket, a wooden sword on pegs, whittled by a boy's hand, clumsy and beloved. On the desk, a slate covered in a child's letters, preserved under dust: practice rows of the alphabet, and then, in larger, prouder strokes, a sentence written out over and over.

*I am Tomas of ———*

The blank space had been scratched out. Not by him. By someone later, with a knife, carefully, the way you'd scrape a mistake off wax. And beneath the scratched-out name, in an older woman's shakier hand, someone had written: *of home.*

*Of home.* Because the actual words wouldn't come. Because she'd sat at her dead-or-vanished son's desk with a knife and a piece of chalk and tried to restore one single sentence to wholeness, and the covenant wouldn't let her, and so she'd invented a grammar of absence. *Of home.* As if that meant anything. As if that filled anything.

I stood there holding the candle and let the guilt arrive properly, the way weather arrives off the mountains—all at once, and colder than forecast.

Because here was the thing I hadn't let myself think on the ridge, or in the gorge, or any of the desperate miles since: I had known. Some part of me had always known. Why else does a girl from inside the safest valley in the world grow up learning to steal memories? Why else does she build a trade out of handling other people's pasts, if not because her own felt like a house with rooms she wasn't allowed to enter? I'd told myself it was talent. Wry competence, marketable and morally neutral, as long as I kept my little rule.

But the rule was an apology. The whole trade was an apology. Every grieving widow I'd lightened, every client I'd helped forget—I'd been paying down a debt I couldn't name, on behalf of a crime committed before I was born, in a currency of other people's nights.

And now the wards were down, and the stars were going out, and the bill had come due with interest, and the interest was creatures with too many joints walking through dead wards toward villages full of mothers keeping made beds for sons the magic had erased.

I blew out the candle and stood in the dark of Tomas's room and did not cry, mostly.

* * *

Near midnight, Maren woke screaming.

Not loudly—that was the worst part. A small, strangled sound, the scream of someone who has learned to scream quietly so as not to wake the house. Ansel moved; I moved; we both ended up in her doorway like a pair of useless guardians, candlelight swinging.

She was sitting up in bed, clutching the blanket, staring at the wall where a miniature hung—this one facing outward, the only one. In it, a young man with dark hair and a crooked grin held a fishing pole over his shoulder like a soldier's rifle.

"I saw him," she whispered. "In the dream. I saw him and I knew him, I knew his name, it was right there—" Her hands opened and closed on the blanket. "And then I woke up and it's gone, it's gone again, it goes *every time*—"

"Maren." Ansel sat on the edge of her bed, slow, careful, the way you approach a spooked animal. "You're safe. You're home."

"But who is he?" She grabbed his wrist with sudden fierce strength, this tiny bent woman, and pulled him close, and searched his face. "You have his eyes. Doesn't he have his eyes? Tell me he has his eyes."

Ansel was silent for a moment. I watched him decide something. "He has your eyes," he said finally. "Both of you. Same grey."

"Same grey," she repeated, and something in her unclenched, fractionally. "Same grey. Yes. Yes, that's—it's something. It's more than I had." She lay back down, still gripping his wrist, and I saw the exact moment exhaustion won over terror. "Stay till I sleep, Tomas. Just till I sleep."

He stayed. I stood in the doorway with the candle and watched a wolf-warden of the outer realm sit beside a stranger until her breathing slowed, and I thought about how he'd known what to say. *He has your eyes.* Not a lie, exactly. Not the truth, either. The precise, surgical kindness of a man who has spent nine years carrying something heavy and has gotten very good at setting it down quietly.

When she slept, he came to the doorway. We looked at each other over the candle flame.

"She dreams about him," I whispered. "Even now. Even with the wards taking the name. The dream gets through."

"Dreams run deeper than names," he said.

"Do yours?" I asked it lightly. Wryly. The way I ask everything, the way I've asked everything since I was sixteen and learned that a joke is a door you can lock from the inside. "Dream deep?"

Something crossed his face then—something old and banked, like coals under ash. He looked at me for a long moment, and the candle guttered between us, and outside, far off on the northern ridge, something howled. Not a wolf. Wolves sound like hunger. This sounded like *arithmetic*—like something counting.

"Get some sleep, thief," he said. "Tomorrow we run."

I slept in Tomas's bed, under the folded blanket, in a dead boy's room in a nameless village, and I dreamed of nothing at all—which, for a memory-thief, is the loudest nightmare there is.

And when I woke before dawn to the smell of Maren already cooking breakfast for two guests she believed she'd fed a hundred times, I found Ansel at the window, awake, watching the northern ridge, and I saw his hand pressed flat against his chest, over his heart, the way a man presses a wound.

"Ansel," I said. "What's wrong?"

He didn't turn around.

"Nothing," he said, in the voice of a man deciding, at last, after nine years and five hundred miles, that it was time to burn a debt to the ground and show me the ash. "Ask me again tonight. By the fire. There's something you should know—something I should have told you at the gate."

Outside, the first grey light came up over the nameless fields, and somewhere in it, faint and patient, the thing on the ridge was still counting.
