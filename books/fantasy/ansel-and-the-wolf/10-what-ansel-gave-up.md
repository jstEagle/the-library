## 10. What Ansel Gave Up

The village had a hearth but no name, and the fire we built in it had a warmth but no honesty, because I was sitting across from the woman I loved and she was looking at me the way you look at a stranger who has just told you something slightly too long a story about his horse.

She'd asked me a question. That was the problem with questions. You could dodge a warden's blade, dodge a council hunter's crossbow bolt, dodge a thing with too many joints in the flooded dark of the Gorge of Sorrows — but you could not dodge a question asked quietly across a fire by a woman who had stolen your whole heart and then, apparently, misplaced it.

*Why do you look at me like that?* she'd asked. *Like you're grieving something I haven't broken yet.*

And I had said, because I am an idiot with a warden's discipline and nine years of rehearsed silences, "I'll tell you. But not while we're still moving. When we stop, I'll tell you everything."

We had stopped. The fire was burning. Everything was due.

Xanthe sat with her knees drawn up and her arms wrapped around them, and the firelight did things to her face that made my chest ache in the old familiar way, the way it had ached every day since the border, since the wolf-light, since I watched her walk into Hollowmere carrying a piece of the world's soul in her head and a piece of my heart in her pocket, though I doubt she knew she'd taken either.

"Ansel," she said. "You don't have to."

"I know," I said. "That's the worst part. I could walk out that door and never say it, and you'd never know what you were missing."

"Is it bad?"

"It's the best thing that ever happened to me," I said. "That's what makes it bad."

She went very still. Xanthe Marchetti, the finest thief in Hollowmere, a woman who could lift a memory off a grieving widow without the widow's teacup rattling, went still the way only guilty people go still — completely, deliberately, like a held breath.

"Tell me," she said.

* * *

Her name was Xanthe Marchetti, and she was twenty-two years old, and I met her on the wrong side of a crime.

I was twenty-four, newly sworn, still carrying my oath-cord like it was a live coal I'd been trusted not to drop. Third year of the border patrol. The border in those days wasn't the dead thing it is now — the wards hummed then, low and constant, a sound you felt in your back teeth, and the magic on the far side pressed against them like a sea against a seawall. My job was to walk the wall of the world and make sure the seawall held.

She came over it at dusk on the autumn equinox, when the wards run thin, and she came over it the way water finds a crack — no force at all, just patience. I found her sitting on a boundary stone on our side, eating an apple, watching the stars come out over the outer realm.

"You're trespassing," I told her.

"I'm admiring," she said. "Trespassing is a side effect."

I should have arrested her. I want that on the record, somewhere, in whatever ledger the world keeps of my failures: I should have arrested her. Instead I asked her what was on the other side worth admiring, and she said, "Everything. That's the problem with your side. It's very safe and very quiet and there's nothing in it that can look back at you."

She was a thief of memories. She told me that on the second night, when I found her at the border again, and the third, and the fourth, because I kept telling myself I was gathering evidence, building a case, being a good warden, and every night she was there with an apple and a story about the things she'd lifted — a first kiss from a man who'd forgotten his wife, the smell of a bakery from a baker who'd gone blind, small things, kind things, things she gave back when she could.

"Never steal anything that matters," she told me, the way other people recite scripture. "That's the whole trade. The moment you take something that matters, you stop being a thief and start being a wound."

"And what are you stealing tonight?" I asked.

She looked at me across the boundary stone, firelight from her little camp lantern moving on her face, and she said, "Ask me in a month."

I have thought about that answer for nine years. Ask me in a month. She knew. She must have known even then, or suspected, or feared — that whatever was starting between us was going to matter, and that mattering was the one thing she'd sworn never to steal.

* * *

We had four months. I want to be exact about that, because memory is the only currency I have left, and I've counted it the way a miser counts coins.

Four months of meeting at the border. She taught me the names of stars from the far side of the sky, stars the valley couldn't see, and I taught her the warden's hand-signs, and she learned them faster than my recruits ever did, and laughed at me when I drilled her anyway. She stole a memory of snow for me once — real snow, from a mountain village in the outer realm, the way it sounds when it lands on pine — and pressed it into my hand like a gift, and I carried it for years. I gave it back to her tonight, before the fire. I don't know if she noticed. She held it like something familiar with the label torn off.

In the second month I stopped pretending I was gathering evidence. In the third month I stopped pretending to myself. In the fourth month, on the night of the wolf-light — when the outer realm's moon runs full and the magic presses so hard against the wards that the whole border glows silver-blue, like the world holding its breath — she took my face in her hands and said, "I'm going to steal something that matters after all. I'm sorry. I'll take good care of it."

And then she kissed me, and I let her, and that was the crime. Not hers. Mine. A sworn warden of the border, fraternizing with the very magic the wards were built to hold out, and loving it — loving *her* — which was worse, because magic can be fought, but I never once tried to fight her.

"You could come with me," she said, after. Wolf-light in her hair. Her thumb tracing the oath-cord at my wrist like she was reading it. "Cross over. Live in the quiet valley. Steal small things and give them back."

"You know I can't."

"I know," she said. "I wanted to hear you say it anyway. So it's a real choice and not just a habit."

It was a real choice. I made it every night for four months, and every night I chose the oath, and I told myself that was honor, and some nights I even believed it.

* * *

Then the order came down. Not for her — for her *kind*. The council had gotten frightened. Thieves crossing the border, magic leaking through in memory-shapes, who knew what else was leaking. The wardens were instructed to detain any crosser on sight and hold them for the archivists. And the archivists, everyone knew, did not give things back.

I had one night's warning and a choice: tell her and watch her run into the valley alone, or say nothing and let the wardens take her at dawn.

I chose the third thing, which was the coward's thing and the lover's thing and I have never been able to separate them. I went to the border early. I told her everything. The order, the archivists, the fact that if she stayed she'd be held and picked apart and what she carried would never be returned to her. And then I did the thing I have replayed every night for nine years, in every version, and it ends the same way every time.

I let her go.

I didn't drag her to the wardens. I didn't cross with her. I stood on my side of the boundary stone with my oath-cord burning on my wrist and my heart walking away from me in the dark, and I said, "Go. Don't look back. If they ask, I never found you."

She looked back. Of course she looked back. She was Xanthe; she broke rules for a living.

"Come with me," she said, one last time, and it wasn't a temptation anymore, it was a door standing open with the cold coming through it, and I stood in the doorway of my whole life and I said, "I can't," and she said, "I know," and she crossed, and the wards closed behind her like water, and that was the last time I saw her for nine years.

I reported that the crosser had evaded me. I took the demerit. I kept her memory — every night, every apple, every star she'd named, the wolf-light, the snow — because someone had to. She'd crossed into a valley that was forgetting itself, a valley where the wards ate names and softened histories, and I understood, in the way you understand a wound, that she would not come out the same. That whatever the valley did to her, someone outside it had to hold the original. Someone had to keep the true copy.

So I kept it. Nine years. Every warden I served with thought I was the most disciplined man on the border, because I never drank, never laughed easily, never took a leave. They didn't know I was drunk every night on a memory. They didn't know I was guarding a border for the privilege of standing forty miles from where she slept.

* * *

The fire had burned down to a red heart. Xanthe hadn't moved. Her eyes were bright and fixed on me, and her hands were white where they gripped her knees.

"The promise," she said. Her voice came out strange, sanded down. "In the vault. The memory that's fused to the dragon's — there's a night on the border. Wolf-light. A promise. I remember the promise, Ansel. I remember *saying* it. But I don't remember—" She stopped. Started again. "I don't remember you."

"I know," I said.

"How can I remember the promise and not the person I made it to?"

"Because the valley took the love and left the words." I fed the fire a stick, because my hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking. "The wards don't erase whole. They erase the weight. They leave the shape of a thing and take the meaning out of it. That's how they got a whole valley to agree to live behind a wall — they didn't take the people's memories of the outer realm, they took what the outer realm *felt like*. You kept the promise because it was in your own voice. They couldn't take your own voice from you without taking everything. But me—" I shrugged, and it cost more than the shrug looked like it cost. "I was the meaning. So they took me and left the sentence."

She was crying. Quietly, without any drama, the way she did everything — competently, even this, tears managed like a pickpocket manages a lift, and it broke me a little that she'd had nine years of practice crying quietly in a valley that didn't allow loud grief.

"Say the promise," she said.

"Xanthe—"

"Say it. I want to hear it in your voice. Maybe it'll — maybe it'll catch. Maybe the words remember you even if I don't."

So I said it. The wolf-light, the boundary stone, her hands on my face, and me, young and stupid and certain I was doing the honorable thing: *"Whatever the fire asks, I'll pay it with you. Whatever it costs, we'll have cost it together. I'll find you on the other side of anything, even if the other side is you forgetting me."*

God. I'd said it like a vow. I'd said it like I was brave. I'd had no idea, then, that I was prophesying — that the fire *would* ask, that the cost *would* come due, that I would spend nine years finding a woman who had forgotten me and that finding her would be the easy part.

She repeated it back. Slowly, testing each word like ice on a pond. And then she said, "I remember the wolf-light. I remember being happy — not what about, just — happy, like a room with the furniture gone but the warmth still in it. Is that what it was? Was I happy?"

"You laughed," I said. "All the time. You said I was the only honest man on the border and that it was a terrible waste of a good face."

A wet, startled laugh out of her now, there at the fire, and for one second the room had its furniture back. Then it went out of her face, and she looked at her hands.

"Ansel. If I'd remembered — if any part of me had known — these nine years—"

"You'd have come back for me?"

"You'd have *waited* for me?" she said, at the same moment, and we looked at each other across the fire, and I answered her question because mine answered itself.

"Every night," I said. "That's not devotion. That's just arithmetic. You were the sum of everything I had."

She crossed the fire. Not gracefully — she knocked a log askew and stepped in ash and didn't care, which was the most like her I'd seen her since the wardhouse. She sat down beside me, shoulder to shoulder, and put her head against my arm, and said, "I don't feel it. I want to be honest with you, because you've just been honest enough for both of us for nine years. I hear the story and I believe it and I don't *feel* it. It's like hearing about a sister I never had."

"I know."

"But the promise remembers you," she said. "And I keep the promise. So — I'll start there. If you can stand starting there."

I could have said something wise. I could have said something about time, about how the valley's forgetting wasn't the end of a thing but a pause in it, about how the new covenant we were carrying north might remember for both of us. Instead I said, "I've waited nine years. I can stand starting anywhere, as long as it's with you."

She was quiet a long moment. Then: "That's a terrible line."

"I've had nine years to work on it."

"It's a terrible line," she said again, and her voice cracked in the middle of it, and she turned her face into my shoulder, and I held her, and outside the wind moved through the nameless village, and somewhere far to the north the last dragon-fire guttered, and none of it, for one whole hour, mattered more than the weight of her against my side.

* * *

I should have known better than to trust an hour.

I heard it before she did — I still had the warden's ears, even if I'd traded away everything else the oath had given me — a footfall on the village's one street, wrong in its rhythm. Too light for the council's heavy hunters, too deliberate for a lost villager. A pause at the well. A pause at the shrine. Someone reading the village the way a hunter reads spoor.

Xanthe felt me go tense and lifted her head, and by the time her hand found her knife, the doorway was already filling.

Sera Vance stood in it, crossbow slung, hands open and empty — the universal gesture of *I could have shot you already, so hear me out* — and firelight ran along the gray at her temples and the scar on her jaw and the council badge she'd made a show of covering with her palm.

"Marchetti," she said. "Warden. Don't run. Running's the one thing I'm better at than either of you." Her eyes went to the fire, to the two of us sitting close enough to share a shadow, and something moved through her face that was not entirely the hunter. "And don't bother with the knives. I'm not here for the memory."

"Then why are you here?" Xanthe said.

"Because I've come to make you both a bargain," Sera Vance said, "and you're going to hate it, and then you're going to take it."
