## 11. The Hunter's Bargain

**Xanthe**

I have been caught before. Twice by market guards, once by a husband who came home early, and once — memorably — by a goat that ate my lockpicks and then looked at me like I owed it money. So I know the anatomy of an ambush: the held breath, the wrongness of a quiet road, the moment your own body betrays you by going still.

What I did not expect was the crossbow bolt thudding into the tree beside my head, quivering, fletched in council grey.

"Good morning," said Sera Vance.

She stepped out of the bracken the way other people step out of carriages — unhurried, immaculate, her leathers brushed and oiled as though she hadn't spent three days tracking us through the valley's spine. Behind her, half-shadowed among the pines, four hunters in matching grey. Ansel's hand found my shoulder and pressed me back a half-step, which would have been romantic if it weren't so insulting. I'd been dodging professionals since he was learning to shave.

"Sera," Ansel said. His voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man greeting someone he'd shared a mess hall with for ten years. "You're a long way from your patrol."

"And you're a long way from your oath." Her eyes flicked to me, cool and cataloguing, the way I look at a purse to decide whether it's worth cutting. "The council has voted, Yarrow. You're declared. Both of you. Bring in the thief and the memory, or be brought in."

"You could try," Ansel said.

"I could," she agreed pleasantly. "Four of me against one of you, and the wolf-shape costs you something every hour you hold it. I've read the reports. You've been running on three hours' sleep." She smiled without warmth. "But let's not pretend this is about capture. Put the crossbows down, girls. We're here to talk."

The bolts lowered. My heart did not get the message; it kept up its stupid drumming against my ribs.

"A talk," I said. "From the council. The same council that voted to burn the only thing keeping the sky attached to the world. Forgive me if I check the tea for poison."

"The council doesn't know I'm here." Sera walked closer, boots silent on the pine needles, and stopped just outside what I judged — professionally — to be arm's reach. Smart woman. "This is my offer. Mine alone."

Ansel shifted beside me. I felt him weighing her, the way he weighed everything: threat first, person second. It occurred to me, not for the first time, how exhausting it must be to love the way he loved — all in, no exits — while living in a body built for suspicion.

"Say it, then," he said.

Sera Vance looked at him for a long moment. Then at me. And when she spoke again, the polish cracked, just slightly, like ice over moving water.

"My sister is beyond the wards."

* * *

**Ansel**

I knew about Maren Vance. Everyone in the warden corps knew about Maren Vance, though we were not supposed to. Nine years ago, when the covenant sealed and the wards went up along the border, there had been names read out in the great hall — the trapped, the lost, the ones who had been outside the valley when magic closed like a fist. Families wept. The council called it the price of safety and never said the word *sacrifice* where anyone grieving could hear.

Maren Vance had been seventeen. A field trip to the hot springs past the ridge, delayed by weather. When the wards fell into place she had simply — ceased, from the valley's perspective. A girl who walked out of one country and could never walk back in.

Sera had joined the hunters within the year. Nobody said why. Everybody knew.

"She's alive?" I asked.

"I don't know." Sera's jaw worked. "That's the truth of it, Yarrow, and I'll thank you not to make me say it twice. But last autumn, before the wards failed, a trader came through the pass with a letter. Smuggled, charmed, whatever. It was in her hand." Her voice stayed level. Her eyes didn't. "She wrote that the outer powers keep people. Not kill them — *keep* them. They trade in them. And now the wards are down and the council has had its panic and its votes, and do you know what they decided?"

I did know. I had heard the vote through the wardhouse door while I was deciding how much of my life I was willing to burn. But I let her say it, because some things need saying aloud to become real.

"They want to give the dragon-memory to the outer powers," Sera said. "Trade it. A dying flame for living hostages. Let the covenant fall all the way, let magic flood back into the valley, and buy our people home with the last breath of the last dragon." She spread her hands. "And you two are running north with the coin they mean to spend."

* * *

**Xanthe**

Well.

There is a particular kind of silence that follows a sentence like that — the kind where everyone waits to see if you'll laugh, or cry, or do arithmetic. I did arithmetic. I'm a thief; it's what my hands are for.

One memory. One dying flame. However many souls beyond the wards — dozens, the archivist had said, maybe a hundred, scattered across nine years of sealed borders. Against: the covenant, the valley, the stars, the continued existence of everything, roughly speaking.

It was almost a fair trade. That was the horror of it. It wasn't greed or madness driving the council; it was grief wearing a sensible coat. I recognized the outfit. I'd sold enough memories to know that grief will pay any price and call it reasonable.

"And if you give them the flame," I said slowly, "and they still don't return your sister? If they take the last fire of the last dragon and laugh?"

"Then I'll have traded everything for nothing," Sera said, "and at least I'll have tried. Can you say the same? You're carrying a dead god's last thought in a lead box because you stole it by accident and now you've decided you're its guardian. What gives you the right, Marchetti? What makes your plan holier than ours?"

"Because mine doesn't end the world."

"Mine ends it *for someone*," she snapped, and for a heartbeat the hunter was gone and there was just a woman with a sister's name worn smooth as a river stone inside her chest. "You talk about the world like it's evenly distributed. It isn't. The world, for me, is a girl who went to the hot springs and never came home. The world, for the cobbler's boy taken in the first year, is his mother, who leaves a bowl out for him every night because forgetting is the one thing the covenant couldn't make her do." She drew a breath. Steadied. The hunter reassembled herself piece by piece, like armor going on. "So. My offer. Surrender the memory to me — to me, not the council, I'll take it to the negotiation myself — and you walk free. Full pardon, papers signed under my seal. Ansel too, if he wants it. No wardhouse, no trial, no burning. Just give it up."

* * *

**Ansel**

I watched Xanthe's face while Sera talked, and I want to record, for the honesty of this account, that I saw her consider it.

Of course I did. Anyone who claims Xanthe Marchetti is made entirely of iron has never actually met her. Iron is what she wraps around the soft parts. Underneath, she is the person who broke her one rule for a grieving father, who carries strangers' worst nights in her skull so they don't have to, who looked at a village that forgot its own name and grieved for it as though she'd known it personally. A woman like that hears a hundred names behind Maren Vance's name. A woman like that does arithmetic with lives, and hates every column.

"No," she said finally. Quietly. Not cruelly. "Not because your sister matters less than my plan, Sera. Because your plan doesn't work."

"You don't know that."

"I steal memories for a living. It's the one expertise I have." Xanthe stepped forward, out from behind my shoulder, and I let her, because she was right and because watching her be brave had always done something ruinous to my composure. "The outer powers fed on the old covenant. They engineered the wards falling — Odile told us, and Odile has no reason left to lie. They didn't break the wards to get *in*. They broke them to make us come *out*, carrying the flame to them. Your council isn't negotiating. It's delivering. They'll take the memory, keep your sister anyway — hostages are worth more than gold, Sera, ask any thief — and let the last dragon-fire die in their vault while the valley drowns in everything the covenant kept out."

"You can't know—"

"I know buyers," Xanthe said. "Nine years of them. I know what a buyer says to close a deal, and I know what a buyer does after the deal closes. There is no version of this where they hand her back. There's barely a version where they let your council leave."

Sera stared at her. And I saw the terrible thing happen — the thing I'd seen happen to good soldiers given bad orders. Doubt. Not the clean doubt that questions everything, but the filthy kind that arrives too late, after you've already marched.

"You're lying," Sera said, but softly, testing the weight of her own words and finding them hollow.

"I lie beautifully," Xanthe said. "Ask anyone. But not about this. This is the one honest thing I've said in nine years, and I hate every second of it."

* * *

**Xanthe**

Here is what nobody tells you about being the bearer of bad truths: you start to envy liars. Liars get to sleep.

Sera Vance stood in the pine-shadow with her sister's whole world balanced on her next word, and I understood her more than I wanted to. I had, after all, once erased my own past rather than carry it. She was being asked to do the opposite — to carry a past she couldn't fix, forward, forever, without relief. That's harder. Forgetting is a mercy. Remembering is a job.

"If I go back empty-handed," she said at last, "the council sends the full hunt. Not four hunters. Forty. Wardens, hounds, the scrying corps. They'll run you down inside a week."

"Then don't go back empty-handed," Ansel said.

We both looked at him. He had that expression he got — I remembered getting it, suddenly, a flash of wolf-light and a younger face — when he'd found the third path nobody else had bothered to look for.

"Come with us," he said. "You've seen the things coming through the dead wards. You know what the outer dark is. Take what we know back to the council — the truth, the whole of it, the ash court, the trap. Make them listen. You're Vance. They trust your name even when they don't trust anything else."

"And if they don't listen?"

"Then you'll have tried," Xanthe said gently, "with both eyes open. Isn't that what you said? At least I'll have tried. Try *this* instead. It costs less blood."

Sera laughed — one short, bitter note, nothing amused in it. "You're asking me to choose between my sister and the world, and offering me a third option that's really just the world with extra steps."

"Yes," Xanthe said. "Welcome to having a conscience. It's awful. There's no cure."

For a long moment the only sound was wind in the pines and, far off, too far off to be near and too wrong to ignore, something crying in a register birds don't use. Sera's hunters shifted. Her hand flexed on the crossbow.

"I should put a bolt through your throat," she told me, conversationally. "It would solve several problems."

"It would," I agreed. "Though I should mention the memory is bound to me now. Fused. Kill me and you kill the seed of the new flame along with the thief. The council's own archivists confirmed it before your friends burned Odile's library." A small lie. A beautiful lie. Odile had confirmed nothing of the sort. But Sera didn't need truth; she needed a reason to lower the crossbow that her pride could live with, and I have built a career supplying exactly that.

The crossbow lowered.

"I'm not helping you," Sera said. "Understand that. I'm not joining your little pilgrimage. I'm going back to Hollowmere to fight for my sister with words, because two thieves with nice eyes made me doubt my orders, and I hate you both for it." She turned, then paused. "Yarrow. The council knows where you're headed. The Ember Altar. They also know the northern passes, and they know what comes out of them at night." Her voice dropped. "Whatever you two are to each other — and it's written on you both like a brand — settle it fast. You won't get time for tenderness where you're going."

They melted into the trees, grey on grey, gone the way professionals go.

* * *

**Ansel**

We stood in the clearing a while after they'd gone. The wrong bird-thing cried again, nearer now, or maybe just more insistent. The light was going amber and long, and I caught myself thinking that I had watched a thousand evenings in this valley and never once noticed how beautiful they were until I started counting how many might be left.

"You lied to her," I said. "About the fusion."

"Mm." Xanthe was already moving, checking the lead box at her hip with fingers that pretended to be steady. "Does it bother you?"

"It should."

"But it doesn't."

"No," I admitted. "It doesn't. That's the part that bothers me."

She glanced up at me then, and there it was — the flicker, the ghost of recognition that had been surfacing more often these past days, like something rising through deep water. Not memory, exactly. Not yet. Something older than memory and harder to kill.

"Ansel," she said. "When she offered us freedom. Papers. Pardons. Walk away, live, forget—" Her jaw tightened. "Why didn't you take it? You could have. She'd have signed for you. You'd be done with me, done with the oath-breaking, done with all of it."

I thought about lying. I'm apparently capable of it now; marriage to a thief changes a man.

"Because I already walked away from you once," I said. "Nine years ago. I told myself it was mercy — letting you go rather than dragging you to the wardens. And I have repeated that story to myself every night since, and every night it has fit worse." I looked north, toward the border, toward the ember I could almost feel guttering on the horizon like a coal under ash. "I'm not doing it twice. Whatever this costs — the oath, the corps, the valley's good opinion — I'm finished choosing the version of the story where I lose you and call it duty."

She didn't answer. She never answered well when the words got big; I'd remembered that much, and the remembering hurt in the good way, the way a set bone aches while it knits.

Instead she touched the lead box, once, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you didn't fully trust. And above the treeline, in the band of sky where stars should have been beginning to show, the darkness had a texture now. A movement. Like something vast turning over in its sleep, or waking.

"We should move," Xanthe said. "Off the ridge. Down where there's stone around us."

"Why?"

"Because," she said, "that sound isn't a bird, it hasn't been a bird for an hour, and it's been following us since noon."

I listened properly this time — dropped into the wolf-part of my mind, the part that counts heartbeats in the grass. And there it was beneath the wind: not one cry, but many, layered, patient, circling wide to cut us off from the low ground.

Things with too many joints, Odile had called them. I had hoped she was being poetic.

"Run," I said.
