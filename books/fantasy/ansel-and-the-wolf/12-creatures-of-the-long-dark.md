## 12. Creatures of the Long Dark

There is a saying among wardens: *the wolf keeps you; you do not keep the wolf.* We say it to recruits on their first night of shifting, when they come back to their own skin weeping and swearing they'll never do it again, and then they always do it again, because a warden who cannot run as a wolf cannot hold the border, and a warden who cannot hold the border might as well be a farmer with a nice sword.

What we don't say to recruits — what no one ever says out loud, because it would empty the order in a season — is that every warden knows one morning he will shift and not shift back.

I had been in wolf-shape for six hours when I started forgetting my own name.

* * *

We had made eleven miles up the valley's spine before dark caught us, which by Ansel Yarrow's standards was respectable and by Xanthe Marchetti's standards was an outrage she intended to file formally at the first available authority.

"There should be inns," she said, dropping onto a shelf of granite with the particular gracelessness of someone whose feet have stopped negotiating. "Inns. With beds. This is a valley full of people, and yet somehow the entire landscape has conspired to be nothing but rock and spite."

"The villages are in the lowlands," I said. "The spine's the only route the hunters won't watch."

"Ah yes. The hunters. My favorite recurring characters." She pulled off a boot and shook river water out of it, glaring at the boot as though it had personally betrayed her, which, to be fair, everything had lately. "And the things behind us? The ones with too many elbows? Do those have a name, or does the realm just call them *the things with too many elbows*?"

"We call them the long dark."

She looked at me. "That's not a name, that's a mood."

"It's accurate," I said. "The outer powers don't send creatures so much as they send *absence*. Things shaped like whatever the dark found last. They've been following us since the gorge."

"I know," she said quietly. "I've felt them. Like walking past an open grave." She flexed her bare foot against the stone. "So we keep moving all night?"

"We keep moving until I say otherwise, and then we move some more."

"Warden Yarrow, if I didn't know better I'd say you enjoy giving orders."

"I don't enjoy anything anymore. It's the oath-cord. It sands a man down to duty and grumbling."

"Liar. You enjoyed the rabbit I cooked this afternoon. You had thirds."

"That was hunger, not enjoyment."

"You made a sound."

"I did not make a sound."

"You made a sound like a man being forgiven for something."

I turned away before she could see my face do whatever it was doing. Nine years I had carried her memory like a coal banked in ash — never dead, never quite warm enough — and now she walked beside me making jokes about rabbits, remembering fragments, remembering *some*, and every hour I spent not telling her the whole of it felt less like patience and more like theft. I, who had arrested memory-thieves for a living, hoarding the one memory that wasn't mine to keep.

But that confession belonged to a fire and a quiet hour, and quiet hours were a currency we didn't have. Because the wards were down, and the long dark was patient, and I could feel it behind us the way you feel a storm building behind a mountain — pressure in the teeth, wrongness in the air.

I shifted into wolf-shape as the last light went. It was practical: my nose worked better than my eyes after sundown, and my four legs outran her two, and if something came out of the treeline I wanted to meet it at the front rather than fumble for a sword. Practical. That's what I told myself.

It is always what I tell myself.

* * *

Here is what holding wolf-shape feels like, for those fortunate enough never to have done it:

Imagine your mind is a house with many rooms. Your name lives in one room. Your mother's face in another. The taste of bread, the sound of rain, the specific grief of a specific Tuesday — rooms, all of them, each with its own small lamp burning.

Now imagine that every hour you spend as a wolf, someone walks through that house snuffing lamps. Not breaking anything. Just — turning them off, gently, room by room, and locking the doors behind them. And the strange mercy of it is that you don't notice the rooms going dark. You notice only that the house feels smaller, cozier, simpler. A house with fewer rooms needs less tending. A mind with fewer names needs less grieving.

The wolf doesn't take anything from you. That's the lie we tell ourselves. The wolf just offers you a smaller house, and every time, some tired part of you says *yes, thank you, yes.*

By midnight I had lost the name of my home village. By two I had lost my mother's face — I knew I'd had one, the way you know a book you've read, but the pages wouldn't open. By three I was losing language itself; words became heavy stones I carried instead of used.

And through all of it, one lamp refused to go out. One room stayed lit no matter how many times the dark came through it.

A woman's voice, saying words I couldn't quite hear. A promise made in wolf-light. A name — hers, hers, *hers* — burning like a coal that would not bank.

Xanthe. Even as the rest of me dissolved, that name held. Which should have frightened me more than it did. Instead it felt like standing in a doorway while the house burned around me, watching one room stay lit, and thinking: *well. If that's all that survives, at least it's the right thing.*

That is how the wolf wins, incidentally. Not by taking everything. By convincing you that almost-nothing is enough.

* * *

They came an hour before dawn, when the night is thinnest and the body coldest, because the long dark understands timing the way a good hunter understands timing, and I have never decided whether that means they are intelligent or merely old.

I smelled them first — or rather, I smelled the *lack* of them, a hole in the world's scent where scent should have been, moving upslope through the pines. Then the forest went silent in a spreading circle, birdsong cut off mid-note, insects simply ceasing, as though the night were being erased from the edges inward.

I put myself between the dark and Xanthe before I had finished deciding to. That's the other thing about the wolf: it makes decisions faster than the man does, and the man wakes up already committed.

"Ansel?" Her voice, sharp with sleep. "Ansel, what—"

Then she saw them.

They came out of the trees like ink poured upward. I want to describe them properly, because Xanthe asked me once, later, and I gave her a soldier's answer — *tall, wrong, too many joints* — and she said that wasn't good enough, so here is the true answer: they were shaped like regret. Tall as doorways, thin as promises, limbs folding in directions that suggested they had been designed by something that hated geometry. They had no faces, only smooth dark where faces go, and where they stepped, the ground forgot how to be ground. Grass grayed under their feet. Stone went soft. One of them paused over a fallen pine branch and the branch simply stopped having ever been a branch.

The covenant had kept these things out of Hollowmere for nine hundred years. I had grown up on stories of them, told to frighten children toward the firelight. Nothing in the stories prepared me for the silence they carried, the way sound died around them like a candle in a closed fist.

There were five. There are always five, in my memory of that night, though Sera Vance later swore there were nine, and Xanthe swore seven, and perhaps we each counted only the ones we could bear to look at.

I did what wardens do. I hit the leftmost one at a full run.

Understand: in wolf-shape, I am fast enough to catch falling leaves. I have taken down stags twice my weight. I have fought men, monsters, and once, memorably, a very confused bear. And the first creature of the long dark I ever struck — I hit it like a hillside hitting a shadow, jaws closing on a throat that wasn't quite there, and it *folded* around me, joints bending backward to wrap me in cold, and it did not bleed and it did not cry out and it did not care.

Worse: where it touched me, I felt rooms going dark. Not slowly, the way the hours had been dimming me. All at once. It touched my foreleg and the memory of my father's hands teaching me to whittle simply *ended*. It raked my shoulder and I lost the smell of woodsmoke, permanently, I think, even now. It was not trying to kill me. Killing is quick and killing is honest. It was trying to *subtract* me, piece by piece, until there was nothing left to fight with.

So I did the arithmetic every warden dreads. Five of them. One of me, and fading. A woman behind me who could not run fast enough to escape them and could not fight them at all.

The math said: hold the line. Hold it as long as possible. Die as a wolf, because dying as a wolf at least means you die without feeling it.

The math said: let the wolf keep you.

And God help me, some deep cellar-part of me — the part that had been tired for nine years, tired of oaths and borders and carrying a love like a stone in the chest — that part *wanted* it. Wanted the small house. Wanted the simple mind. Wanted to stop being Ansel Yarrow, failed warden, traitor to his post, man who let his heart walk out of the world and then spent nearly a decade pretending that was duty instead of cowardice.

The wolf offered me that. *Put it down,* the wolf said, in the voice the wolf uses, which is no voice at all, just the absence of burden. *Put down the name. Put down the guilt. Put down her face. Run.*

I ran.

Not away from the fight — some stubborn knot of training kept my body circling the camp, keeping myself between the dark and Xanthe even as my mind unraveled. But I ran inside myself, down through the rooms of my own house, and the doors were slamming, and the lamps were going out, and I could feel my human shape becoming a story I'd once been told rather than a thing I was.

Somewhere above me, far away, a woman was screaming my name.

I almost didn't recognize it. That's the truth I've never told anyone. She screamed *Ansel* and the word arrived in my shrinking mind like a coin dropped down a well, and I thought: *someone is calling someone. I hope whoever it is answers.*

* * *

Later, Xanthe would tell me what she saw, and I will set it down here because I can't trust my own memory of it — the dark had gotten into my recollections by then, editing as it went.

She saw a wolf fighting five shadows, and the wolf was winning nothing and losing everything. She saw it stagger, circle, stagger again, and she saw — this is the part she says she'll never forgive herself for taking three full seconds to understand — she saw the wolf begin to forget how to be a wolf. Limbs forgetting their angles. Spine forgetting its curve. A shape losing confidence in its own outline, flickering between wolf and something formless and gray, like smoke trying to remember a statue.

She saw a warden dissolving in front of her, and she understood — faster than I did, faster than the wolf wanted her to — exactly what was happening and exactly what it would cost if she did nothing.

And so Xanthe Marchetti, thief of Hollowmere, who had broken every rule she'd ever set for herself except the ones that mattered, stood up in the middle of a battlefield of the long dark, cupped her hands around her mouth, and shouted the words of a promise she didn't remember making.

"*Whatever you become, come back to me as yourself.*"

The words went through me like a blade going through ice.

I don't know how she knew them. She swears she didn't — that they rose out of her the way water rises out of a spring, from somewhere below memory, somewhere the thieves and the dark and the years hadn't reached. Perhaps that's what a promise truly is: not a memory but a shape pressed into you, deeper than recall, that holds even when everything else gives way. Perhaps that's why the lamp in that one room never went out. It wasn't a memory at all. It was a load-bearing wall.

*Whatever you become, come back to me as yourself.*

Nine years ago, on the border, in wolf-light, a young warden had said those words to a girl about to cross into a valley that would steal her past. He had meant it as a gift: permission to change, to survive, to become whatever the valley needed her to be — so long as somewhere underneath, she stayed herself. He had not known he was also forging a key. He had not known that one day he would be the one dissolving, and that the words would come back to him out of her mouth like a rope thrown into deep water.

I caught it. God forgive my pride in saying it plainly: I caught the rope.

Because here is what the wolf never understood, what the long dark never understood, what the covenant itself in all its nine hundred years of enforced forgetting never understood: you cannot subtract a person from a promise. You can burn the memories, lock the doors, snuff every lamp in the house — but if somewhere in the ruins one lit room remains, one voice still saying *come back as yourself*, then the house is not empty. The house is waiting.

I came back the way drowning men surface — violently, painfully, all at once, lungs full of my own name. Ansel. Warden. Traitor. Man. The rooms lit one after another like windows at dusk: my mother's face, woodsmoke, bread, rain, the specific grief of a specific Tuesday. I landed in my own skin on my hands and knees in the frost, naked and shaking and human, with the taste of blood in my mouth that turned out to be my own, bitten through in the struggle to stay a wolf and become a man in the same breath.

The long dark recoiled. I learned something in that moment that I have never had occasion to report to any warden, since there were no wardens left to report to: the creatures of the outer dark fear nothing except a soul that refuses to be simplified. They feed on subtraction, on the slow dimming of complicated hearts. And a man who surfaces from dissolution *shouting* — I was shouting, I realized, her name, over and over, like a bell — a man like that burns their kind the way sunlight burns fog.

They folded backward into the treeline, unhurried, offended, gone. The forest breathed. Somewhere a bird, badly shaken by events, resumed singing in the wrong key.

* * *

Xanthe knelt beside me and put her cloak over my shoulders, and for a long moment neither of us said anything, because there are moments when speech would be like striking a match in a room full of spilled powder.

Then, because she is who she is: "You're heavier than you look. As a wolf, I mean. When you collapsed. I nearly dislocated something dragging you behind that rock."

"Noted. I'll diet."

"And you bit your tongue bloody, which is disgusting, and you're bleeding from approximately everywhere, and—" Her voice cracked, just once, a hairline fracture she covered immediately, the way she covered everything. "You were going to stay a wolf. Weren't you. You were letting it happen."

I looked at my hands. Human hands, scarred, shaking. "The math said—"

"Don't give me the math. I've heard the math. Duty, the border, the greater good." She crouched in front of me, and her eyes were fierce and wet and closer than they had been in nine years. "Say the other thing. The true thing."

"The true thing," I said slowly, "is that I was tired. Nine years tired. And the wolf offered me a smaller house, and I wanted to live in it."

Something moved across her face — recognition, maybe, or its ghost. "I know that offer," she said quietly. "I took it once. Sold my own past room by room so the house would stop hurting." She looked at her hands, the thief's hands, quick and scarred in their own way. "It doesn't work. You just end up haunted by furniture you can't remember owning."

"Is that what I am? Furniture?"

Her mouth twitched despite everything. "You're a badly made table. Lopsided. Too much sentimental carving."

"Careful. I'm wounded and armed."

"You're naked and shivering."

"Also true."

She sat back on her heels and studied me, and I watched her do it — watched her search my face the way she must have searched a thousand locked vaults, looking for the thing worth stealing, except this time she was looking for something she'd already stolen and given back without knowing. "Those words," she said. "*Come back as yourself.* I knew them. Ansel, I *knew* them — they came out of me like I'd rehearsed them in my sleep for years. Where do I know them from?"

And there it was. The fire we'd been promised ourselves, the quiet hour, the confession — delivered at last, in the worst possible place, at the worst possible time, on a frozen mountainside with the long dark listening somewhere in the trees and dawn still two hours off.

I could have lied. I was good at lying; the oath-cord had taught me discretion the way rivers teach stones to be smooth. I could have said *you heard it once, from a warden, on the border — it's a common phrase among my order.* Half-true. Painless. Deferring the wound to a kinder hour that would never come.

But she had just dragged my dissolving body behind a rock. She had just shouted a nine-year-old promise into the dark and pulled a man out of a wolf by the roots. She had earned the whole truth, and the whole truth was owed, and I was done — done — letting debt masquerade as kindness.

"From me," I said. "You know them from me. I said them to you nine years ago, on the border, the night you crossed into Hollowmere. The night you stole from the dragon. The night I let you go instead of arresting you." I pulled the cloak tighter, mostly so my hands would have something to do. "We knew each other, Xanthe. Before the valley took your memories. We knew each other *well*."

The silence that followed was long, and cold, and full of stars — real stars, I noticed distantly, the first I'd seen since the wards fell, a few hard points of light rekindling overhead as if the sky itself were leaning in to listen.

"A warden," she said at last, very carefully, "and a thief."

"Yes."

"And you loved her. The woman I was."

It was not a question, but it deserved an answer anyway. "I love her still," I said. "Whoever she turns out to be."

Above us, unnoticed by either of us in that moment — though Odile, miles south, would later swear she felt it happen — the guttering ember of the dragon-fire on the horizon flared, briefly, like a coal breathed upon.

And below us, at the tree line, something else stirred: not the long dark, but lantern-light. Many lanterns, moving in disciplined formation up the valley's spine, closing fast.

Council hunters. Sera Vance's company. And they had found us.

Xanthe saw them a heartbeat after I did. She stood, pulling me up by the arm, and muttered, "Of course. Why should we get one quiet conversation? Just *one*."

"Welcome to my life," I said, and shifted — carefully, deliberately, holding my name in my teeth like a bone — and we ran.
