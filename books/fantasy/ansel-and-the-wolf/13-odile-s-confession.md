## 13. Odile's Confession

I want it on the record that I smelled her before I saw her.

Not in a romantic way. Odile smells like cold paper and lamp oil and, faintly, of the tallow candles she insists on burning even though she can't see them gutter — a habit she calls "courtesy to the light." Nine years of buying stolen memories from me had taught my nose that particular scent the way a dog learns its own name: not with understanding, but with dread.

We'd made camp in the hollow beneath a leaning shelf of granite, halfway up the valley's spine, where the wind came down off the ridge smelling of snow and something older underneath it. Ansel had gone to check our backtrail — he did that every hour now, with the grim thoroughness of a man who had recently learned that his backtrail required checking — and I was sitting with the dragon-memory wrapped against my chest like a colicky infant, trying very hard not to think about what was inside it.

That was when the smell arrived, riding down the wind ahead of its owner.

"Ansel," I said, before my brain caught up with my mouth. He wasn't there. Of course he wasn't there. He was never there at the exact moment you wanted a wolf the size of a pony standing between you and trouble.

The footsteps came anyway. Slow, deliberate, each one placed like a coin on a counter. A blind woman doesn't stumble; she announces.

"You're a long way from your archive," I said to the dark.

"So are you," said Odile Marchetti — no relation, whatever the gossips claimed, though we'd both stopped correcting people years ago because the fiction was more interesting than the truth. "And you've brought it with you. I can feel it from here. Like standing near an open oven door."

She came into the firelight. She looked terrible, which for Odile meant she looked exactly as composed as always, but her hands were wrong. Odile's hands were always right — they read braille faster than most people read print, they folded documents with surgical precision, they once reassembled a shattered memory-vial while three councilmen shouted at her. These hands were shaking.

"You followed us," I said. "Through hunter country. Blind."

"I had nine years to memorize the route I hoped you'd take." She lowered herself onto the stone across the fire with the care of someone whose knees had opinions. "You went north along the spine. You avoided the lowlands. You didn't stop at Thornhollow, which was wise, or at the ferry, which was wiser. You're doing what I would have done."

"That's not comforting."

"It shouldn't be."

* * *

Here is what you need to know about Odile, and what I knew then, and what I somehow failed to assemble into a single thought until that night by the fire:

Odile ran the Archive of Hollowmere. Every memory legally surrendered, confiscated, or sold in the valley passed through her hands. She was the one who taught me the trade — not the thieving part, that I brought myself, but the ethics of it. The rules. Never steal anything that matters. Never sell a memory twice. Never keep what isn't yours.

She'd said those rules to me across her desk, her blind eyes fixed slightly past my shoulder, the way they always were, and I had taken them as scripture because I was nineteen and desperate and she was the only person in Hollowmere who spoke to me like I might amount to something other than a cautionary tale.

Now she sat across my fire with shaking hands, and I watched her, and the arithmetic began.

"You knew," I said. "In your archive. When I brought you the widow's memory, when I brought you all of them over the years — you could feel what they were. You told me once you could taste a memory through the vial. You said mine tasted like 'rain on hot slate.'"

"They do." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Yours always have. It's why I hired you, if we're being honest. Memory-work runs in some bloodlines. Yours runs deep."

"And the dragon-memory. The one that's been sitting in my vault for nine years, fused to a night I don't remember having. You catalogued my acquisitions, Odile. You held that vial."

The fire popped. Somewhere above us, wind moved through the pines with a sound like a long exhale.

"Yes," she said.

"When?"

"Nine years ago. The autumn the wards first shivered."

* * *

I should explain about the wards' first shiver, because everyone in Hollowmere remembers it and no one talks about it. One night in late autumn, every candle in the valley guttered at once. Every clock lost four minutes. The dogs howled until dawn. The council announced it had been "a minor fluctuation, fully resolved," which is council language for *we have no idea and we would prefer you didn't either.*

I remembered it because it was the week I'd started selling memories full-time instead of occasionally, out of a back room, under a name that wasn't mine.

"The wards shivered," I said slowly, "and around the same time, I acquired a memory so cold it burned. And you catalogued it. And you said nothing."

"I said nothing," Odile agreed, "because saying something would have meant explaining why I helped you steal it."

The words landed in the space between us and just lay there, the way heavy things do.

"Say that again," I said. "Slowly."

She turned her face toward the fire. I realized, watching her, that this was a woman who had rehearsed this conversation for nine years, alone, in the dark, to an audience of nothing — and that all that rehearsal had bought her nothing, because the real version had a witness.

"Xanthe. Do you remember how you got the commission? The one that sent you to the border?"

"No," I said. "That's rather the point. My memory of that whole year has holes in it. I assumed I'd sold them off. I do that. I did that." My voice cracked on the tense and I hated it. "Someone paid me to go to the border. Someone paid me to steal from—" I stopped. "From the dragon. From the last dragon, whose dying flame holds the entire covenant together. And I didn't know who paid me because apparently I stole that too."

"You did," Odile said quietly. "From yourself. At my instruction."

* * *

Let me tell you what she told me, as much of it as I could bear to hold, sitting there with the dragon's last recollection warm against my ribs like a second heart.

Nine years ago, Odile had been younger — sixty-one instead of seventy, which in archivist years is the difference between a fortress and a ruin — and she had believed something. That was the part that frightened me most, listening. Not that she'd done it. That she'd *believed.*

She believed the covenant was a cage. Not the kind the histories described — the noble kind, the protective kind, the kind the council's schoolbooks drew as a warm blanket around the valley. She believed it was the kind of cage that gets built by the people inside it and called a gift.

"Hollowmere sealed magic out," she said, "and called it safety. But ask yourself what else it sealed out, Xanthe. Healing. The old roads. The ability to leave. We are a valley of people who cannot go anywhere and cannot be anything except what we were born, and we thank the dragon for it every festival day. The outer realms call us the Kept. Did you know that? I learned it from a smuggled book when I was thirty. The Kept. Like livestock."

"So you broke the fence."

"I arranged for the fence to be broken." Her voice didn't waver, and I admired that even as I hated it. "I couldn't do it myself. The theft had to be done by someone with thief's blood and no archive connections. So I found a girl with thief's blood, and I gave her a commission through intermediaries, and I made sure the payment was generous enough that she wouldn't ask questions. And then — because I am not a monster, whatever you're deciding I am right now — I made sure she wouldn't remember doing it. Because what you were going to carry out of that vault was going to hurt you, Xanthe. What you'd taken. What you'd given up to take it."

"What did I give up?" I asked, and my voice came out small, and I despised it.

Odile was quiet for a long moment.

"You know," she said finally. "Don't you. You found it in the vault. The fused memory."

"A night on the border," I said. "A young warden. Wolf-light. A promise." I kept my eyes on the fire because looking at her felt dangerous. "I stole my own past to hide the crime. Or you had me steal it. There's a difference and I'm struggling to find it."

"The difference," Odile said, "is that I ordered the erasure and you executed it, and we have both spent nine years pretending the other one decided."

* * *

Here is the thing nobody tells you about betrayal: it doesn't arrive like a knife. It arrives like an audit. You sit there going back through your own life, line by line, looking for the forgery, and the horror isn't any single entry. It's that you can't find where the true ledger ends.

Every kindness Odile had ever shown me rearranged itself. The job she'd given a desperate nineteen-year-old. The rules she'd taught me — *never steal anything that matters* — which I had broken exactly once, catastrophically, without knowing. Had she designed that too? Had she built my rulebook specifically so I'd break it? Was my entire moral architecture a piece of stagecraft she'd constructed around a theft?

"Why erase my memory of the commission?" I asked. "If I was your willing instrument—"

"Willing." She laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as moth wings. "You were willing the way soldiers are willing. You needed coin. I needed hands. But I also needed you clean afterward, because I'd read enough stolen memories to know what happens to thieves who know what they've stolen. They get careless. They get proud. They get caught. And if the council had caught you with the dragon's death in your vault—" She shook her head. "They'd have destroyed it. Or worse. They'd have used it. You see, I needed the memory loose in the world, unowned, unwatched. Sleeping in a thief's vault where no one would think to look."

"And then the wards fell anyway," I said, "nine years later, and suddenly your sleeping weapon woke up."

"Not fell," Odile said. "Were *let* fall."

The wind stopped. Or I stopped hearing it. One of those.

"Explain that sentence," I said, "very carefully."

"There are locks on the wards, Xanthe. Old ones, woven into the covenant itself — fail-safes, the founders called them, though fail-safe implies there's a direction that's safe. If the dragon-fire gutters below a certain strength, the wards loosen on their own. A mercy mechanism. A dying flame shouldn't imprison a valley it can no longer protect." She spread her shaking hands. "But there is another way to open them. A key. Woven from the same substance as the flame itself. Dragon-stuff. And do you know where the only dragon-stuff left in this world has been sleeping for nine years?"

My hand went to my chest. To the wrapped memory. To the thing I had been carrying north like a relic, like a duty, like the only seed that could regrow the fire.

"In my vault," I whispered.

"In your keeping. Where I put it." Her blind eyes found me with unnerving precision. "The wards didn't fail, child. They were opened. By me. Three nights before Ansel ever found wolf-sign at the border. I used a thread of the memory's own substance — a filament, barely a breath of it — to turn the founders' lock. I told myself it was time. I told myself the covenant was dying anyway and better a chosen ending than a collapse. I told myself the valley would be free."

"And instead the stars went out," I said, "and things with too many joints started coming through the dark, and the ash court — whatever they are — started hunting the very memory you loosed." I stood up. I hadn't decided to stand up. My body simply declined to remain seated across from her a moment longer. "You didn't free the valley, Odile. You rang a dinner bell."

* * *

She didn't flinch. I'll give her that. Seventy years of being the smartest person in every room had built a woman who could absorb almost anything without visible damage, and what she couldn't absorb she metabolized into argument.

"Do you think I don't know that?" she said, and for the first time her composure cracked, just at the seam, just enough to show the thing underneath. "Do you think I haven't lain awake every night since the stars went dark, doing sums? I wanted to give the valley magic and instead I may have given it to *them.* I wanted to end a cage and I opened a door onto the Long Dark. Yes. I know what I did." Her hands stilled in her lap, deliberately, the way you still your hands when they want to shake. "But understand what I was weighing, Xanthe. Because you will have to weigh it too, before this is over. The covenant was not free. It cost this valley everything, every single day, and it billed us in forgetting. Ask the villages without names. Ask the woman in the village without a name who thinks a stranger is her son because her actual son was edited out of her by a seal she thanks the dragon for. I chose to pay a different price. I chose wrong, perhaps. But I *chose*, which is more than the founders ever let any of us do."

"And the price you chose," I said, "is being paid by everyone else."

"Yes," she said simply. "That is what righteous causes do. They spend other people."

The honesty of it knocked the wind out of me. I had come into this conversation ready for excuses, for justification, for the elaborate self-forgiveness that clever people wrap around their crimes like cloth around a blade. Instead she handed me the naked fact, and it was worse, and somehow it was also the only thing she could have said that left a door open.

Because I had spent other people too. I'd spent a grieving widow's worst night for coin. I'd spent my own past for cover. I'd spent Ansel — gods, I'd spent Ansel, nine years of his carrying what I'd thrown away. The difference between Odile and me was scale, and scale is a coward's metric.

"Ansel's coming back," I said. "He'll smell you. And he knows what the wards' fall cost his order. His oath. Everything." I paused. "He also knows you were the closest thing I had to family in this valley, and he will have opinions about what you took from me."

"He has every right to them."

"That's suspiciously agreeable."

"I'm seventy, child, walking alone through hunter country to confess to two fugitives by their campfire. Agreeable is all I have left." She almost smiled. "Besides. I didn't come only to confess."

"Of course you didn't."

"I came because I know what's between you and the Ember Altar, and you're going to need me to survive it." She reached into her coat and drew out a flat case of oiled leather, holding it toward the firelight though she couldn't see it — offering it to me, to the light, to whoever was keeping accounts. "The founders wrote the covenant's terms into the altar itself. I've spent forty years reading everything the council allowed into the archive and a great deal they didn't. I know the altar's language. I know what it demands. And I know—" her voice dropped, and the shaking came back into her hands, and this time she let it, "—I know what it asked of the dragon. What the dragon actually said, at the end, that the histories don't record. Because I heard it. Forty years ago. From a memory that passed through my archive and was supposed to be destroyed, and wasn't, because I kept it, because I was already looking for a way to break the cage even then."

The fire burned. The wind resumed somewhere far above us, moving through the pines, carrying the smell of snow and old smoke.

"Odile," I said slowly, "what did the dragon say?"

She turned her ruined eyes toward the north, toward the border, toward the distant coal-glow of a dying star that only the two of us could feel pressing against the dark like a hand against a door.

"It said," she answered, "*ask me again in nine years,*" and somewhere down the slope, a wolf began to run toward our fire.
