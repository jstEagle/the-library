## 14. The Border of Stars

*Xanthe*

The ridge ended the way all good things end: abruptly, and with a view.

We climbed out of the gorge at dawn — or what Hollowmere called dawn, which was really just the wards deciding to let light in on schedule, punctual as a tax collector. Ansel went first in his wolf-shape, testing the scree, and I followed on two legs because my pride had survived everything else and I wasn't about to sacrifice it to gravity.

From the top, the valley spread out below us like something worth saving, which was inconvenient, because I'd spent nine years treating it like something worth escaping. Terraced fields stitched gold and green down the slopes. The memory-market's awnings were just colored dots from here, tiny and ridiculous, where I'd made my living unpicking other people's worst nights for coin. Smoke rose from a hundred chimneys in straight, obedient lines, because even smoke behaved itself inside the covenant.

"It looks smaller," I said.

"Everything does," Ansel said, "from above."

He'd shifted back to human somewhere behind me — I'd heard the wet, grinding sound of it and kept my eyes politely on the horizon, which is a lie; I'd looked, briefly, and then looked away faster. He stood now with his arms crossed against the wind, and there was frost in his dark hair that hadn't been there an hour ago, and he was watching the valley the way you watch a sleeping person you're about to wake up with bad news.

"You've done this before," I said. "Left."

"Once." He didn't elaborate. He never elaborated. Talking to Ansel was like drawing water from a well with a very narrow bucket — you got what you needed eventually, but you worked for it.

Ahead of us, maybe half a day's walk along the spine of the world, the border waited. I could feel it before I could see it, the way you feel a missing tooth with your tongue. A wrongness. A smooth place where something used to be.

Odile's voice came back to me over three days of hard travel: *The dragon gave its last fire to seal the valley, girl. Everything magic stays out. Everything forgotten stays forgotten. And the flame is dying, and you are carrying its seed in your head like a coin you stole without counting.*

I touched my temple. The vault behind my eyes — the one I'd built so carefully, shelf by shelf, stolen night by stolen night — felt different lately. Crowded. Warm in places it had no business being warm. Somewhere in there, folded up small, was the final recollection of the last dragon in the world, and it had started to *stir*, the way a sleeper stirs before waking, and I did not love that.

"Tell me again what crossing feels like," I said.

"I've only crossed once, and I was unconscious at the time."

"Wonderful. So we're pioneers."

"We're idiots," he said, "with good reasons," and started walking, and I followed, because following Ansel north was apparently the shape my life had decided to take.

* * *

*Ansel*

The old ward-stones began at noon.

They marched across the valley's throat in a broken line — standing stones taller than a man, each one carved with the covenant-script that had glowed, once, in colors no one alive remembered. My grandmother had described them to me when I was a boy: *blue as deep ice, boy, blue as a wolf's eye in winter.* Now they were gray. Dead gray. Lichen-gray. They looked less like a wall than like teeth left standing after the jaw had rotted out.

Xanthe stopped walking ten paces short of them and would not come closer.

I turned back. She was staring at the nearest stone with an expression I recognized, because I'd worn it myself for nine years: the look of someone doing arithmetic with their soul and not liking the total.

"I know this place," she said quietly.

"You crossed here. Nine years ago."

"No." Her voice had gone strange. "I mean I *know* it. There was—" She pressed her fingers to her temple again, hard, like she was trying to hold a door shut from the inside. "There was snow. There was someone arguing with me. Someone who said—" She stopped. Looked at me. Looked away. "It's gone. It slides off. Like trying to hold water."

Something in my chest did the thing it always did, the slow tearing of an old wound being politely reopened. She was standing on the exact ground where she had promised me she'd come back, and she couldn't remember promising, and the memory of it lived in me like a coal I'd carried so long I'd forgotten it burned.

"There was snow," I said. "You were wearing a grey coat with a torn pocket. You said the valley was a cage dressed up as a sanctuary. You said—" I stopped myself. Some things a man shouldn't hand back to a woman who can't catch them.

"What did I say?"

"That you'd be back by spring thaw."

She flinched. Actually flinched, like I'd struck her, and I understood — I did — that the cruelty wasn't mine. The covenant had taken it from her, or she had sold it, or she had buried it so deep even she couldn't dig it up, and either way the words landed on her like weather from a country she'd never visited.

"Well," she said, with the brittle brightness I was learning meant *do not touch this*, "clearly spring thaw and I need to have a conversation about punctuality."

Beyond the stones, the world changed.

I'd seen it once, half-dead, dragged through the breach by warden-brothers who hadn't survived the year. But seeing it whole — seeing it under an open sky — was another thing entirely. The outer realm didn't end at the border. It *rose*. Forests that moved when the wind moved them, actually moved, swaying like slow animals. Rivers that ran uphill in silver threads toward mountains that breathed — visibly breathed, their snowlines expanding and contracting like the flanks of sleeping beasts. And overhead, where Hollowmere's sky was a painted ceiling of soft, dependable dusk, the outer sky went up forever, black and enormous and—

Empty.

That was the part that stopped us both. No stars. Not clouded — *empty*, a darkness so complete it had texture, depth, a kind of hungry architecture. Except far away, near the horizon's northern edge, one point of light. Dim. Red-orange. Flickering.

The ember. The dragon-fire, guttering at the heart of the world, visible now that nothing else shone.

"Oh," Xanthe said. Very softly. "Oh, that's what we're doing. We're walking toward the last lit thing in the sky."

"Yes."

"And everything else is—"

"Eating the dark," I said. "Or being eaten by it. Depends on your politics."

She laughed — one sharp, startled note — and I realized I'd missed that sound the way you miss a limb. Then she squared her shoulders, looked at the dead ward-stones, and stepped between them.

Nothing happened. That was the horror of it. The stones should have flared, should have sung, should have turned her back or turned her to ash. They just stood there, gray and silent, while a thief walked through the corpse of the covenant like it was a doorway in a ruin.

I followed her through.

* * *

*Xanthe*

Crossing felt like nothing, and then it felt like everything.

For three steps, nothing. The dead wards passed beside me like fence posts. And then my foot came down on outer-realm soil and the world *inhaled*.

Magic. That's the only word I have, and it's a bad word, the way "ocean" is a bad word for what drowns you. Inside the valley, magic was a rumor, a story grandmothers told, a thing sealed out so thoroughly that even the word had gone soft from disuse. Out here it was *air*. It was pressure. It poured into me through the soles of my boots and the palms of my hands and the thin skin of my throat, and every stolen memory in my vault woke up at once.

I went to my knees in the grass.

Grass, I should say, that glowed faintly at the roots, veins of pale green light running through the soil like the land had a pulse. I barely noticed. I was busy drowning.

They all surfaced — the memories, mine and stolen both, sloshing around in my skull like cargo in a storm-tossed hold. The widow's worst night, cold and slick. The collector's purchased griefs. Odile's archive-smell. And underneath all of it, deeper than I'd ever been able to reach, something vast turning over in its sleep. Something with wings in it. Something that tasted of smoke and starlight and the long patience of mountains, and it was *warm*, and it knew I was there.

*Little thief,* the warmth seemed to say, in a voice like a banked fire settling. *You carry me carelessly.*

I screamed. I'm not proud of it. It wasn't a war cry; it was the noise a person makes when a stranger puts a hand on the back of their neck.

Then Ansel's arms were around me, hauling me back, and his heartbeat was slamming against my ear fast enough to count, and his voice was saying my name — not my market name, not Marchetti, but *Xanthe*, plain, the way you say a word you've said to yourself ten thousand times in the dark — and slowly, slowly, the tide went out.

When I could speak, I said, "It talked to me."

"The memory?"

"The dragon. Or the memory of the dragon. Or—" I pressed my palms into the glowing grass, grounding myself. "Ansel, it knows me. It's been in my head this whole time, and it *knows* me, and it called me a little thief, which frankly is fair but still rude."

His hands were still on my shoulders. I could feel him deciding whether to let go. He decided not to. I decided to allow it.

"Can you carry it?" he asked. "To the Altar. Can you carry it that far?"

I thought about lying. Old habit. Instead I said, "I don't know. It's getting heavier. Or closer. Those might be the same thing out here." I looked up at the starless sky, at that single dying ember hanging in all that black. "Everything out here is awake, isn't it? The grass. The mountains. The dark."

"Yes."

"And how much of it is friendly?"

He was quiet for a moment. In the ember-light his face was all shadow and one line of red.

"When I crossed last," he said, "the friendly parts were mostly dead."

"Wonderful. So we walk toward the only light in the sky, carrying the thing everyone wants, through a country where everything is hungry." I got my feet under me. My legs held. My vault churned. "You know, most thieves retire with a cottage and a cat."

"You could still turn back."

We both looked south, at the valley, small and golden and sealed behind its dead gray teeth. At the home that had never quite fit, that I'd picked locks around my whole life without realizing the lock was me.

"No," I said. And then, because honesty was apparently contagious out here: "And not just because of the world ending. Because I want to know what I forgot. Even if it ruins me." I glanced at him. "Especially if it ruins me."

Something moved in his expression — hope, maybe, handled carefully, like a live coal. He offered me his hand up, though I was already standing, and I took it anyway.

We walked north, toward the dying star, and behind us the grass closed over our footprints, glowing.

* * *

*Xanthe*

We made camp that night in the ribs of something enormous.

Ansel found it first — a sheltered hollow between two hills that weren't hills. Up close they were vertebrae, each the size of a cottage, arching out of the earth in a curve that had once, unimaginably, been a neck. A dragon's grave, or the start of one. The bones were warm. Not sun-warm. *Hearth*-warm, and when I laid my palm against one, the vault behind my eyes stirred again, that vast smoky presence rolling over like a dog hearing its name.

"He died here," I whispered. "Didn't he. Before the covenant. This is where they took the flame."

"This is where the wardens found him," Ansel said. He'd built a fire — actual fire, ordinary fire, and I could have kissed it. "The stories say he chose the valley. Chose it to die in, so his last breath would seal it. They say he was the last because the others flew into the dark and never came back." He fed the flames a branch. "They don't say why."

I sat down across from him, and the bone hummed faintly under me, and far off in the black sky the ember flickered, and I thought about everything I'd ever stolen. Griefs and regrets and one wedding night and a widow's worst morning. Small cruelties, priced fairly. I'd told myself for nine years I had a rule — *never steal anything that matters* — and I'd believed it right up until the moment I learned I'd robbed a dying god of his final thought and couldn't even remember doing it.

"Ansel." The fire cracked. His eyes came up, amber in the light, patient. "When we get to the Altar. If the flame needs more than the dragon's memory — if it needs *mine* —"

"Don't."

"I'm just asking what—"

"I know what you're asking." His voice had gone low and rough, the way it did when the wolf was close under his skin. "The answer is no. You don't get to spend yourself buying back a world that already took nine years from you. That's not sacrifice, Xanthe. That's just the theft going the other direction."

I stared at him. The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, something with too many joints clicked twice and went quiet, and neither of us reached for our weapons, because we'd both heard it often enough now to know it was only listening.

"And what would you call it," I asked, "if you spent yourself instead?"

He didn't answer. He looked into the fire, and the ember-light caught the oath-cord at his wrist — the braided warden's band that was his shape and his rank and, I was beginning to suspect, the last piece of the life he'd dismantled to drag me north — and he covered it with his other hand, casually, the way a man covers a wound.

"Sleep," he said. "I'll take first watch."

I lay down on the warm bone with my pack under my head and the vault churning gently behind my eyes, and just before sleep took me, the vast smoky presence surfaced one more time — not speaking, exactly. More like a hand resting briefly on the top of my head.

And it showed me, for exactly one heartbeat, a picture: a young woman in a grey coat with a torn pocket, standing between the ward-stones in falling snow, kissing a young man with amber eyes, promising him spring.

Then it was gone, and I was crying without knowing when I'd started, and Ansel was crouched beside me saying my name, and far to the north the ember in the sky guttered — dimmed — and did not, quite, go out.

"They're coming," Ansel said quietly, looking at the dark. "Whatever holds that court of ash. They felt us cross. They'll have felt *it* wake."

I wiped my face. I sat up. I looked at the single dying star that was our destination and our doom, and I said the truest thing I'd said in nine years:

"Then we'd better be worth the walk."
