## 15. The Court of Ash

They did not bind my hands. That was the first insult.

I'd expected rope, iron, the whole theatrical apparatus of capture. Instead, when the ash-crowned figures rose from their thrones of cooled lava and gestured, the ground itself opened beneath us—Ansel and me—and swallowed us whole, and spat us out in a hall so vast that the ceiling was a rumor and the floor was warm as a sleeping animal's flank.

No ropes. No chains. Just the dawning understanding that we had never once been walking toward the Ember Altar on our own recognizance. We had been *carried*, like a letter in a pocket, and now we'd been delivered.

"Charming," I said, because my mouth has always been faster than my sense of self-preservation. "I do love what you've done with the place. Very post-apocalyptic. Very *we have no interior decorator and we're not afraid to show it*."

The hall stretched away from us in columns of black glass, each one veined with faint red light that pulsed—slowly, arrhythmically, like a heart deciding whether to bother. And between the columns, they came.

There were seven of them. I counted because counting is what I do when I'm terrified; it gives the fear something to hold onto besides me. Seven lords, tall as doorways, wearing crowns that were not metal but ash compressed into shape by sheer will—crowns that shed a fine grey dust with every movement, so that they left little drifts of themselves behind them like snails leaving slime. Their faces were beautiful the way a burned forest is beautiful: technically, from a distance, if you didn't think too hard about what had been lost.

The one who came first—I would learn he was called Vael, though at the time I thought of him as *the tall one who smiles wrong*—stopped before us and inclined his head with the courtesy of a host greeting guests he intends to eat.

"The thief of Hollowmere," he said. His voice was warm. That was the worst part. It was genuinely, honeyedly warm, like a hearth in winter, and every instinct I owned told me that warmth in this place was a lure the way light is a lure for fish. "And her warden. We are honored."

"He's not my warden," I said. "He's more of an extremely persistent travel companion."

"Xanthe." Ansel's voice, low. A warning. He stood half in front of me, which was ridiculous—he was oathless now, merely human, merely muscle and stubbornness, and these things wore crowns made of the residue of gods—but he stood there anyway, and some traitorous part of my chest went soft about it.

Vael smiled. It did not reach anywhere near his eyes, which were the color of banked coals and about as reassuring. "You needn't be afraid. We have no wish to harm you. Quite the opposite." He spread his hands, shedding ash. "We have been waiting nine years for you, Xanthe Marchetti. Though admittedly, we expected you sooner."

"Traffic," I said. "You know how it is. Bandits. Wolves. Emotional revelations around campfires."

Something flickered across his face—not annoyance. *Amusement.* Which was worse. "Do you know what we are?" he asked.

"Lords of the outer dark," Ansel said flatly. "Eaters of magic. The reason the covenant exists."

"Eaters of magic." Vael tasted the words and found them undercooked. "Is that what the valley teaches its children? How quaint. How—" he paused, savoring it, "—*convenient*. For your founders, I mean. It is very useful, when you steal something, to describe the victim as a monster."

The red veins in the pillars pulsed. I felt it then, faintly, through the soles of my boots—a rhythm, vast and failing, like the heartbeat of something enormous trying to remember why it should keep going.

"You fed on the old covenant," I said slowly. The pieces were arranging themselves, the way stolen memories sometimes arranged themselves in my vault when I wasn't looking directly at them. "Before it sealed magic *out*. Before it became a wall. What was it, originally?"

"A bridge," Vael said simply. "A road. A great flowing exchange between realms—the dragon-fire at the center of it all, generous as sunlight. Magic moved freely. So did memory. So did *power*." His coal-dark eyes fixed on me. "And we drank from that flow, as everything did. All things drink, thief. The valley drinks rain. Your archivists drink knowledge. We drank magic. It is only drinking that your ancestors criminalized—because they wanted the well for themselves."

"And when they turned the bridge into a wall," I said, "you starved."

"We *thinned*," he corrected, with the mild pedantry of the truly ancient. "For three hundred years, we thinned. And then, nine years ago, a thief walked into the dying dragon's hoard and stole its last recollection—the seed of its flame—and carried it beyond the wards, into a valley where magic cannot follow. Do you understand what that theft accomplished? The flame could not reach its own seed. The fire began, at long last, to gutter." He smiled again, wider, ash drifting from his jaw. "We did not engineer the wards' fall, Xanthe. *You* did. We merely... encouraged the weather."

My stomach dropped through the warm floor and kept going. Nine years. Nine years since Odile put the job in my hands, since I crept into the dragon's hoard believing I was stealing from a dead thing, a spent thing, a *safe* thing. Nine years since I sold off my own past to hide the crime even from myself.

"You want the memory," Ansel said. His hand had found mine without either of us announcing it. "To extinguish the flame forever."

"To *end* it," Vael agreed, and his voice went reverent, which is a terrible thing to hear from a monster. "The fire is dying regardless, warden. Days, perhaps hours. When it dies, the covenant dies with it, and the realms crash together like lovers who have forgotten how to speak—and everything burns in the collision. Or—" he gestured, gracious, magnanimous, a merchant unveiling his finest goods, "—you give us the seed, and we end it *cleanly*. On our terms. Gently. No collapse. No dark pouring through the wound. Just... a long exhale. Rest."

"And what happens to the valley?" I asked. "To everyone inside it?"

"They live," he said. "Mortals live beautifully without magic, don't they? They've had three hundred years of practice. They'll lose nothing but a wall they never asked for."

Behind him, the other six lords had gone still in the way of predators conserving motion. The hall breathed its slow red pulse. And I stood there doing arithmetic, because that's what I do instead of panicking—arithmetic and jokes, my two shields, and one of them had already failed.

Because here was the horrible thing: he was *good*. Not kind—good at this. Every word landed like a coin counted into the palm. The flame was dying anyway. The collapse would be catastrophic. The valley's people would survive. And the alternative—my alternative—was a desperate run to an altar I'd never seen, carrying a seed I barely understood, against creatures who had just picked us up like luggage.

"I need a moment," I said.

"Of course." Vael stepped back with courtly grace. "Though before you confer—there is a matter we wished to discuss with you alone, thief. A private transaction. Warden, you'll forgive us."

"No," Ansel said.

"Ansel," I said quietly. "It's fine."

It was not fine. Nothing about it was fine. But the lords parted around him like water around a stone, and other shapes—attendants, or guards, or whatever passes for furniture in a court of ash—escorted him away down the black colonnade, glancing back at me until the dark took him.

* * *

They brought me to a smaller chamber, if a chamber can be called small when its ceiling vanishes into gloom. The walls here were not glass but memory-glass—I knew it on sight, the same substance the collectors in Hollowmere used to store their purchases, except these panes were the size of doors and there were hundreds of them, thousands, receding into the dark like a library that had learned to breathe.

"This," Vael said, "is what we took from the old flow, in the years before the wall. Memories, traded up from the valley's founders. Pledges. Ransoms. The occasional gift." He walked along the panes, trailing ash. "We have been poor these three centuries, thief. Starving on scraps. But we kept our collection. We are, if nothing else, *curators*."

He stopped before one pane and touched it, and it lit.

And I stopped breathing.

It was a market stall. My market stall—or rather, the stall I'd had before this one, nine years ago, younger wood, newer awning. And standing at it, haggling over a jar of preserved plums with a girl whose laugh I recognized the way you recognize your own handwriting, was—

Me.

Younger me. Whole me. The me who existed before I sold myself off in pieces, before the vault, before the hole in my chest shaped exactly like a life. The pane showed her laughing at her own joke, and beside her, half-turned away, a young man in a warden's grey with a wolf's watchfulness in his stance, watching her haggle like she was the only weather worth standing out in.

"That one is yours," Vael said softly. "Traded up through three intermediaries before the wall closed. We have wondered for three hundred years who would come to claim it. And you—" he turned those coal eyes on me, and his voice dropped into the register merchants use for the kill, the one I knew intimately because it was *my* register, "—you have so many others here. Would you like to see?"

I should have said no.

I am a professional. I know every trick in the trade, and this was the oldest one: show the mark what they've lost, let them hold it, and the weight of the empty hands does the rest. I have done it myself. I once sold a man his dead wife's laughter back for a season and watched him weep, and I told myself it was commerce.

Vael showed me eleven panes.

Eleven pieces of me. A first theft, clumsy, exhilarating, my fingers shaking as I lifted a stranger's afternoon. My mother's kitchen, which I had not known I'd lost until I saw it and felt the absence click into place like a bone resetting. The border at night—the wardens' fire, the wine, the promise made in wolf-light, Ansel's face young and open and *mine*, saying words I could feel the shape of but not the content, like reading a letter through frosted glass.

And threaded through all of them, faint as a watermark: the dragon. The hoard. The heat. The moment my hand closed around the last recollection of the last dragon while it still lived, still watched me, still *forgave* me—that was the worst of it, the forgiveness, I could feel it waiting in the panes like a blade wrapped in cloth.

"You sold yourself away," Vael murmured, watching me watch. "Piece by piece, to bury your crime. A remarkable discipline. A terrible price." He turned to face me fully, and the ash-crown on his head seemed to lean toward me like a plant toward sun. "We can give it all back. Every pane. Every year. The mother, the promise, the theft, the love—all of it, whole, restored, *yours*. You would wake tomorrow complete for the first time in nine years. You would know why your chest aches when the warden looks at you. You would know what you agreed to on the border, and whether you regret it."

The pane with the border-night still glowed beside me. Young Ansel's mouth was moving, making the promise, and I couldn't hear it, and I *ached*, physically, in the space behind my ribs where the missing years sat like a pulled tooth.

"And the price," I said. My voice came out steady. I'm good at that. It's the one thing I've always been good at—sounding fine. "The price is the dragon-memory."

"The seed," he agreed. "Which is dying in your keeping anyway. Which will burn out within days wherever it rests. Give it to its natural heirs, take back your life, and walk away whole. Everyone profits. Is that not your own creed, thief? Never steal anything that matters?" His smile widened, gentle, terrible. "Here is your chance to finally keep it."

I looked at the panes. All eleven of them. My whole scattered self, shelved and glowing, waiting for me like a house with the lamps lit.

And somewhere far below us, through the warm living floor, through miles of cooling stone, I felt it—the pulse. Faint. Failing. But *rhythmic*, and stubborn, and familiar now, the way a heartbeat becomes familiar when you've slept against someone's chest.

The dragon had watched me take its last recollection. Had forgiven me. Had spent three hundred years holding up a wall for people who forgot it existed—including, especially, me.

"Give me tonight," I said.

Vael bowed, ash falling like snow. "Of course. We are patient. We have been patient for three hundred years." At the doorway he paused, and added, almost kindly: "The warden, incidentally, is being shown our hospitality as well. Do hurry, thief. Hearts are such perishable things—in both senses."

Then I was alone with the panes, and my whole life glowed around me in the dark, and beneath my feet the last heartbeat of the last dragon counted down the hours like a thief counting another's purse, wondering how much longer before someone noticed it was empty.

I reached out and touched the glass.

Younger me laughed silently behind it, and did not know yet what she would cost.
