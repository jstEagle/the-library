## 16. The Offer of a Whole Heart

They kept me in a room with no doors.

I want to be precise about that, because it was the first thing I noticed and the thing I kept returning to, the way a tongue worries a chipped tooth. Not a locked room—a room with no doors. The ash court had simply decided that walls were enough, and who was I to argue with a civilization that had turned forgetting into architecture?

The room was comfortable. That was the insult of it. A bed with linens soft enough to make me suspicious, a basin of water that steamed without a fire, a window—because of course there was a window—that looked out over a city built of cinders and pale stone, spires rising like smoke that had given up and decided to become solid. Somewhere below, the guttering ember of the dragon-fire pulsed on the horizon, a coal in a hearth the size of the sky, and I could feel it the way you feel a headache starting: not yet pain, but the promise of it.

Ansel had been taken elsewhere. That was the other thing I kept worrying at. They had separated us at the gates with the smooth efficiency of people who had done this before, who had done this for centuries, and I had let them, because what was I going to do—fight? My whole arsenal was sleight of hand and a lockpick I'd swallowed during the capture, which was now either my salvation or my appendix.

I had been in the doorless room for what I judged to be a day, maybe two, when the wall opened.

Not a door. The stone simply became absent, the way a memory becomes absent, and a woman walked through the space where the wall used to be. She was tall and grey-robed, and her crown was actual ash—not ash-colored, but ash, a circlet of grey flakes that drifted off her brow and reformed, drifted and reformed, a small private snowfall she wore the way other people wore jewelry.

"Xanthe Marchetti," she said. Her voice was warm. That frightened me more than anything else so far. Cruelty I could have worked with. "I am Vess of the Third Hearth. You've had time to rest. Good. What I have to show you should not be seen by the exhausted."

"If this is a sales pitch," I said, "I should warn you I'm a professional. I know all the closes."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. The memory of one, perhaps. "I imagine you do. But this isn't a sale, thief. It's a return."

She gestured, and the room changed.

* * *

Here is what I knew about my own past, before that hour: I had come to Hollowmere nine years ago with nothing, and I had built a trade, and I had one rule, and I had broken it, and everything since had been consequences. I had a gap where my childhood should have been, a fog where my reasons should have been, and I had told myself for nine years that this was normal, that everyone's past was a poorly kept archive, that the missing pieces were simply pieces I'd never bothered to look at closely.

I want you to understand that I believed this. I had believed it so thoroughly and for so long that when Vess spread my life out in front of me like a merchant laying out stolen silks, the first thing I felt was not horror.

It was relief.

The room became a memory. That's the only way to describe it. The walls filled with light and the light filled with scenes, and I stood in the middle of my own history like a woman standing in a flooded field, watching the water rise past her knees, her waist, her chin.

A girl of six, in a house that smelled of tallow and river mud, learning to lift a coin from her father's palm while he slept—because he slept so heavily, because he drank, because there was never enough, and the coin bought bread, and the bread kept her mother alive another winter. A girl of nine, caught, beaten, forgiven, catching again. A girl of twelve watching wardens burn a hedge-witch in the town square for the crime of easing a birth, and understanding, with the terrible clarity of childhood, which side of the fire she was standing on.

"Stop," I said. My voice came out strange.

Vess didn't stop. "You asked to know all the closes, Marchetti. This is the opening."

A girl of sixteen with quick hands and a quick mouth, apprenticed—no, not apprenticed, *recruited*—to a memory-thief ring operating out of the river towns, learning that memories could be lifted like purses, that grief had weight and weight had price. A young woman of twenty standing at the border of Hollowmere for the first time, staring at the wards that sealed magic out of the valley, and feeling—not fear. *Longing.* Because inside those wards was the one place in the world her kind couldn't be hunted, and outside them was everything she'd ever been.

The water rose past my chin.

And then—the night. I had known about the night in fragments. Ansel had told me pieces on the road: that we had been lovers, that he had let me go, that he had carried my memory for nine years while I carried nothing. I had believed him the way you believe a map of a country you've never visited. It was accurate. It was not *real*.

The memory made it real.

Wolf-light—actual light, silver and low, the kind of light that only exists in the hour when the moon is down and the stars are doing all the work. A border post at the valley's edge. A young warden with grey eyes and a jaw like a decision, sitting across a fire from a thief who had just robbed the wardens' own vault, and neither of us reaching for a weapon, because we had been reaching for each other for three months by then and the habit was too strong.

I watched myself laugh. I had forgotten—I had not known I'd forgotten—that I used to laugh like that, with my whole chest, unguarded, the laugh of a woman who had decided to trust someone and was still amazed it hadn't killed her.

"You're going to do it," the young man said in the memory, and his voice cracked on it, and I felt my own chest crack in sympathy, nine years late. "You're going to go in there and I'm never going to see you again."

"You could come with me."

"You know I can't."

"I know you can't. I wanted to hear you say it like it hurts."

"It hurts."

And then the promise. I had remembered the promise—Odile had told me it was fused into the dragon-memory, and Ansel had spoken its words in the dark when the wolf-shape was eating him alive, and the words had pulled him back from dissolution like a rope thrown to a drowning man. *Whatever the fire takes, I'll find you. Whatever the dark keeps, I'll carry you home.*

But I had remembered the words without the night. And the night was—gods, the night was the whole point. The night was his hands in my hair and my forehead against his and the fire burning down to embers and neither of us moving to feed it because feeding it would mean the night was ending. The night was me saying, *If I ever forget you—if anything ever takes this from me—find me anyway. Don't be proud. Don't be a warden about it. Find me anyway.* And him saying, *I'm not proud. I'm not anything but yours.*

The water closed over my head.

When I surfaced, I was on my knees on the floor of the doorless room, and my face was wet, and Vess stood over me with her ash crown drifting and reforming, and her warm voice said, gently, "Now you understand what you're holding. Shall I show you the rest?"

"The rest," I managed, "being the part where I ruin everything."

"The part," she said, "where you were *used*. There's a difference. I'd like you to see it."

* * *

She showed me the theft.

I had thought—I had built my whole self on the thought—that I had stolen the dragon-memory for coin, that I'd broken my rule for greed, that the woman who had once refused to take a widow's worst night had eventually sold out completely. It was a clean story. Clean stories are the most dangerous kind, because you never check them.

The truth was dirtier and stranger.

I watched myself, three years ago, sitting in Odile's archive while the blind archivist's sightless eyes aimed at me like two compasses finding north. I watched Odile tell me about the covenant—not the children's version, not the wards-and-wolves version, but the truth: that the last dragon had died at the border a century ago and given its final recollection to the flame, and that the flame sustained the wards, and that the wards kept magic out of Hollowmere, and that "kept magic out" was a phrase that needed unpacking, because magic out meant hedge-witches burned in town squares and healers hanged and children like me born with quick hands and nowhere safe to be quick.

"You want to break it," my past self said in the memory, and I heard the hunger in my own voice and flinched.

"I want to *study* it," Odile had said, which was what Odile always said, and which was, I understood now, the way Odile lied to herself. "The flame is guttering, Xanthe. It will gutter out on its own within a generation. The covenant will fail regardless. The only question is whether it fails blind or fails *prepared*. I need to know what's inside that memory. I need to know what the dragon knew, at the end, about how the fire works. You're the only thief in the valley good enough to lift it."

"And if I'm caught?"

"Then I never heard of you."

I watched myself do it. The dragon's hoard-memory was kept in a reliquary beneath the Ember Altar itself, guarded by wards keyed to the covenant, and I watched myself spend four months preparing, learning the wards' grammar, and I watched myself go in on the night of the long dark, and I watched myself stand in front of the dragon's dying recollection—a sphere of ember-light the size of a heart, warm as a sleeping animal—and I watched myself reach out my hands.

And I watched the memory reach back.

That was the part no one had told me, the part even Odile hadn't known. The dragon's last recollection was not a passive thing. It was the memory of a mind that had spent its final hours deciding to give everything it was to keep a promise, and when a thief's hands closed around it, it *recognized* her. It saw her—the whole of her, her thefts and her hungers and her burned hedge-witches and her wolf-light night—and it did what dragons do with things they claim.

It took a piece of her in exchange.

I watched myself stagger out of the reliquary with the dragon's death in my keeping and a hole in my chest where three months of love had been, and I watched myself—gods, I watched myself, and I could have killed her—walk to the nearest memory-broker and sell the hole's contents in pieces, deliberately, methodically, because a thief who knows she's been robbed of something that important doesn't keep it where she can feel the absence. She scatters it. She makes herself unable to want it back.

I had sold my own love for Ansel Yarrow in six installments to six different collectors, and I had kept only the dragon's memory, because Odile was waiting for it, and because—this was the detail that undid me—because even hollowed out, even robbed, some animal part of me had understood that this one was different. That this one wasn't mine to sell.

"You see," Vess said, and the memories dimmed to embers around us, "you've spent nine years believing you were a coward who broke her own rule. You weren't. You were a woman who was robbed twice—once by the dragon, once by herself—and who built a small clean life on top of the wreckage because it was the only life she could bear."

I sat on the floor. I didn't have the dignity to do anything else. "Why," I said. "Why are you showing me this. The ash lords want the memory to put the fire out. I know that. Sera told us what you are. So why give me back my past instead of just taking the thing?"

"Because," Vess said, "we are not going to take it."

* * *

She helped me up. I let her, which I decided afterward was either a tactical error or a tactical choice, and I genuinely could not tell which.

"The covenant you're rushing to save," she said, pacing now, her ash crown drifting, "was built on forgetting. You've seen it. The village with no name. The old woman who mistakes her warden for her son because the wards ate the years. Your valley bought a century of safety by selling its memory a piece at a time, and the flame that runs the whole machine is *dying*, Marchetti. Not because of us. It was dying before we ever touched the wards. Dragons do not live forever, even in recollection. You've seen it yourself—that coal on the horizon. Weeks, perhaps. Then the wards fail entirely, and everything your valley kept out comes in, and it comes in *hungry*."

"Unless I reforge it at the altar."

"Unless you reforge it. Yes. Feed it the dragon's memory and whatever else it demands—your kind of magic always demands a price, doesn't it, thief? And what would it demand from *you*, I wonder, to rekindle a fire made of recollection?" She stopped pacing and looked at me, and her eyes were the grey of long-dead coals. "We offer a different covenant. Give us the memory. Let the flame go out on our terms, not in a panic, not in a guttering collapse. We will hold the border—honestly, openly, with terms negotiated between realms rather than imposed by a dying animal's last wish. No more burned hedge-witches. No more nameless villages. No more wardens and thieves on opposite sides of a line neither of you drew."

"And the price."

"Your past," she said simply. "All of it. What I showed you tonight, restored whole—the childhood, the ring, the border, the wolf-light, the theft, all of it, every piece you sold and every piece the dragon took. You would walk out of here *complete*, Xanthe Marchetti. The first whole person you have ever been. And the man who carried your memory for nine years—you would remember him. You would remember everything he's been aching to see in your eyes since the moment he found you at your market stall and you looked at him like a stranger."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere far off, the ember pulsed, and I felt it in my teeth.

"That's the offer," I said. "My wholeness for the world's flame."

"Your wholeness," Vess said, "for a *better* flame. Or for no flame at all, and a border built on terms instead of sacrifice. You of all people should appreciate the trade. You've spent your whole life selling memories. We're simply offering you the only purchase you've ever wanted."

And here is the thing I am not proud of: for a long moment—a real moment, minutes maybe—I wanted it.

I wanted the girl in the tallow-smelling house. I wanted the laugh with my whole chest. I wanted the wolf-light night back, not as a story someone told me, not as words that pulled a drowning wolf to shore, but as *mine*, warm in my hands the way the dragon's memory had been warm, and I wanted to turn to Ansel and say *I remember, I remember all of it, I remember the fire burning down and neither of us feeding it,* and I wanted to watch his face when I said it. Nine years he had carried me. I wanted to carry myself back to him as a gift.

Vess watched me want it. She was very good at watching. I suspect the ash court had centuries of practice at exactly this—the moment when the prisoner's eyes go soft and the wanting does the work that torture never could.

"The dragon-memory," I said at last. "Where is it now?"

"Secure."

"Ansel. Where is he?"

"Also secure." A pause, delicately calibrated. "He has been shown the same offer, from the other direction. His oath-cord for your freedom. He is considering it."

My stomach dropped through the floor and kept going. "He wouldn't. He's a warden. It's his shape, his standing, his—"

"He let you go once," Vess said, "rather than drag you to the wardens. Do you truly believe nine years has made him more obedient?" She moved toward the wall that was about to stop being a wall, and paused there, half in the stone. "You have until the ember gutters, Marchetti. That's days, not weeks. When it dies, the choice makes itself. Until then—" the ash crown drifted, reformed, "—the offer stands. A whole heart. It's more than anyone else has ever offered you. I suspect it's more than you ever offered yourself."

The wall took her, and I was alone in the doorless room with my restored past sitting in my chest like a swallowed coal, and the ember on the horizon pulsed, and pulsed, and I thought about Ansel considering the trade of everything he was for everything I'd been.

I had to get out. Not for me—for him. Because if he traded his oath before I made my choice, then whatever I chose, we'd both have paid, and only one of us would have bought anything.

I put two fingers down my throat and brought up the lockpick.

It was bent. Of course it was bent. But bent iron opens bent locks, and the doorless room had one thing a doorless room always has: a window. And windows, in my experience, were just doors that hadn't been insulted yet.

Far off, the ember pulsed—and stuttered.

It had never stuttered before.
