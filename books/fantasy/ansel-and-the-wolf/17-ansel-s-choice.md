## 17. Ansel's Choice

I have been a warden for eleven years, and in all that time I have learned exactly one thing worth teaching: every oath is a rope. It holds you up. It also hangs you.

Mine was braided through my left forearm — three strands of wolf-grey light under the skin, warm as a sleeping animal. The oath-cord. Every warden carried one, knotted at the wrist by the Hand of the Order on the day of swearing, and it was the cord that let us wear the wolf-shape, that let us run the border between the realms and come back still ourselves. Take the cord away and you got a man who had once been something more. Or a wolf who had once been something less. The old wardens used to say there was no telling which.

I was thinking about ropes because I was standing in the Court of Ash with my hands bound in iron thread, watching two ash-crowned lords decide what my love was worth.

They had priced her cheap so far. A cell of black glass, no window, no door she could find. They'd offered her back her whole stolen past in exchange for the dragon-memory, which was generous of them, considering they intended to snuff out the last flame of the last dragon and let both realms go dark. Generosity is easy when you're spending someone else's soul.

"Your warden," said Lord Veyran, the taller of the two, in the tone men use for horses they're deciding whether to geld. "You've brought us a warden, little thief. How thoughtful."

"I didn't bring him," Xanthe's voice came through the glass, muffled and furious. "He followed me. He does that."

Nine years, and she still didn't remember why. That was the wound I kept choosing to press my thumb against, apparently. Some people bite their nails. I revisit the worst night of my life and call it devotion.

Veyran turned to me. His crown wasn't metal; it was ash that had learned to hold a shape, and it moved when he breathed, little grey feathers of it lifting and settling. "The Order will want their wolf back," he said. "The wards are down. The valley is open. There will be hunting, and the wardens do love their hunting." He smiled, and his teeth were the colour of old bone left out in weather. "Unless the wolf would rather stay."

"I'd rather burn your court to its foundations," I said. "But I'm told that's not on the table."

"No. What's on the table—" and here he gestured, and the table assembled itself out of nothing, black stone rising from the floor like a tooth coming up through gum, "—is a trade."

* * *

Here is what I knew by then, and what I had known since the moment the stars went out: the covenant was dying, and everything I had sworn to protect was dying with it, and the only seed that could regrow the fire was locked inside the head of a woman who did not remember my name.

That last part was supposed to make this easier. That's what I told myself, anyway, walking those ash corridors. If she doesn't remember you, then you're trading a stranger's freedom, and strangers are easy to spend. Except she wasn't a stranger. She was the girl who had once fallen asleep against my shoulder on the border stones and talked in her sleep about opening a shop that sold nothing but rain. She was the woman who had crossed the wards with my heart in her pocket and never known it. Forgetting her half hadn't dissolved anything. It had just left me holding both ends of the rope.

And now the rope was going to be cut, and I was going to find out what I weighed without it.

"The terms," Veyran said, when we stood before the table. On it lay a single object: an oath-cord, unbraided, grey as winter river-water, coiled like something asleep. Not mine. Mine was still in my arm, warm and loyal and stupid. This was a *pattern*. A template. The shape of a thing that could be given or taken.

"You want the dragon-memory," I said.

"We want the flame extinguished," Veyran corrected gently. "The memory is merely the kindling. Your woman carries it. We cannot take it from her while she lives — the thief's craft has its own protections, tiresome things — and killing her would scatter it into the dark where we could never gather it. So we must trade. And she will not trade with us." His ash-crown rustled. "She might trade with you."

"And if she won't?"

"Then we wait. We are very good at waiting. Ask the dragon."

I looked at the coiled pattern on the table and understood, finally, what they were offering, and it was worse than I'd thought, and better, and I laughed — I actually laughed, right there in the Court of Ash, which earned me a look from a guard whose face was a smooth grey blank until it decided otherwise.

Because here was the trade. My oath-cord — the living one, the one braided into my arm, the source of the wolf, the mark of my standing, eleven years of service and the only family the Order had ever pretended to be — for her freedom. And the freedom would be real. The ash court kept its bargains; that was the horror of them. They never lied. They just made sure every truth cost everything.

And the fine print, the part they'd let me read myself: a warden without his cord was exile to both realms. The Order would strike his name. The outer powers had no use for a man who couldn't wear fur. He would be nothing anywhere, a creature of the border, belonging to the space between.

Which, if you thought about it, was where I'd been living for nine years anyway.

* * *

They let me see her before I decided. That was part of the cruelty, or the courtesy — with the ash court the two were indistinguishable.

Her cell was black glass curved like the inside of a throat. When I stepped in, she was sitting cross-legged on the floor with her back to me, working at the wall with a sliver of something, scratching marks. Counting hours, probably. Thieves count. It keeps the panic fed and quiet.

"They're going to offer you a deal," I said.

"I know. They already did. My whole past, wrapped in ribbon." She didn't turn around. "Did you know I stole from the dragon? Because I didn't, until about four days ago, and I have to tell you, finding that out is like being handed a letter you mailed to yourself in a language you've forgotten."

"Xanthe."

"Don't say my name like that." Now she turned, and her eyes were red-rimmed and furious and afraid, and even now — especially now — I wanted to put my hand on the side of her face and tell her the thing I'd never gotten to say on the border stones nine years ago, before the wards took her and the Order took my report and I took the coward's road and called it mercy. "Like you know me. You don't know me. You know a story about me that Odile told you around a fire, and I appreciate it, I do, but it isn't the same, and it hurts worse than if you'd never—"

"I'm going to trade for you," I said.

She stopped. The scratching sliver went still in her hand.

"What?"

"My oath-cord. My wolf. My standing. All of it, to them, and you walk out of here free, with the memory, and we go to the Ember Altar and reforge the covenant and the stars come back and everyone lives." I shrugged, because if I didn't shrug I was going to shake. "It's a good deal. I've had worse offers. The Order once tried to post me to the Salt Marches."

"You can't—" She was on her feet now. "Ansel. You can't give them your *wolf*. That's — that's what you *are*."

"It's what I can do," I said. "There's a difference. Took me eleven years to learn it, but there it is."

"That's not — you don't get to do this. You don't get to sacrifice yourself for someone who doesn't even—" Her voice cracked clean through, and she hated it, I could see her hating it, hating that her body betrayed her feelings before her mind had decided what they were. "I don't remember loving you. Do you understand? Whatever you feel, whatever this is for you, I am standing here with a hole where it should be, and you want to hand them the last piece of yourself for a woman who can't even—"

"Yes," I said.

She stared at me.

"Xanthe. Nine years ago I stood on the border and watched you walk into the valley, and I let you go, because dragging you back in chains was the law and letting you go was love, and I chose love and then spent nine years telling myself it was weakness." I took a breath. "I'm not making that trade again. I'm not choosing the thing that costs least. I'm done being careful with the important parts."

"You absolute idiot," she whispered, and then she crossed the cell and hit me in the chest with both fists, once, twice, and then her forehead landed against my collarbone and she stayed there, shaking, and I put my arms around her because some things the body remembers even when the mind has been robbed.

"If you do this," she said into my shirt, "and I walk out of here, and the flame dies anyway, and the world goes dark — I will never forgive you. I'll have plenty of time. I'll be very thorough about it."

"Noted," I said. "Put it in writing. I hear you're good with contracts."

She laughed — wet, furious, real — and it was the best sound the Court of Ash had ever heard, though I doubt the architecture appreciated it.

* * *

The ceremony, if you could call it that, took place in the long hall where they'd first received us. No chanting. No theatrics. The ash court did its worst work quietly, the way a knife slides in.

Veyran held out his hand. "The cord, warden."

I rolled up my sleeve. The three strands of grey light lay under the skin of my forearm, and as I looked at them they seemed to look back, the way a dog looks at you when it knows. Eleven years. Every border-run, every storm I'd outrun on four legs, every night the wolf-shape had held me together when the man-shape wanted to come apart. The first time I'd shifted, I'd been nineteen and terrified, and the wolf had taken the terror and given it somewhere to go, and I had loved it ever since, the way you love the thing that saved you.

"Ansel." Xanthe stood beside me, free — truly free, the iron thread dissolved off her wrists ten minutes ago, the dragon-memory safe behind her eyes. "Last chance. Walk away. Let them keep me. You'll get another—"

"There's no other one," I said. "There was never another one. That's the whole problem with me."

I laid my arm across the black stone table.

What they did, they did with a word in a language that predated words, and what it felt like was this: imagine a splinter you've carried so long you built your hand around it. Now imagine the hand remembering its own shape. The cord came out of me in three long strands of grey light, unwinding from muscle and marrow, and every strand took a year with it — I felt them go, felt the wolf recede down some corridor inside me and stop, and turn, and look back, and then not.

Then it was done, and I was kneeling on cold stone, and I was — I checked, the way you check for a missing tooth — I was only a man.

Lighter. That was the obscene part. It didn't feel like loss. It felt like setting down a pack after a long march, and some traitor part of me sighed with relief, and I wanted to kill that part, and then I realized I'd need both hands free for what came next, so I let it live.

"He is yours," Veyran said to Xanthe, almost kindly. "Exiled from our realm and from yours both. The Order will know by nightfall. They always know." His ash-crown settled. "A curious bargain, thief. You gave up your past to seal the covenant. He has given up his future to unseal it. Between you, you might assemble half a person."

"Half a person," Xanthe said, taking my arm and hauling me upright, "is twice what either of your lords is."

And we walked out of the Court of Ash, the two of us, into the dark under the starless sky, toward the Ember Altar and the guttering coal of the last dragon's fire, which was dying faster now — I could feel it, or rather I could feel the *absence* of feeling it, the wolf-sense gone quiet, the way a deaf man feels silence as pressure.

"How far?" Xanthe asked.

"A day's hard walk. Less if we run." I flexed my bare hand. It was just a hand. It was the strangest thing I'd ever owned. "We can't run. Not anymore. No wolf."

"Then we walk," she said, "and we do it fast, and if anything with too many joints finds us, we do what humans do."

"Die badly?"

"Improvise."

We walked. Behind us, the Court of Ash settled back into its long patience. Ahead, far off, the ember pulsed — dimmer than yesterday, dimmer than this morning, a heartbeat counting down.

And somewhere behind us, I knew, Sera Vance was making her own choice about which truth to serve, and the council's hunters were moving, and the altar was waiting with its mouth open for everything we had left.

I had one thing left. I glanced sideways at Xanthe, striding through the dark with her chin up and her fists clenched, carrying the world's last flame in her head and pretending she wasn't terrified.

No. Two things. But I was saving the second one for after the world was saved. I figured I'd earned the right to say it properly, this time, in daylight, under returning stars — assuming we got that far.

Assuming the rope held.
