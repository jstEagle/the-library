## 18. The Ember Altar

**Ansel**

The ash court did not believe in doors.

That was the first thing I learned about the outer realm's lords: they built their citadel of cinders and bone-white stone with no entrances worth the name, because nothing was ever supposed to leave. Everything that came in stayed in—memories, prisoners, oaths, light. A court is a shape of its appetites, Odile had told me once, back when I still thought she was a harmless old woman who smelled of dust and secrets. The ash court's appetite was simple. It wanted everything, forever, and it wanted it to stop moving.

We were very much still moving.

"Left," Xanthe hissed at my shoulder, and I went left, because her instincts for architecture were better than mine and my instincts for not dying were only marginally better than hers, which made us a functional team by the generous standards of fugitives.

The corridor we took was a throat of black basalt lit by veins of ember-light running through the walls like something circulatory. Far off, deeper in, a red glow pulsed—slow, arrhythmic, guttering. The dragon-fire. I could feel it now that we were close, the way you feel a hearth through a wall on a cold night, except this hearth was holding up two realms, and it was dying.

"How far?" I asked.

"Odile said three turnings past the gallery of teeth."

"The what?"

"You'll know it when you see it," she said grimly. "You'll wish you hadn't."

I wished I hadn't. The gallery of teeth was exactly what it sounded like—a hall lined floor to ceiling with fangs the size of scythes, harvested from something enormous and long dead, and I did not let myself think about whose teeth those were, because I already knew, and grief was a luxury we'd have to schedule later. Between the teeth hung the court's trophies: glass spheres, each one holding a swirl of grey light. Memories. Thousands of them. Taken, not given.

Xanthe stopped walking. I felt her stop more than saw it, the way you feel a change in weather.

"They're all here," she whispered. "Everything they've ever taken."

"Don't look at them."

"I'm not looking. I'm *counting*." Her voice had gone flat and hard, the voice she used in the market when someone tried to haggle down a fair price. "There's a difference."

There was no time for the difference. Behind us, down the corridor we'd come from, the ash-lords' soldiers rounded the corner—not men, not anymore, if they ever had been. They moved like puppets wearing too much armor, joints bending in directions that made my wolf-side want to be sick, and their faces behind the visors were smooth as river stones. Where eyes should have been there was only faint grey glow. Stolen light, worn as lanterns.

"Run," I said, and shifted.

The change came easier than it had any right to, after everything. My oath-cord was gone—I'd traded it to the ash court itself, a fool's bargain I'd make again without hesitation—and by every law of wardenship I should have been only a man now, earthbound, ordinary. But the wolf had lived in me too long to be evicted by paperwork. It came anyway, uninvited, unpaid, and I let it, because the alternative was watching them take Xanthe, and there was no version of me, corded or cordless, man or beast, that would stand aside for that.

I put myself between her and the soldiers and let the wolf do what wolves do.

It was ugly work. Their armor turned my teeth; their bodies didn't care where I broke them. One of them caught my shoulder with a blade of grey glass and cold went through me like winter finding a gap under a door—not pain exactly, but *absence*, a place where feeling used to be. I heard Xanthe shout, heard the crackle of something burning, and then the soldier holding me simply came apart, unravelling into ash from the wound outward, because Xanthe had driven a knife of ember-glass—looted from a shattered sphere along our way, gods bless her thieving hands—into the back of its neck.

"That's for the shoulder," she panted. "Move."

We ran. Behind us the gallery of teeth began to fill with more of them, silent as snowfall, patient as debt.

* * *

**Xanthe**

Here is a thing nobody tells you about saving the world: it involves an appalling amount of stairs.

The Ember Altar sat at the crown of the ash court's citadel, which meant climbing, which meant every landing held another squad of hollow-eyed soldiers, which meant Ansel spent most of the ascent as a wolf and I spent most of it learning to fight beside a wolf, which is a skill I sincerely hope I never need again and suspect I will.

He fought differently now. I'd seen him fight before—or I remembered seeing him fight, in fragments, the way you remember a dream after waking: flashes of grey fur and terrible grace. But that had been a warden's fighting, disciplined, bound by oath and form. This was something rawer. He was holding his wolf-shape longer than he should, and I could see the cost of it in the way he moved—less like a man being a wolf and more like a wolf forgetting there had ever been a man. Twice on the stairs he looked at me and I saw nothing looking back. Just hunger and duty tangled together, the two things the outer dark fed on best.

So I talked to him while we ran. It felt absurd, shouting anecdotes over the sound of breaking armor, but Odile had told me once—back before her confession rearranged everything I thought I knew—that memory spoken aloud is an anchor, that the outer dark cannot wear a face that keeps talking about who it is.

"You owe me eleven silver crowns," I yelled over the crash of a soldier coming apart under his jaws. "From the night we met at the gate. You bought information off me and never paid."

The wolf's ears flicked. For half a heartbeat, the man surfaced in the amber eyes. "I paid you in *protection*," he growled, in a voice mostly wolf.

"You gave me a truce banner and a lecture. That's not protection, that's bureaucracy." I ducked a grey blade and drove my ember-knife upward into a soldier's jaw. "Eleven crowns, warden. Plus interest. Nine years of interest."

Something that might have been laughter rumbled out of him, low and rough, and he threw himself at the next squad with what I can only describe as good humor. We climbed. The pulse of the dying fire grew stronger overhead, and with it a heat that wasn't heat—the feeling of a promise running out of time.

The last stair opened onto the summit, and I forgot how to breathe.

The Ember Altar was not what I expected. I'd imagined stone and ceremony, some grand ring of carved pillars. Instead it was small. A bowl of black iron on a plinth of fused glass, no bigger than a washbasin, sitting alone on a plateau of sky. And above it, suspended in nothing, burned the last dragon-fire: a coal the size of a heart, pulsing its slow failing rhythm, each beat dimmer than the last. Around it the stars were visible—real stars, outer-realm stars, but wrong, smeared, as though the sky itself was losing focus. When the coal pulsed, the stars steadied. When it faded, they blurred.

Two realms, hanging on a heartbeat.

"It's so small," I said.

"So are embers," said a voice behind us. "That's rather the point of them."

Sera Vance stood at the head of the stair with a drawn sword and thirty of the ash court's soldiers arrayed behind her, and my stomach dropped through the plateau and kept going.

"Sera," Ansel breathed, shifting back to human form, bloodied and swaying. "Don't."

"I'm not here to stop you." She raised her sword—and pointed it, not at us, but at the soldiers on the stair behind her. "I'm here to hold the door."

* * *

**Ansel**

I had prepared myself for many versions of Sera Vance at the top of that mountain. Sera arresting us. Sera negotiating. Sera, worst of all, keeping her word to the council and taking the memory by force, because Sera always kept her word—it was the load-bearing wall of who she was.

I had not prepared myself for Sera turning her blade on her own escort.

"Hold the door," Xanthe repeated slowly. "Your sister—"

"My sister is beyond the wards, in a realm that is currently eating itself, because the people who promised to trade for her rescue were never going to rescue anyone." Sera's voice was steady, but I'd known her fifteen years, and I could hear the fracture underneath, hairline-thin. "I read the council's correspondence, Ansel. All of it. There is no bargain. There was never going to be a bargain. The ash lords offered the council a *story* about a bargain, and the council liked the story better than the truth, because the truth required admitting they'd let the wards rot for forty years." She spat. "They sold my sister's name to buy themselves time. So I'm done buying what they're selling."

The soldiers behind her began to move. She met them alone on the narrow stair, and it was the finest and most terrible swordwork I have ever seen, because Sera Vance fights like a woman settling accounts.

"We can't leave her—" Xanthe started.

"You can and you will." Sera didn't turn around. Her blade rang off grey armor. "Every debt gets called in eventually, Marchetti. Consider this one called. Go light your fire."

What do you do, when someone pays your debts with their own blood? You run. That's the answer nobody wants. You run, because staying would waste it, and wasting a sacrifice is the only true way to lose one.

We ran to the altar.

The coal hung above the iron bowl, and up close I could see it wasn't just fire. There were shapes in it—wings, vast and folded, a body curled around its own last warmth, the ghost of something enormous sleeping its final sleep. The dragon had poured itself into this flame nine hundred years ago, and it had been burning ever since, alone, at the top of the world, keeping two realms from devouring each other.

Xanthe reached into her coat and drew out the memory.

Even unopened, even sealed in its thief's wrapping, I could see it was wrong—fused, knotted, two threads braided into one. The dragon's death and something else. Something of hers.

"Xanthe," I said carefully. "What did you steal?"

She stared at the knot of light in her hands, and her face did something complicated. "Not what I thought," she said. "Apparently I stole from the dragon, and I stole from *myself*, and I've been carrying both ever since without knowing either one." A laugh escaped her, thin and cracked. "Nine years a thief, and the biggest heist of my life, and I don't even remember doing it."

"Whatever's in there—you don't have to give all of it."

"Yes," she said quietly, "I think I do. That's rather the point of altars." She looked up at me then, and the coal-light caught her eyes, and for a moment neither of us said anything, because we both knew what the altar asked and we both knew what she was about to pay, and there are no words that improve a moment like that.

The stair behind us erupted.

Sera had held them for as long as any single person could hold thirty, which is to say: not long enough. They came onto the plateau in a grey tide, and worse, behind them came the lords themselves—three shapes of crowned ash, tall as gatehouses, their faces smooth blanknesses that hurt to look at directly. The largest raised a hand, and the tide stopped, and the whole summit went quiet enough to hear the dragon-fire's failing pulse.

*"Thief,"* it said. Its voice was the sound of a library burning. *"Give us the flame's seed, and you may have back everything you lost. Every memory. Every year. Even the ones you sold yourself to forget."*

Xanthe didn't even flinch. That's the thing about her that I keep underestimating, and keep paying for underestimating. She looked at the lord the way she'd look at a bad haggler in the market.

"I know what my memories cost," she said. "I set the prices myself." She turned to the altar. "And I'm afraid I'm the only seller in town."

She opened her hands, and the knot of light rose—dragon-death and stolen love braided together—and fell into the iron bowl.

The coal above it flared.

For one impossible instant the whole summit blazed gold, and the ash lords recoiled, and the stars snapped into focus like eyes opening, and I thought—we'd done it, it was working—

Then the flame in the bowl guttered, and dimmed, and I understood, with a lurch, that a seed is not a fire. The memory was only fuel. Something still had to burn.

The altar turned toward Xanthe like a question.

And she stepped forward to answer it.
