## 19. What Xanthe Burns

The Ember Altar was not what I expected, which is the one thing in this whole catastrophe I should have predicted.

I had imagined something grand—a mountain of black glass, maybe, or a ring of standing stones carved with dragons, the kind of place ballads get written about. Instead we climbed the last ridge of the valley's spine at dawn-that-wasn't, because dawn had stopped meaning anything once the stars went out, and found a shallow bowl of stone no bigger than my mother's kitchen had been. A circle of cracked flagstones. A basin of dull iron at its center, holding a coal the size of my fist.

That was it. That was the covenant. Nine years of hiding, a valley full of people who traded their memories like coppers because they couldn't trade anything else, and all of it balanced on a dying coal you could have covered with two cupped hands.

"It's so *small*," I said.

"Most important things are," Ansel said behind me.

He was human now. That was still sitting wrong in my chest, like a stair that wasn't where my foot expected it. The oath-cord was gone—traded to the ash court for my freedom, the mad, magnificent fool—and without it he moved differently. Narrower. A wolf folded up into a man's shape and told to stay that way. He kept glancing at his own hands as if checking whether they'd remembered how to be hands.

Behind us, Sera Vance held the passage we'd cut through the ash court, her hunters' blades drawn against the creatures of the long dark, against her own former comrades, against every debt she'd called in to be here. She'd chosen truth over her sister. I still didn't know if that made her the bravest person I'd ever met or the most broken, and I suspected the answer was yes.

The coal guttered.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the horror of it. It didn't roar or flare or fight. It simply dimmed, the way a candle dims when the wick is nearly gone, and the darkness around the bowl leaned in a little closer, patient as a collector at an auction.

"The memory," Ansel said quietly. "Now, Xanthe."

I reached into the vault inside myself.

You should understand what that means, for someone like me. Every thief has a vault—the place where stolen goods sit while they wait to be fenced. For a memory-thief it's not a room but a discipline: you wall the taken thing off from your own recollections, label it, keep it cold. I had been carrying the dragon's final recollection fused to a night on the border with a young warden, a promise made in wolf-light, and I had carried it through flooded gorges and ash courts and the particular hell of learning that the man I'd erased was the man walking beside me.

I pulled it up.

It came easily, which frightened me more than resistance would have. The dragon-memory rose first—that vast, slow grief, the last thoughts of the last of its kind, watching the world it had warmed decide it no longer needed warming. Underneath it, tangled through it like roots through a grave, was my own past. All of it. The theft I couldn't remember committing. The years I'd sold off in pieces to forget my crime. And threaded through everything, bright as wire, Ansel—young, laughing, furious, tender, saying *come with me* and then, when I wouldn't, saying *then go, and live, and I won't follow.*

I stood at the edge of the iron basin with both realms' survival cupped invisibly in my hands, and I hesitated.

Not because I wanted to keep them. That's what I'd expected—to flinch, to bargain, to look for a third door the way I always had. But the memories themselves showed me what they were. The dragon's last thought had been *let it be enough.* Mine, apparently, had always been *let me be safe.* Small. Cold. A life built like a locked room with the furniture arranged just so.

The flame was going out because I had spent nine years being comfortable in a lie.

"Well," I said, because wry is what I do when I'm terrified, "at least I'm getting a fair price for once. Everything I am for everything there is."

"Xanthe." Ansel's voice cracked down the middle. He knew—I could see him doing the arithmetic, watching me hold our entire history over a fire. "There may be another way. Odile—"

"Odile helped break the covenant because she thought freedom was worth forgetting." I looked at him. His eyes were still wolf's eyes even in a human face, amber and steady and full of nine years of loving someone who'd thrown him away like loose coin. "She was half right. The price was real. It's just that nobody asked the ones paying it."

I tipped my hands.

* * *

Memory burns strangely.

Not like paper, not like wood. Like light remembering it used to travel. The dragon-memory left me first, pouring out of the vault in a long silver exhalation, and for one suspended moment I felt what the dragon had felt—not its death but its purpose, the enormous patience of a creature that had given its fire to keep small safe things safe, and had never once regretted it.

Then the coal drank it.

Flame leapt up from the basin—not orange, not gold, but the color of the stars that used to be, white shot through with green—and the shock of it knocked us both back a step. The dark things beyond the ridge shrieked. Somewhere below, Sera whooped like a woman at a horse race.

But the flame flickered, unsteady, hungry. New-lit and starving. And I understood, with the terrible clarity that only arrives when it's too late to matter, that a seed is not a forest. The dragon-memory alone would gutter again in hours. The covenant hadn't been sealed by one recollection. It had been sealed by a whole life freely given.

The altar wanted more.

It didn't speak. Altars don't. But the heat reached for me the way a client reaches across a market stall, and I recognized the gesture, because I'd spent my whole adult life on the other side of it. *Everything,* the warmth said, in the wordless grammar of fire. *Or nothing holds.*

"Xanthe, don't—" Ansel grabbed my wrist.

I looked down at his hand on mine. Wolf-callused. Steady. The hand that had let me go instead of dragging me to the wardens, that had traded his oath and his shape and his whole world for my freedom, that was currently holding onto me like I was the last solid thing in a dissolving realm.

"I have to tell you something," I said, "and you're not allowed to argue, because I've recently learned that arguing with you takes years and gets us nowhere."

"That sounds like something I'd say."

"It is. You did. I remember now—all of it, remember? That's rather the point." My throat was closing. I pushed through it. "I stole from the dragon because I thought magic was a cage and I was the key. I sold our nights piece by piece because guilt was heavier than grief and I've never been able to carry anything heavy. I broke the world because I was a coward with excellent taste in locks." I turned my hand over and laced my fingers through his. "And the worst part isn't any of that. The worst part is that you loved me anyway, and I can't bear owing a debt that big to anyone, least of all someone I—"

I stopped. Said it anyway.

"—someone I love. Present tense. Not the memory. Me. Now. Standing here."

His face did something complicated and devastating.

"So I'm going to pay it," I said. "All of it. The love, the theft, the years, the lot. Because the covenant shouldn't run on a dead dragon's leftovers anymore than it should run on everyone's forgetting. It should run on something given on purpose." I smiled, and it hurt, and I did it anyway, because wry is what I do. "Consider it a professional courtesy. One thief to the oldest fence in creation."

"You'll forget me."

"Yeah." I stepped toward the basin. "But here's the thing I learned from a village that forgot its own name: what's forgotten isn't gone. It's just waiting for someone honest enough to tell it back." I squeezed his hand once, hard, memorizing the pressure since I couldn't keep the rest. "Tell me a story sometime, warden. Make it a good one."

And before he could answer—before he could do the noble doomed thing and pull me back, or worse, the noble doomed thing and step in with me—I let go of everything.

* * *

Burning doesn't feel like burning.

It feels like being read.

Every memory went into the flame in the order I'd stolen them, which meant the newest first and the oldest last, and I got to watch my own life pass in reverse, which is either a curse or a gift depending on how much you liked your life. The border crossing. The theft, seen finally from the inside—my hands in the dragon's dying mind, Odile's voice guiding me, the awful ease of it. The selling. The first night in Hollowmere, setting up my stall, inventing my rule—*never steal anything that matters*—and knowing even then it was a rule for other people.

Then the years with Ansel. All of them. The promise in wolf-light. The argument. The letting-go. His face getting smaller behind me as I crossed into the valley, and the specific, surgical way I had cut him out of myself afterward, one clean sale at a time, until I was a woman with a spotless vault and no idea why she sometimes woke at night reaching for warm fur that wasn't there.

I gave it all gladly. That's the part I want recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger the new covenant keeps: *gladly.* Not because it didn't cost—it cost everything, that was the entire transaction—but because for the first time in my thieving life I paid full price without haggling, and it turns out that's what honesty feels like. Terrible. Clean. Like a lock finally opening after years of picking.

The last thing to go was the present tense. Him. His hand. The words *tell me a story* hanging in the air between us.

I held that one a heartbeat longer than the rest.

Then I let it go too, because a covenant built on keeping one thing back isn't a covenant. It's just a smaller cage.

The flame took it, and roared.

* * *

I woke on the flagstones of a shallow stone bowl, staring straight up into a sky full of stars.

Real ones. Hundreds. Thousands—more than I had ever seen, because I had never actually seen them, had I? The wards had eaten the sky over Hollowmere for generations, and I'd grown up under a ceiling of painted dark and called it night. Now the real thing poured down on me, vast and indifferent and absolutely, extravagantly beautiful, and somewhere in the high dark a band of pale fire arched like a river that had decided the ground was beneath it.

The coal in the basin was gone. In its place burned a flame the height of a candle—small, steady, self-sufficient. It didn't gutter. It didn't reach. It simply burned, the way a hearth burns in a house where someone intends to stay.

The covenant held. I could feel it the way you feel a floor under your feet—not as a barrier but as a ground to stand on. Magic and memory, free to cross, guarded by wardens who choose. Someone else's idea, mostly. A better bargain than the old one. I'd have to find out whose.

My head was quiet.

That was the strangest part. Not empty—my own memories were intact, the ones that were truly mine, the childhood and the craft and the nine years in Hollowmere, all of it returned to me clean, the altar apparently considering those payment received rather than fuel consumed. But there was a shape missing. A hand-shaped absence, an amber-eyed absence, a whole country of absence with borders I could trace and no idea what lived inside them.

I sat up. Ansel was kneeling three feet away, watching me with an expression I had no context for and understood completely anyway, which is a trick grief does that I intend to file a complaint about.

"Do I know you?" I asked.

Something went through his face like weather. "No," he said. And then, because he was apparently incapable of lying to me even now: "Not yet."

"Hm." I looked at him properly. Tall. Road-worn. Human, entirely human, no oath-cord, no wolf-shape, exile to both realms by his own choice, and still looking at me like I was the last solid thing in a dissolving world. My chest did something warm and unaccountable, the way a house does when a stranger knocks and some traitor part of you is already glad. "You have wolf's eyes," I informed him. "Has anyone ever told you that?"

"Once or twice." His voice was wrecked and gentle. "I'm told I know a story you might like. It's about a thief."

"Is she clever?"

"Infuriatingly."

"Does she get a happy ending?"

He looked up at the stars—the stars she'd bought, though I didn't know that yet, though I wouldn't know it for a long time, though he would spend a whole winter telling it to me piece by piece until the fragments grew back not as memories but as something better, something newly made and ours—and he smiled like a man who had lost everything except the one thing that mattered.

"Ask me again tomorrow," he said. "I'm planning to stick around long enough for you to need a lot of tomorrows."

Above us, over the valley of Hollowmere, the first festival lights were coming on.
