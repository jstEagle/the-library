## 20. The Covenant Remade

**Xanthe**

I have died before. Twice, if you count the time I sold a memory of my own death to a man who wanted to know what it felt like and then had to live with the knowing. But this was different. This time I woke up on cold stone with no memories at all in my head except the last hour, and the last hour contained a wolf-man holding my hand like it was the only thing keeping the sky up.

Which, to be fair, it might have been.

"Stars," I said, because my mouth has always worked faster than my sense.

Ansel looked up. His face did something complicated—relief and grief fighting for the same real estate—and settled on neither. "You're awake."

"I'm awake." I sat up. The Ember Altar stood behind us, and it was *singing*. Not a sound you hear so much as one you feel in your teeth, in the soft parts behind your eyes. The flame that burned there now wasn't the old dragon-fire, guttering and desperate. It was new. Small. Stubborn. A candle that had decided to be a sun eventually and was working up to it.

And above us, through the broken roof of the ash court's great hall, the stars were coming back.

Not all at once. They came the way memories come—one, then another, then a scatter of them, then a whole river of light where there had been nothing but dark for nine years. I watched a constellation assemble itself piece by piece, and I felt absolutely nothing about it, because whatever I'd once felt about stars was ash in the altar now too.

"You don't remember," Ansel said. Not a question.

"Nope." I flexed my fingers. My hands felt strangely light, the way they do after you set down something heavy you've carried so long you forgot it was there. "I remember the fight. Sera holding the door. You trading your oath-cord to a man wearing a crown made of cinders, which, by the way, was the single worst bargain I've ever witnessed, and I once traded a lullaby for a horse."

"It was a good horse."

"It was a *nag*, Ansel."

He almost smiled. Almost. Then he looked at his own hands—bare hands, human hands, no cord, no wolf coiled underneath waiting for permission—and the smile died before it could commit to anything.

"The covenant held," he said. "You did it."

"We did it." I said it firmly, the way you say things you intend to be true whether or not they currently are. "And now I'd like to know what we did, exactly, because I have a feeling someone is about to explain it to me very slowly, and I'd rather get ahead of them."

As if summoned—and honestly, at this point, I wouldn't rule out summoning—a voice came from the doorway of the ruined hall. Odile stood there, leaning on her stick, her blind eyes turned toward the altar with an expression I can only describe as a scholar watching a proof resolve itself.

"Ah," she said. "You're both alive. Good. That saves me a eulogy, and I've never been good at those. Too much temptation to editorialize."

* * *

**Ansel**

The ash court crumbled over the course of three days, and I mean that literally.

The lords who had worn crowns of cinders and fed on the old covenant's slow starvation—they didn't die. They *settled*. I watched it happen from the steps of the hall while Xanthe slept off the kind of exhaustion that comes from burning your entire self as fuel. One by one, the ash-lords stopped moving. Their courts of smoke and cinder-ornament grew still, and then the wind got into them, and then they were just stone. Ordinary stone, pocked and grey, standing in fields that had never known a season. By the second day, moss had found the first of them. By the third, a bird nested in what had been, until recently, the Lord of the Long Dark's left shoulder.

"They were never people," Odile said, when I asked. She'd taken up residence in the hall's one intact corner, surrounded by the archive she'd carried across half the valley on her back, because apparently even the apocalypse doesn't slow down a woman who believes in proper filing. "They were what the covenant's fear became when it had nowhere else to go. Nine years of dread, given shape. Now the dread is spent." She tilted her head toward the stone figure in the field. "That one is just geology with pretensions."

"And the realms?" I asked. "What did Xanthe actually make?"

Odile smiled, which from her was always slightly alarming. "Come and see. Or rather—come and be told, since seeing is my department's loss."

She led me to the border. The old border, the one I'd patrolled for nine years, the line where the wards had fallen and the magic had poured through like water through a cracked dam. Except the dam wasn't cracked anymore. It was a *gate*.

Where the wards had been a wall of forgetting—magic cannot cross, memory cannot cross, nothing crosses without cost—there was now an archway of living flame, low and steady, fed by the new fire at the altar far to the north. And through it, I could see both worlds at once. The valley behind me, green and small and safe. The outer realm ahead, wild and vast and lit now by returning stars.

And crossing between them, freely: memory.

I felt it happen. A thread of the valley drifted out through the gate—the smell of Hollowmere's bread ovens, the particular grey of its winter light—and passed into the outer realm like a seed on wind. And something came back. A thread of elsewhere. The taste of rain on a mountain I'd never climbed. Not forced. Not stolen. *Offered.*

"The new covenant," Odile said, "binds the realms by exchange instead of erasure. Magic crosses. Memory crosses. What each realm gives, it gives knowingly, and what it receives, it keeps honestly." She turned her blind face up to the archway. "The old dragon sealed the valley out of love and desperation, and it cost everyone inside their pasts, bit by bit, year by year. Your thief rewrote the terms. No more forgetting. Only telling."

"She's not a thief anymore," I said.

"Of course she is. She's just finally stealing for the right side." Odile gathered her stick and her dignity. "There will need to be wardens. Wardens who choose the post, not inherit it. Who guard the exchange, not the silence." She paused at the edge of the flame-gate. "The council is assembling at Hollowmere within the week. They'll want to name a Warden Prime. Someone who knows both sides of the line. Someone who has already given everything the old system could take from him."

"Odile—"

"Don't argue with a blind woman, boy. We cheat."

* * *

**Xanthe**

The council of Hollowmere met in the market square, which I thought was fitting, since the market square was where I'd spent nine years picking pockets full of other people's Tuesdays, and it seemed only right that the reckoning should happen among the stalls.

I stood in front of them with Ansel at my shoulder—he insisted, and I've learned not to argue with a man who traded immortality-adjacent wolfhood for me; it sets a precedent you can't walk back—and I told them everything. The theft. The dragon-memory. The wards. The altar. What I'd burned to reforge the covenant, and what the new terms were.

Councilor Brann, who has the face of a man perpetually smelling something faintly off, spoke first. "You're telling us magic will simply... walk into our valley."

"I'm telling you it already does," I said. "Every day. Through the gate. As gifts, not invasions. The difference between a wall and a door is that a door assumes the other side is people."

"And if we refuse? If we vote to close this gate of yours?"

"Then you'll need a new thief," I said, "because the flame answers to the memory that feeds it, and I'm the only person alive who knows how to steal from a god. Well." I glanced at Odile, seated to one side with the smugness of a woman whose fingerprints are all over history. "Second-alive."

It went to a vote. Of course it did. Hollowmere loves a vote the way I used to love an unattended purse. And here is the thing I did not expect, the thing I am still chewing on like a gristle I can't quite swallow: they voted yes. Overwhelmingly. Because the village that forgot its name sent a delegate—an old woman who'd mistaken a wolf-warden for her son—and she stood up in front of the council and said, "We lost thirty years to that wall. My neighbors' children grew up strangers to them. Whatever comes through that gate, at least it *comes*. At least we'll know what we're giving and what we're getting." 

Honest exchange. That was the phrase that won it. Odile had coined it, naturally, and had it carved over the gate in letters deep enough to survive the next nine centuries of people deciding they knew better.

Afterward, in the square's sudden emptiness, the council's speaker approached Ansel. I hung back, pretending to examine a fruit stall, eavesdropping shamelessly, because some habits are load-bearing.

"Warden Yarrow," the speaker said. "The post of Warden Prime is yours, if you'll take it. The oath-cord can be remade. The wolf-shape restored. The council would consider it a—" he searched for the word, "—a debt honored."

I watched Ansel stand very still. I watched him look at his hands again, the bare ones, the human ones. I thought about what I knew of him—which was, admittedly, one hour of fighting and a handful of stories other people had told me about myself—and I thought about the way he'd said *you're awake* like a man catching something falling.

"No," Ansel said.

The speaker blinked. "No?"

"The cord made me a warden because the old covenant needed guards against the truth. The new one needs wardens who *choose* it. So I'm choosing." He rolled his shoulders like a man setting down a yoke. "No cord. No inherited shape. If I serve, I serve as what I am. Just a man who knows both sides of the line."

"And your wolf?"

Something crossed his face—grief, maybe, or relief, or the strange territory between them where most honest things live. "Gone with the oath. That's the price. That was always the price." He glanced over at me, caught me eavesdropping, and didn't even have the decency to look surprised. "Some prices you pay twice. Some you pay glad."

The speaker bowed and withdrew. Ansel walked over to the fruit stall, picked up an apple, inspected it, put it back, and said, "You know, most people pretend better than that."

"I've never pretended well in my life," I said. "It's why I went into theft. Honesty's too risky."

He laughed. Actually laughed, short and startled, like the sound had surprised him on its way out. And I felt something turn over in my chest—not a memory, the vault in me was empty as a scraped plate—but something adjacent. A warmth with no name attached. A door in a house I didn't recognize, standing open.

"So," I said. "Warden Prime. Just a man. Exiled from your order, stripped of your shape, and employed by the government of a valley you once arrested people for entering. How's that working out for you?"

"Ask me tomorrow."

"Why not today?"

"Because today," he said, "I was going to ask if you'd walk the border with me. All of it. Gate to gate. There are things out there you built and have never seen, and—" he hesitated, and I watched a man who had faced the long dark without flinching struggle with a sentence, "—and I'd like to tell you about them. About all of it. The parts you gave away. If you want. No obligation. No debt. Just..."

"Just a story," I finished.

His eyes came up. Wolf's eyes, still, even without the wolf. Amber and patient and older than his face.

"Yes," he said quietly. "Just a story."

I looked at him for a long moment—at this stranger who had bled for me, traded for me, waited nine years in a country of one—and I felt that unnamed warmth turn its face toward the light like a plant that had been kept in a cellar.

"Well," I said, and picked up the apple after all, and tossed it to him. "I do love a good story. But fair warning, warden: I steal from storytellers. It's practically a religious calling."

"I know," Ansel said, and smiled for real this time. "You told me that once. Under the stars. Before they went out."

I didn't remember it. I believed him anyway. And somewhere north of us, the new fire at the Ember Altar flickered—just slightly, just once—like something laughing.

We walked toward the gate together, two people with one shared past and none of it between us, into a world that had finally learned to trade instead of erase.

Behind us, the first star of evening came up over Hollowmere, and it stayed.
