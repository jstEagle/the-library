## 21. A Stranger at the Door

The trouble with burning your whole past to reforge a covenant is that nobody warns you about the mornings.

Mornings are when it finds you. Not the absence itself — an absence is easy, it's just a hole where a thing used to be, and I've carried holes in other people's heads for a living, so I know the shape of them — but the *echo*. The way my hands know how to fold a memory-vial into cloth before my mind has decided to do it. The way I wake some nights with my cheek turned toward the window, toward the north wall of the valley, as if I'd been listening for footsteps on a road I've never walked.

I have a theory about this. I've had several theories, actually, and Odile has shot down most of them with the serene cruelty of a blind woman who has never once been wrong in my presence.

"Residue," she said, three weeks after the stars came back, when I brought her tea and my complaints in equal measure. "You fed the altar everything you were. But fire doesn't destroy, girl. Fire *transforms*. Some ash always settles back on the hearth."

"That's not comforting."

"I wasn't aiming for comfort. I was aiming for true." She turned her face toward me — she has a way of turning her face toward you that makes you feel seen by something older than eyes. "You want to know what worries me? You don't ask what you lost. Everyone else who came out of that night asks. You only ever ask why you don't mind."

I hadn't noticed that myself, which was annoying. I minded being annoyed about not minding. It was a very tidy little knot of feeling, and I carried it around for days like a stone in my boot.

* * *

Hollowmere rebuilt itself the way valleys do: slowly, stubbornly, and with a great deal of argument about whose fault everything was.

The council had dissolved — formally, ceremonially, with speeches — once the new covenant took hold. Magic could cross the border now. Memory could too. Wardens still patrolled, but they were chosen now, sworn by consent instead of oath-cord, and the first thing the new wardens did was open the archives. All those erased names, all those forgotten villages, all the years the valley had traded away for safety — it came flooding back like water into a dry channel, and half of Hollowmere spent the autumn weeping in doorways over relatives they'd never known they'd lost.

I did not weep. I couldn't. Whatever I'd given the altar, it had taken cleanly, and there was nothing in me that ached except, occasionally, without warning or reason, a warmth behind my ribs like a banked coal.

I went back to work. Of course I did. A thief doesn't stop thieving because the world remade itself; she just finds better clients.

The new covenant changed my trade more than I expected. With memory flowing freely across the border, people didn't need to *sell* their worst nights anymore — they could carry them out of the valley, give them away, trade them honestly. But grief is grief, and some nights are too heavy to carry even when carrying is legal. So I kept my stall in the market, under the crooked awning with the faded blue stripe, and people still came to me in the small hours with their hands shaking and their worst moments folded up inside them like letters they couldn't bear to reread.

My rule held, though. Never steal anything that matters. I'd broken it once, and the world had nearly ended, and I'd paid for it with — well. With whatever it was I'd paid with. There was a joke in there somewhere. I hadn't found it yet.

"You're different," Marta the breadwife told me one morning, sliding two copper loaves across her counter and refusing payment, which was its own kind of strange. "Before, you looked at people like you were pricing them. Now you look at them like—" She waved a floury hand. "Like you're trying to remember where you put something."

"I'm exactly the same as I ever was."

"No," she said, kindly and terribly. "You're kinder. It's unsettling. Take the bread."

I took the bread. I ate it walking home through streets that smelled of woodsmoke and new rain, past the wardhouse with its doors propped open, past children chasing something small and glowing that had wandered in from beyond the border — magic, loose in Hollowmere, tame as a stray cat. One of the children laughed, and the sound caught in my chest like a fishhook.

That happened a lot, that autumn. Small things catching. A laugh. A wolf carved into a door lintel. The smell of pine smoke. Once, horribly, the phrase *"wolf-light"* spoken by a fruit-seller complaining about the moon, and I had to sit down on a barrel for a full minute while my heart did something complicated and unhelpful.

Odile's theory, when I finally brought her this one, was shorter than usual.

"You gave the flame everything," she said. "But some things don't burn. They just go quiet. And quiet things wait."

"For what?"

"For someone to say the right words." She smiled, which she almost never did, and it frightened me more than the dark creatures ever had. "Or the right person. Whichever comes first."

* * *

Winter came early that year, the first winter under the new covenant, and the market moved indoors to the old tithe-hall where the stalls huddled between the pillars like sheep. My corner was near the east door, which let in a draft that I complained about professionally but secretly loved, because it meant fewer customers and more time to read.

It was a grey afternoon, the light going thin, the hall smelling of tallow and wet wool, when he came in.

I noticed him the way you notice weather — peripherally, then all at once. He was tall, dark-haired, dressed plainly in a way that didn't quite manage to look plain; there was a carriage in his shoulders that no laborer earns. He moved through the hall slowly, pausing at stalls, buying nothing, and I catalogued him out of habit: not a collector, collectors fidget; not a council man, the council was gone; not a buyer at all, because buyers walk like they already know what they want.

He walked like a man looking for something he wasn't sure existed.

Then he reached my stall, and I looked up, and the bottom dropped out of the afternoon.

His eyes were amber. Not brown-amber, not the honest amber of cheap honey — these were gold shot through with darker rings, the exact color of lamplight through whiskey, and I knew them. That was the impossible part. I had never seen this man in my life. I would have remembered him; I remember faces the way other people remember debts. And yet some animal part of me, some cellar-dwelling creature below thought and memory, looked up into his eyes and *recognized* him, the way a dog recognizes home, the way a hand recognizes a banister in the dark.

The warmth behind my ribs flared so suddenly and so hard that I gripped the edge of my table.

"You're the memory-thief," he said. His voice was low, roughened, like a road well traveled. "They told me at the gate. Said you kept a stall now. Said you were honest." Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, more the scar tissue where a smile used to live. "I told them I'd believe it when I saw it."

"Rude," I managed, "and accurate. What can I do for you?"

He didn't answer right away. He was looking at my wares — the vials, the labeled boxes, the little brass scale I used for weighing intangibles — and there was something in his face that I couldn't name, something that hurt to look at directly, like staring at a lamp. Grief, maybe. Or patience worn down to the last thread.

"You sell memories," he said.

"I buy them, mostly. Selling's for amateurs."

"And stealing?"

"Stealing's for professionals. Rates posted on the pillar. Discretion guaranteed. Nothing that matters, ever." I heard my own voice doing its market patter, bright and quick, and hated it, because he was watching me the way you watch a fire you're afraid will go out. "Unless you're here to arrest me, in which case the rates double."

"I'm not here to arrest you." He said it quietly, and something in the way he said it made me think he had, once, been the sort of man who might have. "I'm not anything anymore, actually. No office. No oath. Just—" He gestured vaguely at himself, at the plain clothes and the unplain shoulders. "A stranger."

"A stranger," I repeated.

"With a story." He set both hands flat on my table, careful, deliberate, the way you'd approach a bird you didn't want to startle. Which was absurd, because I was not a bird, I was a professional, I had stared down ash-crowned lords — I was fairly sure I had, there was a scar on my wrist that agreed with me — and yet my pulse was behaving like a debutante's. "I'd like to tell it to you. If you'll listen."

"I charge for stories."

"This one's free." His voice caught, just slightly, on the word free, as if it cost him enormously. "It's about a thief. Who broke her own rule, once, and stole something that mattered more than the world. And about what she did with it. And about—" He stopped. Closed his eyes for half a breath. When he opened them, the amber had gone glassy-bright, and I felt that flare behind my ribs again, that banked-coal heat, that terrible unnamed warmth that had haunted me all autumn. "And about a promise," he finished, "made in wolf-light, nine years ago, by two people who were sure they'd have time."

The hall went on around us. Breadwives argued. Children shrieked over the glowing thing they'd smuggled indoors. The draft off the east door stirred the labels on my vials, and none of it touched us; we stood in our own small weather, he and I, and I realized my hands were shaking and pressed them flat against my apron like a child in a lesson.

"I don't know you," I said. It came out steadier than I deserved. "I've never seen you before in my life. Whatever story you think involves me—"

"It doesn't involve you." Gentle. So gentle. "Not yet. That's rather the point. You burned it all, Xanthe. Everything. To save everyone. And everyone includes you, which means you saved yourself right out of—" He exhaled. "Out of knowing what you did. What it cost. What *we*—"

He stopped himself again. I watched him decide, visibly, deliberately, to begin again.

"My name is Ansel," he said. "I was a warden. I loved a thief. She didn't remember me, in the end, and I spent a long time deciding whether that was a mercy or a wound, and I've concluded it's both, and that I don't care, because I'd rather be a stranger she meets twice than a ghost she mourns once." He straightened. Somewhere in the hall a bell rang the hour, and he waited it out like a man who had learned to wait for things. "So. Here's my offer. No price, no debt, no magic in it anywhere — the covenant sees to that now. Just an old man's story, told to a young woman who happens to be neither of those things. One evening. Maybe two. And if at the end of it you feel nothing—" his mouth did that not-smile again, sadder this time, "—then you send me away, and I'll go, and I won't come back. I swear it on whatever I have left to swear on. Which isn't much. But it's real."

I should have sent him away immediately. Any sensible thief would have. A stranger who knows your name, who looks at you like a lamp, who speaks of promises and wolf-light and a love I had no record of owning — that's not a customer, that's a trap, or a tragedy, and I had had my fill of both.

But the warmth behind my ribs was roaring now, silent and enormous, a fire in a locked room, and when I reached inward — the way I reach into every mind but my own, the professional habit of a lifetime — I found, deep beneath the clean burn-scar where my past used to be, something new. Not a memory. Memories have edges, weight, provenance. This was softer than that. This was the shape of a feeling waiting for its facts, the way a glove waits for a hand.

Somewhere in me, something quiet had been waiting. Odile had said so. *Quiet things wait.*

For someone to say the right words. Or the right person.

"Xanthe," I said, and my voice betrayed me at last, going soft in the middle. "My name is Xanthe. Though I suppose you knew that."

"I've known it since before you did," he said. "It's a long story."

"The market closes at dusk." I heard myself say it as if from a great distance, some braver and more foolish version of me leaning over my own shoulder and pulling the lever. "There's a tavern on Tanner's Lane that does a passable stew and doesn't ask questions. If you're telling a story, you'll want a table, and I'll want wine, because I suspect I'm going to need it."

Something broke open in his face — not a smile, finally, but the ground where one would grow, thawing all at once after a long hard frost. He nodded. He stepped back from the table with the care of a man leaving a shrine, and turned toward the door, and the draft caught his coat, and—

"Ansel." His name was in my mouth before I knew I owned it. It fit. That was the horrifying, wonderful thing. It fit like the glove. He turned, and I stood there with a thousand questions and no facts to hang them on, so I said the only true thing I had: "Whatever this story is — whoever this thief was — I hope she deserved you."

He looked at me for a long moment. The lamplight-through-whiskey eyes went bright again.

"She did," he said. "She just forgot. Come to the tavern, and I'll remind you how."

He walked out into the grey afternoon, and I stood alone at my stall among my labeled vials and my brass scale and my honest, lawful, rebuilt little life, with my heart hammering like a fist on a door, and behind my ribs the quiet thing rose up singing, wordless, patient, certain —

and I began, for the first time since the altar, to be afraid of what I might learn to feel.
