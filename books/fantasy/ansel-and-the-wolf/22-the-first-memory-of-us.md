## 22. The First Memory of Us

### Xanthe

The festival was Odile's idea, which should have been my first warning.

"The valley's first starlit festival," she announced at the council table, her blind eyes aimed at a point slightly left of everyone's face, as usual. "Music. Food. Dancing. A public demonstration that the sky works again."

"It's been three weeks," said the councilwoman whose name I still hadn't bothered to learn, because some habits survive even a covenant remade. "People are still adjusting."

"People have been adjusting to forgetting for nine years," Odile said. "I think they can adjust to remembering. Besides—" and here she turned that ruined gaze on me with the precision of a woman who had never needed eyes to find a target, "—it will be good for Xanthe. She needs a night where nobody is selling anything, including herself."

I had opened my stall again. Of course I had. A woman has to eat, and it turned out that in a valley where memory could now be given and taken honestly, with consent and contracts and witnesses, the trade of a thief was suddenly a respectable profession. I had a ledger now. A *ledger*. Clients signed things. There was a waiting list.

I hated how much I liked it.

So on the night of the festival, I closed the stall early, put on the blue dress I'd owned for nine years and never had occasion to wear, and went out into a Hollowmere strung with lanterns and full of people who kept stopping mid-sentence to stare upward, as if the stars might notice the attention and leave again.

They wouldn't. I knew that now, in the way you know a thing in your bones rather than your head. The new covenant held not by sealing anything out but by letting everything through—guarded, chosen, witnessed. The dragon-fire burned at the Ember Altar like a hearth instead of a wall, and the wards sang instead of silenced, and the stars—

The stars were *everywhere*. I had lived twenty-six years under a sky with a handful of dim, apologetic lights, and it turned out the valley had been sitting beneath a river of them the whole time, held back like water behind a dam. Now they poured through. You could almost hear them, if you tilted your head wrong.

I was tilting my head wrong when a voice behind me said, "You're going to give yourself a crick in the neck."

I didn't need to turn around. There are voices you know the way you know your own hands—their weight, their weather, the particular way they go soft at the end of a sentence when the speaker is trying not to say the thing he most wants to say. I had known this voice for three weeks, and for nine years before that, and for not at all, depending on how you counted.

"Ansel," I said, and turned.

He looked—well. He looked like a man who had traded an oath-cord to an ash court for a thief's freedom and been offered wardenship of a new kind afterward, and had said yes, and was still not entirely sure the ground would hold. He wore no uniform. He wore a plain shirt and an expression like a man standing at a door he'd knocked on a hundred times in dreams.

"You came," he said.

"You asked."

"You could have said no."

"I could have said a lot of things." I shrugged. "I've been practicing saying the true ones. It's harder than it looks."

He smiled. And here is the thing I need to confess, because this whole story is about confession in one form or another: when he smiled, something in my chest turned over like a key in a lock that had been rusted shut. Not a memory. I want to be very clear about that, because for a while I kept waiting for memories, and their absence was its own kind of grief. It wasn't a memory. It was *new*. It was the present tense, doing what the present tense does, which is arrive without permission and demand to be felt.

"I brought something," he said.

"If it's a wolf, I'm not keeping it."

"It's a story."

I raised an eyebrow. "I hear stories for a living. I know exactly what they cost."

"This one's free," he said. "It's the only one I've got."

### Ansel

She said yes to the walk, and no to the wine, and maybe to both by the time we reached the edge of the square, which was Xanthe in miniature: a woman who answered every question with three questions and then did whatever she had already decided to do.

We ended up on the low wall by the old market well, the one that had run dry for a generation and started running again the week the covenant remade itself. The festival spread out below us—lanterns, fiddles, Odile holding court by the cake table with the serene tyranny of a woman who had helped break the world and been forgiven for it, mostly, and had decided not to waste the reprieve.

"You said you'd tell me a story," Xanthe said. "I've been waiting three weeks. I assumed you were polishing it."

"I was," I said. "It doesn't polish. It's a true story. True stories just get heavier the more you carry them."

"Those are the best kind," she said. "I steal—*buy*," she corrected, with the ghost of her old wry wince, "—the best kind professionally."

So I told her.

I told her about a border post in the last autumn of the old covenant, and a young warden who was very serious about his duty and very bad at hiding the fact that he watched a certain smuggler's lanterns come down the ridge road with more interest than the regulations strictly required.

"Smuggler," she repeated. "Me."

"You. You used to run memory-silk over the line before the wards tightened. Little things. Songs. A grandmother's lullaby for a family who'd lost theirs. Nothing that mattered."

"Nothing that mattered," she echoed softly. "My rule."

"Your rule," I agreed. "You broke it once. For a night that mattered."

I told her about the night in wolf-light, when the wards had flickered and I'd found her on the wrong side of the line, and instead of arresting her I'd sat down in the grass beside her because she'd asked me to, and because I had already, without noticing the exact moment it happened, stopped being able to refuse her anything.

I told her about a promise. I didn't say the words of it—not yet. Some words you hand over all at once or not at all, and I wasn't sure she could carry them yet.

She was quiet for a long time. Below us, the fiddles played something old that nobody in the valley had heard in nine years, and half the dancers didn't know the steps and were learning them from each other, badly, laughing.

"I remember the grass," she said finally. "I don't remember you. I remember—cold air, and a feeling like standing at the edge of a high place. And I remember thinking, *this is the last good thing I will ever do.*" She looked at me. "That's not a memory of you. That's a memory of me, standing where you were about to be."

"It's a start," I said.

"Ansel. What happened to it? To us. If we—" she made a small, frustrated gesture that took in the whole of the unwritten history between us, "—then where did it *go*?"

"You burned it," I said. "At the altar. To reforge the covenant. You gave the flame everything you were. Including me."

"I know that. I consented to that. I *chose*—" She stopped. Breathed. "I know what I chose. What I'm asking is why you're still here. You got your wardenship back. New covenant, new terms, wardens who choose. You could have taken a post anywhere. You chose Hollowmere. You chose a market stall and a woman who doesn't know you."

"I chose a woman who *chose the world over herself*," I said. "I figured the least I could do was return the favor."

"That's not an answer."

"No," I agreed. "It's a down payment. Here's the rest."

I reached into my pocket and took out the thing I had carried for three weeks, not sure I would ever have the courage, and set it in her palm.

A wolf-tooth. Small, yellowed, old. The one I'd carried at my throat through nine years of patrols, through the dead wards and the long dark, through the ash court, through the altar. It had been hers, once—given to me the night in the wolf-light, as a token, as a joke, as a promise: *So you can find me when I'm lost.*

"I was never lost," I said. "You were. I kept it so I could find you when you were ready."

She stared at the tooth in her hand. And I watched something happen that I had been told about by Odile, and warned about by Odile, and hoped for despite Odile: the new covenant, the honest one, the one that let memory move freely between people who consented to the exchange—I watched it do its quiet work. Not restoration. Not the old memory clawing its way back from the dead.

Something better. Something *newly made*.

### Xanthe

The tooth was warm.

That's the first thing. Not body-warm—*hearth*-warm, ember-warm, the warmth of a thing that had been held against a heart for nine years until it learned the rhythm. I sat there on the cold stone wall with a dead wolf's tooth in my palm and a man's whole history pouring off it like heat off a stove, and the fragments started.

Not returning. I want to be precise, because I am a professional and precision is the only integrity I've ever had: they were not *returning*. A returning memory comes home like a bird to a window it remembers. These came like birds to a window they'd never seen, and pressed against the glass, and asked to be let in, and when I let them—

Grass under my hands, cold with night. A warden's cloak spread over my shoulders because I'd been stupid enough to cross the line in autumn. His laugh—I had his laugh, suddenly, *newly*, the way it went low and surprised, as if it embarrassed him. The weight of his head against mine. The words of the promise, arriving not from the past but from *him, now, telling me*, and my own heart answering them as if it had been keeping them warm all this time in a room I didn't know I had.

*So you can find me when I'm lost,* I heard myself say, in a voice nine years young, pressing the tooth into a young man's hand.

"I found you," Ansel said quietly, beside me, in the present tense, watching my face the way you watch a fire you're afraid to hope in. "What do you see?"

"You," I said. My voice came out wrecked. I cleared my throat and tried for wry and missed by a mile. "Younger. Stupider. In love, apparently, which—rude of her, honestly, falling for a warden. She had a reputation."

"She had a rule," he said. "Never steal anything that matters."

"And she broke it." I looked down at the tooth. "The worst thing she ever stole was the one thing she couldn't give back. She stole herself out of the story."

"She gave herself back," he said. "At the altar. All of it. That's the part I can't get past, Xanthe. You burned *everything*. And now you're sitting here with a hole where nine years used to be, and I'm asking you to—" He stopped. He was very good at stopping, this man. It was one of the first things the fragments taught me about him: he stopped before the hard sentence, and then said it anyway. "I'm asking you to choose me without the memory. To let it be new. I don't want to be a restoration. I don't want to be the ghost of a boy you loved once. I want—"

"I know what you want," I said.

Because I did. That was the terrible, miraculous thing. Nine years gone, and I knew him—not the history, but the *him*. The way he stopped before hard sentences. The way his voice went soft at the end when he was trying not to hope. The way he held his hands still when he most wanted to reach for something, because he'd been trained that reaching was taking, and he had spent his whole life learning the difference.

I knew him the way you know a song you've never heard, the first time the chorus comes around.

"Here's my problem," I said. "Professionally speaking. I deal in memories. I know exactly what they're worth, and I know they're not worth anything like what people pay for them. A memory is a story you tell yourself about someone who isn't in the room anymore." I turned the tooth over in my palm. "And this man you're telling me about—the boy on the border—he's not in the room. He's a story. He's *your* story."

"And?"

"And I'm not interested in him." I looked up. "I'm interested in the one who sat through three weeks of my stall, buying nothing, waiting for nothing, asking for nothing. I'm interested in the man who told a stranger a true story on a cold wall because it was the only thing he had. He's not a memory, Ansel. He's Tuesday. He's *now*. And I have a professional rule, newly amended: never steal anything that matters." I closed his hand around the tooth, and kept my hand closed around his. "So I'm not stealing you. I'm keeping you. In the open. Where it's legal."

He made a sound—half laugh, half something that had been waiting nine years to get out—and then he kissed me, and it was not the kiss of a memory restored. It was the kiss of a first kiss, clumsy and certain, the present tense doing what it does.

Below us, the fiddles swung into the old festival tune, and the whole valley sang the chorus it was relearning, badly, together.

### Ansel

Later—much later, when the lanterns had burned to embers and the dancers had gone home and the stars had wheeled a slow hand's-width across the river of the sky—we walked the ridge road north, out of habit, out of love of it, out of the sheer extravagant novelty of walking a border that was a door instead of a wall.

The wards sang as we passed. Not the old silence. A sound like wind through a house where every window is open.

"Odile wants to interview us," Xanthe said. "For the archive. 'The First Memory of Us,' she's calling it. She's already written the title. I checked. She wrote it before we even—"

"Before the festival?"

"Before the *escape from the ash court*, probably. That woman has been narrating our lives for years and only recently got the decency to admit it."

I laughed, and she looked pleased with herself, and the tooth hung at my throat again where it had always belonged, warm with two hearts now instead of one.

At the ridge's crest we stopped, because you always stop there, because the valley falls away below you like a cupped hand and the outer realm stretches beyond it, lit and open and full of roads. Somewhere out there, Sera Vance was riding toward a border crossing where her sister waited, carrying the truth instead of a trade. Somewhere out there, the ash court was crumbling into honest stone, and the stone was learning to be only stone. Somewhere behind us, in Hollowmere, a dry well ran with water and an old woman had remembered her village's name and wept over it for an hour and then had it carved above her door.

"Ansel," Xanthe said.

"Mm."

"The promise. The night in the wolf-light. You never said the words of it. You told me everything around them."

"I was saving them," I said.

"Saving them for what?"

I turned to her. The stars were in her hair, all of them, the whole shameless river of them, and she was looking at me the way she had looked at me on a border nine years and three weeks and one lifetime ago—like a woman standing at the edge of a high place, deciding the fall was worth it.

"For a night when nobody in either realm needed saving," I said. "When the covenant held and the stars were out and the only thing left to do was the thing I should have done the first time." I took her hand. "Xanthe Marchetti, thief of small hours, keeper of the flame, breaker of every rule worth breaking. Stay. Not because you remember. Not because you owe the past anything. Stay because of Tuesday."

She was quiet for a moment, and I had learned to let her be quiet; it was where she did her truest work.

"Ansel Yarrow," she said at last, "wolf-warden, oathless, exile, teller of true stories. You are aware that I am a thief, that I have a ledger, and that everything I keep, I keep honestly now."

"I'm aware."

"Then witness it," she said, and kissed me under the whole restored sky, and the wards sang, and somewhere below us the valley began its first starlit festival in nine years, and somewhere ahead of us lay every Tuesday we hadn't met yet.

The stars, for the record, stayed.

We counted on it.
