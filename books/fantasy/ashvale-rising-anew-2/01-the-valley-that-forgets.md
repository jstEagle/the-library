## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The roosters of Ashvale have no respect for the divine, which is one of the reasons I like them.

They crow at first light regardless of what the shrine says about sacred hours, and they crow at me specifically, because my window faces the coop and old Marta's birds have decided over twelve years that I am somehow responsible for the sun. I've stopped arguing. Arguing with chickens is a young mercenary's habit, and I am trying very hard to be neither.

I rose, as I always do, before the mist had finished deciding whether it was fog or cloud. Ashvale mornings are like that — the valley can't quite commit to being one place or another until midmorning, when the sun finally burns through and everything sharpens into green. From my window above the guardhouse I could see the whole of it laid out: the wheat fields going gold at their edges, the millpond holding a perfect copy of the sky, the crooked line of rooftops along the stream, and above it all, on its shelf of grey stone, the shrine.

Twelve years I've watched that view. Twelve years of patrols and rites and the particular silence of a valley where nothing from outside is permitted to settle — not news, not travelers longer than a night, not magic. Especially not magic.

I dressed in the dark out of habit, buckled on the sword even though the last thing in Ashvale to threaten anyone was a badger with opinions about the henhouse, and went down into the cold.

* * *

Morning patrol follows the same shape it has followed since before me, since the first warden traced the boundary stones with chalk and prayer. The valley sits in a bowl of hills like an egg in a cup, and the rim of that cup is marked by twelve stones, one for each hour of the god's silence — or so the oldest villagers say, though I suspect they invented that detail for the poetry of it. Farmers love a good number.

I walked the eastern arc first, because the eastern arc has the best light and I am honest enough with myself to admit that half of ritual is aesthetics. Dew soaked my boots within the first hundred paces. The wheat stood in ranks, patient as soldiers who'd been told to wait for an order that never comes, and beyond it the hills climbed up into pine and shadow.

At the third stone I did what wardens do. I pressed my palm flat against it and waited.

Nothing. Same as yesterday. Same as every day for twelve years.

People imagine that guarding a silent god must feel like standing watch over a sleeping giant — every creak a portent, every stillness a held breath. The truth is duller and stranger. The silence doesn't feel like held breath. It feels like an empty room. You knock, and the knock just... stops, against stone that has never once answered, and you stand there feeling like a fool for knocking, and then you do it again tomorrow anyway.

Because that's the rite. Not because it works. Because it's ours.

I made the circuit. Checked the boundary markers for tampering — there never is any, but checking is the job, and a job you do without checking is a job you've already quit. Spoke a word to Tam Weller, who was already in his field though the light could barely find him, and got back the same grunt he's given me every morning for a decade. It's practically a sonnet between us now.

By the time I came down past the millpond, the village was awake and pretending it hadn't been. Smoke from breakfast fires. The smith's hammer finding its rhythm. Children running the ditch-banks with the specific lawlessness of children in a place where nothing has ever gone wrong.

That last part is the point of everything. I try to remember that on the days the silence gets heavy.

* * *

Here is the rule of Ashvale, the only rule that matters, the one carved over the shrine door in letters worn soft by weather and thumbs: *What lies beyond the ridge stays beyond the ridge.*

No one speaks of the outside. Not the wars — and there are always wars, somewhere over those hills, kingdoms chewing on each other like dogs over a bone. Not the roads, not the cities, not the names of kings or the prices of grain in markets we'll never see. Travelers come through maybe twice a year, lost shepherds mostly, peddlers who took a wrong turn, and they're fed, bedded, pointed back the way they came, and forgotten on purpose. Elder Maren keeps a ledger of names — writes each visitor down in her careful hand, and I used to think it was so we'd remember them.

It occurs to me now, writing this, that I never once asked why a valley dedicated to forgetting kept such careful records. But that's a thought for later chapters of my life, and this morning I didn't have it. This morning I had porridge.

Breakfast in the guardhouse is Dain and me and whoever's on rotation, which this season is nobody, because Ashvale's entire defense force is two aging mercenaries and a bell nobody's rung in living memory. Dain sat across from me working through his oats like they'd insulted him personally, which is how he eats everything.

"Third stone's damp," I said.

"Stones are damp," he said. "It's morning."

"Boundary's clear."

"Boundary's always clear." He looked up. He has a face built for scowling, all brow and jaw, softened only slightly by the grey coming in at his temples. "You say that like it's news."

"It is news. Good news. You're supposed to be pleased."

"I'll be pleased when something interesting happens." He said it the way he says everything, flat, but I caught the edge under it. Dain's oath — his actual sworn oath, the words he spoke over the shrine stones fifteen years ago — includes truth above all things. He meant it when he said it. Some days I think the silence of this valley is slowly killing him, a man built for honesty living in a place whose founding principle is a polite lie about the rest of the world.

"Eat your oats," I told him.

"You eat your oats."

It was, all things considered, a warm exchange. We've been friends long enough that hostility is just another dialect of affection.

* * *

Midmorning belongs to the shrine.

I climbed the path alone, as the rite requires — the warden walks up unaccompanied, speaks the morning words, and comes down having heard nothing, and the village pretends this is normal because for them it is. For me it's the hardest hour of the day, and I would sooner swallow glass than tell them that.

The shrine is older than the village. Older than any village, if you look at the stonework honestly — the blocks fit without mortar, tight as teeth, and some of them carry carvings no one can read anymore. Lines and loops in a script that predates every alphabet the traders ever sketched for us. The villagers call it the god's handwriting and leave it be, the way you leave a sleeping dog be.

Inside, it's cool and dim and smells of old smoke and older stone. No idol. That unsettled visitors, back when visitors were more common — a shrine with no statue, no image, nothing to look at except the walls themselves and the single flat altar stone worn smooth by centuries of hands. You worship the absence here. You pray to the empty air and trust that emptiness is listening.

Ashvale. The silent god. The one who went quiet.

Every child in the valley learns the story before they learn to write: long ago the gods warred, and the world burned, and when the ash settled, Ashvale — greatest of them, or kindest of them, depending which grandmother is telling it — fell silent. Withdrew. Sealed the valley against magic so that what happened before could never happen again, and left mortals to keep the peace in the quiet. A gift, the story goes. A sanctuary bought with divine grief.

I've recited that story a thousand times. I believed it the way you believe the floor will hold you — completely, without thinking, right up until the floor does something else.

But that morning the floor was still holding. That morning I knelt at the altar, laid my palms on the smooth stone, and spoke the morning words in the old tongue — the only place that tongue survives, in these rites, passed mouth to mouth among wardens like a candle flame shielded from wind:

*"Ashvale, keeper of the quiet, we hold the valley. We hold the peace. Speak, if it pleases you, or sleep, if sleep is yours."*

And then I waited. The way I've waited every morning for twelve years. Long enough for the dust motes to finish their slow turning in the light from the doorway. Long enough for the cold of the stone to seep through my knees.

Nothing.

Of course nothing. There has never been anything. And here is the strange arithmetic of faith: twelve years of nothing should add up to doubt, but instead it added up to devotion, because someone — I, specifically, I — decided early on that the silence itself was the answer. *Sleep, if sleep is yours.* The god sleeps. The silence is the gift. My job is not to wake it but to keep watch while it dreams.

I told myself that so many times it wore a groove in me, like the altar stone. Smooth. Believable. Hollow.

I stayed a while longer than the rite required. I do that sometimes. Kneeling in the dim with my hands on stone that has never once warmed under them, and doing the thing I don't let myself do on patrol or at breakfast or anywhere the village can see: grieving. Quietly. For a god I've served blind for twelve years and never heard so much as a whisper from. For the warden before me, Halvard, who died still waiting. For whatever it was I actually wanted when I swore my oath at nineteen and meant it with my whole stupid burning heart.

I wanted to matter to something vast. That's the embarrassing truth of it. Every mercenary wants it — we just dress it up as pay or glory. I found a god who'd gone silent and thought, *there, that one needs me.* Someone has to keep the watch. Someone has to speak the words into the empty room.

Nobody tells you that the empty room might be empty because something in it is hiding.

* * *

Afternoon I spent on the small maintenance that makes up most of a warden's life: re-thatching a corner of the guardhouse roof while Dain held the ladder and offered commentary, mending the hinge on the shrine gate, settling a dispute between two farmers about a fence post with the gravity of a border negotiation. The fence post dispute was genuinely the most dangerous thing to happen in Ashvale that day, and both farmers thanked me afterward as though I'd disarmed a siege.

Evening came down gold and slow. I ate at the common table — Maren presiding at the head, straight-backed and silver-haired, her ledger closed beside her plate as always. She asked after the boundary. I reported it clear. She nodded, satisfied, and moved the conversation along to the apple harvest with the practiced ease of a woman who has steered a hundred conversations away from the ridge.

That's Maren's whole art, I realize now. Steering. I thought she was just private. Everyone in Ashvale is private; it's in the water. But there's private and there's *practiced*, and I was too comfortable to know the difference.

After the meal, some of us lingered. The children played a chasing game in the yard, shrieking, and old Sorrel played his fiddle badly and everyone pretended otherwise, and for an hour or two the valley was exactly what the story promised: sanctuary. Warm. Whole. A place where nothing from outside could reach.

Dain found me at the edge of the firelight, both of us watching the kids run their circles.

"Quiet day," he said.

"Best kind."

He grunted. Then, quieter, in the tone he uses maybe twice a year: "You hear anything this morning?"

"No."

"Me neither." He turned his cup in his hands. "Twelve years, Freya. Fifteen for me. Sometimes I wonder what we're keeping watch *for*. If the watch ended tomorrow — if the god woke, or died, or never existed at all — would anything change?"

I'd wondered it too. More than I admitted. But I gave him the answer I'd polished smooth: "The valley changes. Everything we are is the watch."

He looked at me a long moment, firelight in his eyes, and I couldn't tell if he found comfort in that or despair. Maybe both. Maybe that's what faith is — both at once, held carefully, like a full cup on a long walk.

"Get some sleep," he said finally. "Tomorrow's patrol again."

"Tomorrow's patrol again," I agreed.

* * *

I write this now by lamplight, and I'll set down the last true thing about that ordinary day, the thing I didn't know was ordinary until it wasn't.

The valley forgets. That's its nature, its blessing, its whole design. It forgot the war. It forgot the gods. It forgets every traveler within a season, their names going soft in Maren's ledger like pressed flowers fading. It had begun, I think, to forget me — to wear my edges smooth the way it wore the shrine inscriptions, until I was less a person than a function, *the warden*, performing the rite, hearing nothing, kneeling in the empty room and calling the emptiness holy.

I was content. Gods help me, I was actually content. Comfortable routine, honest work, a friend across the breakfast table, children shrieking in the yard, a god asleep behind a wall of silence that I guarded from the outside like a faithful dog guarding a door it has never opened.

If you'd told me that evening, as I banked the fire and blew out the lamp, that everything I knew about Ashvale was a cover story — that the silence was not a gift but a seal, that the god I grieved for had buried something in the ground beneath my knees every morning, that within a season I would break my oath in front of the whole village and be glad of it — I'd have called you a liar and reached for my sword.

Instead, the next dusk brought a knock at the valley gate.

But that's tomorrow's watch. Tonight there was only the mist coming down off the hills, the wheat gold in the last light, and the shrine on its shelf of stone, silent as it had always been, silent as I believed it would always be.

I slept well. That's the part that shames me most, looking back.

I slept well, and the god did not answer, and somewhere south of the ridge — though I didn't know it yet, wouldn't know it for another handful of dawns — a child was walking toward my valley carrying fire in their mouth.
