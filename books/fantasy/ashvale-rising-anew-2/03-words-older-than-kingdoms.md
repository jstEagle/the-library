## 3. Words Older Than Kingdoms

Here is what I know about myself: my name is Wren. I am small for my age, or possibly exactly the right size and everyone else is too big. I know how to whistle through a blade of grass. I know that soldiers in white cloaks smell like cold iron and candle smoke, and I know that when they say *vow* they mean something you bleed on.

Everything else is fire.

Not real fire — I'm not burning now, I checked, twice, thoroughly, while the tall mercenary watched me with the expression of someone deciding whether to feed a stray animal. The fire is in my head, behind my eyes, where memories should be. It's always there if I look for it, which I try not to do, mostly, except at night when there's nothing else to look at.

* * *

The room they gave me smells of cedar and lamp oil. There's a bed with a blanket rough as a sheep's temper, a window with a shutter, and a door that locks from the outside, which the mercenary — Freya, they said their name was, Freya Reyes, said like it was a password they'd been using so long it had worn smooth — pretended wasn't a lock at all.

"It sticks," they said. "Old wood. Swells in the damp."

I looked at them. They looked at me. Neither of us believed a word of it, and I think we both respected the other more for saying it anyway.

I didn't sleep. I sat on the bed and did inventory, because inventory is a thing you can hold onto when everything else has been carried off south by people whose faces I can't remember either.

Inventory: one blanket. One window. One moon, full, sitting in the gap between shutter slats like an eye. One word stuck in my throat that comes out sideways when I'm scared, and I have been scared for — I counted on my fingers — a very long time.

The word came out around midnight. I didn't mean it to. A log settled in the hearth downstairs with a crack like a whip, and my mouth opened all on its own and said:

*Asha-velen, toru-keth.*

It sounded like water over stones. It sounded older than the house, older than the valley, older maybe than the idea of houses. And from somewhere below me — below the floor, below the cellar, below whatever cellars sit on top of — something hummed back.

I pulled the blanket over my head. This is a strategy I recommend to no one over the age of six, but I was operating at reduced capacity.

* * *

In the morning Freya brought bread and cheese and a slate, and set the slate down like it was a peace offering between nations.

"You spoke last night," they said.

"Did I."

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"That." They sat down across from me, all elbows and patience, a person built out of long waits. "The thing where you answer a question with a smaller question until the conversation dies of natural causes. I invented that trick. You're not old enough to use it on me."

I tore the bread in half and ate my half slowly, to establish that I could not be rushed. "You want to know what I said."

"I want to know what language it was."

"I don't know its name. I just know it." I swallowed. "It knows me, sort of. It comes out when—" Fire, said the place behind my eyes. White cloaks. Running. Being carried, someone's heart hammering against my ear like a fist on a door. "—when I'm frightened," I finished, in ordinary words, because some things you keep even from yourself.

Freya nodded slowly, the way people nod when you've confirmed something they were hoping was a dream. Then they picked up the chalk and held it out to me.

"Say it again. Slowly. I'm going to write it down."

"Why?"

"Because words that come out of children in the night and make shrines hum are either very important or very dangerous, and in my experience the important ones are usually both." Their face did something complicated. "Also because I've spent twelve years talking to a god who doesn't answer, and last night something answered. Forgive me if I want to write it down before it changes its mind."

I took the chalk. My hand shook, which annoyed me, so I pressed harder than I needed to and the first letters came out fat and crooked.

"Asha-velen," I said.

Freya wrote it. Then they stopped writing, because their hand had stopped working, which I could see happening from the outside — fingers going stiff, breath going shallow, the whole machinery of the person seizing up around one small sound.

"What," I said.

"That's the valley's name." They turned the slate toward me, and there it was in their blunt soldier's handwriting: ASHA-VELEN. "That's the name carved over the shrine door. In letters nobody living can read."

* * *

We worked through the morning. It went like this: I would say a phrase, usually without meaning to, usually when some noise startled me — a cart outside, a gull, Freya shifting their weight, which they did constantly, like a man standing on a deck only he could feel. Freya would write it down, sound by sound, and then go very quiet and stare at the wall as though the wall owed them money.

By midday the slate was full and they'd started on the back, and I had learned three things about Freya Reyes.

One: they are left-handed but write with their right, and their right-handed writing looks like a spider drowned in ink.

Two: they flinch at certain phrases. Not all of them. When I said *toru-keth*, nothing. When I said *sel-asharu, venn toru* — which felt, coming out of my mouth, like the feeling of a door closing — Freya put down the chalk, walked to the window, stood there for the length of ten slow breaths, and came back without explaining. I didn't ask. We were building something, the two of us, out of chalk dust and silence, and part of building it was agreeing which questions could wait.

Three: they are lonely in a way I recognized immediately, the way you recognize weather. It was in how they listened. Nobody had listened to Freya in years, I'd have bet my blanket on it — really listened, leaning in, hungry — and now here was a child babbling in a dead tongue and Freya drinking every syllable like the first water after a dry season.

I knew that thirst. I'd been carrying it south for however long I'd been carrying anything.

So when Freya asked, carefully, casually, in the tone of a person defusing themselves, "Do you remember where you learned it?" — I surprised both of us by answering honestly.

"No. But I remember who used it." I closed my eyes and let the fire come, just to the edge, just close enough to see by. "A woman. She smelled of rosemary and blood. She held my face and said—" the sounds rose up my throat like water finding a crack, "*—venn asharu, toru-keth imma sel.*" I opened my eyes. "And then she pushed me into a drain and I heard her stop screaming."

The kitchen was very quiet.

"She was speaking it to comfort you," Freya said finally. Their voice had gravel in it. "Or to protect you. Those might be the same words, in that language. I don't know yet. But the shape of it—" They rubbed their face. "Wren, the shrine inscriptions. The oldest ones, the ones under the newer carvings, the ones even Maren Corvane pretends not to see. Some of these phrases match. Word for word. I've copied those stones since I was younger than you are, and I never knew what I was copying, and now you're sitting in my kitchen saying it like it's your mother tongue."

"Maybe it is," I said. "Maybe it's everybody's mother tongue and everyone forgot except me."

"Words don't survive alone. Languages need mouths, whole generations of them." Freya stared at the slate. "Which means either you're carrying something that should be impossible, or there were others, once, and they're gone."

Neither of those options sounded like good news, and I said so.

"No," Freya agreed. "They're terrible news. Both of them." And then — this is the part I keep returning to, the part I'll still be turning over when I'm old, if I get to be old — they smiled. Small and crooked and real. "Terrible news travels better with bread. Eat."

* * *

That afternoon they took me to the shrine.

I want to be clear about what I expected: nothing good. Every stone building I have any memory of is attached to bad things — fire, mostly, and men in white. So when Freya walked me up the hill path, past the wheat gone gold at the edges, toward a low grey building crouched under the oldest tree I had ever seen, my stomach did a slow roll and I grabbed their sleeve without asking permission.

They let me. That mattered more than I could say then, and almost more than I can say now.

"It's just a shrine," they said. "No priests. No services. The god doesn't take visitors."

"The god doesn't take anyone's visitors," I said. "That's the whole problem with gods."

Something crossed their face — quick, like a fish under ice. "You've met others?"

"I've met *stories* about others." I had. I was sure I had. I couldn't find them anywhere when I reached for them, which was becoming a familiar and horrible sensation, like patting your pockets for something essential and finding only holes.

Inside, the shrine was cool and dim and smelled of dust and old smoke. Stones lined the walls, floor to ceiling, covered in carving — some of it recent enough to read, most of it worn down to suggestion, and beneath everything, cut deep and sharp as the day it was made, a band of lettering that ran around the whole room like a river of marks.

My feet stopped moving on their own. My mouth opened on its own. I was learning, in those days, how little of me asked permission.

"*Sel-asharu, venn toru,*" I said. "*Imma keth. Imma keth ashavelen.*"

The hum started in the floor. It came up through my boots, through my bones, and the air in the shrine went thick and bright the way air goes before a storm, and every hair on my arms stood up and saluted.

Freya had gone white. I watched them grip the edge of a stone bench and watch me with an expression I couldn't read — fear, yes, but underneath the fear something rawer. Recognition. The look of a person hearing a voice they'd given up on.

"That's the threshold inscription," they said quietly. "Word for word. It's the first thing carved in this room, older than everything else." They crouched down to my level, which they did a lot, I noticed; they never loomed if they could help it. "What does it mean? Do you know? Does it tell you?"

I listened inside myself, to the place where the language lived. It wasn't like remembering. It was like being remembered — like the words had been waiting in me for someone to ask.

"'The silence is a vow,'" I translated slowly, feeling my way. "'The vow is a door. The door is Ashvale.'" I frowned. "There's more. Underneath. A second line, deeper in the stone, but it's..." I pressed my palm flat against the wall, against the old letters, and the hum climbed into my teeth. "...it's not finished. Or it's not *readable*. Like half a sentence."

"Half a sentence," Freya repeated.

"Like someone started to write something true and then stopped." I pulled my hand back. The hum faded reluctantly, like a dog told to stay. "Why would someone stop?"

Freya stood very still for a long moment. Outside, the wind moved through the great tree, and the light through the doorway shifted, and somewhere down in the village a dog barked at absolutely nothing, the way dogs do when the world is being normal.

"Because the truth was dangerous," they said at last. "Or because finishing it would have cost something. Or—" They stopped. Started again, quieter. "Or because whoever carved it hoped someone would come along someday who could finish reading it. Finish it *for* them."

"Did someone send me?"

"I don't know." They looked at me then, straight on, and I saw how tired they were, tired in the marrow, the kind of tired that comes from keeping watch over a silence for twelve years. "But I know you're freezing, and the bread's gone, and tomorrow we start matching every word you know against every stone in this room. All right?"

"All right," I said.

"And Wren." They hesitated, and for a moment I thought the moment was going to pass them by entirely, the way moments do for people who've been alone too long. But they pushed through it. "Last night. When you spoke and the shrine answered. I haven't heard anything answer in twelve years. I'd forgotten what it sounded like. What it *felt* like." Their jaw worked. "Thank you."

"You're welcome," I said, because I didn't know what else to say, and because it was true, and because I was beginning to understand that Freya Reyes was a person who collected true things the way other people collect coins — carefully, and never spending them.

* * *

That night I slept, actually slept, for the first time since the drain, since the rosemary-and-blood woman, since the south. No fire. Just a dream of walking through a field of wheat with someone tall walking beside me, neither of us talking, both of us fine with it.

I woke near dawn to voices below my window — Freya's, low and tight, and another voice, older, a woman's, dry as August.

"—a stranger child appears speaking the dead tongue and you've hidden them in your *kitchen*, Freya. The council will want—"

"The council will want what it always wants, Maren. To be told nothing troubling. Consider it done."

"Wren," the woman said, pronouncing my name like she was checking it for counterfeit. "Where did the child come from, Freya?"

Silence. Long enough that I leaned closer to the shutter.

"They came from the north road," Freya said. "Alone. That's all I know."

And I lay back in the dark, holding my breath, and understood two things at once, the way you sometimes understand things, all in one piece, like a bone snapping.

First: Freya had just lied for me. I didn't know how I knew — I hadn't known them long enough to know their honest voice from any other — but I knew, the way the language knew me, without asking permission.

Second: whatever was coming for me had names now. It had a council, and an elder, and a woman named Maren whose voice sounded like a gate closing.

I pulled the blanket over my head.

This time, it worked.
