## 4. The Council of Nine

The summons came at midmorning, delivered by Tam the cooper's boy, who had the particular talent of looking like he was delivering a message and apologizing for it in the same breath.

"Elder Corvane wants you at the meeting house," he said. "She wants you *now*, Captain Reyes. She said—" he swallowed— "she said to tell you not to stop anywhere on the way."

"Did she now."

"She said that too."

I tipped him a copper because it wasn't his fault, and because I needed the small ritual of it—the ordinary transaction of coin for errands—to steady something that had begun to rattle loose in my chest since dusk the night before. Twelve years in Ashvale, and I had never once been summoned with instructions attached. Maren Corvane did not send instructions. She sent invitations that were shaped exactly like orders, and you came, and you sat, and you told her what she already knew, and everyone pretended this was governance.

I stopped at my cottage anyway. Not to delay—I told myself it was not to delay—but because Wren was asleep on my floor pallet with their knees drawn up like a comma, breathing in the shallow, watchful rhythm of someone who had learned that sleep was when bad things found you. I stood in the doorway longer than I meant to. The child's pack lay open beside them: a tin cup worn through on one side, a wooden bird with one wing, a scrap of cloth embroidered in thread that had no color I could name. And beneath all of it, wrapped in oilcloth, the thing I had taken from them while they slept—not stolen, I'd told myself, just *held*—the fragment of pale stone that hummed against my palm like a trapped moth.

The shrine stones had sung last night. In living memory, nothing in Ashvale had ever sung.

I covered the fragment back up, tucked it under the loose floorboard by the hearth where I kept the things I didn't want found, which until yesterday had been exactly nothing, and went to lie to nine people I loved.

* * *

The meeting house had been a tithe barn once, back before anyone decided Ashvale didn't owe tithes to anybody. Its beams were black oak older than the village charter, and its long table seated the Council of Nine with room left over for the particular emptiness of people waiting to be difficult. Maren sat at the head. She always sat at the head, though technically the council rotated chairs monthly as a gesture toward equality that fooled no one, least of all Maren, whose chair had developed the specific grooves of permanent occupation.

To her right sat Dain, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a man watching weather come in over hills he couldn't reach. He didn't look at me when I entered. That was the first bad sign. Dain looked at everyone; it was how he counted exits.

"Captain." Maren's voice was dry as August grass. "Sit."

I sat. The chair scraped. Somewhere outside a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"You'll tell me," Maren said, "why Tam Hallow saw a stranger at your gate at dusk yesterday. A child, by his account, carried into your house bleeding from the feet. You'll tell me why your shutters stayed closed past dawn. And you'll tell me why, when Aldous went to fetch water at the common well this morning, he heard singing coming from your chimney."

"Singing," I said.

"That's what he said. Singing. Or humming. He said it sounded like the well does sometimes, if you put your ear down far enough." Her eyes were gray flint, and they did not blink enough. "Freya. There is no rule in this valley more old or more simple than the one about strangers. We do not take them in. We do not feed them. We point them back the way they came, and we forget their faces, and we certainly do not carry them bleeding into our houses and close our shutters like we're hiding something."

"The child was dying," I said. "You want me to point a dying child back down the mountain?"

"I want you to tell me the truth."

And there it was. The whole trap laid out neat as snares on a game trail, because the truth was a country I could not enter without bringing everything behind me—the language, the stone, the shrine stones humming like struck bells—and I could not bring any of it without breaking the valley open along seams nobody knew it had.

"I am telling you the truth," I said, and heard my own voice do something it had never done in twelve years of honest service. It smoothed itself out. It went flat and even and reasonable, a voice like a well-tended road. "A child collapsed at my gate at dusk. Hurt feet, fever, half-starved. I gave water and willow bark and let them sleep. They're still sleeping. When they can walk, they walk on. That's the whole of it."

Maren studied me the way she studied a jar of preserves that smelled slightly off. "Where did the child come from?"

"The north ridge, by the state of their boots."

"No one lives on the north ridge."

"People pass through. Traders lost, pilgrims off the shrine road."

"The shrine road hasn't seen a pilgrim in nine years, Freya. You know that better than anyone. You're the one who counts them."

Gods, but she was good. She wasn't angry—that would have been easier. She was *precise*, laying each fact down like a card she knew the value of, watching which ones made me flinch. And every fact was true, which is the worst kind of weapon to defend against, because you can't dismiss it. You can only stand there and lie again, on top of the first lie, building a little tower of falsehoods and hoping it holds your weight.

"They may have come off the high passes," I said. "There are shepherd tracks up there nobody's mapped since the border wars."

"And the singing?"

"A fever dream. Children sing fevers. Ask any mother in this valley."

Maren leaned back. For a long moment the only sound was the wind worrying the thatch, and Dain's boot tapping a slow, traitorous rhythm against the table leg.

Then Dain spoke, and saved me, and I have never forgiven him for how much it cost him to do it.

"Maren." His voice was easy, unhurried, the voice he used on frightened horses. "I was there last night. Freya sent for me near midnight—child burning up with fever, and Freya wanting a second pair of hands in case it turned. I saw what Freya saw. A kid. Half-dead, scared out of their wits, talking nonsense in some tongue I've never heard. No pack worth robbing, no weapons, no mark of any order or company I know. If it was a spy, they're the worst-trained spy in the history of spying." He shrugged. "It's a stray. Freya's got a soft spot for strays—we all know it, it's why the cats own the granary. Let the child sleep it off and be gone in a day or two, and we'll none of us remember the face. That's the rule, isn't it? Forget the face."

Maren's gaze moved between us like a lamp flame looking for a draft. "You vouch for this."

"With my name on it," Dain said, and my stomach dropped, because I knew what that phrase cost a man like Dain. He'd been born to a family of oath-keepers—his grandfather had been a judge somewhere south, before the south stopped having judges—and Dain treated truth the way other men treated religion: sincerely, constantly, and with a low simmering terror of the day he'd fail it. "If the child turns out to be trouble, you can have my seat on the council and my share of the autumn slaughter both."

Silence. Maren looked at me one moment longer, and whatever she saw—or didn't see—she folded it away somewhere behind her eyes, in the drawer where she kept things she intended to reopen later.

"Very well," she said. "The child stays until they can walk. Then they go, and we speak of it never. Freya—you'll report to me directly if anything further happens. Anything. Do you understand the shape of what I'm asking?"

"I understand," I said, which was true, and also a lie, because what she was asking and what I intended were two different animals entirely.

The council broke up in the usual shuffling way, chairs scraping, small talk reassembling itself like birds after a hawk passes. But as I reached the door, Maren's voice caught me, quiet, pitched for me alone.

"Twelve years, Freya."

I turned. She hadn't risen. She sat with her hands flat on the black oak, an old woman in a barn full of ghosts, and her face held no anger at all—only a tired, terrible kindness.

"In twelve years I have watched you count every coin honest, bury every dead man's name true, and stand in front of this village in the hard winters like a wall that doesn't complain. So believe me when I say I know what your lying face looks like now, because I've spent twelve years learning what your honest one does." She folded her hands. "Whatever you're carrying, child—it's heavier than a stray. And weight like that doesn't stay hidden. It only chooses when to fall."

I had nothing. Every word I owned had been spent on the tower of lies, and there was nothing left but the truth, which I could not afford. So I gave her the only honest currency remaining.

"Thank you, Elder," I said, and walked out into the bright, indifferent morning.

* * *

Dain caught up with me at the well, which was either coincidence or the least subtle ambush in military history. He drew water without being asked—he always did, hands moving on the rope and crank with the absent competence of thirty years' soldiering—and handed me the cup before he spoke.

"You lied to her."

"Yes."

"To the whole council."

"Yes."

He set the bucket down and looked at me, and up close I could see what the council table had hidden: the strain around his mouth, the way his thumb worried the strap of his sword belt, back and forth, back and forth, like a man sanding something down to nothing. "I backed your play. I put my name on it. Which means I lied too, Freya, and I need you to understand what that means for me, because you're standing there drinking water like it's a Tuesday."

"It is a Tuesday."

"*Freya.*"

I set the cup on the well lip. "What do you want me to say, Dain? That I'm sorry? I am. That I'll explain everything right here by the well where nine ears and forty neighbors can hear? Because I can't. Not yet. Maybe not ever, and I hate that, and I know what it costs you, and I'm asking you to carry it anyway."

His jaw worked. The thumb kept sanding the strap. "The child talked in their sleep," he said finally. "When I was there. Did you hear it?"

My blood went cold and careful. "Nonsense syllables. Fever talk."

"It wasn't nonsense." He said it quietly, without accusation, which somehow made it worse. "I've heard fever talk. Fever talk is mothers and rivers and *more water*. This was—" he searched, dissatisfied with every word he found— "*structured*. Same phrases, over and over, like a prayer somebody taught them. And Freya—" he looked up, and his eyes were the eyes of a man standing on ice and listening to it talk— "it sounded like the words carved over the shrine door. The old ones nobody reads. I stood in your kitchen at two in the morning listening to a starving child recite the lintel of our own shrine, and then I came to council and called it nonsense, and I want you to tell me I'm wrong."

The wind moved through the wheat beyond the fence line, a long silver shiver, and for one absurd moment I thought of the fields whispering too, the whole valley leaning in to listen.

"Dain," I said.

"Don't." He raised a hand. "Don't tell me I'm wrong, because we'd both know it's another lie, and I don't think I can hold three in one day. Just—" he exhaled, long and unsteady— "just tell me one thing. Whatever this is. Is anyone in this valley in danger?"

And there, at least, I could be honest, because I believed it: "Not from me. Not from the child. I swear it on the shrine stones themselves."

He laughed, short and hollow. "Careful swearing on those lately. Aldous says they've been singing."

"So I hear."

He picked up his bucket. At the edge of the lane he stopped, back still to me, shoulders square as a palisade.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I hope whatever you're hiding turns out to be nothing. Because I've known you twelve years, Freya Reyes, and you're the straightest blade I ever served beside. And a blade that starts bending—" he shook his head— "nobody blames the steel. But everybody remembers which hand held it."

He walked on toward the smithy, and I stood at the well with an empty cup and a full throat, and I understood at last what the old songs meant by a stone in the belly. It wasn't sharp. That was the terrible part. It was smooth and round and heavy, a river stone swallowed whole, sitting somewhere beneath my sternum where every breath had to go around it. I had lied to Maren. I had let Dain lie. I had sworn honesty on stones that sang, to a friend who deserved better, in a valley built on rules I was beginning to suspect had been built on lies older and larger than mine.

That evening I lifted the floorboard and took out the oilcloth bundle, and the fragment hummed against my palm, patient as a seed.

From the pallet, Wren watched me with dark, unblinking eyes, and spoke one word in the dead tongue—soft, questioning, the rising lilt of a child asking *are we safe?*

"Not yet," I answered, in the only language we shared, which was silence.

And far off, past the north ridge where no roads ran, I thought—for the first time, but not, I would learn, the last—I heard something that might have been hooves.
