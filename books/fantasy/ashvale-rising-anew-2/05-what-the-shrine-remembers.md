## 5. What the Shrine Remembers

I had knelt on those shrine stones every morning for twelve years, and I want you to appreciate the particular indignity of discovering, at thirty-four, that you've been kneeling on a lock.

It started, as most disasters do, with a child who wouldn't sleep.

* * *

Wren had been with us nine days. Nine days of soup, of borrowed clothes too big in the shoulders, of the council asking questions I answered with increasingly elaborate lies. Nine days of the shrine stones humming at dusk whenever the child walked past, soft as a struck bowl, which I had decided—firmly, with the discipline of a professional—not to think about.

On the ninth evening, Wren appeared at my door with the slate I'd given them for practicing Common and said, in that voice like wind through a ruined hall, a sentence I did not understand.

"Again," I said. "Slower."

They repeated it. The words had a shape I knew. Not the meaning—the *shape*. I'd spent twelve years tracing my thumb over the oldest inscriptions at the shrine, the ones worn nearly smooth, the ones Maren called "decorative" in a tone that discouraged further inquiry. The child's frightened mouth was reproducing them from memory.

"Come with me," I said, and grabbed my coat.

The shrine sits at the valley's heart, a low ring of grey stones older than the village that grew around it, older, Maren claimed, than any map. Twelve stones in a circle, a thirteenth laid flat in the center as an altar, and on the inner faces of the stones, carvings. Spirals. Lines. Marks that the devout called blessings and the practical called weathering and that I, in twelve years of honest service and one week of dishonest service, had called nothing at all, because that was the rule: you did not ask what the shrine remembered. You asked it for nothing. You kept the rite, and you kept your mouth shut, and the valley kept you safe. That was the bargain. I'd never once examined the terms, because the terms were the air I breathed.

Wren walked the circle slowly, barefoot—I'd stopped fighting that particular battle on day two—and when they reached the third stone, they stopped and pressed both palms flat against it.

The stone hummed. The sound went through my boots, through my teeth, into the place behind my sternum where I kept my oath.

"It's saying the same thing twice," Wren said, in Common now, halting. "Out loud and... underneath. The underneath part is older." They frowned, a child's frown, absurdly ordinary against the humming. "It's a list."

"A list of what?"

"Names." They looked up at me. "No. Not names. Titles. Like—like when you say 'the butcher' but you mean the person. These are... the god's titles. But crossed out." They dragged one small finger along the spiral groove. "Every one of them has a line through it. Like someone was keeping accounts and then... cancelled everything."

I crouched beside them and looked at the carving I'd touched a thousand times in devotion and seen nothing, ever, because seeing was not part of my duties. Spirals, I'd thought. Pretty spirals.

They were words. They had always been words. And someone had struck them through with the care of a bookkeeper and the thoroughness of a gravedigger.

"Read me the first one," I said.

* * *

We worked for three nights. I want to be honest about what that looked like, because the songs would make it lanterns and revelation and a wise child speaking truth to a hardened soldier. In practice it was a cold shrine, a stub of candle, a mercenary copying carvings onto slate by feel while a nine-year-old said "no, the line goes the other way, you're writing 'goat' again."

The old tongue, it turned out, was less a language than a system of locks. Wren knew it the way you know a song from before you could talk—half-remembered, rising up unbidden when they were frightened or tired. They could not parse it at will. But they could *feel* it, the way I could feel which floorboard in the barracks creaked before anyone stepped on it, and between their instinct and my stubbornness and the shrine's apparent eagerness to be understood—we'd get a phrase, and the stones would warm under our hands like a dog hearing its name after years.

The first full sentence we recovered, on the second night, was this: *Here is set down the weight that was carried here.*

"Carried from where?" I asked the stone. The stone, being a stone, declined to answer, but Wren shivered and said, "It doesn't want to say. The next part is scratched out. Someone chiseled it off." They traced the gouge. "It was done slowly. Not in a hurry. In a hurry you'd smash it. This is—careful. Like they wanted it gone but wanted you to know it had been there."

I copied that down too. I was, by then, keeping a second slate under my bed, and I did not examine the fact that I'd started hiding my findings from the council the same way I was hiding the child. One secret is a stone in the shoe. Two is a stone in each shoe, and you walk like that, and after a while you forget you ever walked any other way.

The third night, the frost came early and hard, and Wren's breath made little ghosts in the candlelight, and we found the sentence that broke the floor out from under me.

*The valley does not shelter. The valley holds.*

"Those are different words," Wren said slowly, working it out with their finger in the frost on the stone. "Shelter. Hold. Like—" they hunted for it, nine years old and reaching for a distinction most scholars never bother with, "—like a mother shelters you. A jail holds you."

"Read it again."

"*The valley does not shelter. The valley holds. What sleeps below is not at rest; it is kept.*" They stopped. Looked up at me with those too-old eyes. "Freya, what's below?"

"Cellars," I said. "Storage. The old crypt, but it's been sealed since before Maren's grandmother. There's nothing down there."

Even as I said it, I heard how it sounded. *Sealed since before Maren's grandmother.* I had accepted that my entire life. Sealed doors, sealed records, a sealed tongue, a sealed god—and I had called all of it *peace*.

The stones hummed. Not the soft dusk-hum. Something lower, longer, a note that went on past the point where a note should have stopped, and Wren grabbed my hand hard enough to hurt, and above us, in the black between the twelve standing stones, the frost on the air itself seemed to hang, to *wait*, the way a held breath waits.

"Freya," Wren whispered. "It's not humming at us. It's humming *through* us. Like we're standing on a drum."

I got the child out of there and back to my rooms and did not sleep. I sat up with the slate in my lap and read my own copies by the last of the candle and did the arithmetic I'd been refusing to do.

The rite I performed every morning—the kneeling, the palm on the altar stone, the recitation of the oath in the old tongue that I did not understand and had never been taught the meaning of. Twelve years. I'd said the words every morning of my adult life, and I had assumed they were a prayer.

They were not a prayer. I knew that now with a certainty that sat in my gut like swallowed river ice. A prayer goes up. Whatever I'd been saying every morning, the stones had been drinking it *down*. Every dawn, every mercenary before me, every generation of them, kneeling on the lock and turning the key a little further.

We hadn't been guarding the valley.

We were part of the mechanism.

* * *

I told Dain on the fourth morning, because I had to tell someone or start talking to the slate, and Dain at least had the mercy to look as sick as I felt.

We stood at the shrine's edge, watching the frost smoke rise off the wheat, and I laid it out for him flat, the way you brief a comrade before a fight: the inscriptions, the struck-through titles, the sentence about holding. Dain listened the way he does all things, completely and without blinking, and when I finished he was quiet for a long time.

"So the protection against magic," he said finally. "The thing that's kept every hedge-witch and war-caster out of this valley for two hundred years. The reason the kingdoms leave us alone."

"Yes."

"It's not protection."

"It's a seal," I said. "We're not the guarded. We're the—wardens on a prison wall who were never told there was a prison." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Twelve years, Dain. I gave that god twelve years of mornings. I knelt on its lock every day and thanked it for my safety."

"You didn't know."

"That's supposed to make it better?" I looked at the stones, grey and patient in the cold. "It's what makes it worse. It means the whole thing works *because* we don't know. The ignorance is load-bearing."

Dain turned his oath-ring around his finger, the way he does when the truth and the comfortable thing are in different rooms. "Maren knows," he said. "She has to. 'Decorative.' She's been answering questions about those carvings her whole life."

"I know."

"And the Order. The white cloaks. If the valley is a seal, then the Order isn't a faith, it's a—" he stopped, because saying it made it real.

"A gaolers' guild," I finished. "With better tailoring."

Wren came out of my rooms then, small and rumpled and wearing my spare coat like a robe, and stood between us looking up at the stones with an expression I was beginning to recognize as the old tongue rising in them. When they spoke, it was that hall-voice, that wind-through-ruins voice, and I copied the sounds on my slate as fast as my numb fingers allowed, and when they came back to themselves and translated, haltingly, they gave me the last piece.

"*The keeper's oath is the lock's third turn,*" they said. "*The keeper who speaks the words without knowing their weight turns it blind, and the blind turning is the strongest.*"

The frost smoke drifted. Somewhere out in the wheat, a crow said something rude.

"They designed it that way," I said. My voice sounded like it belonged to someone standing one step to the left of my body. "The oath. The not-teaching. Twelve years of mercenaries kneeling and reciting words we didn't understand, and that's not a flaw in the system. That's the system. The silence isn't the god's grief. It's the *design*."

And that was the vertigo, right there, the thing I'd been circling for four nights and finally had to stand on the edge of and look down: not that the god had lied to me. That the god had built a world where the lie didn't need telling. Where the truth was carved in plain sight on every stone and struck through so thoroughly that devotion itself became the blindfold. I had not been deceived. I had been *installed*.

"Freya." Dain's hand on my shoulder, steady, the old weight of it. "Breathe."

"I'm breathing."

"You're breathing like a woman deciding whether to burn a building. Breathe differently."

I breathed differently. And that was when Wren went rigid, both hands flying to the third stone, and the hum came back—that long, low, waiting note—and the child said, in Common, very clearly, in a voice gone flat with fear:

"Someone's coming up the ridge road. The stone can feel their boots. A lot of boots. And their cloaks are—" the hall-voice flickered through, one phrase of the old tongue, and they translated it with visible reluctance, "—*white as a kept promise*."

I looked east. The frost smoke. The wheat. The ridge line, empty, for now.

Dain was already counting the arrows in his quiver, because Dain is Dain.

"Get the child inside," I said. "And Dain—whatever happens next, the council can't know what we found here. Not yet. Not until I know who else is standing on this lock."

He looked at me for a long moment, and I watched him weigh his creed against his loyalty, and I watched the loyalty win, and I watched him hate it.

"Twelve years of honesty," he said quietly. "You're keeping a list of what I'm owed, right?"

"Top of the page," I said, and went to wake the council's lies for another day, while on the ridge road, still distant, still patient, the boots came on.
