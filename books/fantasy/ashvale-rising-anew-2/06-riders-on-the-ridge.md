## 6. Riders on the Ridge

I have taken three oaths in my life, and I have kept all of them, which is two more than most men can say and exactly enough to ruin me.

The first was to my sword-brothers, back when I still thought a mercenary company was a family you could drink with. The second was to Freya Reyes, sworn on a night I'll tell no one about, in a ditch outside Harrow Fen, when they pulled me out of it and said nothing about the state I was in. The third is the one that's going to kill me: the Vow of Plain Speech. No lie spoken, no truth withheld where withholding does harm. My mother's order taught it before she taught me letters. Truth above all, above comfort, above kin, above self. A clean tongue in a dirty world.

For thirty-one years my tongue has been clean.

Today, standing on the ridge road with white cloaks flapping at me like a flock of very polite vultures, I discovered that a clean tongue and an honest man are not the same thing, and that the difference is roughly the width of a child's grave-shaped absence from our census rolls.

* * *

It started, as most bad days in Ashvale do, with weather. Low cloud off the western peaks, the kind that swallows riders until they're ten feet away, which meant I heard the horses before I saw them—shod hooves on old stone, four sets, moving at the unhurried walk of men who have never once been made to hurry by anything smaller than a kingdom.

I was checking the boundary markers, which is a job that consists mostly of walking and thinking, and I'd been thinking about Wren. About how the kid had stopped flinching when Freya moved suddenly. About how Freya had started humming again, which they only do when something is either very right or very wrong, and knowing them, both.

Then the cloud thinned and there they were.

Four riders. White cloaks, unmarked except for the sigil at the shoulder—a circle with a line through it, the Unbroken Vow, the closed mouth, whatever your grandmother called it. Their armor was plain steel gone mirror-bright from polishing, their horses were better than any horse has a right to be this far south, and their leader had the particular stillness of a man who has never needed to raise his voice because everyone around him learned early what happened to people who made him.

I knew the type. I'd served beside two of them in the border wars, before the Order bought out half the free companies and the other half retired or died. They're not cruel, usually. That's the worst part. Cruelty you can fight. It's the reasonable ones who frighten me—the ones who've done the arithmetic and found that a few burned villages balance neatly against a quiet world.

"Ho, the valley," the leader called down. His voice carried the way voices do when they've been trained to carry over battlefields. "We seek no trouble."

"Then you're lost," I said, "because this is Ashvale, and trouble is the only thing we grow well."

One of the younger riders snorted. The leader didn't smile. Of course he didn't. Smiling costs a man like that something, and he keeps his accounts balanced.

He dismounted anyway, which surprised me, and walked his horse the last stretch of road so we could talk face to face like neighbors instead of like a wall and a man defending it. Clever. Everything about him was clever in that frictionless way. He introduced himself as Brother Aldous, of the Order of the Unbroken Vow, and he said it the way other men say their own names—like it was nothing, like it cost nothing, like the Order wasn't a word that made mothers in three kingdoms pull their children indoors.

"We're looking for a child," he said. "A girl, perhaps eight years, dark-haired, speaks oddly. She went missing near Dunmarrow six weeks past. Her people are grieving." He produced a small painted miniature, the kind rich families commission, and held it up. The girl in the portrait had Wren's face the way a map has a country's face—all the lines present, none of the life.

Six weeks past. Wren had come to us five weeks ago, half-dead, speaking a language that made the shrine stones sing. Which meant either the Order had been hunting since before the child fled, or the timeline was a courtesy, the way a fisherman's net is a courtesy to the river.

"I'm sorry for her people," I said. And that much was true, so it came easy. "But we've seen no such child."

Brother Aldous looked at me. Not through me—at me, fully, the way you look at a door you intend to open eventually but are willing, for now, to knock upon.

"You're Dain Corvane," he said. "Sworn of the Plain Speech. Your reputation precedes you even here." He smiled then, finally, and it was worse than the not-smiling. "Which is why I'll ask plainly, brother to a brother of oaths: if you had seen such a child, you would tell me. Wouldn't you?"

And there it was. The hook, baited with my own creed, dropped into the water in front of me.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about vows: they don't weigh anything until they do. For thirty-one years the Plain Speech sat on my tongue like a coin in a pocket—I forgot it was there, spent it freely, told the truth the way I breathed, without deciding to. Now, in the space of one question, it weighed as much as the ridge itself.

Because yes. If I had seen such a child, I would tell him. That was the vow. That was the whole architecture of my life: a man who could be relied upon, whose word was load-bearing, who had built himself out of honesty the way other men build themselves out of muscle. Take it away and what's left? A big man with a scarred jaw and a good arm, and nothing underneath worth trusting.

But I had seen such a child. I had watched her fall asleep against Freya's shoulder two nights ago while Freya read aloud from a book neither of them understood, translating by feel, and I had thought: *there*. There is the thing I would burn down a kingdom to keep.

"No," I said. "If I had seen such a child, I would tell you."

The words came out level. Clean. True, even, in the letter of them—and a lie in every other dimension a sentence can lie in, because the vow says *no truth withheld where withholding does harm*, and I was withholding a child, and harm was coming up that road behind these four riders like weather behind a storm front.

Brother Aldous studied me a moment longer. Then he nodded, satisfied, and turned to his men, and I understood that I had just passed a test I had failed. That's the trick of it. The Plain Speech doesn't care about your intentions. It cares about your mouth. And my mouth had just committed treason against everything I am, dressed up in grammar honest enough to fool its own speaker.

Almost.

"Your sister-village records," Aldous said, turning back, casual as a man asking directions. "Census rolls, harvest tallies, births and deaths. The Order keeps its own count of the world's children, you understand. When one goes missing, we check the counts. Yours shows no new soul entered the valley this season." He let that sit. "Curious, isn't it, how complete your records are. Almost as though someone made sure of it."

My heart did something complicated and painful.

Maren Corvane—my aunt, my elder, keeper of the rolls and of considerably more than the rolls—had shown me those ledgers myself, three days ago, after Freya begged her to. *No new souls*, written in Maren's careful hand, dated the week Wren arrived. I'd assumed it was caution. Standing in front of Brother Aldous, I understood it was practice. Old, worn-smooth practice, the kind that comes from doing a thing many times across many years, and I felt the floor of my understanding of my own home tilt beneath me like a deck in bad water.

"Our records are complete," I said, "because our record-keeper is thorough."

"May we see the valley?" Aldous asked. "Just the shrine square. We ask leave, we take nothing, we stay one night. The Order pays its way." He named a sum that would have covered our winter grain twice over, and I realized he'd done his homework on us too—he knew we were lean this year, knew the late frost had bitten the wheat, knew exactly what price a proud poor village might sell its scruples for.

"The council decides such things," I said. "Not me."

"But you will speak for it. They trust you." He remounted, unhurried. "We'll wait at the ridge camp tonight. Send your answer by moonrise." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table. "Or send the child, and spare everyone the arithmetic."

They rode back into the cloud. I stood on the road a long time after the hoofbeats faded, doing my own arithmetic, and hating every number in it.

* * *

Freya was splitting wood behind the shrine when I found them, because of course they were; the woman processes grief and fear through her shoulders, and there was a cord of ash logs stacked to my chin that hadn't existed yesterday. I told them all of it. The riders, the portrait, the question, the offer, the moonrise deadline. I left out exactly one thing: the moment on the road where my mouth betrayed me and my heart caught it, and how close the margin had been. Closer than a blade-width. Close enough that I could still feel the draft coming through.

When I finished, Freya set the axe down with exaggerated care, the way you do when the alternative is throwing it.

"One night," they said. "They're giving us one night."

"They're giving us the appearance of a choice. Men like that don't wait at ridges for hospitality."

"Maren needs to know. The council—" Freya stopped. Rubbed their face. "Gods. The council. Half of them will want to hand Wren over before the firewood's cold, and they'll sleep fine after."

"And the other half?"

"The other half will want to hand Wren over and *not* sleep fine." Freya picked the axe back up, then didn't swing it. "Dain. Why did you lie to them?"

"I didn't lie. That's the problem. I told the truth shaped like a lie, and it worked, and now I get to live inside it." I sat down on the chopping block, which creaked a protest appropriate to the occasion. "Thirty-one years, Freya. Every oath I ever took, kept. You know what I am to this village? I'm the man who tells the truth. That's my whole office. When I stand up in the square and say a thing, it settles arguments, because everyone knows Dain Corvane doesn't bend his tongue. And today I bent it, and it held, and tomorrow Maren asks me to bend it again in front of the council, and next week it's the riders again, and somewhere in there I stop being the man who tells the truth and start being the man who decides *which* truths, and that man—" My voice did something embarrassing. I let it. Some things you owe yourself. "That man is just a liar with good reasons, and the Order is full of those already."

Freya leaned on the axe and looked at me for a long moment. Twelve years I've fought beside this person and I still can't always read their silences, but I know this one: it's the silence where they decide whether to hand you a hard kindness or a soft one.

"Dain," they said finally. "You told me once that the Plain Speech means no truth withheld where withholding does harm. So answer me honestly. Does telling the Order about Wren do less harm than keeping silent?"

"You know it doesn't."

"Then you didn't break your vow. You fulfilled the part of it that actually matters, and broke the part that lets you feel righteous." They swung the axe; the log split clean. "Feeling righteous is not the same as being good. I've been both, and being good is cheaper."

"That's easy for you. Your god doesn't talk back."

Something crossed Freya's face then—quick, buried, gone. Later I would learn what it was. Later I would learn what their god had and hadn't done, and what the silence was made of, and I would wish for a great many things including, briefly, my own ignorance back. But that evening I only saw the flicker and filed it wrong, the way you file things when you don't yet know the shape of the story you're standing in.

"No," Freya said quietly. "It doesn't. Maybe that's the mercy."

* * *

I went to Maren that night. She was in her study with the lamps turned low, and when I told her about the riders she didn't gasp or go pale or do any of the things a surprised woman does. She closed her eyes for a moment, like a merchant hearing a price she'd been dreading, and then she opened the ledger drawer and took out a paper I hadn't seen before—one of several, I'd understand later—and began writing in her small, exact hand.

"What are you writing?"

"A reply to the Order. We'll grant them the shrine square for one night. Hospitality, openly given, nothing hidden." She didn't look up. "It's the only play that works, Dain. Refuse, and we confirm there's something to find. Search us, and they'll find her eventually—children are loud, and frightened children louder. But welcome them, feed them, let them walk among us for a night, and they see exactly what we show them: a poor valley with nothing in it worth a commander's time."

"And Wren?"

"Wren spends tomorrow night with the Hollis family, in the east fields. Sick child visiting sick child—old Tam Hollis has the lung-fever, it's plausible, it's *kind*, even. The girl helps nurse him. Nobody lies to anyone." She set down the pen and finally looked at me, and my aunt's eyes were tired in a way I had never once seen in forty years of knowing her. "You look like a man carrying stones, nephew."

"I lied today, Aunt. On the ridge. To the Order."

"To the Order," she repeated, softly, tasting it. "Dain. Sit down. There are things about this valley, and about me, and about what keeping people safe has cost, that you should hear from my mouth before you hear them some worse way." She poured two cups of the plum wine she saves for funerals and negotiations, which should have told me something right there. "And then, if you still think you lied today, I'll help you confess to whoever you like. Even them. Especially them."

Outside, the cloud was breaking up, and the first stars were coming out over the ridge where four white cloaks sat waiting for moonrise, patient as arithmetic.

I sat down. I drank. And I listened to my aunt tell me the truth at last—which is, I suppose, the only prayer my order ever really taught: that when the reckoning comes, someone will still be willing to speak plain.

What she told me made the riders on the ridge look like the least of our troubles.

But that confession belongs to another chapter, and mine ends here, on the taste of funeral wine and the sound of my own creed cracking—not breaking, not yet, but cracking, the way ice cracks before it gives, quietly, in the dark, under a weight it was never built to hold.
