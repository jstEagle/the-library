## 7. The Oath's True Wording

I have always been a person who reads contracts before signing them. It's the one virtue my mother managed to beat into me before she died, and I've clung to it with the desperation of a drowning woman clinging to driftwood. Read it twice. Read it aloud. Ask what each word costs you. That habit kept me alive through nine years of mercenary work in places where "contract" was just a prettier word for "trap."

So you can imagine how it felt, sitting alone in the shrine at midnight with Wren's translations spread across my knees, when I realized I had never actually read my own oath.

Not properly. Not all of it.

The oath every sworn blade of Ashvale takes is administered orally. That's tradition. The elder speaks the words, you repeat them, and afterward you're given a written copy for your keeping — a copy rendered in Trade Common, clean and simple, the way a good contract should be. *I serve the silent god. I hold the valley. I keep the peace. I speak no word against the shrine. My silence is my devotion.* Four lines. I've had mine memorized since I was nineteen years old and hungry enough to swear anything that came with bread attached.

Four lines. What could possibly hide in four lines?

Here's what hides in four lines: everything.

* * *

It started, as most of my recent disasters have, with Wren.

The child had taken to sleeping in the shrine's back alcove — officially because the nights were cold and the shrine held warmth like a banked oven, unofficially because Maren Corvane's spies had eyes on every other room in this valley and the shrine was the one place even the council didn't dare post watchers. Sacrilege has its uses. I'd learned that much from the god himself, apparently.

Wren talked in their sleep. Not Common, not the valley dialect. The old tongue — the dead language, the god-language — spilling out soft and broken like water finding cracks in a wall. Most nights it was nonsense. Fragments. A name repeated over and over that I couldn't parse. But three nights ago, Wren had sat bolt upright at midnight, wide awake and terrified, and said a full sentence in that voice that wasn't quite theirs, and then burst into tears and remembered nothing.

I wrote the sentence down phonetically. I'm not a scholar. My letters were ugly and my vowels were guesses. But I took it to the oldest inscriptions on the shrine's foundation stones — the ones so worn that generations of villagers had walked past them without a glance — and I matched sounds to carvings the way you'd match a key to a lock, trying teeth until one caught.

And then I did something I hadn't done in twelve years of service.

I went home, dug out my oath-copy from the bottom of my campaign chest where it had lived under spare socks and a whetstone since the day I received it, and I read it against the inscriptions.

Reader, they are not the same document.

* * *

Let me be precise about this, because precision is all I have left tonight.

The written oath I was given is thirty-one words long. I counted them again by candlelight, hands steady, telling myself I was being paranoid. Thirty-one words of clean Common. *I serve the silent god. I hold the valley.* Etcetera. A soldier's bargain: loyalty for sanctuary, silence for belonging. Fair terms, as such things go.

But the paper itself — I'd never looked closely at the paper itself. The copy is written on vellum, and beneath the Common text, pressed so faintly into the skin that you'd need candlelight at an angle and eyes that had spent a decade scanning ridgelines for movement, there is a second text. Rubbed down. Nearly erased. Deliberately buried under the legible version the way you'd bury a body under a floor you wanted people to walk on.

It's in the old tongue. And thanks to a terrified child and a sleepless week, I could read maybe half of it without help.

What I could read made my blood go cold in a way that no battlefield ever managed. Battle-cold is fast; it comes and goes and leaves you shaking but intact. This was slow cold. It crept up from the page into my fingers and settled into my chest and stayed there.

*Obedience unto silence,* the hidden clause began. *Even unto atrocity.*

I want you to sit with those words the way I had to sit with them, alone, at midnight, with a guttering candle and no one to tell me I'd misread them. Every mercenary knows oaths have fine print. You expect fine print. Fine print says *your employer may redirect you,* or *compensation subject to survival.* Fine print is the tax you pay for someone else's ink.

This wasn't fine print. This was the actual contract, and the version I'd been given was the decoration.

I worked through it line by line, matching Wren's phonetic alphabet against the inscriptions, using the shrine stones as my dictionary. Some phrases defeated me entirely. But the shape of it came through like a body under a sheet — you don't need to see the face to know what you're looking at.

*I serve the silent god* became, in the older text, *I am bound to the voice that chose stillness.* Chose. As if the silence were an act. A decision. Something done rather than something suffered.

*I hold the valley* became *I stand upon the seal and call it home.*

And *my silence is my devotion* — the line I'd repeated at nineteen with bread in my belly and gratitude in my throat — became *my silence is his silence, and his silence is the hiding of the crime.*

The crime. The old tongue used a specific word there, a word Wren had translated for me once, weeping, describing soldiers in white vows: *kethrane.* Unmaking. The deliberate erasure of something that existed. Not killing — killing leaves a corpse, a grave, a memory. Unmaking leaves nothing, and worse, it leaves everyone else agreeing that nothing was ever there.

My oath, the true oath, the one pressed beneath the pretty copy like a confession under a smile, bound me to guard an unmaking. Bound me to *be* part of it. Every morning rite I performed, every hour I stood watch, every time I told a new arrival that the valley's quiet was holy — I wasn't serving a god who had fallen silent.

I was standing on the mouth of a sealed thing, singing lullabies to it, and calling the singing devotion.

* * *

I don't know how long I sat there. Long enough for the candle to drown in its own wax. Long enough for the cold to come up through the stone floor and make my knees ache the way they do before storms.

Here is the thing I could not get past, the thought that circled me like a wolf circling a fire: I never questioned it. Twelve years. Twelve years of a god who never answered, and I built a whole theology around the absence. *He listens without speaking. His silence is a form of attention. We are heard, if not answered.* I said those words to frightened travelers. I said them to myself on bad nights, kneeling at the shrine, asking nothing, receiving nothing, and calling the nothing faith.

It was easier than the alternative. The alternative was asking why a god would stay silent, and following that question down to its root, and digging.

Wren asked why. Wren, six years old maybe, seven, with ash-colored eyes and a shard of stolen divinity lodged somewhere behind their sternum, asked *why doesn't he talk to you?* within a day of arriving, and I'd smiled the patronizing smile adults give children, and said *that's what makes him holy, little one.*

A child saw the crack in the wall in forty-eight hours. It took me twelve years and a buried clause in my own soul's deed of sale.

That's the betrayal, if you want to know. Not the clause itself — gods and their priests lie, water is wet, the sun rises in the east. The betrayal is that the lie worked on *me*, specifically, me who reads contracts twice, me who asks what each word costs. Because the oath wasn't presented as a contract. It was presented as a gift. And I was nineteen and hungry, and nobody reads the fine print on a gift, because to do so feels like ingratitude. Like biting the hand mid-offering.

Whoever designed that oath understood mercenaries better than we understand ourselves. They knew we'd check the blade-edges and miss the velvet.

* * *

Near dawn I heard small feet on the shrine floor, and then Wren was beside me, wrapped in the blanket I'd given them, hair sleep-flattened on one side, looking at the papers scattered around me with the particular wariness of a child who has learned that adults' papers mean trouble.

"You found it," they said. In Common. Their Common was getting better by the day — quick, greedy, the way children learn anything they know they need.

"Found what?"

"The underneath words." They crouched, touched the vellum with one finger, right on the phrase I'd been staring at. *Kethrane.* "The white-cloaks say them too. Before the fire." Their voice went flat and careful, the way it did when they touched the memories they kept in a locked room at the back of themselves. "They say the underneath words, and then the loud ones after. The loud ones are for the people watching. The underneath ones are the real ones."

"The real ones," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "And what do the real ones say, Wren?"

They were quiet a moment. Then, haltingly, translating not from memory but from the stone under our feet, from the inscription I'd matched hours ago: "*We keep the door shut on what he took. We do not open it. We do not say his name truly, because his true name is the sound of the taking.*"

The sound of the taking. Ashvale's true name was a description of the theft. The god's holiest syllable, the name we were forbidden to speak, was — what? A warning label? An admission? Or the lock itself, a word that must never be pronounced because pronouncing it would turn the key?

"And the fire," Wren said, smaller now. "The white-cloaks burned the ones who said the underneath words out loud. I remember that. I remember a woman saying them in the square, and everyone going quiet, and then—" They stopped. Children stop at the edge of their own memories the way swimmers stop at the edge of deep water. They know exactly where the bottom drops away.

"Then the fire," I finished for them.

Then the fire. The Order of the Unbroken Vow, riding across kingdoms in their spotless cloaks, hunting a child who carried a fragment of what Ashvale buried. I'd assumed they were fanatics, zealots, the usual machinery of institutional cruelty. Now I wondered. If the oath I carried had a hidden clause, what clauses rode inside theirs? What did *unbroken vow* actually mean, in the old tongue, underneath the white?

Maybe they weren't hunting Wren to destroy the shard. Maybe they were hunting Wren to protect the silence — to keep the door shut, to keep the theft unspoken, to preserve the great agree-that-nothing-was-ever-there. Fanatics, yes. But fanatics with a job description. Custodians of the cover story, armed and armored and certain of their own righteousness, which is the most dangerous thing a person can be.

I had spent my life half-hating the Order from a comfortable distance, the way you hate weather. Now I understood they were my oath's twin. Same contract, different signature.

* * *

When the light came gray through the shrine's eastern slits, I did the morning rite. Habit is a powerful animal; it eats doubt for breakfast if you let it. I knelt, I laid the offering — barley and a cup of well-water, the humblest gifts in the valley — and I spoke the opening words.

And then, for the first time in twelve years, I stopped.

Mid-syllable. Kneeling there with the words dying in my mouth, aware suddenly of every single one of them, hearing for the first time not the devotional shapes but the machinery underneath. *His silence is the hiding of the crime.* I had been reciting a confession daily and calling it prayer.

"Wren," I said. "Come here. I want to teach you something."

They came, wary, blanket-dragging. I put the vellum in their hands.

"When the white-cloaks come — and they will come, soon, I think — they will ask you questions. They will use kind voices. They will say words that sound like the loud words, the safe words." I held their gaze, ash eyes to whatever color mine are. "But underneath, they will be saying the underneath words. Can you listen for the underneath words? Even when the top words are gentle?"

Wren looked at the vellum a long time. Then they nodded, once, the way soldiers nod — and I realized with a jolt that I'd just taught a child to do what I'd failed to do for twelve years. Listen past the offered text. Read the contract twice. Ask what each word costs.

Somewhere beyond the ridge, in the ordinary world, the Order was already moving. I could feel it the way you feel weather change — pressure, stillness, the hush before riders. Maren would be waking to her ledgers and her lists of names. Dain would be waking with his creed of truth sitting on his chest like a stone, and sooner or later I was going to have to hand him this vellum and watch him read the underneath words, and I did not know which frightened me more: that he might not believe them, or that he might.

One betrayal at a time. That's all a person can carry.

I folded the oath along its old creases, hid it back beneath the socks and the whetstone where it had slept for twelve years, and went to wake the village — to stand in the square, as I had every morning of my service, and speak the opening words of the rite in a clear voice while knowing, now, exactly what I was saying.

The difference was that this time, I'd be saying them as a liar. And some traitorous part of me, the part that reads contracts twice, was already composing the words I'd say instead, the day I finally opened my mouth and told the truth about the thing our god had buried beneath us all.

I just didn't yet know it would take blood and fire to pry my jaw loose.
