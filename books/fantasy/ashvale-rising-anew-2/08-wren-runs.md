## 8. Wren Runs

I want it on the record—my record, since I'm apparently the record-keeper now—that I did not mean to eavesdrop. Eavesdropping implies intent. What I had was a full bladder and a bad sense of timing, which is how most disasters in my life have started.

The house Freya kept me in was small, one room and a loft, with walls made of pine boards that had never once pretended to be soundproof. I'd been sleeping in the loft for eleven days, curled up under a blanket that smelled of woodsmoke and someone else's life, and in eleven days I had learned that Freya talked to themself while sharpening knives, hummed off-key during the morning rite when they thought no one could hear, and said "hm" in four distinct tones that meant everything from *interesting* to *we're all going to die*.

That evening, the "hm" was the fourth kind.

I'd gone down the ladder for water, and I was halfway back up when voices came through the floorboards—not raised, not yet, but tight as bowstrings. Maren Corvane's voice I knew by then, dry as old paper and twice as sharp.

"—can't keep pretending the child is a stray cat you've decided to feed."

"I'm not pretending anything," Freya said. "The child is asleep."

"The child," Maren said, "is standing on your ladder holding a cup of water."

I froze. In fairness, so did they, because there was a silence long enough that I could hear the fire pop, and then Freya said, very quietly, "Wren. Come down."

There are moments when running is the obvious choice and you don't take it because your body hasn't caught up to your fear. This was one of them. I came down. I stood in the middle of the room holding a cup of water like it was going to save me, and Maren looked at me the way you look at a letter you already know is bad news.

"Sit," she said.

I sat. Freya stayed standing, arms crossed, which I'd learned meant *bracing*.

"You heard what we were saying," Maren said. It wasn't a question. She didn't believe in questions when she could use statements instead; I understood this about her even then. "So you'll hear the rest. The Order has riders on the ridge. Three days out, maybe less. They're asking after a child who speaks the old tongue." Her eyes didn't soften, but something behind them did, briefly, like weather through a window. "They will burn this valley to get you. Or they will burn it to make an example of us for hiding you. Either way, Wren, you cannot stay."

"And sending me out alone," I said, "fixes that how?"

I surprised myself with that one. The words came out level, almost sharp, and I watched both adults recalibrate—the way people do when they've been talking about you instead of to you and suddenly you're a person standing in the room.

"It fixes it," Maren said, "because whatever they do to one lost child, they will not do to two hundred souls."

"That's arithmetic," I said. "Not mercy."

"Wren." Freya's voice was low. Careful. That carefulness scared me more than shouting would have. "Maren's right. There's a shepherd's track south of the ridge. Dain knows it. He can take you as far as the river crossing, and after that—"

"After that I'm nobody's problem."

"After that," Freya said, "you're alive."

And there it was. The thing underneath the whole conversation, laid bare on the floorboards between us: they wanted me gone so that I would keep breathing, and I couldn't hear the difference between *keep breathing* and *get out*, and maybe there wasn't one, and maybe that was the worst part.

I should say here that I understood, even in that moment, that they were right by every measure that mattered. I was eleven years old. I carried something in my chest that made shrine stones hum and grown soldiers ride three days through cold country to find me. Every hour I stayed was an hour the valley paid for.

Understanding is not the same as agreeing. Understanding is just the thing that makes you hate yourself afterward.

"I need to think," I said, which was a lie, because what I needed was air, and I went out the door before either of them could stop me, and I did not go back.

* * *

Here is what I remember about that night, in the order my memory keeps it, which is not the order it happened:

Cold. The frost coming down off the ridge like a tide. My breath making little ghosts. The shrine stones passing on my left, humming faintly as I went by—they always hummed near me now, a sound like a struck glass that never quite finished ringing—and me putting my hand on one of them anyway, because it was the closest thing to a goodbye I knew how to make.

Then the wheat fields, black-edged where the season had turned, stalks whispering against each other in the dark. Then the slope. Then the trees.

I had a plan. I want that noted too. My plan was to walk south along the shepherd's track until I found the river, cross it, and keep walking until I found somewhere the white cloaks weren't, which shows you exactly how much an eleven-year-old understands about the world. I didn't know where the river was. I didn't know what I'd eat. I knew that the language lived in my chest like a second heartbeat, and that wherever I went, it would sing me out loud to anyone listening for it.

But staying meant watching these people die for me, and I had already watched people die for me once. Fire. Soldiers in white. A woman's hands pushing me into a root cellar, saying words in the old tongue I still can't translate without crying, though I've stopped telling people that part.

So: the trees. The dark getting deeper. The track narrowing to a deer path and then to nothing, and me still walking, because stopping meant thinking and thinking meant going back.

I heard the horses before I saw them. That's not a boast—I didn't hear them so much as feel them, a tremor in the ground through my boots, the particular rhythm of shod hooves moving slow. I got off the path and behind a deadfall, heart slamming, and watched three shapes come down the slope with lanterns hooded to slits of orange.

White cloaks. Even in the dark I knew them. You know that white. It isn't laundry-white or snow-white. It's the white of bone, of ash, of things that used to be alive.

They stopped twenty feet from my deadfall. One of them swung down and crouched, touching the ground where the path passed, and said something I couldn't catch, and another one laughed—short, ugly, the laugh of someone enjoying a hunt.

"We know you're here, little songbird," the first one called out, in trade-tongue, the accent thick and wrong. "Your god's tongue carries. It always has. That's why we wear the Vow—it keeps our ears clean." A pause. Lantern light swept across the deadfall, close enough that I could see the grain of the bark. "Come out and we'll be gentle. Stay, and we'll be thorough. The valley gets the difference either way."

I want to tell you I was brave. I want to tell you I thought of Freya, of Dain's terrible jokes, of the blanket that smelled of woodsmoke, and held my ground out of love.

What actually happened was that my legs decided without me. I bolted. And the old tongue came out of my mouth the way it does when I'm frightened—a stream of syllables I half understand, pouring out of me like blood from a cut—and the nearest scout laughed again and said, "There. Hear that? Mark it," and the lanterns started moving.

I ran. Branches took at my face. The ground dropped away and I went down hard, rolled, came up wet-faced and bleeding from the eyebrow, and the light was everywhere now, three lanterns fanning out through the trees, and a hand closed on my collar—

—and then the night broke open.

I've tried, since, to describe what it sounded like when Freya hit those scouts, and I can't do it honestly, because what I remember isn't sound. It's shape. It was like watching a landslide decide to be a person. Freya came out of the dark upslope with no lantern and no warning, and the first thing that happened was the hand on my collar simply wasn't there anymore, attached to a man folding around a boot to the knee, and the second thing was a lantern hitting the dirt and going out, and the third thing was a sound I did recognize: steel leaving a sheath, quick and businesslike, the way Freya drew it every morning to sharpen it, except nothing about this was morning.

"Run," Freya said to me, calm as a recipe. "Downhill. Don't look back."

I ran downhill. I looked back. Both of those things are true.

What I saw: Freya between me and the scouts, blade low, weight forward, and the second scout lunging and meeting flat steel with a crack that rang off the trees, and the third scout—smartest of the three—turning to run for the horses, and Freya throwing the sword. Throwing it. It took the man through the shoulder and pinned him to a pine, screaming, and Freya was already moving to the second one, and what happened next happened fast and close and I am not going to write it down in detail, not even now, not even for the record. Especially for the record. Some things the record doesn't need.

I'll say only this: I had seen Freya sharpen knives and hum off-key and say "hm" in four tones. I had not seen this person. This person moved like the valley itself had gotten up off its hillside to defend it, and when it was over—when the last scout was down and the pinned one had wrenched free and fled uphill toward the horses, crashing away through the dark—Freya stood in the wrecked lantern light breathing hard, knuckles split, a shallow cut along the jaw, looking at their own hands like they belonged to a stranger.

Then they looked at me, and their face did something complicated, and they said, "You were supposed to run."

"You said don't look back," I said. My voice came out small. "Nobody said anything about not looking."

A sound came out of Freya that might have been a laugh if it hadn't been so close to something else. They crossed the distance and crouched in front of me, and I braced for anger—for the lecture, the *what were you thinking*, all the machinery adults deploy—but what they did was check my eyebrow with two fingers, gentle, tilting my face to the ruined light.

"You're bleeding."

"It's nothing."

"It's not nothing." They pulled off their cloak and wrapped it around my shoulders, and their hands were shaking. I noticed that. Their hands were shaking and their voice wasn't, and I understood, in the way children understand things sideways, that the shaking was the true part. "Wren. Listen to me. When I said you had to go—" They stopped. Started again. "I was wrong about the wording. Sending you out alone isn't keeping you alive. It's just dying somewhere I can't see you." A breath. "I'm not doing that. Whatever it costs. Do you hear me?"

The old tongue rose in my throat then, unasked, the way it does. But this time I pushed past it. I found the trade-tongue words underneath, clumsy and mine, and I said, "I hear you."

"Good." Freya stood, offered me a hand, and their grip was steady now, or pretending hard enough. "Because in about ten minutes there are going to be a lot of people very angry at both of us, and I'd rather face them with you upright."

* * *

They were waiting at the valley gate when we came down out of the trees. Not all of them—maybe thirty, forty, torchlight and worried faces—but enough. Maren at the front with her shawl thrown on over her nightclothes. Dain beside her, armed, having clearly been halfway to organizing a search party. Behind them, the whole shape of the village: farmers and millers and the tanner's boys, people whose names I was only beginning to learn, all of them staring at the blood on Freya's jaw and the boy wrapped in their cloak.

Dain got there first. He looked at Freya, at the blood, at me, and his face went through about six expressions and settled on grim.

"How many?"

"Three scouts. Two down back in the trees—one may live. Third ran for the horses." Freya's voice was flat, report-voice, the voice of someone reading a list so they don't have to feel it. "He'll talk. By tomorrow the Order knows exactly where the child is, and exactly who stands between them."

Silence. The kind that has weight. Somewhere behind the crowd, a baby cried and was hushed.

"They were on our ground," someone said from the back—an older man, a farmer, Halvard I think. "Armed. Hunting a child. On *our* ground."

"They were invited by twelve years of quiet," Maren said, and her voice cracked on it, just slightly, the first time I ever heard that happen. She looked at Freya for a long moment. "You drew blood in Ashvale. In front of witnesses. You know what that means."

"I know what it means," Freya said.

"The council will convene at dawn. Trial, in the shrine, per the old law." Maren's eyes moved to me then, and what was in them wasn't hatred. It was worse. It was arithmetic again—two hundred souls, one child, the terrible ledger she'd been carrying her whole life. "Both of you. Be there."

The crowd began to break apart, murmuring, torches drifting home. Dain put a hand on Freya's shoulder and said something too low for me to catch, and Freya nodded, and then it was just the three of us at the gate with the frost coming down and the shrine stones humming faintly behind us, louder than usual, like they'd felt the blood too.

Freya walked me back to the house. At the door they stopped, and looked down at me, and for a moment all the soldier went out of their face and left just a tired person holding a splint on a broken night.

"For what it's worth," they said, "I'm glad you looked back."

I didn't have words for that. So I did the thing I'd been afraid to do since the fire, since the cellar, since the woman's hands pushing me into the dark: I stepped forward and hugged them, quick and fierce, around the middle, face against the bloody cloak.

Freya went stiff. Then one hand came up, careful, and rested on my hair like I was something that might startle.

"Dawn," they said finally, rough. "Sleep if you can."

I slept. Actually slept, deep and dreamless, in the loft under the woodsmoke blanket, while below me Freya sat up all night with a whetstone and no knife, making that soft rhythmic sound against bare stone—sharpening nothing, or sharpening themself, I couldn't tell.

At dawn, the council convened. And I learned that breaking the valley's peace was only the first price of the night, and nowhere near the largest.
