## 9. Blood on Sacred Ground

They held my trial in the shrine, which I suppose was fitting. If you're going to be judged by a god who never answers, you may as well do it under his roof.

The Nine sat in a crescent of old chairs dragged up from the meeting hall, arranged on the shrine's worn flagstones beneath the carved lintel that read — in the tongue nobody could read anymore, until a few weeks ago — *obedience unto silence*. Maren Corvane occupied the center chair like she'd been born in it, which, given her family's history with this valley, was not entirely a joke. The rest were farmers and smiths and one very nervous beekeeper who kept glancing at the door as though hoping the bees had voted him a proxy.

I stood where they told me to stand: on the circle of pale stone at the shrine's heart, the same spot where I'd performed the morning rite for twelve years. It felt different underfoot now. Everything did, since I'd learned what the stone was for.

Dain stood at the edge of the crescent, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a man watching a cart he'd helped load roll toward a cliff. He'd argued all morning that this was a waste of time. He'd been overruled by everyone including, in a sense, himself.

"Freya Reyes," Maren said, and her voice carried the way it always did in the shrine, flat and clear, no echo because the stones drank sound the way they drank everything else. "You are called to answer for violence done within Ashvale's bounds. For blood spilled on ground that has known none in living memory."

"Living memory," I said, before I could stop myself.

Maren's jaw tightened. "Do you deny the acts?"

"No." I'd rehearsed denial on the walk over. It had lasted about forty paces. "Two Order scouts came over the ridge at dusk chasing a child. They had Wren by the collar when I reached them. One of them will eat soup through a straw for a month. The other will remember me every time it rains. I don't deny any of it. I'd do it again before they finished their next sentence."

A murmur went through the crescent. Old Tam the smith shifted forward in his seat. The beekeeper looked like he wanted to be anywhere else, possibly another valley entirely.

"You broke the peace," Maren said. "Twelve years you've sworn to keep it. You've taught half the children in this village the patrol signs. And you put a man's eye out on our own ridge."

"He was dragging a child off our ridge," I said. "Toward riders in white cloaks who burn things for a living. I'm sorry about the eye. I'm not sorry about the rest, and we both know which of those statements is the lie."

"That's enough—"

"It isn't, actually." My voice came out steadier than I felt, which is the mercenary's oldest trick: the body performs calm while the mind screams into its cupped hands. "Because there's something you all need to hear, and I'd rather say it here than have it arrive later wearing white cloaks."

I let the silence sit. In this valley silence is heavy; it has weight and texture, twelve years of it pressed down like snowfall. Then I told them. Not everything — not the crypt, not the seal, not the thing I'd found in the oath's true wording that still woke me some nights with my heart going like a war drum. But enough.

"The Order of the Unbroken Vow doesn't collect lost children," I said. "They hunt them. Wren speaks a language older than any kingdom, and carries something the Order wants badly enough to cross mountains for. They don't want to return this child to a family. There is no family to return to. There are soldiers in white who burned three villages along the southern road asking after Wren — I've seen the ash fields myself, years back, before I understood what I was looking at. They will take this child, and whatever happens after that, Wren does not come back. Not as Wren."

"How do you know all this?" Tam asked. He wasn't hostile. That was somehow worse. He sounded like a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

"Because I spent eleven years working contracts adjacent to them. Because I've read their writs. Because a commander named Ilse Varn stood at our gate two nights ago and offered me absolution for treason if I handed the child over by moonrise." I looked around the crescent. "Absolution. From an order whose entire doctrine is that nothing can be absolved. They wanted the child so badly they were willing to bend their own scripture to get it. Ask yourselves what that's worth."

The beekeeper made a small noise. Someone shushed him.

"And you hid this from us," Maren said quietly. "You lied to this council. To me. Twelve years of honest service, Freya, and you threw it away in a single council session."

"Yes," I said. "I did. And I'll carry that. But weigh it honestly, Maren. Which lie is bigger — mine, or the one this whole valley has been living?"

Her face did something complicated. I watched her decide not to answer that, and the not-answering told me more than an answer would have.

* * *

They deliberated with the doors open, which meant the whole village heard it, which meant the whole village voted with its feet long before the formal verdict. People packed the shrine's doorway and spilled down the steps into the yard. I recognized faces I'd pulled out of flooded creeks, faces I'd taught to hold a spear, faces whose bread I'd eaten every market day for a decade. Some of them wouldn't meet my eyes. Some of them stared like I was a stranger wearing a familiar coat.

That's the thing about a small community learning a hard truth: it doesn't split cleanly. It frays. I watched Hesta Green, who'd nursed Dain through a fever that should have killed him, stand with her arms folded tight and her mouth set against me. I watched her son behind her, sixteen and stubborn, standing with the group near the door who'd started calling themselves — gods help us — *Wren's people*, as if the child were a banner rather than a burden. Families divided down their own kitchen tables. A husband and wife arguing in whispers loud enough to reach the back row. The valley hadn't fractured along any line anyone could draw. It had shattered along every fault line it had ever quietly pretended didn't exist.

"They should go," someone said from the crowd. Not shouted. Said, the way you say a thing you've been carrying too long. "The child and the mercenary both. We were safe here. We were *forgotten* here, and that was worth more than anything they brought us."

"We weren't forgotten," I said, without turning around. "We were *overlooked*. There's a difference, and the difference has a name, and it wears white."

"Freya." Maren's voice cut across the room. "You'll be silent during deliberation."

"I've been silent for twelve years," I said. "It hasn't worked out well for anyone."

Tam laughed — one short bark, quickly strangled. Even Maren's mouth twitched before she killed it.

The deliberation itself was agony in miniature. Nine people, nine voices, and underneath all of them the low hum of the crowd outside, which had opinions of its own and no chair to sit in. I heard the arguments I expected: law, precedent, the sanctity of the peace, the simple animal arithmetic of *we were safe and now we might not be*. I heard arguments I didn't expect: a woman describing how Wren had mended her fence unprompted, badly, with string; a farmer recalling that the scouts had crossed the boundary stones without the stones so much as warming, which had never happened before, which meant the valley's protection was already failing whether or not we kept the child.

That last one landed hard. I saw it move through the crowd like wind through wheat. The protection failing. Nobody wanted to say the next part aloud: if the protection was failing anyway, then handing over Wren bought us nothing except a clean conscience we could present to the Order like a toll payment.

Or maybe it bought us exactly that. Maybe a clean conscience presented to the right authority is worth more than a child. History suggests it's a popular trade.

Then Maren stood, and the room went quiet the way it only goes quiet for her.

"I'll speak my vote first," she said, "since I suspect it will surprise no one." Her eyes found mine and held. There was no cruelty in them. That was the worst part. There was only grief, and duty, and something older than either. "Freya Reyes broke the peace. Broke it publicly, violently, in front of witnesses, and then lied to this council to conceal a stranger whose presence endangers every soul in this valley. The law is the law because it holds when we would rather it didn't. I vote exile. Both of them. At first light."

The word hit the room like a dropped stone. *Exile.* I'd heard it pronounced over bandits and oath-breakers and once, memorably, a tax collector. I'd never imagined hearing it shaped around my own name. Something in my chest went cold and small, and I thought, absurdly, of my morning rite, of all those years speaking to a god who never answered, and how exile from this valley would just be more of the same — talking into a silence that never talks back.

Votes went around the crescent. Three for exile. Two against. The beekeeper abstained with visible personal anguish, which I believe is the traditional role of beekeepers in all councils everywhere. Four votes remained, and among them Dain, who had said almost nothing all day, which for Dain was louder than shouting.

"Dain," Maren said. "Your vote."

He uncrossed his arms slowly. Walked to the center of the crescent, boots ringing on the flagstones, and turned to face the crowd instead of the council, which was not procedure but was very Dain.

"I swore an oath of truth when I took this post," he said. "Truth above all. Most of you have heard me say it. So here's some." He looked out at the packed doorway, at the frayed and frightened village. "Freya lied to this council. That's true, and I won't soften it. And I've spent every night since trying to decide which bothers me more — the lie, or the fact that if Freya had told the truth that night, half of you would have walked the child to the gate yourselves, wrapped up warm, with bread for the road."

Nobody breathed.

"I nearly told the riders everything," Dain went on. "Stood ten feet from them with the truth in my mouth like a hot coal. And what stopped me wasn't loyalty to Freya. It was this: they asked for a *child*. By description. By name. Soldiers who describe children by name, who cross mountains for them, who burn villages asking after them — you don't hand anything to people like that and stay clean. You just stop being the kind of village that gets to look at itself in the morning." His voice roughened. "I've broken my oath of truth today, to cover for Freya, and I'll answer for that however this council sees fit. But I'm not breaking it twice. Here's my truth: I vote they stay. Both of them. Because the peace we're so proud of was already bleeding out before Freya ever swung at those scouts — the boundary stones went cold, the crops at the field edges are blackening, and no child we surrender is going to change that. Exile doesn't restore what's breaking. It just makes us smaller when it finishes breaking."

Silence. The heavy kind, the valley's native crop.

"Four to four," Maren said finally, "with one abstention." Her gaze moved down the crescent and settled on the beekeeper, who made a sound like a man being asked to choose between his own lungs.

"Master Pollen," Maren said gently. "You must vote."

"I keep bees," he said faintly, to no one, to the god, to the room. "Bees don't ask this much of a person."

But he raised his hand. Against exile. Five to four.

The sound the crowd made wasn't cheering. It was something rawer — relief and fury tangled together, half the room exhaling and the other half inhaling to shout. Hesta Green turned and pushed out through the doorway without looking back. Her son stayed. Somewhere in the press of bodies, someone began to cry, and someone else began, horribly, to laugh.

Maren lifted a hand and the room stilled.

"The council's judgment stands," she said. "They stay." Her eyes came to mine one last time, and what I saw in them frightened me more than the vote had. Not anger. Calculation. The look of a woman rearranging pieces on a board I couldn't see. "But understand this, Freya Reyes. Staying is not the same as being forgiven. And the valley's protection is failing regardless of what we decided here today. Whatever comes over that ridge next—" she glanced toward the door, toward the darkening east, "—we will face it together or not at all. Dismissed."

The crowd drained out slow, reluctant, buzzing. I stood on the pale stone a moment longer, alone in the emptying shrine, listening to the silence that had been my employer, my faith, and my home for twelve years.

It occurred to me, standing there, that I had never once in all that time asked the god a direct question.

I opened my mouth to ask one now — and stopped. Because far off beyond the ridge, thin as a wire, a horn was sounding. Then another. Then a third, lower, closer, answering the first.

Not a summons this time.

An answer.
