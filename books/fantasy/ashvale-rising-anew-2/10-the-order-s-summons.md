## 10. The Order's Summons

I have burned a great many letters in my service to the Unbroken Vow, but I have never once burned a report. Reports are the true scripture of any order of faith. Hymns are for the congregation; ledgers are for God.

So when the courier from the eastern watch arrived at my tent with mud to his knees and terror in his eyes, and told me that a child had walked into a valley that does not exist, carrying words that made stones sing, I did not pray. I opened my ledger.

"Say it again," I said. "Slowly. And this time leave out the part where you apologize for waking me."

He left out most of it anyway. Fear does that—strips a man down to nouns. *Child. Valley gate. Old tongue. The shrine stones hummed.* He kept glancing at the tent flap as though the words themselves might follow him in and take root.

"You're certain it was the old tongue," I said. "Not High Ashvalean. Not the liturgical dialect we use at consecrations."

"Certain, Commander. Brother Anselm heard it. He wept."

Brother Anselm weeping was not, in itself, remarkable. Anselm wept at good bread, at rain, at the sight of healthy livestock. But he had been trained in paleography before his vows took him, and if *he* said the child spoke the dead language—the one that predated every kingdom, every schism, every careful rewriting of doctrine—then the child spoke it.

"Describe the valley," I said.

He described it badly, which is how I knew he'd actually seen it. Men who lie about places describe them beautifully: golden light, picturesque hills, a river like hammered silver. Men who have truly stood before something wrong describe it in fragments. Mud. A gate. A smell like cold iron and wheat. The sense—here he struggled, hands turning over each other—that the valley had been *looking back* at him.

Twelve years I had served the Order of the Unbroken Vow, and six of those as commander of its southern reach, and in all that time the Valley of Ashvale had been a rumor with excellent posture. It appeared on no map worth trusting. Merchants who claimed to have traded there could not agree on which road they'd taken. Our own records—a library of forty thousand volumes, indexed by three generations of obsessives—contained exactly one reference, in a marginal note from two centuries past: *Ashvale closed. Let it sleep. Do not count its people.*

Do not count its people. In an order that counted everything—candles burned, vows spoken, sins tallied against absolution purchased—that instruction had always struck me as the single most honest sentence we possessed.

And now a child had walked through its gate speaking the god's own buried voice.

* * *

I want to be clear about something, because history will be written by whoever survives, and survivors are terrible liars only by accident.

I did not come to Ashvale to burn anything.

The Order has a reputation—I've heard it sung in taverns with more courage than accuracy—and like most reputations it is a caricature drawn by people who have never had to hold a line. Yes, we wear white. Yes, we believe the silence of the gods is not a tragedy but a *discipline*, the last and greatest act of a divinity that looked upon what it had made and chose restraint. Yes, we hunt what threatens that discipline.

But I have commanded soldiers for twenty years, and I will tell you what I have learned about fire: it is the tool of men who have run out of arguments. Every burning I have authorized sits in my ledger with its justification, and every justification is thinner than the last. I am not proud of the arithmetic. I am only honest about it.

What I came to Ashvale to do was talk.

That the talking would end in surrender or in ash was not my choice to make in advance. That is the difference between faith and fanaticism, whatever our critics say: the fanatic knows the ending before the story starts. I only knew the stakes. A shard of divine power loose in the world after three hundred years of silence, carried in the body of a child who could not possibly understand it—that was not a tragedy waiting to happen. It was a war waiting to resume, and I had read enough of the old accounts to know what the last one had cost. Whole coastlines unmade. Kingdoms that exist now only as loanwords. The gods themselves going quiet—not dead, the doctrine is very precise on this point, *not dead*, merely silent, having withdrawn their hands from the world so that the world might continue existing at all.

You cannot negotiate with a resumed war. You can only choose which side of it your village dies on.

* * *

We rode up to the ridge on the fourth morning, thirty riders in white, and I ordered the banners furled.

My lieutenant, Serah, thought this a mistake. "They should see us coming," she said. "It's honest."

"It's theatrical," I said. "Honesty doesn't require costume. Besides—" I nodded down at the valley below us, green and gold and aggressively ordinary, wheat fields rippling in a wind that seemed to carry nothing else, "—they already know we're here. Look at the fields."

She looked. To her credit, she saw it within a minute: the near field harvested, the far field half-cut, and between them a band of standing grain that no farmer alive would leave unless someone had told him to leave it. A sightline. A warning row. Someone down there had been watching the ridge for days and arranging their harvest around what they saw.

"Mercenaries," Serah said.

"At least one," I agreed. "Probably the one who's been hiding the child." I had spent the previous evening reading everything the Order possessed on Ashvale's garrison, which amounted to a single mercenary company's contract, sixty years stale, and a traveler's account describing a guard captain with a scarred jaw and a habit of asking questions twice. Freya Reyes. Oath-sworn to Ashvale itself, which made them either the most loyal creature in the valley or the most compromised. With oath-bound mercenaries, in my experience, both things are usually true at once, and they fight each other in the dark.

We rode down openly, unhurried, weapons sheathed. Thirty riders in white moving at a walk looks like a procession. Moving at a gallop, it looks like an invasion, and I needed these people watching me with their eyes rather than reaching for me with their hands.

The village met us at the crossroads by the shrine—which was itself informative. Not at the gate, where a garrison would make its stand, but at the crossroads, where a community makes its decisions. Elder Maren Corvane stood at the front of perhaps two hundred souls, and behind her, arranged with a casualness so studied it practically announced itself, stood a dozen armed men and women whose stances I recognized the way you recognize a family resemblance. Mercenaries. Good ones, by the set of their shoulders. One of them—a big man with grey coming into his beard—stood slightly apart from the others, and his face was doing something complicated.

I dismounted. You learn quickly in this work that height is a weapon, and I prefer to keep mine sheathed too.

"Elder Corvane," I said. "I am Ilse Varn, Commander of the Southern Reach of the Order of the Unbroken Vow. I've come about a missing child."

"We have no missing children," Maren said. Her voice was dry as August. "Our children are all present and accounted for."

"I'm delighted to hear it." I let the pause stretch just long enough to be felt. "Then you'll have no objection to my riders walking the village and confirming it. We'll be gone by nightfall."

Behind her, the scarred-jawed mercenary shifted their weight. Freya Reyes, then. Up close they were younger than the reports suggested and more tired, which is what twelve years of guarding a god who never answers will do to a person. Their hand wasn't near their sword. That was discipline, not peace.

"That won't be necessary," Maren said.

"No," I agreed pleasantly. "It rarely is. And yet."

* * *

Here is what happened next, and I record it precisely because I know how it will be told otherwise.

I did not threaten the village. I laid out terms, in the open air, before witnesses, because I wanted every soul in Ashvale to hear exactly what was being asked and exactly what was being offered. Sunlight is the only honesty available to people in my profession, and I have always preferred it to the alternative.

"The child came through your gate nine days ago," I said. "Speaking a language older than any kingdom. Your shrine stones responded. I know this because the Order has watched this valley for two hundred years, Elder, and we do not watch it because we fear what's inside. We watch it because we remember what was put there."

Maren's face did not move. But the crowd did—a ripple, shoulder to shoulder, the sound of two hundred people discovering simultaneously that their elders had been keeping something from them. I noted it. I file everything.

"What was put there," I continued, "was a piece of the god Ashvale. Its power, divided from its will and buried beneath your shrine, sealed away so that the war it fueled could finally end. The silence you live in—the reason no magic crosses your boundary, the reason your wells run sweet and your children are born straight-limbed—is not a blessing. It is a seal. And seals, Elder, are made to be broken. That is their entire purpose. They hold until something comes along strong enough to test them."

"And you think a child is strong enough?" This from the scarred mercenary. First words. Voice like gravel settling.

"I think the child isn't doing the carrying," I said, and turned to face them fully. "I think the shard is doing the carrying, and the child is simply where it decided to stop. I think that nine days ago it woke up, after three hundred years, and walked to the one place in the world built to contain it. Shards seek their burial ground, Captain Reyes. It wasn't the child that found your valley. It was the power coming home."

I watched that land. Watched it land in Reyes's jaw, in the tightening of the big mercenary's hands, in the way Maren Corvane went very still in the particular way of a woman hearing a secret spoken aloud that she had hoped would die unspoken. Oh yes. The elder knew. The elder's line had always known; that is what elders are *for*, in valleys like this. Keepers of the quiet.

"So here are my terms," I said, and kept my voice level, almost gentle, because the truth needs no volume. "The child carries a fragment of divine power. Left alone, it will grow into that power until neither the child nor anyone near the child can survive the growth—or until the seal beneath your shrine cracks under the strain of welcoming it home, and everything the god buried wakes up wearing the child like a coat. Restored to the Order, it can be returned to the seal properly, with rite and preparation, and the silence holds for another three hundred years. Destroyed—and destruction is possible, though it grieves me—the silence holds forever. Either way, the war stays buried. Either way, your valley keeps its sweet wells and its straight-limbed children."

"And if we refuse?" someone called from the crowd. A young man, farm-built, frightened.

I looked at him with real sympathy. "Then the seal breaks on its own schedule instead of ours, and everything I described happens without the benefit of preparation. I am not threatening you, friend. I am telling you the weather."

* * *

Serah believed, afterward, that I should have pressed harder in that moment. That I should have named the deadline there at the crossroads, in front of everyone, and forced the crisis into the open while my thirty riders still held the high ground of moral clarity.

But I have taken villages before, in the early years, back when I confused speed with decisiveness, and I can tell you what taking a village costs. It costs you the village. What remains afterward is territory, and territory is just grief with borders.

So instead I asked for Reyes. Alone. And to everyone's visible surprise—including, I suspect, their own—they gave me the meeting.

We spoke in the shade of the shrine wall, out of earshot of the crowd, and I will grant the captain this: they listened. Not the listening of a cornered animal, but the listening of a soldier being briefed, sorting what I said into what matched their own observations and what didn't. I respected it. I have been lied to by professionals for twenty years; a person who genuinely wants to check my claims against their own eyes is nearly restful.

"You believe all of this," Reyes said at last. Not a question. An observation, faintly puzzled.

"With my whole heart," I said. "Which is why I'm offering you what I'm about to offer, and why I'll only offer it once."

I told them: surrender the child by moonrise tonight, willingly, unharmed, and every act committed in its concealment would be struck from the record. The lies to the council. The obstruction of the Order. Treason against an oath sworn to Ashvale itself—for I knew about the oath; of course I knew; the Order keeps better books than God does—all of it absolved, formally, under seal. They could remain in the valley. Their found family, their routine, their silent morning rites at a shrine to a god who never answers. All of it restored, untouched, as though the child had never staggered through the gate at dusk.

"And the child?" Reyes said quietly.

"Goes to the crypt beneath your own shrine, in the presence of clergy, and the shard returns to the seal. The child goes free afterward. I swear it on my rank and my record." I paused, and then—because I wanted them to understand that I understood the weight of what I was asking—I added: "I know what I'm asking you to do. I'm asking you to break a child's trust to save a hundred children's lives. I have done it. It does not get easier. But it gets *clearer*, and clarity is the only mercy this work offers."

Reyes looked at me for a long moment. There was no hatred in their face, which I found interesting, and no agreement either, which I found more so. Just a kind of weary assessment, the look of someone measuring me against something I couldn't see.

"You keep saying *the seal holds*," they said. "Like that's the goal. Like holding is the same thing as *good*."

"The alternative is the war," I said. "I've read the accounts, Captain. Have you?"

"I've read the shrine," they said. "Recently. You might be surprised what it remembers."

And they walked away from me across the square, unhurried, and I stood in the shade of the wall and admitted to myself what I had known since the courier arrived: that I was not going to get my surrender. That somewhere inside that tired, scarred, oath-bound mercenary, something had already cracked open, and cracked-open things do not reseal at the request of commanders.

* * *

At moonrise I stood at the edge of the wheat field with Serah and waited, because I had given my word that the offer expired at moonrise, and I keep my word even when I already know how it will be spent. That is the whole discipline, really. The Vow isn't belief. Belief is cheap; every fool has beliefs. The Vow is doing the thing you said you'd do after you've stopped wanting to.

The moon cleared the ridge. No one came.

"Well," Serah said.

"Well," I agreed. I took out my ledger, and by lantern light I wrote the date, the hour, and the terms refused, in my own hand, because reports are scripture and scripture must be witnessed. Then I closed the book and looked down at the valley, at the darkened windows of a village that had chosen its answer, and I felt the thing I always feel at moments like this, which is not triumph and not regret but a terrible clean sadness, like the air before snow.

"They think we're the storm," Serah said.

"We are the storm," I said. "That's not an insult, Serah. Storms are honest. They arrive, they do what storms do, and afterward everyone rebuilds knowing exactly what the sky is capable of." I mounted. "It's the quiet that kills you. Ask their god."

Below us, in the dark, the shrine stones began—very faintly, very briefly—to hum.

I gave the order, and the torches went out along the ridge, and the first fires bloomed instead in the wheat.
