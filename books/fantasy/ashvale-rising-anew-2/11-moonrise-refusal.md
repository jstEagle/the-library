## 11. Moonrise Refusal

Commander Ilse Varn stood at the edge of the shrine square like a woman who had never once been told no, and had therefore never learned what to do with her hands when the answer might come.

She'd arrived at dusk with six riders and a banner of white so clean it looked like an insult. The Order of the Unbroken Vow did not ride dirty. That was, I understood now, rather the point of them.

"By moonrise," she said again, for the benefit of the crowd that had gathered—half the village, it seemed, drawn out of their houses by the same instinct that pulls people to watch a fire. "Surrender the child, and all is forgiven. The valley's transgressions will be recorded as ignorance, not treason. You have my word."

Her word. I turned it over in my mouth like a coin I suspected was clipped.

The moon was already climbing over the eastern ridge, fat and pale, throwing long shadows from the shrine stones—the ones that hummed when Wren spoke, the ones I'd spent twelve years polishing without ever asking why they needed polishing. Behind Varn, her riders sat their horses with the stillness of men who had done terrible things on schedule and found the schedule comforting.

Maren Corvane stood at my left. Dain at my right. Wren was supposed to be inside the shrine, behind a locked door, with Tam the miller's boy standing guard—a fifteen-year-old with a wood axe and instructions to make noise, not history. I could feel the door at my back like a hand between my shoulder blades.

"You've given us a great deal to consider," Maren said, in the voice she used for negotiating grain prices, which was to say: the voice of a woman who intended to win. "Perhaps we might have until—"

"No," I said.

It came out before I'd decided to say it. Twelve years of discipline, and apparently all it took was one commander saying *all is forgiven* in a voice like warm milk.

Varn's eyes came to me. Grey eyes, patient, the eyes of someone who had already written this scene in her ledger and was merely waiting for me to catch up. "Captain Reyes. Or do you prefer Freya? The Order's records are inconsistent about which name you swore under."

"Freya's fine." I stepped forward, away from Maren, away from Dain, into the open space between the village and the riders. My boots crunched on frost. It was going to be a cold night; I remember thinking that, absurdly, that the wheat would suffer if the frost got into the low fields, because some part of my mind will always be a farmhand's part no matter how many swords I carry. "Let's not pretend this is a negotiation. You want the child. You'll burn our fields if we don't give them up. And you're offering forgiveness for crimes you invented, committed by people who didn't know they were committing them."

"I'm offering mercy," Varn said.

"You're offering a receipt." I raised my voice—not shouting, but pitched to carry to the back of the square, to Old Sefa at the well, to the Halloway brothers by the granary, to every face turned toward me in the blue dark. "You're offering to forgive us for sheltering a child. Think about what that means. Think about what kind of god needs his forgiveness bought with a child's blood, and then think about whether he's worth the price."

A murmur went through the crowd. Not agreement—fear wearing agreement's clothes. I'd expected that. What I hadn't expected was how much it would cost me to keep talking anyway.

"The Order serves Ashvale," Varn said evenly. "As do you. As your own oath binds you. Obedience unto silence, Captain. You've read the old wording by now—I know you have, or you wouldn't be standing there looking like a woman reading her own death warrant."

She knew about the oath. Of course she knew. She'd probably helped write it, or her grandmother had, or somebody three centuries dead whose bones were dust and whose sentence was still being served by people like me.

"I did read it," I said. "Every word. Including the parts your order buried under the pretty ones." I turned, deliberately, to face the villagers. To face the shrine. "I swore an oath to Ashvale twelve years ago. Kneeling right there, on those stones. I said the words they gave me, and I meant them, and I have kept them through every hard season and every hungry winter since. So believe me when I tell you what I found in those words when I finally read them whole."

The moon cleared the ridge fully then, and light spilled down into the square, silvering everything—the wheat stubble beyond the fence, the white cloaks, the cracks in the shrine steps I'd been pretending not to see for two days.

"*Obedience unto silence,*" I said, "even unto atrocity. That's the clause. That's what I knelt on. Not protection. Not faith. Permission. Permission to do anything at all and call it holy, so long as I did it quietly." I let that sit. Let the frost sit too; it was getting into everyone's bones, mine included. "I won't do it. I won't hand over a child to be opened up like a letter, and I won't pretend the god who ordered it deserves my silence anymore."

"So you break faith," Varn said. Not angry. Worse—disappointed, the way a schoolteacher is disappointed, as though I were a clever pupil wasting her potential. "Publicly. Before witnesses."

"Before witnesses," I agreed. "That's the point of renouncing something. If you do it quiet, it doesn't count."

"Then hear the count, Captain." She drew herself up, and for the first time her voice carried the weight of office rather than courtesy, and every villager flinched from it like a whipcrack. "Freya Reyes, sworn blade of Ashvale, I declare your oath void and your protection with it. The valley stands outside the Vow's grace from this hour. Whatever comes, comes unopposed."

Whatever comes, comes unopposed.

I'd braced for thunder. For a curse, a bolt, something cinematic. Instead there was just wind moving through the dead wheat, and the sound of six hundred heartbeats trying very hard to be quiet.

Then the shrine stones cracked.

Not all at once. A sound first—deep, mineral, like ice on a lake in late spring—and then a fissure walked up the eastern pillar, slow as a finger drawing a line, and a chip of stone the size of a loaf fell and shattered on the steps. The humming stopped. I hadn't realized it had started again until it stopped, and the silence it left behind was worse than any noise, because it was the silence the valley had always had, except now it felt less like peace and more like a held breath finally failing.

And out past the fence line, in the low fields where the frost had been sitting gentle as a blanket, the wheat began to blacken.

I watched it happen. We all watched it happen. A darkness creeping along the rows like ink dropped in water, curling the stalks, and the smell that came off it wasn't smoke—it was rot, sweet and wrong, the smell of a growing thing deciding all at once to stop. Old Sefa made a sound I'll hear until I die, a small wounded noise, the noise of a woman watching sixty years of harvests end in the space of ten breaths.

"That," Varn said quietly, "is what leaving the Vow looks like. The seal weakens. The land remembers what it was holding back. Surrender the child by tomorrow's moonrise and perhaps we can restore—"

"Get off my land," I said.

My voice didn't shake. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone, because everything else in me was shaking, and the only thing holding me upright was the pure animal stubbornness of a person who has run out of every other option except the next word.

Varn studied me for a long moment. Then she inclined her head, precisely, courteously, the way you bow to a corpse. "Tomorrow's moonrise, Captain. The offer stands until it doesn't." She turned her horse. Six white cloaks wheeled after her, and they rode out along the ridge road at a walk, unhurried, certain, and the hoofbeats faded and the valley closed behind them like water.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a while after that. The square emptied slowly, the way a wound bleeds—not all at once, in trickles, faces turning away from me one by one. Some wouldn't look at me at all. Old Sefa walked out to her fields and stood at the fence staring at the blackened rows until her son came and put a coat around her shoulders and led her home.

Dain stayed. He leaned against the well post with his arms crossed and watched me watch the wheat.

"Well," he said eventually. "That went well."

"Don't."

"I'm just saying. You wanted to make a statement. Statement made. Very crisp. The part where the crops died was a nice touch—really drove the theme home."

"Dain."

"What?" He pushed off the post and came closer, and his voice dropped out of its wry register into the one underneath, the one he only used when the joke had worn through. "You did the right thing. I want that said plainly, once, by somebody, tonight, because tomorrow nobody's going to say it. You did the right thing and it cost exactly what right things cost, which is everything, immediately." He glanced at the shrine, at the crack running up the pillar like a lightning scar. "How bad is it?"

"I don't know. The seal's weakening. Whether it fails in a week or a year—" I shook my head. "I don't know. Nobody knows. That's the problem with breaking a silence that's lasted three hundred years. There's no manual."

"There's a crypt under the shrine," Dain said. "There's a manual."

He was right, and we both knew it, and neither of us said the rest of it out loud—that whatever was down there, whatever the god had buried and sealed and built our whole comfortable lie on top of, was now one cracked pillar closer to waking up.

Maren appeared at my elbow. I hadn't heard her cross the square; she moved like that when she chose to, which was most of the reason she'd been elder for twenty years. Her face in the moonlight was unreadable, which from Maren usually meant furious.

"Half the council wants to convene tonight," she said. "Half wants to drag you to the border themselves and apologize to the Order on their knees."

"And the other half?"

"There are only two halves, Freya." But something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, more the memory of one. "I told them dawn. If we're going to tear ourselves apart, we'll do it rested, and with bread, like civilized people." She looked out at the black fields, and her voice went quieter than I'd ever heard it. "My grandmother fed names to the Order for forty years to keep this valley invisible. Do you understand what you've done? You've made us visible."

"Yes," I said.

"She'd have hated you."

"Probably."

Maren exhaled through her nose, almost a laugh, mostly not. "Good," she said, and walked away, and I decided not to examine that too closely, because some comforts only work if you don't look directly at them.

* * *

Wren was awake, of course. Sitting up on the cot in the shrine's back room with the blanket around their shoulders like a small grey ghost, watching the door while I came in. Tam was asleep against the wall, axe across his knees, which was either diligent or adorable depending on how charitable I felt.

"They heard everything," Wren signed. Their hands were steady. Their eyes weren't. "Through the door. They said tomorrow's moonrise."

"They did."

"If I go, the fields come back. The stones stop cracking. Everyone stops being afraid of you." They said it flatly, the way children state arithmetic, and it landed harder than any argument the council could have mustered. "That's the trade. I can do sums."

I crouched down to their level. Up close I could see the shard's faint light under their skin, there and gone, like a fish turning in shallow water. "You want to know what I've learned about trades?" I said. "Any deal where one side is a child and the other side is an army isn't a trade. It's a robbery with paperwork. I've spent twelve years keeping my word to a god who didn't deserve it. I'm not starting fresh by selling you."

"But the wheat—"

"The wheat can be replanted. Stones can be mended. People—" I stopped, because the honest ending to that sentence was *people can't*, and Wren deserved better than a lie dressed as comfort. "People are harder. But we hold together, and we figure it out. That's the whole plan. It's not a good plan. It's the only one I've got."

Wren considered this with the grave attention they gave everything, and then, instead of signing, they spoke—one word aloud, in the old tongue, soft as breath. The air in the room changed. The cracked pillar out front gave a single low note, like a bell struck once under a blanket, and the light under Wren's skin steadied instead of flickering.

They caught my expression and shrugged, sheepish, and signed: *"It means 'stay.' I was practicing."*

I laughed. It came out ragged and surprised me both ways. Outside, the moon kept climbing toward tomorrow's deadline, and the wheat lay black in the fields, and somewhere on the ridge the Order was counting hours with the patience of people who had never once been told no.

But in the shrine's back room, a child practiced the word for stay, and the broken stones sang one note back, and I thought: let them come at moonrise. We would not be the only ones with a count to settle.

Below the floor, beneath the seal, something turned over in its sleep—and for the first time, I was almost glad.
