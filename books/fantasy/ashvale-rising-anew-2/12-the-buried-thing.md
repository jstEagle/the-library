## 12. The Buried Thing

I have carried a lot of things down a lot of stairs in my life. Sacks of grain. Wounded men who swore worse than I did. A keg of plum brandy that nearly ended my career and my spine in the same afternoon.

Never before had I carried a lantern into the dark under a god's house, following a woman who'd just been tried for treason, a child who spoke in the voice of dead scripture, and the growing suspicion that everything I'd sworn my life to was a lie with good posture.

The stairs beneath the shrine were older than the shrine. That was my first thought, and it was not a comforting one. The shrine above was old — older than the village, older than Maren Corvane's family, which Maren would tell you is older than sin itself. But these steps predated even that. They were cut from black stone that didn't match anything else in Ashvale, worn in the middle like a riverbed, as if a great many feet had gone down them once, long ago, and none had come back up.

"Nobody thought to mention there was a basement?" I said, mostly to hear my own voice. It's a habit. When the world goes quiet, I get chatty. Freya says it's because silence makes me honest, and I've never found a good argument against that.

"There was no basement," Freya said. "There was a wall."

"And now?"

"Now there's a door."

That was the other thing I hadn't gotten used to yet. Three days since the trial, two days since Varn's summons burned itself into every mind in the valley, one day since the wheat fields started blackening at their edges like paper held too near a flame — and in all that time, the shrine had been *changing*. Stones cracking along seams nobody had ever noticed. Hairline fractures spreading across the floor in patterns too regular to be accident. And last night, at moonrise, the back wall of the inner sanctum had simply opened, the way a fist opens when the hand inside it finally decides to let go.

Maren had wanted to seal it again. Bricks, mortar, prayer, whatever it took. But you can't re-close a door that only existed because something on the other side stopped holding it shut.

So instead we went down. The three of us. Because apparently I've lost what remained of my sense.

* * *

Wren walked between us, small hands wrapped around a stub of candle they refused to light. They didn't need it. That was the part that kept raising the hair on my arms — the child could see down here. Not torchlight-see. *Know*-see. Their eyes moved ahead of us through the dark like they were reading it.

"Is it true," I asked quietly, "what you told the council? That you can feel it?"

Wren nodded. Then, after a moment, in that careful way they had of choosing words like a man chooses footing on ice: "It's sad."

"Sad," I repeated.

"It's been alone a very long time." Wren looked up at me, and in the lantern light their face was grave in a way no child's face should be. "It doesn't hate anyone. I thought it would. Everyone thinks it will. But it's just—" They searched for the word. "It's just full. Too full. Like a cup that can't stop pouring."

Freya said nothing. Freya had said very little since the crypt wall opened, and I'd known them twelve years, so I knew what that meant. Silence, for Freya Reyes, wasn't emptiness. It was weight. They were carrying something heavy and hadn't decided where to set it down.

The stairs ended.

* * *

I want to describe what we saw honestly, because I'm the one writing this down and someone has to, but I'll tell you now that words are going to fail me, and I resent that, because words are supposed to be my whole creed. Truth above all. Say what is. Well. Here is what is, as best I can say it:

The chamber beneath Ashvale was vast. Bigger than the shrine above it, bigger than the whole village could fit into, which should be impossible and I've stopped caring about impossible this week. Its walls weren't built. They were *grown*, or perhaps grown-at — black stone veined through with lines of pale gold, branching and rebranching, and every vein pulsed. Slowly. Rhythmically. Like something breathing, if breathing took a minute per breath.

And in the center, suspended a man's height off the floor, hung a heart.

Not flesh. Not stone. Something between — a mass of that same gold-veined dark, turning slowly on nothing, shedding light the way an ember sheds heat. It filled the chamber the way a bell fills a tower: not by size, but by presence. You felt it in your teeth. In the backs of your eyes. In the old oath scar on my forearm, which had ached ever since the wall opened, and which ached now so hard I had to bite my tongue to keep from crying out.

"That's it," Freya said. Flat. Careful. "That's what he buried."

"How do you know it's a he?"

"I don't." A pause. "Habit."

We came closer. I want to claim I was brave about it. I was not brave about it. My legs moved because they'd followed Freya into bad places for twelve years and saw no reason to develop opinions now.

Up close, the heart was worse. Or better. I still haven't decided. Because the veins running through it — the gold ones — they weren't random. When I tilted my head, squinted, let my eyes go soft the way you do reading old script—

"They're words," Wren whispered.

They were. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Lines of script in the old tongue winding through the dark mass like rivers through country, and Wren stood beneath it with their face turned up and read aloud, haltingly, translating as they went, and this is what the god of Ashvale had written into his own stolen heart:

*I take it with me. All of it. Every spark, every wellspring, every gift I gave and every gift my kin gave, I gather it into myself and I go down, and I close the way behind me.*

*Let them call it protection. Let them build their valley on my lid and thank me for the quiet.*

*It is safer for them to worship a silent god than to know what their gods did with their gifts while the world burned.*

*What I hoarded, I hoarded from everyone. Including myself. Let it rot in me rather than burn in their hands again.*

*This is not mercy. Do not write that it is mercy. Write that it is shame.*

Wren's voice cracked on the last line. They stopped. The chamber breathed its slow golden breath around us.

I am not a man who cries easily. I cried twice in the war, both times over people who deserved better, and I decided after the second time that tears were a luxury I'd trade for function. Standing under that heart, listening to a child translate a god's confession out of the dark, I broke my rule. Quietly. Nobody mentioned it. That's the kind of family we are.

* * *

"So it's true," I said, when I could trust my voice. "The war. The burning. He didn't stop it. He—"

"He robbed it," Freya said. "When the war went wrong — when magic became the weapon instead of the tool — he didn't fight. He *took*. Every source of power in the world, gathered into himself, and sealed himself away with it. And the rest of the gods—" Freya's jaw worked. "The rest of the gods went looking for him, or went mad without their power, or died. And the mortals who survived learned to live without. And built a valley on top of the vault, and called the silence holy."

"The Order knows," I said. "Varn knows. That's what the Unbroken Vow actually *is*. Not priests. Wardens. Keepers of the lid."

"Wardens who want to open it." Freya turned, and the gold light moved across their face like water. "Or restore it. Wren's shard — the piece that got out, that traveled south in a fleeing child — it's a key either way. That's why they hunt. Not because the child is dangerous. Because the child is *decisive*."

I looked at the heart a long time. At the slow pulse of it. At the words of shame written through it in a dead god's own hand.

Here's the thing about serving a silent god for twenty years: you fill the silence yourself. You decide what the silence means. I'd spent two decades telling myself Ashvale's quiet was discipline. Restraint. A god so mighty he didn't need to speak. I'd built a whole faith on the foundation of a god being too proud to answer, and it turns out the truth was that he was too ashamed.

The enemy was never beneath us.

The enemy — if enemy is even the word, and I genuinely don't know anymore — was the thing we worshipped above us. The shrine stones we kissed each morning sat on the lid of a vault containing every miracle the world had ever lost, guarded by a god who couldn't bear to look at what he'd done, and we called that holiness, and we raised our children in it, and I wrote *truth above all* on my own skin and never once asked what truth cost the man who kept it.

"You're doing the thing," Freya said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where your face does arithmetic." They almost smiled. Almost. "Say it."

"All right." I squared up to them, because some things you say standing. "Everything we protected. The patrols, the rites, the rule about not speaking of outside, the whole beautiful quiet machine of this valley — it wasn't sanctuary. It was a cover story. We weren't guarding the village, Freya. We were guarding his *guilt*. Twelve years I kept your watch. Twenty I kept the faith. And the whole time, the thing under our feet was the reason children burned in the war, because their god decided they were safer ignorant than empowered, and made that decision *for* them, forever, and called it love."

"Yes."

"And you knew. Some of it. Since the inscriptions."

"I suspected. Now I know." Freya looked back at the heart, and their voice dropped to something raw. "And here's what I know besides, Dain. That power isn't evil. It's *ours*. It was always ours. He stole it to keep us from hurting each other with it, and maybe he was even right, and it doesn't matter, because it was never his to keep. Not the land's power. Not the people's. And Varn wants it for the Order, and the dissenters want to give it to Varn to make the burning stop, and every single one of them is wrong in exactly the way he was wrong."

"Which is?"

"Deciding for everyone else." Freya met my eyes. "So we don't decide. We offer. We ask the valley — all of it, even the ones who gave the Order the map — and whatever they choose, we do. But someone has to show them the choice first. Someone has to open the lid wide enough that every soul in Ashvale sees what I'm seeing right now."

I looked at the heart. At the confession written through it in gold. At the child standing beneath it with candle wax on their fingers and grief on their face for a god they'd never met.

"And if they choose to hand Wren over?" I asked. "If they choose the Order? We just — stand aside?"

"We stand aside," Freya said, "and then we break every rule I have left. But yes. We stand aside."

"That's a terrible plan."

"It's the only honest one I've got."

Gods help me, I laughed. Down in the dark, under the lid of the world, with my oath-scar screaming and my creed in ruins around my boots — I laughed, because it was either that or weep again, and I'd already used up my ration.

* * *

We started back up the stairs. Wren lingered, one hand raised toward the turning heart, and for a moment the gold veins brightened — just slightly, just briefly — the way a fire brightens when someone sits down beside it.

"It heard you," Wren said softly, to Freya. To both of us. "It says — it says *thank you for reading*. Nobody has read it in a thousand years."

Then the child climbed past me into the dark, and I took one last look at the buried thing beneath my life's work, and I swear on Dain's name, which is the only name I've got left worth swearing on —

the heart was watching us go.

And far above, muffled by a hundred feet of stone, we heard the first horn of the Order sound beyond the ridge.
