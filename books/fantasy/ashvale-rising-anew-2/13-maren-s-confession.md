## 13. Maren's Confession

I have buried three husbands, two harvests, and one daughter, and I will tell you honestly: none of those funerals cost me as much as the walk from my house to Freya Reyes's door.

It was raining, which I took as a small mercy. Rain means people stay inside. Rain means smoke and supper and no one on the lane to watch their elder go make a confession she'd spent sixty years hoping to die without making. I brought a jar of plum preserves as if that were currency that could buy anything here. Foolishness. You don't bring preserves to a woman you're about to tell that you helped hunt her.

Though Freya is not hunted yet. Only watched. There is a difference, and the difference has a name, and the name is *not yet*, and I have lived my whole life in the narrow country of *not yet*.

I knocked. Freya answered with a knife still in her hand from cutting bread, which tells you everything about how the last fortnight has gone for her. Behind her, at the table, sat the child — Wren — bent over pages of that old tongue, copying letters like a monk too young to have taken orders.

"Maren," Freya said. Not *elder*. Just my name, flat as a dropped stone.

"I need to talk to you," I said. "Both of you, though mostly you. The child can stay. It concerns the child."

"It concerns everyone in this valley," Freya said, "if it's what I think it is."

"Then we'll get along fine, because I've been rehearsing what it is for forty years and I've never once gotten the words right."

She let me in. She put the knife down, but near her hand, and I respected her for it. A mercenary who stops being careful the moment someone brings jam is a mercenary who doesn't last.

* * *

Let me start where all the honest stories in this valley start, which is to say: with a lie.

You know me as Maren Corvane, elected elder, keeper of the ledgers, arbiter of fence disputes and well rights and who owes whom a boar for trampling whose turnips. What you do not know — what only nine people in this valley's history have known, each of them elders before me, each of them sworn the way I was sworn — is what the ledger line under the ledger line says.

There is a second book. There has always been a second book.

My grandmother showed it to me when I was seventeen, the same year she taught me which herbs soothe and which ones kill, in case I ever needed to know both for the same reason. It sits behind a loose stone in my hearth. It is not enchanted. It does not glow. It is a plain book with a plain cover and it contains, in the handwriting of every elder back to the founding, a list.

Names. Dates of arrival. Dates of departure. Descriptions — height, coloring, the road they claimed they came in on, whether they asked questions about the shrine.

Travelers' names, Freya. Every traveler who has ever passed through Ashvale and then moved on, written down and sent south in a merchant's cart once a season, delivered to the Order of the Unbroken Vow, so that the Order would always know exactly who was here and exactly who was not.

Do you understand what that bought us? Because I did not, at seventeen, and I argued about it until my grandmother lost her patience with me, which she did rarely and memorably.

The valley is invisible. That is what we are taught, that is what we sing at the founding festival — *the god's hand over the vale, no magic in, no magic out.* And it is even true, in its way. Magic cannot enter. But the Order cannot be kept out by stones and hymns; the Order walks in through the front gate wearing white and asking polite questions, and the only thing that has ever kept them from walking all the way in was their certainty that there was nothing here worth their attention.

Certainty requires maintenance. That is what my grandmother told me. *The Order overlooks us, child, because we give them something better than ignorance. We give them records. We give them the illusion of seeing us clearly. A watched thing is a safe thing. Nobody burns the village they already have a file on.*

So every season, the names went south. And every season, the Order checked them against whatever lists they keep — and I know they keep lists, I saw the room once, when I was young and foolish and went to deliver the book myself instead of trusting a courier — and every season, Ashvale stayed overlooked, because a valley that cooperates is a valley that has already been searched.

We fed them travelers, Freya. Not literally — I want that said plainly, I never once handed a living person over, I never opened our gate to a white cloak and pointed. But I wrote down the name of every peddler, every pilgrim, every runaway, every young man walking away from a war or a marriage, and I sent those names to an order that collects people, and I did not ask what happened after the names arrived, because asking would have made me a participant instead of a clerk, and I very much needed to remain a clerk.

Forty years. Do you know how many names are in that book? Neither do I. I stopped counting at three hundred because counting felt like a sin and arithmetic felt like absolution.

* * *

Wren had stopped writing. The child was watching me with those eyes that see too much for their age, and I found I could not look at them while I said the rest, so I looked at Freya instead, which was hardly easier.

"There's more," I said. "Of course there's more. Confessions come in barrels, not cups."

"The girl," Freya said quietly. "Or the boy. Whatever Wren prefers. Is going to hear this either way, so say it straight."

"The child," I corrected myself, and Wren nodded once, and I loved them a little for granting me that much mercy.

"When the Order came to the ridge last week," I said, "asking after a missing child, they did not come because of rumor. They came because of the book."

Freya went very still. Stillness is a language mercenaries speak fluently, and I watched her translate my sentence into all of its worst meanings before she let me continue.

"Your grandmother's book?" she said. "It listed Wren?"

"Wren was never in the book. Wren never passed our gate. No." I made myself keep going. "Three weeks before the child arrived, a tinker came through. Staying ahead of the autumn roads, he said. He slept one night in the common barn. He asked two questions about the shrine — only two, and idle-seeming, and I nearly didn't write him down at all. But the rule is the rule. Names go south. All names. Especially the ones who ask about the shrine."

"And?"

"And ten days later, a rider came back across the ford, not a merchant's courier but one of theirs, and he asked me a question I have been dreading for forty years without knowing its shape. He asked whether anyone had come through recently who *spoke strangely.*" My hands were shaking. I put them flat on the table, in front of witnesses, so they would stop. "And I said no. Which was true, at the time, as far as I knew. But I understood, standing there in the ford mud, that the tinker had not been a tinker. That the Order tests the gate the way you test a wall — press here, press there, listen for hollow spots. They were listening for the old tongue. They have been listening for years. And my book — my faithful, dutiful, damnable book — had just told them that Ashvale's gate was hollow in exactly one place."

Freya looked at Wren. Wren looked at the table.

"So when the riders came to the ridge," Freya said slowly, "they weren't searching blind."

"They came to check a trap they had already baited," I said. "They knew. Perhaps not that the child was here — perhaps not even precisely what they were listening for — but they knew this valley had gone quiet in a way that interested them. Twelve years you've been here, Freya. Twelve years of honest service, and your name is in that book too. Written in my hand. Sent south in my season's cart. The only reason you are not in a cell beneath their cloister is that your file reads clean, because I wrote it clean, because I chose — twelve years ago, without asking you — to lie in my own ledger to keep a dangerous stranger safe. Or to keep the valley safe. I genuinely no longer know which I thought I was doing."

"Why?" Freya's voice cracked on it, just slightly, which frightened me more than shouting would have. "Why lie for me? Why write anyone clean? If the system works — if the machine runs — why gum it for me and not for the others?"

And there it was. The question underneath all the other questions. I took a breath like a woman stepping off a ferry into deep water.

"Because of Elowen," I said.

My daughter's name. I have not said it aloud in this house — in any house — in nineteen years. Wren's head came up at the sound of it, the way children do when they hear grief spoken plainly, even in a word they don't know.

* * *

Elowen Corvane was born in the spring and died at fifteen, and between those two facts she was the quickest, stubbornest, most unbearably alive creature this valley ever produced, and I will fight anyone who says otherwise, including God, including the silent one especially.

She found the book when she was fourteen. Found it the way I had found it, behind the hearthstone, because Corvane children are apparently constitutionally incapable of leaving loose stones alone. And she read it — all of it, three hundred names in forty hands — and she came to me white-faced and asked the question I had never let myself ask.

*Mama, what happens to them?*

I gave her the answer my grandmother gave me. Cooperation is protection. Records are safety. A clerk is not a killer.

She did not accept it. Children never accept the answers that took their parents thirty years to build, because children haven't yet learned how heavy the alternative is to carry. She started following travelers. Walking them partway down the south road. Warning them, in her artless, obvious, fifteen-year-old way, not to give their right names at the gates of certain towns. She thought she was saving them one by one.

The Order noticed the pattern. Of course they did; noticing patterns is their entire trade. And one evening in autumn, a white-cloaked man came to my door, sat at my table, drank my tea, and explained to me with perfect courtesy that my daughter had interfered with the Order's work four times that anyone could prove, and that the Order's patience with Ashvale's arrangement was not infinite, and that the arrangement would continue — names flowing south, valley staying overlooked — or it would not, and if it did not, the Order would come into the valley and conduct its own search, and we all knew what a search would find beneath the shrine, and what would happen to a valley built on top of it.

He did not threaten Elowen directly. He never had to. He simply left, and three weeks later she drowned in the millrace, and the coroner — a decent man who knew which side his bread was buried under — called it misadventure, and I stood at the third funeral of my life understanding, with total clarity, two things at once.

That they had killed her. And that I would write the next season's names anyway.

Because that is the genius of the machinery, Freya. It doesn't need your agreement. It needs only your arithmetic. One child against three hundred souls. One funeral against a valley. Every year the scales tip the same way, and every year you put your grief on the light side of the balance and call it wisdom, and you sleep, because the alternative to sleeping is looking at what you are.

So yes. When you walked through my gate twelve years ago — scarred, armed, sworn to a dead god, exactly the kind of name that goes south and never comes back north — I looked at you, and I saw Elowen's ghost standing behind you with her hand out, and I lied. First lie in twenty years of ledgers. *No unusual arrivals, Captain.* Clean file. Safe file. And then I spent twelve years watching you patrol my valley and guard my people and never once asking myself whether I'd saved you or merely postponed you, because postponement is the only verb my whole life has been conjugated in.

* * *

The rain had stopped somewhere during the telling. Water dripped from the eaves in slow counts, and Wren had come around the table without my noticing and put one small hand on my arm, and I am not ashamed — no. I am entirely ashamed, but not of weeping. Weeping was the cheapest thing I did today.

"You're telling us this now," Freya said, when I could hear again, "because of the trial."

"I'm telling you this now because Varn offered me a bargain yesterday, at the parley line, with her scribe taking notes." I wiped my face with my sleeve, an elder's dignity being what it is. "She knows about the book. She has always known about the book. She told me that if the child is surrendered by moonrise tomorrow, Ashvale's cooperation will be *gratefully noted*, and the valley will be permitted to continue its existence under renewed — her word — *supervision*. And if it is not surrendered, she will inform the council, publicly, of what the book contains and what my family has done, and let you tear yourselves apart deciding what to do with me while her riders burn your fields."

"She'd burn them regardless," Freya said.

"Of course she'd burn them regardless. But she'd burn them *righteous.* That's the difference that breaks villages." I stood, because my knees wanted to and because I was finished sitting in the shape of my own confession. "So hear the last of it. I am not asking forgiveness. Forgiveness is a coin I stopped carrying nineteen years ago. I am telling you that the woman who voted for your exile did it because exile was the gentlest end of the list of things I could imagine the Order doing to you, and because I have spent my whole life choosing the gentlest end of terrible lists, and I am *tired*, Freya Reyes. I am tired of arithmetic. I am tired of a silence that eats daughters and calls itself peace."

Wren's hand was still on my arm. When the child spoke, it was half in the old tongue and half in ours, and the two languages braided together like they'd been waiting centuries to hold hands, and I understood enough: *The book. Bring the book.*

"The book is evidence," Freya said grimly. "Against her."

"The book is names," Wren said. "Names should go home."

And I looked at that child — a child hunted across half the world for the crime of carrying a god's leftover power, a child who had every reason to hate the machinery that had fed on travelers since before my grandmother's grandmother — asking to carry my dead weight for me.

"The book stays behind my hearthstone tonight," I said. "Tomorrow it goes to the council. All of it. Every name, every hand, mine first and deepest. Let them judge the whole rot at once instead of finding it one plank at a time."

Freya studied me for a long moment with those soldier's eyes that take the measure of a thing and don't flatter the measurement.

"For what it's worth," she said finally, "you're the second person this month to confess to me that the valley's safety was built on bones. At this rate I'll need a bigger shovel."

"Who was the first?"

"The god," Freya said. "Through a child. It's been a strange month."

I was almost at the door when the horn sounded — one short note from the southern ridge, then another, wrong in a way my ears knew before my mind caught up. Not the watch-horn. Not the storm-horn.

Fire signal.

Through Wren's window I could see it already: a line of orange blooming along the far wheat, patient and orderly, the way the Order does everything.

Varn had not waited for moonrise after all.
