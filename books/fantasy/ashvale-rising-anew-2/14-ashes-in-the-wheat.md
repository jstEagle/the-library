## 14. Ashes in the Wheat

The smoke woke me before the bells did.

I'd learned to read smoke the way other people read weather. Woodsmoke meant someone baking bread or mending a roof. Peat-smoke meant rain coming. But this was wheat—green wheat, still weeks from harvest—and there is no innocent reason on this earth for green wheat to burn.

I was out of bed with my boots half-laced and my sword belt buckled crooked, and by the time I hit the common square, Maren Corvane was already standing on the council steps with her arms folded like a woman who had been expecting bad news for sixty years and had finally stopped being disappointed when it didn't come.

"The ridge fields," she said, before I could ask. "East side. Three fires, spaced even."

Spaced even. That word choice did more damage than the fires themselves. Accidental fires are greedy and chaotic; they spread where the wind tells them. These were set by people who understood that a valley watches its own edges, and who wanted us to watch all of them at once.

"Varn," I said.

"Varn," Maren agreed. "Or her people acting in her name. Either way, it's a message." She turned to face the growing crowd, and her voice carried the way it always did—not loud, exactly, but dense, like a stone dropped into deep water. "The outer fields are lost. We don't fight fire with bodies; we fight it with distance. Everyone east of the mill stream gathers what you can carry and moves to the shrine quarter. Now."

The crowd moved the way crowds do—half obedience, half argument, everyone convinced their particular granary deserved special consideration. I found Dain at the well, filling buckets with the grim efficiency of a man who knew the buckets were ceremonial.

"Three fires," he said. "She's not trying to burn us out. She's trying to make us *feel* burnable."

"I know."

"And you're going to tell me you have a plan anyway."

"I'm going to tell you we get everyone behind the shrine walls before dark, and then I'm going to go have a conversation with Commander Varn about the difference between war and arson."

Dain handed me a bucket, which I took as commentary. "Freya. The last time you had a 'conversation' with an Order officer, two scouts went home in pieces and the whole village spent a week deciding whether to thank you or bury you."

"They didn't bury me."

"No," he said. "They voted. That's worse."

* * *

The east fields burned beautifully, which is the cruelest thing I can say about them.

Fire in wheat has a sound—I'd forgotten that, or maybe I'd never known it, because twelve years in a valley where nothing burns uninvited will soften a soldier's education. It sounds like applause. Like ten thousand small hands clapping, dry and eager, while the stalks go up in orange rows and the smoke stands up straight against the morning sky in columns so orderly they might have been planted.

We got most of the eastern households out. Old Sef's family lost their grain stores because Sef himself refused to leave until the third time his daughter-in-law physically pulled him through the door, and even then he stood in the cart shouting figures at nobody—the season's yield, the winter's need—as if arithmetic could argue with fire. Ayelet the weaver went back for her loom. Her loom. I dragged her out myself, and she wept into my shoulder the whole way to the mill stream, and I let her, because grief needs somewhere to go and better my shoulder than the burning field.

By midday the evacuation had found its rhythm: lines of people and carts flowing west, ash drifting down on them like grey snow, children carrying things no child should be responsible for—a hen, a bundle of letters, a grandfather's walking stick. Wren worked the lines.

I hadn't assigned the child to anything. Wren simply appeared at the stream crossing, hauling water skins nearly their own size, passing them hand to hand down the line, saying almost nothing—which, for Wren, was normal—and watching everything, which was also normal. There's a particular look a child gets when they're memorizing a disaster. I recognized it because I'd worn it myself once, a lifetime ago, in a town whose name I've since traded away for silence.

"Wren." I crouched to their height. "You've been at this three hours. Eat something."

"I'm fine."

"You're fine the way a bucket with a crack is fine. It holds until it doesn't." I pressed a heel of bread into their hands. "Eat. Then keep working if you must."

They ate. They kept working. Of course they did. Found family, I was learning, isn't something you declare—it's something you do at a stream crossing while the world burns behind you, one water skin at a time.

* * *

The second wave of fires came at dusk, and this time they were closer.

Two new blazes on the near slopes, close enough that the heat reached the shrine quarter as a dry breath on the back of the neck. And with the fires came the wind, or perhaps the Order had simply waited for the wind, because suddenly the smoke wasn't standing in polite columns anymore—it was rolling downhill toward us in a grey tide, stinging eyes, choking throats, turning the dusk the color of an old bruise.

That was when the Tam boy ran into the fire.

His name was Ollin Tam, eight years old, and I would later learn the whole stupid shape of it: his family's dog had bolted during the evacuation, and Ollin—who at eight years old believes love is a job with duties—went after it. He made it forty yards into the dry grass before the fire front shifted. The dog made it out. Ollin didn't, not entirely.

I heard the screaming before I understood it, and I was moving before I finished understanding, which is the only kind of courage worth having—the kind that skips the deliberation. The heat hit me like a wall with opinions. I got my cloak up over my head and drove into the smoke following the sound, and the world shrank to orange noise and the feel of grass grabbing at my boots like drowning men.

I found him curled around his own arm, his sleeve burning, his voice past screaming into that awful rhythmic gasp. I beat the flames out with my bare hands—my hands, which have done many things I regret, though never anything I regretted less than that—and hauled him up, and turned back the way I came, and discovered that the way I came no longer existed. Fire doesn't respect your exit strategy. It had closed behind me like a door.

So I did the arithmetic every soldier dreads: my speed, his weight, the width of the fire front, the odds. The odds were bad. I started running anyway, because the alternative to running was standing in a burning field doing arithmetic, and I have never once in my life respected arithmetic enough to die for it.

Then the fire bent.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left of that moment. The flames did not part dramatically. They *leaned*—away from us, both sides at once, like wheat under wind, like a crowd stepping back to let someone important pass. The heat lifted off my shoulders. The smoke thinned into a corridor of clean, impossible air, and through it I could see the stream bank, and Dain, and fifty faces turned toward us.

And Wren was standing at the edge of the field with their arms out and their mouth open, making a sound I had heard only once before—in the shrine, in fragments, in the dead tongue older than any kingdom. Except this time it wasn't fragments. It was a sentence. A long, furious, terrified sentence, spoken in a language that should have been buried with the god who spoke it, and the fire was *listening*.

I carried Ollin Tam out through that corridor of leaning flame, and the moment we crossed onto blackened ground, Wren's voice broke off and the child dropped to their knees like a puppet with cut strings.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, and I've replayed it slowly so many times that I no longer trust my memory of its order. But the bones of it are these:

Ollin's arm was burned badly. Badly enough that Maren, pushing through the crowd, took one look and said the word nobody says in front of the injured: *gangrene*, or the valley's equivalent, which is worse because it comes with instructions about knives. His mother was holding him. Half the village was holding its breath. And Wren crawled forward through the ash on hands and knees, still shaking, still grey-faced, and put one small scorched hand on the boy's bandaged arm and said—quietly, in plain valley speech—"Let me try. Please. Let me try."

Maren looked at me. I looked at Maren. Twelve years of law and custom and careful silence passed between us in about a second and a half.

"Let the child try," Maren said, and her voice sounded like a woman signing something she couldn't read.

Wren closed their eyes. Their lips moved without sound this time, and beneath my feet I felt it—that hum, the shrine-stone hum, except it wasn't coming from the shrine anymore. It was coming up out of the ground itself, faint as a heartbeat through a floor. Light moved under Ollin's skin like a fish under ice. The smell of char gave way to something green, something like crushed stems in spring.

And then Ollin Tam opened his eyes, sat up, and asked for supper.

The sound the village made is not a sound I can write down. It wasn't cheering, quite. It was closer to the noise a congregation makes when the bell rings after a long silence—relief and fear tangled together, awe wearing the mask of noise because awe alone is too heavy to hold. Ollin's mother collapsed over her son. Dain put his hand over his mouth. Old Sef, who had been shouting arithmetic at the fire all day, went silent for the first time in living memory.

And Wren swayed, and I caught them, and they were asleep before their head touched my shoulder—boneless, breathing, ordinary, a child again instead of whatever they had just been.

* * *

They woke at midnight in my rooms above the guardhouse, ravenous and confused, and I fed them bread and honey and answered their questions in the order they asked them.

"Did I do that?"

"You did that."

"I didn't mean to. I saw him and I just—" Wren's hands twisted in their lap. "It was like the words were already there, waiting, and I just... let them out. Is that bad? Freya, is that bad?"

I considered lying. I'd gotten recently good at it, God forgive me—or rather, God say nothing, which amounts to the same. But the child had pulled a boy out of a fire with a sentence in a dead tongue, and it seemed insulting to offer them anything less than the truth.

"It's dangerous," I said. "Not because of what you did. Because of who'll hear about it. There are people beyond that ridge who have spent lifetimes making sure power like yours stays buried. Tonight, half our village watched you do the one thing this valley was built to prevent." I held up a hand before the panic could land. "But here's the other half, Wren, and I need you to hear it. Before tonight, some of those villagers saw you as a stranger I was hiding. A problem. A secret with a face. Now they saw you run *toward* the fire. They saw you spend yourself empty to save a boy whose family barely knows yours." I tapped the table between us. "That's not a secret anymore. That's theirs. You're theirs. There's a difference between being hidden and being kept, and tonight you crossed it."

Wren chewed on that along with the bread. Then, quietly: "Ollin's dog came back. Did you see? He was crying about the dog and the dog came back."

"I saw."

"That's the best part," Wren said, with the fierce moral clarity of the very young, and fell asleep again sitting up, mid-chew, honey on their chin.

I sat there a long while, listening to the distant mutter of the watch fires and the nearer sound of a child breathing.

Here is what I didn't say, because they were asleep and because some truths need to ripen: what Wren did tonight changes the war before it starts. Varn came to burn wheat and break our nerve. Instead she gave us a miracle on our own doorstep, witnessed by half the valley, performed by a child saving one of our own. You cannot burn that out of people. Fear spreads faster than fire, but so does wonder, and wonder has deeper roots.

And here is what I said to no one at all, staring at the ceiling while Wren slept: *Ashvale, you silent bastard, I hope you felt that.* Whatever you buried, whatever you stole, whatever crime you sealed beneath our shrine—it just healed a little girl's brother's arm—no. A boy. Ollin Tam, eight years old, who loves a dog. Your buried hoard just chose a side, old god, and it wasn't yours.

Toward dawn, Dain knocked softly and let himself in. He looked at Wren, then at me, and his face wore the expression of a man who has finally accepted a truth he's been fighting for weeks.

"Maren's called the council," he said. "All nine. Dawn session." He paused. "Half of them want to talk about defense. The other half want to talk about surrender." Another pause, longer. "And there's a rider at the gate under a white banner. No escort, no weapons. Says Commander Varn requests parley at moonrise tomorrow." He met my eyes. "She also says—" his jaw tightened around the words like they tasted of ash— "*bring the vessel, or bring your graves.*"

Outside, the wheat fields smoked quietly under the stars, and somewhere beyond the ridge, the Order of the Unbroken Vow waited for our answer.
