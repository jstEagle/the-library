## 15. The Split

I have stood in a lot of rooms where men decided who lived and died. Battle councils, mostly, where the deciding was done at arm's length and the dying was done by other people. I thought I'd built up a tolerance.

The tithe barn had never held so many people. It usually held grain and the smell of grain and the occasional mouse with ambitions. Now it held two hundred souls of Ashvale, packed shoulder to shoulder in the lamplight, and the smell of smoke that had drifted in on everyone's clothes from the western fields, and it was making decisions about a nine-year-old girl.

I want to be fair to them. I've spent three days trying to be fair to them, and I'll probably spend thirty years more, because fairness is the only thing I have left of some of those people. They were frightened. Their wheat was ash. The Hollick farm was a black scar you could see from the shrine steps, and little Tam Hollick — the boy Wren healed, the one with the burn that closed like a closing eye — Tam's own father stood in that room and said the word *surrender* like it was a mercy.

But fairness doesn't stop it from breaking your heart. Fairness just means you can't hate them properly afterward, which is somehow worse.

* * *

It started, as these things do, with a reasonable man saying something reasonable.

"We cannot fight the Order." Bram Holtz had been a farmer his whole life and a sensible one, the kind who mended fences before they broke. He stood near the front with his hands open, palms out, the universal gesture of a man who wants you to know he isn't the villain here. "Commander Varn gave us terms. Hand over the child by tomorrow's dusk and the burning stops. No one else dies. That's not cowardice, Dain. That's arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," I said. "Right. Let's do some."

I'd taken the spot by the door without thinking about it. Old mercenary habit — keep the exit at your back, watch the crowd. Freya stood across the room near Maren, arms crossed, face like something carved for a shrine nobody prayed at anymore. They hadn't looked at me since we came in. I understood why. We'd argued that morning, quietly, in the way old friends argue when they're both right and both wrong, and neither of us had won.

"Two hundred people in this valley," I said. "The Order sent maybe forty riders, and half of those are camp followers and horse-holders. Varn's soldiers are good, but they're stretched along a ridge line they don't know, burning fields instead of holding ground. If we—"

"If we what?" That was Sela Marsh, and her voice cracked down the middle of the sentence, which told me everything about where she was standing inside herself. Her son had been one of the harvest hands on the western fields. He'd come into the village that night with soot in his hair and his eyebrows half gone, and he'd sat outside this very barn for an hour before he could make himself come in. "If we fight? With what? Pitchforks? You were a soldier, Dain. Tell me honestly. Tell me the truth, since that's supposed to be your whole creed."

There it is. There's the knife you keep for yourself, Sela. I felt it go in and I didn't flinch, because she was right to use it.

"The truth," I said, "is that I've seen forty professional soldiers break and run from two hundred angry farmers with pitchforks. It happened at Redford Bridge, eleven years ago, and I was one of the forty, and I'm not proud of it. Numbers aren't nothing. Ground isn't nothing. And this valley—" I gestured at the walls around us, the beams, the whole stubborn bowl of Ashvale sitting in its hills like a cupped hand, "—this valley has been hiding from the world for longer than anyone remembers. It knows how to be overlooked. We could disappear. All of us. Wren with us."

"Disappear." Bram shook his head slowly, sadly, a man watching a child suggest fighting the river. "The Order already found us, Dain. After however many years of being invisible, they found us. Because of her." He didn't point at Wren. Nobody pointed at Wren. She wasn't there — Freya and I had insisted on that much, and Maren had backed it, and so Wren was sitting in Maren's kitchen with a cup of honeyed milk learning to play knucklebones with a woman old enough to be her great-grandmother, while two hundred adults discussed whether she was worth a wheat field. "They followed her here like wolves follow blood. And every day we keep her, we bleed a little more."

"She healed Tam," I said. "She pulled the fire out of a burned boy in front of half the west field crews. That's not blood on the wind, Bram. That's—"

"That's exactly what I mean!" His voice rose, and the room leaned toward him the way rooms do. "A miracle. In a valley built on having no miracles. You think the Order is the only danger? You think when the seal cracks all the way open, whatever comes through will care whose side we took?" He spread his hands again. "Varn offers us our fields back. Our peace back. One child, against everyone."

One child, against everyone. I've turned that phrase over so many times since that I've worn the edges smooth, and underneath I finally found what was always written there: *one child, against everyone*, as if Wren weren't part of the everyone. As if she were coin to spend rather than a person to keep.

Freya spoke then. First words all night.

"Her name is Wren."

Not loud. Just placed, the way you place a stone. The room went quiet enough that I heard the lamp flames working.

"You'll notice," Freya said, "that Commander Varn did not say *the child*. She said *what the child carries*. I've met that grammar before. Armies use it. It's how you get soldiers to do things to people — you stop saying names first." A pause. "I broke my oath publicly. I renounced the god in front of all of you, and the sky didn't fall, and I'd do it again before breakfast. But understand what I actually broke it *for*. Not for a shard. Not for power. For a kid who talks in her sleep in a dead language and laughs at my terrible cooking. If we give her to Varn, we are not buying peace. We are paying a ransom to people who have already shown us what they do to children, and the price will come due again. It always comes due again."

Murmuring. Some of it even agreeing. For a moment — I want to be honest, for a moment I thought we might hold.

Then Odell Bracken stood up.

* * *

You need to understand who Odell Bracken was, because I need you to understand why what he did cut the way it did.

Odell had fought beside me. Not beside Freya — beside *me*, back in the years before either of us found this valley, when I still carried a sword for coin and he carried one for slightly less coin. He'd pulled an arrow out of my shoulder at a place called Catter's Mill with his teeth gritted and a joke in his mouth, and I'd dragged him three miles on a cart bed when his leg got opened at the ford below Harrow Fen. When he came to Ashvale six years ago, vouched for, sworn in, given a cottage with a crooked chimney he complained about every single day with obvious love — when he came here, I thought: *there. That's the life we were swinging swords toward. He made it.*

He stood up now, and his leg — the Harrow Fen leg, the one that ached every winter — he stood with his weight on his stick, and he looked at me with eyes I recognized, and he said:

"Dain. Brother. I love you. You know I do." And the room knew it was true; you couldn't have faked the tired grief in it. "And I'm going to say what you don't have the standing to say, because you're too close to them. Freya broke their oath. Fine. Their choice, their soul. But you didn't. You're still sworn — truth above all, the whole road of it. So tell them the truth. Tell this room the truth. Can two hundred farmers, most of whom have never held anything sharper than a pruning hook, hold off the Order of the Unbroken Vow? Can we hide a beacon-child in a valley whose hiding is *breaking apart at the seams*? Look outside. The fields are burning. The shrine stones cracked last week. Whatever kept us safe our whole lives, Freya smashed it, and now we're being asked to pay for their conscience."

"I never asked anyone to pay for my conscience," Freya said quietly.

"No," Odell said. "You just handed us the bill."

Silence. The bad kind, the kind with weight.

And here is the thing I keep coming back to, the thing I'll carry: he wasn't wrong about any of the facts. Every fact he laid out was true. The fields were burning. The seal was failing. Two hundred farmers against the Order was a butcher's bill waiting to be tallied. He was wrong about only one thing, the one thing that mattered, the thing all the facts in the world can't fix: he'd stopped counting Wren as one of us. Somewhere between the first smoke and that night, in the long gray arithmetic of fear, she'd become *cargo*. And once a person becomes cargo, every argument about them becomes logistics.

"Then let me put it plainly," I said, "since plainness is my office tonight. If we hand her over, Varn keeps her word — for a while. The burning stops. And then the Order does what Orders do, which is consolidate. They'll garrison the ridge. They'll want to inspect the shrine. They'll find the crypt eventually, with or without our help, and they'll dig up what the god buried, and they'll decide what the world gets to do with it. And we will have taught them the lesson that Ashvale folds. That's the trade. Not peace for a child. Peace *now* for servitude *later*, and a debt our grandchildren inherit."

"And if we refuse?" Sela again, but softer now, almost pleading. "Dain. My boy's eyebrows haven't grown back. What do I tell him when the next field goes? That we chose a stranger over his supper?"

"He's not a stranger," I said. "He healed your neighbor's son. He helped Marta with the herb beds. He—"

"He's been here a season!"

"So had I, once. So had Freya. So had every soul in this room who wasn't born under these hills." I heard my own voice doing the thing voices do when the heart starts driving the mouth — rising, cracking. I made myself stop. Made myself stand still. "Wren is ours. That's the whole argument. Everything else is details. Either this valley means what it says when it takes people in, or it's just a wall with better manners than most."

Bram sighed, the deep sigh of a reasonable man carrying unreasonable weight. "Put it to the room," he said. "That's all any of us can do. Put it to the room."

Maren Corvane, presiding from the front bench with her ledger closed in front of her — she'd refused to open it, I noticed, refused the theater of reading names — lifted her hand. "Those for surrendering the child to the Order's custody, stand."

They stood.

I counted. I couldn't help counting; counting is what I do instead of screaming. Ninety-one. Ninety-one people I knew by name. People whose roofs I'd patched, whose children I'd taught to fish the mill pond, whose funerals I'd dug — because in a valley this size, the mercenary ends up digging, it's the one job nobody else wants and I never minded. Ninety-one of them standing up in lamplight to give away a girl who liked honeyed milk and cheated shamelessly at knucklebones.

"Those against."

We stood. Fewer. Far fewer. Freya beside me, straight-backed, staring at nothing. Maren herself, which cost her something visible. Maybe sixty. Sixty-odd, against ninety-one, with the rest sitting on their hands because sitting on your hands lets you feel like you chose nothing.

The room had spoken. The room had chosen fear, and called it survival, and honestly — call it what it was, but don't pretend it wasn't also human. I've been afraid in ways that would have made me stand up in that barn too, if I hadn't already spent my fear on worse tables in worse rooms. That's the only advantage I had over them. Not virtue. Scar tissue.

* * *

Here's what I didn't see that night, because I was too busy standing in a barn losing an argument to watch the doors.

Three of the ninety-one left early. Quietly, separately, the way you leave when you don't want to be followed. Odell Bracken was one. The other two — I'll not write their names here, not even now, not even for honesty's sake, because some debts I decline to collect — the other two were men I'd drunk with, worked with, trusted with my back in the years when my back needed trusting to somebody.

They walked out of the tithe barn and down through the dark lanes, past the black edge of the western fields where the smoke still hung like a curtain someone forgot to take down, and they climbed the ridge road in the cold, and they knocked at the Order's picket line with their hands raised and their faces pale.

I know what they said, because Varn wanted us to know. That was the cruelty of it — or the efficiency, which with her may be the same thing. By morning, pinned to the shrine door with an iron nail, neat as a market notice, was a map. Drawn in a careful soldier's hand, annotated in a careful soldier's script, but the knowledge in it was ours. The crypt entrance beneath the shrine. The outer gate and its counterweight mechanism. The corridor dimensions. The inner seal, marked with a small, patient circle, like a target drawn around a heart.

Underneath, in Varn's own writing, five words:

*Thank you. Dusk tomorrow. Bring her.*

I stood in front of that map for a long time while the village gathered behind me and read it over my shoulder, and I listened to the sound ninety-one people make when they realize what their fear has purchased. It's not a loud sound. It's barely a sound at all. It's the sound of a breath drawn in and not let out.

Odell, if you ever read this — and I hope you don't, and I hope you do — I want you to know I'm not writing this to damn you. I'm writing it because someone has to record how it happens. How good people, tired people, people who have pulled arrows out of their friends' shoulders, walk out of a warm barn on a cold night and sell a child's hiding place for the promise of quiet. It doesn't happen with villains. It happens with arithmetic. It happens one reasonable sentence at a time, until the reasonable sentences add up to a map nailed to a shrine door.

Freya read it twice. Then they looked at me, and for the first time since the argument that morning, since the barn, since the vote, they didn't look alone, and I didn't feel alone, and I understood that this — not the valley, not the shrine, not the god or the absence of the god — this was the actual thing we'd all been defending without knowing its name.

"They'll come at dusk," Freya said. "Through the crypt. Under us." A pause, and then, dry as old wheat, because even at the end of the world Freya could find the joke: "Well. At least they saved us the trouble of wondering where."

Behind us, Maren Corvane closed her eyes, opened her ledger at last, and began to write.

And down beneath our feet, under the cracked stones of the shrine, in the dark where a hoarded god's stolen divinity waited like a banked fire, something that had been silent for a thousand years seemed — though I'd swear no one else felt it, and maybe I imagined it, and maybe I didn't — to lean in and listen.
