## 16. Vows in the Dark

The crypt beneath the shrine smelled of cold stone and old promises, which is to say it smelled like every church I'd ever looted, except this one I was defending.

We had four lamps, three swords, one child, and a corridor. The corridor, at least, was good. Twenty paces long, barely wide enough for two abreast, ending at the inner seal—a door of black stone carved with the god's true name in letters worn shallow by centuries of deliberate forgetting. If you had told me a month ago that I would die for a door, I'd have asked which door, because mercenary work has standards. But this door held back everything Ashvale had stolen from the world, and Wren stood beside it with a shard of that theft glowing faintly under their skin like a coal wrapped in paper.

"Tell me again why we're down here," Dain said. He was leaning on his spear the way he leaned on everything—like it might betray him too.

"Because the Order knows about the lower gate."

"Because *someone* gave them the lower gate," he said, and didn't look at me when he said it, and didn't have to. Half the village had walked out at dusk. Not to fight—to *surrender*. To hand over a map drawn by hands I'd drunk with, eaten with, pulled out of the river flood two springs back. Old comrades. The phrase sat in my chest like a swallowed stone, and I recognized it, because I'd swallowed its twin the day I first lied to Maren Corvane. Apparently guilt came in sets.

"They think they're saving us," I said. "That's the worst part. They'll burn the valley to the ground and call it mercy, and they'll mean it."

"And us?"

"We're the complication." I checked my sword's edge by lamplight. "Complications get removed."

Wren tugged my sleeve. In the lamplight their face was all shadows and eyes, and they signed as they spoke, old habits dying hard: *You said we'd run if it got bad.*

"I lied," I said. "I've gotten bad at it lately."

*That's not funny.*

"No," I agreed. "It's really not."

They were eleven years old and carrying a god's stolen heart under their ribs, and they had learned to read my face well enough to know when I was lying badly. Found family, Maren had called it once, before the split, before the burning, back when the phrase still sounded warm instead of like a siege position. I put my hand on Wren's head anyway, because some gestures don't need words in any language.

Above us, muffled through forty feet of stone, something boomed.

* * *

The lower gate had been built to keep people out. It had not been built by optimists.

The first boom was iron against stone. The second was iron *through* stone. Dust sifted from the ceiling in fine gray threads, catching the lamplight like snow falling upward in reverse, and Tam—the cooper's son, seventeen, who had stayed when his own father walked—gripped his pitchfork hard enough to whiten his knuckles and asked, very quietly, "Is that normal?"

"For a siege? Yes. For a Tuesday in Ashvale? No."

There were six of us who stayed. Six, out of two hundred souls. Freya Reyes, oathbreaker, heretic, and apparently a recruiter of staggering incompetence. Beside me: Dain, who had voted to keep me and hated half the village for making him mean it. Tam with his pitchfork. The widow Sef, who had buried two husbands and one son and had informed the dissenters, in front of everyone, that she had nothing left for them to threaten her with, so they could move along. Young Brannic, who couldn't hold a shield straight but could hold one. And Wren, who should never have been anywhere near this corridor, and who was here anyway, because the shard answered to no one's better judgment.

The gate groaned. Somewhere beyond it, Commander Ilse Varn's voice carried with terrible clarity—not shouting, just pitched to carry, the way a reasonable person speaks to unreasonable people.

"Freya Reyes. You are harboring an object of divine provenance. Surrender it and the child, and the Order withdraws. No further fields burned. No further names taken. This is the last offer made in good faith."

"She keeps saying good faith," Dain muttered. "Like it's a coin she expects to be accepted at face value."

"It usually is," I said. "That's what makes her dangerous. She actually believes it."

The gate came down on the third blow.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about standing in a dark corridor waiting for soldiers: time goes strange. The dust kept falling. My heartbeat got loud enough to seem rude. And my mind, being a coward with excellent recall, went somewhere I hadn't invited it.

It went to the morning rite. Twelve years of mornings, kneeling at the shrine, speaking the opening words of the oath into a silence that never once answered. *By the quiet god, by the hallowed hush, I bind my tongue to your will.* I had thought the silence was holiness. Restraint. A god so vast it had moved past the need for reassurance. I had built a whole life on that interpretation, the way you build a house on ground you've never dug beneath—and then I'd dug, and found the house was a lid.

But something else surfaced with the memory, something I'd never looked at closely because looking closely at your own devotion is like staring into the sun with your eyes open. The oath's wording. The true wording, the old-tongue phrasing I'd finally translated with Wren's help while the wheat burned. *Obedience unto silence, even unto atrocity.* I had read it as a command. *Be silent. Endure atrocity.* That's how the Order read it. That's how Varn read it, with her white cloak and her reasonable voice.

But the old tongue didn't work like ours. Wren had taught me that much, patiently, the way you teach someone who should already know. The old tongue bound *by grammar*. A vow wasn't a statement of intent—it was a mechanism. And a mechanism, unlike a god, could be turned around.

*Obedience unto silence* — or, read the other direction, the way water reads differently depending on which way the channel runs: *silence unto obedience*. Silence given *in order to* obey. Not silence imposed. Silence *chosen*, directed, aimed.

And the name. The god's true name, carved on the inner seal, the name that had power because it wasn't a label but a sentence—Ashvale's whole nature compressed into syllables. *The One Who Hoarded the Light While the World Burned.* I'd flinched the first time Wren spoke it aloud. It sounded like an accusation because it was one.

An accusation could also be an instruction.

"Freya." Dain's hand on my shoulder. "Whatever's happening behind your eyes, finish it fast. They're coming."

* * *

They came the way Varn did everything: precisely, without hatred, in good faith.

White cloaks first, torchlight behind them turning the corridor mouth into a furnace door. Shields locked. The narrowness that favored us favored them too—they only needed two across, same as us. Sef's spear took the first man in the throat before he'd finished raising his sword, and she did it with the bored efficiency of a woman who had outlived everyone she'd ever been gentle with. Tam screamed and stabbed at nothing. Brannic held. Dain was everywhere, spear moving like it had opinions, buying inches we immediately lost.

I fought the way I'd been trained, which is to say without thinking, which is to say exactly as the oath intended. *Obedience unto silence.* My body knew the liturgy of violence better than my mouth had ever known prayer. Cut, step, turn, cut. A soldier went down. Another. The corridor filled with the smell of blood and lamp oil and the copper tang of fear, and above it all, faintly, Wren began to sing.

Not sing. *Speak.* The old language, pouring out of them unbidden the way it always did when they were frightened, and the black stones of the inner seal began to hum in answer, low and rising, a note felt in the teeth more than the ears. The shard under Wren's skin brightened. The torches guttered. Every soldier in the corridor hesitated for half a heartbeat, because whatever language you speak, a child's voice resonating off ancient stone does something to the spine.

In that half-heartbeat, I understood.

The shard wasn't answering Wren's fear. It was answering the *words*. The seal was a vow—Ashvale's original vow, spoken in the god's own name, binding the hoarded divinity into the stone. And vows in the old tongue were mechanisms. Channels. Water reading differently depending on the cut of the bank.

Which meant the oath could be rewritten.

"Freya!" Dain's voice, raw. "Whatever you're going to do—"

"I know."

"—do it *now*."

* * *

Varn came through her own soldiers like weather.

She didn't run. She advanced, shield up, white cloak scorched at the hem from the burning fields, and her face wore the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she found distasteful but necessary. Her blade found Sef's spear and broke it; her shoulder found Sef, and the widow went down against the wall, breathing hard, alive, disarmed. Tam's pitchfork lasted four seconds. Brannic dragged him back toward the seal, both of them retreating, and suddenly the corridor held only Dain between Varn and us, and Varn between Dain and everything that mattered.

"Reyes." She stopped ten feet away. Courteous. Always courteous. "You've killed five of my sworn today. I can account for it—extremity, confusion, the corrupting proximity of the shard. Stand aside now and I will still write absolution into the record. You were misled. You were deceived by a godless elder and a stolen child. The Order forgives what it understands."

"You understand nothing," I said.

"I understand that divinity was *meant* to be held by worthy hands." For the first time, something moved behind her composure—grief, maybe, real grief, the kind that comes from believing. "The war burned the world because power lay loose in mortal grasp. Ashvale gathered it up to spare us. The silence is mercy. The seal is mercy. And you would crack it open over a *village*."

"The god hoarded it," I said. "While mortals burned. I've seen the crypt, Commander. There's no monster down there. There's just the theft."

Something flickered across her face—doubt, quickly executed, quietly buried. The Order was very good at burying things. So, it turned out, was Ashvale.

"Last time," she said. "Stand aside."

Dain didn't stand aside. Dain set his feet, leveled his spear, and grinned at her with blood on his teeth, the same grin he'd worn the day we met, hiring onto the same contract, two idiots who thought the work would make them rich. "Freya," he said, without looking back, "the words. Go."

"Dain—"

"*Go.*"

I went.

* * *

The inner seal rose above us, black stone drinking the lamplight, the god's true name carved in letters that hummed against my palm when I touched them. Wren pressed close beside me, shaking, still speaking in the old tongue, still feeding the stone syllables it remembered. Behind us, steel rang on steel, and then rang on something else, and Dain grunted the particular grunt of a man absorbing weight he cannot return.

I had spent twelve years speaking to a god who never answered. Now I understood: the god had never been listening. Only the *oath* had been. The words were the machinery; the silence was just the room the machine sat in. Which meant I didn't need a god's attention. I needed only the right words, in the right order, spoken by someone the oath recognized.

The oath recognized me. Twelve years of mornings. It knew my voice the way a lock knows its key—even a key being cut new.

I laid both palms flat on the black stone and began to speak.

Not the morning rite. Not the Order's catechism. I took the old vow and turned it, the way you turn a blade to catch light, and the old tongue helped me—it *wanted* to be turned, I realized, it had always wanted it, the grammar running backward as easily as forward, a channel waiting for someone to cut the bank. *Silence unto obedience* became *obedience unto silence* became, in my mouth, something neither had ever been: *witness unto keeping*. Not a god commanding silence. Not servants enforcing it. Just people, standing in a corridor, promising to hold what needed holding—and to give back what had been stolen.

The stone grew hot under my hands. The humming climbed. Light seeped up through the carved letters like dawn through a floorboard gap, gold and slow and unbearably gentle, and the shard under Wren's skin blazed in answer, and Wren gasped—not in pain, in *recognition*, the way you gasp when a stranger turns out to be family.

"What are you doing?" Varn's voice, cracked now, the arithmetic failing her. "Stop. *Stop*—"

Steel behind her. A wet sound. Dain's grunt again, longer, and then the ringing stopped altogether, and I did not turn around, because if I turned around the words would stop, and the words could not stop, they were the only thing in forty years of Ashvale's history that had ever moved toward morning instead of away from it.

*I witness,* I said in the dead god's living language. *I keep. What was hoarded, I return. What was silenced, I release. By the land, by the people present, by the name that was a crime—I rewrite the vow.*

The seal cracked like ice on a spring river.

Light took the corridor. It took the crypt, the shrine above it, the blackened fields, the ridge, and I felt it go through me and out of me and into the ground itself, into the roots and the wells and the sweet old stones of the valley, the stolen divinity pouring home at last after a thousand years of imprisonment in a god's greed. It left Wren gently, like a coat handed back. It left the stone dissolving. It left Varn on her knees with her certainty in ruins around her, and it left—

It left Dain.

He was sitting against the wall where the fighting had ended, spear across his lap, one hand pressed below his ribs, and he was looking at me with an expression of enormous, exasperated tenderness, the expression of a man who has been trying to tell you something for twenty years and has finally settled for showing you.

"Well," he said. "That's torn it."

"Dain—"

"Knew you had it in you." His grin was still there. It cost him more than it used to. "Rewriting oaths. Twelve years I watched you kneel to that silent bastard, and you—" He coughed, and the cough was wrong, and Sef was suddenly there with her hands pressed where his were, and Tam was shouting for someone, anyone, and the light was still settling into the earth like rain after drought, gentle, gentle, and none of it, none of it, was going where I needed it to go.

"Hold on," I told him. I took his face in my hands. "You hear me? You held the corridor. You held it. So hold on."

"Freya." He said my name the way he'd said it the day we met, laughing at the absurdity of it. "Do me a favor. Whatever vow you're writing next—" His breath caught. Held. "Leave room in it for fools."

The light in the crypt dimmed to a glow, then to embers, then to the ordinary dark of an ordinary place, and the silence that followed was not the god's silence, and it was not empty, and it was not kind.

Far above us, through the broken gate and the shattered shrine and the open sky of a valley whose protection had just ended, horns sounded beyond the ridge—one, then two, then many.

The world had felt the seal break.

And the world was coming to see what we had done.
