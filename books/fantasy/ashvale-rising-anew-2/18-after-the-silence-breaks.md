## 18. After the Silence Breaks

The first thing I noticed when I woke was that my mouth tasted like morning instead of lightning.

That sounds like a small thing. It wasn't. For as long as I could remember—and my memory began, as far as I can tell, somewhere in the middle of a burning field with soldiers in white walking through the smoke like they were strolling through a garden—there had been something else living behind my teeth. A hum. A pressure. Words that weren't mine pushing up my throat like water finding cracks in a wall. I used to press my hand over my mouth at night so it wouldn't leak out and wake anyone.

When I woke on the morning after the end of the world, the wall was quiet. The water had gone where water goes.

I lay there for a long moment, listening to the roof of Freya's house creak the way old roofs do when they're thinking, and I tested it carefully, the way you test a bruise. *Good morning,* I thought. Then, out loud, in the plain tongue of the valley—the one I'd been learning all these months, word by word, like picking stones out of a riverbed:

"Good morning."

It came out ordinary. Flat and human and slightly croaky, the way everyone's voice is before bread and water. No echo. No shimmer in the air. No old dead god-language rolling off my tongue like it owned the place.

I said it again, louder, just to be sure. "Good morning!"

From the next room, Freya's voice came back, rough with sleep and suspicion: "If you're talking to the furniture, it's not going to answer. It's seen how you treat chairs."

I laughed. That was new too. Not the laughing—I'd laughed before, mostly at Dain's terrible jokes, may his name be written large—but the ease of it. The way sound came out of me without asking permission from anything older than me.

Then I remembered Dain, and the laugh died in my chest like a moth against a window.

I got up anyway. That's the thing nobody tells you about surviving something. The sun comes up regardless. The kettle still needs filling. Your body insists on being a body—hungry, stiff, bruised along one whole side where the corridor collapsed—and grief has to fit itself around all that mundane business like a guest sleeping badly in a too-small bed.

* * *

The valley had changed overnight, and it hadn't changed at all, which is somehow stranger.

Freya walked me down to the shrine steps because Freya walked me everywhere now, a habit formed in terror and not yet informed that the terror had mostly passed. The shrine itself was a ruin—cracked stones, the great seal-sigil split down the middle like a loaf cut by someone angry—but around it, the world was embarrassingly lush.

"You see this?" Freya said, pointing at the herb garden behind Maren's house, where half the village had gathered and were standing around looking at plants the way you'd look at a cow that had started reciting poetry.

I saw it. The feverfew had grown a full hand-span overnight and was flowering out of season. The comfrey leaves were so green they looked wet. Old Sela the healer was kneeling in the dirt with her hands buried to the wrist in chamomile, and she was crying, which for Sela was roughly equivalent to another person running through the streets shouting.

"It's not magic," Sela kept saying, to no one and everyone. "It's just... more. Everything's just *more* of what it already was." She held up a sprig of yarrow. "This would have taken a month to flower. It took a night. And look—" she crushed a leaf between her fingers and pressed it to a scab on her forearm, an old one, weeks old, "—look what it does now."

The scab lifted away like a leaf in autumn. Underneath, the skin was pink and new.

A murmur went through the little crowd. Somebody dropped a basket. I felt every eye slide toward me sideways, quick and guilty, the way people look at the stove when they're wondering if the fire's gone out.

They were checking whether it was me. Whether I still did it.

"I don't," I said, before anyone asked. My voice cracked on the second word, and I hated that. "I don't feel it anymore. It's not—it didn't come *from* me anyway. I was just carrying it. Like a jug carries water. You don't thank the jug."

"The jug doesn't usually talk back," said Tam, the cooper's son, who was fourteen and had never once missed an opportunity.

"No," I agreed. "But then, most jugs haven't spent a year holding a piece of a god."

That landed wrong. I saw it land wrong—a flinch moving through the crowd like wind through wheat. People here had spent their whole lives being told there was no such thing as magic, and then their whole lives being quietly, structurally wrong about it, and now the wrongness was everywhere, growing out of the ground, sweetening the wells. They wanted it to be safe. They wanted it to be explainable. And I was standing in front of them as the least explainable thing in a valley suddenly full of miracles.

Maren Corvane appeared at the edge of the gathering, leaning on her stick, and did what Maren does, which is take control of a situation by simply refusing to acknowledge that it might resist. "Well," she announced, "if the medicine's stronger, we'll need bigger stores for winter, and if the wells run sweet, we'll want the east cistern scrubbed before anyone drinks from it and gets ideas about miracles. Tam, fetch your brothers. Wren—" and here her eyes found mine, and something in them was gentler than her voice, which is Maren's particular art, "—come with me. There's work for hands that know letters."

Work. She was giving me work. In Maren's language, that was adoption.

* * *

Here is what I have not said yet, because saying it makes it real: I spoke yesterday. Plainly. To everyone.

All those months, the old tongue came out of me only when I was frightened or dreaming or both, and everyone in the valley learned to hear it the way you learn to hear weather—as something that happens *to* you. But when the seal broke, when Freya spoke that new vow over the flooding light and the shard finally tore free of me like a splinter leaving a wound, I opened my mouth and what came out was *help*, in the common speech, cracked and sobbing and utterly, gloriously ordinary.

Freya says I've always spoken plainly, sometimes. That I chattered at them over translation work for months. But Freya is kind and also wrong. What I did before was performance—shaping borrowed words with care, like a deaf child learning to shape sounds. What I do now is just... talk. The words are *mine*. They arrive already belonging to me, and they leave without ceremony, and I keep catching myself mid-sentence, marveling, like a person who's been carrying buckets from a well their whole life discovering rain.

Yesterday I talked until my throat hurt. I told Freya things I'd never been able to tell anyone—not because they were secrets, but because I'd never had the tools. About the fire. About the white-cloaked soldiers and the way their vows sounded when they sang them, like knives being sharpened in unison. About the woman who carried me south, whose face I can almost see now that the shard isn't sitting on my memories like a stone on a page. About how alone I'd been inside my own head, a prisoner in a country whose language only I was sentenced to speak.

Freya listened to all of it with their chin on their fists and their eyes doing that thing where they go very still, which I've learned means *this matters, hold still, don't spook it.*

"And now?" Freya had asked, when I ran out. "How does it feel? Being empty?"

"Not empty," I said. "Unburdened. There's a difference. An empty jug is just waiting to be filled. An unburdened jug can be filled with whatever it wants."

"That's not how jugs work."

"It's how I work."

Freya had smiled at that—one of the real ones, rare as frost in summer—and ruffled my hair in the way that used to make me furious and now makes me feel like a door opening onto a warm room.

Found family, Dain called it once. He said it like a joke but he didn't mean it like one. *You and me and the mercenary with the god problem,* he said. *That's a family. Small, but the food's decent.* I would give a great deal to hear him say something terrible and funny right now. I would give a great deal for one more evening of him complaining about the ale.

Instead there's a marker going up by the shrine, and Maren has asked me to write his name on it, and I've been avoiding the task for two days because I only get to do it once.

* * *

It was late afternoon when the horns came.

I was at the well with Sela's granddaughter, Pia, who is six and has decided I am hers, hauling up water that smelled the way water is supposed to smell—clean and mineral and faintly of stone—when the sound reached us across the valley.

Not close. Far. Beyond the ridge, out in the flatlands where the world actually lived, the world we'd spent generations pretending didn't. Three long notes, then silence, then three more. The kind of signal that isn't meant for the people hearing it. The kind that's meant for other people far away, telling them something has been found.

Pia looked up at me with her bucket dripping. "What's that?"

"Hunters, maybe," I said, which was a lie, and I knew it was a lie even as it left my brand-new, precious, honest mouth, and I hated myself for it, because I understood now—after everything, after Maren's confession, after learning what her family had done to strangers for generations to buy this valley its safety—that lies like that are how it starts. Little kindnesses. Little protections. Little names fed to riders at the gate.

So I took it back. I knelt down so we were eye to eye.

"That's a horn from beyond the ridge," I said. "Someone out there noticed us. I don't know what they want yet. Probably nothing good. But the adults are going to figure it out, and you're going to help by not being scared, because being brave is just being scared where people can't tell."

"That's what Dain used to say," Pia said. "About the thunder."

"Yes," I said, and my throat closed up, and I let it, because I'm allowed to do that now. Grief used to get tangled in the old words and come out as prophecy. Now it just comes out as grief, which is somehow both worse and infinitely better.

By the time I reached the council house, the horns had stopped, and the whole village had arranged itself into the shape bad news makes: clusters of people, heads together, voices low, everyone glancing at the ridge line as if staring hard enough could push the horizon further away.

Freya stood on the steps with Maren, and they were not whispering. That was new too. Freya caught my eye and beckoned, and I climbed up beside them, and together we faced the crowd—which was strange, because a year ago I'd been a bundle of rags at the valley gate, and now I stood on the council steps like I belonged there, which apparently I do, which apparently I always was going to.

"They'll send envoys first," Freya said, in the flat parade-ground voice they use when they're choosing each word like a weapon. "Armies take seasons to move. Questions travel faster. When they come, we answer honestly. All of it. What Ashvale did, what the Order did, what we did. Every name, including ours."

"And if honesty brings soldiers instead of envoys?" someone called out. Renna the miller, whose brother had sided with the Order and left with them, and whose voice carried the weight of that.

"Then they'll know exactly who they're marching against," Freya said. "And why. And they'll have to live with knowing it. That's more than Ashvale ever gave anyone."

Nobody cheered. Nobody jeered either. They just stood there in the failing light, a valley full of people with sweet wells and strong herbs and no protection at all for the first time in living memory, deciding—collectively, visibly, the decision happening in real time on a hundred faces—whether to be afraid.

I stood between Freya and Maren and I realized my hands were shaking, and I realized I wasn't ashamed of it.

Because here's the truth I carried out of the crypt, the truth that cost Dain everything: the silence was never safety. It was just a smaller cage. And cages feel safe right up until you notice the bars.

The horns sounded again, farther off, answering each other across the darkening plains like a conversation we weren't invited to. The wider world had felt the seal break. It was waking up. It was turning toward us.

And tomorrow, Maren says, we start writing letters.

I'm the record-keeper now. Whatever we say to the kings and queens and orders and empires out there, it will pass through my hands first, written in my brand-new ordinary words, in the language of a valley that has decided to stop hiding.

I only hope the world reads as carefully as it listens.
