## 19. Judgment of the Nine

They rebuilt the council bench before they judged me. That's Ashvale for you—mourn first, carp second, judge third, and everyone brings their own nails.

I stood in the shrine yard on a gray morning that smelled of wet ash and turned earth, waiting while nine chairs were set in a half circle where the old council's had burned with everything else. The new seats were rough-cut pine, still bleeding sap. Someone had thought to sand them. Someone else hadn't thought to make mine.

Not that I'd have sat. Mercenaries stand when they're being sentenced. It's in the blood, somewhere between the sword calluses and the bad decisions.

The village gathered the way villages do—slowly, in clusters, with the deliberate casualness of people who absolutely intend to stare. I counted faces out of habit. Old Tam with his burn-scarred hands folded like he was praying, which he might have been; he prayed to anything that held still. The Hollis sisters standing shoulder to shoulder, one glaring at me, one not quite able to. Young Piet who'd carried water buckets until his palms split during the fires. And near the back, Wren, small and stubborn, sitting on the ground because the benches were full and no one was going to tell them to move twice.

Dain wasn't anywhere, which was everywhere.

Maren Corvane came last, as elders do. She looked older than she had a season ago—not weaker, just *carried*, the way a mule looks after a long haul. She'd confessed her family's whole rotten inheritance to the village two nights past: the names fed to the Order, the travelers traded for the valley's invisibility, generations of it. Some had wanted her head for it. Most, I think, had wanted to be angry at someone who wasn't themselves, and Maren had handed them the opportunity gift-wrapped.

She took the center chair and did not look away from me. Credit where due.

"Freya Reyes," she said, and her voice carried the way it always did, like something built for open air. "You are called to answer for the deaths of three riders of the Order of the Unbroken Vow, killed on valley ground. For the breaking of the seal that protected this valley for longer than any living memory. For the burning of the outer fields. For—" she paused, and I watched her decide whether to say it, "—for bringing a child into this valley knowing what followed."

"I brought nothing," I said. "Wren walked in on their own feet. Everything after that walked in behind them."

"Answer the charges or don't," said a voice from the crowd—Sera Hollis, the glaring one. "We all know how this ends anyway."

"We don't, actually," Maren said quietly. "That's rather the point."

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about trials: they're mostly theater with stakes. I've sat through a dozen mercenary tribunals, and they all run the same—the crime recited, the defense recited, the verdict decided over drinks the night before. Justice is a performance where the audience already knows the script.

This one didn't feel like that. This one felt like nine people in sap-bleeding chairs genuinely trying to figure out what we owed each other, and I found it more terrifying than any tribunal I'd ever faced, because there was no script. Nobody here had done this before. We were inventing it as we went, badly, in public.

The charges came one by one. Three Order riders dead—I gave them that plainly. I'd cut down two scouts on the ridge when they came for Wren, and a third had died in the fighting at the inner seal, though truthfully that death belonged as much to the collapsing crypt as to my blade. I described each killing without ornament. A mercenary's habit: you report action cleanly or your commander does it for you, worse.

"The seal," said Aldous Pike from the second chair, a thin man who kept accounts for the granary and had never once liked me. "You broke a protection that stood since before our grandmothers' grandmothers. Crops blackened. The wards failed. Every kingdom east of the ridge now knows exactly where we are. Explain to me how that isn't the single most destructive act in this valley's history."

"It might be," I agreed. "Ask me if I'd do it again."

"Would you?"

"Yes." I let that sit. "Aldous, the seal wasn't protection. It was a lid. Underneath it was a god's stolen power, hoarded while the ancient world burned, and the thing we were guarding all these years was the theft itself. The silence wasn't safety. It was the sound of us being lied to. I broke the lid. What grew back is ours now—not the god's, not the Order's. Ours. But I won't pretend the breaking cost nothing, because it cost Dain."

That landed the way I knew it would. Somewhere in the crowd someone exhaled sharply. Piet bowed his head.

"You loved him," Sera Hollis said, and it wasn't a question, and it wasn't kind, and it wasn't wrong.

"Twelve years," I said. "He held a corridor so a child could live and a lie could die. If the price of that was the wheat field and the quiet, then the wheat field and the quiet were never really ours to begin with. They were rented. The rent collector finally came."

"And the child?" Maren asked. "Did you weigh their life against the valley's?"

"No." I said it flat, because softening it would've been a lie. "I didn't weigh anything. You don't weigh a child against a harvest. Anyone who does has already lost something they'll never get back."

Silence, then. The bad kind, the kind with weight.

* * *

They sent me to wait under guard while the council deliberated—which meant they put me in the shrine's side room with an open door and Piet's younger brother leaning on the frame trying very hard to look like a soldier. I could have left anytime. We both knew it. The courtesy of the thing was almost funny.

Through the wall I heard the argument, because Ashvale builds thin walls and thick opinions.

Exile, first. Sera wanted it—banishment, my name struck from the rolls, let me take my chances with the kingdoms whose attention I'd drawn. "She made us visible," I heard her say. "Visibility is death for a place like this. Send her out and maybe the world follows *her* instead of us."

Then Aldous, dry as chaff: "And when the Order returns for the shard—or what's left of it—and finds the woman who killed their riders gone, do you imagine they'll simply leave? Exile doesn't unbreak things, Sera. It just moves the broken pieces around."

Then voices I couldn't name, overlapping, and under all of it one voice I knew too well, low and steady, cutting through: Maren's.

I pressed closer to the wall. Old habits.

"—punish her, yes," Maren was saying. "Punishment without repair is just cruelty wearing its good coat. What does exile repair? Nothing. It empties us of the only person here who knows the Order's methods, who speaks enough of the old tongue to read what's coming, who bled for this valley when half of us were still arguing about whether to bleed at all. You want to throw away our sword arm because the sword arm made a choice we were all too frightened to make ourselves?" A pause. "I fed names to the Order for thirty years. My mother before me, and hers before that. I know precisely what cowardice costs, because I paid it in installments. Freya paid all at once. There's a difference, and the difference matters."

Someone said something I didn't catch, and Maren answered, louder: "Accountability. Not banishment. She stays, she serves, she answers to this council for as long as it takes to rebuild what was broken. If she runs, then we'll know everything we need to know about her. But I don't think she'll run. Do you know why? Because running is easy, and everything I've seen that woman do has been hard."

I sat back down on the cot and laughed, once, quietly, so the guard wouldn't hear. Thirty years of feeding strangers to wolves, and Maren Corvane had learned to argue like a lawyer for mercy. People change. Sometimes they even change toward something.

* * *

They called me back at midday. The nine chairs were occupied again, and the crowd had grown, and Wren had moved closer to the front, arms crossed, wearing the particular expression of a child preparing to commit civil disobedience if the verdict went poorly. I gave them a small shake of the head. They ignored it, as is right and proper.

Maren stood.

"The judgment of the Nine," she said. "Hear it all before you answer any of it.

"On the deaths of the three riders: the council finds them justified in defense of a child under attack, but not without cost. Blood spilled on valley ground is valley debt. This will be recorded.

"On the breaking of the seal: the council finds the act irreversible, its consequences shared by all, and its motive—by every account, including those given under oath by witnesses present at the inner seal—to have been the saving of lives and the ending of a false worship. The council does not condone the manner. The council does not condemn the aim. Both things are true. We are learning to hold two truths at once. It appears to be the price of honesty.

"Therefore: Freya Reyes is not exiled. Freya Reyes is bound—to this valley, in service, for a term of five years. Service to be determined by this council and reviewed yearly. Labor, teaching, defense, record-keeping, whatever the valley requires and the council assigns. Refusal means exile. Flight means exile. Completion of the term, faithfully served, restores full standing."

She looked at me across the yard, and for a moment the elder fell away and it was just Maren, tired and changed, saying the rest more quietly:

"We don't want your guilt, Freya. Guilt sits in a corner and rots. We want your work. Give us that, and stay."

I looked out at them—all of them, the glaring sister and the grieving one, the account-keeper, the bucket-carrier, the child with the crossed arms—and I thought about every road leading out of this valley, and how every one of them led somewhere worse than here. Not because here was safe anymore. Nowhere was safe. But because here, for the first time in my mercenary life, people were trying to build justice instead of just performing it, and I found I wanted to see how it turned out.

Also, someone had to teach the wardens which end of the sword to hold.

"I accept," I said. "The five years. The service. All of it." Then, because wry honesty is the only honesty I've got: "Though I reserve the right to complain about fence-mending assignments."

"Complaints may be submitted in writing," Aldous Pike said, deadpan, and to my lasting surprise, half the yard laughed.

It wasn't forgiveness. I want to be clear about that, because forgiveness would have been cheap and none of us could afford it. It was something better and harder: a contract. A community deciding that holding together was worth more than feeling righteous, that a person could be both the cause of your ruin and the best chance of your survival, and choosing—deliberately, awkwardly, with sap still bleeding from the benches—to keep them anyway.

Afterward, Wren appeared at my elbow the way they always did, silent as weather.

"Five years," they said.

"Five years."

"That's a long time."

"It's a blink," I said. "Ask anyone who's been dead."

They frowned at me—they hated when I joked about Dain, and loved that I joked about Dain, and hadn't yet worked out what to do with both feelings at once. Then they reached into their coat and produced a scrap of paper, folded into eighths, and pressed it into my hand.

"What's this?"

"My first record entry," Wren said. "As Maren's apprentice. It says: *On this day the Nine judged fairly, and the valley chose to keep its own.* In the new words." A pause. "I'm learning the new words better now. But I wrote your name in the old tongue too. So it would last."

I read it twice. My name, in a dead god's language, written by a living child's hand. Somewhere beyond the ridge, kingdoms were stirring, envoys packing their questions, armies maybe sharpening their arguments. The world was coming to knock, and this time we'd have to answer the door.

But that was tomorrow's problem. Today, the valley had chosen to hold together, and I had five years of work owed, and the first item on the ledger, Maren informed me before the crowd had even dispersed, was the envoy question—because a rider had been spotted on the south road at dawn, flying no colors anyone recognized, and moving slow, the way messengers move when they want to be seen coming.

"They'll reach the gate by nightfall," Maren said. "And Freya—you're the only one here who's ever spoken to the outside world without starting a war. Mostly."

I looked at the south road, empty and long, and felt the five years settle onto my shoulders like a well-fitted pack.

"Well," I said. "Let's go find out what the world wants."
