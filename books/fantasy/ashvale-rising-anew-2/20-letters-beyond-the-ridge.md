## 20. Letters Beyond the Ridge

The envoys arrived on a Tuesday, which felt insulting. If the world was going to come knocking after a year of silence broken and gods unmasked, I'd have preferred it come on a day with some drama to it—a storm, at least, or a festival, so the villagers could say afterward that they'd been dancing when history showed up. Instead it was gray and drizzling, the mud was ankle-deep in the gate yard, and Tam Ostell came running up from the watch post with his hood dripping and his voice cracking in a way that suggested he'd rehearsed this announcement three times on the walk and lost all of it.

"Riders," he said. "On the south road. Six of them. They've got—" he paused, visibly searching for the right word, "—*banners*."

"Banners," Maren repeated from beside me, dry as old parchment. She'd been elder for long enough now that surprise had mostly stopped being available to her. "How festive."

"They're not Order colors," Tam added quickly, because we'd all flinched at the same memory. White cloaks still made half the village go quiet mid-sentence. "Blue and gold. And one of them's carrying a staff with a—well, it looks like a little house on top of it."

"A reliquary pole," Maren said. "Diplomatic escort, then. Somebody sent actual diplomats." She looked at me, and there was something in her face that wasn't quite a smile but lived next door to one. "Congratulations, Freya. You're about to be interesting again."

"I've been working very hard to avoid that."

"And how's that going?"

I watched the six riders pick their way down the switchback road, unhurried, weapons slung rather than drawn. A year ago—gods, eleven months—I'd have read that formation as a threat assessment and started counting exits. Now I just counted banners. Progress, maybe. Or exhaustion wearing progress's clothes.

"They've felt it," I said. "Everyone's felt it. The seal breaking wasn't quiet. The whole valley lit up like a struck bell." I remembered the night of it: the power flooding out of Wren and into the soil, the shrine stones singing themselves apart, every warding charm within fifty miles waking up and screaming *here, here, something happened here.* We'd traded our invisibility for our integrity in the space of one spoken vow, and the world had heard the receipt printing.

Maren followed my gaze down the road. "Then let's go be honest at them," she said. "It's the only trick we have left."

* * *

They were, to my mild disappointment, not monsters.

The lead envoy was a woman somewhere past sixty, silver-braided, with the sun-darkened face of someone who'd ridden farther than any sensible person should. She introduced herself as Ambassador Selune Vair, envoy extraordinary of the Concordance of Three Rivers, and then she did something no one from outside had done in my lifetime: she took off her gloves before shaking hands.

"You'll understand the gesture," she said, watching my face. "We know what happened here. The Order told its version first—of course it did—but the Order's version has you as a heretic, the child as a stolen relic, and the valley as a wound that needs cauterizing. We thought we'd hear the rest before anyone started swinging."

"The Order told its version," Dain would have said, if Dain had been standing there. He wasn't. He was under a stone by the rebuilt shrine, and every conversation I had with strangers now had a hole in it shaped exactly like him. "And you believed them enough to ride three weeks through mountain passes."

"I believed them enough to worry," Vair said. "There's a difference. May we water our horses? It's been a long road, and I suspect the answers are longer."

I gave them the guesthouse—the good one, the one with the roof that didn't leak—and posted Tam and two of the new wardens at the door. Not as guards, exactly. As witnesses. There was a difference there too, and I was learning to live inside it.

* * *

That evening, the council met in the shrine—not the inner sanctum, which stayed closed except for remembrances, but the wide front hall where the stones had been mortared back together with more stubbornness than skill. The cracks were still visible. We'd voted to leave them visible. Some things you don't plaster over; you point at them instead.

Six envoys, four kingdoms' worth of credentials. The Concordance of Three Rivers. The Margrave of Callowmere, whose territory bordered ours on the east and who had apparently spent eleven months in a state of bureaucratic apoplexy over the fact that a divine event had occurred without filing the proper paperwork. Two delegates from the river temples, who kept touching the walls like they expected the stones to bite. And one man in plain gray who introduced himself only as "an observer" and whose boots said soldier no matter what his coat said clerk.

"They want an account," Maren summarized, after the courtesies had been exhausted. "A formal one. What Ashvale did, why the silence held for so long, what broke it, and what happens next. In writing. Signed."

"Signed by whom?" asked the gray observer, and everyone pretended not to notice that his question had the cadence of an interrogation.

"By me," I said.

Every head turned. I'd been expecting it. I'd spent the walk over composing the sentence, and I'd spent the last week composing everything behind it, because here was the thing nobody tells you about honesty: it's easy to commit to in principle and absolutely miserable in practice, because honesty isn't a single grand gesture. It's a document. It's dates and names and the parts where you were wrong. The Order had lied beautifully, in polished prose, with margins. The truth was going to look like a confession written by a tired mercenary with ink-stained fingers, because that's exactly what it would be.

"There are things in that account," I said, "that will not make us look good. That will not make *me* look good. Twelve years I served a god whose silence I never questioned, holding a seal I didn't know was a seal, guarding a prison I thought was a sanctuary. When I found the truth, I hid a child from my own council. I lied to Maren Corvane's face. I killed three men of the Order on ground that hadn't seen blood in living memory. If the account is honest, all of it goes in. The god's crime and mine, side by side."

Silence. The river temple delegates exchanged a look that theologians probably reserve for moments like this—the moment a layperson says something accidentally doctrinal.

"And why," said the gray observer, slowly, "would you include your own crimes?"

"Because the Order's account doesn't have any crimes in it at all," I said. "That's how you know it's a weapon and not a record. A story with no guilt in it is a story looking for someone else to carry the guilt. I'm done handing mine off, and I'm sure as the hells not letting them hand theirs onto a child."

Wren's name sat in the room with us, unsaid. Everyone knew whose account this really was. The envoys had come asking about a god; what they actually wanted to know was whether the child who'd carried the shard was a threat, a treasure, or a person. I intended to make the third answer so boringly obvious that even kingdoms would get it.

* * *

I wrote the first draft that night, in the guesthouse kitchen, because my own rooms felt too much like a cell and the kitchen at least smelled like bread. Wren sat across from me doing translation exercises—Maren had them copying out the old-tongue inscriptions in parallel columns, patient as stone—and occasionally looking up when my quill scratched hard enough to mean I'd hit something difficult.

"What part is that?" Wren asked, around midnight.

"The part where I explain the oath." I turned the page toward them. *Obedience unto silence, even unto atrocity.* "How do you tell a foreign ministry that you swore, in good faith, to serve a god who'd written a loophole into his own contract? How do you explain that none of us ever read the fine print because the fine print was in a dead language only the god spoke?"

Wren considered this with the grave seriousness they brought to everything, the seriousness of a child who'd learned early that words could be cages. "You write it the way you told it to me," they said. "In the crypt. You said, 'I thought I was keeping people safe, and I was keeping a secret.' Just put that down. Then put down that you were wrong."

Out of the mouths of babes, etcetera. I wrote it down.

The account took four days. Four days of Maren bringing me corrections in her precise elder's hand—dates, names, the full text of her family's confession appended as a separate document she insisted on signing herself. Four days of the river temple delegates drifting through the shrine taking rubbings of the inscriptions and getting into increasingly heated arguments about whether the buried divinity constituted a *theft*, a *hoard*, or a *wound*. Four days of the gray observer asking questions that were slightly too well-informed, until finally I cornered him by the well and asked whose army he actually served.

He smiled like a man caught doing something mildly embarrassing. "Callowmere's margrave keeps veterans on his diplomatic staff," he admitted. "For assessment purposes."

"Assessing what?"

"Whether this valley is worth invading, currently."

"Well?" I said. "What's the assessment?"

He looked past me, at the terraced fields going green, at the wardens drilling in the yard—clumsy, under-equipped, but present, which was more than most villages could field—at the shrine with its honest cracks and its stone marker bearing Dain's name in two scripts. He looked a long time.

"Inconclusive," he said. "Which is the best result you're going to get from anyone this year."

* * *

The fifth morning, we read the finished account aloud in the shrine hall, to the council and the envoys together, because Maren argued—and I agreed—that a thing spoken in front of witnesses can't be quietly edited later. It took the better part of two hours. I won't reproduce it here; the fair copy lives in the shrine archive now, and copies went out with each delegation, and I'm told at least one of them is already being argued over in a scriptorium in Three Rivers, which is either fame or infamy depending on the decade.

But I'll tell you the ending, because I wrote the ending last and meant it most.

*I do not ask the world's forgiveness,* it finished. *Ashvale stole from the world for a thousand years—the truth, the magic, the deaths the hoarded power might have prevented. I served that theft willingly and called it devotion. What I ask is harder than forgiveness. I ask to be believed. Everything in this account is true, including the parts that shame us, especially those. Judge us by what we did with the truth once we had it. We opened our gates. We broke our own seal. We chose to be known. Whatever comes over that ridge next, it will find us standing in the open, and it will find us telling the truth, and if that is not enough—if the world prefers comfortable lies to uncomfortable neighbors—then at least we will be destroyed honestly, which is more than the god who made us ever managed.*

Vair listened to the whole reading with her eyes closed. When it ended, she sat in silence for a while, and then she said, "Do you know what the Order's account ends with?"

"No."

"'Restoration pending.'" Her mouth twisted. "Three words. No names, no dates, no crimes. Just a promise that everything will be put back the way it was." She looked around the cracked, mended, honest room. "I believe yours. For whatever an ambassador's belief is worth. Which, in the current climate, may be considerably less than an army's doubt."

"That's the trade," I said. "We weighed it. Safety against integrity. We picked integrity. Ask me again in ten years whether we were idiots."

"I'll put it in my report," she said, "and check back in nine."

* * *

They left on the eighth day, six riders and a box of copied documents, and the valley stood along the south road to watch them go—not because anyone ordered it, but because it seemed important, somehow, that the envoys see us all lined up in the drizzle, ordinary and armed-with-nothing-but-our-faces. Wren waved. Half the village waved back before catching themselves. The other half pretended they'd been stretching.

That evening, Maren found me on the shrine steps, where I'd developed a habit of sitting with the last light and doing nothing, which Dain used to mock and which I now understood completely.

"Word from the watch post," she said. "Tam's rotation spotted dust on the north road today. Not riders. Carts. A lot of carts."

"Traders?"

"At that distance, could be. Could also be the advance party of something that isn't traders." She lowered herself onto the step beside me with the groan of a woman whose knees had opinions. "Your account's been gone four days. Even fast riders can't have reached Three Rivers yet. So whoever's coming didn't come because of the letters."

"No," I agreed. "They came because of the bell. The whole world heard the bell." I turned my cup in my hands. Eleven months ago I'd have been reaching for my sword. Now I reached for the list of things that needed doing—warden rotations, grain stores, the message we'd need to send to Callowmere—and found, to my surprise, that the list steadied me more than the sword ever had. "How many carts?"

"Maren-the-younger counted twenty. Maybe thirty."

Thirty carts. That wasn't an embassy. That wasn't even an invasion. That was something stranger—something that had decided, in the space between the seal breaking and now, that the valley where the god woke was worth traveling to. Pilgrims, maybe. Refugees. Looters. People with questions bigger than the ones we'd answered in writing.

"Send word to the wardens," I said. "Quiet readiness, no alarms. And wake the council." I looked north, where the road dropped over the ridge and out of sight, carrying toward us whatever the broken silence had summoned. "If the world's coming to ask what we did with its god, it can knock like everyone else."

Maren snorted. "And if they don't knock?"

"Then they'll meet the door," I said, "and the people behind it."

Below us, the valley lay green and open in the dusk, hiding nothing, guarded by everyone.
