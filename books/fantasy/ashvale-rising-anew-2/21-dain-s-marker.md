## 21. Dain's Marker

The stone arrived on a Tuesday, which felt wrong, because grief is supposed to arrive on days with weather to match.

It came up from the quarry in the back of Halvard's cart, wrapped in sacking like something shameful, and it sat in the yard of the rebuilt shrine for three days while the masons argued about whether the base should be square or octagonal. I listened to them argue through the open window of Maren's records room, where I was supposed to be copying the winter grain tallies and was instead drawing small pictures of Dain in the margins. He would have hated all of them. He'd have said my rendering of his beard made him look like a startled goat, and then he'd have laughed until he coughed, and then he'd have stolen one of my drawings anyway and kept it in his breast pocket where he kept everything that mattered, which was a very short list: his oath-token, a whetstone older than the valley, and, for the last year of his life, me.

I am getting ahead of myself. Maren says I do that when the writing hurts. She says good record-keepers approach the hard entries sideways, the way you approach a horse that has already thrown you once. So let me come at this sideways.

My name is Wren. I am eleven years old — or possibly twelve; the Order burned whatever papers might have said, and Freya and I settled on eleven-and-a-bit as an honest compromise. A year ago I could not speak. Not couldn't — wouldn't, mostly, except when I was frightened, and then the old tongue poured out of me like water finding cracks, words older than any kingdom, words that hummed in shrine stones and woke things that had been sleeping since before the valley had a name. The god Ashvale put those words in me, or left them in me, the way you leave a coal banked in ash. For a while I was the most dangerous thing in the valley. Then Freya spoke a new vow over me at the inner seal, and the power went out of me and into the land itself, and I became just a child again — a child who could finally say *bread* and *Dain* and *I'm scared* in plain words anyone could understand.

I have been trying ever since to decide if that trade was worth what it cost. I have not finished deciding. Maren says some entries never get closed, and that's what the ledgers are for.

* * *

The morning they raised the marker, frost sat on the shrine grass like a held breath. The shrine itself was only half-rebuilt — the east wall still open to the sky where the stones had cracked the night Freya broke the seal, the crack lines filled now with pale mortar that looked, in the early light, like scars on a face that hadn't decided whether to heal. Freya had argued for leaving the cracks visible. "We're not pretending nothing happened," they'd said at the council meeting, in that flat voice they use when they've already lost the argument in their head but are going to fight it out loud anyway. "The wall can be strong and honest at the same time." In the end they compromised: new stone, but every repaired seam left unpolished, so you could read the history of the building like lines on a palm.

Everyone came. That surprised me. I thought maybe half the village would show — the half that had voted to hand me over, the half whose map-giving had nearly gotten us all killed in the crypt. But grief is funny that way. It doesn't check your voting record at the door. Old Sela came, whose son Torv had been one of the dissenters, and she stood at the back with her arms folded like she was attending a trial. Torv himself didn't come. But Sela did, and when the masons tipped the stone upright and the ropes creaked and everyone flinched at the sound, Sela flinched too, and I saw her hand go to her mouth, and I understood that she wasn't there for Dain exactly. She was there because a boy had died holding a corridor so that other people could live, and even a heart that had chosen fear over family could not look at that stone and feel nothing.

Freya stood at the front in their mercenary coat, the one with the sigil of Ashvale cut off the shoulder. They'd cut it off themselves, the week after the battle, with a kitchen knife, badly, and sewed the wound shut with thread that didn't match. They stood the way Dain used to stand at attention, except Dain had always ruined it by shifting his weight every few minutes like standing still was a personal insult.

Maren spoke first, because Maren speaks first at everything now, having decided somewhere between her confession and the judgment of the nine that silence had done enough damage in this valley to last several lifetimes. Her voice carried without effort. She talked about service, about truth, about how Dain had been sworn to honesty above all things and had broken his own creed once — once — to protect a child, and had hated himself for it every day after, and had died without ever learning whether the lie had been forgiven by the god he no longer believed in.

"It doesn't matter," she said, and paused, and looked straight at me. "Because forgiveness was never his to need. He told the truth with his life. That's the whole entry."

That's the whole entry. I wrote it down later, word for word, because that's my job now, and because I suspected — correctly — that I would want it when my hands were steady enough to hold a pen.

Then it was my turn. Nobody had asked me to speak. Maren had asked me to *write*, which was different and worse. The plan was this: the stone would bear Dain's name twice. Once in the common script, cut deep by the masons, legible to every farmer and trader who passed. And once in the old tongue — the dead language of Ashvale, the language that had lived in my mouth before I knew it lived anywhere — carved shallow, almost hidden, along the base of the stone where only someone kneeling would find it.

"You kneel to grieve," Maren had explained. "So put the old words where the grieving happens."

I had spent four nights on the translation. This is the part I have not told anyone yet, so I will tell it here, where the record keeps its secrets better than I keep mine. The old tongue has no word that means simply *dead*. It has words for *returned to the ground*, for *finished speaking*, for *given back*. And it has one more word — *sennava* — which the shrine inscriptions used for the god's own fallen warriors, and which translates, as near as I can make it, as *kept speaking*. Kept speaking. As in: the voice stops in the air but continues in the stone, in the soil, in the people who heard it. As in: nothing that changed you truly goes quiet.

I checked that translation eleven times against the inscriptions. I cried through six of the checks. On the seventh, Freya found me in the records room at midnight, sat down across from me without a word, and pushed their own lamp closer to mine so we'd have more light. We worked the rest of the night side by side, not talking, and when I finally wrote *sennava* in the margin and underlined it twice, Freya looked at it for a long moment and said, "Yes. That's him," in a voice like a door closing gently, and left the room before I could see their face properly.

Some things you give people privacy for even when they don't ask.

* * *

At the raising, I knelt at the base of the stone with a chisel borrowed from the masons and a mallet I could barely lift with both hands, and I carved.

I will not pretend it was graceful. My first stroke went crooked. My second chipped the edge of the character for *kept*, and I had to start the whole line again, and behind me I heard two hundred people breathing, and none of them hurried me, and somehow that was worse than hurrying. Frost soaked through my knees. My fingers went numb around the mallet. Somewhere in the middle of it, my breath started coming out in the rhythm of the old tongue's cadence, because the language lives in my body now even when I'm not speaking it, the way a song lives in your hands after your mother dies.

And here is the strange thing, the thing I will enter into the record exactly as it happened because I promised Maren I would never embellish: as I carved, the stone hummed.

Not loudly. Not the way the shrine stones had hummed the first night I came through the gate, when the whole valley's buried magic had answered me like a struck bell. This was smaller. Warmer. Like a cat choosing to sit near you. The seal was broken; the god's hoarded power belonged to the land now, to the wheat and the wells and the sweet-running springs, and I had thought that meant the stones were just stones. But the marker drank the sound of the chisel and gave a little of it back, and the old characters seemed to settle into the granite the way water settles into a dry bed, and when I finished the last stroke of *sennava* and sat back on my heels, the letters were warm under my fingertips.

Nobody else noticed. Or if they did, they were kind enough to say nothing, and I have learned that the valley's kindnesses often disguise themselves as discretion.

Freya knelt beside me when it was done. They ran their thumb along the shallow carving — the hidden name, the secret name, the name that meant *kept speaking* — and then they pressed their forehead briefly against the cold stone above it, the way soldiers greet a fallen comrade's shield, and stayed there for the length of three breaths. When they straightened, their eyes were dry and terrible and calm.

"He'd have complained about the font," Freya said.

"He'd have complained about everything," I said.

"Yes." Freya's mouth did the thing that was almost a smile, the one I spent a year learning to read. "Good. Then he'll complain forever. That's the point of the stone."

* * *

Afterward, when the crowd had thinned to stragglers and the masons were packing their ropes, I stayed. I do that. Maren has stopped waiting up for me on marker-days; she leaves a candle in the records-room window instead, which is her way of saying *come home when the grief is done working*, because Maren understands that grief is not a feeling, it's a job, and jobs take as long as they take.

I sat with my back against the stone and told Dain about my day. This is our arrangement. I tell him about the grain tallies and the argument between Halvard and the well-wardens and the new apprentice girl, Pella, who copies her letters upside down and cries when corrected, and whom I have taken under my wing in the manner of someone who was recently under a wing herself. I tell him about the envoys' letters that arrived last week from beyond the ridge — kingdoms that felt the god wake, asking questions nobody in the valley knows how to answer yet. I tell him the parts I can't say to living people: that I miss him in specific places, the way you miss a tooth. The records room at midmorning, when he used to lean in the doorway and pretend he had business there. The training yard, where he taught me to fall properly, which is to say he threw me down forty times and called each one education.

The light was going gold and long across the frost when I heard footsteps on the shrine path, and I didn't turn, because I knew the weight of them. Freya came and sat down next to me against the stone, close enough that our shoulders touched, which is as much affection as Freya permits in public and considerably more than they permit anywhere else.

"Varn's banner was seen on the north road today," they said, without preamble, because Freya does not believe in preamble. "A scouting party. Three riders, white cloaks. Ilsabet watched them from the ridge and they turned back before dark."

My stomach did the thing it does. "They're coming back."

"They're always coming back. The question is what they're bringing." Freya picked up a frost-killed stem of wheat and turned it over in their fingers. "Commander Varn lost soldiers here. Lost her claim. Lost her god's little treasure box. People like Ilse Varn don't forgive losses. They audit them." They crushed the stem slowly. "The council meets tomorrow night. Maren wants the valley's answer ready before they arrive — the honest account, the whole truth of what Ashvale did, sealed and signed. She wants you to write it."

"Me?"

"You're the only one who reads the old tongue. You're the only one who was inside the crypt and can write about it without lying. And—" Freya paused, and I recognized the pause; it was the one they used when handing someone something heavy and pretending it was light. "—Dain believed the truth was worth dying for. I'd rather we proved it was worth living for. Cheaper, for one thing."

I looked up at the stone. The last sun caught the shallow carving at its base, and for a moment — and I know how this sounds, and I am writing it anyway, because the record does not flinch — the old characters seemed to hold the light a beat longer than the granite around them, warm as banked coals, like something kept speaking.

"All right," I said. "I'll write it."

"Good." Freya stood, and offered me a hand up, and I took it, and we walked back toward the village together through the blue cold, past the candle Maren had lit in the records-room window, burning steady against the coming dark.

Behind us, the stone stood in the frost with two names on it — one for everyone who passed, and one for whoever knelt — and I understood, finally, what Maren had been teaching me all along: that a record is not a memorial. A record is a promise that the voice continues. And somewhere beyond the ridge, in the dark, three white-cloaked riders were carrying news south to a woman who would soon want very much to know what we had written.

We would write it first. And we would write it true.
