## 22. Ashvale Rising Anew

A year ago, if you'd told me I'd spend my mornings teaching farm children how to hold a spear without falling over, I'd have laughed in your face and then checked your pockets for the knife you were surely hiding. A year is a long time. Long enough for wheat to grow where the Order's fires burned it. Long enough for a scar to stop aching when the weather turns. Long enough, apparently, for me to become someone who owns a whistle.

The whistle was Maren's idea. "You can't shout over thirty children, Freya," she said, with the particular patience of a woman who has buried three husbands and outlived every argument worth having. So now I stand in the east field at dawn, blowing a tin whistle like a demented goose, while the first wardens of Ashvale—ages nine to fourteen, plus Tam Osgar, who is nineteen and joined because he heard there would be snacks—attempt to form two lines.

"Again," I call.

They shuffle into something that is not quite a line but is no longer a mob. Progress. In my mercenary days we'd have called it a disaster. In my mercenary days, though, nobody had ever asked me to teach them anything except how to die slightly slower than the other fellow.

"Halna, point of the spear down. You're guarding wheat, not threatening it."

Halna, who is eleven and already taller than I am, which I resent, adjusts her grip. She's Corvane stock on her mother's side—Maren's grandniece—and she has the family talent for looking at you like she's already found the flaw in your argument. I like her enormously. This worries me.

* * *

The morning drills end when the bell rings from the shrine square—not the old cracked bell; we melted that one down along with most of the Order's abandoned armor and cast a new one, inscribed with nothing at all, which felt right. The new bell means trade wagons, and trade wagons mean the day's real work begins.

We trade openly now. That sentence still catches in my throat sometimes. For twelve years the valley's survival depended on being forgotten—on Maren's quiet lists of travelers' names passed down the ridge, on roads that went unmended on purpose, on a whole architecture of invisibility built by people too frightened to ask what they were protecting or why. Now there are mended roads, and a market square that didn't exist before, and a grain contract with the river towns signed in Wren's careful hand because Wren has the best script of anyone here, including the scribes who come asking.

The envoys still come, too. Fewer armies than I'd feared, more questions than I'd expected. Last month it was a delegation from the Salt Coast cities wanting to know whether the "Ashvale phenomenon"—their phrase, delivered with the delicacy of men handling something that might bite—would affect shipping. Before that, a scholar from the capital who wanted to see the shrine stones and left disappointed that they were just stones now. Before that, a woman in grey who asked very precise questions about the night the seal broke and stayed three days helping Dain's mother repair her roof, and whom I liked so much I nearly forgot to be suspicious, and then remembered just in time.

Vigilance without paranoia. That's what I tell the wardens. It sounds wise when I say it. Living it is harder—it turns out paranoia is comfortable, like an old coat. You know exactly where all the pockets are.

* * *

My shrine—if you can call it that—sits behind my house, where the woodshed used to be. It is a single stone, knee-high, uncarved. Beside it stands a board with names burned into the wood: Dain first, largest, because he earned it holding a corridor against armed men so that a child could live and a lie could die. Then the others—the villagers lost in the burning, the dissenters who died on the wrong side of their own fear, even two Order soldiers whose names we learned later and who deserved better masters. And at the bottom, smaller: Ashvale. Not because I forgive the god. Because somebody should remember that it existed, and that its silence cost us everything, and that silence is never neutral. Somebody always pays for it.

Wren's translations hang under a wax cloth beside the board. Pages of the old tongue rendered into plain speech—the oath as it was truly worded, the shrine inscriptions, fragments of the god's last recorded thoughts before it sealed itself away with its stolen hoard of divinity. Wren copies them fresh each season, because paper fades and memory fades faster, and because Wren says the work feels like "keeping the door open from this side." I've stopped pretending I understand everything Wren says. I've started trusting it anyway.

I visit the stone most evenings. I don't pray. I tried once, early on, and got about halfway through "if you're listening" before I heard myself and stopped, disgusted. What I do instead is report. Tally-style, mercenary bookkeeping applied to the dead: wardens trained, contracts kept, wells sweet, children fed. Dain, your corridor held. The valley stands. Nobody worships here. But somebody remembers, and somebody keeps watch, and I think that might be the only honest form of prayer left.

* * *

Autumn came in gold this year, the first harvest grown entirely under our own open sky. We held a festival—Maren insisted, on the grounds that a community that doesn't celebrate will eventually forget what it's defending. There was music, badly played. There was a pie contest that Halna won through what I can only describe as intimidation. Wren danced with everyone and spoke ordinary words all evening, laughing in plain Ashvale dialect, and I watched from the edge with a drink I wasn't drinking and thought: this is what we bought. This is what it cost. Worth it. Still worth it. Ask me again on a bad day and I might waver, but tonight—worth it.

It was at the festival that Halna told me she wants to lead her own patrol by spring. She's eleven. I told her wardens don't lead patrols until they can sleep outside in any weather and navigate home blindfolded. She took this as a challenge rather than a refusal, which is exactly what Maren would have done, and I made a mental note to start sleeping badly out of professional solidarity.

* * *

Now it is dusk on an ordinary evening in late autumn, and I am mending a fence post when the knock comes.

Not at the gate. At my door. Which is unusual—people who want Freya Reyes go to the shrine square or the warden yard. People who come to my door after dark want something else.

I set down the mallet. My hand does not go to the sword, because I have spent a year teaching myself that reflexes are choices, but I note where the sword is, the way you note where the well is in a dry country. Old habits, kept on a leash.

Through the window: a figure on the path, travel-stained, hood back, hands visible and empty. Deliberately empty. Someone who knows how to arrive at a stranger's door in a world that has learned to be careful.

I open the door without fear. That's the whole point of everything we built—that a knock at dusk is a question, not an attack. I open it and I look at the traveler, and the traveler looks at me, and for a moment neither of us speaks.

Then the traveler says, in accented but unmistakable Ashvale dialect:

"I'm looking for the one who broke the god."

And behind them, up the darkening road, I count—without hurrying, without panic—seven more figures walking down from the ridge, unhurried, in no livery I know, carrying staffs topped with something that glows faintly, softly, like embers deciding whether to wake.

Well. So much for an ordinary evening.

I step back from the doorway and hold it wide.

"You'd better come in," I say. "All of you. And someone put the kettle on—around here, we've learned that everything starts with talking."

Behind me, Wren appears in the lamplight, and goes very still, and says—involuntarily, softly, in the old tongue—a single word I have never heard before.

The travelers hear it. All eight of them smile at once.

I reach for my whistle.
