## 2. What the Valley Cannot Hold

The founding myth of Ashvale was the first thing every child in the valley learned, and Rafael had learned it twice: once from a wet-nurse's lullaby, sung soft over a cradle in the foundling ward, and once from a formal text they copied out at age nine, quill scratching through the long vowels of Old Ashvalan while Mother Ilsette stood at their shoulder like a shadow that had opinions.

*When our people fled the burning lands, they carried nothing but their names. And magic, which is a hunger and follows its people like a dog follows a butcher, came to the border of this valley and could not enter. For the founders set a lock upon the world, and the lock said: here, no power but the power of hands.*

That was the whole of it, more or less. Magic could not follow its people inside the wards. The valley was a room with the door shut against the storm. Everything else — the harvests, the peace, the long unbroken centuries of debt ledgers and tithe schedules and children born, indentured, and buried within sight of the same bell tower — everything else flowed from that one mercy.

Rafael had never questioned it. Questioning the founding myth in Ashvale was like questioning rain.

But myths, Rafael would come to understand, are like ledgers. They record what the tellers wanted remembered, and they omit — precisely, deliberately, in a fine clerical hand — everything else.

* * *

It began, as most of Rafael's troubles did, with an errand.

The autumn of their nineteenth year came down the mountainsides early, gold and rust spilling through the terraced orchards, and with it came the Festival of the Sealing, when the temple opened its reliquary vaults to the public for exactly one day. Rafael was sent down to the undercroft three days beforehand to prepare the inventory — dust the cases, check the seals, copy the manifest in their small, exact hand. Scribe's work. Invisible work. The kind of work that let a person move through the temple's oldest rooms unnoticed, which was, though Rafael would not have phrased it so boldly even in the privacy of their own skull, the closest thing to freedom their indenture allowed.

The undercroft lay beneath the eastern transept, down stairs worn into shallow bowls by four hundred years of feet. It was cold there in a way that had nothing to do with season — a cold like held breath, like a cellar where something waited to be needed. Oil lamps burned in iron brackets along the walls, and their light made the reliquaries glow: gilt boxes, sealed jars, a sword said to have belonged to a founder whose name changed depending on which priest told you.

Rafael worked through the morning, copying tags, noting discrepancies. One case refused to open.

It was a plain thing compared to its neighbors — dark wood, iron bands gone black with age, no gilt, no glass window. Its manifest entry read only: *Relic 44. Provenance: Founding. Do not display.* Every other relic had a story attached, a saint, a miracle. Relic 44 had a prohibition.

Rafael told themselves it was scribe's diligence that made them try the case again. Told themselves it was thoroughness, not the strange pull low in their chest, the way the air near the case felt *thicker*, the way the lamp flame leaned toward it very slightly, as if drawn by a draft that wasn't there.

The lock was old. Old locks yield to patience. Rafael worked a thin copying-knife into the seam the way they'd once worked open the pantry lock as a hungry child of eleven, heart hammering, and on the third attempt the hasp gave with a sound like a sigh.

Inside, on a bed of rotted velvet, lay a stone.

Not a beautiful stone. A lump of grey granite the size of a fist, cracked clean through one side, as if it had been broken off something larger. But when Rafael's fingers closed around it — and later they could never say why they touched it, why their hand moved before their mind caught up — the stone grew warm.

Then warmer.

Then it *sang*.

Not aloud. In the bones. A single sustained note that Rafael felt in their teeth, their sternum, the roots of their hair, a note like the moment before a bell is struck, like the silence inside a temple at midnight stretched taut until it hummed. The lamp flames all leaned toward Rafael at once. Somewhere above, faintly, through forty feet of stone, the temple bell trembled in its yoke without being rung.

And beneath Rafael's skin, in the veins of their wrists, something *answered*. A warmth rising to meet the stone's warmth, a recognition, the way a tuning fork answers its twin across a room. Rafael looked down at their own forearm and saw — or thought they saw, in the lamplight, in the singing dark — faint lines beneath the skin, pale as scar-tissue, tracing patterns up from the wrist like the veins of a leaf.

They dropped the stone.

It landed on the velvet without a sound. The note cut off. The lamps straightened. Rafael stood in the cold undercroft shaking, pressing their wrist against their chest, listening to their own heartbeat as if to confirm it still belonged to them.

"Interesting," said a voice behind them.

Mother Ilsette stood at the foot of the stairs.

She was past seventy, a small woman dried tough as jerky, with eyes the color of river ice and hands that had baptized half the valley and buried the other half. She had been old when Rafael arrived at the temple as a foundling of four; she seemed now the sort of old that might simply continue forever, like the mountain. She looked at Rafael. She looked at the open case. Her face did none of the things faces were supposed to do upon finding a scribe breaking into a forbidden reliquary.

"Mother Ilsette," Rafael managed. "I — the manifest was incomplete, I thought I should verify the contents, I—"

"Give me your hand."

Rafael gave it. There was no thought of refusing; her voice had the same authority as the bell. She turned Rafael's wrist toward the lamplight and studied the skin there for a long time. Whatever she saw — or didn't see — made her mouth press into a line.

"You touched it."

"Yes, Mother."

"With your bare hand."

"Yes, Mother."

She released the wrist. Then she picked up the stone herself, wrapped in a fold of her sleeve, and held it out toward Rafael — not offering it, holding it *toward* them, the way one holds a candle toward a dark corner to see what's there.

The stone stayed inert. Cold. Silent. A rock.

Ilsette watched Rafael's face while she did it, and whatever she was watching for, she found it. Something in her expression closed, the way a ledger closes — finality, a sum reached, a page turned before anyone can read it.

"Put it back," she said. "Seal the case. You saw nothing unusual. The manifest will read *contents verified, condition stable*."

"But I—"

"*You saw nothing unusual.*" Not loud. Just absolute, the way bedrock is absolute. "Go and finish the inventory, child. And Rafael—" she paused at the stair, small and straight-backed in the lamplight, "—come to my cell after evening bell. Bring nothing. Tell no one."

* * *

Rafael went, of course. Disobedience was not a muscle they'd ever been permitted to develop.

Mother Ilsette's cell was on the top floor of the bell tower, which everyone knew was because she was deaf-ish and liked the quiet, and which Rafael now suspected was because she liked being able to see the borders. The room was narrow as a coffin and twice as crowded: books stacked in leaning towers, dried herbs hanging from the rafters, a single narrow window looking east toward the pass where the wards ran — invisible, always invisible, marked only by waystones carved with the seal of the founders.

On the table between them sat a bowl of water, a knife, and a scrap of parchment covered in diagrams Rafael didn't recognize — circles within circles, lines radiating from a central point like the spokes of a wheel, and at the center, drawn small and precise, a shape like a key.

"Sit," Ilsette said.

Rafael sat.

"I'm going to ask you questions," the old priest said. "You will answer honestly, because lying to me is a waste of both our remaining years. When were you brought to the temple?"

"Nineteen years ago. I was four."

"Found at the gate?"

"At the gate. No note. The register says *foundling, sex unrecorded by finder, age approximated*."

"And before that? Do you remember anything? A mother's face? Another valley? A road?"

"Nothing." This was true, and had always been true, and Rafael had stopped grieving it somewhere around age twelve. "Sister Maren said some foundlings are like that. The ones who walk out of bad places. The mind shuts the door."

"The mind shuts the door," Ilsette repeated softly, and looked at the diagram on the table as if it had said something amusing. "Yes. Doors."

She took Rafael's hand again — hers dry and strong as tree bark — and turned it palm-up. With the knife she opened a shallow line across the pad of the thumb, quick and impersonal as a butcher. Rafael hissed. Blood welled, dark in the lamplight.

"Hold it over the bowl. Don't clench. Let it fall."

Three drops fell into the water.

For a moment, nothing. The blood bloomed and thinned, threads of red unwinding in the clear water, ordinary as anything.

Then the water began to move.

Not swirling — *organizing*. The threads of blood drew together into lines, fine and branching, spreading outward from the center of the bowl in patterns too regular to be accident: a lattice, a web, a geometry. And at the center, where the first drop had fallen, the blood gathered itself into a shape that Rafael recognized instantly, because they had just seen it inked on the parchment an arm's length away.

A key.

Small, precise, unmistakable, formed of their own blood floating in a bowl of water, turning slowly, patiently, as if waiting to be inserted into something.

The warmth rose in Rafael's chest again — that same answering hum, deeper now, and with it a vertigo, a sense of standing at the edge of a stair in the dark and finding one more step than expected. "What is it?" Their voice came out thin. "What does it mean?"

Ilsette said nothing.

She was looking at the bowl the way Rafael had once seen her look at a dying man — total attention, total grief, held perfectly still behind her eyes. The lamplight moved on the water. The key turned. And Mother Ilsette, who had answered every question Rafael had ever asked her across fifteen years — *why do we fast, why does the bell ring nine times, why can't magic cross the wards, Mother, what's actually out there* — said nothing at all.

"Mother. Please."

She emptied the bowl out the window. The water fell forty feet into the dark garden and the key went with it, and Rafael felt its going like a small death, like something being taken out of them.

"What does it *mean*?" Rafael was on their feet now, and their voice cracked on the word, and they hated it. "You've known. Haven't you. All this time. That's why you tested me — not today, today was just confirmation — you've *known* since I was—"

"Since you were six," Ilsette said quietly. "You drew the founder's seal in the margins of your practice letters. Perfectly. No one taught you. Children don't do that, Rafael. Children copy crooked suns and stick-figure goats. You copied a seal that had been locked in the undercroft vault for four hundred years, that you had never seen, that *no living soul had ever shown you*."

The tower creaked in the wind. Below, faintly, the evening bell began to ring — nine strokes, as it always did, marking the hour the wards were renewed, though no one alive performed any rite Rafael had ever witnessed. The bell rang itself, the priests said. Tradition. Mystery. Don't question it.

"Why won't you tell me?" Rafael whispered.

Ilsette lowered herself onto her stool with the care of a woman whose joints had opinions. For a long moment she studied her own folded hands.

"When you were a child," she said at last, "I asked myself the same question you're asking me now. *What does it mean.* I prayed about it. I consulted the archives. And I found, in the restricted folios — folios your rank will never touch, so hear me and remember — I found that the founding myth has a second half. An older half. Written before the wards were finished, when the founders still believed someone might someday need to know."

Rafael's heart was slamming. "What does it say?"

"It says that magic cannot follow its people into the valley." Ilsette raised her eyes, and they were terrible and tired and ancient. "It says nothing about what happens when the lock and the key are standing in the same room."

Outside, the ninth stroke of the bell faded. Somewhere below in the cloister, a novice laughed at something, ordinary and human, and the sound drifted up through the floor like a voice from another world.

"That's not an answer."

"No," Ilsette agreed. "It isn't. Because the full answer is written in a place I cannot take you, guarded by people who would kill us both for asking, and because you are nineteen years old and bound by indenture to this temple for eleven more years, and knowing what you are would make those eleven years unbearable." She rose, suddenly brisk, suddenly the old dry priest again, and pressed a stoppered vial into Rafael's hand — thick syrup, bitter-smelling. "For the thumb. Change the dressing daily. And Rafael—"

"Mother?"

"If the stone ever sings for you again — if anything in this valley ever *answers* you again — you come to me before you go to anyone else. Before the abbot. Before the archive. Before anyone." Her grip on Rafael's arm was shockingly strong. "*Before anyone.*"

Rafael walked back down the tower stairs with their thumb throbbing and the word *key* ringing in them like the struck bell overhead, and did not sleep that night, and did not speak of it, and kept the vial on their desk among the ink pots where anyone might see it and no one would know.

Eleven more years of ledgers. Eleven more years of copying other people's debts in a fair hand while their own blood wrote keys in water.

They almost managed to believe it would hold.

* * *

Autumn deepened. The Festival of the Sealing came and went, and Relic 44 remained in its case, undisplayed, its manifest reading *contents verified, condition stable* in Rafael's own obedient hand. Serah delivered dispatches twice that month — Rafael counted, though they pretended not to — and each time the ward-keeper stood at the scriptorium desk in her travel-leathers smelling of cold air and horse, and each time Rafael's eyes did the traitorous thing they always did, and each time Serah's gaze passed over them with exactly the weightless courtesy owed to furniture.

Except once.

The last delivery of the month, near dusk, Serah set the dispatch tube down and paused. Her eyes flicked — briefly, involuntarily, the way a person glances at a sound — to Rafael's left wrist, where the bandage showed beneath the sleeve. Then to Rafael's face. Something moved behind her eyes, some calculation begun and abandoned, and then she was gone, boots ringing on the flagstones, and Rafael stood in the fading light with their heart doing something complicated and unwelcome.

*She noticed. Ward-keepers notice everything. It's their trade.*

It meant nothing. It couldn't mean anything. Rafael was a scribe with an eleven-year indenture and a bandaged thumb and a secret shaped like a key, and Serah was the Temple's blade, sharp and distant and utterly beyond the reach of anything Rafael was permitted to want.

Rafael dipped the pen. Straightened the ledger. Began the night's column of small debts.

And far to the east, past the orchards and the waystones and the invisible line where the world's oldest lock held shut against the dark, the first star over Mount Anew flickered — once, softly, like a candle deciding whether to stay lit — and no one in the valley saw it at all.
