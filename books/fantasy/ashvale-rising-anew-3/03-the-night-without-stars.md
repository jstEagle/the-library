## 3. The Night Without Stars

Serah Vane had kept the border for eleven years, and in all that time the wards had never once surprised her.

They hummed. That was the first thing a ward-keeper learned — before the sigils, before the patrol rotations, before the long lessons in what to do with people who tried to cross. The wards hummed, low and constant, a sound felt more than heard, somewhere behind the teeth. Old keepers called it the Valley's Breath. New ones called it nothing at all, because you stopped noticing it inside a month, the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat.

Serah had never stopped noticing. That was her particular affliction, her mother had said. The girl who listens to the walls.

So when the humming changed — thinned, stuttered, caught like a breath held too long — she knew before she saw anything wrong at all. She reined her mare up on the ridge above the Salt Road crossing and sat very still in the dark, one hand flat against the mare's neck, feeling the animal's unease rise through her palm like heat off a stone.

The wards were flickering.

Not failing. Flickering. She could see it now that she looked: along the border stones that marched away northward into the black, faint veins of light ran between them, pale as frost on a windowpane, and those veins pulsed. Bright. Dim. Bright. Dim. Like something enormous breathing badly in its sleep.

"That's not possible," she said aloud, because saying things aloud made them smaller, and this thing needed to be small. "That is not—"

The stars went out.

All of them. At once. No cloud rolled across them, no mist rose from the river valley below; the sky simply closed like an eye. One heartbeat the heavens over Ashvale were salted with their familiar constellations — the Ploughwife, the Three Sisters, the Long Antler wheeling slow above the mountain — and the next there was nothing. A ceiling of unbroken black, so total it seemed less like absence of light than presence of its opposite, pressing down.

The mare screamed. Serah let her spin once, twice, then gathered the reins and forced her still with her knees and her voice, low and steady, the voice she used on frightened conscripts and frightened townsfolk and, once, on a ward-breach that had swallowed half a village's memories.

"Easy. Easy. I'm here. I'm here."

She was not sure she believed herself.

Her hand went to the dispatch case at her belt — sealed orders from the Temple, delivered only that morning by a scribe's runner, instructing her to hold the eastern stretch through the night watch and report anomalies. *Anomalies.* The word had seemed bureaucratic then. Now it seemed obscene. What word did they have for this? What word did anyone have?

She thought, absurdly, of the scribe who sometimes brought the dispatches himself. Rafael Vasquez. Quiet hands, ink-stained to the second knuckle, eyes that flicked up when they thought no one was watching — always toward the window of the scriptorium, always toward the sky, as if checking whether it was still where they'd left it. Serah had noticed. Noticing was her affliction. She had told herself the noticing meant nothing, the way she told herself many things.

*If the sky can go out,* she thought now, *then everything I told myself was nothing might be something.*

She turned the mare east, toward the temple, and rode into a valley without stars.

* * *

The silence was the worst part.

Ashvale at night was never truly quiet. Frogs sang in the irrigation channels. Dogs traded complaints across farmyards. Night birds called from the orchards, and somewhere, always, a wind moved through the high pines with a sound like distant surf. Serah had fallen asleep to that layered noise her whole life. It was the sound of a world running properly.

Now there was nothing. No frogs. No dogs. When she passed the hamlet at Millbrook the windows were dark and the doors stood shut and not even a shutter creaked, though the night was cold enough that every family should have been awake, every hearth should have been burning. She saw smoke rising from one chimney — but straight up, unnaturally straight, as if the air itself had forgotten how to move.

She did not stop. Stopping felt dangerous in a way she couldn't name, the way deep water feels dangerous to someone who has only ever seen it from shore. Something in the valley was holding its breath, and she had the terrible instinct that whatever came next would come for anything that exhaled.

At the ford below the temple hill, the river had gone glass-still. Her mare balked at the water's edge, nostrils flaring, and when Serah leaned down she saw why: the river reflected nothing. Not the black sky, not the moonless dark, not the torch she carried. The surface showed only depth, fathomless and smooth, like looking down into a well that went somewhere else entirely.

She crossed fast, heart hammering, and did not look back at the water.

The temple appeared above her as a shape cut from deeper black — the old ash-stone walls, the bell tower, the long roofline of the scriptorium wing where a single window glowed amber. One lit window in a valley of darkness. Someone was awake.

Someone was always awake in the scriptorium. Debt ledgers didn't copy themselves, and the Temple of Ashvale had never met a debt it wasn't willing to count twice.

Serah urged the mare up the switchback road, and it was halfway up, rounding the shoulder of the hill, that she saw the shards falling.

They came down through the dark like snow that had decided to be sharp — slivers of pale light, some as long as her forearm, drifting rather than dropping, turning slowly as they descended. Where they touched the ground they did not shatter. They sank, silently, into the earth, and the grass beneath them grayed and stiffened as though winter had kissed that single spot and moved on.

Ward-shards. She knew them from the training yards, from controlled breaches, from the one catastrophic failure at Northgate when she was nineteen — fragments of the ward-lattice shed when the lattice strained. But those had been rare, precious, swept up by keepers in lead-lined boxes and carried to the Temple vaults. These fell like weather. These fell like the sky itself was molting.

And they were falling everywhere. She could see them now across the whole slope, hundreds of them, thousands, a silent silver blizzard descending on the temple grounds, and among them — walking slowly, arms raised, face turned up — stood a figure in a scribe's plain robe.

Serah's blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the night.

She kicked the mare forward.

* * *

Rafael Vasquez did not hear the hoofbeats. Later, Serah would understand why: the whole world had gone deaf, or the whole world had gone quiet, or Rafael had gone somewhere the two were indistinguishable. But in the moment, riding hard across the temple yard, Serah saw only a person standing in a rain of impossible light, utterly still, utterly exposed, and every trained instinct in her body screamed *get them under cover.*

She hauled the mare to a stop close enough that the horse's breath stirred the scribe's robe.

"Inside. Now."

Rafael turned. Their face was streaked with tears — or perhaps with light; the shards' glow made it hard to tell — and their eyes were wide and wet and fixed on her with an expression she recognized from mirrors, from the faces of survivors at Northgate. Not fear exactly. Recognition. The look of someone seeing the shape of a truth they had been refusing all their life.

"The stars," Rafael said. Their voice was hoarse, scraped raw. "Keeper Vane. The stars are gone. Did you see? Did you—"

"I saw." She dismounted, boots crunching on grayed grass. "Get inside. Both of us. Now."

"They're falling." Rafael lifted a hand as another shard drifted past, close enough to touch. Up close Serah could see writing on it — fine, dense, curling sigilwork etched into the light itself, and her stomach dropped through the earth, because she knew that script. Every keeper did. It was the Founding Script, the language of the original ward, the one no living person was supposed to be able to read.

Except the shard was drifting toward Rafael's open palm like a moth toward a lamp, and as it settled against their skin it dimmed, softened, and slid beneath the flesh without a wound, without a mark, without anything at all except a small shudder that ran through the scribe's whole body.

Serah stopped breathing.

"You—" Her voice failed. She tried again. "What did you do?"

"Nothing." Rafael stared at their own hand as if it belonged to a stranger. "I've been standing here since the sky went out and they just — they come to me. Keeper, I don't understand any of this. I copy ledgers. I copy *ledgers.*"

Behind them, the great doors of the temple banged open, spilling torchlight and noise into the yard — shouting, running feet, the clatter of the night brothers roused from their cells. Somewhere a bell began to ring the alarm pattern, three-and-one, three-and-one, though the sound fell strangely flat, as if the silence of the valley were swallowing even the bronze.

Mother Ilsette emerged onto the steps, ancient and upright, and even at forty paces Serah saw the old woman's face and understood that Ilsette had been waiting for this night for a very long time. There was grief in it. But underneath the grief, worse: confirmation. The look of a scholar whose worst hypothesis has just been proven in front of her.

Her gaze found Rafael across the yard. Then it found Serah. And she beckoned — sharply, imperiously, the beckon of a woman who had buried three High Priests and outlasted every rival the Temple had produced in fifty years.

"Vane!" Her voice cracked across the silence like a whip. "You'll take the scribe and ride. Tonight. Before Maro's people reach the valley — and they will reach it, girl, they've been circling for years like wolves around a lamb they haven't yet identified."

"Mother, the wards—"

"The wards are dying." Ilsette said it flatly, terribly, the way you say a diagnosis. "They have been dying since before you were born, and we have been keeping them alive with—" She stopped. Looked at Rafael. Something in her ancient face folded. "With a cost none of you were told about. And tonight the account has come due."

Another shard fell, and another, and where they struck the flagstones of the yard the stone aged, grayed, crumbled gently into powder. The temple itself seemed to be shedding time.

"Take the east road," Ilsette said, gripping Serah's arm with fingers like bird talons, pulling her close, speaking fast and low. "The Door of Anew. Find it before he does. Everything depends on—" 

The light died.

Not the torches — those guttered and steadied. The light *inside* Ilsette. Serah felt it go the way you feel a door close in a far room: a change in pressure, a wrongness in the air. The old woman swayed. Her grip on Serah's arm loosened, slid, and she sat down on the temple steps with the unhurried motion of someone simply very tired, and did not get up.

Around the yard, the night brothers were falling too — quietly, one by one, folding down into the silver rain like harvest workers resting in a field. No wounds. No cries. Just a valley exhaling at last, after holding its breath all night.

Serah knelt. Pressed her fingers beneath Ilsette's jaw. Nothing.

When she looked up, Rafael had not moved. They stood in the falling light with their arms slack at their sides, tears cutting clean lines through the dust on their face, staring at the dead Mother on the steps.

"She knew," Rafael whispered. "She tested my blood last month. Against the relic. She went white as bone and told me it was nothing. She *knew*, and she let me keep copying ledgers, she let me keep counting my debt, she—" Their voice broke apart. "What am I?"

Every answer Serah had — every doctrine, every lesson, every comfortable lie the Temple had fed its keepers — lay dead on the steps in front of her. She had spent eleven years guarding a wall. She had hunted crossers, burned smugglers' caches, dragged a weeping child back over the border stones once, only once, and told herself the wards were mercy, order, protection. The wards were a cage, and the lock had just walked up to her wearing a scribe's robe and asking what it was.

She didn't know. She didn't know anything anymore.

But she knew Maro's soldiers were coming — Ilsette had said so, and Ilsette had been right about everything else tonight — and she knew that a person who could pull ward-shards out of the sky was the most valuable, most hunted thing in Ashvale, and she knew that she was the only one left standing in this yard with a horse and a sword and eleven years of knowing how to move unseen through broken country.

Dread had been riding with her all night. Somewhere between the starless ford and the dead Mother's hand slipping from her arm, it had hardened into something else. Something with edges. Something she could use.

Resolve.

"Later," Serah said, and heard her own voice come out rough and certain, a stranger's voice, a keeper's voice, the voice of the woman she would have to become before dawn. "I'll find out what you are later. Right now you're the scribe who knows the back ways into the crypts, and I'm the keeper who knows how to kill men in the dark, and together we're leaving before Maro's black-and-gold gets here. Can you walk?"

Rafael looked at her — really looked, the way people look when the world has ended and someone has offered them a rope — and nodded once.

"Then run."

Behind them, far to the south, beyond the dead wards and the starless sky, a red glow was rising over the hills like a false dawn.

It was not dawn.

It was the temple's future, coming up the road to meet them.
