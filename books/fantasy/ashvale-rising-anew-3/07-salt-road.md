## 7. Salt Road

The salt road was older than the valley, older than the wards, older than any law Serah had ever sworn to uphold. It ran west out of the broken country like a scar that had never quite healed — a ribbon of white-crusted earth where nothing grew and nothing rotted, where the bones of old pack oxen lay bleached and whole as if they had simply lain down one day and decided to be done with it.

Serah rode with her hood up and her hand never far from her knife. Behind her, Rafael clung to the saddle of their second horse with the grim determination of someone who had learned to ride in a single week out of necessity rather than affection. Three days from the temple ruins, and they still hadn't spoken much beyond what the road demanded. *Water. Rest. Watch the treeline.* The grammar of survival.

It was Rafael who broke the silence first, as the sun bled low over hills that looked almost right but not quite — the angles of them subtly wrong, the way a portrait is wrong when the painter has only heard the face described.

"You've been here before," Rafael said. Not a question.

"Once." Serah kept her eyes on the road. "Ten years ago. Before I took my vows."

"What's it called?"

"It doesn't have a name anymore." She nodded at the white crust glittering under the dying light. "Salt remembers what people would rather forget. That's why the trade died. That's why the road is empty. Everything that happened here got soaked into the ground, and now the ground won't grow anything but memory."

Rafael was quiet for a while. Then: "That's not how salt works."

"No," Serah agreed. "But we're not entirely in the world where salt works the way it should. Not since the wards fell."

She felt more than saw Rafael shiver behind her. Since leaving the ruined borderlands, the scribe had been changing — slowly, in ways Serah could see because she'd been trained to see. Their color was better. Their eyes caught light strangely at dusk, holding it a half-second too long. And twice now, when Rafael had slept, the grass around their bedroll had grown in a perfect circle overnight, green and soft as moss, while everything around it stayed gray.

Serah hadn't mentioned it. Some things you named aloud only gave them teeth.

* * *

They smelled the smugglers' camp before they saw it — woodsmoke with something sharp underneath, resin or tar or something burned to hide the smell of whatever else was burning. The road bent along a dry riverbed, and there, tucked into the cutbank like a knife into a sheath, stood a cluster of wagons and lean-tos strung with lanterns of colored glass. Red, green, amber. No temple white. No crown gold.

"Hold," said a voice from the rocks above, and Serah reined in so fast the horse sidestepped.

Three figures dropped down from the scree with the ease of people who had been born on slopes. Crossbows, all three, held low and casual and absolutely ready. The one in front was a woman with a shaved head and a burn scar running from jaw to collarbone, and she looked at Serah for a long moment before her mouth split into a grin missing two teeth.

"Well," she said. "Ashes keep. I thought you were dead, ward-keeper."

"I get that a lot lately," Serah said. "Is he here?"

"He's always here. He's nowhere else." The woman jerked her chin toward the camp. "You'll want to leave the horses. He'll want to talk about them first, and it goes better if you offer."

* * *

Brannoc had gotten fat. That was Serah's first thought, and her second was that the fat sat on him like armor sits on a man who has decided armor is more comfortable than speed. He sat by the largest fire in a chair made from a wagon bench and a barrel, drinking something dark from a tin cup, and when he saw her his face did three things in quick succession — surprise, calculation, and finally a kind of weary delight, like a man greeting a debt collector he'd been expecting for years.

"Serah Voss," he rumbled. "In the flesh. Last I heard, you were the Temple's favorite hound. Chasing folk like us across three provinces."

"The Temple burned four nights ago."

"So I heard." He didn't sound sorry. Around the fire, the other smugglers had gone quiet in the particular way of people listening without appearing to listen. "So I heard the wards came down too. Whole sky went black as a closed eye. Half my people think it's the end of the world. Other half think it's an opportunity." He took a drink. "I haven't decided which half I am yet."

"Neither have I," Serah said.

"And who's this?" His eyes moved past her to Rafael, who stood at the edge of the firelight with the wary posture of someone accustomed to being measured — for debt, for usefulness, for worth. Brannoc looked at them the way he looked at everything: like a man pricing goods. Then his gaze snagged, and Serah watched the pricing stop and something older take its place. "Well now. You're not temple stock. You're not crown stock. What are you?"

"They're with me," Serah said.

"That's not what I asked."

"It's the only answer you're getting tonight."

A muscle worked in Brannoc's jaw. Then he laughed — a big, rolling laugh that loosened the circle of listeners and sent a couple of the younger ones back to their dice. "Still the same. Ten years, and you still talk like a locked door." He kicked a stool out from beside his chair. "Sit, both of you. Eat first. Debts after. A man who talks business hungry says stupid things, and I've said enough stupid things in my life to know."

* * *

The food was better than it had any right to be — river fish packed in salt from the very road they'd traveled, roots roasted in ash, flatbread cooked on a stone. Rafael ate like someone who had recently remembered hunger could be answered, and Serah found herself watching them instead of eating, cataloguing again: the way they held the bread in both hands, close to the chest, the habit of a childhood spent rationing. Temple-raised. Debt-bound. Every kindness priced and entered in a ledger.

*You were the lock,* Ilsette had whispered, pressing the sigil into Rafael's palm as the roof beams came down. Serah hadn't seen it happen — she'd been dragging Rafael toward the crypts — but she'd seen the sigil since, hanging at Rafael's throat on a cord, and she knew what it was. Knew what it meant. Knew, in the cold arithmetic part of herself that had designed wards and hunted unregistered children and called it duty, exactly how many people would kill for what hung against that thin chest.

Brannoc waited until Rafael drifted toward the fire's edge, drawn by a knot of smugglers arguing cheerfully about a card game whose rules seemed to change with every hand. Then he leaned toward Serah, and his voice dropped into a register she remembered from ten years ago, from a night of rain and blood and a promise made over a dying man.

"You're carrying trouble, Serah. I can smell it on you like smoke. So let's not dance. What do you want?"

"Passage west. Off every marked road. To Mount Anew."

The name landed on the fire like a wet log. The crackle seemed to hush. Brannoc set down his cup slowly.

"Nobody goes to Anew," he said. "Anew is a story grandmothers tell to frighten children about mountains. There's no Door there. There's no anything there. Just rock and weather and the ghosts of an empire nobody misses."

"There's a Door," Serah said quietly. "And a man named Kestrel Maro is going to reach it if no one gets there first. And when he does, there won't be smugglers or temples or crowns or roads. There'll just be Maro, forever, and everyone else living inside his sentence."

Brannoc studied her for a long time. Firelight moved in the deep lines of his face like water finding channels worn over years.

"You believe that," he said at last. "That's the strange thing. Ten years ago you'd have told me the same story with the Temple's name in place of his, and I'd have laughed. But you believe this." He turned the cup in his thick fingers. "What changed?"

"Everything I believed in burned down around me, and I'm still standing in it." Serah looked into the fire. "You owe me a debt, Brannoc. I've never called it in. I'm calling it now."

"I know you are." He sighed, vast and slow, like a landslide deciding to happen. "Kellin's son. You carried the boy eleven miles through floodwater with a broken arm, and then you let us go when your own captain ordered you to burn the camp. You lost your standing for that. Maybe your career." He shook his head. "I've owed you ten years, and I always figured the day you collected, it'd be for something impossible."

"This is impossible."

"Aye." He drained the cup and stood, joints cracking like green wood in a hot fire. "Then it's fitting. Wake your scribe. We ride at first light — and Serah?" He paused, looking down at her, and for a moment the fat and the years fell away and she saw the man who had once fought beside her against slavers on the coast, before either of them belonged to anyone. "If this goes wrong, if your Maro catches us out on the open salt with no law and no cover — my people die for your conviction. Remember that you chose this for them."

"I remember everything I choose," Serah said. "It's the curse of my profession."

* * *

Rafael didn't sleep that night. Serah found them sitting apart from the camp, on a shelf of rock above the dry riverbed, knees drawn up, the sigil in their hand. They weren't wearing it around their neck anymore; they were holding it, turning it, watching the starlight — such starlight as remained, a sky scrubbed nearly bare — move across its surface.

"It's warm," Rafael said without looking up. "It's been warm since the temple. Is that normal? Do you know what it is?"

Serah sat down beside them. Close enough to share warmth, not so close as to presume. The distance between those two points had become, over three days, the most carefully measured distance in her life.

"I know what it isn't," she said. "It isn't temple work. The sigils I was trained on are stamped metal, blessed ink, ceremony. This is—" She hesitated. "Older. It looks like something that was made before making was invented. Or something that made itself."

"Mother Ilsette knew." Rafael's voice was flat, and beneath the flatness, a fault line. "She tested my blood against a relic weeks before the wards fell. She went white as salt and sent me back to copying ledgers like nothing happened. She *knew*, Serah. She knew what I was, and she let me go on counting other people's debts."

"She was protecting you."

"By lying to me?" Rafael's hand closed around the sigil, knuckles whitening. "Everyone protected me by lying to me. The Temple. The wards. You—" They stopped. The word hung between them, unfinished, and Serah felt it settle against her ribs like a swallowed stone.

"Yes," she said quietly. "Me too. When the time comes, I'll tell you everything I did, and you can decide what I'm worth. But not tonight. Tonight I'm too tired to be honest and too armed to be safe."

That startled a breath of laughter out of Rafael — small, surprised, real. They looked over at her, and in the dark their eyes did the thing they sometimes did now, catching light that wasn't there. "Too armed to be safe. Is that a ward-keeper proverb?"

"It's a Serah proverb. I'm the only one who uses it. It hasn't caught on."

Below them, the smugglers' fire burned low. Someone was singing — a rough, tuneless song about a highwayman and a magistrate's daughter, all verses irreverent and the last one unexpectedly tender. Rafael listened with their head tilted, the way they listened to everything, like a person who had spent a lifetime transcribing the world and couldn't stop even now.

"They don't seem afraid," Rafael said. "The wards are down. Magic is loose in the world. The sky is wrong. And they're singing about magistrates' daughters."

"Most of them have never had anything a law ever protected," Serah said. "No land. No title. No temple standing surety for their souls. When you live outside the law, the law falling doesn't take anything from you." She paused. "It's not freedom, exactly. It's the shape freedom leaves behind. But some nights, it's closer than most people ever get."

Rafael was quiet a moment. "Is that why you brought us here? Of all the roads?"

"Partly." Serah watched the fire. "Because I needed help I couldn't buy and couldn't command. Because Brannoc owes me and pays his debts. And partly because—" She exhaled. "Because you've lived your whole life inside a system that told you what you were worth down to the copper. I wanted you to see people who decided their own worth. Before you have to decide yours."

Rafael looked down at the sigil in their hand. The starless sky gave back almost nothing, and yet the thing glowed faintly anyway, a coal refusing the dark.

"And what if I decide wrong?" they asked.

"Then you decide again tomorrow," Serah said. "That's the whole secret of it. That's the thing the Temple never taught you. Worth isn't a ledger. It's a practice."

Rafael turned the sigil over once more, then slipped the cord back over their head, letting it rest against their chest. "You're different out here," they said. "Less like a blade."

"I'm exhausted," Serah said. "It looks similar."

This time Rafael laughed properly, and the sound went out over the dry riverbed and the white road and the sleeping camp, small and human in a country that had forgotten what it was.

* * *

They rode at first light, and the salt road took them west.

Brannoc led with six riders and two wagons, and he led them off the road itself within the hour, onto tracks that weren't tracks — routes that existed only in the memory of people who had spent generations moving goods around every law ever written. He navigated by things Serah couldn't follow: a leaning stone, a pattern of cairns, the absence of crows in one valley and their abundance in another. When Rafael asked how he knew the way, Brannoc grinned his gap-toothed grin and said, "Law can't find these paths because law needs things written down. We keep our maps in scars and grudges. Both last longer than ink."

They crossed a province in three days that should have taken a week, and everywhere the world was wrong in new ways. A village where every child had the same name. A forest that shed autumn leaves in spring. A church with a saint's statue whose face was smooth as an egg, as though no one could agree anymore what the saint had looked like, and reality had split the difference.

On the fourth evening, camped in the shadow of foothills that might have been the shoulders of Mount Anew itself — a vast darkness rising against the bruised horizon — Brannoc came and sat across from Serah and Rafael at their fire. He was uncharacteristically sober, and that frightened Serah more than anything the road had shown her.

"My scouts came back," he said. "From the ridge line." He turned his cup in his hands, not drinking. "There's an army in the pass ahead of us. Black and gold. And they're not marching toward the mountain, Serah. They're digging in. Fortifying. Like men guarding something." He looked up, and firelight showed something in his face she had rarely seen there: genuine fear. "And there's something else. The stars over the mountain. My scout swears there are more of them than there were yesterday. New ones. Arranged in lines."

Serah felt the cold arrive in her spine and spread outward, precise as frost on glass.

Lines. Stars arranged in lines.

Maro wasn't marching toward the Door. He was already writing with it.

Beside her, she heard Rafael's breath catch, and when she glanced over, the sigil at their throat was glowing — bright now, unmistakable, answering something in the dark above the mountain like a voice calling a name.

"Get some sleep," Brannoc said quietly, rising. "All of you. Tomorrow we stop being smugglers." He looked at the mountain, and his jaw set. "Tomorrow we decide what we are instead."
