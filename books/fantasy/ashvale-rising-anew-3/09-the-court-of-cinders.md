## 9. The Court of Cinders

The city had been called Vellmarin once. Serah knew it from maps drawn before her birth — a river town of slate roofs and bell towers, famous for its dyers' quarter where cloth hung from every window like banners in a permanent festival. Now the name meant nothing. The dyers' quarter was ash to the second story, and the bells had been pulled down and melted, she supposed, for shot. What remained of Vellmarin was a stage.

Maro had built his court in its central square, in the shell of the old assize hall whose roof had burned away but whose stone columns still stood, blackened and patient as ribs. He had hung lanterns — hundreds of them, gold-veined glass on iron chains — so that the ruin glowed from within like a lamp with a corpse for a wick. The effect was deliberate. Serah had served under men who understood spectacle, and she recognized the grammar of it: *I can make even destruction beautiful. I can make you grateful I spared what I spared.*

She stood at the edge of the crowd with her hood up and Rafael pressed close at her side, close enough that she could feel the tremor running through them like a plucked string that would not stop singing.

"I don't want to be here," Rafael whispered.

"Neither do I." She kept her voice low and flat, a ward-keeper's voice, the voice she'd used on frightened recruits. "But we need to see how he holds court. We need to know what he does with people like us."

"People like us," Rafael repeated. There was something raw in the way they said it — not fear exactly, but the vertigo of a person discovering they belong to a category they never knew existed. Three weeks ago Rafael Vasquez had been a scribe counting down an indenture in neat copperplate. Now they were one of the hunted, and the hunt had come to town.

* * *

They had entered Vellmarin at dusk, through the tannery district, because Brannoc's smugglers knew which walls had eyes and which did not. The salt road had delivered them south along the river, and the river had delivered them here, because there was no way around: every bridge, every ford, every ferry crossing within fifty miles now answered to Maro's black-and-gold. If they were going to reach Mount Anew, they would have to pass through the country he was busy remaking, and Serah had decided — against every instinct that had kept her alive for thirty years — that it was better to walk through the wolf's den in daylight than creep past it in the dark and wonder forever what they'd failed to see.

So they came as pilgrims. That was the cover Brannoc's people suggested: Maro encouraged pilgrimage to his courts. He wanted witnesses. Witnesses were half his weapon; terror only worked if it traveled home in ordinary mouths.

The square filled as the light died. Farmers with cart-stiff hands. Townsfolk with the gray, rationed look of the occupied. A knot of Maro's soldiers in their lacquered black plate, gold filigree catching the lantern-light like fat in broth. And everywhere the cinders — the city's dead ground up fine and drifting on the evening wind, settling on shoulders and eyelashes, so that the whole crowd slowly turned the same soft gray, mourners at a funeral no one had been invited to but everyone was required to attend.

Serah watched Rafael watch the crowd, and saw them doing what scribes do: cataloguing. The set of a soldier's jaw. The number of archers on the assize hall's broken cornice. The way the townsfolk stood — not huddled together but spaced apart, each person an island, because in Maro's country neighbors had learned not to trust neighbors. Rafael's lips moved faintly, counting, recording. Even now. Even here. Serah felt something twist in her chest that she refused, for the moment, to name.

Then trumpets sounded — three notes, flat and joyless — and the court began.

* * *

Maro did not appear at first. First came the condemned.

They were brought out in chains across the square, twelve of them, and Serah's stomach dropped before her mind caught up, because she knew that procession. She had designed it. Years ago, in another life, in the Temple's border garrisons, she had helped formalize the protocol for parading captured hedge-mages and unregistered talents — the spacing of the guards, the hoods removed at the threshold so the crowd could see their faces, the slow walk that gave everyone time to look. She had told herself then that it was mercy, of a kind: better a public accounting than a quiet disappearance. She had believed the Temple's justice was clean.

Maro had taken her protocol and made it honest. That was the obscenity of him. He had stripped away the liturgy and left only the machinery, and the machinery worked exactly as she had built it.

Twelve prisoners. All wearing the gray-and-silver of the ward-keepers' order — her order, the order that had guarded Ashvale's borders for three hundred years, the order Maro had declared traitors the day the wards fell. Of course he blamed them. The wards had fallen on their watch; more, the wards had fallen *through* them, drained of their anchor, and whether or not any individual keeper had known why, Maro needed the story simple. The keepers had failed. The keepers had betrayed. The keepers would pay, publicly, repeatedly, until the last gray cloak in the country was ash or silence.

The prisoners were chained to posts on the assize hall's steps. Hoods off. Faces to the crowd.

Serah looked, because looking was the penance, and in the third prisoner from the left she found a face she had last seen eleven years ago across a training yard in the mountain garrison at Corveth: Warden-Commander Edeya Solm, silver at her temples even then, a woman who had taught Serah everything worth knowing about reading a wardline — and several things about when to look away from one.

Edeya had pulled Serah out of a flooded culvert when Serah was nineteen and too proud to admit she couldn't swim. She had stood over Serah's first disciplinary hearing and said, *the girl's judgment is sound; her methods are her own,* and saved her career with nine words. She had taught her that a ward is a promise made in public and kept in private, and that the keeping is the whole of the honor.

Now Edeya Solm knelt on scorched marble with her wrists chained behind her, and she held her chin up the way she'd taught Serah to hold hers, and Serah stood in a crowd of strangers with her hood low and did nothing.

* * *

Maro came out at full dark.

He was smaller than the stories made him — that was the first thing Rafael would say later, and Serah had thought it herself the one other time she'd seen him, from three hundred yards, at a review of troops. Kestrel Maro was a lean, gray-templed man in plain black with a single gold chain, the kind of officer who might have managed supply depots competently for twenty years and retired unnoticed. He had a scholar's stoop and a clerk's careful hands. When he spoke, he did not shout. The square was built to carry his voice, and the crowd was built — by hunger, by fear, by the soldiers spaced through it — to listen.

"People of Vellmarin," he said. "People of the valley country. You have heard lies about me. You have been told I burned your city."

A pause. Long enough for the denial to rise in throats and die there.

"I did not burn your city," Maro said. "Your protectors did. The ward-keepers — the ones who swore to stand between you and the wild magic — drained your protections to feed their own hidden works, and when their theft collapsed, the fire that followed was theirs. These twelve—" he gestured, gentle, almost sorrowful, "—are not all guilty. But they are all responsible. In my new order, those will be the same thing."

Serah's hand found Rafael's wrist without her deciding to move it. Rafael's pulse hammered against her fingers, quick as a bird's.

"He's lying," Rafael breathed. "Ilsette — the temple — they didn't—"

"They didn't know," Serah murmured. "Most of them didn't know. It doesn't matter. Lies don't need to be true, only useful." She had said almost the same words to recruits, years ago, about enemy propaganda. Hearing them in her own mouth about her own order's death was a different education entirely.

Maro continued, walking the length of the steps, and the lanterns moved with him so that his shadow swung huge across the crowd. He spoke of the age of wards ending. He spoke of protection — real protection, ordered protection, protection with a single accountable hand. He spoke, briefly and beautifully, of suffering: how much of it came from ungoverned things, wild magic, chance, the cruelty of a sky that sent storms where it pleased. *Suffering can be curated,* he said, and the phrase went through the crowd like cold water, and Serah saw farmers nodding. That was the horror of him. He was not wrong about the world's cruelty. He was only wrong — catastrophically, unforgivably wrong — about the cure, and he had an army to make his wrongness law.

Then the sentences began.

* * *

Serah had witnessed executions before. Temple justice, border justice, the grim arithmetic of a hard frontier. She had told herself she was hardened to it, and she had mostly believed it.

What broke her was not the dying. It was Edeya's composure. One by one the prisoners were asked to recant — to swear loyalty to the new order, to name their fellow keepers, to kneel to Maro and live — and one by one they refused, and Edeya, kneeling third in line, watched each refusal with the calm attention of a woman grading an examination. When her own turn came, the herald read her crimes — *abandonment of post; concealment of ward-secrets; treason against the protectorate* — and Edeya laughed. Actually laughed, short and sharp, a sound that carried impossibly far in the dead air.

"Concealment," she said, loud enough for the square. "Yes. We concealed. Ask your Protector what we concealed, and where, and who was locked inside it. Ask him what he's marching toward under the mountain. Ask him—"

The gag went in fast. Two soldiers, practiced. But the words had gone out over the crowd like sparks over dry thatch, and Serah saw them land: heads turning, shoulders stiffening, a murmur moving through the gray-cindered mass of people like wind through wheat. *Ask him what.* People would carry that question home tonight. Maro knew it too — she watched the small tightening around his eyes, the flicker of a man recalculating — and she understood, with a cold drop through her middle, that Edeya had done it deliberately. The old woman had spent her last minutes planting a seed in a thousand minds, because that was the kind of teacher she had been.

And Serah stood there and let her die.

It happened quickly after that. Maro did not give speeches over the condemned; he gave them over the living. A signal, a line of crossbows, a volley that sounded like the sky cracking. Twelve bodies slumped against their posts. The lanterns swayed. Cinders drifted down over everything, softening the shapes, and a soldier with a shovel began the work of making the stage ready for the next performance.

Serah did not remember deciding to stay silent. That was the thing she would hate later — that there had been no decision, no single moment she could point to and say *there, that was the betrayal.* There was only Rafael beside her, warm and breathing and utterly unaware of what it would cost to move, and the arithmetic she had learned in twenty years of service: one life weighed against the mission, the mission weighed against the valley, the valley weighed against everything. Edeya Solm had taught her that arithmetic. Edeya Solm had died by it, and would have approved of it, and Serah hated that most of all.

Rafael's hand slid into hers. Small, ink-stained, trembling.

"We should go," Rafael whispered. "Serah. Your hand — you're shaking. We should go."

"Yes," Serah said. Her voice came out steady, which felt like the final obscenity. "Yes. Now, while the crowd's breaking up."

They moved with the dispersing throng, two more gray ghosts in a city of them, and behind them the lanterns burned on over the empty posts, and Serah fixed her eyes forward and did not look back, because she had been trained never to look back, and for the first time in her life the discipline felt like drowning.

* * *

They made the tannery district in silence. Only at the safehouse — a dyer's cellar Brannoc's people kept, smelling of oak gall and old rust — did Rafael finally turn to her in the dark, and Serah saw by the thin light through the grate that they had been watching her all night, the way they watched everything, cataloguing.

"You knew one of them," Rafael said. Not a question.

"The third one. Edeya Solm." Saying the name aloud cost something; Serah paid it. "Warden-Commander of the Corveth garrison. She trained me. She—" Her throat closed. She stood very still and waited it out, the old trick, breathe through the body and not the chest. "She pulled me out of a flood when I was nineteen. She vouched for me when no one else would. She taught me nearly everything I've used to keep you alive these past weeks."

Rafael was quiet a long moment. In the cellar dark, their face was mostly shadow and one pale stripe of lamplight.

"And you stayed silent," they said. "To protect me."

"To protect the mission," Serah corrected, and heard immediately how hollow it rang, how much it sounded like the Temple's language, like Maro's language, like every hierarchy that ever spent a person and called it duty.

"That's not what I asked."

"No," Serah admitted. "It isn't."

Rafael stepped closer. They did not touch her; they simply stood near, the way you stand near someone standing at an edge, and their voice when it came was gentle and unbearably direct, the way it always was, the scribe's habit of getting the record right.

"You're going to carry this," they said. "You're going to tell yourself you chose correctly, and some nights you'll believe it, and some nights you won't. I've watched you do this with smaller things since we left the valley. So I'm not going to tell you it wasn't your fault, because you wouldn't hear it, and maybe you're right not to. I'm just going to say — I saw her plant that question in the crowd. Whatever she bought with her life, she chose to spend it. And what you bought with your silence is still walking. I'm still walking. Don't waste it."

Serah stared at them. Somewhere above, boots crossed the street and faded. The lamplight stripe lay across Rafael's collarbone like a blade laid flat.

"When did you become the one who says things like that?" she asked, and her voice cracked on the last word, just slightly, just enough.

"Somewhere on the salt road," Rafael said. "You weren't paying attention. You were busy teaching me warding."

A breath that was almost a laugh escaped her, ragged, and she sat down heavily on a crate in the dark and put her face in her hands, and Rafael sat beside her, shoulder to shoulder, saying nothing more, which was its own kind of warding.

* * *

Sleep would not come. Near midnight Serah gave up on it and climbed to the cellar's high slit window to take her watch, and that was where she was when she saw the lights.

Not lanterns. Fires — a line of them coming down the river road from the north, dozens, scores, torches and campfires of a column on the march. She counted by habit, estimated by worse habit, and the number she arrived at sat in her stomach like swallowed stone: a thousand, perhaps more. Infantry in black. Supply wagons. And at the column's head, riding under a standard that caught the firelight in a long gold gleam, a banner she had seen only once before, at a distance, and hoped never to see again at any distance at all.

Maro's army was leaving Vellmarin. Marching south. Marching, by Serah's reckoning of the roads and the river and the shape of the country beyond — straight toward the ford at Callowmere, the last crossing before the mountain country, the only way through to Mount Anew.

Behind her, below, Rafael slept at last, curled on the crate, the sigil's outline faintly visible through their shirt where it lay against their skin, warm enough now that Serah could sometimes feel it radiating like a banked coal whenever she stood close.

She watched the column wind southward and did the arithmetic again, the only arithmetic she had ever trusted, and for the first time it gave her an answer she could not accept: *we cannot beat them to the ford.*

We cannot go around. We cannot go through. Which meant tomorrow, at the water, she would have to tell Rafael the truth — not about the army, but about the other thing, the thing she had been carrying since the wards fell, the thing about the wards themselves and who had written them and what, exactly, they had been built to hide. Because if they were going to die at a river crossing, Rafael deserved to hear it from her first.

Serah watched the fires dwindle down the dark road and began, silently, to rehearse the confession she had no right to delay any longer.
