## 11. Vane's Bargain

The camp woke to the smell of woodsmoke and wet wool, and Rafael woke to the memory of the ledger.

It was strange, the things that came back in sleep. For eleven years they had copied debt into columns, and now debt had followed them out of the valley like a dog that would not be left behind. In dreams they still sat at the scriptorium desk with its worn groove for their elbows, dipping the pen, writing *Vasquez, R. — term remaining: four years, two months, eleven days* — and every morning the number had been smaller, and every morning they had felt the peculiar grief of a prisoner watching his sentence shorten toward a world he did not know how to live in.

Now there was no number at all. The temple was ash. The papers that bound them were somewhere in a soldier's saddlebag or burned, and they could not decide which was worse.

They sat up in the grey light under the salt road's overhang, where Brannoc's people had made camp in the lee of a fallen watchtower. The smugglers moved quietly even at rest — no fires above knee-height, no voices above a murmur, sentries posted on the rocks with the patience of herons. Serah was already awake, of course. She sat sharpening a knife against a whetstone with the slow, metronomic rhythm she used when her thoughts were bad company, and when she saw Rafael stir she did not stop, only tilted her chin toward the kettle.

"Drink something," she said. "You talked in your sleep."

"What did I say?"

"Numbers." The whetstone whispered along the blade. "Columns of them."

Rafael took the tin cup she'd filled and held it in both hands without drinking. Three days since the ford. Three days since Serah had told them the truth in the flat voice of someone reading an execution order — *I helped design the wards. I helped hide you from yourself* — and three days since Rafael had discovered that rage and love could occupy the same body at the same time, the way fire and a house can, if the house is stubborn enough. They had not forgiven her. They had not left her either. They walked side by side through the mornings like two people carrying opposite ends of something heavy, and neither of them had yet said what it weighed.

Brannoc came down from the rocks with the sun not yet fully risen, chewing on a strip of dried fish and frowning at the eastern sky.

"You feel that?" he said.

Rafael felt many things these days. Since leaving the valley, the sigil Ilsette had pressed into their dying hand had become less an object than an organ — a second heart under their sternum, warm, attentive, occasionally hungry. It warmed now, faintly, the way it warmed before weather changed or before wild things watched them from the treeline. But what Brannoc meant was simpler than magic.

"Horses," Serah said. She was on her feet before the word finished, knife vanishing into her belt. "Many. Coming up the switchbacks."

"How many is many?"

"More than us." She looked at Brannoc. "How much do you trust this camp?"

"I don't trust anyone," Brannoc said comfortably. "But I've scattered better soldiers than Maro's with worse ground than this. Get your scribe behind the tower. If it goes wrong, there's a goat track down the west face that drops into the ravine. You'll want to not look down."

"It won't go wrong," Serah said, in the voice she used when she was daring the world to argue.

* * *

It went wrong slowly, which was somehow worse.

The detachment came up the salt road in column, black-and-gold banners hanging limp in the windless morning, maybe forty riders and twice that on foot. They did not charge. They did not spread out. They simply arrived, the way weather arrives, and halted in the flat below the ruined watchtower with the terrible discipline of men who have done this a hundred times and expect to do it a hundred more. At their center rode a single figure on a grey mare, and Rafael's stomach dropped before their eyes confirmed him — the long coat, the patient face, the voice like a man reading terms aloud.

Vane.

He had caught them once before, on the road out of the ruined country, and he had been almost gentle about it. That was the thing nobody warned you about enforcers: the good ones never raised their voice. He had held Rafael's indenture papers then, unrolled them like a gift, and offered freedom — true freedom, signed and sealed — in exchange for the sigil. Rafael had refused, and Serah had put a knife to his throat, and he had ridden away smiling as though they'd concluded a pleasant negotiation that could be resumed at convenience.

Now he dismounted below the tower and stood with his hands visible, empty, palms out. A man showing a dog he carried no stick.

"Scribe Vasquez," he called up. "May I approach? Your keeper has my measure by now; I'd rather not lose another finger's width of ear."

Nobody answered. Above, on the rocks, smugglers lay among the stones like lichen. Rafael stood in the shadow of the tower arch with Serah half a step ahead of them, and the sigil beat against their ribs like a fist knocking on a door.

"I'll take silence as consent," Vane said, and walked his horse forward alone.

He stopped at conversational distance and looked up at the archway, and his gaze found Rafael with the ease of a man who had spent weeks thinking about exactly where they would be standing.

"You look better fed," he said. "That's the captain's doing, I imagine. Brannoc keeps his people well. It's one of his virtues." He let the word sit there. "One of several. Loyalty is another. I mention it because loyalty is about to become expensive for him, and I thought you should know the price before you let him pay it."

"Say what you came to say," Serah called down.

"I'm saying it." Vane didn't raise his voice. "To the scribe, if it's all the same. It concerns them most closely." He drew something from his coat, and Rafael's whole body went cold expecting steel — but it was paper. Their indenture papers, travel-worn but intact, the temple seal still legible through the scorch mark along one edge. "I hold these. I have always held these. Do you understand what that means, Scribe Vasquez? Not metaphorically. Legally. The Temple of Ashvale assigned its claims to the Crown's stewardship when the Temple fell, and the Crown's stewardship is currently exercised by Kestrel Maro, and Kestrel Maro exercises it through me. You are not a fugitive. You are property that has wandered off, and I am very good at finding property."

"You're a long way from anywhere that law lives," Brannoc said from somewhere above, lazily, dangerously.

"The law travels," Vane agreed. "It's traveling right now. Forty riders behind me, two hundred more a day south. Now—" and here he did something Rafael had not seen him do before. He hesitated. He glanced back at his own column, then up at the arch, and lowered his voice as though the next part were a confidence between professionals. "I'm going to tell you what the papers are actually for. Not the fiction. The truth. Because I think you deserve to make your choice knowing it, and because — call it professional courtesy — I don't much care for the truth myself."

Rafael's mouth was dry. "Then say it."

"Maro doesn't want the sigil." Vane turned the papers over in his fingers. "The sigil is a key, yes. Keys are useful. But keys are portable, and portable things can be stolen back, and Maro does not intend to ever have anything stolen back again. What he wants is the lock. And the lock, Scribe Vasquez—" he looked up, and for one unguarded moment there was something in his face that was almost pity, "—the lock is you. You were made to hold the wards. Every ward in this country was written through blood like yours, and the Door of Anew is the first of them, the oldest, the master-script. A key opens it. But a living lock *becomes* it. Maro means to feed you to the Door. To bind you into it, alive, forever, so that the power flows through you and answers only him. You will hold the wards of his new country until the mountains wear down. You will not die. That is the part his priests are proudest of. They've checked."

The words went into Rafael like cold water into a crack in stone — painless at first, and then everywhere.

Serah had gone very still beside them. Stillness, Rafael had learned, was what Serah did instead of screaming.

"So bring me the sigil," Vane went on, reasonably, horribly reasonably, "and perhaps we negotiate. Perhaps the papers burn. Perhaps you spend the rest of your life as a free person of no particular interest to anyone. Or refuse, and I take you today, and the sigil besides, and Captain Brannoc learns what it costs to shelter stolen goods. I would rather not. I have read his file. He is a better man than his profession usually produces." He smiled thinly. "The world is full of such disappointments."

Rafael heard themselves speak before they knew they were going to.

"No."

Vane waited.

"You think I'm property," Rafael said, and their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect, and underneath it the sigil was burning now, a slow bright heat spreading up through their chest and throat. "Eleven years I copied ledgers. I wrote other people's debts in columns so small and neat that I forgot I was one of the entries. And you stand there holding my life on a piece of paper and you think the paper is the power." They stepped forward, out of the arch's shadow, into the open, and heard Serah's sharp intake of breath behind them. "Burn the paper. Eat it. It was never mine and it was never yours. *I* am mine. And I would rather be fed to that Door than hand you one grain of what I carry — do you hear me? I would rather hold those wards until the mountains wear down than spend one day as Maro's instrument. At least then I'd be holding something shut instead of opening it for him."

The words rang off the rocks. Somewhere above, a smuggler shifted. Vane regarded them for a long moment, and then, quietly, he folded the indenture papers away into his coat.

"That," he said, "was the wrong answer. But it was well spoken. I'll give it that." He turned toward his horse, and paused. "For the record, Scribe — I told you the truth because I thought you might be sensible. Sensible people survive. The rest of you make such interesting ruins."

He put two fingers to his lips and whistled.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way Brannoc had promised it wouldn't need to.

The detachment moved up the slope in three lines, shields locked, crossbows behind, and the smugglers came off the rocks like water finding every crack at once. It was not a battle so much as a dismantling. Brannoc's people had spent their lives learning ground the way priests learned scripture; they knew which boulders could be levered, which scree would run, which goat paths let six armed shadows appear behind forty soldiers' backs. Rafael crouched in the archway with Serah's arm barring their chest and watched the neat black-and-gold column come apart into shouting fragments. A boulder the size of a cottage went over the edge and took the road's outer lip with it. Crossbow bolts flickered and were answered from three directions at once. Horses screamed. Somewhere a sergeant's voice rose and rose and then stopped rising altogether.

"They're breaking the road behind us," Serah murmured. Her arm hadn't moved from Rafael's chest. "We ride east through the trees while they're busy. Brannoc will hold the pass an hour, no more."

"We're not leaving him to—"

"He knows the arithmetic. He chose it before Vane finished talking." But her voice had gone rough, and Rafael understood suddenly that roughness was its own kind of grief, pre-spent, already paid.

They ran for the horses through falling dust. Behind them the fight churned and thinned, and just as they reached the treeline Rafael looked back — everyone looks back, that is the whole curse of being a person — and saw Brannoc on the high rocks with his axe, laughing, holding the narrow neck of the pass against a dozen men because holding it was the job and he had taken the job. Rafael saw the moment it turned. A crossbowman who should have been dead wasn't. A bolt that should have missed didn't. Brannoc staggered, sat down almost politely on the rock, and kept swinging from where he sat, and then the press of soldiers closed over him like water over a dropped coin, and Serah's hand closed over Rafael's eyes and turned their head away, and they rode east through the pines with the sound of the pass fading behind them into ordinary birdsong.

* * *

They rode until the light went copper and then grey, and made a cold camp in a hollow full of ferns because smoke would carry. Nobody spoke for a long time. The surviving smugglers — five of them, out of eleven — tended their horses with the exaggerated competence of people avoiding their own thoughts. The eldest, a woman with a burn-scarred jaw whom the others called Maret, sat apart sharpening nothing in particular.

Rafael sat with their back against a pine trunk and discovered that fury and mourning, which they had always imagined as opposites, were in fact the same fire burning different fuel.

*I would rather hold those wards until the mountains wear down.* They had said it to Vane's face, and meant it, and meant also the thing underneath it that frightened them more: that some part of them had wanted Vane to flinch. Had wanted the whole black-and-gold machine of Maro's ambition to flinch. And it hadn't. It had folded its papers away and whistled for its dogs, and Brannoc — Brannoc who owed Serah an old debt and paid it in full on a rock in the pass, Brannoc who had fed them stew and called them *little scribe* without mockery — was under the stones of the salt road now, and the machine had not even slowed.

"Say it," Serah said quietly. She had settled beside them without seeming to move, close enough that their shoulders touched. "Whatever it is. Say it out loud. It rots quieter in the dark."

"He died for me." Rafael's voice cracked on the second word. "He didn't even know me. Three days. He fed me once and died for me, and Maro will write him out of the world entirely if he gets the Door, and none of it — none of it *counts*, Serah, unless we—"

"Unless we finish it." She was looking at the darkening trees. "Yes."

"And I'm furious." The fury came up now like floodwater, and Rafael let it, because she had told them to say it. "I'm furious at Maro, at Vane, at the Temple, at Ilsette for dying before she could explain, at the wards, at the sky, at — at you, sometimes, still, I'm sorry, I know we—"

"Don't apologize for that." Serah's voice was low and even. "You get to be furious at me. You earned it honestly. Just aim it. Fury without aim is just weight. Aimed, it's a tool." She turned her head, and in the last grey light her eyes found Rafael's with the directness that had terrified them in the scriptorium a lifetime ago and steadied them ever since. "So aim it. Maro wants to feed you to the Door. Fine. Then we arrive at the Door first, and we arrive angry, and we show him what a living lock does when it chooses its own turning. Can you walk with that?"

Rafael breathed. The sigil beat under their sternum, warm and patient, and for the first time it did not feel like a burden or a key or a debt.

It felt like an answer.

"Yes," they said.

Maret crossed the hollow and crouched before them both, and her scarred face was grim in the way of someone delivering numbers she had already counted twice.

"There's a place," she said. "Two days north. Old road shrine, abandoned since before the wards. People go there when they need to know things the normal ways won't give. Singers, they're called, or they were." She spat to one side, an old habit against ill luck. "If anything's left of them. After what Maro's been doing to mages on the roads — taking their fire and leaving the rest standing — I don't know what you'll find. But if you want to reach Mount Anew ahead of an army, you'll want what they know."

Serah and Rafael looked at each other, and the same word passed between them without either speaking it.

"Then tomorrow," Serah said, "we go and find the choir."
