## 12. The Hollow Choir

The song reached them before the city did.

Serah reined in at the crest of the ridge and held up a hand, and behind her Rafael stilled the mule without being told — they had learned each other's signals over the long gray weeks of road, the small vocabulary of survival that needed no words. The wind came up the valley carrying something thin and high and wrong, a sound like glass rubbed at the rim, like a hymn sung by throats that had forgotten what hymns were for.

"You hear it," Rafael said. Not a question. Their voice had changed since the ridge came down; there was a carefulness in it now, the way a person who has handled fire once speaks more softly around candles.

"I hear it." Serah dismounted, boots finding the frost-stiff grass. "Ward-song. Or what's left of it."

She had heard ward-song all her life. It was the first thing they taught you in the keepers' barracks at Cael Harrow: close your eyes, press your palm to a boundary stone, and listen. The wards sang. A low, patient chord, older than the temple, older than the valley's founding, woven by hands whose names had been burned out of every record Serah ever read. *We hold. We hold. We hold.* She had fallen asleep to it as an apprentice, that chord humming through the stone floors of the barracks, and she had never once thought to ask what it cost to sing.

She was asking now.

The city below them had been called Vessany once. Serah knew it from patrol maps — a river town, prosperous enough, famous for its bell-foundry and its autumn market. What remained of it clung to the riverbank like a drowned thing washed ashore: roofs caved in, streets drifted with ash, the foundry's great chimney standing alone against a sky the color of old pewter. And rising from the heart of it, visible even from the ridge, a pale glow. Not firelight. Fire flickered. This light stood still, steady as a held breath.

"It's Maro's," Rafael said quietly. "The glow. I can feel it from here." They touched the sigil at their throat — Ilsette's key, worn now on a cord against their skin, warm as a sleeping bird. "It feels like the night the stars went out. Like the wards, but... bent."

"Bent is generous." Serah checked the crossbow slung across her back, then wished she hadn't; the gesture was habit, and habits from Cael Harrow sat poorly on her these days. She had spent eleven years hunting people who carried power the way Rafael carried it. She had designed three of the containment protocols herself. Every mile they traveled together peeled another layer off that history, and underneath, always, was the same raw question she could not stop asking: *what did you think you were protecting?*

"Stay close," she said instead. "If there are soldiers—"

"There won't be soldiers." Rafael slid down beside her, and their eyes had that distant look, the one that meant they were listening to something Serah couldn't hear. "There's nothing left here to command. Can't you feel it? The whole place is..." They searched for the word and didn't find it. "Empty. But not quiet."

They left the mule hobbled among the pines and went down into Vessany on foot.

* * *

The ash lay knee-deep in the streets, gray on gray, and their footsteps made no sound in it. That was the first wrongness. The second was the bells — the famous bells of Vessany hung silent in their tower, and yet when the wind gusted, Serah heard them ring, faint and far away, though the towers themselves were cracked and empty. Reality still limped here, wounded by whatever Maro's people had done, remembering sounds the town no longer had throats to make.

Rafael walked with one hand raised slightly, palm out, the way they'd learned to feel for edges of things. "This way," they murmured, turning down a lane toward the river. "It's louder near the water."

The glow strengthened. It came up between the buildings like fog made luminous, and Serah felt the fine hairs on her arms lift, felt the old keeper's instinct rise in her — *measure the field, find the anchor, cut the line* — and hated that it rose. Some part of her would be a warden until she died. The question was whether she'd be the kind who built cages or the kind who opened them.

The choir was singing in the foundry.

They saw it through a broken wall of casement windows, and Serah stopped so abruptly that Rafael walked into her shoulder. She did not move. She could not move.

There were perhaps forty of them. Men and women both, old and young, arranged in tiers on the foundry's casting floor where molten bronze had once been poured into molds — they stood in the mold-shapes, each one cradled in a hollow of pale stone that glowed with the same steady light as the sky-glow above. They were dressed in the rotted remnants of ordinary clothes: a baker's apron, a scholar's robe, a child's coat grown too small. Their eyes were open. Their mouths moved in unison, shaping the ancient syllables of the ward-song, and their voices were the voices Serah had heard on the wind — beautiful and terrible, because there was nothing behind them. No weariness, no grief, no hope. Just the song, pouring out of them like water through a broken dam, day after day after day.

"Hollowed," Serah said. Her voice came out flat, which was how her voice went when feeling got too large to fit through it. "Gods below. He hollowed them."

"What does that—" Rafael began, and then they understood, because Rafael always understood faster than anyone Serah had ever known, and she watched the comprehension land on their face like a blow. "He drained them. He took their magic and left them singing."

"He took everything else too." Serah stepped closer to the window, forcing herself to look, forcing herself to catalog it the way she'd been trained — details, numbers, mechanisms, because understanding was the only weapon horror left you. "Look at the molds. They're ward-anchors. Old pattern — pre-temple, maybe older. Each singer holds one note of the great chord. Together they're..." She swallowed. "They're a portable ward. A moving piece of the border. He's not just using stolen power, Rafael. He's rebuilt the wards' engine out of people, and he's carrying it with him."

Forty lives, tuned like instrument strings. Serah thought of the barracks at Cael Harrow, of the doctrine she'd recited to frightened recruits: *the wards protect us all; the wards ask nothing of us.* The wards asked exactly this. They had always asked exactly this. Someone, somewhere, in some century Serah had never been permitted to read about, had stood in a room like this one and decided that forty people was the price of a border — and then everyone agreed to forget, so the song would stay pure.

Her hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against the cold sill.

"They're alive," Rafael whispered. They had come up beside her, close enough that Serah could feel the heat of them through her cloak, and their breath fogged the broken glass. "Serah — their hearts are beating. I can feel it, faintly, like — like embers under ash. They're in there somewhere."

"Don't." The word came out sharper than she intended. "Don't promise yourself that."

"It's true."

"It might be true." Serah turned to face them, and the glow of the foundry lit half their face, the scar along Rafael's jaw from the gorge, the exhaustion carved under their eyes. "And if it is, then every hour we spend looking for answers is an hour they spend singing. So we look fast. And we don't make promises we can't keep. I've kept enough of those for one lifetime."

Rafael held her gaze for a long moment. Then they nodded, once, and reached past her through the broken window — and the sigil at their throat flared, brief and gold, and every singer in the foundry faltered.

Just for a heartbeat. Forty mouths stumbled on the same syllable, forty pairs of hollow eyes turned — not toward the window, but toward Rafael, tracking something they could no longer see but still remembered how to want. Then the song caught itself and resumed, seamless, awful.

But in that heartbeat, Serah saw it: a woman in the third tier, in the rotted remains of a keeper's uniform, whose hand twitched. Whose fingers curled inward, once, like a person reaching for something in a dream.

Serah knew that uniform. She had worn it for eleven years.

* * *

They went in through the loading doors at the river side, because the front approach was open ground and Serah's nerves could not bear one more minute of watching through glass. Inside, the foundry smelled of cold metal and something sweeter and worse beneath it — the smell of the glow itself, which was like ozone and honey and old incense all at once. Up close, the molds were worse than she'd imagined: channels ran from each hollow to the next, veins of that pale stone, and through the veins the light pulsed in time with the song. The whole floor was one organ. The singers were its pipes.

"Don't touch the stone," Serah murmured. "Any of it."

"I wasn't going to."

"You touched the boundary stone at the ford."

"That was different, and you know it was different." But Rafael pulled their hand back from the nearest vein, and Serah caught the ghost of a smile on their mouth — tired, gallows-faint, but real. Even here. Especially here. She found she was grateful for it in a way that scared her more than the choir did.

They worked the tiers methodically, Rafael reading the anchors while Serah searched the singers themselves — pockets, sleeves, the space beneath the molds where a person might have hidden something before the hollowing took their hands. Most of them carried nothing. Of course they carried nothing. Maro's people would have stripped them clean.

It was the keeper who had something.

Serah found her on the third tier, in the fourth mold from the river wall — a broad woman gone gaunt, gray hair cropped short in the style Serah herself had worn until two years ago, and stitched inside the collar of her ruined uniform, a name-tape: *K. VASSER.* Serah went very still. Keeper-Captain Katrin Vasser. She had trained under her at Cael Harrow. Vasser had taught her the third containment protocol — the one Serah had later helped refine, the one that hid children like Rafael from their own names. Vasser had a wife in the hill country and a laugh like a door slamming and she had once told Serah, drunk on festival wine, *the wards are a kindness, girl, but someone has to remember what they cost.*

Someone had to remember what they cost.

"Katrin," Serah said. Her voice broke on the second syllable. She cleared her throat and tried again. "Katrin Vasser. It's Serah. Serah Dane. You drilled me on the north march, you — you said my stance was an insult to my ancestors." Nothing. The mouth sang on. The eyes looked through her at some middle distance where nothing lived anymore. "Katrin. Please."

"She can't hear you," Rafael said softly, from the tier below. There were tears on their face; Serah realized distantly that they'd been crying since they entered, silently, without any change in their work. "Or if she can, she can't answer. I'm sorry, Serah. I'm so sorry."

Serah knelt. She did not touch the stone. She let her hand hover near Vasser's shoulder, close enough to feel the strange warmth coming off the anchor, and she said the things you say to the dead even when they aren't dead: that she was sorry, that she would carry this, that if there was anything left of Katrin Vasser anywhere in the hollow house of her body, it should know that the wards were falling, all of them, everywhere, and that Serah Dane was helping to pull them down.

Vasser's hand twitched again. And this time, as Serah watched, the fingers moved deliberately — twice inward, twice outward. An old keeper's field-sign. *Come closer.*

Serah looked up at Rafael. "She's signaling me."

"Can she—"

"No. But something in her is." Serah leaned in, following the gesture: twice inward, then Vasser's fingers pressed against her own chest, over the breast pocket. Serah hesitated one heartbeat longer — every instinct she owned screaming that this was a trap, that Maro's people left bait in their machines — and then she did the thing she had decided, somewhere on the salt road, to do for the rest of her life: she trusted the person in front of her, even hollowed, even ruined, even beyond reason.

Inside the breast pocket, folded into a square the size of a coin and sewn into the lining, was a scrap of leather.

* * *

They did not open it in the foundry. Serah could not have said why — superstition, maybe, or the sense that some things should not be read in front of the dying — but she waited until they had climbed back to the ridge, until the mule was saddled and the song was a thread of sound behind them and the pale glow of Vessany sank below the tree line. Only then, sitting on a fallen log with Rafael's shoulder warm against hers and the first false stars pricking through the cloud-break overhead, did she unfold the leather.

It was a map fragment. Old — the vellum was cracked and browned, the ink faded to the color of dried blood — but drawn in the precise hand of the original ward-wrights, the hand Serah knew from the sealed archives at Cael Harrow, the hand she had been sworn to protect and had spent her whole career copying without understanding. It showed the eastern ranges. Rivers she recognized. And there, at the center of the fragment, circled twice in a different ink, newer, written by a steadier and more desperate hand:

A mountain. Mount Anew. And beneath it, in the old tongue, in letters Serah had only ever seen in the founding liturgy and had never dared translate aloud:

*Here stands the Door. Here the world was written. Let no key turn it. Let no hand claim it.*

Beneath that, in the newer ink, in trade-common, someone had added: *The key walks. Find the scribe.*

Serah read it three times. On the third reading, her hands had stopped shaking, and what replaced the shaking was colder and cleaner than fear.

"The Door is real," Rafael said. They had read it over her shoulder; of course they had. "It's not a myth. It's not temple poetry. It's under Mount Anew, and Maro knows, and he's known long enough to hunt for me by name." They laughed — one short, ugly note with no humor in it. "Ilsette gave me a key to a door I thought was a story."

"She gave you a key to the oldest lock in the world." Serah folded the leather carefully along its creases and handed it back. "And every hollowed soul in that foundry is singing the song that used to guard it. He's not building a new ward, Rafael. He's taking the old one apart and wearing it."

Below them, faint on the wind, the choir sang on. *We hold. We hold. We hold.* Except it wasn't holding anything anymore. It was being held — strung and stretched and played by a man who wanted to write the world in his own hand.

Serah stood. Her legs ached; her shoulder, still healing from Vane's blade, throbbed with her pulse; and none of it mattered, because for the first time since the stars went out, she knew exactly where they were going and exactly what was waiting there, and knowing was its own terrible fuel.

"Mount Anew," she said. "Fourteen days' hard ride, less if we cut through the Ashen Steps and don't sleep much. Maro will expect us to come — he's baiting us, probably, the whole way." She swung up into the saddle and looked down at Rafael, at the sigil glinting at their throat, at the person the whole world seemed determined to make into either a weapon or a doorstop. "So we go anyway. Faster than he thinks. And when we get there—"

"When we get there," Rafael said, swinging up behind her, arms settling around her waist, "we decide what happens. Not him. Not the temple. Us."

The mule picked its way down the north slope, toward the mountains, toward the dark. Behind them the song faded, note by hollow note, and Serah did not look back, because if she looked back she would hear Katrin Vasser's voice saying *someone has to remember what they cost*, and she could not afford to remember right now. She could only carry it.

Ahead, above the black teeth of the ranges, the false stars wheeled in patterns no sky had ever held — and one of them, low on the horizon, burned brighter than it had the night before.

Growing. Coming down.

Coming home to its mountain.
