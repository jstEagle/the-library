## 13. Stars Returning Wrong

The stars came back on the ninth night, and they were wrong.

Rafael felt it before they saw it — a prickle along the spine, the sigil at their breastbone warming like a stone pulled from a fire. They had been asleep against Serah's shoulder, wrapped in Brannoc's dead smuggler's cloak, dreaming of nothing for once, when the warmth woke them. They opened their eyes on a sky that had no business existing.

For eight nights since the Hollow Choir, the heavens above the high country had been a black wound. No stars, no moon, only a darkness so complete it made Rafael's eyes ache to look into it, as though the night itself had been erased rather than merely empty. Serah had navigated by memory and by the sigil's faint pull, which tugged them ever eastward, ever upward, toward Mount Anew. But now —

Now there were stars. Hundreds of them. Thousands. And not one of them was where a star should be.

Rafael sat up slowly, afraid that any sudden movement might shatter whatever fragile thing hung overhead. "Serah," they whispered. "Serah, wake up."

She woke the way she always did — instantly, silently, her hand already finding the knife beneath her bedroll. Her eyes went from Rafael's face to the sky, and Rafael watched something in her expression crack. Serah, who had held steady through temple fires and Vane's ambushes and the singing horror of the hollowed mages, looked up at those impossible constellations and went pale as bone.

"That's not our sky," she said.

"No."

"I know every star over Ashvale. Every one. Ward-keepers learn them because the wards were keyed to them — the old patterns, the *true* patterns." She stood, slow, her neck craned back, and Rafael saw her hands trembling at her sides. "That's the Wagon there — except it has five wheels instead of four. And that bright one due north — Rafael, there is no bright star due north. There has *never* been a bright star due north. The northern sky over Ashvale is empty on purpose. It was left empty on purpose."

"Why would it be left empty?"

"Because that's where the Door looks through." She said it flatly, the way you'd report a broken fence. Then she seemed to hear herself, and her voice dropped. "The founding warders left it blank the way you leave a page blank where a seal will go. Maro is writing in it now."

Rafael got to their feet beside her. The cold of the high meadow bit through the cloak, but they barely felt it, because the sigil was growing warmer still, and with the warmth came something else — a rising pressure behind their ribs, like a held breath that had been held for twenty years and was done being held.

They could *feel* the wrong stars. That was the terrible part. Not see — feel. Each one of them hummed at a slightly different pitch, and the pitches did not agree with one another, and the discordance scraped across Rafael's senses like a nail drawn down slate. It was Maro's handwriting, they realized. Each new star was a word in his gospel, each constellation a sentence of his design, and the whole sky was being rewritten in a hand that cared nothing for what had been written before.

"He's close," Rafael said. Their voice came out strange, resonant, as if someone else were speaking through their throat. "To the Door. He's already using it."

"Then we're close too." Serah turned to look at them, and whatever she saw in Rafael's face made her take a half-step back. "Rafael. Your eyes."

"What about my eyes?"

"They're — there's light in them. Like looking down a well at noon."

Rafael raised a hand toward their own face and stopped, because their hand was doing something worse than glowing. Light was leaking between their fingers, thin as thread, drifting upward toward the sky as if it recognized kin up there among the false stars. And the sigil — Ilsette's key, pressed into their palm as she died — was burning now, genuinely burning, and Rafael could not make themselves let go of it.

"It hurts," they said, and heard the strangeness again, layered voices, theirs and something vaster underneath. "Serah, it hurts, and it wants —"

"Wants what?"

"*Out.*" The word cracked the air. Somewhere down the meadow, a horse screamed. "It wants out, it wants *through*, it's been locked up my whole life and I'm the lock and the lock is *tired*, Serah—"

"Rafael." Serah's voice cut clean through the rising hum. Ward-keeper's voice, command voice, the voice that had once hunted people exactly like them. "Look at me. Not the sky. Me."

But Rafael couldn't. The power was cresting now, a wave building behind their sternum, and every false star overhead was a hook set into their flesh, pulling. They could feel the land around them the way they'd once felt ink under their fingers — malleable, waiting, *asking to be written*. The ridge to the west, black against the starlight, seemed to lean toward them like a listener. All it would take was a word. A gesture. A breath. They could flatten it. They could raise mountains. They could reach up and *correct* the sky, strike Maro's sentences out, write their own —

And that thought, that seductive, whispering thought, was the moment Rafael understood — truly understood, from inside — why the Temple had locked magic out of the valley. Not because magic was evil. Because this feeling was. This certainty. This rightness. The absolute conviction that the world was clay and you were its author.

*I could fix everything,* the power murmured in their blood. *I could unwrite the debt ledgers. I could unwrite the indentures. I could unwrite Kestrel Maro himself, unmake him before he ever marched, and no one would ever have suffered, and it would be so easy—*

"Rafael!" Serah caught their face in both hands and forced their gaze down from the sky, down to her — gray-eyed, scarred, human, terrified. "Listen to me. Whatever it's promising you — it lies. Do you hear me? It lies the way Vane lies. It offers you the world and hands you a leash."

"The ledgers," Rafael gasped. "I could burn every ledger. Every debt. Every child bound to a scriptorium bench—"

"And then what? You'd be the Temple, Rafael. You'd be worse than the Temple. You'd be Maro with a kinder face." Her thumbs pressed hard against their cheekbones. "You told me once — at the ford, after — you told me the worst thing the Temple ever did wasn't the wards. It was deciding other people's lives for them, for their own good. Is this different? Is this *anything* different?"

The words landed like cold water. Rafael shuddered. The wave inside them crested higher, higher, and the sigil shrieked heat against their skin, and somewhere in their body a decision was trying to be made—

It came out sideways. It always comes out sideways, when a thing is too large for the vessel holding it.

Rafael screamed — or laughed, they never knew which — and the power discharged.

The western ridge simply ceased. There was no explosion, no drama of falling stone; the hillside unfolded like a hand opening, rock and pine and forty years of soil spreading outward into the valley below in a long silent sigh, a soft grey bloom of dust catching the starlight. The sound arrived late, a deep rolling *whump* that Rafael felt in their teeth. Where the ridge had stood there was now a low shoulder of earth, smooth as a healed scar.

Silence followed. Real silence, total, even the horses gone quiet.

Rafael stood at the center of it, shaking, emptied and yet somehow fuller than before, the sigil cooling against their chest. They looked at their own hands as if they belonged to a stranger. "I killed it," they said numbly. "A mountain. I just — it asked me to, Serah. It *asked.*"

"The land doesn't ask. It agrees." Serah hadn't stepped away. That was the thing Rafael would remember afterward, the thing they would carry: she had not stepped away. She stood in the dust-fall with grey powder settling on her shoulders and hair, and she took Rafael's burning hands in her scarred ones, and she held on. "Breathe. Just breathe. In through your nose. That's it."

"I'm dangerous."

"You are." No flinching, no softening. She had never lied to them since the ford, and she didn't lie now. "You are the most dangerous thing in this valley, maybe in this country. And you just leveled a ridge instead of leveling the sky, which means somewhere in all that fire, you chose. Badly aimed, badly timed — but chosen. That matters."

"I wanted to rewrite the stars. I wanted to *fix* them."

"I know. I watched your face while you wanted it." Her grip tightened. "Here's what I know about wanting to fix things, Rafael. I spent fifteen years fixing people. Finding children like you, marking them, arranging their whole lives around a secret they weren't allowed to know. And every single time, I told myself it was protection. It was always protection." Her voice frayed. "It was never protection. It was control wearing protection's clothes. What you felt tonight — that's what I felt every day of my ward-keeper's life, and I fed on it for fifteen years before I understood what I was."

Rafael looked at her. Dust in her lashes. The old scar through her eyebrow that they had traced once, shyly, by firelight, and she had let them. The woman who had designed the cage they were born in, standing here holding their hands while their power smoked off them like frost in sunlight.

"You're not her anymore," Rafael said.

"I'm exactly her. That's the point. I'm her, and I'm telling you what she learned too late." Serah lifted one of their hands and pressed it flat against her own sternum, over her heart, the way you'd gentle a spooked animal. "Feel that? Heartbeat. Ordinary. Mortal. That's your anchor, not the sigil, not the sky. When it gets loud in your head — you come back to this. To me. Can you do that?"

"Yes." It came out steadier than they expected. "Yes. I can do that."

"Say it properly."

"If it gets loud," Rafael said, "I come back to you."

Something moved through Serah's face then — relief, and grief, and underneath both a tenderness she usually kept armored over like a breastplate. She didn't let go of their hand. Neither of them moved to break the contact. The dust was still settling over the ruined ridge, fine as flour, gilding them both in the light of stars that had no right to exist.

"We should move," Serah said finally, without moving. "Light like that carries. Someone will have seen the ridge fall."

"Let them see."

"Rafael—"

"No, I mean—" They laughed, ragged and real. "I mean, let whoever Maro sends come and find us. Let him see what his stolen sky bought him. I'm done running like prey, Serah. I ran from the temple, I ran from Vane, I ran from my own blood. But I'm the lock. And locks don't run. Locks *decide*."

Serah studied them for a long moment. Then, slowly, she smiled — the rare one, the true one, the one that transformed her whole severe face into something young. "There you are," she said softly. "There's the person who's going to shatter a Door."

"You believe I can?"

"I believe you're the only one who's been offered the world twice in one night and refused both times." She released their hands at last, only to pull the cloak straighter around their shoulders — a small, domestic gesture, absurd and precious amid the devastation. "Come on. We'll walk while it's dark. Or while it's whatever this is." She gestured at the impossible constellations with distaste.

They struck camp together, quickly, the practiced choreography of two people who had been sharing danger long enough to share silence comfortably. As Rafael kicked dirt over the fire's embers, they glanced up one last time at the rewritten sky — and froze.

The blank place in the north was no longer entirely blank.

At its center, faint as breath on glass, a new pattern was forming: a single line of stars, straight and deliberate, descending toward the horizon. Toward Mount Anew. Toward the Door.

It looked, Rafael thought with a chill that had nothing to do with the cold, like a staircase.

Like an invitation written in someone else's hand, in someone else's sky — and addressed, unmistakably, to them.

"Serah," they said quietly. "He knows we're coming. He's leaving the door open."

Serah followed their gaze upward, and Rafael heard her breath catch. When she spoke, her voice was grim and level and utterly certain.

"Then we'd better arrive before he finishes writing."
