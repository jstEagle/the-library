## 15. Maro's Gospel

They took Rafael's boots first.

That was the detail that stayed with them afterward — not the hood, not the rope at the wrists, not the long jolting ride in a wagon that smelled of tar and old blood, but the moment a soldier with careful hands knelt in the mud and unlaced their boots and set them aside like something borrowed. As if even theft, in Kestrel Maro's army, was conducted according to a proper procedure.

The hood came off in a tent that was not a tent.

Rafael had expected canvas and camp-stink, but the space they were pushed into was vast and wrong, its walls hung with silk that rippled though there was no wind, its floor paved in slabs of white stone that belonged to no quarry Rafael knew. Candles burned without melting. Somewhere beyond the silk, an army breathed — ten thousand men and women, the sound of them like surf against a cliff — and yet inside this pavilion it was so quiet Rafael could hear their own pulse ticking in their ears.

And in the center of all that silence sat a man at a plain wooden desk, writing.

Kestrel Maro did not look up when they entered. He was not what Rafael had imagined, which was itself a kind of shock. They had pictured a warlord out of the temple's cautionary histories: huge, scarred, voice like a landslide. Instead there was a lean man in undyed wool, gray at the temples, spectacles perched on his nose, ink to the second knuckle. He might have been the master of any scriptorium in Ashvale. He might have been Rafael's superior, hunched over some tedious ledger, and the recognition of that — the awful kinship of it — made Rafael's stomach turn before he'd said a single word.

"Sit," Maro said pleasantly, still writing. "There is tea. It's terrible. I confiscated it from a merchant who was watering his stock, so we shall both suffer together."

A chair stood waiting. Rafael did not sit. The soldier behind them pressed gently on their shoulder, and after a moment — because their legs were shaking, because defiance needs a body that can hold it upright — Rafael sat.

Maro finished his line. Blotted it. Set down the pen with the exact care of a man who loved his tools, removed his spectacles, and looked at Rafael for the first time.

His eyes were brown. Ordinary brown. That was the worst thing about him.

"You're smaller than I expected," he said. "Forgive me. That sounds like disappointment, and I mean it as relief. Great power wears poorly on large frames, in my experience. It has too far to travel." He gestured at the sigil-shaped absence at Rafael's throat — they had hidden it beneath their shirt, sewn into the lining weeks ago, but his gaze went to it as surely as if it hung naked on a chain. "You keep it close to the skin. Good instinct. It should be close to the skin."

"How do you know what I—"

"Mother Ilsette wrote to me for eleven years." Maro said it mildly, folding his hands. "Did you know that? Long letters. She was a brilliant woman and a coward, which is the most common combination in history. She told me about a foundling left on the temple steps with a birthmark shaped like nothing. About the ledgers that never balanced around you. About how the wards sang differently on the nights you dreamed badly." He smiled, small and genuine, and Rafael hated how much it looked like kindness. "She begged me to leave the valley alone, you know. Right up until the end. She thought if she gave me enough information about you, I would find the information sufficient and spare her walls."

The candles guttered. Or perhaps they didn't; perhaps it was only Rafael's vision swimming.

"She died," Rafael said. Their voice came out flat as a ledger line. "In the fire. Holding my hand."

"I know. I'm sorry. Truly." And the terrible thing was that he sounded sorry. "Ilsette and I disagreed about everything except one principle: that suffering should be *organized*. She believed the Temple's ledgers accomplished it. Debts recorded, penances scheduled, grace dispensed in measured portions. I merely propose to do the same work at scale."

"At scale," Rafael repeated.

"Come. Look." Maro rose, and two guards moved to flank Rafael instantly, but Maro waved them back with a flicker of irritation. "If I wanted you restrained, child, you would be restrained. What I want requires your *consent*, which is why we are having tea instead of surgery."

He drew back a fold of silk, and behind it the wall was window — real glass, real night — and beyond the glass the land fell away in terraces toward a city burning softly in the distance, and above the city, the sky.

The stars were wrong. Rafael had seen them returning wrong for days now, constellations shuffled like a cheated deck, but here, near the Door's gravity, they were worse than wrong. They were *arranged*. Lines of light ran between stars like ruled margins on a page, and whole regions of sky had been blanked to clean white emptiness, awaiting text.

"Do you know what the old warders found, when they finally climbed high enough to study the first-light seam?" Maro asked conversationally. "They found that reality is *writable*. That the world is a draft. Every miracle, every horror, every law of nature — someone drafted it once, and it could be amended." He tapped the glass. "The founders of Ashvale understood this, and it terrified them, so they built their little valley and locked the door and called their fear *piety*. The Temple inherited the lock and called it *tradition*. And meanwhile the world outside went on bleeding — plague, famine, warlord after warlord after warlord, each one certain he alone saw clearly. Unregulated creation, Rafael. It is a *mess*."

"And you're the editor," Rafael said.

"I'm the *proofreader*," Maro corrected, with real feeling. "I have spent nine years collecting the hollowed choirs, the drained mages, the stolen ward-song — not to hoard power, whatever my enemies sing about me in their taverns, but to prepare a single, final, *careful* revision. A world warded shut under one hand. No more random magic burning villages. No more plagues arriving uninvited. Suffering, curated. Grief, rationed. Every child born into a sky that has already been proofread." He turned from the window, and his ordinary eyes were bright with something that in a holier man would have been called faith. "I will be the last tyrant, and then there will be no tyrants, because tyranny requires alternatives."

Rafael thought of Serah's face at the ford. Of Brannoc laughing with his smugglers under a lawless sky. Of the valley's founding myth, the one every indentured child learned: *magic cannot follow its people inside the wards.* All those centuries of safety, purchased with a lie and paid for by children like Rafael, who *were* the lock and were never told.

"It won't hold," Rafael said quietly.

"Excuse me?"

"Your revision. It won't hold. Not because you're not clever enough — you are, obviously, horribly — but because a warded world needs a living key, and keys are people, and people die, or run, or fall in love with the wrong person, or read the ledger too closely." Rafael's hands had stopped shaking. That surprised them. "You don't want a proofread world, Kestrel Maro. You want a world where no one can ever again do to you what was done to you. I've copied enough debt ledgers to know what that grief looks like. It looks exactly like yours."

For a long moment the warlord said nothing. The silk rippled. Far away, the burning city glowed.

Then Maro laughed — softly, delightedly, like a man hearing his own argument returned to him improved.

"Oh, Ilsette taught you *nothing*," he said. "You arrived at that yourself. Do you understand what you just did? You read me in four sentences. That is the gift. That is precisely the gift." He crossed the room and, to Rafael's astonishment, pulled the second chair around and sat facing them, knee to knee, intimate as a confessor. "So hear my true offer. Not surrender. *Authorship.*"

"No—"

"The Door will need a key that chooses. That is its design, and I cannot amend the design without opening it, and I cannot open it without the key. So the key must choose me — or rather—" his smile widened, patient as erosion "—the key must choose *with* me. Co-author. Not my prisoner, not my instrument. My colleague. You would sit beside me while we write the next age of the world, and your name would be in the margin of every page. Vasquez. The scribe who helped write the sky." He leaned closer. "Think of what you could *amend*, Rafael. Your indenture — gone, retroactively, so that you were never bound at all. Ilsette — alive again in the text, if you want her, though I'd advise against sloppy retcons. Serah's shame — unwritten. Every debt in every ledger in the valley, struck through in your own hand. You have spent your whole life copying other people's accounts. I am offering you the pen."

And there it was. The temptation did not arrive like a storm; it arrived like warmth, like the sigil's heat against Rafael's sternum, like the memory of every cold night in the scriptorium counting years off a sentence they hadn't chosen. *Never bound at all.* Rafael closed their eyes and let themselves want it, honestly, completely — let the wanting fill them the way floodwater fills a cellar — because Serah had taught them that power refused is power you don't understand, and power unexamined is power that owns you.

They saw it: the valley, rewritten gentle. Serah's confession at the ford smoothed into innocence. No Vane, no fire, no choir of hollowed singers. A clean page.

And on that clean page, no one would ever again choose anything that hadn't been proofread. No smuggler's lawless mercy. No reckless, borrowed magic keeping a dying woman breathing. No marker stone that said *choose*. Just Maro's tidy margins, forever, and Rafael's name in them — complicit, comfortable, and dead in every way that mattered.

Rafael opened their eyes.

"No," they said.

Maro sighed, and for just a heartbeat the mask slipped and Rafael saw beneath it: not a monster, but a tired man who had rehearsed this refusal many times and dreaded it every time. "May I ask why? Genuinely. I collect reasons."

"Because you're offering me the pen," Rafael said, "and the first thing I'd want to write with it is *him, gone* — and the second thing is *me, forgiven* — and by the third page I'd be you." They stood. Their legs held. "Suffering isn't a typo, Maro. It's the cost of the story being written by everyone who lives in it. Including the parts that burn."

The warlord regarded them for a long moment. Then he put his spectacles back on, and the scholar returned, and the scholar said, regretfully:

"Take them to the pavilion. We proceed at dawn."

Guards took Rafael's arms. They did not resist; there was nowhere to resist *to*, and some cold scribal part of their mind was already recording details — the layout of the camp, the number of steps to the silk wall, the fact that Maro's soldiers kept glancing northward at the wrong stars with expressions that were not devotion.

They were halfway across the white stone floor when the singing started.

It began at the edge of the camp, thin and ragged — a human voice, then ten, then hundreds, and Rafael knew the melody the way they knew their own heartbeat, because they had heard it falling from the burning temple, because it was the wards' old song, the song of the hollowed choir. But this was not the drained and ghostly version. This was sung by whole throats, angry and alive, and it rolled over Maro's perfect camp like weather.

"What is—" a guard began.

The first fire-arrow arced over the tents, and then the sky was full of them.

* * *

Chaos has a grammar, and Rafael had spent a lifetime learning to read.

The uprising hit the camp's eastern edge first — smugglers, farmers, deserters, faces Rafael half-knew from the salt road, armed with billhooks and fury and, here and there, small ugly bursts of wild magic that made the new-made stars shudder overhead. Maro's soldiers, drilled for armies, were not drilled for their own countrymen singing the wards' song while they died. The line bent. The line broke. Tents caught, and the silk walls of the great pavilion filled with orange light, and somewhere in the smoke a voice was bellowing orders in Maro's calm, reasonable tone, and no one was listening anymore.

The guard holding Rafael's right arm took a smuggler's sling-stone across the temple and folded. The other guard turned toward the sound, and Rafael did the thing Serah had drilled into them across a hundred cold miles: they dropped their weight, twisted against the grip, and drove the heel of their bare foot down onto the man's instep. He swore and loosed them, and Rafael ran — barefoot, bootless, ridiculous — straight into the smoke.

The sigil woke against their chest like a second heart.

Heat poured through them, and the fire parted — actually *parted*, curling away from Rafael's path as if the flames had consulted a ledger and found no entry under their name. Wild magic, unbound, answering the key that had held the world's oldest ward. Rafael didn't command it. They barely dared breathe near it. They simply ran, and the burning camp made an exception of them.

They burst out of the pavilion's far side into a scene from a fever: soldiers and smugglers tangled in the mud, the wrong constellations wheeling overhead, and the mountain — Mount Anew — black against the ruined sky, wearing its crown of impossible light.

And cutting through the melee toward them, sword drawn, coat scorched, moving with the grim economy Rafael would have known anywhere in the world —

Serah.

"You," she gasped, catching their arm, checking them for wounds with rough, frantic hands, "you absolute *scribe*, I told you not to let them take you—"

"You followed me into a war camp."

"You were *taken*. There's a difference." Her eyes were wet and furious and alive. Behind her, the uprising surged; ahead, the mountain waited, and the Door beneath it, and the last pages of everything. She gripped Rafael's hand hard enough to hurt. "Can you run?"

Rafael looked back once — at the burning pavilion, at the small figure in undyed wool standing untouched amid the flames, watching them go with his ordinary brown eyes and making, unmistakably, a note.

"Yes," Rafael said, and ran.
