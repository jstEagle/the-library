## 16. The Ascent

The mountain did not want them climbing it.

Serah had known mountains all her life — the gray shoulders of the Ashvale range, the passes she'd patrolled in winter with frost in her eyelashes and a ward-lantern swinging at her hip. Mountains were honest things. They rose because stone rose. They killed you with weather and gravity, plainly, without malice.

Mount Anew lied.

From the valley floor it had looked like any other peak, dark with pine and scarred with old snowfields. But as they climbed past the tree line, the lies began. The path ahead would bend around a granite spur, and when they rounded it, the path was behind them. Rafael's footprints in the scree sometimes ran uphill beside their own, sometimes downhill, once — Serah made herself look twice to be sure — sideways across a slope that had no business holding them.

"Stop counting switchbacks," she said, when she caught Rafael mouthing numbers under their breath.

"It helps."

"It helps until the number changes." She adjusted the strap of her pack, and hissed through her teeth. The wound Vane's blade had left along her ribs had closed into an angry seam, but every twist of the trail pulled at it like a hook. "This place doesn't want arithmetic. It wants something else from us."

Rafael looked up at her — that steady, ink-stained gaze that had undone more of her certainties than any battle ever had. "What does it want?"

"Honesty," Serah said, before she could stop herself. And then, because the mountain seemed to lean in and listen: "Or maybe just everything we've buried. I don't know yet. Keep walking."

* * *

They made camp that first night in the lee of a boulder field, and Serah dreamed of the training yard at the Warders' Hall, which should have been nothing — a memory so ordinary it barely qualified as one. Children in gray practice coats. Wooden staves. Her own voice, younger, calling corrections.

She woke with frost on her lips though the night was mild, and found Rafael already awake, sitting with their knees drawn up, watching the sky.

"The stars are wrong again," Rafael said quietly.

Above them, where the constellations should have wheeled in their ancient patterns, the stars had rearranged themselves into lines. Columns. Rows of light like script on a page too far away to read. Maro's rewrite, spreading outward from whatever he was doing beneath this mountain, writing the heavens over.

"How long do we have?" Rafael asked.

Serah didn't answer right away. She pressed her palm flat against the ground instead, feeling for what she'd been trained, long ago, to feel: the hum of anchored reality, the deep note the wards had sung beneath everything. There was no hum here. There was a vibration, yes, but wrong — a plucked string still shuddering, a note held too long by someone who didn't know when to stop.

"I don't know," she said. "Days. Maybe less. He's not building anymore, Rafael. He's *editing*. And edits go faster than foundations."

Rafael nodded slowly. In the starlight their face looked carved, older than their years. "Then we climb faster too."

* * *

The second day, the mountain began showing them things.

It started small. A smell of bread baking where there was no village. A snatch of song — a temple hymn, the one about the valley's sheltering arms — drifting down the wind with no source. Rafael stopped dead on the trail and said, "Did you hear—"

"Yes." Serah's hand went to her knife out of pure reflex, then dropped. "It's not real. Or—" she corrected herself, because correcting herself had become necessary on this mountain, "—it's real somewhere. Just not here. Not now."

By midday it wasn't smells and songs anymore. It was places.

They came around a switchback and the trail opened onto a courtyard Serah knew intimately: the Warders' Hall, its flagstones cracked exactly as she remembered, its bell tower leaning two degrees east from the quake of thirty years past. Except the Hall stood three hundred miles behind them and had burned in Maro's purge besides. Rafael walked straight through its archway as if it were fog, and it rippled, and they emerged on the other side onto bare rock, blinking.

"It showed me the scriptorium," Rafael said. Their voice was careful, the way it got when they were holding something fragile. "My desk. My ledger. It was — warm. Like someone had just left."

"What did you do?"

"I sat down." A shaky breath. "For a minute. Then I remembered it was a lie and got up."

Serah took their hand. She was not, by nature or training, a person who reached for hands; she had spent twenty years keeping hers free for weapons. But she took it now, and felt the fine tremor running through Rafael's fingers, and understood that the mountain was not merely showing illusions. It was *testing*. Offering each climber the thing they most grieved, and watching to see whether they chose the grief or the path.

"Whatever it shows you," she said, "you come back to me. That's the whole rule. You can look. You can even want it. But you come back."

"And if it shows me you?" Rafael asked softly. "A version of you that never hunted anyone? That I could love without — without all of it in between?"

Serah had no answer for that. So she squeezed their hand and kept climbing, and the question climbed with them.

* * *

On the third day, the mountain turned to her.

She should have expected it. She had spent the first two days half-dreading, half-hoping it would pass her by — as if the mountain might judge her memories too common, too grim, not worth conjuring. But Mount Anew was thorough. It had been built, according to the oldest texts, as a place where the world's first ward was written, and wards were nothing but memory given structure. Of course it dealt in memory. Of course it collected debts.

The first vision caught her at dawn, on a narrow shelf of trail above a drop of several hundred feet. One moment she was walking; the next she was standing in a border village called Merrow's Ford, and it was eleven years ago, and she was twenty-four years old and certain of everything.

"Serah." Rafael's voice, distant, coming through the vision like light through water. "Serah, your eyes are open but you're not here."

She was here. She was entirely here. She was standing in the square of Merrow's Ford while a woman named Odile Brant argued with the ward-assessment team, and Serah — young Serah, uniform crisp, ward-sight bright in her eyes — was reading the readings off her slate.

*"Anomalous resonance, origin within the household,"* her younger self recited. *"Pattern consistent with unregistered talent."*

"She's a child," Odile Brant was saying. "She's seven years old. Whatever she is, she's mine, and we keep our own."

"The law keeps its own," young Serah said, and the words came out of her mouth with the smooth certainty of doctrine, and Serah-in-the-present wanted to scream at her, wanted to shake that confident girl by the shoulders and say *look at her face, look at the mother's face, look at what you're about to do.*

They took the child that afternoon. Serah remembered the child's name — Wren, Wren Brant — because she had made herself remember every name, back then, as a kind of professional pride. She remembered the mother's scream following them down the road for a mile. She remembered telling herself, that night, that the wards protected everyone, that untrained magic was a wildfire, that the girl would be raised safely in a Hall school and thank them someday.

Wren Brant had died at fifteen, in a Hall facility whose records Serah had later read with shaking hands. *Resonance failure during containment exercise.*

"Serah." Rafael's hand on her arm, solid, present. The village dissolved like sugar in rain. They were back on the trail, wind in the scree, and Serah realized she was crying — not sobbing, just leaking, tears running freely down her face as if the mountain had simply opened a valve.

"I'm fine," she said automatically.

"You're not."

"No," she admitted. "I'm not. And it's going to get worse. This is how it works, isn't it — it doesn't show you the pretty ones first. It shows you the ones you've built walls around, and it takes the walls down one at a time."

Rafael studied her. Then, gently: "You don't have to hide them from me. Whatever it shows. I'd rather see the truth than watch you carry it alone."

"You say that now."

"I'll say it after, too. That's rather the point of me."

Despite everything — the wound pulling at her ribs, the dread pooling cold in her stomach — Serah almost smiled. "Come on, scribe. We're losing daylight."

* * *

The second vision came at noon, and it was worse, because it was not a memory of cruelty. It was a memory of cowardice.

She was in the Hall archive, eight years ago, cross-referencing ward ledgers against intake registries — a routine audit, or what passed for routine in the hidden machinery of the Temple. And she found it. The pattern. Children taken for "anomalous resonance" whose names appeared nowhere in any ledger afterward. Children whose resonance signatures matched, with eerie precision, the harmonic profile of the valley's border wards themselves.

Living keys. Living locks. Children harvested, generation after generation, to hold magic out of Ashvale — and Rafael's signature, when she finally traced it, glowed at the center of the pattern like a brand.

She had found it. She had *known.* And she had closed the ledger, reported only the surface anomaly to her superiors, and told no one.

Because telling would have meant becoming the kind of ward-keeper who burned. Because the wards held, and the valley was safe, and safe was worth anything — wasn't it? Wasn't that the entire catechism?

The archive faded. The trail returned. Rafael was in front of her this time, having stopped, having turned, and their expression was not horror. It was something quieter and harder to bear: recognition.

"That's the night you decided," Rafael said. "Isn't it. When the wards fell — you weren't surprised. You'd known for years that they were fed on children. You just hadn't let yourself finish knowing."

"Yes." Her voice came out flat as slate. "There it is. The worst of me. Not the raids — those I could tell myself were duty. This. I found the truth and I folded it away like a letter I didn't want to answer. Eight years. Every debt ledger you copied, every year of your indenture — I could have ended it with one report. One act of courage. And I chose my career."

The wind moved through the rocks. Somewhere below them, reality shivered — a patch of hillside flickering between grass and ash, unable to decide.

Rafael stepped closer. "I'm not going to tell you it's forgiven, because we did that at the ford, and forgiveness isn't a thing you say once. It's a thing you keep choosing." They took her hand and pressed it flat against their own chest, over the heartbeat. "But listen to me, Serah. The woman who closed that ledger isn't the woman standing here. The woman standing here has been shot, cut, hunted, and has *still* walked toward the thing that frightens her most, carrying me. That's also true. Both are true. The mountain only shows one."

"The mountain shows the truth."

"So do I." Rafael's eyes held hers, fierce and clear. "I am a scribe. Truth is my trade. And the truth is that you're here."

Serah breathed. Once. Twice. The wound in her ribs ached in time with her pulse, and she let it ache, because pain was honest and the mountain was not.

"All right," she said. "You've seen the worst ones now. The raid, the ledger. Whatever else it digs up will be smaller."

It was not smaller.

* * *

The third vision came at dusk, and it was the smallest of all, and it broke her open completely.

A room in the Warders' Hall. Her own quarters, age nineteen. A girl named Cass — fellow cadet, quick laugh, a scar through one eyebrow from a training accident — sitting on her bunk, saying, *come away with me. There's a ship out of Salt Harbor, Brannoc's cousin runs cargo, we could be gone before the winter assessments. You're wasted here, Serah. You're better than this work.*

And Serah, nineteen, wanting it so badly her teeth ached — and saying no. Saying *the wards need people who understand them.* Saying *duty,* that beautiful, terrible word, and watching the light go out of Cass's face, and letting her walk out of the room, and never asking the Hall what happened to her afterward, because asking would have meant admitting what she'd chosen.

Cass had died six years later in a smuggler's raid, according to the incident reports Serah finally read last month, huddled by a fire with Rafael asleep beside her. Shot by ward-keepers. Possibly by people Serah had trained.

The quarters dissolved. The trail returned, silvered with early starlight — the wrong stars, the scripted stars — and Serah found she couldn't move. Her legs simply declined. She sat down on the cold rock with her back against a boulder and put her head in her hands, and this time she didn't say *I'm fine,* because the word had lost all meaning.

Rafael sat down beside her. Didn't touch her, at first. Just sat, shoulder near enough to feel warmth, and waited, the way they waited in the scriptorium for ink to dry — patient as paper.

"My whole life," Serah said finally, to her own knees, "I thought strength was carrying things alone. That was the lesson of the Hall. You take the weight and you don't set it down and you certainly don't hand it to someone else, because that's weakness." She laughed, and it came out broken. "And now I'm sitting on a haunted mountain that's making me hand you every weight I own, one at a time, and I keep waiting to feel weaker, and I don't. I feel — lighter. Which is its own kind of horrifying."

"Why horrifying?"

"Because if this is what strength actually is, then everything I was taught was backwards. Twenty years, backwards."

Rafael leaned their head against her shoulder, carefully, minding the wound. "Maybe that's why the mountain does it. Maybe it's not testing us. Maybe it's *unpacking* us. Stripping everything down to what's true before we reach the Door." A pause. "You can't write on a page that's already full, Serah. Maybe it's clearing ours."

She turned that over. Scribe logic. She was learning to think in it.

"Show me the rest, then," she said. "If it has more. I'd rather meet them on my feet than have them ambush me at the summit."

"There might not be more."

"There's always more. That's what a life is."

* * *

They climbed through the night, because waiting felt more dangerous than exhaustion. The fraying worsened with every switchback, exactly as the Hollow Choir's map fragment had promised — *as the Door draws near, the world forgets its shape.* Whole slopes would stutter between seasons, snow flashing to wildflowers and back. Once they crossed a stream that flowed uphill, laughing soundlessly, and neither of them remarked on it, because remarking had become pointless.

At what might have been midnight, the mountain showed Serah one final thing, and it was not a memory at all.

It was a possibility.

She stood on the summit ridge — a summit they had not yet reached — and watched a version of herself kneel before the Door of Anew with the sigil in her hand, having taken it from Rafael, having decided that *she* should be the one to choose, because Rafael was too good, too trusting, too easily corrupted by offers of power, and she — she was strong enough to refuse, wasn't she, she had refused everything her whole life—

The vision-self looked up at her with her own eyes and said: *Is that what you believe? That taking it would be strength?*

And Serah, standing on the night trail with Rafael's breathing steady beside her, answered aloud, to the empty air:

"No. Refusing it is the strength. I learned that watching them." She nodded at Rafael, asleep against a rock, exhausted, ink-stained fingers curled loosely around the sigil that hung at their throat. "Every hard thing I ever did, I did alone, and it made me a wall. Everything brave I've ever seen, they did with their heart open. That's the difference. That's the whole difference."

The vision-self smiled — sadly, or perhaps approvingly — and unraveled into starlight that drifted up to join the false constellations, and for just a moment, one wrong star flickered and burned true.

Serah woke Rafael an hour before dawn. The summit was close now; they could see it against the graying sky, a black crown of rock — and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, a seam of light in the mountainside, gold-white, pulsing slow as a sleeping heartbeat.

The Door.

And on the ridgeline above it, tiny with distance but horribly distinct, torches. Dozens of them. An army encamped at the threshold, black banners stirring in the wind, and among the banners, catching the torchlight, a gleam of gold.

Maro had arrived first.

Serah's hand found Rafael's in the dark. "Whatever happens up there," she said, "we climb it together. Promise me."

Rafael looked at the torches, then at her, and gripped her hand hard.

"Together," they said. "All the way to the writing on the world."
