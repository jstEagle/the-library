## 1. The Charm-Seller's Silence

The rain in Ashvale didn't fall so much as seep, the way guilt seeps through a floorboard into the room below. It came off the harbor gray and thin, and by the time it reached Charm Row it had soaked into everything—stone, wool, bone. Rafael Pemberton had lived fifteen years under that rain, and he had learned its first lesson well: nothing stays hidden that isn't kept dry.

His shop sat between a chandler's and a shop that sold, inexplicably, only buttons. The sign above his door read PEMBERTON'S CURIOSITIES & TRINKETS in letters that had once been gold and were now the color of old teeth. Inside, the shelves held charms against headache, charms for luck at cards, charms to keep moths from wool and husbands from taverns. Every one of them was worthless. He made them himself from river clay and chicken bones and thread bought in bulk, and he charged three coppers apiece, and the people of Ashvale bought them because hope was cheaper than medicine and easier than prayer.

That was the trick of it. Sell people something that does nothing, and no warden will ever look twice. A man who sells real magic gets burned. A man who sells clay beads shaped like acorns is just a merchant with a soft heart and a hard margin.

Rafael knew both trades. He'd only ever been allowed to practice one.

He was arranging a tray of protection knots—he tied them left over right, never right over left, because wardens had learned that covenant-knotwork went the other way—when the rats started talking.

"Man comes," said the big female who lived behind the flour sacks next door. She had a voice like dry leaves being crushed slowly. "Boots. Two pair. Gray. Walking slow."

Rafael's hands did not stop moving. Fifteen years of practice lived in his fingers: they could tie a knot, weigh a charm, smile at a customer while every hair on his arms stood up. But inside, everything went quiet and cold, the way it always did when the gift spoke.

Gray boots meant wardens. Wardens walking slow meant they were looking.

"How many doors down?" he asked—the words barely breath, shaped toward the wall where she nested.

"Chandler's," she said. "They stop. They look at his sign. They laugh."

Wardens laughing was better than wardens writing, but not much. He set the knot down, wiped his palms on his apron, and did what he always did: he became boring. He straightened trays. He dusted glass that wasn't dirty. He arranged himself behind the counter in the posture of a man whose whole soul fit inside his ledger.

The bell over the door rang anyway.

There were two of them, which was standard—a senior and a junior, the junior carrying the inspection ledger the way an altar boy carries a censer, with reverence and terror in equal measure. The senior was a woman with a face like a closed door and a badge polished bright enough to signal ships. Rafael had seen her before. Warden Vess. She had inspected him twice in five years and found nothing either time, because there was nothing to find, and somehow that was worse than if she'd found something. Suspicion fed on absence the way mold fed on damp.

"Pemberton." She said his name like a diagnosis.

"Morning, Warden." He gave her the smile he kept for officials—warm but not too warm, deferential but not groveling. It had taken him years to calibrate. "Rain's come early."

"Rain comes when it comes." She walked the aisle slowly, trailing one gloved finger along his shelves. The junior stood by the door, ledger open, quill ready. "New stock?"

"Protection knots. River clay. Local work." He tapped the tray. "Three coppers. Keeps moths out of wool."

"Does it?"

"Customers say so." Which was true, in the way that all placebos are true. "Can't argue with results."

Vess picked up a knot and turned it over. Her glove rasped against the clay. Rafael watched her with the part of his mind that wasn't busy keeping his heartbeat level, and that part noted, distantly, that the mice in the walls had gone silent, listening through him, waiting to see what he would need.

He sent them a thought—not words exactly, more a shape of stillness—and they obeyed. That was the cruelty of the gift. It never asked whether he wanted company. It simply opened, the way a wound opens, and everything with blood and hunger poured its small voice in.

* * *

Here is what Rafael Pemberton could do, and what no living soul in Ashvale could know he could do:

He could talk to beasts.

Not command them—that was the stuff of fireside stories, the kind of story that got children taken away for repeating it. No. He could speak with them, and they with him, in a language older than the city, older than the kingdom, older than the word *kingdom*. The covenant-tongue, his teacher had called it, back when Rafael had been nine years old and stupid with wonder. The language the world made when the world agreed to be understood.

And here is what the law said about it: Article Seven of the Ashvale Codex, posted on every gate and church door in letters tall enough to read from horseback. *The practice of beast-speech is sorcery. Sorcery is death.* Not imprisonment, not fines. Death, by hanging, in Gallow Square, with the body left up for three days as instruction.

Fifteen years ago, Rafael had watched a woman hang for it. He hadn't known her name. He'd stood in the crowd with his cap in his hands like everyone else, and the woman had looked out over their heads as the rope tightened, and her lips had moved, and Rafael—only Rafael, in a crowd of four hundred—had heard what she said.

She'd said goodbye to the crows on the gallows beam. And the crows had answered.

He had gone home and put his hands in a bucket of ice water until the feeling in them matched the feeling in his chest, and the next morning he'd opened a shop selling nothing, and he had not spoken a single word of the covenant-tongue aloud since.

Almost none. Almost never. There were nights, some nights, when the loneliness got so large it pressed against his ribs like water against a dam, and he would whisper one word to the mice just to hear something answer. One word. A sin the size of a coin, spent slow so it would last.

* * *

Vess set the knot down without buying it.

"You've been quiet lately, Pemberton."

"I'm always quiet."

"That's what I mean." She leaned on the counter, and the wood creaked under her weight of office. "Quiet men worry me. Loud men, I know what they want. Quiet men are hiding something, or they're empty, and I haven't decided which you are."

"Mostly empty," Rafael said. "It's the trade secret."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite humor; wardens weren't issued humor—but close enough that he let himself breathe. Behind her, the junior warden wrote something in the ledger. Rafael caught the motion and felt it in his stomach like a dropped stone. Whatever went in that book stayed in the book, and the book went to the Inquisitor General, and the Inquisitor General—

Well. Nobody in the charm quarter talked about Corvane above a whisper, and most didn't whisper.

"Ledger says your supplier changed last spring," Vess said. "Clay from the eastern kilns now. Why?"

"The western pit flooded. You can check."

"I do check. That's my job." She straightened, and her eyes went around the shop one more time—shelves, counter, the curtain over the back room where Rafael slept among crates of unsold inventory. "Your back room. Mind if I look?"

Every plan he had ever made, every careful year, narrowed to this moment, and Rafael discovered that fear, fully matured, doesn't feel like panic. It feels like arithmetic. *What does she expect to find? Nothing. What must the room look like? Boring. What is boring? Crates, dust, a cot, a cold stove.*

"Mind?" He laughed, and the laugh came out easy because he'd rehearsed it in mirrors for years. "Warden, I sleep back there. If you find anything interesting, I'll want to buy it off you."

She looked at him a long moment. Then she pushed through the curtain.

Rafael followed her with his eyes and, beneath hearing, with everything else. The mice in the walls crept along the joists above the back room, and through them he watched her the way you watch through keyholes: the cot, neatly made; the stove, cold; the crates, stenciled CLAYWARE — EASTERN KILNS in his own careful hand. She lifted a lid. Clay pots. She lifted another. Clay pots. She ran her finger along the windowsill and examined the dust, which was thick and even, undisturbed except by mice, and the mice had been instructed to walk in patterns that looked like nobody at all.

Even his vermin were accomplices. Even his dirt was a lie.

Vess came back through the curtain looking faintly disappointed, which was the best outcome available in the world.

"Clean," she told the junior. Then, to Rafael, on her way out: "We'll see each other again, Pemberton. Quiet men get quieter or louder. I'll be curious which."

The bell rang. The door shut. The rain went on seeping.

Rafael stood in his shop full of nothing and let out a breath he felt he'd been holding for fifteen years.

* * *

That evening he counted the till, ate bread and hard cheese standing up, and listened to the city through the walls.

This was the ritual, though he'd have denied it under torture. After dark, when the shop was shuttered and the lamp turned low, he opened the door in his mind just a crack—never wider, never longer than a pipe's worth of smoke—and let the neighborhood report in.

The cats of Charm Row were gossips and snobs. The pigeons were fatalists. The rats were the best-informed creatures in Ashvale, because they went everywhere and were owed favors everywhere, and because nobody had ever thought to interrogate a rat.

Tonight the big female came down behind the flour sacks and settled in for what she considered a social call and Rafael considered the closest thing he had to family dinner.

"News," she said, around a crust he'd left for her. "The chandler's cat says the gray boots went to six shops today. Six. All on our row."

"Inspections?"

"Worse. *Counting.*" She said the word with a rat's contempt for human categories. "Not taking. Counting. Like a farmer counting before the cull."

Rafael's cheese stopped halfway to his mouth. "Counting what?"

"Shops. Doors. People." She washed her whiskers. "The chandler's cat also says—" and here her voice dropped into the hushed register rats used for things that frightened even rats, "—that the counting-men asked about *strangers*. Anyone new on the row. Anyone coming in from the hills."

"From the hills," Rafael repeated. The hills east of Ashvale were hills he tried not to think about. There had been waystations out there once, in the old days, places his teacher had spoken of in a voice gone soft and faraway. Covenant sanctuaries. Most were ruins now, or rumors, or ash.

"Strangers from the hills," the rat confirmed. "Gray cloaks asking after them at every gate. And the pigeons say the same, and the pigeons are wrong about everything except directions."

He should have felt nothing. A charm-seller with a clean ledger had no stake in strangers from the hills. That was the whole architecture of his life: no stake, no risk, no name in any book but his own dull accounts.

But the door in his mind, cracked open for the evening, let something else in with the news—a feeling, directionless, like weather coming off the sea. Something was moving toward Ashvale. Something the wardens already knew about. Something that made the rats use their frightened voice.

"Anything else?" he asked, and hated how much he wanted the answer to be *no*.

"One thing." The rat finished the crust and looked at him with small black eyes that held, as rat eyes always did, an ancient and unimpressed patience. "The dogs by the south gate are howling at nothing. All night, two nights now. Dogs don't howl at nothing, charm-man. Dogs howl at things men can't smell yet."

She left then, the way rats leave, as if the conversation had been her idea and had concluded favorably.

Rafael banked the stove, checked the lock twice, and lay on his cot staring at the ceiling beams. Rain worked at the roof. Somewhere south, dogs were howling at a thing men couldn't smell yet, and gray cloaks were counting doors, and he was fifty-one years old and fluent in a language that would kill him, and the loneliest thing in Ashvale was not the silence—it was that he had gotten good at it. Good enough to pass inspection. Good enough that even the silence fit him like a tailored coat.

He was almost asleep when the feeling came again, stronger, closer, rolling up Charm Row like fog off the harbor. Not a sound. Not a word. Just pressure—the sense of something speaking the covenant-tongue badly, or newly, or desperately, somewhere out in the wet dark, the way a child cries in a language it was born knowing.

Rafael sat up in the dark with his heart going like a trapped bird, and told himself it was nothing, and knew he was lying, and lay back down anyway.

Outside, the rain kept seeping. Southward, past the gates, past the counting-men, something small was coming toward his door through the night, and the dogs of Ashvale had begun, all together, to howl.
