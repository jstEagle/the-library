## 3. What the Rats Remember

Rafael did not sleep.

He sat in the back room of his shop with a single candle burning and a child asleep on his only chair, wrapped in his only blanket, and he listened to the walls.

Not with his ears. Fifteen years of discipline had taught him to keep his ears for ordinary things—the creak of the shutter, the drip of rain off the eaves, the footsteps of the night watch two streets over. His ears were safe. It was the other listening that was dangerous, the one that came up through the soles of his feet and the roots of his teeth, the one that had no off-switch except fear.

He had kept it switched off for fifteen years. He was very good at it. He had built a life the way other men built walls, brick by careful brick: a shop that sold nothing, a face that showed nothing, a tongue that said *yes, warden* and *no, warden* and *just tin and paint, warden* in exactly the flat tone that made inspectors lose interest before they finished their tea.

And then last night a mud-caked child had walked out of the rain, looked at him with eyes like wet river stones, and spoken three words in a language that had been dead longer than Ashvale had been a city—and every rat in the building had gone still at once, like a held breath.

Rats didn't go still. Rats were never still. That was the whole of what rats were: motion and appetite and the small scrabble of a world that lived underneath the human one, indifferent to it. When they all stopped at the same moment, it meant something had spoken in the old tongue, the covenant-speech, the language that predated kingdoms and would presumably outlast them. The language Rafael's teacher had bled to keep alive. The language Rafael had run from.

The child slept on. She was maybe eight, maybe ten—hard to tell under the mud and the hollows. Her breathing had the ragged rhythm of someone who had been running for days and whose body hadn't yet gotten the news that it could stop. There was a burn on her forearm, an angry red stripe from wrist to elbow, and she had flinched away from the candle flame when he'd first lit it, hard enough to knock a jar of painted tin charms off the shelf.

She'd looked at the scattered charms without recognition. Good. Whatever she was, she wasn't here for his stock.

Rafael rubbed his eyes and made the mistake he'd been avoiding all night. He let the listening come back on.

The walls woke up.

It was like opening a door onto a market after years in a quiet room—sudden, overwhelming, layered. The mice behind the plaster, chattering their small territorial grievances. The moths against the shutter, saying nothing worth hearing. And beneath it all, everywhere, the rats: the long gray parliament of them, running their commentary on the city above, which food cellars were guarded, which traps were sprung, where the cats slept and which ones were liars.

They noticed him noticing. They always did. A ripple went through the near galleries, a shift in tone, the rodent equivalent of a room going quiet when a dangerous man walks in.

*He's awake,* said one, close by, inside the wall behind the shelving. An old voice, gravelly, unhurried. Rafael knew it. Every building has one rat who is old enough to be its historian, and this one had lived in his walls since before he'd bought the shop. He thought of it privately as the Alderman, though he'd never told it so. Flattery was wasted on rats; they found it suspicious.

*I'm not awake,* Rafael said, in the mind-voice that wasn't words at all, just pressure and intent. *Go back to sleep.*

*You've been not-awake for hours,* the Alderman said. *Sitting there. Smelling like fear and old promises. And you brought meat.*

"She's not meat."

*Everything is meat,* the Alderman observed comfortably. *The question is only who eats whom, and when. She smells of hills. Of ash. Of dog.*

Rafael went cold. "Dog?"

*Dog and horse and man,* the Alderman said. *Man most of all. Man-smell baked into her skin, the way smoke gets into wool. She has been carried. Chased. Carried again.* A pause, the wet sound of whiskers against plaster. *You want to know. I can smell you wanting. You've spent fifteen years not-wanting, charm-man. It's coming off you like heat off a forge.*

Rafael looked at the child. Her brow furrowed in sleep. Her fingers curled tighter around the blanket's edge, knuckles pale.

He should wake her and put her out. That was the sane arithmetic. He had survived by being nobody's business, and a hunted child was everybody's business eventually; the gray cloaks always came asking, and wardens had a way of finding the one door in a street that had opened. Fifteen years of careful nothing, weighed against one night of charity. Any fool could do that sum.

But she had spoken the covenant-tongue. Not learned it—*spoken* it, the way water speaks downhill, the way fire speaks upward, native and unforced. He had heard that accent exactly once before, from an old woman in the hills who had smelled of woodsmoke and dried herbs and had called him *little keeper* right up until the day the wardens came for her too.

"Tell me," he said. "What do the rats remember?"

*Ah,* said the Alderman, and Rafael heard the trap in it, the pleasant, patient trap of a creature that traded in debts. *Now we're bargaining.*

"I'm not bargaining. I'm asking."

*Asking is bargaining with the price left off,* the Alderman said. *But fine. Fine, charm-man. Not for you. For her.* And then the old rat began to talk, and the walls gave up their memory.

* * *

This is what the rats knew, and they knew it the way rats know everything—secondhand, sideways, stitched together from a hundred small witnesses, but accurate in the way that only the despised and overlooked are accurate, because nobody bothers to lie to a rat:

There was a place in the hills, two days' walk north and east, where the road forked above a river valley. Humans called it a waystation—a rest house for travelers, unremarkable, slate-roofed, old. But the beasts of the hills had another name for it, older than any road sign: *the warm stone*. For generations, every wild thing within a day's travel had known the warm stone as a place of truce. Foxes and hares drank from the same trough in its yard. Owls and mice shared its rafters under an unspoken law. Dogs gone feral dened in its cellar in winter, and no hawk touched them while they slept beneath it. Something about the place made tooth and claw forget themselves. The birds said the stones hummed. The worms in its foundations said the hum went down and down, deeper than roots, deeper than worms cared to follow.

Two nights ago, the warm stone burned.

It did not burn the way accidents burn. The rats' cousins in the hills—field rats, hedge rats, creatures with no love for city folk and less patience for city questions—had described it in detail, because beasts describe disasters the way humans describe weather: obsessively, repeatedly, needing to say it until someone said it back. The fire had started at all four corners of the building at once. Men had poured oil at the corners. Men in gray, riding horses whose flanks were lathered though the pace had been easy, men who moved wrong—not like hunters, the foxes said, but like collectors. Like men gathering rather than killing, except that everything in their way died anyway.

The people of the waystation had not run. That was the part the hill-rats kept circling back to, the part that didn't fit any pattern a rat understood. When the gray men came, the people of the warm stone had stood in the yard and waited. An old woman had raised her hands and spoken words that made the horses shy and the dogs howl, and a gray-cloaked man had cut her down mid-word, and the words had stopped, and after that the others stopped too. Nine of them. The rats counted. Rats always count the dead; dead things feed the ground, and the ground feeds the rats, and even grief is a kind of bookkeeping.

Nine dead. One missing.

The missing one was small. The hill-beasts had tracked her scent to a culvert below the burning building—a child-sized hole, child-sized exit. Dog-smell all around it, real dogs, hunting dogs, casting back and forth along the stream for a day and losing the trail where the child had waded. And after the dogs, stranger things: the gray men had brought a beast of their own, or something shaped like a beast, something that made the whole valley's animals go silent and press themselves flat, something that *smelled wrong*—smelled, the foxes said, of cages. Of magic kept in boxes and fed on slowly. The hill-rats had one word for it, passed trembling from burrow to burrow, and the word translated roughly as *the hunger that walks upright*.

The child had run. Downhill, along the water, through the gorse, sleeping in ditches, eating—here the Alderman paused with something like respect—eating whatever a rat would eat, and being quick enough about it that the field-mice bore her no grudge. Two days of running. And at the end of it, yesterday evening, in the rain, at the mouth of a street that smelled of fish and tar and human noise, she had done the strangest thing of all.

She had stopped running.

She had stood in the rain, the starlings reported—starlings see everything and understand none of it—and she had turned her face up toward the gray sky and the gray city, and she had listened. The way the beasts listen. Then she had crossed the street, straight past a lamplighter and a drunk and a pair of watchmen, and she had knocked on one particular door. This door. Out of every door in the charm quarter, out of four thousand doors, this one.

*Birds don't know why,* the Alderman said. *Mice don't know why. But I'll tell you what I think, charm-man, since you're paying attention for once. Things that speak the old tongue don't knock on random doors. The old tongue knows where it's going.*

Rafael sat very still. The candle had burned down an inch without him noticing.

"The gray men," he said. "Did they follow her into the city?"

*Into the city, yes,* the Alderman said. *Past the gates, yes—that surprised everyone; the gate-wardens saw the gray cloaks and stepped aside like men stepping aside for a cart they have no wish to argue with. Through the market, yes. And then—* the old rat paused, and for the first time its voice lost its comfortable drawl, *—and then they stopped. Last night. Near the fountain square. They stood in the rain for an hour, doing nothing, and the pigeons wouldn't go near them, and then they walked to the inn by the grain exchange and took rooms, and they are there now. Sleeping. Or whatever it is they do instead.*

"They're waiting."

*Everything waits,* the Alderman said. *Cats wait. Traps wait. You waited fifteen years, charm-man, behind your little counter full of nothing. The difference is what you're waiting for.* A scratch, a shift, the sound of a heavy body settling against the studs of the wall. *They're waiting for her to move. And she's in your shop. So either you move her, or you're waiting too.*

* * *

Rafael got up because sitting still had become unbearable, and paced the length of the back room, four steps and turn, four steps and turn, the track worn into the floorboards by fifteen years of exactly this pacing, and hated himself a little for knowing that.

Complicit. The word arrived without being invited and settled in his chest like a swallowed stone.

Here was the arithmetic he'd been refusing to do. If he put her out—if he opened the door and pointed her at the alley and went back to his harmless tin charms—she would survive perhaps a day. The gray men weren't searching the city; they were watching the exits and letting the net do the work. A lone child, mute, marked, half-starved, moving through Ashvale alone? The beggars would sell her location for bread. The watch would hand her over for a pat on the head. She would die in a cellar somewhere with her boots taken, and it would take the rats of the charm quarter approximately six hours to tell each other about it, and Rafael would hear the story through his walls, in the language he pretended not to speak, and he would live with it for however many more years his caution bought him.

Fifteen years of silence, purchased at the price of one child. That was the trade on offer. That was always the trade on offer; that was the entire shape of a hidden life. Every year of safety was bought from somebody, and he had simply never been shown the bill before.

He looked at her again. In sleep she had turned toward the wall, toward the rats, her cheek nearly touching the plaster, and the old rat had gone quiet on the other side of it, and Rafael realized—with a feeling like a floor giving way—that the Alderman was keeping watch over her. That the whole gray parliament of his walls had rearranged itself around the child's sleep, sentries posted, the building's endless small traffic rerouted to silence. Beasts protected covenant-speakers. It was in the bones of them, older than training, older than fear. His own walls were telling him what he already knew and wished he didn't.

She hadn't knocked on a random door.

*Little keeper*, the old woman in the hills had called him, twenty years ago, pressing a smooth stone into his palm. *The gift isn't yours to hide, boy. It's yours to keep. There's a difference, and someday you'll learn it, and I pray it doesn't cost what it cost me.*

He had learned the difference. It had cost her everything, and taught him nothing, and he had spent two decades proving that a man could hold a sacred trust and treat it like a disease.

Rafael crouched beside the chair. Up close, the child smelled of ash and river water and, faintly, underneath it, of something green and living that had no business surviving two days of flight—the smell, he realized, of the covenant itself, of the old roads and standing stones, clinging to her like burrs.

"Wren," he said softly. He'd fished the name out of her in fragments earlier, gestures and patience, the one word she'd given him. "Wren. Listen to me. I'm going to get you out of this city. I don't know how yet. But I need help, and the person I'm thinking of is going to be furious with me, and I probably deserve it."

The child's eyes opened. Wet river stones, watching him without fear, without much of anything—as if she had already seen everything there was to see of him and filed it away, and was waiting, patiently, for him to catch up to whatever she'd decided the moment she'd knocked.

Then she lifted one muddy hand and pressed it flat against the wall, against the plaster, over the exact spot where the Alderman kept his lodgings, and she looked at Rafael, and she smiled.

Behind the wall, the old rat made a sound Rafael had never heard a rat make. It took him a moment to translate it, and when he did, the bottom dropped out of his stomach.

It was the sound rats make for *keeper*.

Outside, far off across the sleeping city, near the fountain square, a dog began to bark—one sharp note, then another, urgent and rhythmic, and every beast within earshot picked it up and passed it along, roof to gutter to cellar, a message relayed through the dark at the speed of fear:

*They're moving. The gray men are moving.*
