## 4. The Inquisitor's Ledger

The wisp had been beautiful once.

Corvane could still see the ghost of that beauty in it, even now, even after eleven years in its cage—a small blue radiance the size of a fist, drifting against the glass of its prison like a moth that had forgotten windows were not sky. It had been caught in the marsh-fens south of Ashvale, singing to the reeds in a voice like water over stones. The wardens who brought it in had wept without knowing why. That was the thing about wild magic: it made ordinary men feel their own smallness, and ordinary men hated the feeling.

Corvane did not hate it. He had simply stopped feeling small a very long time ago.

He sat at his desk in the Hall of Judgments with the ledger open before him and one hand resting flat on the wisp's cage, and he drank.

There was no other word for it, though no word in any of the Inquisition's manuals dared name it. The manuals spoke of "containment protocols" and "asset interrogation" and "the responsible stewardship of confiscated arcana." They did not speak of an old man's hand on cold glass and the slow, sweet drain of something living into something that should have died forty years ago. The wisp dimmed by degrees so fine that only Corvane would ever notice. A little less blue at the edges. A little slower in its drifting. It pressed itself against the far side of the cage now, as far from his hand as the glass allowed, and it had stopped singing to the reeds. There were no reeds in here. There was only the Hall of Judgments, with its black stone floors and its high narrow windows, and the smell of ink and candle smoke and old fear soaked so deep into the walls that no amount of scrubbing would ever lift it.

Corvane turned a page.

The ledger was the true heart of the Inquisition, more than the pyres, more than the gray cloaks, more than the gallows on Warrant Hill whose beams had gone silver-gray from use. Anyone could burn a witch. It took patience—real patience, the kind that outlasted empires—to keep accounts. Every scrap of magic seized in the city of Ashvale and the hundred miles of hinterland around it passed across this desk: every charm broken, every amulet melted, every hedge-witch's jar of stormlight poured into Inquisition glass. Each entry was dated, catalogued, assessed for potency, and assigned a disposition. Most dispositions read *destroyed*. That was what the public believed, and belief was a tool like any other.

Very few entries read *retained*.

He ran his finger down the column, reading numbers the way other men read scripture. Three vials of hearth-light, taken from a widow in Tanner's Row—weak stuff, guttering already, but useful for topping off the lesser cages when the wisps grew dull. One luck-knot, braided horsehair, confiscated from a stable-boy who'd sworn it only made the horses calm. It had done rather more than that; Corvane had felt it humming through the leather of his gloves when they brought it in, a warm animal contentment that had been almost pleasant to drain. Almost. Wild magic was always better than the crafted kind. Crafted magic was bread and water. Wild magic was meat.

His own entry, written in his own hand, thirty-one years ago, sat at the top of the first page of the first ledger, because he had never let them move it: *One (1) covenant-shard, provenance unknown, potency extreme. Retained for study.* He had been a young Inquisitor then, hungry and clever and already dying—the physicians had given him two years, maybe three, some rot of the blood that was eating him from the inside the way fire eats a house. The shard had been pried from a dead keeper's hands in the hills, still warm, still whispering. Corvane had held it in his bare palm and felt the rot in his body pause, like a dog hearing a whistle.

Two years, the physicians said. It had been thirty-one.

Patience, he reminded himself, turning another page. Patience above all. The rot always came back when he went too long between feedings—it lived in him now as a tenant lives in a house it intends to inherit—but it came back slowly, and he had learned its rhythms the way a farmer learns weather. A wisp lasted him a season if he was careful. A captured sprite, a year. There had been a river-spirit once, early in his tenure, magnificent and furious, that had kept him strong for nearly four. He remembered it fondly. He remembered all of them fondly, in the way a man remembers meals.

The candle gutted. Outside the high windows, Ashvale lay under its usual lid of chimney-smoke, gray upon gray, the city he had spent three decades making safe. Safe from what, the young wardens sometimes asked—not often, and never twice—in their training lectures. *From the fire,* Corvane told them, and let the word do its work. Everyone in Ashvale carried some inherited memory of fire: the dragon-scorch stories, the border wars, the great burning of the old quarter that grandmothers still used to frighten children. Fear of fire was the bedrock the Inquisition stood on, and Corvane had built deeper foundations than anyone knew. Magic was fire. Fire was hunger. And he—he was simply the most honest man in the city, because he admitted what the magic was for.

A knock at the door. Two beats, precise.

"Enter."

Warden Vess slipped in the way he always did, sideways, as though hoping to present less surface area to the room. A thin man with ink-stained cuffs and the permanent apologetic stoop of someone who had grown up being shouted at. He was Corvane's chief clerk, and he was terrified, and this was exactly as it should be. Fear kept clerks accurate.

"The evening summaries, Inquisitor General." Vess set the folder down at the corner of the desk, well away from the wisp's cage. He never looked directly at the cage. None of them did. "Eleven items requiring your seal. Nothing urgent."

"Nothing is ever urgent, Warden. That is why we win." Corvane took the folder. "The Vermin Office?"

Vess blinked. "The—the rat-catchers' reports? They're filed separately, sir, quarterly—"

"I know how they're filed. Summarize them anyway."

It was an odd request and Vess knew it was an odd request, which was why Corvane had made it. For six months now he had required a digest of the vermin office's mundane reports alongside the magical seizures, and he had never explained why, and Vess had never asked, because asking the Inquisitor General *why* was the last question a clerk ever got to ask. The truth was that Corvane had noticed something himself, something small, the kind of thing that would have escaped a younger man drunk on power but not an old man sustained by it. The rats were moving. Not dying—moving. Away from certain districts, toward others, in patterns too consistent to be chance. Animals fled magic; every hunter knew it. But animals also *served* it, in the old days, in the covenant days, and if the vermin of Ashvale were organizing themselves along some invisible fault line, then there was a fault line, and faults meant pressure, and pressure meant—

"Charm quarter," Vess was saying, shuffling papers with trembling fingers. "Standard complaints. Unlicensed charm-sellers, mostly. There's a fellow on Gildersleeve Lane, Pemberton, sells trinkets—wardens have inspected him four times, found nothing, he's clean as a whistle. Rat-catcher filed a note about his street, though. Says the rats won't touch the buildings within twenty yards of the shop. Won't nest, won't gnaw, won't even cross the gutter. Calls it 'unnatural tidiness.'" Vess managed a weak smile. "Rat-catchers call everything unnatural, sir. Man's been drinking."

"Has he." Corvane did not smile. He held out his hand, and Vess placed the vermin digest in it, and retreated two steps to stand by the door where he could pretend to study the wall.

Corvane read.

*Report of Warden-Catcher Odell, Charm Quarter beat, autumn quartering.* The handwriting slanted with drink but the observations were methodical enough. Rats abandoning the drains near Gildersleeve Lane. Pigeons roosting on one roof and refusing another. Cats—this line was underlined twice, hard enough to tear the paper—cats sitting in a row outside a certain shuttered shop at dusk, sitting still, *like churchgoers*, and leaving without a sound when the lamp inside went out.

Corvane set the page down.

For a long moment he did nothing at all. He listened to the Hall of Judgments breathe—that vast settling silence, the tick of cooling stone, the faint wet flutter of the wisp behind its glass. Under his hand, the cage had gone cold. He lifted his fingers, examined the blanching of his own skin, and pressed them back down. Later. There would be time to feed later. Right now something else was awake in him, older than hunger, and it was leaning forward.

Cats did not sit in rows. Cats did not wait for lamps. And rats did not observe property lines unless something had drawn the lines for them—something with a voice they recognized, a voice from before the Inquisition, before the burnings, before the covenant-stones went dark one by one across the hinterland like lamps going out in a house where the owner had died.

He had spent thirty-one years collecting the shards of that dead owner's house. He knew its architecture better than any living soul. He knew that the covenant had not been destroyed, merely starved—knot by knot, stone by stone, keeper by keeper—and he knew, with the certainty of a man who has read ten thousand ledgers, that starving things do not die quietly. Starving things wait. And then, one day, something comes to the door carrying food.

*A child*, the gull-report had said—no. No, that hadn't come yet. That was later. Corvane closed his eyes briefly, steadying himself against the strange vertigo of foreknowledge, and returned to the present tense, where he belonged. In the present tense there was only a rat-catcher's drunken note and a charm-seller named Pemberton who had passed four inspections and was therefore either innocent or very good, and after thirty-one years Corvane had learned that nobody was innocent, only unexamined.

"Pemberton," he said aloud.

Vess started. "Sir?"

"The charm-seller on Gildersleeve Lane. Pull his file. All of it. Licenses, tax records, birth registry, the inspections—every warden's notes, verbatim, I want their exact words, not the summaries." He turned another page of the ledger, unhurried, a man doing bookkeeping. "And check when he arrived in Ashvale. Where he came from. Who vouched for him."

"Fifteen years, I believe, sir. He's been on the lane fifteen years. Quiet as anything."

"Fifteen years." Corvane tasted the number. Fifteen years ago the last confirmed keeper had died on Warrant Hill—an old woman, half-blind, who had gone to the gallows singing in a language the priests swore was nonsense and Corvane alone had understood. He had stood in the crowd that day, hat over his heart like any decent citizen, and he had listened to her sing, and he had thought: *the house is empty now, but houses can be reoccupied.*

Fifteen years was long enough to hide. It was also long enough to forget that hiding is not the same as being gone.

"Anything else, Inquisitor General?" Vess hovered at the door, desperate to be elsewhere.

"One more thing." Corvane finally looked up, and gave his clerk the smile he saved for special occasions—the one that reached his eyes, which was precisely what made it terrible. "Send a discreet inquiry to the vermin office. Tell Warden-Catcher Odell that his diligence has been noted at high level, and ask him to continue his observations of the Gildersleeve beat. Daily reports. Written, not verbal." He paused. "And tell him nothing else. If he asks why, tell him the Inquisition concerns itself with all irregularities, however small. He'll believe that. It's even true."

"Yes, sir." Vess hesitated, one hand on the door. He was young enough that curiosity occasionally outran terror, and Corvane watched the struggle happen in real time, watched the question rise in the man's face like a fish rising toward light. "Sir—if I may. The rats. Is it... is it a sign of something? The men in the yard say when the vermin act strange, it means—"

"It means," Corvane said gently, "that the world is full of small creatures telling each other things, Warden, and it is the business of this office to know what they are saying before anyone else does. Good night."

The door clicked shut. The candles burned. Somewhere below, in the holding cells, a prisoner began to weep in the flat exhausted way that meant the weeping would go on for hours, and no one would come, because no one ever came, and that too was policy.

Corvane rose and walked to the window.

Ashvale at night, seen from the Hall of Judgments, was a carpet of embers that had forgotten they were embers—ten thousand hearth-fires banked low, ten thousand families locking doors against a danger none of them had ever seen. He had made them this way. He had taken a city that remembered dragons and taught it to flinch at candle-flames, and the flinching fed him, because frightened cities seize magic, and seized magic came across this desk, and across this desk it came into him. It was a perfect circle. He had designed it to be. Circles were patient shapes.

And yet.

He pressed his palm flat against the cold glass of the window, and beneath the smug geometry of his design he felt it again—the thing the rat-catcher's note had stirred up, the thing he had been feeling for months now in the gaps between ledger entries. A stirring. A turning-over, deep in the ground of the world, like a sleeper changing position. The beasts were restless in the hills. The birds flew wrong over the marshes. Old stones that his wardens had catalogued and dismissed for decades had begun, according to three separate field reports he had buried in the lower drawers of this very desk, to *hum*.

The covenant was not dead. It was convalescent. And somewhere in his city, in his gray obedient city, something had begun to feed it again.

Corvane turned back to the desk, to the ledger, to the neat columns of everything he had ever taken from the world, and he felt no fear. Fear was for creatures with something to lose. He had lost everything decades ago—his health, his humanity, his name in any registry of honest men—and what remained was appetite and patience, and of the two, patience was the sharper instrument.

He dipped his pen. Beside the charm-quarter summary, in his small precise hand, he wrote a single line in the margin, a note intended for no eyes but his own:

*Gildersleeve Lane. Watch the quiet ones. The quiet ones are listening.*

Then he blew out the candle, laid his hand once more upon the wisp's cage, and began, slowly, to feed.

* * *

Three streets away and half a mile down, in a shop that smelled of dust and harmless lacquer, a lamp went out.

The cats on the opposite roofline rose as one, stretched, and dispersed into the dark, each in a different direction, carrying the news.

By morning, every rat in Ashvale would know that the child was safe—for now—and that the man with the quiet voice had opened his door. And by morning, in a black office above the sleeping city, a clerk named Vess would place a fifteen-year-old file on the Inquisitor General's desk, and Corvane would read the name *Pemberton* and feel, for the first time in years, something very much like joy.

The hunt, when it came, would be patient.

It always was.
