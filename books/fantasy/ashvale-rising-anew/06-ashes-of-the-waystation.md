## 6. Ashes of the Waystation

The lamp-oil smelled wrong.

Sable had trimmed and filled Ashvale's street lamps for eleven years, and she knew her oils the way a priest knows his psalms. This batch was cheap tallow cut with something sharper—warden requisition, probably, the surplus they sold back to the districts they'd bled. It burned hot and fast and it reminded her, every time she lit a wick, of another fire. One that had not smelled of tallow at all.

She crouched beneath the lamp at the corner of Candlemakers' Row, her brass pole in her gloved hands, and let the flame catch. The light spread in a soft circle, and for a moment—just a moment—the gray cobbles looked like the flagstones of the sanctuary at Highmoor Waystation, lit by a hundred votive candles that never went out.

Then the moment passed, the way moments did, and she was a lamplighter again, an old woman in a man's coat, and Highmoor was ash.

She finished the row. She always finished the row. Routine was a kind of prayer, if you said it long enough, and Sable had been saying hers for six years—since the night the waystation burned and the world told her to be quiet, and she, fool that she was, had obeyed.

Tonight she broke routine. Tonight she went home by the long way, past the charm quarter, and stopped at a shop with a painted sign of a tin bird that did nothing at all.

* * *

Six years ago, Highmoor Waystation had stood in the hills three days north of Ashvale, and it had been the last place in the world where Sable had felt safe.

It had not looked like much. That was the point. A long, low stone building with a slate roof, a stable, a well, a kitchen that smelled of barley and onions. Travelers called it a hospice for the road-sick. Wardens called it a charitable house licensed under the Relief Acts. But the waystation was older than the Acts, older than the kingdom that wrote them, and its true business was written nowhere at all.

It was a sanctuary. One of the last.

In the years before the Purges, there had been dozens—waystations strung along the old covenant-roads like lamps along a street, each one a place where the gifted could stop, rest, be fed, be known. Each one anchored by a covenant-stone in its cellar, and each stone kept by a keeper, and each keeper bound into the great web that held the barrier between the realms taut as a drumhead. Sable had been a priestess of the covenant then. Not the gray-robed priests of the city temples, who sang to a god with no face and asked for nothing but obedience—she had been one of the *other* kind, the kind the histories had scraped away. A keeper's hand. A speaker at the stones.

She had been young. She had thought the web would hold forever.

The night the waystation burned, she had been away. That was the wound she could not stop picking. She had gone down to Ashvale for supplies—lamp oil, salt, a new whetstone—and she had lingered two days because an old friend was dying and she stayed to hold his hand, and when she came back up the covenant-road, she smelled the smoke from a mile off.

The roof had gone first. She could see that even from the road—the black gap in the skyline where slate had been, the columns of smoke standing up straight in the windless evening like things planted. She had run. Forty years old then, and she had run up that hill like a girl, her lungs tearing, and the heat had hit her at the gate and driven her back three steps, and she had stood there with her arms over her face and screamed a name into the fire.

Maren. The keeper of Highmoor. Her teacher. The woman who had found Sable half-starved on the road at fourteen and heard, in the nonsense syllables the child muttered to the crows, the first broken words of the covenant-tongue.

The stable had survived, half of it. Sable had gone through the smoking wreckage with a wet cloth over her mouth and found what the fire left. She would not describe it, even now, even to herself, in anything plainer than this: she found them. Some of them. Not all. The ones she did not find had not burned.

The cellar was the worst. The covenant-stone of Highmoor had stood in that cellar for six hundred years, a pillar of gray granite carved all over with the old script, warm to the touch the way a living thing is warm. Sable had gone down the broken stairs expecting to find it shattered.

It was worse than shattered. The stone had been *cut*. Deliberately, with tools, by someone who knew exactly what they were doing—chiseled into blocks, the blocks pried apart, the heart of it—the keel, the anchor-root, the part that sang—hacked out and carried away. The chisel marks were still bright. The work had been done *before* the fire. The fire was to hide the masonry.

Sable had knelt in that cellar among the chips of the oldest thing she loved, and she had understood, in the slow cold way understanding comes, that this was not a raid. Raids took people. This had taken the *stone*. And stones were not taken for hatred.

Stones were taken for what was in them.

* * *

The bell over the charm-shop door rang, and Rafael Pemberton looked up from a tray of tin birds with the expression of a man watching a wasp land on his wrist.

"Sable." He said it flat, but his eyes went past her shoulder to the street before the door had even shut. Fifteen years of habit. "You shouldn't be here."

"Probably not." She set her lamplighter's pole against the wall, deliberately, the way you set down a weapon you intended to pick back up. The child was asleep on a pallet behind the counter, a small mud-colored shape rising and falling. Sable looked at her a long moment. "The rats told you she was hunted."

"The rats told me she ran from a fire in the hills." His voice was carefully bored. "Rats exaggerate."

"Rats don't exaggerate." Sable pulled a stool from the corner and sat, uninvited, her knees cracking. "Men in gray. That's what they said. Gray cloaks, riding hard, going through the wreckage with rakes and tongs. Not looking for bodies." She watched his face. "Looking for *stone*, Rafael. Did the rats mention that? Chips of it in the ash. The vermin were fighting over them like meat scraps."

Something moved behind Rafael's eyes—recognition, and under the recognition, fear, and under the fear, a door closing.

"I don't know anything about stones," he said. "I sell birds that don't sing and rings that don't turn your finger green. If you've come to confess something, find a temple."

"The temples are ash too," Sable said. "All of them, all the way back. You know that. You were there."

The silence stretched. Behind the counter, the child turned over in her sleep and made a small sound—not a word, not quite, but Sable's spine prickled anyway, because she had heard that sound before, in another cellar, from another child, forty years ago. The sound of someone talking in a tongue that had no business being in a human mouth.

"Highmoor burned six years ago," Sable said, quieter. "You'd have been—what, nine years into your little shop by then? Did you hear about it? A hospice fire in the hills. Eleven dead. The wardens called it a cooking accident." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "A cooking accident. In a stone building with a stone hearth, and the fire started in four places at once."

Rafael had gone very still. It was the stillness of a man standing in a river feeling the current strengthen around his knees.

"Four places," he said.

"Four places." Sable leaned forward, elbows on her knees. "I counted the burn patterns myself, because I'm the kind of woman who counts. Oil was used. Warden oil—the same cheap cut tallow they sell back to us, which I would know, because I burn it every night of my life. And before the fire, someone cut the covenant-stone out of its cellar and hauled it away in a cart, and the ruts of that cart went north. Toward Ashvale." She let that sit. "Toward the Inquisitor's vaults, though I didn't know it then. I know it now."

"Why now?" Rafael's voice was barely above the hiss of the brazier. "Six years, Sable. You've been lighting lamps and keeping your mouth shut for six years. Why come to me now?"

"Because of her." Sable nodded at the sleeping child. "Because six years ago they took a stone, and I told myself it was one stone, one mad act, one horror that was over. And now a child walks out of those same hills, out of the same fire's aftermath, speaking the covenant-tongue like she was born to it—and she's *mute in everything else*, Rafael. Do you understand what that means? I do. The old keepers had a word for it. *Vesseling*. When the covenant needs a mouth, it borrows one, and it takes the one it borrows down to the quick." Her hands had closed into fists without her noticing. She opened them, finger by finger. "They didn't burn Highmoor for the people. They burned it because they're *collecting*, and the girl is the next piece on the list."

"Collecting what?"

"Keys." Sable said the word and felt six years of silence crack down its whole length. "Covenant-keys. The stones were keys, Rafael—each one anchored to the barrier, each one a lock and a hinge and a singing wire in the web. Corvane has been buying and burning and bleeding his way across the country for twenty years, and everyone thinks he's a zealot stamping out magic. He's not stamping it out. You don't stamp out a fire by gathering all the embers into your own house." She looked at the child again, at the small chest rising and falling. "He's hoarding it. He's been hoarding it, and a man who hoards fire is cold, and a man who is cold will burn down anything to get warm. The stones. The sanctuaries. And if the stories are true, and I'm old enough to believe them—the last dragon's fire itself. He means to own the barrier. Or break it, if owning it fails."

* * *

Afterward, Sable would remember the rest of that night in pieces, the way you remember a storm: the lamp she'd left burning on Candlemakers' Row, visible through the shop window, steady as a heartbeat. Rafael pacing. The child waking and not crying, just watching them with those too-old eyes, and Sable kneeling to her level and speaking—slowly, half-remembered, her accent terrible after forty years—the greeting the keepers used. *Stone to stone, fire to fire.*

The child had reached out and touched Sable's cheek. One finger, light as ash falling.

And in the touch, Sable had felt it: the web. Faint, frayed, thinner than a spider's last thread, but *there*—the thing she had been bound to as a girl, the thing she had mourned as dead, still humming somewhere far away under the world. Felt it and felt, at the end of that thread, an immense slow warmth turning over in its sleep, like a coal the size of a mountain deciding whether to go out.

Emberlain. Alive. Barely.

Sable had sat back on her heels in a charm shop that smelled of tin and dust, and wept without any dignity at all, and the child had watched her do it with perfect seriousness, and Rafael had stood in the corner looking like a man watching his last hiding place burn down around him.

* * *

"I'll take her," Sable said at last. Her voice was raw. "Tonight. My rooms above the lamp-house. No one watches a lamplighter; we're furniture. But I have conditions."

Rafael's jaw tightened. "Conditions."

"One." She held up a finger. "You tell me what you did, Rafael. The whole of it. Whatever broke you so badly that you've spent fifteen years selling nothing to no one, because I watched a child touch my face tonight and I felt the covenant *breathe*, and I know—*I know*—that a tongue like hers doesn't survive in the hills by accident. Someone kept it alive. Someone taught it. And someone else, somewhere along the way, broke something that let the gray cloaks get close enough to burn it." She held his gaze. "I broke my silence tonight. You'll break yours. That's the price of my roof."

"And the second condition?"

"Second," Sable said, "is that when the time comes—and it's coming, Rafael, it's been coming for twenty years, and now it's coming *fast*—you don't hide. Not her, and not yourself. Whatever you've got buried in you, whatever you've been starving all these years, it's going to be needed. And I am done," and here her voice did not shake, she made sure of it, she built the sentence like a wall, "I am *done* watching fires from a road."

The lamp on Candlemakers' Row burned on behind the glass, patient, ordinary, keeping its little circle of light against the gray.

Rafael looked at the child. The child looked back at him, and said nothing at all, in the oldest language there was.

"Get your coat," he said finally. "You're both staying here tonight. We leave at first light."

"Leave?" Sable stood, her knees protesting. "Leave for where?"

Rafael was already pulling a floorboard up with the flat of a chisel, and what came out of the gap was not money and not papers but a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, old and soft with handling.

"North," he said. "To the hills. To what's left of Highmoor—and past it, if the rats are right, to a fire that's going out." He met her eyes, and for the first time in fifteen years, Sable thought, the man looked less like a hiding thing and more like what he'd been born to be. "The gulls will want something for the crossing. And I have the feeling," he added grimly, "that everything is going to want something, from here to the end of the world."

Outside, far off toward the Inquisitor's quarter, a bell began to ring. Not the hour. Not the alarm.

The mustering bell.

Corvane had found the rats' testimony in a caged bird's throat, or he had found nothing at all and simply grown hungry—but either way, the gray cloaks were moving, and Sable pulled her coat on in the dark and understood, with a calm that surprised her, that the silence was over. Hers. Everyone's.

The fire was coming to Ashvale. This time, she intended to be holding the lamp.
