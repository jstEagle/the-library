## 7. A Bargain with Gulls

The harbor smelled of fish guts and tar and the particular rot of a city that had decided long ago to stop pretending it was clean. Rafael walked the quay with Wren's hand swallowed in his own, his collar up against the wind off the Graywater, and he tried very hard to look like a man doing nothing at all.

He was good at that. Fifteen years of practice.

*Nothing to see here,* his posture said. *A trinket-seller taking his niece for a walk. Bored. Cold. Thinking about dinner.*

Wren's grip tightened. She'd been doing that all morning — small pressures of her fingers, a language of squeezes they were inventing between them without ever agreeing to. One squeeze meant *I'm scared.* Two meant *look.* Three, so far, had no meaning, but she used it anyway, the way children use words they've only half learned.

Three squeezes now.

Rafael looked.

The gulls were watching them.

Not the way gulls watch everything — the flat, greedy accounting of a bird that sees the world as either food or not-food. This was different. A dozen of them lined the pilings above the fish-market, heads cocked in perfect unison, and when Rafael's eyes passed over them, every head turned together, like weathervanes finding the same wind.

His stomach dropped through the boards of the quay and into the cold water below.

*They know.*

Of course they knew. The rats had known; the pigeons on Sable's roofline had gone quiet as stones when he'd passed beneath them yesterday. Word moved through the city's beasts the way rumor moved through washerwomen. *The charm-seller speaks. The charm-seller has the child. The child speaks the old tongue.*

And beasts talked to anyone who'd listen.

Which meant Corvane would hear eventually. Maybe already had.

Rafael steered Wren toward the end of the quay, past the fish-stalls and the rope-walkers and a drunk singing something mournful about a wife and a river, until they stood at the harbor's edge where the planks gave out and the Graywater slapped gray against gray stone. He crouched, pretending to tie his bootlace, and let his eyes do what his voice could not.

He looked at the nearest gull — an old one, feathers gone the color of dirty snow, one eye clouded with cataract — and he opened the door he'd kept shut for fifteen years.

It was like unclenching a fist he'd forgotten he was making.

The world *flooded* in.

Sound first — not sound exactly, but meaning wearing sound's clothes. The harbor spoke all at once: crabs muttering in their cages about the indignity of bait, a herring shoal turning beneath the surface in a single silver thought, the barnacles chanting their slow stone-hymn to the tide. And over it all, sharp as broken glass, the gulls.

***Charm-man.*** The old gull's voice was a rasp, a knife dragged across a hull. ***We heard you woke up.***

*I didn't wake up,* Rafael said, and the words came out of him in the covenant-tongue, and gods, gods, it felt like scratching an itch he'd endured for half his life. His whole body shivered with the rightness of it. *I'm just a man tying his boot.*

***Liar well-spoken,*** the gull said, with something like professional appreciation. ***But boots don't bleed fear the way you do. What do you want, charm-man? You didn't walk all the way down here to smell the fish.***

Rafael glanced up. Wren stood beside him, staring at the gulls with an expression he was beginning to know well — that terrible, patient attention, as if she could hear every word and was politely waiting for the adults to finish.

Maybe she could. He wouldn't have bet against it.

*I need passage out of the city,* he said. *For two. Quietly. Tonight.*

***Out past the walls?*** The gull laughed, a harsh clatter that made three fishermen look up. ***Birds don't carry people, charm-man. We're not horses.***

*I'm not asking you to carry us. I'm asking what moves out of Ashvale unseen. What flies over the walls without a gate, without a ledger, without a warden counting heads. I'm asking you to tell me the road the city doesn't watch.*

The gulls went still. All twelve of them, all at once, and the silence they made was worse than the noise.

***You want the smugglers' sky,*** the old gull said at last, softer. ***The lane the dead-freighters use. That's not free knowledge, charm-man. That's not even cheap knowledge.***

*Name your price.*

***Ohhh.*** The gull hopped closer along the piling, head tilting until its good eye fixed on Rafael from inches away. ***There it is. There's the hunger under the fear. You've missed this, haven't you? Talking. Fifteen years of swallowing your own tongue, and now it's loose again and it tastes sweet, and you hate how sweet it tastes.***

Rafael said nothing. The gull was right, and they both knew it, and there was no dignity in denying a bird.

***Here is my price,*** the gull said. ***Not coin. Not fish. Coin means nothing to me and fish you'd shortchange us on, because you're a man and men always shortchange. My price is this: a debt. Unnamed. Unshaped. One favor, of my choosing, called when I choose it, owed by you, the speaker, and binding on your tongue itself.***

The wind came off the water and Rafael felt it go straight through him.

*A blank debt.* His teacher's voice rose in his memory, dry as autumn leaves: *Never sign a covenant you can't read, boy. The old tongue binds both ways.* He'd been eight years old, sitting cross-legged on a hillside while old Maren taught him the grammar of promises, and she'd spent a whole week on debts alone. *A named debt has edges. An unnamed debt has teeth.*

*I can't agree to that,* he said. *I don't know what you'll ask.*

***No,*** the gull agreed cheerfully. ***You don't. That's rather the point.***

*Ask for something else. Fish. Bread. Shelter on my roof through winter—*

***We have fish. We have roofs. What we don't have,*** and here the old gull's voice changed, dropped its mockery, went flat and cold as the water below, ***is a future. Ask your rats, charm-man. Ask them what happens to beasts in this city when the gray cloaks come through. Ask them about the wagons that leave the Inquisitor's tower at night, heavy, and come back light. Your Corvane eats magic, the small folk say. Well, maybe he does. But he eats *us* too, or feeds us to whatever does. The sparrows vanish. The cats vanish. Last winter, the foxes in the warren-park — all of them, in one night. So no, I don't want your bread. I want a hold on a man who can speak for us, when speaking matters. That's my price. Take it or swim.***

Rafael knelt there at the edge of the quay and felt the trap close around him with a click so gentle he almost admired it.

Because here was the thing: he needed the gulls. The gates were watched — he'd confirmed it twice this morning, walking past the Northgate and the Bridge Gate with a trinket-seller's aimless amble, counting wardens, noting which captains checked faces and which just checked purses. Every road out of Ashvale had eyes on it now, and somewhere in the city a report was surely crawling up through the ranks: *anomalous vermin behavior, charm quarter, investigate.* Corvane was patient. Patience had kept that man alive decades past his natural span, if even half the whispers were true.

But patience always ended in the grab.

And Wren — mud-caked, mute, three squeezes of the hand Wren — was hiding in a lamplighter's loft above a street that stank of oil, and every hour she stayed there was an hour the net drew tighter.

Sable had said it last night, leaning in her doorway with a lamp guttering behind her: *"Get her out, Rafe. Whatever it costs. Because if they take her, the cost will be the kind you pay forever."*

*Take it or swim.*

Rafael looked at Wren. She was watching the old gull with her head tilted, and there was something in her face he couldn't read — not fear. Recognition, maybe. The way you look at someone speaking your mother-tongue in a foreign country.

She squeezed his hand once. Then, deliberately, twice.

*Look.*

She pointed — small finger, steady — at the water below the pilings. And Rafael, helpless, opened the door wider and listened down.

The harbor depths answered. Old things down there, slow things, crabs and eels and something vast and patient near the channel floor that might have been a grouper or might have been older than the city. And threaded through all of it, a current of gossip, of warning, passed fin to fin from the river mouth:

***Gray cloaks at the fish-gate. Gray cloaks asking after a child. Asking the fishmongers' cats. Paying cats in cream.***

His blood went to ice water.

They were using beasts. Corvane's people had found a way to buy beast-eyes — cream for cats, scraps for dogs, whatever crude bribery men use when they can't speak but need ears. Which meant the network Rafael had counted on to warn him was already turned, bought, compromised. Which meant the gulls — proud, hungry, unbribable gulls — were the only ears left in Ashvale that belonged to themselves.

*Take it or swim.*

"I'll pay your debt," Rafael said aloud, in plain trade-tongue, because some vows wanted air and witnesses even if the witnesses were birds. Then, in the old tongue, feeling each word settle into him like a hook setting in flesh: ***One favor, unnamed, uncalled, binding on my tongue and my gift. Spoken before the harbor and the tide. Witnessed. Kept.***

***Witnessed,*** said twelve gulls, in twelve voices, and the tide lapped the stones once, twice, like a nod.

The old gull ruffled its filthy-white feathers, satisfied, and became abruptly businesslike. ***Good. Now listen, charm-man, because I say this once. The smugglers' sky runs east. Tomorrow night, the dead-freighter *Cormorant's Daughter* leaves the salt-quay on the evening tide, riding high, carrying nothing the harbor-master wants counted. Her captain owes the gulls more than you owe me — we've kept her masts free of our messes for six years. Tell her the White-Eye sends you. Stow in the second hold, under the tarred canvas. She puts in at Marsh Landing, two days downcoast, past the walls, past the ledgers, past the wardens. From there, your legs are your own problem.***

*Why help me?* Rafael asked. *Beyond the debt.*

The gull's clouded eye caught the light and flashed pale as bone.

***Because the child is the old thing,*** it said simply. ***And the old things are waking up, charm-man, all over the world, in burrows and nests and deep water. We remember the covenant. We remember what it was like when men who spoke kept the roads safe for the small folk. You broke it — oh yes, we know who broke it, the stones cried out and every beast in ten leagues heard — but you're what came back. So we'll spend a debt on you. Cheap odds, on a long shot.***

Rafael's mouth was dry. *What do you mean, the stones cried out? When?*

***Fifteen years ago,*** the gull said, and hopped back along the piling, conversation over. ***Sunset tomorrow. Salt-quay. Don't be late, and don't be followed. And charm-man —*** it paused, one foot lifted, ***— stop flinching every time you use it. It shows. Fear smells like burnt sugar to us. To the wrong ears, it smells like dinner.***

Then the twelve heads turned away in unison, and the harbor was just a harbor again, full of screaming birds fighting over fish heads, and Rafael Pemberton knelt at the edge of the world he'd renounced with his heart hammering and his hands shaking and a grin trying desperately to break across his face.

Gods help him. It had felt *good.*

That was the dangerous part. Not the debt, not the risk, not even Corvane. The dangerous part was how natural it had felt, opening that door. Like a drunkard's first sip after a decade dry — smooth going down, and already the thirst rising higher than before. Fifteen years of silence, and one conversation with a gull, and now every crab and pigeon and rat in Ashvale was a voice calling to him, and he wanted to answer all of them, and answering them was exactly how men like him ended up hanging from the Traitor's Arch with their tongues cut out first.

"Rafe." Wren tugged his sleeve. She couldn't say the word — hadn't spoken since the night she arrived, except in that other tongue, that older one — but she'd learned his name somehow, or made one for him, two syllables she shaped with her lips though no sound came out.

He looked at her. "Sunset tomorrow," he said quietly. "We sail."

Her eyes went to the gulls, then back to him, and slowly — deliberately, the way she did everything — she nodded.

Then her gaze slid past his shoulder, and every muscle in her small body went rigid.

Rafael turned.

Two figures stood at the mouth of the quay where it met the fish-market. Gray cloaks, hoods back, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who owned the ground they walked on. One carried a ledger-book under his arm. The other held, on a leather glove, a hooded falcon — and even at forty paces, Rafael could see the bird wasn't right. It sat too still. Its talons gripped the jess too tight. And beneath the smell of tar and fish, the wind brought him something else, faint and wrong, like a candle burning in a sealed room.

Caged magic. The cloaks smelled of caged magic, and so did the bird.

The gray-cloaked man with the ledger raised his head and began, unhurried, to walk toward the end of the quay.

Toward them.

* * *

Rafael did the math in the time it took the man to cross thirty feet of cobbles. Two exits off the quay: the fish-market stairs, wide and open and directly in front of the gray cloaks, and the rope-walk, narrow, choked with drying nets, behind the stalls. Behind them, then. But running told every watcher in the market everything, and there were always watchers—

"Wren." He kept his voice at market-murmur, bent as if adjusting her scarf. "Hold my hand. Walk. We're going to look at nets."

She took his hand. Three squeezes.

"Yeah," he breathed. "Me too."

They walked. Past the fish-stalls, past the drunk, who had progressed from mourning his wife to cursing the river, past a pile of lobster pots reeking of the sea's own graveyard. Behind them, boot-heels on cobbles, unhurried, closer. The falcon's silence pressed against Rafael's back like a hand.

At the mouth of the rope-walk, a lamplighter's ladder leaned against a post — Sable's crew worked this district; of course they did, Sable's fingers were in every shadow of this city — and Rafael had a mad, wonderful thought.

He stopped. Set Wren behind a stack of pots. Looked up at the gulls wheeling over the masts, and opened the door one more time, quick and clean, a pickpocket's touch.

***White-Eye! The gray cloak with the bird — blind it. Three breaths, no more, no harm. Do it now.***

***Oho, spending already,*** the old gull's rasp came back, delighted and distant. ***Very well, debtor. Watch.***

Above the fish-market, the wheeling flock collapsed inward like a fist closing. Twelve gulls dove at once, screaming, a white-and-gray storm of wings and fury, and the falcon's handler stumbled back swearing as talons raked at the hooded bird and beaks hammered at his gloved hand and the whole orderly procession of gray authority dissolved into flapping chaos. Fishermen shouted. The drunk cheered. Somewhere a woman laughed like breaking glass.

Three breaths. Four. Rafael took Wren's hand and walked — *walked*, gods, it was the hardest thing he'd ever done — into the rope-walk, between the hanging nets, into the green dimness smelling of hemp and salt, and did not run until the first corner put the market out of sight.

Then he ran, Wren flying beside him, through the rope-walk and up the lampman's stair and along the rooftops' back-shadowed lanes, until they burst through Sable's door below, chests heaving, and Sable looked up from trimming a wick with the calm of a woman who had been expecting exactly this.

"They're at the harbor," Rafael gasped. "Gray cloaks. They had a falcon. Sable — they're buying the beasts. Cream for cats. The whole city's ears are for sale."

Sable set down her trimmer. Something moved behind her eyes, old and hard and settling into place like a keystone.

"Then tonight," she said. "Not tomorrow. You leave tonight — there's a coal barge, and I know her master, and I'll pay what he asks. And Rafe?" She blew out the wick she'd been trimming, and in the sudden dark her voice came low and level. "Whatever bargain you made to get out — I hope you read the fine print. Because in my experience, the things that save us are never free, and the bill always comes due at the worst possible hour."

Outside, far off toward the harbor, a gull screamed once — long, and sharp, and unmistakably, horribly, like laughter.
