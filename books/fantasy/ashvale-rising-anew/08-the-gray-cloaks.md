## 8. The Gray Cloaks

The gulls taste like the sea and like old, old words.

Wren rides inside their eyes. That is how she thinks of it, though there is no word for it in the language of people, because Wren does not have many of those words yet. The gulls wheel over the rooftops and their eyes are cold and bright and they see everything: the gray water, the gray stone, the little crooked streets full of smoke and morning bread-smell.

Below them, small as a beetle, Rafael walks through the fish market with his hood up. He carries a basket. In the basket, under herring and a folded blanket, is Wren.

She can feel the basket against her back. She can feel the salt wind on her face at the same time. Two bodies at once is strange, like wearing two coats. The gulls scream — *UP UP UP, BREAD-BRINGER, BREAD* — and somewhere below a fishwife throws a crust into the air and a hundred wings beat at once, and for one moment Wren forgets to be afraid.

Then she looks down through the gull-eyes again, and sees the gray cloaks.

There are four of them. They move through the market the way oil moves through water. People step aside without looking at them, the way you step around a hole in the dark. Their cloaks are not wet even though it rained all night. Their boots do not splash.

Wren makes herself very small in the basket.

The man who smells wrong — the biggest one, with a voice like a door closing — stops near the charm-stall where Rafael stood yesterday. He picks something up. Even from this high, through the gull's fierce little heart, Wren can feel what he holds: a charm that used to be nothing, a bit of glass and string, but now it *aches*. It aches the way her own chest aches when she remembers fire.

He puts it in a leather bag at his belt. The bag is full of other things that ache.

*Caged,* says the oldest gull, perched on the chimney above Rafael's shop. The gulls talk in pictures more than words. The picture they send Wren is of a bird in a box too small, beating its wings forever. *They carry cages. They walk past and the rats go quiet and the dogs will not bark and even the fleas jump off.*

Wren curls her fingers around the edge of the basket. Below, Rafael has stopped. His shoulders are stiff. She knows that stiffness. It is the way he stands when he is trying to be nobody.

One gray cloak turns its head. Slowly. Like a weathervane finding the wind.

It looks at Rafael.

*Not him,* Wren tells the gulls, though she doesn't know if telling does anything. *Please. Not him.* She doesn't know why it matters so much. She met him two nights ago, mud-soaked and burning-fevered, and he fed her soup and hid her from the wardens and did not once ask her to speak. Everyone else had asked. Everyone else had wanted the word, wanted the thing she could do, wanted to *use* her. Rafael just said, "You don't have to talk. Rest."

Nobody had ever said that before.

The gray cloak looks at Rafael for three wingbeats. Four. Then a cart overturns — an accident, or maybe not an accident; Wren will learn later that Sable paid a fishmonger two coppers to be clumsy — and eels spill everywhere, silver and furious, and the crowd surges, and when the gulls find Rafael again he is walking away, fast, toward the harbor stairs.

*Down,* Wren tells the gull whose eyes she shares. *Follow him down.*

But softly. Softly as snow.

* * *

The tunnel under the harbor smells of rot and rope and things that died long ago.

Rafael sets the basket down and lifts the blanket and there he is, his face, his tired gray eyes, his hands that shake a little when he thinks no one is watching. He puts one finger to his lips. Wren nods. She is good at quiet. Quiet was the first thing the fire taught her.

He speaks anyway, barely moving his lips, and the words are almost nothing, almost swallowed by the dark: "Gulls say four cloaks at the market. One of them stopped at my stall." His jaw works. "They took a charm. A nothing-charm. Why would they take a nothing-charm?"

Wren shrugs. Then, because she cannot help it, she reaches out and touches his sleeve.

Through her fingers she can hear what his body says, the way every living thing talks whether it means to or not. His pulse is a trapped bird. His bones are tired, tired, tired, fifteen years tired. And underneath all of it, so faint he probably doesn't know it's there, there is a thread of the old tongue running through him like a vein of gold in gray rock.

He doesn't know what he is. That is the strangest thing about Rafael Pemberton. He carries a whole language inside him and he has nailed the door shut.

The gulls told her about him before she ever came to his door. The gulls tell each other everything. *The charm-seller,* they had said, in their cold bright picture-way. *The one who hears us and pretends he doesn't. The one who answered a fledgling once, years ago, and then never again.* They had shown her his window, his crooked sign, the smell of glue and cedar. *If you must go to a human,* the oldest gull had said, *go to the one who is ashamed. Shame bends easier than pride. Shame keeps promises.*

So Wren had gone. Mud to the knees, feet bleeding, the waystation still burning behind her in the hills. She had stood at his door in the rain and spoken the only word she had strength left for, the word that made the rats go still, and watched his face come apart like wet paper.

She chose him. She wants to tell him that someday. She practices it sometimes in her head, in people-words, which are heavy and slow: *I picked you. Out of everyone. I picked you.*

Now he kneels in the dark tunnel and studies her with those tired eyes and says, "Can you walk? There's a boatman. Sable fixed it. We go out with the tide, quiet as anything, and by morning we're past the point and—" He stops himself. Smiles the smile that isn't really a smile. "Sorry. You don't need my worries. Come on."

He offers his hand.

Wren takes it.

His hand is warm and rough and it trembles, just slightly, and she grips it tighter to tell him: *I've got you too. It goes both ways now. That's how covenant works. Nobody told you?*

* * *

The boat is small and black and stinks of tar, and the boatman is a huge woman with a scarred jaw who never asks questions, which Wren likes immediately. Rats line the harbor pilings to watch them go. The rats send her a picture: the gray cloaks standing at the top of the harbor stairs, four of them, not moving, not talking, faces turned toward the water like compass needles.

*They know,* the rat-picture says. Rats are honest. It is their best and worst quality. *They don't know where. But they know.*

Wren tugs Rafael's sleeve. Points up the stairs with her chin.

He follows her look. His breath goes thin. "Right," he whispers. "Right. Okay." He turns to the boatwoman. "Marta. How fast can this thing—"

"Sit down, charm-seller," Marta says, and pushes off from the pilings, and the night swallows them.

For a while there is only water-sound and oar-sound and the city sliding away behind them, its thousand lamps reflected in pieces on the black water. Wren watches Ashvale shrink. Somewhere in that jumble of light is the waystation's ghost, and the burned sanctuary, and the people who smelled like her, the ones who spoke the old tongue, the ones who are ash now. She does not cry. Crying is loud. Loud is dead. The fire taught her that too.

Instead she listens to the harbor.

The harbor is full of voices. Barnacles singing their slow stone-hymns. A conger eel grumbling in its cave about the cold. Crabs quarreling over a dead fish. And beneath it all, far down, something enormous breathing — slow, patient, older than the city — some vast sea-thing turning over in its sleep, murmuring fragments of the covenant-tongue in its dreams. Wren presses her palm flat against the hull to listen better. The wood hums.

Rafael notices. Of course he notices. "What is it?" he whispers.

She points down. Then she makes the shape with her hands: something big. Something sleeping.

He stares at the water like it might stare back. "There's something down there?"

She nods.

"Of course there is," he mutters, half to himself. "Fifteen years I rowed over this harbor and there was a whole conversation going on underneath me the entire time." He laughs, very quietly, and it is a real laugh this time, cracked and surprised. "I used to think the silence was peace. You know that? I thought I'd found a way to live in the quiet."

Wren looks at him. Through the boat's planks, through the water, the great sleeping thing breathes. Above, the gulls circle the mast-head, keeping watch, keeping their promise, keeping count of their debt. The world is so *loud*, everywhere, always, every living thing singing the old song in its own throat. She cannot imagine choosing silence. She cannot imagine being so afraid of a gift that you bury it alive for fifteen years.

But she saw his face the first night, when the rats went still. Under the terror there had been something else. Hunger. A starving man smelling bread.

*I picked you,* she thinks again. *Because you're the only one who locked it away and hated yourself for it. The others who hear — they use it, or they sell it, or they feed people to Corvane for coin. You just... suffered. Suffering people keep faith. The gulls said so.*

The oldest gull lands on the gunwale, folds its wings, and fixes Rafael with one yellow eye. It says, in the old tongue, clear as a bell across the water: *"Debt-counting begins at dawn, keeper-that-won't-be. Three favors spent. The fourth will cost."*

Rafael goes white. "I know," he whispers. "I know. I'll pay it. Whatever it is."

The gull cocks its head. *"Will you?"* it says, and there is something in its voice that is not unkind but is not kind either, something old and patient and terribly sure of itself. *"We shall see, little breaker. We shall see."*

And it flies off into the dark, and Wren watches Rafael sit very still, watching it go, and she sees him mouth the word *breaker* like it tastes of blood.

She doesn't understand yet. But the water understands, and the wind understands, and far ahead, past the point, past the hills, something vast and weary and burning-low lifts its head from an ancient sleep — and listens.

* * *

Dawn finds them in the reeds past the river mouth, hull grinding soft into the mud, mist lying on the water like a held breath.

Marta ties off the boat and says only, "Walk east till you hit the old road. Don't walk it after dark." Then she is gone into the fog, and Wren decides she likes her very much.

They eat hard bread and dried fish. Rafael keeps checking the sky. Wren keeps checking the ground, because the ground talks too — earthworms gossiping, roots drinking, and under it all, faint and getting fainter, a thread of the old song running east like a buried river. Toward the hills. Toward whatever is left of the sanctuary-road.

That's when the crickets stop.

All of them. At once. The silence rolls toward them through the reeds like a wave, and Wren is on her feet before she knows she's moved, every hair on her arms standing up.

Rafael feels it too — she can see it hit him, that animal prickle he pretends not to have. "What?" he whispers. "What is it?"

She grabs his hand and pulls him down into the reeds.

Through the mist they come. Four shapes on the far bank, walking the waterline where no boat has landed. Gray cloaks dry in the river-damp. Boots that leave no prints in the mud. And the leader stops, and turns its hooded head, and breathes in — long and slow, tasting the air the way Wren tastes air, except what it tastes is not scent.

It is tasting magic. Tasting *her*.

The cloaked thing speaks to its fellows in a voice like a lid closing, and the sound crosses the river flat and wrong, and every word of it is people-language but underneath, wound through it like wire through wax, is a sliver of the old tongue — stolen, bent, screaming quietly. Caged magic made to serve. Wren claps both hands over her mouth so the whimper stays inside.

*Rafael,* she screams inside her own skull, *they're coming, they're coming, run, RUN—*

But he is already moving. He has her up in his arms before she finishes the thought, crashing through the reeds, east, east, toward the old road, and behind them the mist parts and the gray cloaks step onto the water and do not sink.

Wren looks back once, over Rafael's shoulder.

The lead cloak raises its hand. Not toward them.

Toward the sky — where, high and high above, a single gull wheels, screaming the alarm in the old tongue, screaming *east, east, they go east* — and the gray sleeve flicks, and the gull drops out of the air like a stone.

Wren feels it die. Feels it the way you feel your own tooth pulled. Her scream finally escapes, small and ragged, and Rafael runs faster, and the last thing she sees before the reeds close is the mist swallowing the four shapes as they begin, unhurried, to follow.

East. Toward the old road. Toward Bracken, who waits in the roots, and does not yet know what is coming.

Toward the dragon-door, with death walking on the water behind them.
