## 9. Corvane's Cage

The wisp had been beautiful once. Corvane could still see the shape of that beauty beneath the gray—like a candle flame seen through dirty glass, guttering in its cage of iron and salt. It had been caught three years ago in the marshes south of Ashvale, singing to the reeds. Now it sang to nothing, and its song kept him alive.

He sat in his study above the Inquisition hall, ledger open before him, and drank.

Not wine. Wine was for men who feared death honestly. What Corvane drank came from the cage: a thin thread of light that crossed the room from the wisp's prison to his open mouth, tasting of ozone and summer and something older than either. Each swallow bought him a day. He had been buying days for sixty years.

The ledger's pages were vellum scraped thin as skin. In his own hand, in ink mixed with ash and tallow, he recorded every confiscation: the hedge-witch's familiar taken in the spring, the charmer's cantrips seized at the Saltgate, the eleven small magics pulled from the waystation in the hills before it burned. Eleven entries there. He ran one dry finger down them and felt each one like a coin in a purse. Small things. Trinkets, mostly. But trinkets added up. Everything added up. That was the whole secret of his long life—not power, but arithmetic.

A knock. Two raps, precise, eager.

"Enter."

Warden Ilsa Vane came in the way she always did, as if the door might be an ambush. She was twenty-three, sharp-boned, with a scar through one eyebrow she'd earned putting down a riot in the Tannery Quarter at nineteen. She had reported the riot herself, unprompted, and had wept afterward—not for the dead, but because one of her own wardens had hesitated. Corvane had marked her that day. Zeal was common. Zeal that examined itself was rare, and useful, until it wasn't.

"General." She saluted. "The harbor report."

"Read it."

She unrolled the paper. "Patrols in the charm quarter logged anomalous vermin behavior over three nights. Rats emerging in daylight, motionless, oriented toward a single building on Chandler's Lane. Then, two nights past, gulls massing over the rooftops near the seawall—hundreds of them, silent, in formation. The patrol captain writes—" she glanced down, and something flickered across her face, quickly mastered, "—he writes that it was like watching soldiers."

"And what did you think, Warden, when you read that?"

"I thought vermin don't form ranks, General."

"No," Corvane said softly. "They don't. Not for anyone but a speaker."

He rose and went to the window. Below, Ashvale lay in its usual shroud of chimney smoke and drizzle, gray stone stacked on gray stone, a city that had learned to be afraid so thoroughly it had forgotten fear was ever a choice. Good. Fear was load-bearing. He had helped build this city on it, brick by brick, hanging after hanging, and it held him up as much as he held it.

Beast-speech. The oldest gift, the first gift, the one the old covenant had been woven from. He had spent decades pulling its practitioners out of attics and cellars and poorhouses, one by one, and grinding their small talents into the ledger. A rat-speaker here, a horse-whisperer there. Never enough to matter alone. Always enough to add.

But a speaker who could command gulls in formation—that was not a trinket. That was a thread leading somewhere.

"The child," Ilsa said. "You believe the vermin behavior is connected to the fugitive from the waystation."

"I believe nothing, Warden. I follow evidence, and evidence follows pattern." He turned. "The child fled a burning sanctuary in the hills. Sanctuary buildings do not burn themselves. Someone carried her toward my city, and someone—or something—in my city has now made contact with her. Birds do not gather in silence around empty air. There is a speaker on Chandler's Lane, and the child is close enough to touch."

"You want the lane swept."

"I want the lane *understood* first. Sweep it and we get a frightened shopkeeper and a dead end. Watch it and we get a thread." He smiled, and knew from long practice how the smile looked on his face—the one his wardens called the ledger-smile behind their hands. "Pull the patrol reports for every street within a mile of that building. Every complaint about animals, every oddity, however small. Fifteen years of records if they go back that far. Patterns are patient things, Warden. They wait to be found."

"Yes, General." She hesitated. It was a hair's hesitation, barely a breath, but Corvane had spent sixty years reading hesitation the way priests read scripture. "May I ask—why does the child matter so much? The report from the waystation listed her as mute. Unmarked. No recorded gift."

Corvane considered her. This was the moment, and he chose it deliberately, the way he chose everything. Trust was a currency too, and he paid it out in drops precisely because he never truly spent any.

"Because the sanctuary did not shelter a mute child, Warden. Sanctuaries sheltered *keys*. The old covenant was a lock on something vast, and locks have keys, and keys are born, not made. If the covenant-priests hid a child in the hills, then that child is worth more than every entry in my ledgers combined." He let his voice drop, let the hunger show just enough to be instructive. "And whoever is hiding her now knows what she is. Which means they will run her to whatever remains of the old faith. And that path leads to only one place in this world."

"The dragon," Ilsa said. Her voice had gone carefully flat.

"Emberlain. Yes." He watched her face. Young wardens always flinched at the name; they'd been raised on stories of the fire that ended the Dragon Wars, fire that had not been seen in a hundred years and was said to be dying anyway. "There is no dragon, Warden. There is a legend, and legends make excellent bait. Whatever is left out there in the caldera is an animal, and animals die. But if our fugitives believe otherwise—if they walk into the mountains believing they carry salvation—we will know exactly where to find them, and we will take everything they bring us. The child. The speaker. Whatever relics the old faith stuffed into their pockets when they ran."

He returned to his desk and dismissed her with a nod. At the door she paused again.

"General. The patrol captain also wrote that the gulls... after they dispersed, three of them were found dead on the seawall. No wounds. As if they'd simply stopped."

"Exertion," Corvane said. "Command costs the commanded more than the commander. Note it in the file."

She left. He listened to her boots recede down the stair—too fast, he noted, too brisk—and then he turned to the cage.

* * *

They brought him the gull at dusk.

It had been netted off the seawall by a warden with quick hands and no imagination, one of the three survivors of the strange gathering, and it came into the interrogation room wrapped in sacking, screaming in the ordinary voice of a frightened bird. Corvane waited until the door was barred and the two duty wardens had withdrawn to the corridor, where they would hear nothing and report nothing, because they were paid in the only coin that guaranteed silence.

Then he set the cage on the table, uncovered it, and spoke.

"Not the bird-voice," he said. "The other one. You know the one I mean."

The gull went still. Its head cocked, one yellow eye finding him, and in that eye Corvane saw the thing he had seen perhaps a dozen times in sixty years: recognition. The animal understood that it was not looking at a man. It was looking at a thing wearing a man the way a coat is worn.

"*Thief*," the gull said.

The covenant-tongue had no sound in the air, properly speaking—it lived in the space between ear and understanding—but Corvane heard it anyway, had always heard it, since the night in his childhood when something in the fen had spoken to him and he had answered wrongly and been changed by the answering. The word landed in him like a hook.

"Yes," he agreed pleasantly. "I am. And you are going to tell me what you were doing over the rooftops of my city."

"*Sky is ours. Wind is ours.*"

"Everything is mine," Corvane said, "that I have paid for." He opened the ledger to a fresh page. From a drawer he took the instrument—a rod of black iron, coiled at one end with wire drawn from melted covenant-silver, confiscated from a priestess forty years ago whose name he no longer bothered to remember. He did not enjoy this part. That was important to remember about himself, he had always felt: that he did not enjoy it. Enjoyment was sloppy. He did it the way a farmer culls a herd, without appetite, because appetite corrupted arithmetic.

"What happens next," he told the gull, "is that I press this to the bars of your cage, and it will pull the speech out of you the way a tap pulls sap. You will feel it leave. Most birds describe it—I say most, I mean the ones capable of description—as cold. When it is done you will be an ordinary gull, which is to say food for cats within the week. Or you can tell me what I want to know, and I will release you at the harbor with my compliments."

The gull screamed at him in both tongues at once, and the covenant-word it used was old and filthy and very much alive.

Corvane sighed and touched the rod to the iron.

The effect was immediate and ugly. The gull's scream cut off mid-note. Its feathers lifted as though in wind, though the room was still, and beneath them its skin dimmed visibly, the way a lamp dims when another lamp is lit elsewhere. The wire drank. Corvane watched the light travel up the rod and into his own hand, into the veins of his wrist, where it joined the slow river of everything else he had ever taken, and he felt the day's payment arrive early, sweet and cold, sixty years of borrowed mornings all agreeing with one another.

"*Who sent you*," he said, and the rod pulsed, and the question was less a question than a key turning.

And the gull broke.

Not all at once. Birds were not built for resistance; they had no doctrine of silence, no cause greater than the flock. It came out of the animal in fragments, images more than words, and Corvane took them and laid them in order the way he laid everything in order:

A rooftop in the charm quarter. A man standing below in the rain—thin, gray at the temples, smelling of lamp-oil and dried herbs and *fear*, fear worn so long it had become a kind of clothing. The man's mouth moving in the old tongue, clumsy, rusted, but true. True speech. A keeper's cadence under the rust.

Coins of bread thrown upward. Words exchanged. A bargain struck: watch the roads, count the travelers, sing when you see the small one.

*The small one.*

The image came through sharp as a fishhook: a child, mud-caked, hooded, walking beside the thin man through a lamplit door. And around the child, in the gull's seeing, a shape that made even the bird's simple mind recoil—a brightness, a *keyness*, the same signature the old sanctuaries used to radiate, the signature Corvane had spent a lifetime learning to smell.

The child lived. The child was in Ashvale. The child had found a speaker.

Corvane withdrew the rod and sat back, and for a long moment the only sound was the gull's ragged breathing and the tick of the wisp's cage in the study above, faint through the floor, keeping its own time.

"Well," he said quietly. "Hello, little key."

He looked at the gull. It huddled at the bottom of the cage, duller-eyed now, its feathers gone gray at the edges where the color had been drunk out. There was perhaps a season of life left in it, maybe less. He found, examining himself, no pity and no guilt, only a mild professional satisfaction, and he noted that about himself too, in the ledger of his own character that he kept as scrupulously as the other one. Pity was a leak. He had sealed his leaks long ago.

"Chandler's Lane," he said aloud, to the empty room. "Fifteen years of quiet, and now this."

Because that was the part that snagged in him like a burr. He knew the charm quarter's inventory of speakers—he had personally closed that account twice over, the last time nine years ago, a rat-charmer named Odell who had wept beautifully and yielded a talent worth four entries in the ledger. The quarter should have been empty. Unless someone had been sitting there all along, hidden so deep and so patiently that even Corvane's patterns had missed him.

Someone who had known how to stay invisible. Someone who had learned fear the way the city had learned it.

Someone who had, perhaps, been there the night the covenant-stone cracked.

Corvane's hand stilled on the ledger. Memory stirred, old and cold, the fen again, the voice in the reeds, the boy he had been before the answering. He put the memory away. It was the one thing he never let himself count.

He rang for the duty warden.

"Take this to the mews," he said. "Feed it. Keep it alive. It may still be useful." The warden blinked—mercy, from the General?—and Corvane smiled his ledger-smile. Dead birds told no further tales, but a living gull in a cage full of living gulls was a listening thing, and speakers always came back to their instruments. "And send Warden Vane to me at first light. Full authorization. She'll want to lead the Chandler's Lane detail herself."

* * *

Ilsa Vane stood in the corridor outside the interrogation room for a long time after the duty warden carried the cage away.

She had not been meant to hear anything. The door was thick, the corridor empty. But she had come back for her gloves—left on the table during the evening briefing—and she had heard the General's voice through the wood, low and reasonable, and she had heard the other voice answer.

Birds did not talk. Ilsa had grown up on a farm; she had killed chickens and driven crows off the seed-barley and she knew, with the flat certainty of a practical woman, that birds did not *talk*.

Except the General had asked one, and it had answered, and then it had screamed, and the screaming had stopped in a way that screaming between people did not stop—abruptly, like a snuffed wick—and afterward there had been a silence with a texture to it that she felt in her teeth even now.

She flexed her gloved hands and made herself think like a warden. The General took magic from the convicted; everyone knew that; it was the whole architecture of the law, the tithe of the guilty, power surrendered to the state that protected the innocent from it. She had never asked where the confiscated gifts *went*. Wardens didn't ask. Asking was the first step down a road that ended with you in a cell, or worse, in a ledger.

But she had heard a bird speak. And she had seen, when the General read the patrol report, the way he had said *my city*—not the Protectorate's, not Ashvale's. His.

At first light she would stand in his study and accept the promotion he was clearly preparing for her, and she would lead the sweep of Chandler's Lane, and she would do it well, because she was good at her work and her work was all she had ever wanted to be good at.

She pushed open the door to fetch her gloves. The interrogation room was empty, scrubbed, the table wiped clean. Only the black iron rod remained, lying in the center of the table where the General had left it, and as Ilsa crossed the room she found she could not quite keep herself from touching it—one fingertip, brief, the way you touch a stove to learn what heat is.

The metal was warm. Warm, and faintly singing, a note below hearing, like a hive heard through a wall.

She snatched her hand back, took her gloves from the hook, and left, and did not let herself run until the corridor turned and she was out of sight of the door.

Behind her, in the darkened room, the rod lay on the table and went on singing to no one—except that far above, in the study, the wisp in its cage pressed itself against the bars and listened, and for the first time in three years, it sang back.
