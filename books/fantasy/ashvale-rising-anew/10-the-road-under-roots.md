## 10. The Road Under Roots

The old covenant-road began where the King's Road ended, which was to say it began in a ditch.

Rafael stood at the edge of the drainage culvert three miles north of Ashvale's walls and looked at what fifteen years of forgetting had made of the way his teacher used to walk. It wasn't a road anymore. It was a suggestion. Alders had marched across it in ranks, their roots shouldering up through paving stones that had once been fitted so close a coin couldn't slip between them. Moss had eaten the rest. Somewhere under all that green was a highway that had run from the sea to the mountains when Ashvale was a fishing village with a name nobody remembered.

"It's under there," Sable said beside him. She had her lamplighter's pole slung across her back like a spear and Wren's hand wrapped in hers like she'd been doing it for years instead of four days.

"I know it's under there."

"You're staring at it like it owes you money."

"It might." He crouched and pulled back a curtain of bramble. Beneath, half-swallowed by root and loam, a stone still bore its carving: an interlocking spiral, worn nearly smooth, the mark of the road-keepers. He touched it and felt nothing, which was the whole of his life in one gesture. Once, stone like this would have hummed against his palm. Once, he could have asked the moss how long it had been growing and the moss would have answered, slowly, in the way moss answers everything.

He let the bramble fall.

Wren tugged his sleeve. She pointed down the drowned line of the road, then at her own eyes, then made a shape with both hands—wings, or maybe leaves—and looked at him with the particular patience of a child who has explained something twice already and is prepared to explain it forever.

"Yes," he said. "I see it."

She shook her head, exasperated, and stamped one small boot on the ground. Then she pointed at the ground again, harder, as if the ground were being deliberately obtuse.

"She's saying there's someone coming," Sable said. "On the road. She's been trying to tell you for a minute."

Rafael went cold. "Gray cloaks?"

Wren frowned at him with the full weight of seven-year-old contempt, held up two fingers, and wiggled them.

"Two someones," Sable translated. "Small ones. Four legs."

The bracken at the roadside exploded.

Rafael had fought in no wars and won no duels; his body's entire repertoire of courage consisted of flinching, and it flinched now, staggering back into Sable, who shoved him upright with the ease of long practice. Out of the dead alders came a wolf.

Not a young wolf. That was the first thing. The second thing was that the wolf was gray-muzzled and stiff-legged and walking with the deliberate care of a creature whose hips had filed a formal complaint about the arrangement, and the third thing—the thing that stopped Rafael's heart and restarted it wrong—was that the wolf looked at him and said, in the old tongue, slow and gravelled as stones turning over in a riverbed:

*So. The little coward grew a beard.*

Every beast-speech had its own texture. Rats spoke quick and sly, all corners. Gulls spoke in shrieks and demands. But wolves—old wolves—spoke the way winter speaks. Nothing wasted. Nothing hurried.

"You knew Master Odell," Rafael said. His voice came out strange.

*I knew a boy who was supposed to be standing where you're standing,* the wolf said. *Fifty years I've been standing here myself, waiting to see if he'd ever come back down this road. And look what the road sent me instead. A trinket-seller.* The wolf's nose wrinkled, pulling back a lip over yellowed teeth. *You smell like tin and candle smoke.*

"That's the trade."

*That's the hiding.*

Sable's breath caught. "Bracken," she said softly. "You old ruin. You're alive."

The wolf turned his head to her, and something in the flat winter of his voice thawed by a single degree. *Lamplighter. They told me you were dead.*

"They tell everyone that eventually. It saves on correspondence." Sable crouched, and the wolf allowed her to scratch behind one scarred ear with the dignity of a king permitting tribute. "You knew we were coming?"

*I know everything that walks this road. It's my road. Has been since your priestess-kind stopped walking it and left it to those of us who stayed.* His eyes—pale gold, clouded at the edges with age—swung back to Rafael. Then to Wren.

Everything about the wolf changed. Not dramatically. Wolves don't do dramatic. But the set of his shoulders eased, and the growl that Rafael hadn't even realized was running under every word fell silent, and Bracken lowered his great gray head until it was level with the child's.

Wren reached out and put her small hand flat on his nose.

And Bracken, who had just called Rafael a coward to his face, who had waited fifty years on a dead road for a man who never came, closed his eyes and leaned into the touch like a dog.

*Well,* he said quietly. *There you are.*

* * *

They walked the old road for six days, and the road taught Rafael things about himself he didn't want to learn.

The first thing it taught him was that he'd forgotten more than he thought. Bracken led them along the buried spine of the covenant-way, but the wolf expected Rafael to find the safe footing, the hidden culverts, the places where the paving had collapsed into sinkholes deep enough to swallow a cart. *Your teacher made you walk this road blindfolded,* Bracken said on the second morning, watching Rafael feel along a root-wall for the handhold Odell had shown him twenty-five years ago. *Did you forget that too?*

"I forgot most of it on purpose."

*Yes. That's what cowards do. They call it forgetting. It's just hoarding fear until it's too heavy to carry, and then calling the pile wisdom.*

"You're very talkative for someone who spent fifty years alone."

*I wasn't talking to anyone worth the breath.* But there was no real venom in it, and by the third day Rafael understood that the insults were a kind of ritual, almost a comfort—that Bracken insulted him the way Odell used to, the way teachers insult the students they've decided not to give up on, and that the wolf had decided something about him somewhere between the culvert and the first night's fire, and had decided it without consulting Rafael at all.

The second thing the road taught him was Wren.

She was different out here. In Ashvale she had been a held breath—a small silent presence pressed into corners, flinching from boots on stairs. On the covenant-road she unfolded. She ran ahead along the root-ridges with her arms out for balance. She learned to skip stones off the black pools in the sunken culverts. She collected things—acorns, a blue jay feather, a river-smoothed stone with a spiral worn into it that she carried in her cheek pouch of a pocket like treasure—and presented each find to the group with the solemnity of a magistrate.

And she talked. Not aloud. But constantly, ceaselessly, to everything. To the jays, who answered in rude jokes Rafael pretended not to translate. To the roots themselves, which answered slowly, if at all, in the vegetative murmur of things that measure time in centuries. To Bracken, endlessly, and Bracken—this was the miracle—answered back. Grumpily, tersely, but he answered, and Rafael watched the old wolf's stiffness loosen day by day under the sheer persistence of a child's attention, and understood that Wren was doing for Bracken what Sable was doing for Rafael: refusing to let the silence win.

On the fourth evening she found Rafael sitting apart from the fire, keeping the watch he'd assigned himself, and climbed into his lap without asking, the way children do, the way daughters do, and fell asleep against his chest mid-thought, one hand still curled around her spiral stone.

Rafael sat very still.

He had held the moment at arm's length for four days. He knew what it was. Naming it felt dangerous in a way Corvane's wardens never had, because wardens could only kill you, and this could unmake you. Fifteen years he had kept his life small on purpose—small shop, small circle, small wants—because small things don't break loudly when they're taken away. And here was a child asleep on his chest breathing trust into his shirt, and Sable thirty feet away laughing at something Bracken had said, and the fire warm, and the old road humming faintly under the moss the way it hadn't hummed in decades—not to him, never to him again, but humming nonetheless, alive, because Wren was near—and Rafael Pemberton sat in the middle of all of it and understood that he had already lost. Whatever happened at the end of this road, whatever Emberlain wanted, whatever the covenant cost—he was no longer a man with a shop. He was a man with a daughter, and a family, and something to protect, and there was no smaller size available after that.

Wren murmured in her sleep. Her fingers tightened on the stone.

"All right," Rafael whispered, to her, to the fire, to nobody. "All right."

*You're talking to yourself now,* Bracken said from the darkness. *That's the second stage. First you hide, then you mutter, then you start apologizing to trees. I've watched it happen.*

"Go to sleep, you miserable fossil."

*I'm keeping the other watch.*

"You can barely keep your own hips."

The wolf's laugh was a low rumble, almost subsonic, and Rafael felt it through the ground and through the sleeping child and thought: *this is what I gave up. This is what I hid from. God help me, what was I afraid of?*

He knew what he'd been afraid of. He'd spend the whole confession on it soon enough—Sable's eyes said so every time she looked at him across the fire, patient and terrible, a debt accruing interest. But lying there with Wren's weight warm against him, listening to the night birds speak their small bright gossip in a language he could still understand for a few more weeks, maybe days, Rafael let himself not think about it. Just for tonight.

Just for tonight, the road was kind.

* * *

It stopped being kind on the sixth day.

They had come up out of the lowlands into hill country, where the covenant-road climbed along a ridge above a river valley, and the going was slower—Bracken's hips worsening, the ruins thicker, the weather turning. Rain came in off the western hills around noon, a fine gray sift that got under collars and turned the leaf-mold slick. They sheltered midafternoon in the ribs of a waystation—not the burned one, thank all mercies, but a smaller cousin of it, a covenant-rest with its roof gone and its hearthstone split, and while Sable got a fire going in the lee of the wall and Wren chased a hedgehog around the foundations in what appeared to be a formal debate, Rafael climbed to the ridge crest to look back the way they'd come.

He told himself it was habit. Watchfulness. Good practice.

But his eyes went to the road behind them the way a tongue goes to a cracked tooth, and what he saw made his stomach drop through the wet moss and into the cold stone underneath.

Smoke. Not woodsmoke—woodsmoke rises lazy and domestic. This was a thin straight column, disciplined, far back along the valley, perhaps half a day behind them. Camp smoke. Someone was following the same road they were, at marching pace, and not bothering to hide it.

He watched until the rain blurred his eyes, counting. He couldn't count them. But the smoke said numbers, and the numbers said more than a patrol. Patrols moved fast and light. This moved like a column.

He went back down. Sable took one look at his face and started kicking dirt over the fire.

"How far?" she said.

"Half a day. Maybe less. They're not hurrying."

"They don't need to hurry," Sable said. "They know where we're going. There's only one place this road ends." She glanced at Wren, who had stopped chasing the hedgehog and gone very still, head cocked, listening to something none of them could hear—or rather, something only Rafael and Bracken could, and Wren could, and the hedgehog presumably could, and the world itself could, and Rafael was the only one pretending otherwise.

"What is it?" he asked her gently.

Wren pointed at the ground. Then at the ridge. Then she made the wing-shape with her hands again—but different this time, heavier, and her face was frightened.

"Crows," Rafael translated, and his mouth went dry. "A lot of crows, moving ahead of the column." Crows followed the gray cloaks the way gulls followed fishing boats. Crows meant Corvane's people fed well, because Corvane's people left a lot behind.

Bracken limped out of the trees, rain beading on his muzzle, and stood looking north up the road with his ears flat.

*The ford at Marrow Bridge,* he said. *It's the only crossing for twenty miles, and the bridge is broken. Your teacher broke it himself, the year the purges started. Burned the pilings so the wardens' horses couldn't come up the road.* He turned his pale eyes on Rafael. *Which means the gray cloaks will have to stop and rebuild, or find another way, and either way we have a day on them. Maybe two.*

"And if we don't cross?"

*Then we die in these hills in winter, slowly, which is worse than dying quickly at a ford.* The wolf snorted rain off his nose. *There's a third option. There's always a third option. It's just never a good one.*

"Tell me anyway."

*Under the road.* Bracken nodded at the ruined waystation, at the split hearthstone. *The covenant-roads have roots, boy. Real ones. The road-keepers cut ways beneath the ways—for messengers, for the wounded, for the nights when the surface belonged to enemies. Most of them are flooded or fallen. But there's one that runs under the ridge to the river gorge, and I've slept in its mouth every winter for forty years, and it's dry, and it goes where we need to go.*

"Then what's wrong with it?"

The wolf looked at him for a long moment. Rain dripped off his whiskers.

*Nothing's wrong with it,* he said at last. *Except that it's a covenant-way, and covenant-ways remember who walks them. And the last time you stood on covenant stone, you broke it.* He began to limp toward the split hearthstone, speaking over his shoulder, and his voice was not cruel—it was tired, tired the way only very old things are tired. *So we'll see, won't we. Whether the road lets you pass. Whether you've grown anything under that beard besides hair.*

Sable caught Rafael's eye. She didn't say anything. She didn't have to. The confession was coming; the road itself was dragging it out of him mile by mile, and they both knew it.

Wren slipped her cold little hand into his.

Together, the four of them—two liars, a lamplighter, and a child who was neither—pried up the hearthstone and looked down into the dark throat of the old road, where a draft rose cool and mineral-smelling, and somewhere far below, faint as memory, something ancient stirred and listened for footsteps.

The stone under their feet began, very softly, to hum.
