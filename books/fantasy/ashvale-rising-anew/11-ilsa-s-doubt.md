## 11. Ilsa's Doubt

The rain had followed Ilsa Vane out of Ashvale for three days, and she was beginning to suspect it was not weather at all but a message.

She crouched at the treeline where the old covenant-road vanished into overgrowth, watching the mud for sign. Behind her, six wardens waited with the particular patience of men who did not believe in the assignment. Warden Doss chewed a stem of something. Warden Pell had already twice suggested that the Inquisitor General's interest in a runaway child and a charm-seller was a waste of the Gray Cloaks' time, and both times Ilsa had let the suggestion die without answering, because she did not yet know how to answer it.

"Sign's cold," Doss said, spitting the stem. "Three days of rain, Warden. Whatever came through here, the ground's forgotten it."

"The ground forgets nothing," Ilsa said. "It just stops telling."

Pell snorted. She ignored him. She had learned tracking from her father, a poacher turned gamekeeper turned hanged man, and the first thing he had taught her was that the land was a witness that could be bribed by weather but never fully silenced. Mud held. Moss held. The way a broken fern bent back toward the light held, if you knew how old the bend was.

And the mud here held *something*. A boot-heel pressed deep on the left side, favoring it. A smaller print beside it, half-washed, the toes turned inward — a child, walking close, walking *trusting*. And here, a day older, the drag of something heavy and four-legged, moving slow, favoring a forelimb.

A lame animal traveling with them. A wolf, by the gait.

"Here," she said. "They went north along the old road. Toward the waystation."

Doss frowned. "The waystation burned. Two years back. Nothing up there but ash and the dead."

"Which is why they'd go there," Ilsa said, standing. "Nobody watches a graveyard."

She did not say the other thing, the thing that had been sitting in her chest since the Inquisitor General had handed her the assignment with his dry, patient voice and his ledger-open hands: *Why does the Inquisitor General of Ashvale care so much about one mute child?* Corvane did not spend resources idly. She had served under him for nine years and had never once seen him move wardens on a guess. Whatever the child was, Corvane knew. And knowing, he had sent *her* — the youngest warden ever promoted to field command, the loyalist, the one whose file said *unswerving* — because she would ask no questions.

She was beginning to ask questions. She told herself it was diligence.

* * *

They reached the waystation at dusk, and Ilsa understood at once why the fugitives had come.

It sat on a shoulder of hill above the tree line, a long stone building that had once been handsome — she could see the bones of it under the black, arched windows, a slate roof now mostly fallen in, a bell-tower whose bell had melted in its yoke. Fire had taken it thoroughly and strangely: not the messy chaos of an accidental blaze but the clean, total consumption of something *fed*. Every beam charred to the same depth. Every door burned from the outside in.

"Doors burned from outside," she said quietly, to no one. "Someone kept them shut."

"What was this place?" Pell asked. He had gone subdued. Even the rain seemed quieter here.

"A sanctuary," said Warden Hesk, who was old enough to remember. "Before your time. The covenant-folk kept it — healers, mostly. They took in anyone. Gifted children, sometimes." He crossed himself in the old way, thumb to sternum, the gesture the Reforms had technically outlawed. "They said the beasts came here to be healed too. That the stones remembered every creature ever carried through them."

"And then?"

Hesk's jaw worked. "And then the Reforms came north. There was a fire. There were always fires."

Ilsa walked the perimeter while her wardens made cold camp in the lee of the ruin. She had ordered no fire of their own; something about the place made flame feel obscene. The rain had softened to a fine drifting mist, and in it the ruin gave up its dead slowly.

She found the grave behind the bell-tower, where the hill dropped away and the ground dipped into a hollow no one passing on the road would ever see.

It was not a pyre. Pyres were for the honored dead, the Reformed dead, the ones the wardens burned with prayers and paperwork. This was a trench, long and shallow, and the earth over it had been turned by shovels in a hurry, and it was too short for the number of people who had died here, and so they had been laid in rows, folded, like linens.

She knew because the rain had opened a seam at the trench's edge, and through the seam she could see a hand. Small. A child's hand, the bones showing white through the skin that the wet earth had not fully claimed.

Ilsa Vane had buried her father at fourteen. She had pulled three drowned children from the Vesseny canal and failed to revive the third. She had stood witness at forty-one hangings and written the reports. She was not a soft woman, and she had never wanted to be.

She knelt at the edge of the trench and was sick, quietly, into the wet grass.

* * *

Afterward she did the thing she could not afterward explain to herself. She went back to the ruin and she began, alone, in the dark, with only a hand-lantern shuttered to a coin of light, to read it.

The waystation had been a sanctuary, and sanctuaries kept records. That was doctrine — old doctrine, covenant doctrine, but the Reforms had kept it, because records were how you hunted. Somewhere in this ash there would be a ledger, a strongbox, something. Corvane wanted the child. Corvane had sent her. If there were records, they would tell her what the child *was*, and knowing that, she told herself, she would serve better. Diligence. Only diligence.

She found the strongbox in the sub-cellar beneath the collapsed kitchen, behind a wall that had been broken open from the inside — someone had died trying to reach it, or trying to hide it. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had kept the worst of the wet out, lay a ledger bound in gray leather, and beneath the ledger a smaller thing: a slate, cracked, with a child's letters scratched into it over and over, the same word, dozens of times, in a hand that got steadier as it went down the slate.

The word was *Wren*.

Ilsa sat down on a fallen beam with the ledger in her lap and her lantern between her feet, and she read until the candle guttered.

The ledger was a sanctuary register. Names, dates, ailments, dispositions. Healed and sent home. Healed and kept. Born here. Died here, with causes written in a careful clerical hand: *fever, birthing, age, the long sleep*. Page after page of ordinary mercy, years and years of it, and then the entries changed.

*Received: two families out of Ashvale, fleeing the Third Purge. Kept.*

*Received: the miller's boy, marked by the wardens for the tongue he was born with. Kept.*

*Received: a child of the Vesseny docks, four years old, mute since the fire that took her family. The beasts bring her gifts. She speaks to them in the old way. Kept — and hidden. She is a key. We do not yet know what it opens.*

The hand writing that last entry was different. Older. Firmer. And beneath it, dated two years past, in that same hand, a final entry, the ink hurried, the letters pressed deep enough to tear the page:

*Gray cloaks came at night. Not a purge — a requisition. They asked for the key by name. When Mother Anselm refused, they burned the doors and kept them shut. I write this in the cellar. If anyone finds this: the order came from above. It bore the seal of the Inquisitor General. Corvane wants the key. Corvane has always wanted the key. He is collecting them. God of the green and the gray, whoever you are, whoever finds this — she is only a child. She is only a child.*

Ilsa closed the ledger.

She sat in the dark of the ruined sanctuary for a long time, hearing the rain, hearing her own heart, which had gone strange and loud.

*The order came from above. It bore the seal of the Inquisitor General.*

There was a version of this that was still loyal. She built it carefully in her mind, the way she built every report: the entry was the testimony of a dead traitor, a covenant-priestess, a criminal, writing under duress, slandering the man who had spent thirty years purging the very magic that had rotted the old orders. Corvane had hung sorcerers by the dozen. Corvane had made Ashvale safe. Corvane had promoted *her*, a gamekeeper's daughter with no family and no faction, on merit alone, because he was fair, because he was just, because he was —

Because he was the man who had sent six wardens and three days of hard travel after one mute child, with orders that read, in his own dry hand: *recover alive; the rest is immaterial.*

*The rest is immaterial.* She had read those words a hundred times since. She had taken them for efficiency. Now she knelt in a trench's shadow with a dead child's hand showing through the rain-loosened earth, and the words opened under her like a trapdoor.

*The rest is immaterial.* The wardens who burned the doors and held them shut. The healers folded into the ground like linens. The rest. All of it. Immaterial.

She thought of Corvane's hands, which she had never once seen age in nine years. She thought of the caged light in his office, the thing behind the glass that he glanced at, always, the way a man glances at a cup. She had assumed it was an instrument. A tool of the office, like the ledgers.

She thought: *what if the office is the tool?*

The vertigo came then, the true vertigo, the sick swinging of the world on its pin. Not grief — she could have carried grief. This was the floor of her life tilting. Nine years she had been a blade, and a blade does not ask what the hand intends. But she had always believed the hand was clean. That was the whole architecture of her loyalty: not love of Corvane, but faith that the law he served was real, that the hangings meant something, that her father's rope and the drowned children and the forty-one witnesses were all in service of a city that was *safer*. Take that away and what was she? A gamekeeper's daughter with a sword and a talent for finding people who only wanted to be left alone.

And she was very good at finding people who only wanted to be left alone. She thought of the small inward-turned footprints in the mud, the child walking close, walking trusting, and she understood that she was the best possible person for Corvane to send, and the worst.

* * *

Dawn came gray and thin. Ilsa stood before her wardens with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth inside her coat, against her ribs, where it felt less like evidence than like a coal.

"The trail splits at the ford," she said. "The wolf's gait worsens; they're slowing. I estimate four days to the high passes. We'll follow to the ford and report."

"Report to the General directly?" Pell asked. "He'll want word."

"The General's orders were to recover the child," Ilsa said. "I'll report when there's a child to report." She heard her own voice and was alarmed at how level it was. "Until then, we track. That's all. We track."

They moved out. She rode at the rear and made herself look back once at the waystation, at the bell-tower with its melted bell, and she made herself say the true thing to herself, because she was Ilsa Vane and she had never learned any other way: *You knew the doors burned from the outside. You knew the trench was wrong. You read the ledger and you believed it. You are no longer his.*

The rain had stopped. The mud held the fugitives' prints going north, and the mud held hers following, and for the first time in her life Ilsa Vane did not know which set of tracks she was on — theirs, or her own, out of the only country she had ever belonged to.

At the ford, she knew, the trail would tell her more. And at the ford, she also knew — though she could not yet have said how — something had happened there. The river ran high and strange, and the birds along its banks were silent, all of them, silent as if listening.

She spurred her horse north, carrying a dead priestess's ledger against her ribs, a coal she could not put down and could not, she was beginning to understand, survive.
