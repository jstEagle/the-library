## 12. The Guttering

Emberlain had counted ten thousand winters, and she was tired of counting.

She lay in the caldera's throat with her chin on the old stone, the way she had lain for a hundred years now, wings folded like a cathedral closing its doors. The mountain held her the way a hand holds a dying bird—gently, uselessly. Once her body had filled this crater and more, her tail coiling out over the rim, her breath scorching the snow off three peaks at once. Now there was room to spare. Now there was room everywhere, because she was smaller than her own legend, and shrinking still.

Her fire sat low in her chest like a coal buried in ash.

She did not sleep. Dragons past a certain age do not sleep; they only wait, in the long amber light behind their eyes, remembering. And so Emberlain remembered.

She remembered the first covenant, when the world was thick with voices. Every river had spoken then, every crow, every worm turning in the dark loam. The tongues of beasts and the tongue of men had been two dialects of one language, and the keepers stood at the crossroads between them, translating the world to itself. She remembered the stones—white, warm, humming—anchored at every threshold of the realm, holding the door shut against what lived on the other side. She remembered the other dragons, whose names were sung rather than said: Vaskarion of the northern ice, Mereth-sunder-the-clouds, little Quillwing who could fit inside a barn. She remembered them going out one by one, not in battle but in dimming, their fires guttering as the covenant thinned beneath them, until Emberlain alone remained, the last wick of a faith that had forgotten it was a faith.

She remembered Ashvale being built on the bones of the first sanctuary. She remembered the edicts, the ropes, the pyres. *Magic is a hanging crime.* As if magic were a thing men could outlaw, the way you might outlaw tide or morning.

And she remembered—a newer memory, an ugly one—the boy.

The boy who should have been a keeper. The stone that cracked under his refusal, a sound like a tooth breaking in the jaw of the world. She had felt it from here, felt it the way a mother feels a child stumble in another room. A hairline fracture running through the covenant, widening by fractions each year, letting the cold of the other realm seep through like water through a dam.

She did not blame him. That was the worst of it. She had watched him through the eyes of crows and foxes all these years—dragons see through any beast that will lend them its sight, if they spend the strength—and she had watched him flinch from his own gift, bury it, build a small gray life on top of it like a man building a house over a well. He was afraid. Fear was honest. Fear was human. She had eaten kings who felt no fear at all, and they had been far more dangerous.

But honesty did not mend stones.

* * *

On the day the chapter of her long waiting turned, Emberlain woke—for dragons do wake, briefly, when something changes—to the taste of wrongness in the air.

The barrier was fraying.

She had known it was thinning for decades; thinning is slow, a garment wearing smooth. This was different. This was a thread parting. She rose onto her forelegs, joints cracking like green wood in a fire, and stretched her senses out along the covenant-roads, the old white veins of the world, reading them the way a physician reads a pulse.

Faint. Fainter. In the east, where the waystation sanctuary had burned last summer, the vein was nearly dark—and something on the far side of it had noticed. Something patient. She felt it pressing, the way a prisoner presses a palm against a wall, testing. Not entering yet. Testing.

The things beyond the barrier had waited as long as she had. They were better at waiting than men were. Men forgot; the cold things beyond never forgot anything, because forgetting requires warmth.

Emberlain lowered her head and did the arithmetic she had been avoiding for a century. Her fire would fail within the year—two, if the winter was kind, and winters had stopped being kind. When the last dragon's fire went out, the covenant would not merely fray. It would unravel, all at once, like a cut net, and everything the door held back would come through into a world that had spent fifteen years teaching its children to fear the very people who could have fought it.

Unless the covenant was reforged.

Which required a keeper. Which required a key. Which required—

She felt it then. Felt *her.*

It came faintly, at the very edge of perception, the way you can hear your name spoken across a crowded market—not the words, just the shape of them, aimed at you. A small voice, speaking the old tongue. Not the broken dialect the beasts used among themselves these days, worn smooth and simple, but the true speech, the deep grammar, the language the covenant itself was written in. A voice that made the rats go still and the rivers listen.

A living key, walking toward her through the gray streets of Ashvale, and she had thought herself beyond surprise.

Emberlain opened her eyes. The caldera filled with their light, gold shot through with the green of banked fire, and the snow on the rim slid hissing into the clouds below. Somewhere in the mountain's roots, things that had denned near her for safety stirred and pressed themselves flat against the stone, awed and afraid, the way mice are afraid of a struck match.

*A keeper,* she thought. *And a key. At the end, when there is almost nothing left to keep.*

She reached out along the beast-minds of the city, spending strength she could not afford, the way a miser spends his last coin on flowers. It was difficult. Everything was difficult now. But she found threads: a gull's gossip, sharp and salt-flavored; a cat's contemptuous report of gray cloaks asking questions in the charm quarter; a rat's trembling account of a child who spoke one word and silenced a wall.

And the man. Rafael Pemberton. Charm-seller. Coward. Keeper-that-was-not. Walking beside the child with a face like a man carrying a full cup across a frozen lake.

Emberlain considered him with the long patience of her kind. She had watched him refuse his rite at twelve years old, weeping, his teacher's face going gray as the stone cracked. She had watched him choose silence for fifteen years and call it survival. If you asked her whether he could be trusted with the reforging, she would have answered honestly: she did not know. He had already failed once, and the world had paid the interest on his failure ever since.

But he was moving. He was moving toward her, with the child, and after fifteen years of standing still that was nearly a miracle in itself.

*Come, then,* she thought, sending it down the thin bright thread of the covenant, knowing he could not hear it, saying it anyway because a dragon at the end of her life may be permitted one indulgence. *Come slowly or come fast, come whole or come broken. I have waited ten thousand winters. I will wait a little longer. But not much longer, little keeper. Not much.*

* * *

Winter closed over the mountains like a lid.

Emberlain passed the weeks in the old rhythm: watching, listening, burning less. She rationed her fire now the way a starving man rations bread, feeding it only enough to keep it alive, letting her blood run cool, her scales dull. There was a temptation, some nights, to simply let go—to open her chest and pour the last of it out in one glorious hour, melting the caldera snows, lighting the sky, and ending on a note worthy of her name instead of fading like a lamp left in an empty room.

She did not do it. Hope is a discipline. She had learned that from the keepers, long ago, the ones who tended the stones through plagues and wars without ever being thanked. Hope is not a feeling. It is a thing you do, daily, stupidly, against all evidence, the way you carry water to a garden everyone says will die.

So she carried water.

Through the eyes of a migrating hawk she tracked the fugitives out of Ashvale and onto the old roads. Through the ears of a farm dog she heard them argue and laugh around a cookfire—an old lamplighter woman with a priestess's vocabulary, a wolf gone gray at the muzzle who grumbled like a grandfather, the man, and the child. The child did not speak. But the beasts along the road spoke *to* her, constantly, shyly, the way birds gather near a window in hard weather, and Emberlain understood what she was seeing and it frightened her more than Corvane did.

The key was waking. And keys get used up. That was written in the covenant's oldest verse, the verse no keeper liked to read aloud: *that which opens may also be spent.* A living key could turn a lock—or break in it.

She watched the gray cloaks follow, too. She watched them through the eyes of carrion crows, who liked the gray cloaks' smell the way flies like a wound. Caged magic, leaking. The Inquisitor General had drunk so much confiscated power that he had become a bottle walking, and bottles crack, and what was inside him was not entirely him anymore. Emberlain had seen that sickness before, in the last age, in men who ate what they should have served. It always ended the same way. The question was only how much of the world burned before it ended.

At a ruined covenant-stone in the hills, she watched the lamplighter force the confession out of the man at last, and Emberlain listened to Rafael Pemberton say aloud, for the first time in fifteen years, the words *I broke it, I was afraid, I broke it* — and she felt the old fracture in the covenant shiver, as if it too had been waiting to be named.

Grief moved through her, vast and slow as weather. But beneath the grief, absurdly, stubbornly: relief. A wound that is finally spoken of is a wound that can finally be treated.

*You were a child,* she told him across the miles, though he could not hear. *It was a terrible thing and you were a child and both of those are true. Now finish it. Finish it, and I will forgive you what the world has already forgiven.*

* * *

They crossed the river at the ford in the last week of autumn, and that was where the gray cloaks caught them, and that was where the child spoke.

Emberlain felt it from three hundred miles away and rose to her feet for the first time in a season, every scale ringing like a struck bell. The covenant-tongue, spoken whole, spoken *true*, poured through the veins of the world like spring melt through a frozen field—and the river stood up. She felt the water rise in obedience to a girl smaller than its reeds, felt the gray cloaks scatter, felt the enormous cost of it as the child's bright voice dimmed to half of what it had been.

Half her voice, spent in a moment. Turning a lock.

Emberlain settled back down slowly, and the mountain breathed again, and in the dark behind her eyes she did the arithmetic once more, and the answer had changed.

The key was real. The key was waking. The key was spending herself faster than any keeper could protect her, and the man who walked beside her had one gift left to give and did not yet know he would have to give all of it.

*So it comes to this,* Emberlain thought, watching the snow fall into the caldera, each flake hissing to nothing long before it reached her. *An old dragon with an hour of fire left. A coward with one gift. A mute child with half a voice. A lamplighter, a lame wolf, a doubting warden. Against the cold at the edge of the world.*

It was a poor army. It was, in ten thousand winters, the poorest army ever mustered for the fate of everything.

She found she loved it completely.

Far to the south, she felt Corvane begin to move—felt his stolen power gather like a storm front, felt him set his sights on the caldera at last, done with patience, done with sending others. He would come with fire of his own, the drained and borrowed kind, the kind that eats what it burns. He would come before winter's end, and he would come hungry.

Emberlain looked up through the falling snow at the hole in the clouds above her caldera, the eye of the sky, and spoke to the mountain, to the stones, to the sleeping veins of the old covenant, in the voice that had once made kingdoms kneel:

*Hold together. One season more. Hold together, and I will make you a dawn worth the waiting.*

Deep in her chest, the last coal of her fire flickered—and held.

Then, at the edge of her hearing, faint as a heartbeat, the child's voice began again on the road, learning to speak around what she had lost, and Emberlain closed her eyes and listened, and waited, and hoped.
