## 14. Wren Speaks

The river talks all night.

Wren lies in the crook of Bracken's flank with Rafael's coat over her and listens to it. The water is saying the same thing over and over, the way water always does: *come down, come down, come down.* Rivers are impatient. They were born running and they have never once stopped to think about it.

Bracken is already asleep. His breath comes out slow and warm and smells like old leaves. Rafael is on the other side of the dead fire, sitting up, watching the dark between the trees. He does that a lot now. He thinks Wren doesn't notice. He thinks a lot of things she doesn't notice him thinking.

The rats in Ashvale used to say he had a loud inside-voice even when his outside one was quiet. *That one,* the oldest rat had told her, back before the burning, before the waystation came apart into sparks and screaming, *that one speaks our tongue and pretends he doesn't. That one is full of doors.*

Wren had come to find the doors.

She closes her eyes and lets the river's voice wash over her, and somewhere in the middle of its endless *come down, come down* she falls asleep.

* * *

They wake to gray light and gray cloaks.

It's Bracken who smells them first — a low rumble in his chest that isn't quite a growl yet, just the shape of one. Wren feels it through her ribs before she hears it, and then Rafael is up, hand on her shoulder, pulling her behind him, and Sable is snuffing the cold fire with dirt like burying something alive.

"How many?" Sable whispers.

Bracken's nose works the air. He answers Rafael in the beast-speech, that low flowing language that sounds like wind deciding which way to go, and Wren understands every word of it because she has always understood every word of it, even before she could speak any at all.

*Six,* Bracken says. *Coming from the ridge. Two more circling toward the water. They have iron nets and they smell wrong — like magic kept in boxes until it goes bad.*

Gray cloaks. The men in gray who burned the waystation. Who burned everything.

Rafael looks at the trees, then at the river, then at Wren, and she watches him do the math on his face. There aren't enough trees. There is never enough anything.

"Ford," he says quietly. "If we cross, the current will muddy our scent for the hounds. Sable, take Wren's hand. Bracken—"

*I'm too old to fight six men,* Bracken says flatly. *But I'm not too old to bite one.*

* * *

The ford is wide and brown and moving fast with snowmelt. The stones under it are slick and dark. On the far side, the land climbs into pine shadow, and if they can reach the pines, Bracken says there are old covenant-roads under the roots, roads the wardens' maps forgot on purpose.

They get halfway across.

Wren is holding Sable's hand, and Rafael is ahead of her, arms out for balance, and Bracken is at the rear with his lips peeled off his yellow teeth, and that is when the two circling wardens step out of the reeds on the far bank.

And the other four come out of the trees behind them.

Eight, not six. The hounds had been lying, or the men had made the hounds lie.

"Well," says the tall warden, the one with the iron net coiled at his hip like a dead silver snake. "The charm-seller. The lamplighter. The wolf." His eyes find Wren and stay there, and his smile makes her stomach fold in on itself. "And the little key."

Rafael steps in front of her. It happens fast, without him deciding it — she can see that, see the way his body moves before his face catches up. "There's no key here," he says. "Just children. Just travelers."

"Just travelers," the warden repeats, soft and amused. He draws something from his cloak — a rod of dull metal, and when he holds it up, it *hums*, and the hum goes through Wren's teeth, through her bones, and everything inside her that is quiet suddenly wants to scream. "This is a stilling-rod, charm-seller. It eats speech. Beast-speech, witch-speech, any speech that isn't licensed by the crown." He tilts his head. "Shall we see what the child screams when nothing will hear her?"

Behind Rafael, Sable's hand finds Wren's shoulder. Her grip is steady but her pulse is not. Wren can feel it, quick and frightened, right through her palm.

The river keeps talking underneath them. *Come down, come down, come down.*

The wardens start forward. Bracken lunges, snapping, and a net comes down and he twists away from it, growling, buying seconds, only seconds. Rafael backs toward Wren with his hands open and empty, and Wren sees it in his shoulders, in the set of his neck: he has already decided what he'll do. He'll put himself between her and the net. He'll spend himself the way he spends everything, quietly, pretending it costs nothing.

No, Wren thinks.

No.

* * *

Here is what nobody knows about muteness, the kind Wren was born with:

Her throat was never broken. Her voice was never missing. It was waiting. All the words she would ever say were standing inside her like a crowd pressed against a door, patient, patient, fifteen thousand of them, waiting for the one word worth spending them on.

She looks past Rafael. Past the wardens and their humming rod. At the river.

The river, which has been talking to her all night. *Come down, come down, come down.*

The river, which remembers. Because rivers remember everything; that is their curse and their craft. This river remembers the covenant-stone upstream, cracked and starving. It remembers the waystation's ashes coming down through it last spring, gray film on its brown skin. It remembers being young, when dragons drank from its source and said thank-you in the old tongue, and the water had shivered with pleasure at being spoken to properly.

Wren opens her mouth.

The crowd of words surges against the door.

She reaches for the first word — the oldest one, the root-word, the one the beasts use for *water* and *mercy* and *sister*, because to the old covenant they were all the same word — and she pushes it out of her body and into the morning air.

"*Aelun.*"

The world stops.

Not the wardens. Not the men. The *world*. The wind quits mid-gust. The birds quit mid-song. Even the light seems to lean, like a listener cupping an ear.

Then the river rises.

It does not rise like a wave. Waves belong to the sea. This is older and stranger: the whole ford swells upward at once, brown water lifting into a shape — a shoulder, an arm, a long reaching hand of current — and the nearest warden gets one second to look up at it before it takes him. It takes his net too, the dead silver snake, and swallows both into the deep with a sound like a door closing underwater. The man surfaces twenty feet downstream, coughing, weaponless, clawing at the reeds.

The stilling-rod's hum dies. Its owner drops it as if it burned him, and maybe it did, because the river is everywhere now, and the river does not like what the rod smells of.

*Caged things,* Wren thinks, and the thought is not entirely hers anymore. *The water hates caged things.*

One warden turns and runs. Another raises a crossbow, and Bracken hits him at the knees, and Sable has her lamplighter's hook out and swinging. Rafael grabs Wren's arm — "Wren, stop, you don't know what it's doing to you—" — but he's shouting it in human words, and she barely hears him, because she is hearing everything else. She is hearing the gravel on the riverbed grinding their names. She is hearing the fish flee downstream in a silver ribbon, telling each other *upstream is god now, upstream is god.* She is hearing the roots of the pines on the far bank whisper to each other across forty years of silence, waking up, remembering there was supposed to be a keeper.

So she gives them another word.

"*Sathiel.*"

*Sister. Water. Mercy.* The second word costs more than the first. It comes up her throat like a hook coming out of flesh, and something tears on the way — she feels it go, some bright thread inside her snapping — and the river answers anyway, joyfully, terribly. A wall of white water crashes across the ford where the wardens stood, hurling two of them into the shallows, tearing the third off his feet entirely, and the fourth — the tall one, the smiling one — scrambles backward up the bank, and the river follows him, a single rope of current chasing him up onto dry stone until he flees into the trees and does not stop fleeing.

Then it is over.

The river settles. The birds come back one at a time, cautious, like people returning to a hall after a fight. And Wren opens her mouth to say something to Rafael — *I'm fine, I'm fine, didn't you see, the river knew me, the river remembered me* —

and no sound comes out.

Not the old silence. Not the patient, waiting silence she was born with, the one with the crowd of words behind it.

This silence is empty.

She tries again. Her lips move. Her chest pushes air. Nothing. Half of her voice — half the crowd of waiting words — is simply gone, spent, poured into the river along with those two syllables, and what's left stands in the doorway of her throat and stares at her and cannot follow where she went.

The panic comes up fast. Her hands fly to her mouth. She claws at Rafael's sleeve. *Say something, make sure, please—*

He's already kneeling. Already holding her face in both hands, thumbs at her jaw, looking into her eyes the way he looked at the inspection wardens in his shop — that careful, terrified attention. "Wren. Wren, look at me. Say your name."

Nothing.

"Whisper it. Anything."

She pushes. Something scrapes out of her — thin, torn, half a sound, like wind through a cracked shutter. Enough to be heard. Not enough to be a voice. Not anymore, not all the time, not the way it was.

Sable is beside them, wet to the waist, blood on her knuckles, breathing hard. She puts her fingers to Wren's throat, gentle, professional, and her face does something complicated. "The cords aren't damaged," she murmurs. "It's not in the body. It's—" She stops. Looks at Rafael. "You know what it is."

"I know what it is," Rafael says, very quietly.

Bracken limps over, one foreleg held stiff, muzzle wrinkled where a crossbow bolt grazed it. He lowers his great gray head and breathes Wren's scent in, and what comes out of him is so soft she almost misses it:

*Little key. Keys get used up. That is what keys are for.*

Wren wants to hit him. Wants to scream at him in the old tongue until the trees fall over — but the old tongue is the half that's gone, mostly, or half gone, and the injustice of it burns behind her eyes.

Instead she does what she has done her whole life. She takes Rafael's hand and presses it flat against her own throat and looks at him, and her eyes say everything the crowd of lost words used to say.

Rafael's face is doing something terrible. Grief and awe fighting for the same ground.

"You gave it your voice," he says hoarsely. "To save us. You spent—" He stops. Swallows. Starts again. "Wren, listen to me. We're going to Emberlain. Emberlain will know how to give it back. Dragons made the covenant; dragons can mend what the rite breaks. Do you understand? We're close now. Three days, maybe four. You hold on, and we fix this."

Liar, Wren thinks. Kind liar.

Because she heard the river. She heard what the river knows, in the moment she was the river, and the river is older than hope and it does not lie to the ones who speak to it properly. She heard that the key is waking, and that keys get used up, and that the door at the caldera does not give back what it takes. It only takes, and takes, and takes, until the door is strong enough to stand for another age.

And she heard something else. Something the river saved for last, tucked small under its roar, meant only for her:

*The gray cloaks are not following you, little sister. They are following your sound. Every time you speak, you ring like a bell in the dark. And the man who eats bells is coming.*

Far away, back up the ridge, in the direction of the city, something answers her thought. Not a sound. A pressure. A cold, patient hunger turning over in its sleep, opening one eye.

Wren presses closer to Rafael's side as they climb the far bank into the pines. Above them, on the heights, the wind moves through the high passes carrying the faintest taste of ash and iron.

Three days to the caldera.

Something is coming faster than three days.
