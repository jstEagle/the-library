## 15. The Traitor's Price

Ilsa Vane had been a warden for eleven years, and in eleven years she had learned one rule above all others: the General's ledger room was never entered uninvited.

She stood outside its door now, at the end of a corridor that smelled of tallow and old iron, holding a folder of reports she had spent three sleepless nights editing. The waystation figures. The mass grave counts. Every number smoothed, every anomaly buried under plausible clerical error. Corvane read ledgers the way other men read faces. He would notice a lie the way a jeweler notices paste.

But he would notice the truth faster.

She knocked.

"Enter." His voice came through the door thin and dry, like paper turning.

The ledger room was smaller than she'd imagined, though she'd imagined it often. No grand archive, no rows of scribes. Just a single desk beneath a high slit window, and shelves of bound volumes rising into shadow, and — in the corner, where she'd never seen it before because she'd never been invited — a cage.

It was iron, no larger than a birdcage, hung from a hook in the ceiling beam. Inside it something burned without fuel: a wisp, pale gold, pressing itself against the bars in slow, exhausted pulses. Like a moth that had forgotten how to stop. Like a heart.

"Close the door, Warden."

She did. The latch clicked, and the sound seemed very loud.

Corvane sat behind the desk with his hands folded on an open ledger. He was not a large man. People expected the Inquisitor General to be large; they expected menace to have shoulders. Instead there was only this narrow, precise figure in gray, his face smooth in a way that made her think of river stones, of things worn featureless by long water. His eyes were the color of the cage's iron.

"You've brought me the waystation audit," he said.

"Yes, General."

"Read me the casualty figure."

Her mouth went dry. "Forty-one souls recorded lost. Thirty-eight recovered and interred at the site. Three unaccounted for, presumed—"

"Presumed fled." He turned a page without looking down. "And the grave count?"

"Thirty-eight," she said. "As reported."

Something in the room changed. She couldn't name it. The candle flame didn't gutter. The wisp didn't flare. But the air acquired a pressure, the way it does before summer lightning, and Ilsa understood that she had just said the wrong thing in front of the wrong man, and that the door was twelve feet away and might as well be twelve miles.

"Thirty-eight," Corvane repeated softly. "Yet your own field report — filed nine days ago, before you began your revisions — states thirty-nine."

The folder trembled in her hands. She set it on the desk to hide the trembling, which was a mistake, because now he could see it.

"An error in the original count," she said. "The light was failing. One body was counted twice."

"One body was counted twice." He considered this the way a man considers a wine. "Warden Vane. Do you know how long I have held this office?"

"Twenty-two years, General."

"Longer." He smiled, and the smile reached nothing above his cheekbones. "The records say twenty-two. Records are written by men who assume their superiors will die. I have outlived four Inquisitors General, two Lord Chancellors, and every woman who trained me. Do you know what that requires?"

"Vigilance," she said.

"Patience," he said. "Vigilance is for hunting. Patience is for *waiting*. And do you know what else it requires?"

He rose. He was not tall. He crossed the small room in five steps and stood before the hanging cage, and up close she saw that the wisp inside had gone still — pressed flat against the far bars, as far from him as its little prison allowed.

"Nourishment," Corvane said.

* * *

Later — much later, when she had time to arrange the memory like evidence in a case file — Ilsa would try to establish the exact order of what happened next. Whether he spoke first or reached first. Whether she drew her baton or simply froze. The order mattered to her then, because order implied causation, and causation implied that some different choice might have ended differently.

In truth it happened all at once, and no choice was available at all.

He opened the cage.

The wisp shot out — not toward freedom but toward him, dragged, the way water is dragged down a drain — and Corvane caught it in both hands the way a man cups a candleflame against wind. And then he *drank*. There was no other word. The gold light sank into his palms, up his wrists, into the gray sleeves, and his face — his smooth, worn-stone face — flushed briefly with color, with warmth, with something almost like youth, and his eyes brightened from iron to silver, and for one naked second Ilsa saw what he actually was:

Hungry. Old. A hollow thing wearing a man's office the way a hermit crab wears a stolen shell.

Then he closed his fists and the light went out entirely, and the wisp was gone, and Corvane exhaled like a man finishing a meal, and looked at her over his shoulder.

"Now," he said pleasantly. "About your arithmetic."

* * *

She ran for the door. Of course she ran for the door. Eleven years of wardens' drills, and every drill assumed the enemy was outside the room.

His hand closed on her forearm before she'd taken three steps, and his grip was cold — not dead-cold, not corpse-cold, but the cold of deep cellars, of places the sun has never bothered with — and the strength in it was impossible, absurd, the strength of a man half again his visible weight.

"Warden Vane." His voice stayed gentle. That was the worst part, she thought distantly. Gentleness. "You counted thirty-nine bodies. You filed a report saying thirty-nine. Then you rewrote it. You altered a sworn document of the Inquisition. Do you know what we call that?"

"Treason," she whispered.

"We call it *concealment*," he said. "Treason is so dramatic. Concealment is quieter. More common. More instructive." He turned her, slowly, until she faced him, and his silver-bright eyes examined her face with the detached interest of a physician. "You found something at the waystation. Something that made you lie to protect — whom? The fugitives? The child? Or yourself?"

"I don't know what you mean."

"No," he agreed. "Not yet. But you will. Understanding always arrives. It's the one thing that can't be interrogated out of a person." He sighed, and the sigh carried the weariness of decades, of a patience older than her whole life. "I am going to show you something, Warden, because you have potential, and potential should not be wasted on confusion. Hold still."

"I serve the Crown," she said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "I serve the law. Magic is a crime, and you're—"

"I am the law's *custodian*," Corvane said. "Someone must hold the dangerous things. Someone must carry them so that decent people needn't touch them. That is all magic is, in the end — a weight. And weights must be carried by the strong." His grip tightened. "Now hold still. This will feel like winter."

* * *

It felt like winter.

It felt like the worst fever of her childhood running backward, heat pouring out of her instead of in, her blood slowing in her veins like a river icing over. She saw frost on the inside of her own vision. She heard her heartbeat drop — felt each beat arrive later than the last, longer gaps between them, the rhythm of a clock winding down.

And through it all Corvane watched her, drinking, and his face grew smoother, and somewhere beneath the pain she understood with perfect clarity that this was not punishment. Punishment was a lesson. This was *accounting*. She had concealed assets from the Inquisition, and the Inquisition was collecting interest.

When he released her, she collapsed against the shelf of ledgers and slid to the floor among the record of fifteen years of confiscated lives. Her hands were white. Her breath came in shallow pulls. She felt — emptied. Not wounded. *Emptied*, like a house after the movers have come, like a purse turned out on a table. Years. She knew, without knowing how she knew, that he had taken years. Some part of her future self was already spent, burned in that gray throat, and would never arrive.

"Do you see now?" Corvane crouched beside her, and his voice was almost kind. "This is why the covenant must stay broken. This is why magic must remain a hanging crime. Because power like mine exists whether we permit it or not — and better it sits in disciplined hands than wild ones. Better a warden's few years feed the wall than a witch's fire burn the city." He straightened. "You lied to me, Warden. I have taken the price of the lie. We are square. Return to your duties."

He walked back to his desk. Back to his ledgers. As if she were a clerk dismissed after a reprimand.

Ilsa lay among the confiscated records and stared at the ceiling beam where the empty cage still swung, gently, on its hook.

*We are square.*

No, she thought. No, we are not.

* * *

She did not run. Running was what the guilty did, and everything about her conduct from this moment forward had to be the conduct of an innocent, tired, chastened warden. She walked out of the ledger room with steady legs — mostly steady — saluted the duty clerk, descended the stairs of the Inquisitorial Hall into the gray afternoon, and reported for evening patrol as if her blood had not been partially decanted into another man's veins.

She walked her route. She checked the charm-quarter locks. She nodded to shopkeepers who nodded back without warmth, because everyone nodded to wardens without warmth; fear kept its distance. And all the while, beneath the routine, her mind worked with a clarity she had never known. Pain, it turned out, was a whetstone.

*Thirty-nine bodies.* She had counted them herself, kneeling in the ash of the waystation, lifting each blanket corner. Thirty-nine. And the official ledger said forty-one lost, thirty-eight recovered — numbers that reconciled only if three fugitives had escaped and one corpse had vanished. But the graves held thirty-nine. Which meant the ledger's arithmetic was not sloppy. It was *designed*. It was built to hide exactly one thing: that the waystation's people had not died fighting, had not died resisting, had not even died fleeing.

They had died surrendered. Bound. On their knees. She had seen the cord-marks on the bones.

And the order authorizing the action bore no signature she could trace — except that requisitions for the operation had flowed through the office of the Inquisitor General, and the gray cloaks who'd done the burning answered to no one else.

Corvane was collecting covenant-keys. Sable had said it — the lamplighter woman, the ex-priestess, in that low furious voice across Rafael Pemberton's counter — and Ilsa had logged it as sedition, as rumor, as the kind of tale traitors told each other to feel righteous. Now she had put her own hands on the proof. Now she had paid for the proof in years of her own life.

*Better a warden's few years feed the wall.*

Whose wall, General? What wall?

* * *

Three nights later, she went back.

Not to the ledger room — never again to the ledger room — but to the records annexe, where the Inquisition's older documents slept in dust and nobody watched, because nobody believed anything worth stealing lived in paperwork. Corvane himself had taught her that, in her first year: *Men guard treasure and ignore receipts. Be the receipt-keeper, Vane, and you'll know more than any thief.*

She was the receipt-keeper now.

By lamplight shielded behind her cloak, she copied what she needed onto plain paper in her own careful hand: the requisition orders for the waystation action, countersigned in Corvane's private cipher. The transfer manifests showing forty-one "confiscated assets" moved from the waystation to the Hall — forty-one, when the village had held thirty-nine dead, meaning two living prisoners had been brought here, meaning the cage in the ledger room was not the first and the wisp was not the last. And the oldest item, pulled from a sealed drawer she'd needed three forged countersignatures to open: an inventory from twenty-two years ago, the year Corvane took office, listing assets received from his predecessor — including, in faded ink, a notation she read four times to be sure.

*One (1) vessel, human, male, age 34. Vitality intact. Transferred per succession rites.*

Age 34. Twenty-two years ago.

Ilsa sat back in the dark and let the arithmetic finish itself. Corvane had been old when she was a child learning her sword-drills. He had been old, and he had been fed, and he had been fed ever since — on wisps, on witches, on whatever the confiscations brought him, on the *years* of a warden who told one lie too many. The Inquisitor General was not a man who hunted magic.

He was a man made of it. A leech with a seal on his coat. And every hanging, every burning, every gray cloak kicking in a charm-seller's door had been, at the bottom of it, a harvest.

She copied the last line, blotted the page, folded the papers into the lining of her coat, and restored every document to its place with a forger's precision. Then she doused her lamp and sat a moment longer in the dark, listening to the building breathe.

Somewhere above her, behind a locked door, the Inquisitor General slept — if he slept — on a hoard of stolen years.

Somewhere beyond the walls, a mute child and a charm-seller and a lamplighter were running toward something, pursued by men in gray cloaks who took their orders from that sleeping hunger. She had spent eleven years hunting people like them. She had believed, right up until the frost filled her vision, that the wall she served was the same wall that kept the city safe.

The wall was the wolf. The wall had always been the wolf.

Ilsa Vane buttoned her coat over the stolen pages, picked up her patrol lantern, and walked out of the annexe a traitor for the second time in her life — and for the first time, glad of it.

There was a road north out of Ashvale, through the hills, along the old covenant-ways. Wardens didn't ride it alone. Wardens didn't ride it at all.

She was done being what she had been.

She saddled her horse in the dark, and rode.
