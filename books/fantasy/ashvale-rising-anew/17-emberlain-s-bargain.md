## 17. Emberlain's Bargain

*Emberlain*

I have counted ten thousand winters, and I have never counted one so slowly.

The keeper-child sleeps in the hollow of my foreleg, curled like a coal that has not yet decided to be fire. Her breath is small. Her dreams are small. And yet the covenant gathers around her like moths around a lantern, drawn to the thing she carries without understanding—the tongue, the key, the old promise wearing the shape of a girl who cannot speak above a whisper since the river.

The others sleep too, or pretend to. The lamplighter keeps her vigil at the door of the caldera, hands folded, lips moving in prayers she half-remembers. The wolf lies where he fell and does not complain of his hurts, because wolves are proud in ways that shorten their lives. And the charm-seller—

The charm-seller sits apart, as he always does, at the edge of my firelight, where the warmth reaches him but the light does not quite claim his face. Fifteen years I have felt him through the thinning covenant. A flicker of the old tongue, banked like a stove in an empty house. He thought no one knew. He thought his silence was a secret.

Nothing is a secret from a dragon. Not for long.

He is afraid. I can taste it on the air, bitter as frost. But it is not the fear I expected—not fear of me, not of the gray cloaks who will come, not even of dying. It is a smaller fear, and therefore a larger one. The fear of a man who has just understood the price of a thing he wants.

I let him sit with it. I have ten thousand winters of patience, and only one night left to spend it.

* * *

*Rafael*

The dragon's fire was going out.

Rafael had known it in his bones from the moment they passed the covenant-door, but knowing and seeing were different things. Emberlain was vast—her body alone filled the caldera floor like a hill that had decided to lie down—and she was *dimming*. The scales along her spine, which should have burned gold, glowed the dull orange of a banked stove. When she breathed, the flame that curled from her nostrils was thin, wavering, the flame of a candle in a drafty room rather than a fire that had held two realms apart for a thousand years.

Beyond the caldera's rim, through the gap in the clouds, Rafael could see the stars. Too many stars. Stars that should have been on the *other side* of the barrier, shining through the thin places like light through a worn cloak.

Wren slept against Emberlain's foreleg, one small hand resting on a scale the size of a dinner plate. Even asleep, the girl's fingers curled around it like she was holding on to something that might be taken from her.

"She is dreaming of the river," Emberlain said.

Her voice arrived without sound, or rather, it arrived *as* sound in a way that had nothing to do with ears. It settled into Rafael's chest and became words there. He had not heard it in fifteen years, and still it undid something in him that he had spent fifteen years keeping tied.

"She dreams in the old tongue," the dragon went on. "She does not know she is doing it. The covenant speaks through her while she sleeps. It is... pleasant. I had forgotten what it was like to be spoken to."

Rafael's throat was tight. "How long does she have to—"

"Not yet." The great head turned. One eye, large as a cartwheel, fixed on him. It was the color of deep amber, and in it he could see his own reflection, small and hunched and afraid. "First, charm-seller, you and I are going to have the conversation you have been avoiding since you walked through my door."

* * *

*Emberlain*

He flinches. Good. Flinching is honest.

"You know what the covenant is," I say to him, and his mouth opens, and I do not wait for the answer. "You think you know. A promise between the old kingdoms and the fire. A treaty written in stone. Your teacher taught you the words, yes? The words of keeping. But words are only the *shape* of a thing, the way a cup is the shape of water. Do you know what the covenant *is*, Rafael Pemberton?"

He is silent. He is very good at silence. It has been his trade and his armor for fifteen years.

"It is a voice," I tell him. "The barrier between the realms is not stone. It is not a wall. It is a *spoken thing*—a word that has been said continuously for a thousand years, and must continue to be said, or it stops. The covenant-stones were throats. The keepers were tongues. And the fire—" I let a thread of flame curl from my throat, thin and sad. "The fire is the breath behind it all. Mine is nearly spent. The word is faltering. Listen."

I let him listen. I open the covenant a crack, the way one opens a door onto a storm, and I feel him hear it—the great word stuttering, syllables dropping out of it like teeth from an old jaw. The silence where the word has failed is not empty. It is *hungry*. Things on the other side of the barrier have been waiting a very long time in that hunger, and they have noticed the gaps.

He goes gray. "The stones," he says. "The one I—"

"The one you cracked. Yes." I do not soften it. Softness would be a lie, and I have no time left for lies. "A keeper does not merely *speak* to beasts, child of Pemberton. A keeper's gift is a thread of the covenant itself, woven into mortal flesh. When you refused your rite of keeping—when you ran from the stone with your teacher's blood still on your hands from the accident you could not forgive yourself for—the thread went slack. The stone cracked. And every year since, the word has grown thinner, and I have burned a little more of myself to hold the syllables together."

"I didn't know," he says, and it is true, and it changes nothing. "I was twelve. I was *afraid*—"

"You were twelve, and you were afraid, and the world has been paying the interest on your fear for fifteen years." I let that sit in him like a stone in a shoe. Then, because I am old and tired and cruel only in the way that surgeons are cruel: "But hear the rest, because the rest is why I called you here."

* * *

*Rafael*

"The covenant can be reforged," Emberlain said.

The words went through Rafael like a spear of sunlight through dirty glass. For one vertiginous moment he let himself feel it—*reforged*—the barrier whole, the word unbroken, the realms sealed, the hungry things on the other side shut out forever. And Wren safe. Wren alive, Wren whole, Wren growing up in a world that didn't fall apart because a man once failed to be brave.

"The rite of keeping," the dragon said, "can be performed anew. A keeper gives their gift to the stone—the whole of it, every thread—and the stone speaks again, and the word is made whole, and the fire that remains to me is enough to kindle it. One fire. One word. One keeper."

"Then we do it," Rafael said. "We do it now. Tonight. Before Corvane—"

"The keeper must be willing," Emberlain said. "Willing *entirely*. Half a gift makes a cracked word; that is the lesson of your life, charm-seller. And there is more. Hear it all before you speak again."

Rafael heard it all.

He heard it the way a man hears the ice crack under his feet—each word a separate, distinct sound, each one impossible to unhear.

*The gift does not return. The rite is not a loan. A keeper who gives their gift gives it *forever*. Deaf to beast-speech for the rest of their mortal life. The birds will go silent. The rats will go silent. The wolf at your fire, the dragon before you—all of it, gone, as if it had never been. You will live in the silence you chose at twelve, and this time there will be no door out of it.*

And then the last of it, the part that broke something in him he suspected would never mend:

*And the child cannot bear it alone. She is seven years old. Her gift is new and raw and vast, a river through a channel cut for a stream. To pour the whole of the covenant through her alone would burn her out like a moth in a candle. She needs an anchor. A second keeper, grown, whose gift can take the weight and divide it. Two keepers, or none.*

"Two keepers," Rafael said. His voice came out strange, flat, like someone else's. "You're saying it has to be me."

"I am saying," Emberlain said, and her great dim head lowered until her breath stirred his hair, warm and smelling of old smoke, "that you are the only grown keeper left in the world. I am saying that the child will die if she bears the rite alone, and the word will fail if no one bears it at all. I am saying that you have spent fifteen years hiding the only thing that can save everything, and now the hiding is over, and the bill has come due."

* * *

*Emberlain*

Now he understands. Now I watch him understand, and it is the cruelest thing I have done in ten thousand winters, and I have eaten kings.

He looks at the child. That is the whole of it, really. That is the entire man, laid open in a single gesture. He does not look at me, or at the door, or at his own hands. He looks at the sleeping girl with her hand curled on my scale, and I watch him do the arithmetic that has no good answer.

If he gives his gift, he loses the only language he has ever been fluent in. The rats and the gulls and the grumpy wolf who bled for her—all silent. He will stand in a world suddenly, permanently mute, the way a man struck deaf stands in a world of moving lips. He built his whole gray life on silence, yes—but it was silence with a *window*. He could always hear. He chose not to speak. Now the choice will be taken from him, and choice, for men like this, is the whole of the soul.

If he does not give it, the girl burns. Or the word fails. Or both.

I could tell him there is another way. There is not. I could tell him the rite might fail and spare him the choosing. It will not fail. I could tell him I am sorry.

Dragons are not sorry. Dragons are *old*, and being old means knowing that every salvation is also a loss, and saying it plainly, and letting the small mortal creature decide what he is made of.

"Ask your question," I say, because his lips have been moving.

His voice is very quiet. "If I do this. If I give it—" He stops. Starts again. "Will I remember? What it sounded like? The—the gulls, when they bargained, the way Bracken complained about his hips. Will I remember what it was like to hear them?"

"You will remember that you heard," I say. "You will remember it the way you remember a dream on waking. The shape of it. The warmth of it. Never the words."

He closes his eyes.

And I watch him, this small gray man in a caldera at the end of the world, and I think: *here it is. Here is the thing I have waited ten thousand winters to see—not the rite, not the fire, but this. A mortal choosing, with open eyes, to become less so that something smaller than him might become more.*

The lamplighter would call it grace. The wolf would call it pack. The child, if she were awake, would call it nothing at all—she would simply take his hand, because that is what she does, because she chose him before any of us knew why.

"Corvane is coming," I say gently, because the covenant trembles on the far hills, gray cloaks and drained magic, a hunger in the shape of a man. "You have until dawn to decide. But charm-seller—decide with your heart, not your guilt. Guilt makes poor keepers. It makes keepers who give the gift and resent the giving, and a resentful word is a cracked word. Whatever you choose—" I lower my head until my eye fills his whole sky, amber and ancient and, I am surprised to find, a little afraid myself. "Choose it *freely*. That is the only door the covenant opens."

* * *

*Rafael*

He walked to the caldera's edge and stood looking out at the thin stars, and for a long time he let himself do the one thing he had forbidden himself for fifteen years.

He listened.

The mountain spoke. Not in words, exactly—stone was always the slowest of speakers, its thoughts taking a season to form a sentence—but in pressures, in the deep patient groan of rock settling, in the far-off mutter of the magma veins that fed Emberlain's fire. *Cold coming,* the mountain said, in the way mountains say things. *Cold coming, and small feet on my skin, and iron. Iron and hunger.*

Beyond the mountain, faint as a memory: the winter hawks riding the high winds, arguing about a dead hare. A fox somewhere on the lower slopes, awake and wary, catching the scent of strangers. And below it all, so faint he had to lean into it like a man leaning into wind, the covenant itself—the great word, stuttering, syllables falling away, and underneath the stutter, *listening back*. The hungry things. Patient. Counting the gaps.

Fifteen years he had treated this as a disease. A shameful secret, a liability, a thing to be hidden behind trinkets and gray humility. And now, standing at the edge of losing it forever, he understood that it had never been the disease. The silence had been the disease. The gift had been the *health*, and he had amputated it himself at twelve years old, and the world had gangrened ever since.

"Rafa."

He turned. Wren stood behind him, wrapped in Sable's spare cloak, her eyes huge in the firelight. She didn't sign. She just looked at him, the way she did—like she was reading something written on the inside of his face.

He knelt. "Hey, sprout."

She touched his chest, over his heart, the way she did when the beasts' voices were too loud for her and she wanted to borrow his stillness. Then her small fingers moved, slow and careful, the signs Sable had taught her, mixed with gestures of her own invention that he understood anyway, because fathers understand.

*You're sad.*

"I'm thinking."

*About the fire words.*

He almost laughed. She had heard everything, of course. She heard everything; the covenant poured through her whether she willed it or not. "Yeah. About the fire words."

Her hand flattened against his chest again. And then, in the old tongue—not the signed one, the *spoken* one, the one that made the rats go still and the river rise—she said a single word. Her half-voice, cracked and small since the ford, made it sound like a bird with a broken wing.

Rafael didn't know the word. He had never heard it before. But the covenant carried its meaning to him the way it carried everything, and he felt it land in his chest like a coal:

*Father.*

Not the word itself. Something older, something the word *father* was a later, smaller translation of. *The one who keeps. The one who stays.*

Wren looked up at him, waiting, the way children wait—not for an answer, but for the world to confirm what they already know.

And Rafael Pemberton, charm-seller, coward, keeper who ran, felt the last of his hesitation die not in a blaze of heroism but quietly, the way a candle goes out when the flame finally finds the wick it was always meant for.

"Go wake Sable," he said, and his voice didn't shake, which surprised him. "Tell her it's time. Tell her I said yes."

Wren's face broke open like dawn. She flung her arms around his neck, squeezed once, and ran toward the firelight, and the covenant rippled around her like water around a bright stone.

Rafael stood alone at the caldera's edge a moment longer. Below, in the dark passes, he could hear it now, clear as a bell: the gray cloaks coming. Iron and hunger, and behind them a colder thing that wore a man's shape, climbing toward the only door left in the world.

He had said yes.

Now all he had to do was survive long enough to keep his word.
