## 22. A New Tongue

The first thing Wren teaches them is not a word.

It is how to listen.

She learned this from Rafael, though he doesn't know it. She watched him for years before the night the rats went still at her voice — watched him tilt his head at sparrows, at cart horses, at the pigeons strutting through Ashvale's gray streets like little merchants with somewhere important to be. He never spoke back. He only listened, with his whole face, the way other people listen to music they're afraid to admit they love.

So on the first morning of the first class, in the back room of Pemberton's Curiosities & Charms, with six children sitting on crates and one sitting on Bracken's old blanket because she refuses chairs, Wren holds up one finger.

*Listen.*

The room goes quiet. The stove ticks. Somewhere above, gulls argue over a fish head. A cat on the fence outside is singing the long complaint of cats who have been fed late.

The children hear noise. That's all. Wren can see it in their faces — the small disappointment, the way the smallest boy, Tobin, starts to fidget with his bootlace.

Then she opens her mouth and gives them the word for *listen*, the old word, the deep one, the one that predates kingdoms, and the room changes.

Not the sounds themselves. The children. Their faces open like shutters. The gull-argument stops being noise and becomes two sisters fighting about who saw the fish first. The cat's complaint becomes a song with verses. The stove's tick becomes the iron remembering the fire that shaped it.

Tobin bursts into tears.

Wren kneels beside him and puts her hand flat on the floorboards, and through the wood she says, in covenant-speech, *You are not broken. You were only waiting.*

He cries harder, which is correct. She remembers crying like that, the night Rafael opened his door and the rain came in behind her and the rats went still, still, still — and a man knelt down in the wet and looked at her like she was not a monster, like she was a door he'd been afraid to knock on for fifteen years.

She was six then. She is eleven now. Half her voice lives in the old tongue and half in the new, and some days the halves don't agree, and some mornings she wakes up and the world is so loud with listening that she has to pull the blanket over her head until it sorts itself into sense.

But today the world is kind. Today there are six children on crates, and one of them — the girl on Bracken's blanket, whose name is Sorrel and whose gift is for stones, poor thing, stones that speak slower than glaciers — raises her hand and asks, in careful, halting covenant-speech:

*Teacher. What was before?*

Wren looks out the window. Ashvale is gray no longer, or at least less gray; the Council repealed the magic laws eight months ago, and the city has been arguing about it ever since, loudly, in taverns and pamphlets and one memorable fistfight outside the Wardens' Hall. But the light comes in gold this morning, and the harbor smells of salt and tar and fish, and somewhere below the cobbles the old covenant-roads hum like plucked strings.

*Before,* she signs-and-speaks, both hands and both voices, *there was a promise between people and beasts and fire. We broke it. We are learning to keep it again.*

Sorrel considers this with the gravity of someone whose best friends are rocks.

*And if we break it again?*

Wren thinks of Rafael pouring himself into the stone. Of the silence where his gift used to be. Of him smiling anyway.

*Then we learn again,* she says. *That's what keeping means. It isn't doing it once. It's doing it always.*

* * *

Emberlain lights the beacons on the eve of winter.

They all go up to watch — Rafael, Sable, Ilsa, the six students crammed into coats too big for them, and Wren riding on Rafael's shoulders because her legs are short and the caldera road is long and because she has decided, with the absolute authority of an eleven-year-old, that this is where she rides now.

Rafael grumbles about it. He grumbles beautifully. He has gotten good at grumbling since the rite took his hearing — as if all the words he used to spend on beasts had nowhere to go except into complaints about weather and steep roads and children who steal dried apricots from his pockets. (Wren steals the apricots. He knows. He keeps buying more.)

The mountain path winds up through pines that talk to each other in slow green whispers. Wren translates without being asked, because translating is what she does now, what she *is*: *The pine on your left says the pine on your right owes it three squirrels' worth of cones.* Rafael laughs, and the sound startles a jay, and the jay scolds them, and Wren translates that too, and Rafael laughs again, and this is the whole economy of their family now: she hears, he laughs, everyone is rich.

Then they crest the shoulder of the mountain, and everyone stops talking, even the pines.

The beacon-towers stand along the spine of the world — seven of them visible from here, old ones, covenant-built, dark for longer than any kingdom has existed. They were built to carry fire between realms, Sable told them once, when the veil was thin enough that a lit tower could be seen from either side. When the covenant cracked, the towers went cold, and the realms drifted apart like boats slipping their moorings.

Now the nearest tower stands black against the dusk, and Wren feels Emberlain before she sees her — a warmth rising through the rock, vast and slow, like summer deciding whether to come back.

*Little key,* the dragon says. Her voice arrives through stone and bone and the soles of Wren's boots. *Are your children watching?*

*They're watching.*

*Good. Let them see what keeping looks like when it is very large.*

Emberlain breathes.

It is not like the stories. The stories say dragons breathe destruction. What Emberlain breathes is *continuation* — a ribbon of gold that climbs the tower's throat and catches at the top and blooms, and the bloom is answered, miles away, by the second tower catching, and then the third, and the fourth, a line of dawn lit backward across the evening sky, realm answering realm answering realm, until the horizon itself seems to hold its breath.

The children scream with joy. Sable weeps openly, the way she does now, having spent forty years not weeping and making up for lost time. Ilsa stands apart in her warden-gray — she kept the coat, changed what it means — and salutes the towers with something between a soldier and a pilgrim.

Rafael watches with his head tilted, the way he watched sparrows for fifteen years. He cannot hear the towers. Wren knows they have a sound — a low chord, felt in the chest, the same note every beacon sings because fire speaks one language everywhere. She takes his hand and presses it against her own sternum so the chord can travel through her into him.

"Is that it?" he asks. "Is that what it sounds like?"

"That's it."

"Hm," he says. "Louder than I expected." And then, quieter, almost to himself: "Worth it, though."

Wren doesn't answer. Some things you don't translate. You just let them sit in the chest, warm as banked coals.

* * *

Winter passes. The school grows.

It shouldn't work, logically. A charm shop is a strange place for a school. But Rafael's shop sits at the crossing of four streets in the old charm quarter, and the charm quarter turns out to be where the gifted children are, because magic hides in poor neighborhoods the way mice hide in walls — quietly, hungrily, surviving.

By spring there are fourteen students. By summer, twenty-two. Sable teaches the history of the covenant, which is mostly the history of its breaking, which she teaches honestly, including the parts about lamplighters who stayed silent too long. Ilsa teaches defense — not attack, never attack, just the warding-signs and the running routes and how to be brave without being stupid. Rafael teaches nothing officially, but he is always in the corner mending something, and the children orbit him like planets, and he pretends not to notice, and everyone lets him pretend.

Wren teaches the tongue.

Some days it is hard. Some days a child's gift frightens them, the way hers frightened her, the way Rafael's frightened him, and she has to sit with a shaking boy in the back room and tell him about the river at the ford, about the day she stopped being mute — how the power didn't make her strong, it made her *responsible*, and responsibility is heavier but it holds you up better.

Some days Corvane's ghost walks through her lessons. Not literally. Literally he is ash under a cairn outside the city, and the crows who nest there tell her he died listening — that in his last minutes the covenant flooded back into the world and into him, and every living thing around his dying body was suddenly speaking, and he heard it all, and there was nothing left in him to answer with. The crows don't pity him exactly. But they remember him. Even villains get remembered by somebody.

On those days, Wren teaches harder.

Because that is the lesson underneath all the lessons: fear is the crack. Fear is what split the stone when a boy named Rafael Pemberton stood before his rite of keeping and said *no, I'm sorry, I can't*. Fifteen years of hiding grew from one moment of terror, and one moment of terror nearly unmade the boundary between worlds. So Wren teaches the children not to be fearless — she isn't — but to be afraid *and keep anyway*. To do the rite scared. To speak the word shaking.

Hope earned, Sable calls it. Never given. Given hope is a gift someone else carries for you. Earned hope has calluses.

* * *

On a bright morning in early autumn, a year and a season after the rain-lashed night, Wren finds Rafael in the shop doorway with a sparrow on the sill in front of him.

The sparrow is chattering. Sparrows always chatter — the street-level gossip of the whole city, delivered at speed, with opinions. This one is complaining about a hawk near the granary and asking, in the way birds ask, whether the tall quiet man will do something about it.

Rafael cannot understand a word. He hasn't been able to for over a year. The rite took it all — permanently, irrevocably, the price written plain before he paid it. He is deaf to the living world in the way he spent fifteen years pretending to be, except now it isn't pretending, and it isn't hiding, and somehow it isn't grief either. He gave the language away the way you give away a coat to someone colder than you.

Wren stands in the doorway and listens to the sparrow, and waits.

She knows what he'll do. He does it every time. His head tilts — that old tilt, the listening tilt, pure habit now, muscle memory of a vanished fluency. His eyes go soft and attentive. He watches the sparrow the way he once watched everything: like it might be saying something worth hearing.

And then Rafael Pemberton smiles.

Not the polite shopkeeper smile from the old days, the armor-smile. This one starts crooked and ends real. Because he can't hear the sparrow, and the sparrow is telling him about a hawk, and none of that matters — what matters is that he is standing in the doorway of his own shop, in a city that chose dawn over fear, with a daughter who translates the world for him and twenty-two children inside learning the oldest language there is, and a dragon's fire burning gold on seven towers, and a bird on his windowsill who trusts him enough to complain to him.

He smiles anyway.

"You going to tell me what it said?" he asks, without looking away from the sparrow.

"It wants you to know there's a hawk by the granary."

"There's always a hawk by the granary."

"I think," Wren says, climbing onto the sill beside the bird, settling her shoulder against his arm, "it was really saying thank you."

"For what?"

"For listening anyway."

The sparrow hops closer. Below the cobbles, the old roads hum. On the towers, the fires burn. In the back room, a child with a gift for stones asks her first question in a language older than kingdoms, and another child answers, and the covenant — remade, reforged, carried now in small stubborn hands — continues.

Wren leans her head against Rafael's shoulder and begins, gently, to teach him the first word.

He'll never speak it. That's all right.

There are others coming who will.
