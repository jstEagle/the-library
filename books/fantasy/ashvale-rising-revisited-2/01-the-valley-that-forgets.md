## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The trouble with Ashvale, Sable Brightwater had decided by her seventeenth autumn, was that it fit too well.

A valley should hold you the way cupped hands hold water — gently, temporarily, ready to let you spill over the rim and go somewhere. But Ashvale held you the way a jar holds a firefly: snug, dark at the edges, and built with the unspoken understanding that nothing inside it was ever meant to get out.

She stood in the garden behind Mistress Hobb's cottage, pulling weeds that didn't need pulling, and watched the evening light come down the mountainsides like honey poured slow. The peaks ringed them all around — the Gray Sisters to the north, the Sawtooths east and west, and south, where the river left through a gorge so narrow the water went through screaming. From anywhere in the valley floor you could see all of it, every ridge and crag, and Sable had spent seventeen years memorizing those ridges the way other girls memorized the faces of far-off princes in traveling shows.

Not that traveling shows came to Ashvale. Nothing came to Ashvale. That was rather the point.

"Sable!" Mistress Hobb's voice carried from the cottage, cracked as old paint but carrying the way it always did, as though the words themselves had been steeped in something bitter. "If you're going to loiter, loiter productively. The comfrey won't cut itself."

"It's not loitering if I'm holding shears," Sable called back, and heard the old woman snort loud enough to startle the hens.

The cottage sat at the valley's western edge like something the forest had coughed up and then decided to keep. It leaned. It had always leaned; Mistress Hobb claimed the lean kept the rain honest. Bundles of dried herbs hung from every rafter inside until the ceiling seemed to grow them, and jars crowded every shelf and most of the floor — nettle tincture, feverfew oil, a salve of goose fat and yarrow that smelled like a wet dog's apology. The whole place smelled of drying things, of things being preserved, held still, kept from changing. Sable had grown up breathing that smell and had only recently understood it for what it was: the scent of a life refusing to move.

She went inside, because there was no use pretending otherwise, and began bundling the morning's cuttings. Her hands knew the work without her. That was the problem with everything here — her hands knew it all without her. Tie the bundles with red thread. Hang them rafter-high where the mice can't argue. Label the jars in a hand steadier than your temper. She could do it blind, drunk, or dead, and some mornings she suspected she'd done all three.

"Where are you off to tonight?" Mistress Hobb asked from her chair by the hearth. She didn't look up from her mending. She never looked up when she asked questions like that, which was how Sable knew the questions weren't really questions. They were tests with answers already written.

"The ferry landing," Sable said. "Tam Vess is walking me home along the near shore."

"Mm." The needle paused. "Home along the near shore. And which home would that be? Mine, I suppose, since you sleep under my roof."

"You know what I mean."

"I know exactly what you mean." The needle resumed its patient work. "I mean to know when you'll be back."

"Before the moon clears the Sawtooths." It was an old rule, one of the valley's rules, worn smooth as a river stone from a century of repetition. *Be home before the moon clears the mountain.* Nobody remembered why anymore. Nobody asked why anymore. That was the thing about Ashvale's rules — they had stopped being rules somewhere back down the generations and become weather instead, simply the conditions one lived under. Don't wander past the standing stones after dark. Don't sing the old songs past the second verse. Don't carry anything out of the valley that you couldn't bear to lose forever.

That last one was Mistress Hobb's own, delivered so many times it had worn a groove in Sable's skull: *Nothing leaves the valley, girl. Not letters, not keepsakes, not promises. What belongs to Ashvale stays in Ashvale.*

"Why?" Sable had asked once, aged nine, with a child's blunt courage. "Why can't anything leave?"

Mistress Hobb had looked at her for a long moment — a look Sable hadn't understood then but understood now, having seen it again on the faces of people catching themselves before saying something true — and said, "Because the valley looks after its own. And its own look after the valley. That's the whole of the covenant between us, and covenants aren't questions."

*Covenants aren't questions.* As if the word itself were a locked door and the lock had rusted shut on purpose.

Sable tied off another bundle and glanced, as she did a dozen times a day without meaning to, at the shelf above the hearth. The prophecy shelf, the household called it, though nobody took it seriously — a sagging plank of books nobody read. Herbals with broken spines. A farmer's almanac forty years out of date. A book of charms so cautious it mostly consisted of instructions *not* to do things. And at the end, half-hidden behind a jar of pickled ramsons, a slim black volume with no title on its spine that Sable had never once seen anyone open.

Tonight, for reasons she couldn't have named, her eyes snagged on it and stayed.

Later, she told herself. You've somewhere to be.

And she did. She had somewhere to be, and the thought of it made her fingers clumsy on the red thread for the first time in years.

* * *

Tam Vess was waiting at the foot of the ferry path, and he was nervous, and the fact that he was nervous made Sable feel suddenly, absurdly brave.

He'd dressed for the occasion, which in Tam's case meant he'd washed his hair and worn the shirt his mother had sewn him last spring, blue as a jay's wing, slightly too tight across the shoulders because Tam had spent the summer hauling the ferry rope and it had remade him broader than his mother's patterns allowed. He stood with his hands jammed in his pockets, watching the path, and when he saw her coming he did the thing he always did — that quick, helpless grin, like the sun coming out over the Gray Sisters — and Sable felt the ache in her chest that wasn't quite longing and wasn't quite fear but lived in the same neighborhood.

"You came," he said.

"You doubted it?"

"I doubted everything up to and including my own existence." He offered his arm with a formality so stiff it circled around into comedy. "Mistress Brightwater. May I have the honor of this entirely unprecedented walk?"

"You may." She took his arm. His sleeve was warm from the evening sun. "Though I should warn you, unprecedented walks have been known to lead to unprecedented conversation."

"Gods forbid." They started down toward the water. "What shall we talk about? The price of eels? The weather, which is always the same? The fascinating question of whether Old Marta's goat has finally learned to open latches, and if so, what this means for the future of our civilization?"

"The goat," Sable said gravely, "is clearly a sign of the end times."

"There it is. There's the doom and gloom I courted you for."

They laughed, and the laugh loosened something in her, and for a while they just walked, down the switchback path to the river, past the willows trailing their fingers in the current, out onto the flat shingle bank where the ferry landing jutted into the slow brown water. The evening was doing its best work: the light gone long and gold, the mountains standing around them like patient elders, the river muttering to itself about whatever rivers mutter about when they think no one's listening.

This was the thing about Tam, Sable thought. He made even the valley seem almost big enough. When they were together, the same twelve miles of river and the same ring of mountains turned into a country worth exploring. He saw jokes in fence posts and stories in driftwood. He'd once spent an entire afternoon convincing her that the eddy below the landing was haunted by the ghost of a fish so large and so offended at being caught that it now devoted eternity to tangling lines out of spite.

But the valley always reasserted itself. Always. She could be laughing with Tam on the shingle bank and her eyes would still travel, helplessly, up to the notch in the southern gorge where the river escaped — the one gap in the wall, the one place the water got out — and the ache would come back. The ache of a jar. Of a firefly.

"My da says the ferry's been in the family six generations," Tam said. He'd found a flat stone and was skipping it, badly, three hops and a plop. "Six generations of Vesses, rowing the same hundred yards of water. Sometimes I try to imagine the first one. Great-great-great-whatever-grandfather Vess, standing right about there, thinking, *yes, this is it, this is my life's work, and my son's, and his son's, unto the ending of the world.*"

"And what do you think he'd say if he saw you?"

"He'd say I skip stones like a girl." Another throw; four hops this time, grudgingly respectable. "And then he'd ask me why I keep looking south."

Sable went still. "Do I do that?"

"Constantly." Tam turned to face her, and his grin softened into something more careful. "You look at the gorge like it owes you money. Or like you owe it something. I can't decide which."

The river filled the silence between them, patient and brown and going somewhere.

"It's just—" She picked up her own stone, weighed it. "Don't you ever feel it? How small it all is? Not the valley. The *life*. Everyone in Ashvale gets one story, and it's the same story with different names in it. You're born, you learn your parents' trade, you marry someone whose trade you already know, you raise children who'll do the same, and the mountains watch you do it, and that's it. That's the whole book. One chapter, printed a thousand times."

"That's a bit bleak for a first date."

"This is a first date?"

"Sable." He put his hand over his heart. "I have planned this walk for eleven days. I rehearsed opening lines in front of a mirror. If this is not a first date, I have humiliated myself for nothing, and I will be forced to throw myself in the river, where I will haunt the eddy alongside the offended fish."

She laughed despite herself, and threw her stone, and it skipped clean across the dusk-silvered water — five hops, six, a personal miracle — and Tam whooped like she'd won a war.

"See," he said, "that's the thing you don't give yourself credit for. You say the valley's small, but you're not. You're the least small person I know. You're the only person in Ashvale who looks at the rules like they're a puzzle instead of a fact. Everybody else just... wears them. Like coats. You keep trying to find the seams."

"Somebody has to check if the coat comes off."

"And does it?"

She didn't answer. Because the honest answer was *I don't know*, and the truer answer was *I'm terrified to find out*, and the truest answer of all was one she hadn't yet found words for: that lately, when she stood on the shingle bank and looked at the notch in the gorge, she felt less like she was looking at an exit and more like she was looking at a door that was looking back.

"Come on," Tam said gently. "Walk me to the willow bend. The moon's nearly up, and I'm told certain apprentices must be home before it clears the Sawtooths, on pain of extra weeding."

"On pain of lectures," Sable corrected. "Weeding I can survive. Mistress Hobb's lectures have killed hardier souls than mine."

They walked. The moon rose fat and white over the eastern ridge, and the willows at the bend trailed silver, and Tam talked about his father's plans to rebuild the ferry deck come spring, and Sable listened and laughed and let herself be happy, genuinely happy, in the way that was only possible here — small happinesses, well-made, like the valley itself.

And yet.

As they reached the willow bend and turned back toward the landing, the black book on the prophecy shelf surfaced in her mind again, unbidden, the way a fish surfaces in still water. She'd dusted that shelf a thousand times. She'd never once touched that book. But tonight, dusting it, her rag had snagged on the cover — snagged as though the leather had caught her, held her, a heartbeat longer than any book should — and in that heartbeat she'd felt something she had no name for. Not warmth. Not cold. Something older than either. Something like attention.

Like the valley, noticing her noticing it.

"Earth to Sable." Tam waved a hand. "You went somewhere."

"Just thinking about the goat again."

"Liar. But I'll allow it." He stopped at the landing, where the path split — his way along the shore road, hers up the switchback to the cottage — and stood there working up to something, visibly, the way he worked up to cold-water swims. "So. Eleven days of planning, and here's what I actually wanted to say, which I'm going to say quickly before my nerve goes. Tomorrow night there's a moonrise over the gorge that Da swears happens only once every few years, when the light comes straight down the notch and sets the river on fire all the way to the landing. Everyone says you have to see it from the water. So. Will you come out with me? In the boat? Just us, and the river, and the burning water?"

The gorge. The notch. The door that looked back.

Every sensible thread in her wove the same answer. Say yes, said the sensible threads, because it's Tam, and he's kind, and he makes the jar feel bigger. Say yes, and stop staring at gaps in walls.

"Yes," Sable said. "Tomorrow night. The boat, the river, the burning water."

His grin could have lit the landing without a lantern. "It's a date. A real one. Second of your unprecedented walks."

"First real date," she corrected, and he laughed, and raised two fingers to his brow in a ferryman's salute, and went off along the shore road whistling something old and half-remembered, a tune with more verses than anyone in Ashvale dared sing.

Sable climbed the switchback with the moon climbing beside her, and the ache in her chest had changed key — still restless, still too-big-for-the-valley, but threaded now with something warmer. Tomorrow. The boat. The burning water.

At the cottage door she paused, hand on the latch, and looked back once at the valley laid out below her in blue dusk — the fields, the roofs, the river running quiet toward the notch in the south. *Nothing leaves the valley,* Mistress Hobb always said. *What belongs to Ashvale stays in Ashvale.*

Then why, Sable wondered, did it feel like the valley was holding its breath?

Inside, the hearth had burned low. Mistress Hobb slept in her chair, mending folded in her lap, and above her the prophecy shelf sat in shadow — and at the end of it, behind the pickled ramsons, the slim black book waited with its blank spine, patient as the mountains, as though it had been waiting a very long time indeed.

Sable hung up her cloak, banked the fire, and tried not to look at it.

She looked anyway.
