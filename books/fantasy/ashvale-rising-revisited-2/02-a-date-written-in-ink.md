## 2. A Date Written in Ink

Mistress Ivenna's cottage smelled the way it always did on a Thursday: beeswax and dried yarrow, woodsmoke from a fire that had been banked since dawn, and underneath it all the faint mineral tang of the river stones she kept in every windowsill for reasons she had never once explained to me. I had stopped asking questions like that years ago. In Ashvale, the answer to *why* was almost always *because it has always been so*, and after sixteen years of that answer, a girl learns to swallow her curiosity like a stone — whole, and with difficulty.

I was supposed to be dusting.

That was my punishment for Tuesday's transgression, which had been nothing more than suggesting — *suggesting*, mind you, in the mildest possible tone — that perhaps the valley's rules about what could leave Ashvale might have exceptions. A letter, say. A pressed flower sent to a cousin over the ridge. Mistress Ivenna had gone very still in the way she did when I brushed against something old, and then she'd handed me the feather duster and pointed at the prophecy shelf, and that had been the end of the conversation.

The prophecy shelf was the worst shelf in the house to dust. It stood in the back room, past the drying racks of foxglove and the jars of tinctures labeled in her cramped hand, and it held forty-odd books that nobody read. They were leather-bound and swollen with damp, their spines cracked into pale seams, and they smelled of cold cellars and long silence. Prophecy, in Ashvale, was considered slightly embarrassing — like a relative who talked too much at gatherings. The founders had written volumes about the valley's destiny, and the valley had responded by settling comfortably into its destiny of ferry crossings and harvest fairs and never, ever crossing the border stones, and the books had gathered dust accordingly.

I worked down the shelf row by row, pulling each volume out, wiping its cover, setting it back. The duster left gray streaks on my sleeves. Outside the small window, the light was going gold and long, the way it does in late autumn when the sun gives up early, and I kept glancing at it because tonight —

Tonight.

Tam Verrin was meeting me at the ferry landing at moonrise. My first real date. Not a shared walk home from market where our hands happened to brush, not the time he'd helped me carry a basket and we'd laughed about the crooked handle — an actual, deliberate, planned evening, agreed upon three days ago beside the well, where he'd said, "Moonrise, at the landing," and looked at me with such open, terrified sincerity that I'd nearly said yes before he finished asking.

I had spent the intervening days in a state I can only describe as humming. Even Mistress Ivenna had noticed. "You're grinding the mortar wrong," she'd said that morning, watching me attack dried lavender like it owed me money. "Whatever's distracting you, set it down until your work is done."

*It will be done by moonrise,* I'd thought. *Everything will be done by moonrise.*

Which is why, when I pulled the thirty-first book from the shelf — a slim volume bound in gray-green leather gone soft as moths' wings — I was already half-distracted, already thinking about what one wore to a ferry landing in October, when the title caught my eye and stopped my hand mid-air.

*The Brightwater Reckoning.*

My name. Or near enough. Brightwater was my family name, though there were precious few of us left to bear it — just me and Gran, who had died two winters ago, and before her a line of Brightwater women stretching back through Ashvale's founding like a rope frayed to its last strands. I turned the book over. No author. No date on the spine. But when I opened the cover, the flyleaf bore an inscription in faded iron-gall ink:

*Set down in the hundredth year of the Valley's peace, that what is coming may be known.*

A hundred years old. Older than Gran. Older than anyone living in Ashvale.

I should have put it back. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger keeps accounts of girls who find things they shouldn't: *she knew better, and she reached for the duster, and she hesitated.* Because I did hesitate. Mistress Ivenna's voice was in my ear — *some books are shelved for a reason, Sable* — and the cottage around me seemed to lean in, the way a room does when it wants to hear what happens next.

But the inscription said *what is coming may be known.* Coming. Future tense. And I was a girl with a date at moonrise and a life too small for her body, and curiosity is the one hunger Ashvale's rules never managed to starve out of me.

I carried the book to the window, where the last of the light could reach it, and I began to read.

* * *

The first pages were history — dry as the shelf it had slept on. The founding of Ashvale, the covenant with "the far realm" (unnamed, as if even the name had been worn away), the sealing of the borders. I skimmed. I'd heard the story at every Founding Day festival of my childhood: the brave founders, the necessary walls, the peace. It was the one story Ashvale told itself on repeat, and like all stories told too often, it had gone smooth and featureless as a river stone.

Then, a third of the way in, the handwriting changed.

The earlier entries were formal, careful, the script of someone writing for posterity. This new hand slanted hard to the right, letters crowded together, ink blotted in places as if the writer couldn't wait for the pen to drink. And the words stopped being history and started being something else entirely.

*A girl will come of the Brightwater line,* it began. *Not the first of the line, but the last. She will be restless — mark that, for restlessness is the sign. She will chafe at the borders of her life as water chafes at ice. And on the night of her first true courting, under the rising moon, at the place where the water meets the wood—*

I stopped reading.

The cottage was very quiet. The banked fire ticked in the other room. Somewhere outside, a crow said one flat syllable and thought better of it.

I told myself it was coincidence. Of course it was coincidence. A girl of the Brightwater line, restless, chafing — that described me, yes, but it described any girl who had ever grown up inside a valley with rules, and the ferry landing was the natural place for anything in Ashvale because everything in Ashvale eventually touched the water. Coincidence. Probability. The mathematics of a small place.

I turned the page.

*She will go to meet him at moonrise,* the book said. *He will be the son of the ferryman, and he will have practiced his words, and he will say them anyway: "I wanted this to be somewhere the water could witness it."*

The floor did not move. The walls did not fall. I simply stood there in the gold-going-gray light with a century-dead stranger's handwriting crawling up my arms like cold water, because those were Tam's words. His exact words. He'd said them to me three days ago beside the well, awkward and earnest, and I had laughed — kindly, I hoped — and he'd flushed and said, "Well. It sounded better in my head."

No one else had heard them. We had been alone. The well yard had been empty but for us and the bucket and the long shadows of evening.

*I wanted this to be somewhere the water could witness it.*

Written down a hundred years before Tam Verrin was born.

My hands were shaking. I noticed them the way you notice weather happening to someone else — distantly, with a strange lack of ownership. I sat down on the stool by the window without deciding to, and I made myself keep reading, because the alternative was to stop, and some part of me understood, in the wordless way you understand a current under calm water, that stopping would be worse.

*The hour will be moonrise,* the book went on, patient now, terrible in its patience. *The place will be the landing below the elder tree. He will wear his father's blue coat. She will braid rosemary into her hair and forget she has done it, and he will notice, because noticing is his nature.*

My free hand went to my hair.

There was rosemary in it. I had braided it in that morning without thinking — a sprig tucked behind my ear, the way I do when I want luck, the way I've done since I was small enough to believe it worked. I hadn't decided to wear it tonight. I hadn't thought about it at all until this moment, standing in front of a sentence that had been waiting a hundred years to describe me doing exactly this.

And Tam — Tam noticed things. That was true. That was so precisely, painfully true that my eyes stung. He noticed when I was quiet instead of merely silent. He noticed which of Mistress Ivenna's herbs I liked the smell of. He noticed, once, months ago, that I'd stopped singing while I worked, and he'd asked why, and no one else had ever asked why.

The light through the window had gone from gold to copper to the first bruised purple of dusk. I had lost an hour. Maybe more. The book lay open across my knees, and I realized my thumb had been holding my place, pressing into the paper like a pulse.

One page left.

* * *

I want to say I read it slowly. I didn't. I read it the way you pull a splinter — fast, because slow would be unbearable.

*At the landing, the water will remember what the valley has made it forget. The veil will thin, and the girl of the Brightwater line will be called through — for the covenant was broken by her blood, and only her blood can mend it or end it. What waits beyond is not written here, for the writer did not live to see it. But let her know this much: the prophecy names her, and the name is—*

The name was gone.

Not torn out — erased. Someone had scraped the vellum with a blade or pumice, carefully, thoroughly, taking the letters down to the skin of the page. But scraping leaves ghosts. Held at an angle to the dying light, the damaged patch showed faint ridges where ink had once sat, and I tilted the book, and tilted it again, and breathed on the page the way you breathe on glass, and the ghost-letters surfaced like fish rising in dark water.

Three letters survived the erasure. Three, at the start of the name.

*S-a-b.*

Sable.

The crow outside spoke again, closer now, and I flinched so hard the book slid off my knees. I caught it against the wall. My heart was going like a bird in a closed room, throwing itself at the bars.

Someone had erased my name. A hundred years ago, someone had read this prophecy — had written it, maybe, or received it, or found it — and had come to the final line, the line that named the girl who would be called through the veil, and had taken a blade to it. Had scraped my name out of the future.

Why? To hide it? To protect whoever came after? To unmake the prophecy by unmaking its anchor? I thought of Mistress Ivenna's stillness on Tuesday, the way she'd gone quiet when I mentioned exceptions, the way she'd assigned me to this shelf — *this* shelf — as punishment. Had she known this book was here? Had she read it? Was the erasure her work, or her teacher's, or her teacher's teacher's, a sin passed down the line like a candle flame?

Or — and this thought arrived cold and unwelcome — was the erasure meant for me? A warning. *Do not finish this sentence. Do not be the girl at the end of it.*

The fire had burned down to embers in the next room. Through the window, above the black line of the western hills, the sky was clearing toward night, and I could see the first stars, and I knew — I knew with the certainty of the rosemary in my own hair — that the moon would rise soon over the eastern ridge, and that at moonrise Tam Verrin would be waiting at the ferry landing in his father's blue coat, saying words a dead man had written for him.

Every sensible thing in me said: stay. Burn the book. Braid no more rosemary. Tell Tam the well-yard promise was a joke, a child's game, and stay inside the walls of the life that fit me whether or not it fit me well.

But the book had said *restless*. It had said I would chafe at the borders of my life as water chafes at ice. And it was right, it had always been right, it had been right for a hundred years, and there is a particular kind of terror in being known so completely by a stranger dead before your grandmother was born — but there is a kind of relief in it too. Someone, somewhere, had looked down the long corridor of time and seen *me*, not the apprentice, not the hedge-witch's drudge, not the girl who ground mortar wrong — *me* — and had thought me worth writing down.

Worth erasing, too. Both things could be true.

I stood. I smoothed my skirt. I took the sprig of rosemary from behind my ear, looked at it, and — hands steadier than they had any right to be — tucked it back.

The book I wrapped in the dust cloth and pushed deep beneath my mattress, spine inward, like hiding a knife. Then I blew out the lamp, let myself out the back door past the sleeping herb beds, and started down the path toward the river, toward the elder tree, toward the landing where the water met the wood.

Where the water met the wood. Where, if a century-old stranger was to be believed, the water was about to remember something the valley had made it forget.

The moon cleared the eastern ridge just as I reached the top of the ferry path, round and white and rising, and below me the river lay still as poured metal, and Tam's lantern was already lit at the landing, a small gold heartbeat in the dark.

I went down to meet my fate. I told myself I was only going to meet a boy.
