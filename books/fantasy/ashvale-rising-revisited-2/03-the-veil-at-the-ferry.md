## 3. The Veil at the Ferry

The prophecy book lay hidden in the bottom of Sable's satchel, wrapped in her spare shawl like something sick that needed keeping warm.

She had told herself a dozen ways to leave it behind. Leave it under the loose floorboard where she'd found it. Burn it in the hedge-witch's stove and let the smoke carry the words somewhere they couldn't follow her. But every time she reached for the door, she felt it against her hip like a second heartbeat, and she could not make herself set it down. A book that knew tonight's hour. A book that knew Tam's voice before she did. A book with half a name at its end — *Sable*, or close enough to *Sable* that the missing letters hardly mattered.

Mistress Ivy had gone to the far orchards for a birthing among the goats and would not return until well past dark. That was luck, or it was design, and Sable had stopped trying to tell the difference.

She walked the river path as the light went long and gold, and Ashvale did what Ashvale always did: it forgot itself into beauty. The valley was a cup of green held between two ridgelines, and the river ran through it slow and silver, patient as an old dog. Willow shade. Cattle lowing in the near pasture. Someone's supper smoke rising blue from a chimney, smelling of onions and thyme. It was lovely. It had always been lovely. And for the first time in her nineteen years, Sable looked at all that loveliness and saw the walls of it — not stone, nothing so honest, but a boundary she had never once been permitted to test. No one left the valley. No letters went out. No strangers came in except by accident of weather, and they never stayed past the season. When she was small she'd thought the world beyond the ridges was simply empty, the way children think the sea ends at the horizon. Mistress Ivy had corrected her gently: *The world is very full, child. It is only full elsewhere.*

*Elsewhere.* The word had been a splinter in her ever since.

Tonight, maybe, the splinter would come out.

* * *

Tam was already at the ferry landing when she arrived, and the sight of him knocked something loose in her chest that she pretended was just her breath.

He stood on the worn planks of the dock with his sleeves rolled, coiling rope he didn't need to coil, because Tam fidgeted with rope the way other people fidgeted with worry. He was the ferryman's son and built like it — broad through the shoulders, sun-brown, with dark hair that the river wind never managed to discipline. When he saw her he smiled, and the smile started crooked the way it always did, one corner first, as if the rest of his face needed convincing.

"You came," he said.

"You doubted it?"

"I doubted everything up to and including myself." He set the rope down. "I've been here since the bell rang. I asked Old Maren if I could take the ferry out tonight and she looked at me like I'd proposed to her."

"Did you propose to her?"

"I'm saving my proposals." He said it lightly, but his ears went red, and Sable loved him a little more for it.

Their first date. The phrase still felt absurd, said inside her own head. In Ashvale you didn't *date*; you were paired by proximity and your mothers' patience, and you married the boy who helped your father with the harvest because he was there and decent and the valley was small. But Tam had cornered her by the mill three weeks ago, red as a boiled beet, and said, *I know this isn't how it's done, but I don't care how it's done. Walk with me. Just walk.* And she had walked, and kept walking, three weeks of evenings along the river, until tonight — tonight he'd promised her something better than walking. He wouldn't say what. He'd only grinned and said, *Trust the ferryman's son about water.*

The prophecy book had known that too. *The ferryman's son will promise her the water,* the old ink read, *and the water will keep the promise instead.*

She had read that line forty times since dawn. She still didn't know what it meant. She only knew that when Tam took her hand — warm, calloused, certain — and led her to the ferry's edge, her heart was pounding for reasons that had nothing to do with love and everything to do with the book pressing against her ribs in its bag.

"Close your eyes," Tam said.

"If you push me in the river, I'm telling your mother."

"My mother would help me hold you under. Close them."

She closed them. The world went soft — wind in the willows, water slapping the hull, the creak of the dock boards, Tam's breath close enough to feel. His hand tightened around hers.

"Sable Brightwater," he said, and her whole name in his mouth sounded like a spell someone else had written, "I've been practicing this speech for three weeks and I've forgotten all of it, so you're getting the rough draft. I know we're supposed to be sensible. I know the valley says we're too young and the river says wait and everyone's mother has opinions. But I've watched you look at the ridge line like it owes you money, and I thought — nobody should have to spend their whole life looking at a wall. So." A breath. "Whatever's out there, whatever comes — I want to be the one standing next to you when you find out."

The words landed in her one by one, like stones dropped into deep water, each ripple wider than the last.

*Tonight, at the hour of the lamp-lighting, the ferryman's son will speak her heart back to her,* the book had said. Word for word. *And the water will answer.*

"Tam," she whispered, eyes still shut, "open your eyes. Look at the water."

"Why? What—"

"Look."

The sound of the river had changed.

It wasn't loud — that was the terrible part. It was quiet. All the small busy noises of moving water, the slap and gurgle and rush, had drained away, replaced by a silence so complete she could hear her own pulse in her ears. Beneath it, faint as a hymn heard through a church wall, something hummed. Not a voice. Not quite a note. A vibration in the teeth, in the breastbone, in the planks under their feet.

Sable opened her eyes.

The river had gone flat as poured glass. Every ripple stilled. The last of the sunset lay across its surface in a sheet of hammered copper, and in that copper she could see reflections that did not match the sky — lights moving down there, deep down, pale gold and slow, like lanterns carried through drowned halls. The willows on the bank hung motionless. Even the wind had stopped, as though the whole valley were holding its breath around them.

"What is that?" Tam's voice had gone thin. His hand found hers again and gripped hard. "Sable, what *is* that?"

"I don't know." Her mouth was dry. "But I think it knows us."

The humming swelled. The copper light beneath the surface brightened, gathered, rose — and the water began to open.

Not like ice breaking. Like a page turning. The river split down its center in a seam of white-gold radiance, peeling back in smooth curling sheets, and what lay beneath was not riverbed. It was depth without bottom, a vertical ocean of slow-turning stars, and rising out of it came a smell she had no name for — rain on hot stone, night-blooming flowers, ozone, and underneath it all something older, like the air inside a house that had been closed for a hundred years.

Every instinct she owned screamed *run*. Her feet did not move. Terror and wonder had locked arms inside her chest, and neither would let the other lead.

"The book," she breathed. "Tam, there's a book — I found it today, it's a hundred years old, and it described tonight. Your speech. This. It said—" Her voice cracked. "It said the water keeps the promise instead."

"A book can't—" Tam stared at the opening water, at the light climbing his stunned face. "That's not possible. Nothing crosses the valley line. Everyone knows—"

"Everyone knows wrong."

The pull came then.

It began at her ankles, gentle as a tide taking sand, and Sable had one lucid, screaming instant to understand that the water was not pulling her — the *other side* was, the way a lit window pulls a moth, the way home pulls the homesick. She dug her heels into the dock planks. It made no difference at all. The world tilted; the stars below grew larger; and Tam's hand wrenched in hers as he threw his whole weight backward, boots skidding, hauling against a force that did not care about his shoulders or his love or anything else.

"Don't let go!" he roared. "Sable — *don't let go!*"

"I'm not — I'm not letting—" 

The light took her to the waist and she felt it everywhere at once, cold and burning, like plunging into a river of memory. The dock, the willows, the gold sky — all of it smeared sideways like paint in the rain. She could see Tam's face above her, contorted, shouting words the hum swallowed whole. She could see the rope he'd been coiling, abandoned, snaking toward the edge. She saw him lunge for it, loop it, hurl the end down to her —

She caught it. The rope burned across her palms and she held on with everything her hedge-witch hands had ever learned about holding — grip like roots, mistress Ivy's voice in her skull, *a plant survives by holding, girl, now HOLD* —

For one heartbeat, one glorious heartbeat, she stopped sinking.

Then the light reached her shoulders, and the rope passed through her fingers like smoke.

No — worse. She still felt it. She felt the rope, the burn, Tam's grip on the other end, all of it continuing somewhere behind a skin of brightness she was falling through, the way you continue to feel a limb after it sleeps. Two worlds, and she was being pulled out of the one where her hands worked.

The last thing she saw of Ashvale was Tam's face, framed in the shrinking oval of the opening — mouth open, eyes wild, arms straining against nothing, holding a rope that no longer connected to anything he could reach. Holding on anyway. Because he had promised her the water, and he was the kind of fool who kept promises past all reason.

*Tam,* she tried to say, and had no mouth to say it with.

The seam of light closed over her like an eye.

* * *

Falling.

There was no other word for it, though *falling* suggested down, and down had stopped meaning anything. She tumbled through a dark shot with drifting motes of gold, and the motes were not dust — when they touched her they bloomed into brief images against her skin: a tower of white stone. A field of glass flowers ringing in wind she couldn't hear. A face, ancient and kind, mouthing words that dissolved before she could read them. Fragments of somewhere else, pressing against her like faces at a window.

*I'm dreaming,* she told herself. *I fell asleep on the dock waiting for Tam to finish his speech, and this is a dream, and dreams end.*

She pinched herself. It hurt, which was either reassuring or deeply not.

Then the dark ended all at once, and she hit real ground — wet grass, mercifully solid, knocking the breath out of her in a wheeze. She lay sprawled on her back under a sky that stopped her heart a second time.

Two moons. One silver and familiar as a coin, one vast and bruise-purple, banded like an onion cut crosswise, hanging so low she felt she could pitch a stone into its craters. Stars beyond counting, arranged in constellations she had never once seen from Ashvale's fields — and she had memorized every constellation Mistress Ivy taught her, all fourteen of them, twice over. These were strangers. An entire sky of strangers.

"Oh," Sable said aloud, to no one, to the moons. "Oh, that's not the valley."

She sat up. Her satchel had come through with her — the prophecy book a solid, blessed weight against her hip — and her palms still stung with rope burns that proved, at least, that some part of her had truly held on. Around her stretched a meadow of grass gone silver-grey, each blade tipped with a tiny bud of pale light, so that the whole field glimmered like a sea at dusk. Beautiful. Wrong. Beautiful *because* wrong.

Beyond the meadow, the land darkened into a horizon of ash-grey hills, and the air smelled of that closed-house age again, stronger now, threaded with something faintly sweet and faintly rotten, like fruit past its perfect day. Everything here seemed to exist at the exact peak of beauty and the exact beginning of decay, both at once, forever.

Somewhere in the grey hills, something howled. Not a wolf. Wolves sounded alive. This was a sound like wind through a burnt forest, high and hollow and hungry, and it was answered by another, farther off, and then a third, closer.

Sable got to her feet very slowly, the way you move when you hope stillness is a disguise.

The prophecy book. Her fingers shook as she dragged the satchel around and pulled it out, unwrapping the shawl. The pages fell open on their own — she would swear later they fell open on their own — to a passage near the end, and the ink, which had been faded brown all day, gleamed fresh and black:

*She crosses at the hour of the lamp-lighting, and the water keeps the promise. On the far shore the unremembered are waiting, and they know her name. Let her run, or let her stand; either way, the first keystone waits in the deep.*

The howling came again — nearer now, two voices braided together — and out of the treeline at the meadow's edge poured shapes that made her blood go to ice water. They were man-shaped and they were not, figures of grey ash held loosely together, embers guttering in the hollows where eyes should be, trailing smoke like torn veils. They moved without steps, flowing over the grass, and where they passed, the little buds of light on the grass-blades winked out, dead.

The unremembered. Waiting. Knowing her name.

Sable ran.

She ran with the book clutched to her chest and the alien moons wheeling overhead and terror shrieking in her ears — and underneath the terror, shameless and irrepressible, a spark of the other thing, the thing she'd carried all her life and never had a name for until this exact moment, sprinting through a meadow of dying light under two moons in a world her grandmother's generation had sealed away:

Exhilaration. The ache of a life too small, finally tearing at the seams.

The wraiths flowed after her, silent as spilt ink — and ahead, at the meadow's far border, a figure rose up out of the grass with a drawn blade and a voice like gravel:

"Down! Get *down*, you idiot girl — you're leading them straight to—"

The world went white at the edges. Sable's legs gave out beneath her, whether from the running or the crossing she couldn't say, and the last thing she saw before the dark took her was the stranger's face — young, exhausted, furious — going suddenly, strangely pale.

"Brightwater," the stranger said. "Of course. Of *course* it's a Brightwater."

Then the meadow, the moons, and the hunger at her heels all dissolved together, and Sable Brightwater fell into her first sleep in the world of Vael.
