## 4. Where Magic Remembers

Sable woke to the smell of rain that had never fallen.

It hung in the air — petrichor without a storm, the ghost of water — and beneath it something older, like the inside of a book left closed for a hundred years. She was lying on ground that was soft as moss and cool as riverstone, and above her, where the sky should have been, there was a ceiling of light: not sun, not cloud, but a slow drift of luminous motes, pale gold and silver, moving with the unhurried patience of snow that had forgotten which way was down.

She sat up too fast. The world tilted, righted itself, tilted again.

*The ferry,* she thought. *The water opened. I fell—*

She had not fallen. Falling was fast. Whatever had happened to her had been slow and total, like being poured through a keyhole, and the last thing she remembered was Tam's face on the landing, his mouth open around a word she'd never hear, and then the dark, and then this.

She was in a forest. Or what had been a forest, once, and could not quite bring itself to stop being one. The trees were enormous — oaks, or the memory of oaks — with trunks wide as cottages, but their bark was translucent at the edges, and when she squinted she could see faint shapes moving inside the wood: leaves, birds, a whole green summer pressed and preserved like flowers between pages. Some of the trees still had leaves, but the leaves were the wrong colors — faded violet, washed-out copper, a green so pale it was almost a rumor. Here and there a branch ended in mid-air, dissolving into a shimmer, as if the tree had simply run out of the idea of itself.

Everything was beautiful the way old photographs are beautiful, and everything was fading the same way.

"Right," Sable said aloud, because saying it aloud made it slightly less impossible. Her voice sounded strange here — it carried too far and came back too slowly, like a shout across a valley that took its time answering. "Right. You're not in Ashvale anymore."

The words hung in the moted light. Somewhere, far off, something answered her — a sound like a bell made of ice, struck once, and then a silence that felt like listening.

She got to her feet. Her boots were intact, her coat was intact, and in her coat pocket her fingers found the small tin of hedge-salve she'd carried since she was twelve, and that — absurdly, wonderfully — made her feel braver than anything else had. Her mistress's salve. Ashvale-made, Ashvale-simple, beeswax and comfrey and a charm stitched into the lid that was supposed to keep it from spoiling. It was here. She was here. Things crossed over. Things could, presumably, cross back.

*Presumably.* She held onto that word like a handrail.

She started walking, because standing still felt like agreeing to whatever this place wanted to do with her. The ground gave slightly under each step, springy and warm, and the light-motes drifted down around her and did not land, hanging a hand's width above the earth as though the ground were too holy — or too fragile — to touch. As she walked, she noticed that the motes left faint trails behind them, threads of brightness that hung in the air for a few heartbeats before unraveling. The whole forest was writing and erasing itself, over and over.

She passed a stream and stopped, because the water in it was flowing *upward* — gently, without urgency, climbing the streambed in slow silver ribbons and disappearing into a spring that fed nothing. She crouched at the bank and looked at her reflection and flinched, because for a moment the reflection had looked back at her a half-second late.

"Okay," she whispered. "Okay."

That was when she heard the footsteps.

Not footsteps — she corrected herself a moment later — but the *absence* of footsteps. A dry, papery rustling, like wind through winter reeds, moving in the trees to her left. She turned. Between two of the great translucent oaks, something was standing very still, and the stillness was worse than movement would have been.

It had been a person once. She was sure of that the way you're sure of a face in a fire. It was tall and thin and gray, its edges crumbling softly, endlessly, little flakes of ash lifting off its shoulders and dissolving before they reached the ground. Where a face should have been there was a smooth dark hollow, and in the hollow — she had to look, she couldn't not look — two faint points of light, like embers seen through a keyhole.

The wraith raised its head. The ember-lights fixed on her.

Every story she'd ever been told about the borders of Ashvale — and there were more than her mistress knew she'd heard, told in ferry-side whispers and forbidden in the cottage — agreed on one thing about the other side of the veil: *if the ash-people find you, do not run. Running is an invitation.*

So she didn't run. She stood there with her heart slamming against her ribs and her hand pressed flat against the tin in her pocket, and the wraith took one step toward her, and another, ash sifting from it with every movement, and she thought, with a strange, detached clarity: *this is what the prophecy meant. This is where the book was sending me. I walked into it with my hair braided for a date.*

A second wraith emerged from the trees behind the first. Then a third. They moved without haste, the way water moves toward a drain.

Sable's hand closed around the only weapon she had — the tin — which was not a weapon at all, and she did the thing her mistress had drilled into her a thousand times in the herb garden, the thing she'd rolled her eyes at: she grounded. Feet flat. Breath low. *Name the plant before you pick it. Name the thing before you face it.* Her lips moved.

"You were people," she said.

The nearest wraith stopped.

The word hung in the fading air, and the ember-lights in its hollow face flickered — and Sable, watching, understood with a jolt that the flicker was not menace. It was *attention.* It was the way a starving animal looks at a sound it hasn't heard in years.

"You were *people,*" she said again, louder, and the other two wraiths had gone still as well, ash drifting off them in slow gray snow. "I don't know who. I don't know your names. But you were people, and I'm sorry."

The first wraith took a shuddering step forward, its crumbling hand rising, and Sable braced herself for cold or pain or the keyhole-dark—

—and something whistled past her ear and struck the wraith in the chest, and the wraith came apart like a doused fire, collapsing into a drift of ash that the light-motes immediately began to gather, tenderly, the way one gathers spilled letters.

Sable spun.

A figure stood on a fallen branch ten feet up one of the great oaks — actually *stood* on it, balanced, as though gravity were a suggestion he'd heard and declined. He was lean and sharp-featured, somewhere between twenty and forty in the way that made her suspect neither number was true, with wind-tangled dark hair going gray at the temples and a long coat the color of wet slate, patched at the elbows with what looked suspiciously like birch bark. In one hand he held a sling. In the other, a smooth riverstone that glowed faintly, sullenly, like a coal refusing to die.

"Congratulations," he said, in a voice like a door that hadn't been oiled in years. "You talked to it. Nobody *talks* to them. Do you know what happens to people who talk to them?"

"They get rescued by rude men in trees?"

He blinked. Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile; more the memory of where a smile used to be kept. Then the other two wraiths moved, both at once, and the moment for smiles was over.

What followed was the fastest ninety seconds of Sable's life. The man came down from the branch in a single flowing drop, sling singing — the glowing stones struck with a sound like a struck bell and a struck match at once, and where they hit, the wraiths simply *ended*, folding into ash. But there were more coming; she could hear the papery rustle multiplying in the trees, five, six, a dozen, drawn perhaps by her voice or by the light of his stones, and the man swore with the fluency of long practice, grabbed her by the sleeve, and said, "Run now, ask questions at the part where you're still alive."

She ran.

They ran together through the fading forest, over ground that swallowed their footprints behind them, the man leading with the unerring certainty of someone who had made this exact run many times and hated it slightly more each time. The trees thinned. The light-motes thickened until Sable was running through a snowfall of gold. Ahead, the land dropped away into a hollow ringed with standing stones — real stone, solid and gray and blessedly *une*thereal — and the man hauled her down the slope, through a gap in the stones that hummed faintly as they passed it, a hum she felt in her back teeth.

The rustling pursuit reached the ring's edge and stopped. Sable turned, chest heaving, and saw the wraiths gathered beyond the standing stones, a dozen gray shapes, motionless, their ember-lights fixed on her through the gap. Not advancing. Not leaving. Just — waiting. Watching. Like mourners who had missed a funeral and weren't sure it was over.

"They can't cross the ring," the man said, leaning on his knees, breathing hard. "Old ground. It remembers being a border, even if nothing else does." He straightened, looked at her properly for the first time, and his eyes — gray, tired, and sharper than they had any right to be — narrowed. "You. You're not Vaeli. You're not *anything* I've seen. Where did you come from?"

"Ashvale," Sable said.

He didn't flinch at that. What he flinched at came next, because she added — because it seemed important, because she was still holding onto it like a handrail — "My name's Sable. Sable Brightwater."

The sling dropped from his hand.

He didn't seem to notice he'd dropped it. He had gone very still, the way the wraiths had gone still, and for a moment Sable thought — absurdly — that he was about to crumble too. His face did something complicated and fast: recognition, then denial, then a bitter arithmetic that ended, visibly, in a number he didn't like.

"Brightwater," he repeated.

"You know the name."

"Everyone in Vael knows the name." He crouched, picked up the sling without looking at it, and when he stood his face had closed like a shop shutting early. "It's carved into the covenant-stone. It's in half the songs, the bad ones and the worse ones. The Brightwater who broke the world and got away with it." His gray eyes raked her, cataloguing: the Ashvale wool of her coat, the hedge-salve tin, the braided hair she'd done for a boy at a ferry landing a lifetime ago. "A hundred years, and then one falls out of the sky in the middle of the Ashlands, talking to wraiths like they're parishioners." He laughed, short and airless. "Of course. Of *course.* The prophecy said a Brightwater would come. I told the Archive-wardens it was nonsense. I told the Ember Court it was nonsense. I told *myself*—"

"You could start," Sable said, and was surprised to hear how steady her voice came out, "by telling me what prophecy. And what covenant. And who broke it. Because I've been here for about an hour, and everything I've learned so far is that your trees are dying, your dead don't sleep, and my name makes you look like someone walked over your grave."

The man studied her for a long moment. The wraiths watched through the standing stones. Somewhere above, the light-motes drifted down and down and never landed.

"Wren," he said at last, as if paying out the word reluctantly. "Wren of the Ashlands. Keystone-keeper, which is a grand title for a man who guards three rocks nobody can use, in a country that's forgetting how to be a country." He slung the sling across his back and jerked his chin toward a path leading out of the stone ring, away from the watching dead. "Come on, Brightwater. You'll want to sit down for the rest, and there's a fire two miles east that doesn't judge people. Much."

"And then you'll tell me the truth?"

Wren paused at the ring's edge. He looked back at her, and behind the cynicism and the tiredness there was something older and rawer, something that had been waiting a hundred years for exactly this conversation and had hoped, right up until an hour ago, never to have it.

"I'll tell you what the wraiths couldn't," he said. "The covenant that kept your valley safe broke the day a Brightwater sealed it. And everything that's wrong with my world—" he gestured, one sweeping, bitter arc that took in the fading trees, the waiting dead, the snowfall of dying light, "—started with your family's name."

* * *

They walked east through the Ashlands while Wren talked, and the talking was less a conversation than a door being unlocked one rusted inch at a time.

Vael, he said, had not always been like this. There had been a covenant — an agreement older than either realm, cut into a standing stone at the place where the worlds wore thinnest. Magic had flowed through it both ways: Vael's wonders into the human lands, human memory and human *belief* back into Vael, because wonder, it turned out, was not self-sustaining. It was a fire, and memory was its wood. The humans had remembered Vael — told stories, left offerings, kept the old hedge-craft that was really just Vaeli magic worn down to something a valley could carry — and Vael had burned bright for it.

"And then," Wren said, "a hundred years ago, your side broke it. One act, one name on the stone, and the border sealed like a wound scabbing over. No magic in. No memory out. Your people forgot us — not chose to, *forgot*, the way you forget a dream by noon. And we—" he spread his hands at the translucent oaks, the upward stream, the ash drifting on the wind, "—we started running out."

"Running out of what?"

"Everything. Light. Color. *Time.*" He kicked a stone off the path and watched it land in grass that was more gray than green. "The Ember Court froze their part of the world to slow it down. The Archive drowned theirs on purpose — water remembers longer than air. And out here, in the Ashlands, we got the wraiths."

"The dead."

"The *unremembered.*" His voice dropped, and for the first time it wasn't cynical at all. "When the border sealed, everyone Vaeli who had ever been known by a human — every friend, every lover, every child of a mixed marriage — lost their anchor on the human side. And a Vaeli without a human memory holding them in the world doesn't die, Brightwater. They come apart. Slowly. And what's left walks the Ashlands looking for someone who'll say their name."

Sable thought of the wraiths at the stone ring, waiting. Of the ember-lights. Of the way they had *stopped* when she said *you were people.*

"And the prophecy?" she asked. "The one you don't believe in."

"I believe in it the way you believe in weather." Wren didn't look at her. "It's carved under the covenant-stone. A hundred years old, same as the breaking. *When the covenant lies in ruin, a Brightwater will come again, and by her hand the door shall open — or by her hand it shall close forever.*" He finally turned, and his gray eyes were flat and cold as slate. "The factions have spent a century arguing about which half of that sentence they like. The Ember Court wants the door shut. The Archive wants it opened on their terms. And the wraiths—" his jaw tightened, "—the wraiths just want to be remembered by somebody, and they think you're the somebody."

The path crested a low ridge, and below them Sable saw the fire: a small stone shelter, a cook-pot, a thread of honest smoke. Ordinary. Almost painfully so. Beside the shelter, half-buried in the earth, stood a slab of stone covered in carvings worn nearly smooth — and at the top of it, deep-cut and deliberate, a name she had known her whole life without ever knowing it was a key.

*BRIGHTWATER.*

Wren watched her read it.

"Welcome home," he said, and there was nothing kind in it at all.
