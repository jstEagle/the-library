## 6. Three Keystones, One Thief

Wren drew the map in the dirt with a stick that wasn't a stick. It looked like a stick — gray, knuckle-length, ordinary — but when he dragged it through the ash, the lines it left glowed faintly blue and refused to blow away.

"Three keystones," he said. "That's the whole prophecy in a sentence, so don't let anyone make it sound more poetic than it is. The covenant was bound with three of them: one for the sky-pact, one for the earth-pact, one for the memory-pact. When your grandmother broke the covenant, the keystones didn't shatter — they scattered. Magic doesn't destroy things like that. It just... loses track of them."

"Like socks," Sable said.

Wren's chalk-blue eyes flicked up at her. "What?"

"Nothing. Go on."

He studied her a moment longer, as if deciding whether she was mocking him, then went back to the map. "Three keystones, three keepers. And here's where I lose my enthusiasm for the whole enterprise." He tapped the first glowing mark, set in what looked like the drowned ruin of a city rendered in miniature. "The Sunken Archive. It holds the keystone of memory. Second—" his stick moved east across the ash to a cluster of jagged strokes "—the Ember Court. They hold the keystone of the sky-pact. Third is the worst one, out past the Ashlands, and we'll discuss it when you've had more sleep and I've had more to drink."

"And each keeper wants something different."

"Each keeper wants *everything* different." Wren sat back on his heels. "The Archive's scholars think the covenant should be reforged with knowledge as its foundation — every secret written down, every truth catalogued, nothing ever hidden again. Sounds noble until you realize they'd happily burn down a village to recover one footnote. The Ember Court believes magic is dying because it's been given away too freely; they want it sealed behind gates and bloodlines, hoarded like treasure. And the third faction—"

"The wraiths," Sable said quietly.

Something moved across Wren's face — there and gone, like a fish under ice. "You remember that part, then."

"I remember them coming out of the dark like the dark itself had learned to walk." She wrapped her arms around her knees. They'd made camp in the lee of a fallen archway, some structure so old that even its ruins had ruins, and the wind moving through the broken stone made a sound like slow breathing. "You said they were unremembered dead."

"I said we'd discuss it later." He snapped the stick against his knee — the glow died instantly — and stood. "Later isn't now. Now, we sleep, and tomorrow we walk to the Sunken Archive, and you try very hard not to die before you've done anything useful."

"That's your pep talk?"

"I'm a keystone-keeper, not a nursemaid." But he paused at the edge of the firelight, and without turning around he added, "Eat the rest of the bread. You'll need the strength. The Archive doesn't suffer fools, and it especially doesn't suffer hungry ones."

Sable ate the bread. It tasted like bark and honey and something faintly metallic, and it was, without question, the best thing she'd ever eaten, because she'd never been this hungry in her life.

She lay back against the cold stone and stared up at Vael's sky, which was not properly a sky at all — more a vast, slow ceiling of drifting color, violet bleeding into green bleeding into a deep bruised gold, with stars that moved like fish in a current. Somewhere beyond that ceiling was Ashvale. Somewhere beyond it was Tam, standing on the ferry landing, watching the water close over the place where she'd been.

Her chest did the thing it had been doing since she woke in this world — a slow, grinding ache, grief and wonder fighting for the same space.

*I'm going home,* she told the moving stars. *I'm going to fix whatever my grandmother broke, and I'm going home.* She didn't entirely believe it yet. But she'd discovered something about herself in the last two days, something that surprised her: she could hold a fear in one hand and a stubbornness in the other, and the stubbornness weighed more.

She slept.

* * *

They walked for most of the day through country that broke Sable's heart in ways she couldn't explain.

It wasn't ugly. That was the terrible thing. Everything in Vael was beautiful the way an old photograph is beautiful — colors slightly faded at the edges, details softening, as if the world were being gently erased by time itself. A meadow of glass flowers chimed when the wind passed through them, but half the flowers were clouded now, opaque, silent. A river ran beside their path, clear as breath, except that in places the water simply stopped mid-current, hanging like ribbon, waiting for permission to move that no longer came.

"What happens when it all fades?" Sable asked. "The whole realm. What's left?"

"Ash," Wren said. "And wraiths. The wraiths are what's left of things already forgotten. Don't ask me more about—"

"—later. I know." She kicked a pebble. It rolled ten feet and dissolved into dust, which seemed rude. "You know, in Ashvale we have stories about Vael. My mistress used to tell them. Gardens that sang. Cities grown from seed-crystals. A market where you could buy a year of good luck." She gestured at the fading meadow, the stuttering river. "None of the stories mentioned any of this."

"No," Wren said. "They wouldn't. Your valley's stories were written after the breaking. People don't put the wound in the story. They put the pretty parts in and leave the bleeding out." His voice was flat, but Sable was learning to hear the thing underneath his flatness — a kind of tired anger, banked like coals. "A hundred years ago, this was the singing-garden country. Now the flowers chime, and every year fewer of them do. That's what a broken covenant does. It doesn't kill quickly. It starves."

Sable walked in silence after that. Her grandmother's face floated up in her memory — Gran Ivy, who had died when Sable was nine, who had smelled of rosemary and woodsmoke and whose hands were always warm. *The Brightwater hands,* everyone called them. Good for finding things. Good for mending.

*Good for breaking worlds*, apparently.

"You knew my name," Sable said suddenly. "When you found me. Before I said anything. You flinched."

Wren didn't break stride. "Brightwater is a name with history here."

"What history?"

"The kind we'll discuss later."

"Wren."

"Sable." He mimicked her tone perfectly, and she hated how well he did it. Then, perhaps because she'd stopped walking, he sighed and turned. "Fine. A piece of it, since you'll only imagine worse otherwise. A hundred years ago, when the covenant broke, the human who broke it had help. An accomplice on our side. Someone who opened the way from within." His jaw tightened. "The accomplice's family name was Brightwater too. Or so the records say. The Archive keeps records. That's rather their entire personality."

Sable felt the ground tilt under her, though it hadn't moved. "My grandmother's family came *from* here?"

"I said the records say. Records can lie. Scholars can lie better." He started walking again. "Which is why, when we get to the Archive, you let me do the talking, and you touch absolutely nothing, and above all—" he pointed the stick at her like a sword "—you do not tell them your name until I say so."

"Why? If they already know—"

"Because names have weight here, and yours has a hundred years of it." He faced forward again, shoulders rigid. "Now come on. We're losing light, such as it is."

Sable followed, but her mind was a churned field. *An accomplice on our side.* A Brightwater who had helped break the covenant — from within Vael. Which meant her family's roots went down through the veil itself, deeper than Ashvale, deeper than the cottage and the garden and the hedge-craft lessons she'd resented every day of her life. She had thought she was stolen from one small world into another. Now it seemed she might have been brought *back*.

The strange part — the part she'd examine later, when she had privacy and courage — was that underneath the dread, some small stubborn coal in her chest had begun to glow. Because if her family belonged to both worlds, then maybe she did too. Maybe the prophecy book hadn't chosen a stranger. Maybe it had chosen exactly the right thief.

* * *

They smelled the Archive before they saw it: ink and brine and old paper, a smell so rich and specific that Sable's eyes stung with sudden homesickness for her mistress's cottage, which had smelled of almost exactly the same thing.

Then the land opened ahead of them, and she forgot homesickness entirely.

The Sunken Archive had once been a library the size of a town. Now it was a library the size of a town, drowned — sunk halfway into a lake of water so still and so clear it acted as a second sky. Towers of pale stone rose out of the flood at angles, leaning like tired scholars, their windows glowing with lamplight beneath the surface. Staircases descended into the water and kept going, and Sable could see — actually see — figures walking down those stairs into the deep, robes trailing, unhurried, breathing water as easily as air.

"They live under there?" she whispered.

"The Archive flooded a century ago, the night the covenant broke. The scholars' answer was to learn to breathe water." Wren shrugged. "Scholars are like that. Tell them the world has ended and they'll ask if the world left notes."

At the lake's edge stood a gate — or what had been a gate, its doors long rotted away, leaving only an arch of black stone carved over with script in a language Sable almost recognized. Almost, the way the valley's stories had almost mentioned the fading. The letters tugged at some corner of her mind her mistress had trained without either of them noticing.

As they approached, a figure surfaced in the shallows without a ripple: a woman, ageless, her hair floating around her head like ink poured upward, her eyes the flat silver of a fish's. She regarded them with the expression of someone interrupted during a very important footnote.

"Business," she said. Not a question.

"We've come for the keystone of memory," Wren said. "We invoke the old law of petition."

The woman's gaze slid to Sable, and Sable felt it like a lantern beam. "Petitioners must state their lineage."

Ah, Sable thought. There it is.

Wren opened his mouth — to lie, she was certain, to bury her name under some safe fiction — and Sable heard herself speak first.

"Brightwater," she said. "Of the Ashvale Brightwaters. Of the Vael Brightwaters, if the records are true. I'm the one the prophecy book named, and I've come to take back what my family broke."

The words rang in the still air. Under the water, lights flickered on, window by window by window, spreading outward through the sunken towers like a lit fuse — the whole drowned Archive waking up to look at her.

Wren closed his eyes as if praying for patience. "*Absolutely nothing*, I said."

"You said let you do the talking," Sable whispered back. "You were talking too slowly."

The silver-eyed woman drifted closer, until she stood on the flooded threshold, water sheeting off her robes. Up close, Sable could see that her skin bore fine lines of script, faded, like tattoos worn down by decades of reading.

"A Brightwater," the scholar said. "Returned. How the currents turn." Something that was almost a smile touched her mouth, and it was not a comforting sight. "Very well, petitioner. The Archive does not give. The Archive *tests*. Descend, answer truly, and the keystone may pass to you. Answer falsely—" her silver eyes glittered "—and the water will keep you. We are always short of research assistants."

Below, in the green deep, a staircase waited, descending into the drowned dark toward a glow that might have been lamplight or might have been something hungrier.

Sable's stomach dropped through the bottom of the world. Every sensible bone in her body — and she'd always had plenty, her mistress said so constantly — screamed at her to run.

Instead she heard herself say, "Do I get to dry off afterward?"

The scholar's smile widened by one degree. "Descend."

Sable looked at Wren. Wren looked at her, and for the first time since she'd fallen through the veil, something other than cynicism crossed his face — something that might, in better light, have been respect.

"Don't drown cleverly," he said. "Drown stupidly if you have to. Just don't drown."

"Your pep talks need work."

And Sable Brightwater, hedge-witch's apprentice, thief of her own inheritance, put one foot on the submerged stair and began to climb down into the dark.
