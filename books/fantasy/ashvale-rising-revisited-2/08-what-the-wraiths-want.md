## 8. What the Wraiths Want

The wraiths came at dusk, the way they always did, rising out of the ash like smoke deciding to be people.

Wren smelled them first — that dry, mineral stink of burnt memory — and his hand was on Sable's shoulder before he'd finished thinking, shoving her down behind a fallen arch of white stone. She made a sound of protest. He clamped his other hand over her mouth and put his lips against her ear.

"Not one word," he breathed. "Not one breath louder than mine."

Above them, the sky over the Ashlands had gone the color of an old bruise, violet bleeding into gray, and against it the wraiths drifted in their slow, terrible procession. There were nine of them tonight. Wren counted out of habit, the way other men counted coins or scars. Nine shapes of gray cinder and drifting soot, roughly human once, now worn smooth as river stones by a hundred years of being forgotten. They moved without legs. They moved without hurry. That was the worst part — the patience of them, as if they had all the time in the world, because in a sense they did. Time was the only thing anyone had ever left them.

Sable's heart was hammering under his palm; he could feel it through her coat, quick as a trapped bird. She was staring up at the wraiths with an expression he hadn't seen on her face before. Not terror. Something worse. Recognition, almost — the way you look at a stranger's profile and think *I know that jaw from somewhere.*

"Don't," Wren whispered, though she hadn't said anything. "Whatever you're about to feel, don't feel it yet."

* * *

They had been two days out from the Sunken Archive, one keystone wrapped in oilcloth against Wren's ribs, and the plan had been simple: cross the Ashlands high ground by night, rest by day in the hollows where the ash lay thick enough to muffle scent, and reach the Ember Court's outer roads before the queen's wardens finished licking their wounds. Simple plans were the only kind worth having in Vael anymore. Complicated plans required resources, and Vael's resources were mostly memories, and memories faded.

He should have known the Archive would send something after them. The librarians below the water had let Sable take the keystone too easily — Wren had said so at the time, and she'd laughed at him, actually laughed, this hedge-witch's runaway with ash still in her hair from falling through the veil, laughing at a keystone-keeper with forty years of dead country behind his eyes. *Maybe they just liked my answers,* she'd said.

Nobody in Vael liked anything for free. Liking was a currency too, and the Archive spent it carefully.

So: nine wraiths, moving along the ridge line in their patient procession, and beneath them in the hollow, a keeper and a Brightwater girl holding their breath while the ash sifted down around them like grey snow.

Wren had been hunted by wraiths before. It came with the office. Keepers carried keys, and keys were the last solid things in a dissolving world, and the unremembered dead wanted solid things the way drowning men wanted rope. But they had never hunted him like this — not in formation, not with purpose, not fanning out across the ridge in a pattern that looked uncomfortably like a net closing.

"They're searching," Sable breathed against his hand. Her eyes were huge in the failing light. "Not patrolling. Searching. For us?"

"For you." He hadn't meant to say it aloud. It slipped out the way guilt always slipped out of him, sideways, when he wasn't guarding the door.

Her head turned slowly. In the half-dark her face went very still, the way it had gone still in the Archive when the riddles turned personal, when the drowned library had asked her questions about hearth-smoke and grandmother's knots and the names women stitched into hemlines to keep them from unraveling.

"Me," she repeated. "Why me? I've been here four days."

*Four days,* Wren thought, *and already the dead know your name.* He took his hand off her mouth. There didn't seem to be much point anymore.

"Because of what you are," he said. "Because of who broke it. Because—" And there the sentence snagged, the way it always snagged, on the thing he suspected and could not prove and had been carrying since the moment he'd heard her surname spoken in the dark of the veil-crossing. *Brightwater.* A name out of the old ledgers. A name attached to a debt.

The nearest wraith drifted off the ridge and began, slowly, to descend into the hollow.

* * *

It came down like ash falling upward. That was the only way Wren could ever describe it afterward, if he described it at all, which he usually didn't. The wraith did not walk and did not fly; it simply agreed, gradually, to be lower, its edges smearing, its core a knot of darker gray where a heart might have been. Up close, the details resolved the way details do in nightmares: the suggestion of a collarbone. The ghost of braided hair, long undone. And where a face had been, a smoothness — not blankness exactly, but erasure, the way a page looks after the ink has been scraped away and written over and scraped again until the vellum gives up.

Sable rose out of cover before Wren could stop her.

Later — in the years after, when they told this story to travelers on both sides of the new-opened border, and they told it often — he would insist she'd been reckless. But standing there in the hollow with the dusk dying around them, watching her step out into the open with her chin lifted and her hands open at her sides, he understood with sick clarity that it wasn't recklessness. It was the same instinct that had made her answer the Archive's riddles honestly instead of cleverly. Some people met the unknown with a blade. Sable Brightwater met it with her whole face.

"I'm here," she said to the wraith. Her voice didn't shake, which surprised them both; he saw her own surprise flicker across her expression. "You've been looking for me. I'm here."

The wraith stopped. Ten feet away, close enough that the cold coming off it raised the fine hairs on Wren's arms. It inclined toward her — toward her chest, he realized, toward the oilcloth bundle against her ribs, no, wait, toward *her*, toward whatever it was that recognized her — and from somewhere inside the knot of it came a sound.

Wren had heard wraith-sounds all his life. Wind through burnt rafters. The sigh of a fire long out. This was different. This was a voice trying to remember how voices worked, a rasp and a crackle and then, threaded through it, almost lost, almost nothing:

*"...told her... we told her not to..."*

Sable swayed. "What did you tell her? Tell who?"

The wraith reached out. Not to strike — Wren knew striking when he saw it, had seen villages end in a single night of reaching gray hands — but to touch, the way the bereaved touch a photograph. Its fingers passed through the air an inch from Sable's cheek, and where they passed, the air itself dimmed, colors draining briefly from her skin, and she gasped and staggered back and Wren was moving, finally moving, hauling her behind him with the old practiced fury of a man whose entire job description was *stand between.*

"That's enough," he snarled at it, at all of them, at the century. "She doesn't remember you. None of us remember you. That's rather the point, isn't it?"

The wraith hung there. And then, horribly, gently, it nodded — a slow, grinding inclination of that erased face — and withdrew, rising back up the way smoke returns to a chimney, and the eight others turned with it, and the procession moved on along the ridge, patient as ever, patient as the grave, patient as forgetting.

* * *

They walked most of the night after that, because neither of them could bear to stop.

Wren chose the route without choosing it, his feet finding the old paths out of habit — the pilgrim road, the charcoal-burners' track, the causeway over what had been the Merrowfen before the fen drank ash instead of rain. He talked to fill the silence, which was unlike him, and he knew why he was doing it even as he did it: because if he stopped talking, she would ask questions, and the questions had teeth.

"The covenant-breaking wasn't a battle," he said. "People imagine it was a battle. It wasn't. It was a signing."

"A signing?"

"A document. An agreement. Sealed in blood and witnessed by both realms, back when both realms still bothered to witness each other's paperwork." He smiled, and felt the smile go wrong on his face, and abandoned it. "One night, a hundred years ago, the flow stopped. Magic stopped crossing the border in either direction. Vael began to starve — magic is food here, girl, it's water and bread and sunlight, and we've been on short rations for a century. And Ashvale forgot us. Not chose to forget. *Forgot.* The breaking ate the memory of itself. Nobody in your valley remembers that Vael exists, except in stories scrubbed clean of truth. And nobody remembers the ones who died in the breaking."

Sable walked three steps in silence. "The wraiths."

"The wraiths." He watched the road ahead, the pale ribbon of it winding through black fields. "When the covenant shattered, everyone standing at the border-stone died. Both sides. The humans who'd come to seal it, the Vaeli who'd come to protest it. A hundred souls, give or take. And because the breaking consumed memory — because forgetting was the price paid, the coin that bought your valley its safety — those hundred weren't mourned. They weren't buried. They weren't *remembered,* and in Vael, Wren, in Vael especially, to be unremembered is worse than death. Death ends you. Forgetting unmakes you, slowly, forever. So they walk. So they search. Looking for anyone who carries the shape of what was taken from them."

"And they found it in me." Her voice was flat. Careful. "Because of my family."

"There it is," Wren said quietly. "There's the question you've been not-asking since the ferry."

* * *

They stopped at dawn in the lee of a ruined watchtower, one of the old border keeps, its stones fused glassy by whatever heat had passed through them a century ago. Wren built no fire. Fires drew wraiths the way honey drew flies — warmth was memory's cousin, and the unremembered craved anything that remembered how to burn.

He sat with his back against the tower wall and the keystone against his ribs and told himself, for perhaps the thousandth time in forty years of keeping, that duty was enough. That a keeper needed no truths, only tasks. That the past was a country better left ungoverned.

Then Sable sat down across from him, knees drawn up, ash-grey light on her face, and said, "Tell me about the Brightwaters," and every one of those thousand resolutions went up like straw.

"Why?" he asked, buying time, hating himself for buying it.

"Because the Archive asked me riddles about hedge-craft I learned from my mistress, but the *answers* felt older than her teaching. Because the wraith said *we told her not to.* Because you flinched when you heard my name, the very first hour we met, and you've been flinching ever since, every time someone says it. You're a keeper of keystones, Wren. What else do you keep?"

The honesty of it was the worst part. She wasn't accusing. She was simply standing in front of him with her hands open, the way she'd stood before the wraith, offering her whole face to whatever came.

Wren looked out over the Ashlands. Somewhere under that ash were orchards he'd climbed as a boy. Somewhere under it was a house with a blue door, and a woman who'd sung off-key, and a brother who'd owed him money and would have paid it back eventually, given another fifty years. Unremembered, all of it. He was the keeper of keys because he had volunteered, at nineteen, furious and grieving, for a job that was mostly grief with paperwork.

"The Brightwater name appears in the border ledgers," he said at last. "Three generations back. Your grandmother's generation. The woman who signed the breaking — the human half of it, the hand that held the pen — her name was struck from every record, both realms, the same night the covenant fell. Struck so thoroughly that even the ledgers forget, pages smoothing over like water closing. But the ledgers were copied once, early, by a keeper who didn't trust forgetting. I've read that copy. I've read it more times than any text alive." He made himself look at her. "The ink is damaged where the name was. Burned, maybe, or wept on. But the letters that survive spell B-R-I-G-H-T—"

He stopped. Not because he wanted to. Because saying it plainly would make it true in a way that suspicion, however well-founded, never quite managed, and some part of him — the part that had liked her laugh at the Archive, the part that had started, treacherously, to hope — wanted one more hour of not knowing.

But Sable was already there ahead of him. He watched it land on her, watched her arrive at the arithmetic herself, her lips shaping the rest of the name without sound. *Brightwater.* Her own mouth finishing the word her grandmother's had begun.

"No," she said. It wasn't denial, exactly. It was smaller than that. It was the sound a person makes when a floor they've stood on all their life turns out to have been a trapdoor.

"She sealed your valley to save it," Wren said, and his voice came out gentler than he intended, gentler than he'd intended anything in decades. "That's the shape of it, as far as anyone can guess. Whatever happened that night, whatever threat she thought she was answering — she traded truth for safety. She bought Ashvale a hundred years of peace with a hundred Vaeli lives and a hundred human ones, and then she let the whole world forget the bill had ever been paid." He paused. "And the wraiths, girl — the wraiths aren't hunting you to punish you. I've watched them for forty years. They don't punish. They *search.* They're looking for the family that forgot them. They've been walking the ash for a century looking for anyone who might say their names."

Sable had gone very white. Her hands, he noticed, had gone to her own arms, gripping them, the way you hold yourself together in a wind.

"One of them," she said. "On the ridge. It tried to touch my face. Like it knew me."

"Yes."

"It said *we told her not to.*" Her eyes came up, wet and blazing. "Told her not to do it. Wren — they were *there.* They tried to stop her. They died trying to stop her, and then they were forgotten for it, and their families' families have been living safe and warm in the valley those deaths bought, and nobody knows, nobody has ever known—"

"Now you know," Wren said. "That's what they want. That's all they've ever wanted. To be known."

The silence that followed was long and full of ash. Far off across the black fields, the wraiths' procession crested a distant ridge, nine gray notes in a song with no singer, and Sable watched them go with an expression Wren recognized because he wore it in mirrors: the look of a person realizing that grief can be inherited too, that debts pass down bloodlines like eye color, that you can be born owing strangers everything.

"We need to move," he said finally, because the sun was up and movement was the only prayer he trusted. "The Ember Court's roads are two days east. If we're fast—"

"Wren." She hadn't moved. She was still watching the ridge. "The second keystone. The Ember Queen wants Vael sealed off forever — frozen, saved by stopping. And my grandmother sealed Ashvale to save it by forgetting." A breath that was almost a laugh, and had nothing of laughter in it. "Everyone in this story thinks salvation means shutting a door."

He looked at her then — really looked, the way he hadn't let himself since the ferry — and saw that something had changed in her face. The restless girl from the cottage full of herbs had walked into Vael four days ago. Something else was standing in her place now, something with a ledger of its own opening behind its eyes.

"Come on," he said softly, and held out his hand. "Before the court sends something worse than ghosts."

She took it. And behind them, far up on the ridge, the lead wraith turned — just once, just briefly — to watch them go, and Wren could have sworn, though he never said so, that where its face should have been, the ash had begun, very faintly, to remember a smile.
