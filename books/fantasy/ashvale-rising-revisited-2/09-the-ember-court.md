## 9. The Ember Court

The road to the Ember Court was paved with cooling glass.

Sable felt it through her boots even before Wren pointed it out — a faint warmth rising from the ground like the memory of a fire that had burned a thousand years ago and never quite finished being sorry about it. The path wound up through hills of black slag where rivers of once-molten stone had frozen mid-pour, hanging off cliffsides in ropes and curtains, amber and deep red, catching the light of a sky that never quite decided on a color.

"It used to be warm here," Wren said. He walked with his staff planted at each step, favoring the bandaged shoulder where the wraith's touch had scored him back in the Ashlands. "Before the breaking. The Court's forges ran day and night. Now they bank the fires down to embers and call it tradition." His mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Everything in Vael is tradition now. It's what you call a thing when it stops working."

"You're in a mood."

"I'm always in a mood. You're only now close enough to hear it properly."

They climbed. The glass sang under their feet — a low harmonic hum, like wet crystal rimmed by a finger, and Sable realized after a while that it wasn't random. The road was telling them things. Old things. She caught fragments: a name repeated like a bell struck over and over; the word *oath*; the word *witness*. Hedge-craft had taught her that roads remembered, but her mistress's roads were dirt tracks and sheep paths, and their memories were of lost lambs and boundary disputes.

This road remembered a kingdom.

"Does it do this for everyone?" she asked.

"No one else walks it," Wren said. "That's rather the point of a court built on a volcano nobody can put out. Either you're invited or you're ash." He glanced sideways at her. "You're invited. Don't ask me how I know. Ask yourself why the road likes you."

She didn't have an answer, so she kept climbing.

The Court announced itself first as light — a glow above the ridge line, gold shot through with green, flickering like sunlight through leaves though there wasn't a living tree in sight. Then as heat, rolling down the slope in slow waves that smelled of iron and honey. And then, cresting the last rise, Sable saw it.

The Ember Court was not built. It was *grown* — or perhaps forged, though no hammer she could imagine made shapes like these. Towers of black glass spiraled upward and bloomed at their tops into petals of translucent amber, and inside each petal burned a flame the size of a house, steady and soundless. Bridges arced between towers in spans of woven fire, solid enough to walk on, casting no smoke. At the heart of it all rose the central spire, and around its crown coiled a serpent of pure ember, miles long if it was an inch, asleep with its head tucked beneath one burning wing.

"That's the Queen's," Wren said, following her gaze. "Or she's its. Nobody's sure anymore, including, I suspect, the Queen."

"And we just walk in?"

"We knock." He nodded toward the gates ahead — twin doors of dark bronze taller than the ferry landing's whole boathouse, worked with reliefs of hands passing fire hand to hand, a chain of givers stretching up beyond sight. "And then we bargain. And then, whatever happens in there—" he stopped, turned, and took her by both shoulders, which was more contact than he'd initiated since the wraiths, "—you listen more than you speak. The Ember Queen has been alive since before your valley had a name. She does not win arguments. She wins *people*. Understand?"

"Understood," Sable said, and meant it, and knew already that understanding a thing and surviving it were different crafts entirely.

* * *

The doors opened before Wren could knock.

Beyond them lay a hall of impossible proportions, lit by chandeliers of drifting flame that moved through the air like slow fish. Courtiers lined the walls — tall figures in robes of layered ash-silk, faces smooth and ageless, eyes banked low like covered coals. They did not whisper. They did not move. But Sable felt their attention settle on her the way heat settles on skin: total, patient, and slightly hungry.

At the far end, on a throne of cooled lava veined with live gold, sat the Ember Queen.

Sable had expected grandeur. What she got was smallness — a woman who might have been forty or four hundred, slight and dark-haired, dressed plainly in charcoal gray, with a single thread of gold at her throat. Her stillness was the stillness of the flames overhead: absolute, and only pretending to be calm.

"Sable Brightwater," the Queen said. Her voice carried without effort, soft as banked coals and just as capable of burning. "Daughter of Ashvale. Granddaughter of the woman who broke the world." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "Welcome. We have been expecting you for a hundred years."

"I'm twenty-two," Sable said. "So that's flattering and confusing."

Somewhere along the walls, one of the courtiers made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

The Queen rose. Up close, crossing the hall under those watching eyes, Sable saw that her plainness was itself a kind of armor — every other soul in the room wore wealth like a shout, and the Queen wore restraint like a blade. She stopped three paces away, close enough that Sable could feel the warmth coming off her, and studied Sable's face with the unhurried attention of someone reading a very old letter.

"You have your grandmother's chin," the Queen said. "And her stubbornness, I'm told. I knew her, you know. Before. She came here once, young and terrified, asking me to hide her people from the plague that was eating magic out of their blood. I offered her everything I had." Something moved behind the Queen's eyes — old grief, or old anger, worn smooth by a century of handling. "She chose differently. And now her granddaughter walks into my court asking for my keystone."

"Not asking," Sable said. "Asking is what I'll do if you say yes."

Wren made a tiny sound beside her, possibly a prayer.

The Queen tilted her head. "Then let us skip past the part where we pretend this is diplomacy. You want the second keystone. There are three ways this ends. You take it by force — you cannot. You take it by trickery — the Sunken Archive let you live because riddles are generous. My court is not generous." She lifted one hand, and a flame detached itself from the nearest chandelier and drifted down to hover between them, no bigger than a candle, burning white at its heart. "Or you take it as a gift. Freely given, freely bound. A bargain."

"A bargain," Sable repeated. Every story her mistress had ever told her about fae courts, hedge-law, and the price of pretty words stood up in the back of her mind and paid strict attention.

"A simple one." The Queen's voice gentled, and that gentleness was worse than any threat. "Look at Vael, Brightwater. Truly look. You've crossed the Ashlands. You've seen the wraiths. You know what we are now: a beautiful room with the windows painted shut. Every year the veil leaks less. Every year another wonder goes gray. My people are not dying of wounds. We are dying of *thinness*." She extended her hand, palm up, and the little flame settled onto it like a tame bird. "I can stop that. Not restore the covenant — that door closed a century ago, and the prophecy's answer is a corpse on an altar. But I can seal the borders. Fully. Permanently. Fold Vael into itself like a letter, self-contained, self-sustaining. No more leaking. No more fading. A preserved country, perfect and unchanging, forever."

The hall was silent except for the flames.

"And the cost?" Sable said quietly.

"The cost is that nothing changes. Ever again." The Queen said it simply, without shame, and somehow that was the most frightening thing yet. "No new magic crosses either border. No new life crosses. The wraiths remain, because the dead are already dead. My courtiers sleep and wake to the same century until the mountains themselves wear down. But they *wake*, Brightwater. They are warm, and fed, and whole, and safe. Is that not mercy? I am offering to save my entire realm the only way left to save it. All I require—"

"—is that the borders stay shut," Sable finished. "Forever. Including mine."

"Including yours." The Queen smiled, and it was a real smile, warm and sad and utterly immovable. "Ashvale keeps its safety. Your valley forgets magic ever touched it, and sleeps soundly behind its hedge. Vael keeps its life, frozen mid-breath instead of bleeding out. Two worlds, both saved, neither sacrificed. No altars. No deaths. No prophecies honored." She closed her fingers gently around the flame. "It is everything your grandmother wanted, done *properly*. Done completely. She sealed one side out of fear and half-measures, and it starved us slowly. I offer to seal both sides out of love, and end the starving. Tell me, daughter of Ashvale — when the choice is between a slow death and a long sleep, which is cruelty?"

* * *

They gave her a chamber while she considered it. Of course they did — silk and shadow and a window of smoked glass looking out over the frozen-fire gardens, and a tray of food that smelled like honey and hot bread and probably wasn't. Wren stood by the door with his arms crossed, saying nothing, which from Wren was a speech.

"You think I'm considering it," Sable said.

"I think anyone sane would consider it." He didn't look at her. His shoulder was bleeding through the bandage again; she could see the dark bloom spreading. "A hundred years of dying, ended tonight. One word. That's cheaper than anything else on the table."

"One word that costs everyone who comes after."

"There isn't anyone coming after!" He turned on her then, and his voice cracked on the last word, and Sable saw — really saw — the thing underneath all his cynicism, the thing he'd been carrying since the Archive: a man guarding a country the way you guard a bedside. "There's us, and there's the factions, and there's a prophecy that wants your blood, and there's a queen offering the only exit from a burning building. Take the exit, Sable."

"Preserved isn't saved." She stood up. "My mistress taught me preserving. Jam, Wren. You kill the fruit to keep the fruit. That's what she's offering — a jar with a lid on it. Forever is a long time to be jam."

"She'd say the same about a corpse on an altar."

"Yes. Which is why I'm going to find a third option, and if there isn't one, I'll build one out of the wreckage of the other two." She crossed to him. "Your family's in the Ashlands' memory somewhere. The wraiths. If she seals Vael, they stay wraiths forever. Unremembered, unreleased, forever. You told me that's the one thing you couldn't bear."

His jaw worked. "That's a dirty blow."

"It's the truest card I hold. I'm playing it."

For a long moment the only sound was the faint crackle of the garden fires below. Then Wren exhaled through his nose, something between surrender and respect. "The Ember Queen will have anticipated refusal. She's had a century to prepare for a Brightwater walking through her gates."

"Good," Sable said. "Because I've had two days to prepare for her, and I intend to cheat."

* * *

The throne room received her again at what passed for evening — the flames dimmed to a deeper gold, the courtiers rearranged like pieces on a board. The Queen waited alone at the foot of the dais this time, which Sable understood immediately as theater: the audience was the message.

"You've decided," the Queen said.

"I have a question first." Sable kept her voice level, her mistress's cadence — the one reserved for buyers who thought they smelled a desperate seller. "Your bargain. Sealing both realms. When you bind the covenant-keystones into the seal, what happens to them?"

"They become the seal. Their purpose fulfilled."

"Their purpose," Sable said, "is to anchor a covenant. A *living* agreement between worlds. If you repurpose them, they stop being keystones and start being locks. And locks, Majesty, don't care what they lock in." She stepped closer, into the warmth, refusing to flinch from it. "Here's my counter-offer. Not your bargain. Not the prophecy's altar. The third thing."

The Queen's expression didn't change, but the flames overhead all leaned slightly toward Sable, like a crowd leaning in.

"The covenant broke because my grandmother tried to save one world by cutting it off from the other. Half a seal. You're offering the same cut, doubled — both worlds sealed, both worlds safe, both worlds *dead*. I won't carry that. But I'll tell you what I will do." She held out her open hand, empty, palm up, the way the Queen had held the flame. "Reforge the covenant so magic flows *both* ways again — and make both realms share the burden. Ashvale carries Vael's fading. Vael carries Ashvale's fear. Equal weight, equal stake, no one sealed away, no one sacrificed. The keystones stay keystones. The wraiths get remembered and released. And your people don't freeze — they *thaw*."

Silence. Long enough that Sable heard her own heartbeat.

Then the Ember Queen laughed — a short, startled, genuinely human sound, gone almost before it arrived. "You would renegotiate a hundred-year-old apocalypse in my own hall."

"I'd renegotiate it anywhere. This hall has better lighting."

Something shifted in the courtiers' ranks — a rustle, a hardening, several pairs of coal-dark eyes fixing on Wren with sudden arithmetic. The Queen raised two fingers, and they stilled.

"You mistake my mercy for weakness," the Queen said softly, "and my patience for infinite. I have watched my realm fade for a century, child. I will not watch it fade for your *experiment*. Refuse my bargain a second time, and you leave this court as an enemy of the Ember Throne — and the keystone leaves with you nowhere near it." She descended one step, heat rolling off her in visible waves. "Choose."

And Sable Brightwater, twenty-two years old, granddaughter of the woman who broke the world, stood in a hall of frozen fire and discovered that she was, at bottom, her grandmother's granddaughter after all.

"No," she said.

The word hit the hall like a dropped bell.

The Queen's face went still in a way that made the stillness of the courtiers look like fidgeting. "Wren of the Ashvale Marches," she said, without turning her head. "Keeper of nothing, guardian of a dead covenant. Take your Brightwater and go. You have until the gates close behind you to be off my mountain. After that—" the temperature in the hall climbed five degrees, ten, the chandelier-flames shifting from gold to a hungry orange, "—everything on this mountain belongs to the fire. Including guests."

They went. Fast. Wren's hand hard around her wrist, the courtiers parting like a blade through water, the great bronze doors swinging shut behind them with a boom that rolled down the glass road like thunder.

They made it half a mile down the singing slope before the mountain answered.

Behind them, the towers of the Ember Court began to blaze — not the steady ceremonial gold but something wilder, brighter, and the sleeping serpent of ember around the central spire uncoiled, lifting its vast burning head against the darkening sky. And pouring down the slopes on every side, flowing over the slag hills like lava given legs, came the Queen's soldiers: figures of living flame, hundreds of them, converging on the road ahead and behind.

"Wren," Sable said. "Tell me you have a plan."

"I have a direction," Wren said grimly, hauling her off the road toward the black cliffs, "and a strong opinion about running."

Behind them, the gates of the Ember Court flared like an opened furnace — and in the heart of that blaze, small and distant and unmistakable, Sable saw the second keystone's light, locked away, burning on without them.
