## 11. Letters Home

The ferry landing had never looked so empty.

Tam Voss stood at the rail of his father's flat-bottomed boat and watched the water where Sable Brightwater had been standing three nights ago. The river moved the way it always moved — slow, brown-green, patient as an old dog — and it gave nothing back. No splash. No body. No girl.

The constable had written it down as drowning, because that was what constables wrote when a person vanished at a river in Ashvale. *Accident. Tragic. Young woman, nineteen.* Tam's father had squeezed his shoulder and said grief made rivers look guilty, and that time would sand the edges off it, the way time sanded everything in the valley.

But Tam kept seeing her face in the last second before she went. Not terror. That was the thing nobody else could understand, because he hadn't told anyone — couldn't tell anyone without sounding like a boy who'd lost his wits along with his almost-sweetheart. Her face hadn't been afraid. It had been *lit*. Like she'd finally read the last page of a book she'd waited her whole life to finish.

People didn't look like that when they drowned.

He poled the ferry across for the evening travelers — two weavers, a tinker with a squeaking cart, old Marta who paid in eggs — and every crossing he found himself watching the waterline where the reeds grew thickest, where Sable had stood with her lantern and said, *Wait, I want to show you something first.*

She'd shown him nothing. She'd gone into the water like the water had asked her to.

* * *

It was four days later, in her mistress's cottage on the hill, that he started finding things.

Hedge-witch Ivy Fenner had taken to her bed after the disappearance — not ill, exactly, but hollowed out, the way a house is hollowed when the fire goes cold. She let Tam in because he was the ferryman's son and because grief made strange allowances, and she set him to work sorting Sable's things, because she could not bear to do it herself. "Keep what's useful," she said from her chair by the dead hearth. "Burn what's private. A hedge-witch's notes are half medicine and half nonsense, and only she knew which was which."

So Tam sat at Sable's worktable, in her chair, surrounded by her clutter — jars of nettle tincture, bundles of dried yarrow hanging from the beams, a mortar stained green — and began going through her papers the way you'd go through a wound: carefully, flinching.

Her handwriting was quick and slanted, always leaning forward like it was in a hurry to get somewhere. Lists of herbs. Recipes for poultices. A page of sums balancing what she owed Ivy against what Ivy owed her, annotated with small exasperated marginalia — *she forgets again, third time this month, charge her in eggs.*

And then, folded into a ledger of tallow-and-soap accounts, a separate stack of pages. Thinner paper. Denser writing. He recognized it at once as different, because these pages weren't lists. They were arguments.

*—the book is not metaphorical. I have checked the hour against the almanac twice. If it is a forgery, it is a forgery that knows things about my family that no forger could know—*

Tam went very still.

He turned the page. Another. The handwriting was Sable's, but faster now, ragged at the downstrokes, the writing of someone chasing a thought downhill.

*—it names the ferry landing. It names the hour. It names HIM, or near enough — "the son of the crossing" — and I have not told anyone, not even Ivy, because what would she say? What would ANYONE say? That I found a hundred-year-old book describing my own date? They would take it away and put me to bed with willow bark and tell me I've been breathing too much mugwort fumes.*

*But here is what frightens me. Here is what I keep circling back to like a moth at glass:*

*Why does a book in Ashvale know about Vael at all?*

Tam read the word twice. *Vael.* He did not know it. He was certain no one in Ashvale knew it — and yet there it was, in Sable's hand, underlined hard enough to tear the paper, as if she'd needed to stab it to believe it.

There were more pages beneath. He spread them out across the worktable among the herb jars, and the shape of them assembled itself slowly, terribly: a girl doing what girls in Ashvale were taught never to do. Asking questions past the edge of the valley. Following a thread.

One page was copied out in a different hand — older, formal, the letters tall and narrow:

*The covenant stands upon three stones. When the stones are sundered, the door is shut, and what lies beyond shall fade, and what lies within shall forget. One of Brightwater blood shall stand at the crossing when the veil wears thin, and shall pass through, and shall mend the covenant — or end it.*

Below the transcription, Sable had written, in the margin, very small:

*Brightwater. Me. Why me? Why US?*

Tam sat back. The cottage creaked around him. Somewhere below, Ivy coughed, a dry sound like paper tearing.

He should stop. He knew he should stop. This was Sable's privacy, and she was — whatever she was — gone, and reading a dead-or-drowned girl's secret papers was the kind of thing that got a boy talked about in a valley where talk was the only weather.

He kept reading.

Because one line had snagged him, and would not let go. On the oldest page, at the bottom, pressed so hard the pen had nearly gone through:

*Ivy won't answer about Grandmother. Everyone won't answer about Grandmother. In Ashvale, "won't" and "can't" have always sounded suspiciously alike.*

* * *

He went home that night and slept badly, dreaming of brown-green water and a lit-up face, and woke before dawn with a single thought sitting on his chest like a cat: *Grandmother.*

Sable's grandmother, Maren Brightwater, had died six years ago, before Tam had ever worked up the nerve to speak to Sable at market. He knew her only as a name and a legend — the woman who had supposedly settled the Brightwater family in Ashvale a lifetime ago, who had been, according to Ivy, "a great one for rules." Rules about the valley. Rules about what came in and what went out. Ashvale was full of such rules; every child learned them with their letters. *Nothing leaves the valley. Nothing crosses the border. Don't ask why.*

Tam had spent nineteen years not asking why. It occurred to him, standing in the grey light of his attic room, that he had never once seen anyone else ask either. Not really. Not the way Sable's marginalia asked — furious, hungry, *why me, why us.*

The Brightwater cottage had stood empty since Maren died. Ivy used part of it for drying herbs, but the upper rooms were shut up, and everyone agreed they should stay shut up, and everyone agreed this without ever discussing it, which Tam was beginning to understand was how Ashvale did most of its thinking.

He told his father he was helping Ivy clear the property. His father, mending a net, grunted the grunt that meant *grief makes boys restless,* and let him go.

The cottage smelled of dust and dried lavender and something older underneath — a faint mineral smell, like the inside of a cave. Tam walked through the ground floor first, telling himself he was being respectful, methodical, just a boy doing a chore. Then he climbed the stairs to the shut rooms, and respect fell away behind him step by step.

Maren Brightwater's bedroom was spare as a cell. Bed, chest, washstand, window facing the river. But the chest —

The chest was locked, and the lock was old iron, and Tam stood over it for a long moment feeling like a thief. Then he remembered Sable's voice from the ferry landing, teasing him: *You're the honest one, Tam Voss. Someone has to be.* And he thought: *I'm not stealing. I'm finishing her errand. Somebody in this valley has to.*

The lock surrendered to a poker and stubbornness.

Inside: clothing, folded and faded. A christening gown. And beneath all of it, wrapped in oilcloth tied with wax-sealed cord, a bundle of letters.

Dozens of them. He carried them to the window and opened the first, and the handwriting hit him like cold water — because he'd seen it already, transcribed in Sable's notebook, tall and narrow and formal. Maren's hand. But these weren't prophecies. These were letters, addressed to no one, or to everyone, or perhaps to whoever finally came looking — and the first one began:

*If you are reading this, then the valley held, and I am dust, and the lie is still holding. Forgive me. Or don't. But hear it whole before you judge it.*

Tam's hands were shaking. He made himself sit on the floor, back against the bedframe, in the shaft of morning light from the window, and he read.

He read about a plague. Not a sickness of the body — a sickness of magic, a rot that had come out of the world beyond the veil a hundred years ago and begun to unmake whatever it touched. He read about a covenant, an old bargain between Ashvale and a realm called Vael, a two-way door that had kept both worlds fed and balanced since before the founders' grandparents were born. He read about fear — Maren's fear, specific and enormous, the fear of a young woman watching her neighbors' children come apart at the seams into light and ash.

And then he read about the choice.

*They offered me a way to seal the valley. Cut the door. Shut it forever, and the rot cannot cross, and Ashvale lives. But the door is not ours alone. Shutting it starves the other side. Shutting it kills a world to save a village. And they asked me to do it quietly, and to make sure no one after us ever learned the price, because a valley that remembers what it bought will never sleep well again.*

*So I took the truth and I buried it. I broke the covenant with my own hands, and I let them make me a founder, and I taught my daughter to teach her daughter that the borders are simply the way things are. That is my confession. I traded a world for a valley, and I would do it again, and that is the most terrible sentence I have ever written.*

*But I could not burn the truth entirely. Some coward corner of me kept these letters, and kept the keystones' hiding-places, in case — in case what? In case someone braver came after me. In case the door could be opened without letting the rot back through. I did not know. I still do not know. I only knew that a lie this heavy needs at least one witness, or it stops being a lie and becomes the world.*

Tam lowered the letter. The light had moved; he'd been reading for hours. His legs were numb and his eyes burned and somewhere outside a cart rattled past toward the ferry, and life in Ashvale went on exactly as it had gone on for a hundred years, built on top of a grave the size of a world.

*Sable,* he thought. *Oh, Sable. You didn't drown. You were called. Your own grandmother's sin rang the bell, and you answered.*

And she was alone on the other side of it. Alone in a realm full of — what? Rot? Wraiths? Whatever the plague had left? Alone with three broken stones and a prophecy that wanted something from her, and no one — no one at all — who even knew she existed.

His father's voice in his head: *Nothing crosses the border, Tam. Nothing can. That's the whole point of the valley.*

But Sable had crossed. Which meant the border wasn't absolute. Which meant it could be crossed — maybe only from one side, maybe only by her, maybe only once — but it *could be*, and everything in Ashvale's thousand polite rules was built on the claim that it couldn't.

Tam gathered the letters, wrapped them back in their oilcloth, and then — after a moment — did not retie the cord. He tucked the bundle inside his coat, against his chest, where he could feel it with every breath.

Downstairs, Ivy's herbs hung drying in the front room of the old cottage, and beside them, forgotten on a shelf, he found the last piece: a slim black book, spine cracked, cover stamped with a symbol like a stone split in three. Sable's prophecy book. The original. She must have hidden it here before the ferry — hidden it from Ivy, from the valley, from everyone — and it had sat waiting, patient as its own century.

He opened it to the final page. The erased name was still half-legible, ghosted in the vellum: *B——tw——r.*

"Not anymore," Tam said aloud, to the empty cottage, to the buried grandmother, to the whole conspiring quiet of Ashvale. "I remember. That's one."

He walked down to the river in the long gold light of evening and stood at the exact spot on the landing where she'd vanished, letters against his heart, and he did the most rebellious thing a boy in Ashvale could do.

He asked the water *why* — out loud, in a clear voice, where anyone might have heard him.

And far out past the reeds, where the river bent toward the hills and the hills hid the border, the water shivered — though there was no wind — and for half a heartbeat Tam would swear, all the rest of his life, that it shivered *toward him.* Like something on the other side had heard.

Like something on the other side was listening for exactly one voice, and had just found it.
