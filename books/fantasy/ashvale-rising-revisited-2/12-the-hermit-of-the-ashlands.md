## 12. The Hermit of the Ashlands

They walked for two days through a country that had forgotten how to be anything but ash.

Sable had imagined the Ashlands would be empty — a dead place, a blank page. She had been wrong. The dead were everywhere here, just not alive in any way she understood. Ash drifted on winds that never seemed to move it anywhere in particular, and in the drifts she saw shapes: a hand here, the curve of a shoulder there, half-buried like figures in snow. Once, passing a ridge of grey dunes, she heard singing — thin and wordless, like a lullaby hummed by someone who had forgotten the tune but not the tenderness.

Wren walked ahead of her with his wounded arm bound in strips of his own shirt, saying almost nothing. The wound from the Ember Court's guards had gone bad in the way wounds did in Vael — not festering red, but greying, the flesh around it turning the color of old paper. He refused to speak about it. He refused to speak about much of anything since they'd fled the court, and Sable had stopped trying to draw him out somewhere around the tenth hour of silence.

It was the wraiths that finally broke it.

They came at dusk on the second day — three of them, rising out of an ash-drift like smoke given skeletons, their faces smooth as river stones where faces should have been. Sable's hand went to the first keystone at her throat, warm even now, humming faintly like a struck bell that wouldn't quite stop.

But the wraiths didn't attack. They tilted their heads — all three at once, in eerie unison — and then turned and drifted away, back toward a crooked shape on the horizon that might have been a house if houses grew out of the ground like teeth.

"The hermit," Wren said, and his voice was rougher than she'd ever heard it. "They won't touch us while we're on his ground."

"You could have mentioned there was a hermit," Sable said. "Two days ago. When my feet still worked."

"He'd have mentioned you when he was ready." Wren paused at the edge of what was unmistakably, impossibly, a garden.

It shouldn't have existed. Nothing green should have existed within a hundred miles of this place. But there it was: a square of dark soil ringed with pale stones, growing things that Sable had no names for — silver-leafed vines, flowers like folded paper lanterns, a tree whose fruit glowed faintly from inside, like embers wrapped in skin. And among them moved the wraiths, dozens of them, drifting between the rows with something that looked horribly, heartbreakingly like care. One bent over a paper-lantern flower. Another traced its stone-smooth fingers along a vine, gentle as a mother checking a sleeping child.

"They're tending it," Sable whispered.

"Someone has to." A voice from behind the glowing tree. "The dead forget how to be gentle if no one shows them."

* * *

The hermit was not what she expected, though she couldn't have said what she'd expected. Old — older than old, the way mountains are old — with skin like grey bark and eyes the color of deep water. He wore robes stitched from what looked like pages, hundreds of them, layered and layered until the words had worn away to softness. He moved slowly, but the wraiths moved around him like water around a stone, and when he raised one hand they settled into the ash around the garden like birds coming to roost.

"Wren," he said, and something in the way he said it made Wren flinch. "You look terrible."

"I've been told it's becoming."

"And you've brought me a Brightwater." The hermit turned those deep-water eyes on Sable, and she felt seen in a way that was less like being looked at and more like being read. "I wondered which of you would come first. The factions, or the girl."

"You knew I was coming?" Sable said.

"I've known since before you were born, child. Sit down. You're swaying."

She hadn't realized she was. She sat on a bench of woven silver-vine, and the hermit poured something from a clay jug into three cups — tea, or Vael's version of it, tasting of smoke and honey and something underneath like rain on hot stone. Wren took his cup but didn't drink. The hermit noticed. The hermit seemed to notice everything.

"Your arm," he said.

"It's fine."

"It's ember-touched. Left untended it will go grey to the shoulder by week's end, and then we'll be having a different conversation entirely." He set down his cup, rose with the creak of an ancient door, and disappeared into the low doorway of his house. When he returned he carried a salve that smelled of crushed leaves and cold iron, and he knelt — knelt, without asking, this ancient man who commanded the dead — and began working it into Wren's wound with hands that trembled only slightly.

Wren sat rigid. "I don't need—"

"No one needs anything," the hermit said mildly. "That's rather the point of everything going wrong, isn't it? Hold still."

Sable watched the grey recede from the edges of the wound, slow as frost melting, and felt something loosen in her chest that she hadn't known was clenched. When it was done the hermit washed his hands in a basin, dried them on his page-robes, and finally sat across from her.

"Now," he said. "You want to know about the breaking of the covenant."

"How did you—"

"Everyone who comes here wants to know about the breaking. The wraiths bring them, eventually. The wraiths remember what everyone else has spent a century forgetting." He smiled, and it was a kind smile, and somehow that made it worse. "But you — you want to know something more specific. You want to know *who*. Don't you?"

The keystone at her throat pulsed once, warm against her skin.

"Yes," Sable said.

Wren shifted. "Maro—" he started, using the hermit's name for the first time, and there was warning in it.

"She deserves the truth," Maro said. "More than anyone in either world, she deserves it. Though I'll warn you both—" and here he looked at Wren too, "—the truth is not a comfortable thing. It never is. That's why people trade it away so often."

He was quiet for a moment. Around the garden, the wraiths had gone still, every one of them facing inward, toward the bench, as if they too were listening.

"A hundred years ago," Maro began, "Vael and your valley were one country with two skins. Magic crossed the veil like weather. Your hedge-witches drew on Vaeli currents; our healers learned from your herb-lore. Children on both sides grew up thinking the border was just a line on a map that nobody bothered to enforce. It was — forgive an old man his sentiment — it was beautiful. And then the plague came."

"Plague?" Sable said.

"A magical plague. We called it the *sundering cough*, because that's what it did — sundered things. A person caught it and their shadow left their body. Then their reflection. Then, piece by piece, everything about them that magic had ever touched. It didn't kill quickly. It unmade slowly, and it spread through magic itself — through every current, every charm, every crossing. Within a season it had taken a third of Vael. And it was crossing the veil. Your valley's first cases appeared in the spring."

Sable thought of her mistress's cottage, the cluttered shelves, the rules. *What may never leave the valley.* Rules that had always seemed arbitrary, suffocating, small. Rules with roots, suddenly, in blood.

"There was a cure," Maro said. "Or near enough — a warding so complete it could burn the plague out of a place entirely. But it required severance. Cutting the infected land off from the flow of magic altogether. Starving the sickness of the medium it traveled in." His deep-water eyes held hers. "Do you understand what I'm telling you, child? The ward that sealed your valley was not an act of cruelty. It was medicine. Brutal, desperate medicine."

"But it cut off Vael too," Sable said slowly. "If the covenant let magic flow both ways—"

"Then sealing one side dammed the other. Yes. Your valley survived. Vael began to die." He gestured, one slow hand, at the ashlands beyond the garden, at the drifting shapes of the unremembered dead. "Not all at once. Slowly. The way a fire dies when you close the flue. A century of it."

"Who did it?" Sable asked. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "Who cast the ward?"

Maro looked at her for a long moment. Behind him, the nearest wraith drifted closer, its smooth face catching the light of the ember-fruit.

"You already know," he said gently. "Some part of you has known since you read that book. Since you saw your own name half-erased in ink a century old. Ask it properly, child. Names have power, and this name has been buried long enough."

Sable's mouth was dry. The tea tasted like nothing now. She thought of the prophecy book, the careful handwriting, the way certain pages had been torn out. She thought of her grandmother — dead six years now, a woman she remembered mostly as lavender water and strong hands and a laugh like a hinge that needed oiling. A woman who had taught her to skip stones. A woman who had never once spoken of Vael.

"Elowen Brightwater," Sable said. "My grandmother broke the covenant."

"Not broke," Maro said. "*Made*. Made a new one, sealed with blood and keystones, that traded Vael's future for Ashvale's safety. She came here herself, do you know that? Crossed the veil at the ferry landing — the same landing, the same hour, nearly the same age as you are now. She came to beg us to accept the ward. To ask Vael to die slowly so her valley could live." His voice cracked, just slightly, on *live*. "And when we refused — when the covenant-keepers of that age told her that no single valley's safety justified a whole world's death — she took the keystones anyway. All three. She was stronger than anyone guessed. A hedge-witch, they said, unremarkable. But grief makes remarkable people of us all. The plague had reached her village by then. It had reached her *family*."

The garden was very quiet. Even the wind had stopped moving the ash.

"She sealed the borders from your side," Maro continued, "and the keystones scattered — some say she hid them, some say the breaking itself threw them, I've never learned which. And the people of Vael who died in the years after, the ones the fading took, the ones whose names were written in covenant-stone and erased when the covenant broke —" he spread his hands at the wraiths, at all of them, the whole silent listening crowd of the dead — "these are what remained. Unremembered. Unmourned in any way that reaches them. Because memory itself was part of the covenant, child. When it broke, the remembering broke with it. Your valley forgot Vael existed. And Vael forgot its own dead."

"That's why the book was hidden," Sable whispered. "Why the pages were torn out. Why my family never—"

"Your grandmother spent forty years making sure Ashvale forgot," Maro said. "Every letter burned. Every record altered. Every story reshaped. She called it protecting the valley. Perhaps it was. But protection and burial look very similar from the outside, and only one of them lets the buried rest."

Sable stood up. She didn't remember deciding to stand; her legs simply did it. The keystone at her throat was burning now, or felt like it, and her eyes were hot, and the garden tilted gently around her.

"She killed all of you," she said. "To save us. She killed a *world* to save one valley, and then she made everyone forget, and then she went home and smelled like lavender and taught me to skip stones, and nobody — nobody *told* me—"

"What would it have changed?" Maro asked softly. "A child cannot undo her grandmother's work. Knowing would only have made her carry it earlier."

"That's not yours to decide!"

"No," he agreed. "It wasn't hers to decide either. That's rather the tragedy of it." He rose, slowly, and stood before her, and up close she could see that his eyes weren't just deep-water colored — they had depth, layers, sediment, a century of looking at ash. "Listen to me, Sable Brightwater, because this is the last kindness I have to give. Your grandmother was not a monster. She was a woman in a plague year who chose her children over the world, and then spent the rest of her life paying for it in the only coin she had left: silence. You may hate her. You will probably hate her, for a while. But understand what you're inheriting. Not her guilt — guilt is not an heirloom. What you inherit is the *choice*. The same choice, come round again. Seal it or share it. Bury it or tell it. Every generation gets the choice fresh. That's the only mercy in any of this."

Sable's hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against her thighs and breathed, the way her mistress had taught her — in for four, hold for four, out for four — until the garden steadied.

"The keystones," she said at last. "I have one. Where are the others?"

Maro's expression flickered — something moving under it like a fish under ice.

"One you hold. One sits in the Ember Queen's vault, and you know the road to that bargain already, gods help you." He paused. "The third..."

"The third what?" Wren said sharply. Sable had almost forgotten he was there.

"The third keystone is not in a vault," Maro said. "It's not in a court or an archive or any place with walls. It's closer than that. It's been waiting a hundred years for a Brightwater to come and take it." He turned, slowly, and looked out at the garden — at the wraiths settled in the ash like a congregation at the end of a very long service. At the oldest one, the one that had drifted closest, whose smooth face was not quite as smooth as the others, whose outline suggested — Sable's stomach dropped — the suggestion of a stooped back, of long hands, of someone who had once been *somebody*.

"It's inside one of them," Sable said.

"Inside the oldest wraith in Vael," Maro confirmed. "My oldest friend, as it happens. The last covenant-keeper of the old order. The one who stood across from your grandmother at the ferry landing and refused her, and paid for the refusal with a century of walking these ashes." He met Sable's eyes. "She has waited all this time to give the keystone to one of Elowen's blood. But she will not hand it over freely, child. Taking it means hearing everything. The full confession. Every word of what happened at that landing, from the one who stood there. Your grandmother's own voice, insofar as the dead can keep such things. Are you prepared to carry that as well?"

Sable looked at the old wraith. The wraith looked back — or seemed to — with a face like a smoothed-over grave.

Behind her, she heard Wren exhale, long and slow, and when he spoke there was something in his voice she'd never heard before. Not cynicism. Not weariness.

Calculation.

"Sable," he said quietly. "Before you answer him — there's something you need to hear. About what the prophecy actually says. About what it costs."
