## 13. Loyalty Is a Kind of Lie

The fire in the hermit's hollow burned low and green, the way fires burned in Vael when they fed on old magic instead of wood. Wren watched it rather than watch her.

Sable Brightwater sat across the flames with the hermit's words still sitting on her like ash. *Your grandmother broke it. Your grandmother sealed your valley and let mine starve.* She hadn't cried. He'd expected crying — humans always cried at the edges of things — but she had just gone very still, the way a keystone goes still when you lift it from its setting, and then she'd said, quietly, "Then I'm the one who has to fix it."

He should have told her then. He should have said what he'd been carrying since the Ashlands, since the wraiths, since the wound in his side that the hermit's salves only slowed: that there was a way to fix it. That the prophecy had never been vague. That it named a price as plainly as it named a name.

Instead he'd watched her hands, small and human and stubborn, turning a twig over in the firelight, and he'd hated himself for what he was about to do.

*Later,* he told himself. *When we're moving. When she's not looking at you.*

But there was no later. The hermit had gone out to tend his wraiths, the green fire sank another finger's width, and Sable looked up at him with her grandmother's eyes — no, that wasn't fair, he didn't know her grandmother's eyes, he only knew what people said they'd been, bright and honest and full of a valley's worth of love — and asked, "What aren't you telling me?"

Wren had been keeper of keystones for sixty years. He knew every way a lock could lie. He had simply never been one before.

"The prophecy," he said. "The whole of it."

"I've read the whole of it."

"You've read the copy in the Sunken Archive. The one the archivists let travelers see." He turned his palm up, and the third shard of knowing — not a keystone, nothing so grand, just an old splinter of covenant-glass that keepers carried the way soldiers carried scars — caught the green light. "There's a second ending. It was cut away a long time ago because it frightened people. But glass remembers what it's shown."

Sable stood. "Show me."

*Don't,* said some small surviving decent part of him. *Find another road. There is always another road if you walk long enough.*

He pressed the shard flat against his tongue, the old way, and spoke the showing-words, and the fire between them went white.

---

The vision rose out of the flame like something drowning coming up for air.

Vael as it had been — green to every horizon, rivers with voices, cities built of grown stone where the Ember Court's towers now stood black. And threaded through all of it, the covenant itself: a great river of light running between two worlds, magic flowing both ways like breath, in and out, in and out.

Then the breaking. The wound. The river of light thinning to a stream, the stream to a thread, the thread to nothing — and Vael beginning, century by century, to forget how to be alive.

And then the second ending. The one the archivists had cut.

A figure standing at the altar-stone with three keystones in her arms. A Brightwater — the shape of her, the set of her shoulders, unmistakable even in firelight. The keystones going into their settings. The covenant reforged — but narrow, a door instead of a river, a door that opened exactly once and exactly one way. Magic pouring through it, all of it, everything the dying world had left, flowing into the human realm in a single great exhalation. Enough to buy Vael's ghost another hundred years of half-life. Enough to seal Ashvale against wonder forever, so that no one there would ever again have to know what had been traded for their safety.

And the figure at the altar-stone burning as the magic passed through her, because a door must be held open by something, and the prophecy had always known what that something would be.

*A Brightwater mends the breach. A Brightwater pays the toll. One life against a world's slow death.*

The vision folded down into the fire. The green came back.

Sable was looking at the flames where her own burning had just been, and her face did the still thing again, the keystone thing, and Wren understood with terrible clarity that he had made a mistake he could not take back.

"That's why the factions want me," she said. Her voice was level. That was worse than screaming. "That's why the Archive tested me politely and the Ember Queen offered me bargains. They don't need my cleverness. They need my body on the altar-stone."

"The Archive doesn't—"

"Everyone knows but me." She laughed once, short and awful. "Even you. *Especially* you. How long?"

"Sable—"

"*How long?*" The stillness cracked at last, and under it was not fear but fury, and Wren found, absurdly, that he respected it. "Since the wraiths? Since the Archive? Since the moment you fished me out of the shallows and heard my name and flinched?"

"Since the moment I heard your name," he said, because lying to her now would be one lie too many, and he was already going to burn for the ones he'd told. "A Brightwater. A hundred years after the breaking. I did the arithmetic before you'd finished coughing up river water."

"And you saved me anyway."

"I saved you because you were *there.*"

"No." She shook her head slowly. "You saved me because I was the answer. You just hadn't decided yet whether I was the answer to Vael or the answer to your guilt."

The green fire popped. Somewhere outside the hollow, one of the hermit's wraiths moaned its low unending note, the sound of a name no one remembered saying.

Wren got to his feet, and his wounded side tore at him, and he let the pain steady him the way he let everything steady him, because steadiness was the only inheritance his order had left him. "You want the truth? Here it is, plain as plain-speech. In ninety days, maybe a hundred, the last of the deep springs fails. I've watched three fail already. After that, Vael doesn't die — dying is too lively a word. Vael *fades.* Every wonder becomes a memory, every memory becomes a story, every story becomes nothing. The Ember Court freezes into pretty statues. The wraiths finally rest. My order's last duty completes itself, and the world ends not with a breaking but with a *forgetting.*"

"So the prophecy gives us a hundred years."

"The prophecy gives *Vael* a hundred years," he said. "And gives Ashvale forever. Your valley keeps its safety. Your valley never has to know. Everyone you love lives out their whole small lives behind a wall your blood paid for, and no one ever tells them, and the world gets a century of grace to put its affairs in order." He spread his hands. "It's not nothing, Sable. It's the only mercy anyone's offered either realm in a hundred years."

"It's not mercy." Her voice dropped low. "It's arithmetic. You're doing to me what my grandmother did to Vael. Trading one life for everyone else's comfort and calling it love."

"Yes," Wren said. "I am. Because it *works.*"

The word hung between them, ugly and honest.

She stared at him for a long moment. Then she said, very quietly, "Say the rest of it. Say what you actually believe."

"What I *believe*—" He stopped. Started again. "I believe I have buried forty-one keepers. I believe I have watched a world I loved go gray at the edges like a page left in the rain. I believe that when I look at you, I don't see a person. I see the first thing in sixty years that looks like a door, and I am so tired, Brightwater. I am tired all the way down to whatever's under the tired. If the price of one more century is one girl who was always going to be spent anyway — the prophecy says so, the glass says so, *your own grandmother's covenant* says so — then yes. Yes, I'll pay it. I'll carry you to the altar-stone myself if I have to, and I'll hate myself for a hundred years, and it will still be worth it."

The silence afterward had weight, like snow.

Sable picked up her pack. She moved around the hollow gathering her things with small precise motions, and Wren recognized the motions because he'd seen them on the dying — the tidying, the ordering, the making of the self ready to be left behind.

"You're wrong about one thing," she said at last.

"Which thing? I'm wrong about most of them, apparently."

"You think refusing the prophecy means choosing Vael's death." She slung the pack over her shoulder. "But my grandmother chose the prophecy's logic. Safety for her people, cost paid by someone who couldn't refuse. That's not the alternative to sacrifice, Wren. That *is* the sacrifice — just with someone else's blood. The only real refusal is to say the bargain itself is rotten. Both versions of it. Hers and yours."

"There is no third version."

"Then I'll write one." She looked at him across the green fire, and her eyes were wet and absolutely unbending. "You taught me the factions each hold a keystone because each wants the covenant remade their way. Fine. Then here's my faction: the one that doesn't get to decide alone anymore. Not Ashvale over Vael. Not Vael over Ashvale. Not a prophecy over a girl. *Both worlds, sharing the weight.* If magic comes back, it comes back to everyone, and everyone carries what it costs. That's the covenant I'll forge or none at all."

"And if it can't be done?" His voice came out rougher than he intended. "If the old covenant was shaped like a river and rivers need a source, and the source was always meant to be a life? What then, Brightwater? Good intentions and a loophole?"

"Then I'll find the loophole." A ghost of a smile, fierce and exhausted. "I out-hedged a drowned library. I'll out-hedge a dead world."

She walked to the mouth of the hollow, where the Ashlands stretched away under a sky the color of old tin, and paused there without turning around.

"When the Ember Queen's guard comes," she said, "you took a blade meant for me. Remember that. Whatever you decide I am to you — answer, burden, debt — remember that you bled for me before you ever told me the truth. That's the man I'm choosing to believe is still in there somewhere. Don't make me wrong."

Then she was gone into the gray, her footsteps quick and human and fading, and Wren stood alone in the green firelight with sixty years of duty and one new wound that had nothing to do with blades.

---

He followed her. Of course he followed her. That was the whole rotten architecture of him: he betrayed her in the morning and guarded her back by afternoon, and both things were sincere, and neither thing excused the other.

He kept to the ridgelines, where the ash lay thin and the old road-stones still remembered their purpose, and he told himself he was giving her space while keeping her in sight, which was the kind of sentence liars used to feel better about following people.

By late afternoon the sky changed.

Not darkened — *changed*, the way a bruise changes, color rising underneath. Far to the south, above the black towers of the Ember Court, a signal burned: a column of red fire, straight and deliberate, the court's oldest summons. Every faction in Vael read that column the way humans read smoke. *Come. Decide. Or be decided for.*

And below it, closer, much closer, moving along the valley floor toward the pass Sable would have to take — wraiths. Dozens of them. Not the scattered grieving few the hermit tended, but a host, drawn up and driven, herding like sheepdogs toward the one road she could walk.

They weren't hunting her. Wren had watched wraiths hunt, and this wasn't it. This was *steering.* Someone wanted the Brightwater girl exactly where the prophecy said a Brightwater had to stand — at the altar-stone, with the factions converging, with no road left but the one through the fire.

Someone wanted the second ending after all. And they wanted it soon.

Wren stood on the ridge with the red column burning in the south and the wraith-host flowing north, and did the arithmetic he was always doing, and for the first time in sixty years the sum terrified him.

Because if the factions were converging now — tonight, days early, all at once — then someone had told them something. Something that made a hundred years of patience suddenly unnecessary.

Something about a third keystone.

He turned toward the pass and ran, wound and all, running the way he hadn't run since he was young, shouting her name into the gray air ahead of the gray tide:

"SABLE! *They're not waiting for the prophecy anymore!*"
