## 14. The Second Keystone

The Ember Court kept its laws the way other courts kept jewels: displayed, polished, and never meant to be touched by common hands. I had learned that in the two days since we fled — learned it from a tinker-goblin named Brack who traded me a night's shelter and a bowl of something almost like stew for the story of how I'd insulted the queen to her face.

"You didn't insult her," Brack had said, picking his teeth with a sliver of ember-glass. "You *out-lawed* her. That's worse. That's why she's still talking about you."

"She's talking about me?"

"Oh, aye. The whole court talks about the human girl who read the bargain aloud before signing it." He'd grinned with too many teeth. "Nobody reads the bargain aloud. It's rude."

Rude. I turned that word over all through the sleepless dark of his wagon while the Ashlands hissed outside like a kettle never quite boiling, and somewhere around the coldest hour of the night, the word cracked open and showed me what was inside.

Bargain-law was the Ember Court's spine. Every title, every throne, every keystone-guardianship existed because some contract bound it so. The queen had offered me the second keystone in exchange for sealing Vael away forever, and when I refused, she'd declared me an enemy of the court — but she hadn't taken back the *offer*. You can't take back an offer. Not under bargain-law. An offer once spoken stands until answered, refused, or superseded.

And I had said no. But Wren had bled for that no, and he was feverish now in the wagon's corner, his wound wrapped in Brack's boiled linens, his gray eyes closed and his jaw set against the pain. He'd told me not to go back. He'd told me the Ashlands would kill us both if we lingered, that the court's hunters flew on wings of cinder and never lost a scent.

He hadn't told me what to do about the fact that Vael had maybe a season left unless someone grew a spine and a plan.

So I made one alone.

* * *

Brack drove me as far as the slag-flats at dawn, then wouldn't go further. "The Court's gates are flame-writ," he warned. "They know your face now."

"They know my face," I agreed, pulling my hood lower. "That's why I'm not going in through the gates."

The Ember Court rose out of the Ashlands like a bonfire that had decided to become architecture: towers of smoked glass, bridges of cooled lava-thread, everything the color of a hearth at midnight. But courts — I had learned this from the Sunken Archive, where I'd won the first keystone by knowing things my mistress taught me in a cluttered cottage that smelled of comfrey and dish soap — courts have backs. Courts have service ways, ash-chutes and coal-deliveries and the doors through which nobody important ever looks.

My mistress, old Maren the hedge-witch, had a saying for this too: *Every fine house is held up by plain work.* She'd meant it about scrubbing floors. It turned out it applied equally to infiltrating a fairy court.

I came in with the coal-carts, dressed in Brack's spare leathers, my face smeared with ash, hauling a barrow of black slag like I'd done it all my life. The gate-wardens — tall figures of living cinder with eyes like banked coals — barely glanced at me. Why would they? Nobody looks at the person carrying the coal. Maren's second rule: *Be the plain work, and the fine house forgets you're holding it up.*

Inside, the court was worse than I remembered, because this time I saw it clearly. Courtiers of glass and smoke drifted through halls lit by captive suns no bigger than apples. Everything gleamed. Everything was beautiful. And underneath the beauty, everywhere I looked, there was the faint gray dusting of ash — on sills, in corners, along the seams of things — the slow death of a realm starving without the covenant, settling like snow no one would sweep because sweeping would mean admitting it fell.

I hated them a little less for the dusting. I hated them a little more for pretending it wasn't there.

The vault, according to Brack — who knew things, the way all people who carry goods through back doors know things — sat beneath the queen's hall, behind a door of fused glass that answered only to royal blood or to a bargain properly struck.

I couldn't manage royal blood. So I'd come to strike a bargain.

Just not the kind they expected.

* * *

Getting down to the vault was the easy part; the coal-chutes went down as well as in. Getting past the door was where the night's real work began, because standing before it — of course — stood a herald.

It was small, that was the first mercy. A creature like a folded letter given legs, its body creased with lines of glowing script, its voice the sound of a page turning. It didn't ask my name. It asked only the question it had been made to ask, ten thousand years ago or ten thousand minutes, I couldn't tell:

"What bargain brings you to the Queen's vault?"

Here was where I should have died, probably. Here was where any sensible intruder lied, or ran, or drew steel. I put down my empty barrow, pulled back my hood, and let the little creature see my ash-smeared human face.

"I come to answer a bargain already offered," I said. "Spoken by Her Majesty's own mouth, in her own hall, three nights past. Under bargain-law, an offer stands until answered, refused, or superseded. I refused it. I've come to un-refuse it."

The herald's pages fluttered. Somewhere inside its folded body, text was rearranging itself frantically, checking itself against itself. "The offer of the Crown stands open," it recited at last. "The terms were thus: the Keystone of the Second Flame, in exchange for the sealing of Vael from all passage forevermore. Do you accept these terms?"

"No," I said.

The herald went very still. Pages rustled. "Then you—"

"Under bargain-law," I said, and here my heart hammered so hard I could feel it in my teeth, "an offer may be *answered*, not only accepted. Answered with amendment. Countered. That's in your law. I've read it."

Where? In the Sunken Archive, actually — in the hour after I won the first keystone, when the drowned library's ghost-light showed me shelves of mortal hedge-records nobody had consulted in a century, and I'd found the Ember Court's own codified law transcribed by some long-dead scholar with a wry hand and a footnote habit. *Article Nine,* the note read. *The Court believes its law is a wall. It is a door with very complicated hinges.*

"Amendment," the herald repeated slowly, its creases glowing brighter, computing. "State your counter-offer."

"The Keystone of the Second Flame," I said, "in exchange for the following: that I, Sable Brightwater, do swear before witnesses that I will deliver the keystone to the reforging of the covenant, and that the reforged covenant shall include a provision — binding, written into the covenant-stone itself — that Vael's borders may be sealed *by Vael's own choice*, freely, whenever its people vote it so. Sealed by consent, not by my sacrifice. Frozen if they choose to freeze. Open if they choose to open."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight.

"That is not what the Queen offered," the herald said.

"It's better than what the Queen offered," I said. "She offered to save Vael by locking it in a box. I'm offering Vael the key. Your queen gets what she actually wants — Vael preserved, sealed, safe — but only if Vael chooses it. Which means if Vael chooses to live instead, to take the risk of being part of both worlds, that's Vael's doing, not mine, not hers. Nobody's hands get dirty. Nobody dies. And the Court keeps its perfect record of bargains kept, because I *will* deliver the keystone to the covenant-site. Nothing in my oath says what happens there has to go the way anyone expects."

I was babbling. I made myself stop. Maren's third rule, scrawled on the inside of the pantry door in charcoal: *When you've said the true thing, shut your mouth and let it work.*

The herald unfolded slightly, like a letter being reread. Its script-lines flared gold, then white, then settled into a deep satisfied amber, the color of law fulfilled.

"Counter-offer received," it intoned. "Terms examined. The amendment preserves the Crown's interest, honors the form of the original bargain, and adds no obligation upon the Court beyond that which the Crown itself proposed. Under Article Nine, Article Twelve, and the Third Custom of the Founding Fire—" it paused, and I would have sworn the folded thing was enjoying itself— "*the offer stands amended and accepted.*"

Somewhere far above, a bell tolled once, and every courtier in every hall above us must have felt it in their glass bones: the Queen's own bargain, moved and shaken, without her hand anywhere near it.

"The vault," I said, when I could breathe again, "will open now?"

"The vault answers to bargains properly struck," said the herald, and stepped aside. "It does not answer to crowns. That is the point of vaults."

Behind the fused-glass door, locks turned themselves with sounds like snapping icicles. The door swung inward on a room the size of my mistress's pantry — that was all, a pantry — and there on a shelf of black iron, between a jar of imprisoned thunderstorms and a crown nobody wore anymore, sat the second keystone.

It was smaller than I'd imagined. A wedge of stone the size of my two fists, veined with light the color of dawn through fog — silver and green and one thin thread of gold, like a memory of the world before the ash. When I picked it up, it was warm, and it hummed against my palms, and I felt it recognize me the way the first one had: *Brightwater. Brightwater, again, at last.*

"I know," I murmured to it. "I'm late. We're all late. Let's go be less late."

* * *

I made it four steps out of the vault chamber before the Queen found me.

She didn't come with hunters, didn't come with fire. She simply arrived, the way heat arrives, suddenly present in the corridor ahead of me — tall, crowned in slow sparks, her gown a spill of embers that never quite touched the floor. Her eyes were the orange at the heart of a coal, and they fixed on me with something I hadn't expected.

Not rage. Recognition.

"You read the bargain aloud," she said. "In my hall. Before my court. And now you amend my offers in my own vaults, under my own law." She drifted closer, embers trailing. "Do you know how long it has been since anyone did either?"

"A century?" I guessed. "Since my grandmother—"

"My grandmother," the Queen corrected, softly, and the word landed in me like a stone dropped down a well. "Yes. Alva Brightwater stood where you stand, human, and she could not bring herself to read the terms aloud. That was her flaw, in the end. Not cruelty. Cowardice dressed as love. She took the easier bargain — the one spoken quickly, signed blind — and a hundred years of ash followed." The Queen tilted her head, sparks raining from her crown. "You are not your grandmother."

"No," I said. My voice didn't shake. I noticed that. It didn't shake. "I'm the one who reads it aloud."

Something moved across the Queen's burning face — grief, perhaps, or the closest thing fire has to grief. "Then take your stone and go, before I am forced to remember that I declared you an enemy. Bargain-law binds me to honor your counter-oath. It does not bind my courtiers, who are neither lawyers nor sensible." She turned away, and over her shoulder, quietly: "The third keystone is not in a vault, Brightwater. It walks. It remembers. And it has been waiting a hundred years for your name. When you find it — and you will — listen to all of it. Even the parts that will make you wish you hadn't read the bargain aloud."

Then she was gone, heat fading from the air like a hand withdrawn, and I stood alone in the amber-lit corridor with a keystone warm against my chest and the terrible gift of a warning from the last person I'd expected one from.

I ran. Through the coal-chutes, past the wardens who somehow didn't see me, out across the slag-flats where Brack's wagon waited in the failing light. I climbed up beside him, breathless, ash-faced, richer by one keystone and poorer by every certainty I'd walked in with.

"Well?" Brack said, flicking the reins. Behind us, the Ember Court glowered against the dark like a hearth banked for the night. "Did you die?"

"Worse," I said, and felt the keystone pulse against my ribs like a second heartbeat. "I won."

The wagon rolled north, into the Ashlands, toward where Wren lay fevered and waiting — toward the confession I now knew was coming, the truth the Queen herself had confirmed, walking toward me at the speed of a hundred patient years.

The third keystone waited inside a wraith who remembered my grandmother.

And I was going to have to listen to everything.
