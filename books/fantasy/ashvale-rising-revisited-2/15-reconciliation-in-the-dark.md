## 15. Reconciliation in the Dark

The wound in my side had stopped bleeding an hour ago, which I knew because I'd stopped caring whether it did.

I sat with my back against a slab of fallen masonry in the deep fold of the Ashlands where Sable and I had crawled to hide — some old watchtower, maybe, from before the breaking, its stones still holding a ghost of warmth that the ash around us had long surrendered. Above us the sky was the color of old pewter, and the ash fell soft and endless, sifting down through the dark like snow that had forgotten what it was supposed to cover.

Sable was ten feet away, sitting with her knees drawn up, her arms wrapped around them, her face turned toward the gray nothing. She hadn't spoken since we fled the Ember Court's hunters. Neither had I. The silence between us wasn't the comfortable kind. It was the kind that has weight, that presses on your chest like a hand.

I'd said things back on the ridge. Cruel things. True things, which is worse, because you can't take truth back and pretend it was only temper.

*Let the prophecy have you,* I'd told her. *One death buys Vael another hundred years. That's not cruelty, Brightwater. That's arithmetic.*

And she'd looked at me — this girl who'd been pulled out of her own world by the collar of her coat three days ago, who'd won a keystone from a drowned library and refused a queen to her face — and she'd said, quietly, in that stubborn hedge-witch voice of hers: *Then you don't want me, Wren. You want a name in a book. And I'm not going to be it.*

Then she'd walked into the Ashlands without waiting to see if I followed.

I followed. Of course I followed. My side screamed the whole way, and I followed anyway, because apparently I was the kind of coward who couldn't even commit to abandoning someone properly.

I pressed my palm against the bandage I'd tied in the dark. The Ember Queen's blade had been thin and elegant, like everything in her court, and it had gone in just under my ribs at an angle designed less to kill than to make a point. The point was made. I made a small sound despite myself, and Sable's head turned.

"You're bleeding again," she said. Not a question. Hedge-witches, I was learning, could hear a body lie.

"It's fine."

"Nothing in Vael is fine. That's rather the whole problem." She got up, crossed the ash without a sound — she'd learned that fast, faster than I liked — and knelt beside me before I could decide whether I wanted her to. "Let me see."

"I've kept myself alive for a hundred and forty years without—"

"Wren." She said my name the way she'd said it at the Sunken Archive, when I'd told her the riddles were impossible and she'd answered one anyway. Not angry. Just certain. "Let me see."

I let her see.

Her hands were cool and sure. She unwound the bandage, clicked her tongue at the state of it, and dug into the little satchel she'd carried across the veil — a hedge-witch's kit, jars and twists of cloth and dried things that smelled like her mistress's cottage must smell, like gardens and smoke and patience. She crushed something green between her fingers that had no business being green in the Ashlands, and the smell cut through the ash-stink like a knife through fog.

"That shouldn't work here," I said. "Magic doesn't hold in the Ashlands. Nothing holds here."

"Hedge-craft isn't magic." She packed the poultice against the wound, and I hissed, and she had the grace to look only slightly pleased about it. "It's memory. My mistress says magic is what the world does for you; craft is what you do for yourself. It travels anywhere because it doesn't ask permission." She tied off the bandage with a sharp tug. "There. It'll scar."

"Everything scars here."

"Yes," she said. "I'm starting to understand that."

She didn't move away. She settled back against the stone beside me, close enough that our shoulders almost touched, and for a while we watched the ash fall.

I should tell you what I was thinking, since this is my telling. I was thinking about my sister.

Not the wraiths. Not the covenant. Not the arithmetic of a hundred years bought with one life. My sister, whose name was Lark, who had been eleven years old when the covenant broke, who had loved the flooded meadows in spring and hated the taste of riverfish and once spent an entire summer trying to teach a marsh-heron to whistle. When the veil sealed, she had been on the wrong side of it — visiting our cousins in the low country near the crossing, the human side, the side that became Ashvale. She'd been there for two days. Two days. The border came down while she was picking berries, and the last anyone saw of her was a girl waving at the shimmer where her brother should have been able to walk through.

The founders of Ashvale called it a necessary sealing. A clean cut. Better a lost child than a dying valley, they said, though the valley they saved was theirs and the child was ours.

I was fourteen. I spent thirty years walking the length of the veil looking for a gap. There was never a gap. There was never going to be a gap. And somewhere in those thirty years the searching curdled into something else — into the sneer I wore like armor, into the keystone-keeper's oath I took mostly because it meant standing guard over the thing that had taken her, into the philosophy that if the world was going to be cruel then the smartest thing you could be was unimpressed by it.

Cynicism is fear wearing a clever coat. I'd known that for a century. Knowing it and admitting it are different animals entirely.

"You're doing the thing," Sable said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stare at the ash like it owes you money and decide not to say whatever you're actually thinking." She glanced sideways at me. "You've done it four times since the Court. I counted."

"You count everything."

"Everything worth counting." She waited. She was good at waiting. It was, I suspected, most of what hedge-craft actually was — knowing how long to leave a thing alone before you touched it.

The ash sifted down. Somewhere far off, a wraith moaned — one of the unremembered dead, drifting through the gray, carrying its stolen fragment of somebody's face. I thought about the hermit we'd met, tending them like a shepherd tends sick sheep. I thought about how he'd looked at Sable when he said the word *grandmother*, and how she'd stood there and taken it, and how she hadn't run, and how that had terrified me more than any blade.

Because if she could carry that truth, then I had no excuse left for mine.

"My sister was on the other side when it fell," I said.

Sable went very still.

"Lark. Her name was Lark. She was eleven." The words came out of me like splinters coming out of a wound — each one a small relief and a small fresh hurt. "She was picking berries two miles from where Ashvale's ferry landing is now. Our mother sent her to our cousins' for the harvest festival. Two days. The covenant broke on the second day, and the border came down between her and everyone who loved her, and nobody on your side ever even knew she existed. Your founders wrote their histories as if the low country had always been empty." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Clean cut. Necessary. That's what the records say. I've read them. I keep the archives; I've read every justification your founders ever wrote, and every single one of them adds up to the same sentence: *it wasn't our children.*"

Sable said nothing. Her hands had gone white around her knees.

"For thirty years I walked the veil looking for a way through. Then I stopped looking and started guarding, and I told myself guarding was a kind of loyalty. It wasn't. It was just a slower way of grieving." I turned and looked at her straight on, because if I was going to do this I was going to do all of it. "When I told you to let the prophecy take you — when I did the arithmetic at you on that ridge — that wasn't duty either. That was fear. Because if you refuse the prophecy, then there's no script anymore. No book telling us what happens next. Just two people with two keystones and no plan, trying to fix a hundred years of ruin with nothing but truth, and truth has never once, in the entire history of either of our worlds, been enough on its own. I'd rather lose you to a prophecy than lose you to hope, because a prophecy at least tells you when the funeral is."

The words hung in the ash-dark between us.

"There," I said. "That's the whole ugly ledger. Are you satisfied?"

Sable was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was rough.

"I had a grandmother," she said. "I thought I knew her. Apron, jam, stories about the valley. And it turns out she traded a world for a valley and let a child stand on the wrong side of the border while she did it." She swallowed. "Lark might have been real to her, Wren. She might have seen her. And signed anyway."

"Yes."

"And I still chose truth over the founders' version. Do you know why?" She turned to face me fully. "Because the moment I bury it, I become them. The moment I decide some grief doesn't count because it's inconvenient, I'm writing my own clean-cut history, and in a hundred years some kid of mine is going to find the ledger and hate me for it." She reached out and put her hand flat on the stone between us — not touching me, just offering the space. "So here's what I think. Prophecies are just lies with good posture. Sacrifice is just arithmetic that only counts one side. And I am done with both."

I looked at her hand on the stone. Small, chapped at the knuckles, stained faintly green from the poultice. The hand of someone who had crossed a world and refused a queen and heard the worst thing her family had ever done and was still, somehow, sitting here offering terms instead of curses.

"No prophecies," I said slowly.

"No prophecies."

"No sacrifices."

"Not yours, not mine, not anyone's. If the covenant can't be reforged without feeding it a person, then it doesn't deserve reforging." Her chin came up, that stubborn set I was beginning to dread and rely on in equal measure. "We do it with truth or we don't do it at all. We tell the factions what actually happened — all of it, the founders' sin, the wraiths' names, your sister, my grandmother, every buried piece — and we let them choke on it. And then we build something that counts *both* sides of the arithmetic. Magic flowing both ways. Burden shared. Nobody sealed, nobody starved, nobody sacrificed."

"It's never been tried."

"Neither has honesty. We're innovating."

A sound came out of me that I realized, after a moment, was laughter — rusty, cracked, half pain from my ribs, but laughter. First time in decades, probably. It felt like a door opening in a house I'd boarded shut.

"All right," I said. "New pact. No prophecies, no sacrifices, no scripts. Only truth — even when it's ugly, even when it costs us, especially when it would be easier to bury." I held out my hand, keystone-keeper's fashion, palm up, open. "Sworn in plain speech. No bargain-law, no loopholes, no fine print. The Ember Queen would be horrified."

"She'd be apoplectic." Sable took my hand. Her grip was stronger than it looked. "Sworn. Both sides of the border, both sides of the ledger."

We stayed like that a moment, hand to hand in the falling ash, and something in my chest that had been clenched for a hundred years loosened by maybe a finger's width. Not healed. Healing isn't a place you arrive; it's a direction you walk. But pointed the right way for the first time.

Then her grip tightened suddenly, and her head snapped up toward the ridge above our hollow.

"Wren," she breathed. "The ash."

I followed her eyes. The ash was falling wrong — swirling, parting, drawing back from the ridgeline in a slow spiral, the way water parts around a rising thing. And through the gap it left, silhouetted against the pewter sky, came lights. Dozens of them. Ember-orange, archive-blue, and a third color I had never seen in a century of keeping keys: a deep, patient green, moving down toward us from the heart of the Ashlands.

Every faction in Vael had found us. And they hadn't come to fight.

They'd come to watch.

"They know," Sable whispered. "Somehow they already know about the third keystone. They're all converging on the covenant site — and they expect us to be there."

I got to my feet, ribs screaming, and looked at the girl who had just sworn with me to burn every script in either world.

"Well," I said. "Hope was always going to be more dangerous than prophecy. Let's go prove it."
