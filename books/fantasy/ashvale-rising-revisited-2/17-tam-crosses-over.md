## 17. Tam Crosses Over

The letters smelled of lavender and lies.

Tam had read them eleven times now — twelve, if he counted the first night, when he'd sat up in the loft above the ferry house with a candle guttering and Sable's cramped handwriting swimming before his eyes until dawn. They were his grandmother's age, these letters, older than his father, folded into oilcloth and hidden behind a loose stone in the Brightwater cottage chimney where only someone who knew hedge-witches kept their secrets would ever think to look. Sable had known. She'd left him a note in her own hand, tucked into the prophecy book like a bookmark: *If I don't come back by the third moonrise, look behind the chimney stone. Then decide if you still want to find me.*

He had decided in about four seconds.

*The valley was dying,* wrote Maren Brightwater, Sable's grandmother, in a hand that started steady and grew shakier with every page. *The blight came out of the north woods riding on the magic itself — you could see it move, a gray tide through the wheat. The founders called a conclave. I was young then, and I had friends across the veil who told me what the Vaeli knew: that the plague could not cross a border sealed against magic entirely. Not slowed. Sealed.*

*They asked me to do it because I loved both worlds. They said only someone who grieved the cutting could make it clean.*

*They did not tell me what it would cost the other side. Or perhaps they did, and I did not want to hear. There is always a moment, at the making of any great mercy, when you must choose not to know something. I chose not to know. I have chosen not to know for sixty years, and the not-knowing has grown so heavy I can hardly carry my own water.*

The last letter was different. Shorter. Written in a hand gone thin as spider silk.

*If you are reading this, then someone has crossed over, and the debt is coming due. Do not let them pay it the way we paid it. We bought Ashvale's safety with a world's slow death and called it wisdom. Wisdom is just fear that's had time to put on nice clothes.*

*Tell the truth. Even if it costs the valley. Especially then.*

Tam folded the letters back into their oilcloth and pressed his forehead against the cold chimney stone.

Outside, the third moonrise had come and gone two nights ago. Sable hadn't come back.

* * *

The trouble with living in a valley that forgot was that forgetting was a kind of weather. It got into everything. Tam had spent the days since reading the letters watching it happen in real time — watching Old Marta at the mill squint at the ferry landing as though she couldn't quite remember why there was a landing there at all, when the river was barely deep enough to float a coracle. Watching his father polish the ferry lantern without knowing why the family had kept a lantern lit at a crossing no one used. Watching the whole valley lean away from its own edges like a man avoiding a bruise.

And watching the veil itself.

That was the thing nobody in Ashvale talked about, mostly because nobody in Ashvale could remember there was anything to talk about. But Tam had grown up ferryman's-son, and ferryman's sons learn water the way farmers' sons learn soil. The river at the landing had changed. It ran slower now, thicker, as though it were flowing through honey instead of water. And twice — twice — he had seen the surface go glass-still and show him something that wasn't a reflection. A sky the color of bruised plums. A tower made of woven light, leaning. A figure standing on the far bank of somewhere else, small and dark-haired, gone before he could blink.

Sable.

Or the memory of her. In Ashvale, memories were already starting to fray at the edges. He'd caught himself yesterday unable to recall the exact sound of her laugh, and the terror of that — the pure animal horror of it — had sent him running to the cottage to copy out every letter word for word, in his own hand, in ink, so that paper at least would remember even if the valley wouldn't.

Tonight he was going through.

He'd worked it out from the letters and the book together, cross-referencing Maren Brightwater's confessions against the prophecy's fevered margins. The covenant had been broken here, at this landing, on this stretch of river. Which meant the scar ran deepest here. And scars, his father had once said of the millpond ice, are where the surface is thinnest. You can fall through a scar in July.

The letters said more. Maren had written, in that last trembling hand: *The seal is a wound, and wounds ache in bad weather. When the veil strains — when someone crosses, or nearly does — the scar will open for a breath. What was shut from one side can be pushed from the other, if the pusher carries true weight. Not gold. Not blood. Truth. Truth is the only coin that spends on both sides of a border.*

Truth. He looked down at the satchel at his feet: the copied letters, the original pages, the prophecy book itself with Sable's note still marking the place, and — wrapped separately, because it deserved its own wrapping — the founders' ledger he'd pried out of the meetinghouse floor three nights ago while the elders slept. The real ledger, not the one displayed under glass. The one that recorded, in columns as neat as hymnbook verses, exactly what the valley had traded and exactly what it had agreed never to speak of again. Payments rendered to the Ember Court. Wards purchased with Vaeli lifeforce. A line item, entered annually for sixty years, labeled only *maintenance of silence.*

Truth enough to sink a boat. He hoped it was truth enough to open a world.

* * *

He went down to the landing at moonset, when the river ran darkest.

His father would have stopped him. His father would have locked him in the root cellar and posted Marta's boys at the door, if his father had known — but his father, like everyone, was forgetting, and each day the forgetting reached a little deeper. This morning he'd introduced Tam to a neighbor as *my boy — Tom, isn't it?* The valley was eating itself alive from the inside, name by name, and calling it peace.

The lantern hung on its post. Tam lit it, because some rituals mattered even when you didn't believe in them anymore, and stood at the edge of the water with the satchel strap gripped tight in one fist.

"Come on," he said aloud, to the river, to the scar, to whatever was listening. "You took her. You can damn well let me follow."

The water didn't answer. Of course it didn't. Water never answered; that was the whole trouble with water. But he'd watched it for two weeks now, and he'd noticed the pattern: the stillness came at intervals, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. Irregular. Weakening. He counted under his breath, the way he counted strokes when poling the ferry, and waited for the next beat.

A fish jumped downstream. An owl asked its question of the dark. Somewhere in the village a dog barked once and thought better of it.

Then the river went still.

Not calm — still. Every ripple died at once, all along the landing, as though the water had been turned to black glass, and in that glass Tam saw the plum-colored sky again, closer now, and the leaning tower, and — gods, yes — a smudge of green on a distant hillside that made his chest ache with how badly he wanted it to be real.

He stepped off the bank.

The truth-weight in the satchel seemed to grow heavier with every stride, as though the letters themselves were leaning into the work, and the water rose around his boots, his knees, his waist — cold, impossibly cold, cold like the space between stars — and the black glass surface closed over his head and he did not drown, because he was no longer in water at all. He was falling through a place that had no floor and no ceiling, where the lantern-light stretched into ribbons and the last thing he heard from the world behind him was the valley's long, forgetful silence.

And the first thing he heard from the world ahead was screaming.

* * *

He came through hard, face-first, onto ground that crunched like old bread.

For a long moment he simply lay there, breathing, cataloguing his body piece by piece — ribs intact, teeth accounted for, satchel still strapped across his back and bulging with its impossible cargo. Then he rolled over and looked up.

The sky was the color of bruised plums shot through with veins of silver. Two moons hung in it, one full and one cracked like dropped porcelain. The air tasted faintly of woodsmoke and something sweeter underneath, something like the smell of a book opened for the first time. And everywhere he looked, the land was — wrong. Beautiful and wrong. Trees with leaves like stained glass. Grass that whispered in a wind he couldn't feel. And threaded through it all, a grayness, a fading, as though the whole world were a painting someone had left out in the rain for a hundred years.

Ashvale had been the dry room. He understood that now, viscerally, the way you understand a thing with your skin. Everything here was dying of thirst, and his valley had drunk the well.

The crunching beneath him resolved, when he sat up, into ash. Gray ash, drifted in places like snow, and rising out of it —

Figures. Dozens of them. Wraith-thin, translucent, faces like smoke trying to remember having been faces. They were moving past him — through him, one of them passed straight through his shoulder and left a cold spot that ached like an old wound — streaming in a silent river toward a rise in the distance, where light flickered against the dark.

Toward the tower. The leaning tower of woven light he'd seen in the water-glass.

And around the tower, converging from three directions like the spokes of a wheel, came armies.

Even at this distance he could tell they were different from one another the way storms are different. To the west, a column of figures in ember-orange armor that glowed from within, marching in perfect ranks behind banners that burned without being consumed. From the south, something looser and wilder — riders on creatures that might have been deer once, before antlers became branches and eyes became lamps — carrying staves crowned with living green light, the only green Tam had seen in this whole faded world. And from the east, walking rather than riding, a procession robed in gray, hooded, bearing between them a palanquin on which something pale and veiled lay like a relic.

Three factions. Three keystones' worth of wanting, if Sable's notes had taught him anything, and they had. He'd read her marginalia so many times he sometimes dreamed in her handwriting.

*All three factions converge on the covenant site,* she'd written, copying from the prophecy's final pages. *When the last keystone comes to the altar-stone, the choice must be made. The book says blood. The book always says blood.*

Tam got to his feet. His legs shook. His hands, gripping the satchel strap, did not.

He had crossed a collapsing border between worlds carrying a bag full of other people's sins, and he had done it because a girl with ink-stained fingers had once told him, on this very landing, in another life, that she felt like a letter addressed to somewhere she'd never been. Well. He'd found the somewhere. Now he had to find her.

"You're a long way from your river, ferryman."

Tam spun. A man leaned against a shard of standing stone nearby — a man he might have missed entirely in the ash-light, so thoroughly did the grayness claim him. Young-ish and old at once, with a keeper's chain across his chest from which hung a single key-shaped pendant, and eyes that had the specific exhausted sharpness of someone who had been awake at their own funeral. One arm was bound in a sling of woven light, badly, the binding fraying at the edges.

"Wren," Tam said. It wasn't a guess. Sable had written the name in the margin of the prophecy book half a dozen times, underlined, once with a small exasperated asterisk. "You're Wren. Where is she?"

Something moved behind the man's eyes — surprise, quickly buried, and under it something rawer. "She talks about me?"

"She writes about you. Constantly. Mostly complaints." Tam shouldered the satchel higher. "Where. Is. She."

"At the altar-stone, presumably, doing something reckless and principled." Wren pushed off the standing stone and winced. "The Ember Queen wants the covenant frozen. The Verdant Court wants it reborn under their rule. The Pale Concord—" he nodded at the gray-robed procession "—want it remade as it was, which means remade with a sacrifice, which means—" He stopped. Looked at Tam properly for the first time. "You're from Ashvale. You're actually from Ashvale. How in the nine names did you get through the seal?"

"The letters." Tam swung the satchel off his shoulder and held it up. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, the truth of a hundred years pressed against the fabric like a living thing. "Her grandmother's letters. The founders' real ledger. Sixty years of payments and cover-ups, written down in ink by the people who made the bargain." He met Wren's eyes. "Maren Brightwater sealed your world to save mine. These say so. Every line of it, in her own hand, with her own shame. I'm told truth is the only coin that spends on both sides of a border."

Wren stared at the satchel the way a starving man stares at bread. Then he laughed — a short, disbelieving, slightly unhinged sound — and pressed his good hand over his face.

"A hundred years," he said through his fingers. "A hundred years we've fought over keystones and prophecies and whose version of the covenant gets carved into the stone, and the whole time the answer was *paperwork.*"

"Not paperwork." Tam looked past him, toward the tower, toward the flicker of light on the rise where three armies were closing like a fist. Somewhere in that convergence was a girl who belonged to two worlds and had been told by all of them that she belonged to none. "Proof. There's a difference. Paperwork gets filed. Proof gets *told.*"

Wren lowered his hand. For a moment the cynicism fell away entirely, and what was underneath was just a tired man looking at a stranger carrying the bones of his grief. "The wraiths," he said quietly. "The unremembered dead. They've been pulling toward the altar-stone all week — I thought it was the keystones drawing them. But it's you, isn't it? They can feel what you're carrying. They've been waiting a century for someone to bring the truth across." He turned and started up the slope, fast despite the bound arm. "Come on, ferryman. If she's about to do what I think she's about to do, she'll need all the stubborn people she can get."

Tam settled the satchel on his hip and followed.

Ahead, the three hosts converged on the tower of woven light, and above them the cracked moon hung like a broken seal waiting to be broken properly — and somewhere inside that knot of armies and ancient grievances, Sable Brightwater was standing at an altar-stone with the fate of two worlds in her ink-stained hands.

He intended to reach her before anyone asked her to die for it.

He was too late by thirty seconds.
