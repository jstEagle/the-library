## 20. Two Horizons

The first green thing in the Ashlands was a weed, and Wren loved it more than he had ever loved anything in Vael.

It came up through the ash-crusted ruin of an old waystation three days after the reforging — a single pale shoot, trembling in a wind that had carried nothing but cinders for a hundred years. He found it on his morning walk, which was itself a new habit; for a century there had been no reason to walk anywhere, because everywhere looked the same and everything looked dead. Now he knelt in the grit like a fool and put his face close to the thing, the way you'd lean toward a sleeping child to confirm the breath.

"About time," he told it.

The weed did not answer. Weeds rarely did. But down the slope of the valley, where the Ashlands began their long gray slide toward the covenant site, other shoots were pushing through — dozens now, then hundreds, then too many to count. The wraiths were gone, remembered and released, and whatever they had carried in their long captivity had come loose with them: seeds, spores, old green memory sunk deep in the poisoned soil, waiting a century for someone to say *you may grow again*.

Sable Brightwater had said it. Not with words. With terms.

Wren stood, brushed ash from his knees — real ash, but already going soft at the edges, already becoming something closer to soil — and looked out over the land that had been his whole world's slow dying. The horizon, which had always been a flat gray smear, had texture now. Color crept along it like ink soaking into cloth. Somewhere far off, past the drowned Archive and the cooling courts, the Ember Queen's territory still glowed its stubborn orange, but even that fire had changed register: less a fever, more a hearth.

He had been keeper of keys for sixty years. It occurred to him, walking back toward the settlement that was growing up around the covenant site — tents at first, then timber, then stone, because hope builds fast once you let it — that he had no idea what he was now.

The answer arrived, as answers tended to around Sable Brightwater, by way of an argument.

* * *

"You're doing it wrong," Sable said.

She was sitting cross-legged on the altar-stone, which the factions had finally agreed to stop fighting over now that none of them could claim it. She had a ledger open on her knees — an actual paper ledger, salvaged from the Sunken Archive and dried page by page over Wren's fire — and she was writing in it with the fierce, untidy hand of someone who had learned penmanship from a hedge-witch who believed rules were suggestions with good posture.

"The terms are the terms," Wren said. "Both realms carry the burden equally. Both realms share the flow. That's what you wrote into the covenant with your own stubborn blood. I was there."

"And what does 'carry the burden' mean, Wren? Say it plainly."

He hated when she said *plainly*. It was her hedge-craft voice, the one she'd used on the Ember Court, the one that had made a queen sign away a vault with a loophole written in words a child could read. Plain speech was dangerous. Plain speech was how a ferryman's daughter had unmade a century of careful ambiguity in a single afternoon.

"It means," he said slowly, "that when magic flows into Ashvale, Ashvale tends what grows from it. And when magic flows into Vael—"

"—Vael tends what grows from it. And when it crosses the border?" She tapped the ledger. "Because it will cross. It's crossing now. Look." She turned the page toward him. On it, in her cramped script, was a list. *Ferry landing: veil thin as gauze. Hedge-row on the Brightwater side blooming out of season. Tam reports lights in the orchard that aren't fireflies.* "Magic doesn't respect your neat categories. It never did. That's why the founders broke it in the first place — they were afraid of exactly this. Of not being able to say *this is ours* and *this is yours*."

"So what do you want? A third category?"

"I want stewards," Sable said. "On both sides. People whose job isn't guarding the border or hoarding the keys, but tending what comes through. Remembering what crosses. Writing it down." She looked up at him, and her eyes were the same eyes that had stared down ash-wraiths and bargain-law and a prophecy that wanted her dead, but there was something new in them now — something almost gentle. "You spent sixty years keeping keys, Wren. What were the keys for?"

"To open the covenant. To reforge it."

"No. Before that. Before the breaking. What were they for originally?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. He genuinely didn't know. The keystones had been old when his grandmother's grandmother held them; their purpose before the shattering was one of the things the breaking had eaten.

"Exactly," Sable said. "Nobody remembers. Because remembering was nobody's job. Everyone was so busy guarding and hoarding and fearing that the actual work — the tending, the recording, the *keeping* — fell out of the world." She snapped the ledger shut. "So I'm making it somebody's job. And since you're the only person in either realm who's spent a century being responsible for things he didn't choose—"

"That is the least flattering job offer I've ever received."

"—I'm offering it anyway. Keeper of Memory. Both horizons. You'd travel. You'd write things down. You'd settle disputes about what crossed the border and when, and you'd tell the truth about all of it, even the parts that make people — including me — look bad." She paused. "Especially those parts."

Wren looked at her for a long moment. Behind her, the Ashlands went on quietly refusing to be dead. A bird — an actual bird, brown and ordinary and impossible — landed on the altar-stone's edge and regarded them both with the frank insolence of creatures that have survived something.

"You realize," he said, "that a hundred years ago, a Brightwater broke the world to keep her valley safe. And now another Brightwater is rebuilding it and handing the pieces to a man she's known for a season."

"I realize," Sable said, "that my grandmother traded truth for safety, and it nearly killed two worlds. So I'm trading truth for everything else." She stood, brushing off her skirts, and for a moment she looked so much like the girl who'd been pulled screaming through the veil at the ferry landing that Wren's chest tightened. Then she grinned, and she was just Sable again, insufferable and alive. "Besides. You flinch every time someone says my family name. You might as well spend that flinching usefully."

* * *

The Ember Court sent its delegation on the fortieth day.

Wren met them at the boundary of the burned country, where the green was thickest and the Queen's pride would be most visibly wounded. There were six of them, ember-knights in armor like cooled lava, and at their center walked the Queen herself — smaller than Wren remembered, or perhaps the world had simply gotten bigger since the reforging. Her crown had been restrung with living vine, whether by choice or court fashion he couldn't tell, and he suspected she'd have the gardener flogged for it later.

"Keeper," she said. The title came out like a bone she had to swallow.

"Your Majesty."

"We have reviewed the terms." She produced a scroll sealed in wax the color of banked coals. "Our court honors them. Grudgingly. I want that word entered into the record — *grudgingly* — so that future generations understand this was done under protest."

"I'll enter it myself," Wren said. "In plain speech."

Her eyes narrowed to embers. "You've been spending too much time with the Brightwater girl."

"Yes," Wren said. "It's improved me immeasurably."

For a moment he thought she'd burn him where he stood, old law or no. But then her gaze went past him, to the hillside behind — to the green climbing the slopes of the Ashlands like a tide coming in, to the settlement's smoke rising thin and domestic against a sky that had color in it again. Something moved across her face that Wren, after sixty years of reading the guarded faces of the powerful, recognized despite himself.

Hunger. The hunger of someone watching other people eat.

"The Sunken Archive has reopened its upper shelves," she said, quieter now. "The drowned levels are draining. My scholars wish to study the old covenant texts. The *original* ones. Before the Brightwater woman rewrote them."

"They're available to any realm under the terms. Your scholars need only register with the Archive and declare what they intend to copy."

"Declare," the Queen repeated, tasting the word like spoiled wine. "In writing. Openly."

"In plain speech," Wren said, and could not keep a certain satisfaction out of it.

The Queen rolled up her scroll with a snap. As she turned to go, she paused, and without looking back said, in a voice pitched low enough that only he could hear:

"When the green reaches my borders, Keeper — and it will — my court will remember that it was a human child who forced this on us. Whatever comes of the sharing, the blame will be kept warm. Like a coal. Do you understand me?"

"Perfectly," Wren said. "And I'll write that down too. Word for word. So that when the blame finally goes cold, everyone will know exactly whose it was, and exactly why it was wrong."

The Queen's shoulders stiffened. Then, remarkably, she laughed — one short, crackling note, like a log splitting — and walked back into her burning country, and Wren stood in the young grass and felt, for the first time in sixty years, cautiously optimistic about the future of diplomacy.

* * *

Sable left on the sixty-first day.

Wren knew the date before she announced it, because she'd written it in the ledger weeks earlier, in plain speech, where anyone could read it: *Departure: when the ferry crossing holds steady for seven nights.* The veil at the old landing had been strengthening daily as the covenant settled into its new shape — no longer a wall, no longer a wound, but a door. A door needed both sides to hold. Ashvale's side had been holding for a month. Tam's letters — carried across by travelers bold enough to try the crossing, and there were more of them every week, traders and wanderers and one extremely confused Vaeli botanist who had come back raving about hedges — spoke of a valley learning to hum.

On the last evening, Wren found her at the landing, packing nothing, because she'd arrived with nothing and was leaving with less. Just the ledger, and the three keystones' empty settings worn on a cord at her throat — the stones themselves now set into the doorframe of the crossing, humming faintly, doing at last the work they'd been made for.

"You'll stay for the dawn," she said. Not a question.

"I'll stay for the dawn."

They sat on the ferry landing's broken edge, legs dangling over water that was no longer quite Vael's water nor quite Ashvale's, but something shared, like the light. Below them the current ran silver. Above, two moons rose — Vael's pale sister and, fainter, a wash of glow that Wren suspected was Ashvale's sky bleeding through, the first time either realm had ever seen the other's heavens.

"I used to think," Wren said, "that duty was carrying weight alone. That was the whole job. You took the keys because someone had to, and you didn't complain, and if it killed you, well, at least you died responsibly." He turned the new ledger over in his hands — his ledger, the first volume, already half full of crossings and disputes and the names of wraiths, all of them, every name he could recover. "Then a hedge-witch's apprentice fell through the sky and informed me that duty was actually paperwork and delegation."

"Flattery will get you everywhere."

"It wasn't flattery. It was a complaint." He looked at her. "You could have taken the prophecy's version. Die at the altar-stone, buy Vael a century, be a name in a book. It would have been easier. Gods know it would have been tidier."

"Tidiness," Sable said, "is what people call fear when they're trying to make it sound wise." She bumped her shoulder against his. "You taught me that. Mostly by being afraid at me until I got annoyed enough to be brave."

"I did no such thing."

"You absolutely did. Chapter and verse. It's all in the ledger."

They sat while the moons climbed. Somewhere behind them, in the settlement, someone was singing — a Vaeli harvest song that had not been sung in a hundred years, its words half-remembered, its tune patched with guesses. It was terrible. It was the best thing Wren had ever heard.

"When you get back," he said, "they'll want to make you something. A founder. A saint. The Girl Who Reforged the World. They'll build you a statue with a solemn face, and in fifty years nobody will remember that you bit a bargainer at the Ember Court, or that you cried for three days after the wraiths released, or that you talk in your sleep."

"I do not talk in my sleep."

"Wren the First, Keeper of Memory, records otherwise."

She laughed, and then she didn't, and then she was quiet for a while, looking at the water. When she spoke again her voice had the careful evenness of someone handling something fragile.

"My grandmother sealed the border because she loved her home too much to watch it suffer. That's all it was, in the end. Love, pointed the wrong way." She drew a breath. "I don't want to love anything that much ever again. Not one valley, not one realm, not one story about myself. I want—" She gestured at the two skies, the shared water, the terrible singing. "I want all of it. Even the parts that hurt. *Especially* the parts that hurt."

"Then you'll do fine," Wren said. "Hurting parts are the only ones worth keeping."

The dawn came up the way it now did in Vael — slowly, reluctantly, gorgeously, as though the sun were still getting used to being welcome. Light spilled across the Ashlands and lit the green all the way to the horizon, and the horizon itself, for the first time in Wren's long life, was not an ending. It was just the place where the eye gave out, and beyond it, presumably, more world.

Sable stood. Straightened her skirts. Tucked the ledger's twin — her copy, the Ashvale record — under her arm.

"Well," she said. "Keeper."

"Brightwater."

She hesitated at the top of the landing, silhouetted against the doubled sky, and for a moment Wren thought she might say something enormous and final. Instead she grinned at him, that insufferable grin, and said:

"Write to me. In plain speech. And Wren — if anyone in either realm tries to make a statue of me, I want it recorded, officially, that my nose is not that big."

"Noted," he said. "Witnessed. Entered into the permanent record."

She laughed, turned, and stepped through the veil — and the door took her gently, the way a threshold takes someone who belongs on both sides of it, and she was gone, and the water ran silver, and the singing from the settlement had found its verse again.

Wren sat alone on the landing for a long time, the first volume of the memory open on his knees, the second horizon brightening in the east. Then he dipped his pen, and beneath the record of the day's crossings he wrote, in plain speech, for whoever came after:

*Day sixty-one. The Brightwater went home. The door held. Green to the treeline, and past it. The Ember Court grumbles; the Archive dries; the dead are named. All debts entered, all debts open.*

*One item outstanding.*

*This morning, at first light, a traveler came through the crossing from the Ashvale side — a boy, ferryman's kin by his look, carrying letters and a face like a question. He asked for the Keeper of Memory by name. He said he had proof of what the founders did, and that some in his valley would very much prefer he hadn't.*

*I told him to wait for dawn.*

*Dawn is here. Time to see what truth the other horizon has sent me.*
