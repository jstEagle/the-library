## 1. The Quiet Trade

The fog came off the river before dawn and never quite left, so that Vell's Crossing lived its whole life inside a gray shawl. Isandro had learned to love the fog the way a fugitive loves anything that hides him: completely, and without ever letting himself admit it was love.

He set up his stall in the third row of the gray market, between a woman who sold eels by length and a man whose entire inventory was rusted nails sorted into jars by how much they'd failed. Third row was deliberate. First row caught the eye of the town guard on their morning circuit. Second row caught the eye of everyone else. Third row was where you went when what you sold required a customer to already know she wanted it.

His wares were laid out on a square of undyed wool: bone charms, small as knucklebones, each one drilled through with a leather cord. To any passing eye they were trinkets. Carved bits of sheep and deer bone, maybe a little rabbit, polished smooth, marked with patterns that might have been decoration or might have been letters in an alphabet no one taught anymore.

They were not trinkets.

Each one held an echo. A whisper. The way a river stone holds the cold of the winter it spent under ice. Isandro carved them from bones he bought honestly at the butcher's — that was the trick of it, the safety of it. He never touched human bone. Never would. What he could do was listen to what a creature had carried near the end: the sheep's last warm afternoon, the deer's long drink at a stream, the dumb animal peace of a body that had never once wondered what it was for. People bought them for comfort. A widow wore one against her breastbone and felt something like company. A frightened child slept with one under her pillow and dreamed of grass instead of the dark.

Faint echoes. Safe echoes. Nothing that could speak, nothing that could name itself, nothing that would make a witch-hunter's compass needle jump.

Seven years he'd kept the trade this small. Seven years since the kingdom erased its own name from every map and made magic a crime worse than murder, because at least murderers left the crown's silence intact. Isandro had been nineteen when the edicts came down, nineteen and carving charms in his mother's kitchen while she watched him with an expression he'd never understood until much later — grief and pride braided together so tight he couldn't tell which strand was which.

She'd told him then: *Small, Isandro. Keep everything small. A fire that fits in your hand can be hidden. A fire that fills a room gets seen.*

He hadn't known then what she meant by *fire*. He'd thought she was talking about his temper.

The market filled around him the way water fills a lock — slowly, then all at once. Eel-woman haggled. Nail-man arranged his failures. Somewhere in the second row a fiddler started up a tune about a girl who drowned herself for love, which was either very stupid or very brave programming, given the mood of the country these days. Nobody sang songs about drowning girls in the capital anymore. In Vell's Crossing the fog made people forget to be afraid until midday.

Isandro sold four charms before the bells rang terce. A drover bought one for his arthritic hip — echo of a horse, easy stride, nothing more. Two sisters bought matching ones for their mother's grave-visit, and Isandro lied smoothly that they were "just carved bone, ma'am, just pretty," while his thumb found the grain of the deer femur and coaxed the warmth up where the sister with chapped hands could feel it. Her face did something complicated. She paid without asking questions. That was the unspoken contract of the third row: I will not tell you what you're buying, and you will not make me say it out loud.

The fourth sale was a young man with ink-stained fingers who lingered too long over a charm holding the echo of a songbird. "It doesn't — talk?" he asked, too quietly, eyes flicking sideways.

"It's bone," Isandro said. "Bone doesn't talk."

"But it remembers."

Isandro looked at him for a long moment. Ink-stained fingers, a clerk or a scrivener, someone who copied documents for the magistrate's office and therefore someone Isandro should refuse on principle. Someone who knew exactly the right word to use.

"Everything remembers something," Isandro said. "That one remembers being warm."

The clerk bought it anyway. Isandro watched him walk away with his fist closed around it like a coal, and hated himself a little, and didn't stop selling.

That was the arithmetic of his life now, the sum he performed every morning and arrived at every night: small enough to hide, useful enough to matter, guilty enough to keep breathing. He amputated pieces of himself daily and called the bleeding *prudence*.

* * *

The girl came at midmorning, when the fog had burned down to rags and the light lay flat and honest across the market, showing everything for what it was.

She couldn't have been more than eight. Barefoot, though the season argued against it, hair the color of wet rope, standing at the edge of his stall with the particular stillness children have when they've rehearsed something. She'd been watching him for a while. He'd noticed her twenty minutes ago and pretended not to, the way he pretended not to notice most things that might require him to act.

"You're the charm man," she said.

"I'm a carver."

"Tam says you're the charm man." She jerked her chin toward the nail-seller, who suddenly became fascinated by a jar. Traitor, Isandro thought. "Tam says your bones remember things."

"Tam should mind his jars."

The girl set both hands flat on the edge of his stall. Her knuckles were white. Up close he could see she'd been crying recently and often — the skin around her eyes had that scrubbed-raw shine, and there was a bruise on her shin shaped like an adult's thumbprint, which told him something about why a child was out alone in a gray market barefoot in autumn.

"My brother died," she said. "In the millrace. Eleven days ago."

The fiddler in the second row changed tunes. Something slower. Of course.

"I'm sorry," Isandro said, and the words tasted like the ash they swept off hearths in Vell's Crossing, which was to say: familiar, and good for nothing.

"Tam says if a person drowns, sometimes their voice goes into the water bones. Fish bones. Pike teeth." She recited it like scripture, which meant some adult had said it carelessly within her hearing, and the words had rooted in her the way words do in children — fast, deep, indifferent to whether they're true. "Pike live in the millrace. Tam says—"

"Tam says a lot of things." Isandro heard his own voice come out flat and hard, harder than he intended, and saw her flinch, and hated himself for that too. He softened it. Barely. "Girl. What's your name?"

"Pell."

"Pell. Even if such a thing were possible — even if a pike tooth could hold a boy's last breath, which it cannot—" It could. That was the obscene thing. It could, if the drowning had been slow enough, if the fear had been loud enough, if the water had done what water does and pressed everything down into the small dense dark of the bone. He'd felt echoes like that once, years ago, before he'd sworn them off. They didn't sound like voices. They sounded like the moment before a scream, stretched out forever. "Even then, I don't sell those. I sell sheep bones. Warm afternoons. Grass. That's all."

"I have money." She dug in her dress pocket and brought up a fistful of coins — coppers, mostly, and two buttons, and a bird skull that made his stomach drop through the floor of the world. She'd already tried. She'd already gone to the millrace and picked through the shallows. "I saved it. It's real money, look."

"Pell."

"Please." And there it was, the word he'd been bracing against for eleven days' worth of other people's griefs, delivered at last by a barefoot eight-year-old with a dead brother and a pocket full of buttons. "Please. Mama just sits. She sits facing the wall and she doesn't turn around anymore, and Da drinks, and nobody says his name, we're not allowed to say his name, and I think — I think if I could just hear him, even a little, even just—"

"Pell." He made himself lean across the stall and put his hand over hers, over the coins and buttons and the little bird skull, and close her fingers shut. Her hands were freezing. Barefoot in autumn, freezing hands, and somewhere behind her a mother sat facing a wall. "Listen to me. There is nothing of your brother in these bones. Nothing. What I sell is animal warmth and nothing else, and anyone who tells you different is selling you a lie at a price you can't afford."

Her face did what faces do when hope is taken away slowly enough that they can watch it go. She didn't cry. That was somehow worse.

"You won't even try," she said.

"No."

"Why?"

Because the last time I listened to a drowning, I heard it for three years afterward. Because echoes of the violent dead aren't comfort, they're wounds that stay open. Because if I open that door in myself one inch, I don't know what comes through after. Because the witch-hunters burned a woman in Harrow Ford in spring for less than what you're asking me to do. Because I'm a coward.

"Because it isn't possible," he said, and used the voice he'd practiced for seven years, the one with no doors in it.

She took her hand back. Counted her coins back into her pocket, button by button, with the terrible competence of a poor child. Then she looked up at him, and her eyes were dry and old, and she said:

"You're scared."

The market noise went distant, the way it does when blood leaves your head. Isandro became aware of the fiddler, of the guard walking the first row with their halberds and their white sashes, of the fog burning off, of every ear within thirty feet.

"I'm a carver," he said. "Come back in spring. I'll carve you a lamb charm. Lambs are gentle. Good for sleeping."

Pell looked at him a moment longer. Then she turned and walked away through the crowd, small and straight-backed, and did not look back, and Isandro stood in his stall full of safe, faint, lying little comforts and felt the shape of the piece he'd just amputated, clean at the joint, still warm.

* * *

He didn't sell anything else that day. He packed early, wrapping the wool square around the remaining charms with hands that moved automatically while the rest of him stayed in the moment where a child had said *you're scared* in a voice with no anger in it at all, only recognition, the way you'd say *that's rain* or *that's night*.

He walked home along the river road because the millrace road went past the mill, and he could not have walked past the mill today. The river ran brown and quick with autumn rain. On the far bank, cattle stood in the mist like half-finished thoughts.

His mother had loved this river. That was another thing he hadn't understood until later — why a woman who grew up in the hill country, who talked about mountains the way other women talked about absent lovers, had settled in a river town and never once, in all his childhood, suggested they leave. *We stay where we're planted,* she'd said, whenever he'd asked. *Some doors only work from one side.* He'd thought that was just a saying. Mothers collected sayings the way magpies collected tin.

The cottage was cold when he let himself in. He built up the fire without lighting it properly — embers banked low, smoke drawn thin, the chimney trick his mother had taught him that made the house look abandoned from outside. He hung his coat. He set the satchel of unsold charms on the table.

Then he sat down across from it and put his head in his hands.

The trouble with surviving by amputation, he thought, is that eventually you run out of pieces. You start whole — a boy whittling in a kitchen, a mother singing, a gift he hadn't even known was a gift because it had always been there, the way sight is there, the way hunger is there. And then the edicts come, and you cut. You cut the big obvious things first: the human bone, the speaking spells, the names of the dead. Then the medium things: the honest answers, the public work, the friends who ask too directly. And you tell yourself the cuts are temporary, that you'll regrow, that this is just winter.

And then one day a barefoot girl asks you for the smallest, most human thing in the world, and you reach for the piece of yourself that could give it to her, and you find the socket empty, and scar tissue where the piece used to be, and you understand that winter was seven years long and counting, and that you are not waiting it out. You are becoming it.

He thought about Pell's mother sitting facing the wall. He thought about the bird skull in her pocket, already gathered, already hoped-for, already refused.

*I could find her tomorrow,* he thought. *I could take the pike teeth. I could listen.*

He knew what he'd hear. Not a voice. Not a message. Just the last eleven seconds of a nine-year-old boy — the cold shock, the tumble, the impossible weight of water — pressed into bone like flowers pressed in a book. And Pell would carry that. She'd wear it against her chest and sleep with it under her pillow, and instead of dreaming of grass she'd dream of drowning, every night, forever, because that was the truth about echoes that the gray market never learned: the dead don't speak through bone. They persist. And persistence is not the same as presence, and giving a grieving child a haunting is not the same as giving her her brother.

That was what he told himself. It was even true.

But underneath the truth, quieter, in the place where he kept the things he'd amputated: *and also, if I did it, someone might see, and someone might tell, and I might burn, and I have gotten so good at staying alive that I have forgotten how to be anything else.*

The fire ticked in the grate. Outside, the fog was coming back in off the river for the evening, drawing its gray shawl over Vell's Crossing, hiding everything for what it was.

Isandro lifted his head. His eyes fell on the north wall of the cottage — the wall his mother had always kept a chest against, the wall she'd once, laughing, told him never to pry into unless the world ended first — and he realized he was looking at it the way Pell had looked at him.

Like a person deciding whether to beg.

He got up, crossed the room, and put his palm flat against the plaster. Cold. Solid. Ordinary.

Behind him, in the satchel on the table, every charm he owned began — faintly, all at once, like birds going silent before a storm — to whisper.
