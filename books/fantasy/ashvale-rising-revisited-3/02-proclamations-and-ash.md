## 2. Proclamations and Ash

The edicts went up before dawn, pasted to every post and shutter in Vell's Crossing while the town still smelled of banked fires and wet wool. Isandro saw the first one on his way to market, a broadsheet of heavy cream paper that stood out against the gray plaster like a wound dressed in silk.

He stopped. He read it twice, because the first time his eyes slid off it the way eyes slide off things they don't want to be true.

**BY ORDER OF THE CROWN**, it began, in letters tall enough to read from horseback.

**LET IT BE KNOWN THROUGHOUT THE REALM THAT A GREAT WINNOWING IS DECLARED. ALL PERSONS POSSESSED OF UNLAWFUL ARTS SHALL PRESENT THEMSELVES AT ANY ROYAL GARRISON WITHIN FORTY DAYS TO REGISTER THEIR GIFTS AND RECEIVE THE CROWN'S MERCY.**

**THOSE WHO DO NOT REGISTER SHALL BE SOUGHT. THOSE WHO ARE FOUND SHALL BURN.**

**MERCY IS A DOOR. IT OPENS ONCE.**

Below the proclamation, in smaller script, was a list of what counted as an unlawful art. It was long. It included things Isandro had never thought of as magic at all—finding lost objects, calming colicky infants, knowing when rain was coming in your teeth. Someone in the capital had spent a great deal of time cataloguing every small human knack that couldn't be explained, and now all of it was heresy.

At the bottom, stamped in red wax the size of a fist: the seal of the Office of Inquisition.

Isandro read the whole thing a third time, slowly, the way you'd read a diagnosis. Then he looked up and down the street. The baker's boy was reading it too, lips moving. An old man with a hand cart had stopped beside him, and neither of them said anything. That was the thing about edicts—they didn't need anyone to read them aloud. Silence did the work. Silence was the crown's favorite instrument; it had been for seven years, ever since the country erased its own name from its own maps and pretended the silence was peace.

He walked on. He did not run. Running drew eyes.

* * *

The gray market of Vell's Crossing opened at the same hour it always did, in the crook of the old tannery wall where the watch didn't look too closely because the tolls were paid and the questions weren't asked. Isandro set out his cloth the way he always did: charms arranged in rows, each one tagged with a plain paper label bearing a lie. *For restful sleep. For safe travel. For a good harvest.*

All true, in their way. All lies, in the way that mattered.

What he actually sold were echoes. Bone carved thin and tuned right held the last sounds a person made—their voice, mostly, sometimes laughter, once a woman's off-key humming that had made a widower weep into his ale for an hour. Nothing powerful. Nothing that would make a charm-seller worth remembering. Seven years of practice had taught him exactly how much soul a piece of bone could carry before it started to glow in the wrong hands, and he kept every piece well under that line. He amputated his gift daily, cut it down to something sellable, something survivable, and every morning the stump of what he'd cut away ached like weather.

Business was slow and strange. People came to the market the way people come to a funeral—out of obligation, moving carefully, speaking low. A woman bought a sleep-charm without haggling. A drover asked twice whether the travel charms really worked and bought one anyway, which meant he suspected they did, which meant he was scared, which meant everyone was scared.

Then the child came.

She was maybe eight, wearing a coat two sizes too big with the sleeves rolled four times, and she stood at the edge of his cloth for a long moment before working up the courage to step inside it. She had the particular stillness of a child who has recently learned that adults can be dangerous.

"I want to talk to my brother," she said. "He drowned. In the millrace. In spring."

Isandro's hands went still over the charms.

"He was eleven," she said, as if this were evidence, as if age were a credential. "Mama says he's gone but I can *hear* him sometimes, under the floor, and Mama says I'm making it up but I'm not, I'm not, and Tam—" she caught herself on the name and her face crumpled and reassembled itself with terrifying speed, the way children's faces do, grief and composure sharing the same small muscles. "Tam always talked to me. Nobody talks to me now."

Behind her, the market moved on. Somewhere a man laughed at a joke. The tannery smell drifted, sour and organic.

"Child," Isandro said, and his voice came out rougher than he intended. He cleared his throat. "What's your name?"

"Pell."

"Pell. What you're asking for—it isn't a charm. Do you understand? What I sell is..." He gestured at the rows of bone, the neat little lies on their labels. "Echoes. Like a voice heard through a wall. Your brother isn't behind that wall anymore. And even if he were, even if I could give you his voice—" he leaned forward, lowered his tone so only she could hear, "—it would be dangerous. For you. For your mother. For me. There are men whose whole work is finding people who ask for things like this."

"They're going to burn people," Pell said. Not a question. She'd read the edict, or someone had read it to her. Eight years old and she already knew the shape of the world she lived in. "My aunt says the gifted should just register. Take the mercy."

"The mercy is a cage," Isandro said, then wished he hadn't. You didn't say things like that to eight-year-olds. You didn't say things like that anywhere, really, but especially not to eight-year-olds who might repeat them at supper.

Pell looked at him with eyes too old for her face. "You're one of them," she said. "The gifted. Aren't you."

The market noise seemed to recede. Isandro became very aware of the satchel at his hip, the weight of it, the faint chorus it carried—two dozen small dead voices, murmuring their two-inch-deep echoes.

"I'm a charm-seller," he said. "That's all."

She didn't believe him. Children never believed him, which was the worst part, because it meant he was bad at hiding, or because children could see the truth through the seams in a lie the way light comes through a badly hung door. She put a copper on the cloth—where she'd gotten it he didn't know—and took a sleep-charm at random, and walked away with her sleeves flapping, and Isandro sat there hating himself with a thoroughness that felt almost professional.

He could have done it. That was the thing that would keep him up tonight. He could have taken her somewhere quiet, held a piece of river-bone, and let Tam's last sounds move through it. Ten minutes of work. Ten minutes of comfort that would cost him nothing but risk, and risk was the currency he'd been hoarding for seven years, spending none of it, dying rich in safety and poor in everything else.

*Refused a child her dead brother,* he thought. *There's the epitaph.*

* * *

It happened at noon.

He heard it before he understood it—a change in the market's texture, a ripple of heads turning toward the square, the particular hush that precedes either a procession or a hanging. Then the bells began, the garrison bells, three strokes and a pause, three strokes and a pause, and Isandro's stomach dropped through the floor of the world because he knew that pattern. Everyone knew that pattern. Three-and-pause meant a burning.

He told himself to pack up. He told himself to go home, bar the door, be nobody. Instead his feet carried him toward the square with the rest of the crowd, because that was the other thing about fear—it made you want to look. Some animal part of you needed to see the shape of the thing hunting you.

They'd built the pyre in front of the assize hall, fresh timber stacked knee-high around an iron stake, and the crowd formed a ring around it the way water forms around a stone. Isandro slipped into the edge of it, hood up, heart going like a trapped bird.

The hedge-witch was dragged out at half past noon.

He knew her. That was the detail that would live in him longest—not the fire, not the screams, but the fact that he knew her. Wren. Old Wren from the fen road, who sold poultices and pregnancy charms and knew every herb in the county by its folk-name. Who had once, three winters ago, traded him a jar of feverfew for a charm that let her late husband's voice say goodnight to her, and had cried and thanked him and never told a soul. Who was seventy if she was a day and walked with a stick and had never harmed anyone in her life except by knowing too much about which weeds cured which hurts.

Her hands were bound. Her stick was gone. Two witch-hunters in gray-and-crimson held her arms, and a third walked ahead carrying the iron collar on a pole like a lantern, and the crowd watched with that terrible passive hunger crowds have, faces doing nothing, doing everything.

Wren wasn't screaming yet. She was talking. Pleading, at first—*I registered, I registered years ago, check the rolls, I paid my fee*—and then, as they reached the stake, something else. Something that turned Isandro's blood to ice water.

"You know me," she said to the crowd, her voice cracking across the square. "You've bought my salves. Your children—I've delivered half the babies in this valley. Look at me. *Look at me.*"

People looked. People always look. Looking costs nothing.

A clerk in Office colors mounted the assize steps and unrolled a scroll and read the charges in a bored administrative voice: unlawful arts, failure to renew registration, possession of proscribed materials. The materials were displayed in a basin—dried herbs, a knotted cord, a clay whistle. A clay whistle. Isandro stared at it and felt the ground tilt. He owned six bone whistles in his satchel that could do ten times what that clay trinket could.

Wren was lashed to the stake. The iron collar went on. And then the magistrate, a soft man with a soft voice, stepped forward and spoke the words of abjuration, offering her one last time the mercy of the register—a mercy that would have meant the collar anyway, the collar and the draining, the slow leeching of everything she was until what remained could sit in a pew and nod along.

"No," Wren said.

The magistrate blinked. "Woman, consider—"

"I said no." And here her voice changed, steadied, rose into something that filled the square, and Isandro understood that old Wren had decided how she would die and had chosen the version with her spine intact. "I delivered your children. I healed your fevers. I kept your cows calving and your beer from souring, and this is my wage. Fine. But hear me—" and she lifted her bound hands as far as the ropes allowed, addressing the whole square, the whole gray sky, "—you can burn the keepers. The keeping goes on. Fire doesn't end a craft. It only drives it underground, where it grows *teeth*."

The torch went down into the kindling.

And Wren screamed.

Not at first. First there was smoke, and coughing, and the horrible ordinary crackle of timber. The crowd pressed closer despite itself. Isandro stood frozen, hood shadowing his face, telling himself *look away, look away,* and being unable to.

Then the flames found her properly, and she screamed, and that was when the impossible thing happened.

Every charm in Isandro's satchel went silent at once.

He felt it like a blow. Twenty-odd small voices, murmuring their murmur all day every day for years—a sound so constant he'd stopped hearing it, the way you stop hearing a river—cut off between one heartbeat and the next. Total silence against his hip. As if the dead themselves had turned their faces away. As if whatever Wren was, whatever poured out of her in those last moments, was loud enough in the world-under-the-world to drown every echo in a quarter mile.

Or as if the dead had gone to stand witness.

He didn't know which. He would never know which. He stood in a crowd of several hundred people, most of whom felt nothing, some of whom felt a chill they'd blame on the wind, and one of whom—he could see her, a girl down the lane with her hand pressed flat to her breastbone, eyes wide, another one, there was always another one—who felt exactly what he felt, and both of them stood silent among the silent, and no one said a word, because saying a word was death.

Wren died in nine minutes. He counted. Counting was the only prayer he had left.

Afterward the crowd dispersed with the awful ordinariness of crowds after executions—shuffling, murmuring about the weather, about the price of flour, about anything else. The ash men came with their shovels and buckets. The clerk rolled up his scroll. A dog licked spilled fat off the cobbles until someone kicked it away.

Isandro walked home the long way, because the short way passed the fen road, and he could not bear to walk past the turning that led to Wren's cottage, where her kettle was probably still warm.

* * *

His room above the chandler's shop was exactly as he'd left it, which was to say: a lie. A life arranged to look temporary, a man arranged to look harmless. He barred the door and stood in the middle of the floor with his satchel clutched to his chest like a child's toy, and the silence inside it roared.

They'd known about Wren. That was the part his mind kept snagging on. *Failure to renew registration.* They'd had her name in a book for years. Which meant they had other names in other books. Which meant somewhere in a garrison office there was a ledger, and ledgers had columns, and one of those columns said *charm-sellers, Vell's Crossing,* and how many names were in it? One? Three?

How long had he been selling echoes to frightened people in a gray market while the crown wrote his name in a book?

He unpacked the satchel onto the bed with shaking hands. The charms lay there in their rows, silent, and he picked up the oldest one—river-bone, his first real piece, carved seven years ago the week after the erasure, holding the voice of a ferryman who'd sung as the water took him. He held it to his ear. Nothing. He shook it. Nothing. Whatever had silenced them at the square, it hadn't come back.

*The keeping goes on,* Wren had said. *It only grows teeth.*

"Teeth," Isandro said aloud, to the empty room. His voice sounded strange. It had been days since he'd spoken above a whisper.

He worked until dark, and what he did was this: he destroyed his stock. Every charm, every echo, every scrap of carved bone in his possession, snapped and burned in the chandler's own furnace downstairs, fed piece by piece into the coals while the little voices went to their second deaths—one by one by one, the ferryman's song, the widow's goodnight, the off-key humming, all of it, twenty years of careful small work, ash. He wept while he did it, silently, furiously, hating himself for weeping, hating the crown for making tears feel like surrender, hating most of all the small cowardly arithmetic in his head that kept whispering *this is practical, this is necessary, this is survival* while his hands shook.

When it was done he sat on the floor with his back against the cold furnace and let the silence have him.

Forty days. The edict gave the gifted forty days to walk into a garrison and sign themselves into a collar. Forty days, and then the seeking would begin in earnest, house to house, market to market, ledger to ledger. And he had just burned the only proof of his trade—but ledgers didn't need proof. Ledgers needed names, and informers, and a bounty, and Vell's Crossing was full of all three.

He thought about registering. He actually thought about it, lying there in the ash-smelling dark, and hated himself for thinking it. Register, take the collar, let them drain the gift out of him drop by drop until he was a hollow man who sold honest whittling at honest markets. Live. Wasn't living the point? Wasn't survival the whole religion he'd been practicing for seven years?

But he'd seen the collared ones. Everyone had. They swept the church steps on market day, gray-faced and placid, and when you spoke to them they smiled a smile with nothing behind it, and their eyes were windows with the shutters painted shut. That wasn't mercy. That was a door that opened once, all right—onto a cell.

And beneath the fear, beneath the arithmetic, something else had been growing since noon, since Wren's voice cracked across the square: *fire doesn't end a craft, it only drives it underground, where it grows teeth.*

He had teeth. He'd been filing them down for seven years, pretending they were gone, selling them off a cloth in a gray market a whisper at a time. But they were there. And there was a letter-shaped hole in his understanding of his own mother—a hole shaped like everything she'd hidden from him, in the walls of the cottage he'd inherited and never fully searched, because searching meant hoping, and hoping was the expense he'd never dared.

*If she hid anything, it's still there. If she was what I think she might have been—*

He got up. He packed the way you pack when you mean it: fast, ruthless, leaving the life behind like a shed skin. Carving tools. Blank bone. The clothes on his back and one spare shirt. His mother's old knife, the one with the worn horn handle, which he'd carried for seven years without knowing why.

One hour past midnight, Isandro Iversen climbed out the window of his room above the chandler's shop and dropped into the alley, and the alley took him, and Vell's Crossing slept on around him, dreaming its obedient dreams.

He went east, toward the hills, toward the cottage he'd sworn never to return to. Behind him the town lay dark and silent, holding its dead and its registered and its unregistered alike in one blind embrace, and Isandro did not look back, because looking back was a luxury, and because if he had looked back—if he'd let himself think of Pell with her rolled-up sleeves, of the girl with her hand on her breastbone, of every quiet neighbor who'd buy a charm tomorrow from someone braver or more foolish than him—he might have stopped.

He owed them. He knew that. He carried the debt east with him like a stone in his boot, and he told himself the oldest lie survivors tell: *when I'm safe, I'll come back for you.*

The hills swallowed him by dawn.
