## 4. The Inquisitor with My Father's Face

The rumor reached me the way most things did in Vell's Crossing—half-drunk, overpriced, and whispered sideways.

"Riders on the south road," said Tam Vetch, sliding onto the bench across from me at the Drowned Gull with the particular slouch of a man who wanted to look casual and was failing badly. "Six of them. Black cloaks. Crown colors under the ash."

I kept my hands still around my cup. "Hunters pass through all the time."

"Not like this one." Tam leaned in, breath sour with cheap ale. "They're asking names. Not just *any* gifted, mind—they've got a list. And the man leading them—" he hesitated, and I watched him decide whether to say it, "—the man leading them is asking after a carver. A bone-carver who sells in the gray market. That's you, Isandro."

My cup was clay, unglazed, warm from the hearth. I studied it as if it might tell me something useful.

"They describe him?" I asked.

Tam's voice dropped to almost nothing. "Tall. Gray at the temples. A scar through the left eyebrow." He swallowed. "Isandro. The way they said it—the scar goes left to right, hooked at the end. Like a fishhook."

The hearth popped. Somewhere behind me a dice game broke into argument and dissolved into laughter, ordinary sounds from an ordinary evening, and none of it touched me, because I was seven years old again sitting on my father's knee with his calloused thumb tracing the line of that scar while he told me how he'd earned it falling out of Aldous Pine's apple tree, laughing, always laughing—

"That's not possible," I said.

"No," Tam agreed. "Course it isn't." He drained his cup and stood, not meeting my eyes. "But you should know they set up in the old customs house. And they're paying silver for names."

He left me alone with my father's face waiting for me three streets away.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat in my rented room above the chandler's shop with every charm I owned laid out on the floorboards, sorting them the way a man sorts his life when he expects to lose it: what could be burned, what could be buried, what could never be allowed into a hunter's hands.

Most of my stock was harmless. Whisper-charms, I called them—cow bone and river stone, carrying nothing but the faintest residue of the dead. A grandmother's hum. A sailor's two-note whistle. Echoes so thin that even a sensitive inquisitor would need to press the bone to his ear and *want* to hear something before anything answered. Seven years of careful, deliberate smallness. Never enough power to draw notice. Never enough to matter.

At the bottom of the satchel, wrapped in oilcloth, were the four pieces I had never sold and never would. My mother's work. Whatever lived in those, it wasn't echoes.

I was rewrapping them when I heard the knock.

Three raps, measured, patient. Not Tam's knock, not the chandler's. I blew out the candle and stood very still in the dark, and the knock came again, same rhythm, same patience, and then a voice—low, courteous, carrying easily through old wood:

"Isandro Iversen. I know you're awake. I saw your lamp."

My whole body went cold and hot at once, because I knew that voice too. I had spent twenty years learning to forget it and failed completely in the space of six words.

"I'm not going to break down your door," the voice said. "That would be rude, and your landlady sleeps light. But I'll be at the customs house until dawn, and then I ride north. If there's something you want to say to me, Isandro, you should come say it."

Silence. Boots on stairs, unhurried. The street door opening and closing softly, like a man raised to have manners.

I stood in the dark for a long time, holding my mother's oilcloth bundle against my chest like a shield, and I made myself say it out loud, because saying it made it only words:

"My father is dead. I buried him. I carried the box myself."

The room didn't answer. Rooms never do. That's the first thing grief teaches you and the last thing it lets go of.

* * *

I went anyway. Of course I went. You can tell yourself a hundred times that it's a trap, that it's bait, that no dead man walks in crown colors—and then you put on your coat and walk toward it, because the alternative is lying awake forever wondering.

The customs house squatted at the river end of Market Street, all black beams and boarded windows, a building that had been dying for thirty years and been given new purpose by men who liked their business unseen. Two riders flanked the door, spears crossed. They looked at me, looked at each other, and stepped aside without a word, which told me everything about how expected I was.

Inside, candles burned in ranks along a long table, and maps were pinned to the walls—maps of Ashvale-that-had-no-name, its rivers and roads renamed, its towns relabeled in a clerk's careful hand. And at the table's head, reading a ledger by candlelight, sat the man from my childhood.

He was older. Of course he was older—seven years older than the day I'd watched them nail the coffin shut, and the gray at his temples was honest gray, the kind time puts there. The scar hooked through his eyebrow exactly as memory held it. He wore the black-and-ash of the Winnowing like it had been tailored to him, and when he looked up, my father's eyes—my father's exact eyes, brown going amber in candlelight—found mine across forty feet of dusty air.

"There you are," Serel Iversen said. "Sit down, son."

*I buried him.* The words rang in my skull like a struck bell. *I carried the box. I felt its weight. Forty pounds, maybe less. He was a big man; there should have been more.*

"You have the wrong name," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved.

"Do I?" He closed the ledger. "Serel Iversen. Inquisitor Second Rank, Order of the Quiet Flame. Seventeen years' service." He recited it flatly, a man reading his own label. "And you are Isandro Iversen, of no fixed address, licensed as a trader in curiosities, which we both know means charms. Sit down, Isandro. I'm not here to arrest you."

"Then what are you here for?"

"To ask you some questions." He gestured to the chair opposite, and I realized with a lurch that the chair had been pulled out already. Waiting. He'd known I'd come. He'd counted on the thing I hated most about myself—that I would always, always walk toward the shape of my dead father.

I sat. It seemed safer than standing, and standing near him felt suddenly unbearable.

Up close, the resemblance was worse. It wasn't just the scar or the eyes or the way he held his shoulders slightly rolled forward, protective of the chest, the way a whittler does after decades hunched over a bench. It was the small things. The way he turned the ledger pages by the corner, pinching them, because heavy fingers smudge ink. My father turned pages exactly that way. The way his left boot tapped, twice, restlessly, against the chair leg—a habit I had inherited, was tapping right now, and stopped the moment I noticed.

"Your mother," Serel said. "Wren Iversen. Carver. Died... seven years ago, I'm told. Same year as your father." He let that sit. "Hard year."

"What do you want?"

"I want to understand the trade." He opened a drawer and lifted out a bone charm—mine, I knew it instantly, the wren-and-thorn piece I'd sold to the cooper's widow three weeks ago. Seeing it in his hand made my stomach drop through the floorboards. "The cooper's widow sold this back to us for two marks. She cried while she did it, which I found interesting. Guilt, or fear?"

"Fear," I said. "You're paying silver for names. Everyone's afraid of what happens to people whose names get written down."

"Mm." He turned the charm over. His thumbnail found the maker's mark on the base—the little spiral I cut into everything, half-conscious, the way other men sign their names—and he pressed it, gently, like testing a bruise. "This mark. Where did you learn it?"

"Nowhere. It's just how I cut."

"It's just how you cut," he repeated, and something moved behind his eyes—not suspicion, something stranger, almost pain, gone before I could name it. "My orders concern a network of gifted persons moving north along the river road. Charms, relics, unregistered workings. I'm told a carver supplies much of it. I'm told that carver is good." He set the charm down between us, facing me, the spiral up. "How good are you, Isandro?"

"I sell trinkets. Echoes. Nothing that would interest the Order."

"Everything interests the Order. That's rather our problem." He leaned back, and the candlelight moved on his face, and for one horrible half-second the shadows made him look exactly—*exactly*—like the man who'd taught me to hold a knife, correcting my grip with two fingers on my wrist, patient as stone. "Tell me about your father."

The question hit like cold water. "Why?"

"Humor me."

"He was a carpenter. He died seven years ago. There's nothing to tell."

"Nothing? No habits? No stories?" His voice stayed mild, but underneath it ran something taut, a wire strung too tight. "A man I interviewed yesterday—the ferryman, old fellow, half-blind—he swore he knew your family. Said your father used to sing while he worked. Doorwarden songs, the ferryman called them. Old songs. Songs nobody sings anymore." He tilted his head. "Did your father sing, Isandro?"

Every hair on my arms stood up. *Doorwarden.* The word went through me like a splinter working toward the heart, and I thought of the letter hidden in my mother's wall cavity, the word *Doorwarden* in her careful hand, and I understood with total clarity that this interview was not about charm networks at all.

"I don't remember," I said.

"You don't remember whether your own father sang."

"People forget things."

"People do," Serel agreed softly. "People forget all kinds of things. Whole countries, even." He smiled, and it was my father's smile, crooked on the left side, and I had to grip the edge of the chair to keep from reacting. "One more question. Then you can go, and I'll leave Vell's Crossing tomorrow, and perhaps we never speak again."

I waited.

"The night your father died," he said, "where were you?"

The room got very quiet. One of the candles guttered. Somewhere upstairs, a rider's boots crossed the floor.

"At home," I said carefully. "With my mother."

"With your mother." He nodded slowly, watching me with those amber-brown eyes, and I realized he was watching *how* I lied, cataloguing it, filing it away. "Because here's what troubles me, Isandro. Here's what has troubled me since I read your file." He rose, walked to the window, stood with his back to me—a silhouette I knew, gods help me, a silhouette from a thousand evenings, my father at the cottage door looking out at the rain. "There is no record of your father's death. No burial registry entry. No grave tax paid. In a kingdom that taxes its dead like its living, a man cannot simply *vanish*. And yet you say you carried the box."

"The plague years. Records burned. Whole parishes lost."

"The plague years," he echoed. "Convenient years." He turned around. "Do you know what I think, Isandro? I think you're lying to me. I think you've been lying since you walked in. And I think—" he stopped, and his jaw worked, and for a moment the inquisitor vanished and something rawer looked out of my father's face, confused and hurting, "—and I think I know you. I don't know how. I don't know from where. But when you walked through that door, some part of me stood up and shouted, and I have been an inquisitor for seventeen years and that has never happened before, and I would very much like it to stop."

I couldn't breathe. The longing came up in me like floodwater—all those years of hungering, of waking some mornings reaching for a voice that wasn't there, and now it stood ten feet away wearing my father's face and asking questions in my father's voice, and every instinct I had screamed that this was the trap, that kinship was the bait, that the Order had built this man or remade him precisely to open doors that torture couldn't.

But gods, he sounded *lost.*

"Maybe you knew him," I heard myself say. "My father. Maybe that's why I seem familiar. Men have faces that repeat."

"Maybe." He didn't believe it either. I could see he didn't. "Go home, Isandro. Think about what I asked. About the singing. About where you learned that spiral." He picked up the cooper's widow's charm and held it out to me, handle first, an odd courtesy. "Keep your trinket. Tell the widow she needn't fear it was destroyed."

"Why would you give it back?"

"Because it's yours," he said, "and taking it felt like stealing," and then his face closed over, the inquisitor sealing up the crack, and he waved me toward the door like a man dismissing the whole impossible evening.

* * *

I walked home through streets gone blue with pre-dawn, and the charm sat in my pocket like a coal, and I made myself think instead of feel, because feeling was going to drown me.

Either Serel Iversen was my father—risen, remade, his death a fiction or my memory a lie—or he was something else wearing my father's face the way a lure wears a fly, dressed in my longing to hook me. The Order erased names. Why not faces? Why not build a hunter out of a dead man's features, calibrated to unsettle exactly one target?

But the page-turning. The boot-tap. The way he'd said *taking it felt like stealing* with genuine bewilderment, as if his own hands were reporting facts his mind had never authorized. You couldn't fake that. Could you? Could the Order fake the small unconscious grammar of a man? Could they carve a person the way I carved bone—hollowing out everything true and leaving the shape?

And if they could—if my father's face was walking around attached to the Order's will—then somewhere in that remade skull was whatever remained of him, and the hunters hadn't just taken the gifted this seven years. They'd taken my father twice. Once from the world, and once from himself.

I stopped at the corner of Chandler's Lane and pressed my forehead against the cold doorpost, and I let myself want it, just for a moment, just privately: that he was real. That the box I'd carried had been stones and sawdust and a mother's desperate mercy. That somewhere inside the inquisitor was the man who'd sung while he worked, songs he'd called nothing, songs I'd forgotten on purpose because forgetting was safer—

Doorwarden songs.

The ferryman's words landed in me like a key turning. My father sang *Doorwarden* songs. My mother's letter named her Doorwarden. And the Order's inquisitor, wearing my father's face, had come asking about both.

They weren't hunting charm networks. They were hunting the door. They had been hunting it for seven years, and they'd sent the one face alive that could make me open my mouth without thinking.

I straightened. North, Serel had said. He rides north at dawn. And my road—the road my mother's map burned toward, the road to Maren and the drowned chapel—ran north too. The same road. Of course it was the same road. Everything in my life had become the same road.

I climbed the stairs, took down my satchel, and began packing for a journey I'd sworn I'd start tomorrow, knowing now that tomorrow belonged to him.

Behind me, through the window, the eastern sky was going gray, and somewhere on the south road, six riders were saddling in the dark, and one of them wore my father's face north toward the drowned chapel, and I had to reach Maren before he did—or learn, on that road, which of us was chasing and which was being drawn.
