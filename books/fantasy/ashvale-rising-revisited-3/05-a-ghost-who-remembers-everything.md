## 5. A Ghost Who Remembers Everything

The drowned chapel sat three miles east of Vell's Crossing, in the hollow where the river had changed its mind a century ago and never bothered to change back. Locals called it the Sunk Church and avoided it after dark, which suited Isandro fine. Places people feared were the last honest real estate in Ashvale.

He came at dusk, when the light went thin and gray as old linen, carrying a shovel over his shoulder like a pilgrim's staff.

The chapel's roof had collapsed inward long before the flooding, so what remained was a stone ribcage rising out of black water—arches, half a bell tower, the suggestion of windows that no longer held glass or anything else. Reeds grew from the sills. A heron stood in the shallows of the nave, regarding him with one cold yellow eye, then lifted off without hurry, as if to say *you may have it; it was never much use to me.*

Isandro waded in to his knees. The water was colder than the season justified. It always was, here. He'd heard stories about this place since he was small—stories his mother had told differently than other mothers told them. *There's a lady in the Sunk Church,* she'd said once, drying his hair by the fire, her voice carefully light. *She remembers things nobody else does. If you ever need to know something the world has forgotten, you go and you ask her nicely.* He'd thought it a fairy tale. He'd thought a lot of things were fairy tales, seven years ago.

"Maren," he said, to the water, to the stones. His voice came out smaller than he wanted. "My name is Isandro Iversen. My mother was Elira Iversen." He swallowed. "I think she knew you."

The reeds stopped moving.

That was the first sign. Wind came off the river constantly, worrying the rushes into whispers—but now every stalk stood still, and the water went mirror-flat, and the cold deepened until his breath plumed white though the evening wasn't yet frost-cold.

Then she was there.

Not rising from the water—*arriving*, the way a thought arrives, all at once and already complete. A woman made of the memory of a woman: a gray dress that might have been blue once, hands folded at her waist, a face like a portrait left too long in sunlight, features worn down to their essentials. Her eyes were the only sharp thing about her. They were the color of the river in winter, and they looked at him the way an archivist looks at a book someone has brought in dripping wet—with recognition, and pity, and a kind of professional interest.

"Elira's boy," she said. Her voice sounded like water finding its way through stone. "You have her chin. And her habit of standing in cold water talking to ghosts."

"You know me?"

"I know *of* you. She talked to me for years, before the end. Came every season, spring and autumn, regular as tides." Maren tilted her head. "She never told me why she stopped."

The guilt arrived on schedule, punctual as a tax collector. Seven years ago. The Winnowing. The edicts. Isandro had begged his mother to stop traveling, stop being conspicuous, stop being whatever she was—and she had agreed, and he had believed himself the savior of the family, and two winters later she was dead anyway, quietly, of a fever a Doorwarden should perhaps not have died of, and he'd never even learned what a Doorwarden *was* until four days ago, prying open his own wall with a crowbar while the country burned its gifted in public squares.

"She stopped because of me," Isandro said. "I asked her to. I didn't know—"

"No. You never do, the living." There was no cruelty in it. That was somehow worse. "Ask your question, Elira's boy. You didn't walk three miles through the dark and stand knee-deep in my church to trade apologies."

He'd rehearsed this. Standing in his mother's cottage with the scorched map spread across the table, he'd rehearsed it a dozen times, and now every word had evaporated except the truest ones.

"They're burning people," he said. "In the squares. The crown posted a Great Winnowing—register or burn. I watched them take a hedge-witch in Vell's Crossing four days ago. Her name was Bess. She sold cures for colicky babies." His voice cracked and he hated it and kept going. "And there's a door. My mother kept a door—the last way out of Ashvale—and it needs three keys, and the letter said the first key is a memory. A memory of what the country was, before they erased it. Before they took the name off the maps." He looked up at her. "They say you remember everything."

Maren was quiet for a long moment. The water held its breath around them.

"I remember everything," she agreed at last. "That is not a blessing, Elira's boy. Do you know what it is to be the only one who remembers? To watch a whole country forget itself and be unable to tell anyone?" She unfolded her hands—her first movement since arriving, and it seemed to cost her something, like a swimmer pushing against a current. "I drowned in this church in the Year of the Long Rain, when Ashvale was still Ashvale and proud of it. I have been here ninety-one years. I watched them flood the valley. I watched them build the new road on the wrong side of the river. I watched the crown burn the Doorhouse and salt the ground and pass laws making the country's own name contraband. I remember the lullabies. I remember the names of the firedrakes. I remember what the mountains were called before they were just *the mountains*." Her winter eyes fixed on him. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? I am a library that no one may enter. I am a scream that has been going on for ninety-one years at a pitch only I can hear."

"I'm listening," Isandro said. "I'll carry it. Whatever you remember—I'll carry it out of here. I'll speak it to whoever will hear."

"That isn't enough." Something moved across her worn face—not anger. Hunger. "It isn't nearly enough, and you know it isn't, because if speaking were enough you wouldn't need a key carved out of my remembering. You want to take a piece of me. So let us talk honestly about pieces, charmer." She drifted closer, and the water around her ankles—if she had ankles, if the hem of that gray dress ended in anything but dissolution—did not ripple. "What did you bring me? What's in the satchel? I can feel them from here. Little voices, little echoes. You make your living selling the dead in scraps, don't you? A grandmother's hum. A soldier's last joke. Enough to comfort, never enough to matter. Your mother told me about you. She was so careful never to be ashamed of you where I could hear it, which is how I knew she was."

Each sentence landed like a small precise blow. Isandro stood in the freezing water and took them, because they were true.

"Yes," he said. "That's what I've been. For seven years. Small magics, small sales, keep your head down, survive." He set the satchel down on a half-submerged pew, where it sat dark and heavy as a heart. "I refused a child two days ago. Ten years old. Wanted me to talk to her drowned brother, and I said the charms were just trinkets, and she looked at me like—" He stopped. Started again. "I'm done being small. But I can't be anything else unless I get out, or unless the country comes back, and either way I need what you have."

"And what will you give?"

"Anything I have."

"You have very little." Maren smiled, and it was terrible and gentle at once. "But you have one thing I want. One thing only a living man can give a dead woman."

"Name it."

"My bones."

* * *

They lay beneath the flooded floor, she explained, in the crypt below the nave, where the water ran coldest and darkest. Ninety-one years ago they had buried her there with her family, and when the river came, everyone who might have moved her was dead or scattered, and so she stayed—anchored, chained to her own remains like a boat tied to a dock it despises.

"A ghost walks where her bones lie," she said. "It is not poetry. It is law, older than your crown, older than your erasure. As long as Maren Ostreth's bones sit under this water, Maren Ostreth goes nowhere. Not onward. Not onward is the part you can't imagine yet, because you're alive. The living think death is the end. Death is not the end. Death is a corridor, and I have been standing in the same ten feet of it for ninety-one years, listening to the door at the far end, unable to reach it." Her voice frayed. "Your mother promised. Twenty years ago she stood where you're standing and swore she'd dig me up and carry me to the Doorhouse herself, so I could cross while the way was open. Then the Doorhouse burned, and she stopped coming, and I thought—well. I thought that was that. Another promise the living couldn't keep."

Isandro's throat was tight. "The door opens again. The letter said—it opens while dragon-fire still burns somewhere in the land. There's still fire somewhere. There has to be."

"Maybe." Maren drifted toward the submerged altar, and the water dimpled faintly where she passed, the only trace of her. "Maybe there's fire. Maybe there's a door. Maybe a boy with his mother's chin can open it. I stopped betting on maybes decades ago. So here is my price, and it is not negotiable, and you will agree to it before I pour one drop of remembering into you: you will dive into my crypt tonight, in the dark, in the cold, past everything that guards a drowned grave, and you will gather every bone of Maren Ostreth, and you will carry them—every one, and I will know if you lose so much as a finger-joint—to the door. And when it opens, you will lay me across the threshold first, before yourself, before your escape, before anything. And I will finally, *finally*, go onward." She turned. "That is the price. The memory of a country for the passage of one dead woman. Cheap, I think. I think it's cheap. But grief always thinks it's cheap, and no one bargains harder than the grieving."

Isandro looked down through the black water, at nothing, at everything.

"The guards," he said. "You said *everything that guards a drowned grave*. What guards it?"

"Me," said Maren, "when I am frightened. And eels that have grown old on ghost-light and forgotten they were ever small. And the simple fact that the water wants to keep what it takes." She paused. "Also, possibly, a curse. We'll find out."

A laugh escaped him—short, disbelieving, half hysteria. Ninety-one years anchored to a flooded crypt, and she could still deliver a punchline dry as August. He wondered if that was what ninety-one years of loneliness built, or whether she'd been like this alive.

"My mother promised you," he said. "I can't promise more than she did. But I'll promise the same thing, and I'll keep it, because I'm apparently the only Iversen who gets the chance."

Something flickered across her worn face at that—grief for his mother, maybe, or the old anger, or both braided together the way they always are in the long-bereaved. Then she nodded, once, formal as a treaty.

"Then we have a bargain, Elira's boy. Take off your coat. Empty your pockets. The crypt mouth is under the third pew from the altar, and the water above it runs nine feet deep, and it is colder than any water has a right to be, because I am cold, and the water has caught my cold the way a room catches a mood." She was already fading at the edges, her attention sliding downward, homeward, hungry. "Dive. I'll hold the dark open as long as I can. And Isandro—" His name in her mouth sounded strange, official, like a word being entered into a ledger. "When you find my skull, speak to it kindly. It's been waiting a long time to be spoken to."

* * *

He stripped to his shirt and stood at the edge of the crypt-mouth, where the black water breathed slow and cold against the stones. Below him, nine feet down, a darkness darker than water waited, full of ninety-one years of a woman's patience.

He thought of Bess the hedge-witch, screaming in the square while every charm in his satchel went silent at once. He thought of the child with the drowned brother, and the look on her face. He thought of his mother's letter, hidden in a wall for seven years: *If you are reading this, the fire is failing, and you must go to the door.*

Everyone wanted passage. The living south, away from the pyres. The dead onward, through whatever waited at the end of corridors. And him, somewhere in the middle, holding a lantern and a bag of borrowed voices, about to dive into a grave because a ghost had finally found someone desperate enough to make a fair trade.

"Kindly," he muttered to himself. "Speak to it kindly."

Isandro filled his lungs and went down into the cold, and the cold closed over him like a mouth.

His fingers found the crypt-mouth's edge almost at once—stone, slick with a century of silt—and beyond it, the dark. Somewhere below, pale in the blackness, something long and patient uncoiled from among the bones and began, slowly, to move.
