## 6. The Caravan of Small Magics

Three days north of Vell's Crossing, I learned that grief has a smell.

Maren's bones rode in my satchel where the charms used to be, wrapped in the chapel's rotted altar cloth, and they smelled of river silt and candle smoke and something older underneath—something like the inside of a sealed room. I told myself it was my imagination. I told myself a lot of things on that road, walking alone with my hood up and my hands in my sleeves like a man with nothing to hide, which was the whole performance, wasn't it. A man with nothing to hide doesn't walk like that.

The second night it rained, and I was grateful, because rain meant no fires, and no fires meant no light, and no light meant I could stop pretending to be ordinary for a few hours and simply be tired instead.

That was when I heard the singing.

Not with my ears. My ears heard wind in the pines and the creak of axles somewhere off the road to the east. But under all of it, in the place where bone charms whisper, there was a voice threading through the storm—low, patient, coaxing. It wasn't calling the rain down. It was asking the rain to fall *gently*, please, gently, we have cargo that bruises.

I stood in the dark with water running off my hood and understood two things at once. One: someone out there was gifted and careless enough—or desperate enough—to work magic in the open. Two: whoever they were, they were better at this than me, because I'd spent seven years doing nothing but hiding, and here was a person who hid *and* worked, the way you might keep a bird singing inside your coat.

A smart man would have walked the other way.

I followed the song.

* * *

The caravan sat in a hollow below the road: four wagons, eight horses, and a ring of canvas shelters beaded with rain. The horses were the first wrong thing. They were too calm. Rain hammered the trees and thunder walked the ridgeline, and those animals stood hipshot and drowsy as churchgoers, because someone had sung the weather soft around them and the beasts knew it.

A man stepped out from between the wagons before I was within fifty paces, which meant he'd heard me coming long before that. Big through the shoulders, gray at the temples, dressed like every horse trader between here and the coast—oilskin coat, cudgel at his belt, a face built for haggling. He looked at me the way traders look: measuring what I'd cost and what I'd fetch.

"Road's south," he said. "You're going the wrong way."

"I'm going north."

"North's where the pyres are." He said it lightly, testing.

"North is where I'm going," I said, "and I heard your rain."

Something shifted behind his eyes—not fear exactly. Recognition. The look of a man finding another survivor in a flood.

"Did you," he said.

"The rain was falling too politely. Rain doesn't apologize unless someone asks it to."

For a long moment neither of us moved. Rain ticked on canvas. Somewhere in the camp a horse sighed like an old woman.

Then he jerked his chin toward the nearest wagon. "Bram Fellweather. Horses and tack, if anyone official asks." A beat. "And if nobody official asks, I sing the sky. You'll forgive me if I don't shake your hand yet. I don't know what you are."

"Nobody," I said. "A carver. Bone trinkets. Nothing that works anymore—I stopped."

"Stopped," Bram repeated, in the tone of a man filing the word away to examine later. "Come sit by the fire you can't see, then, Nobody. There's stew."

* * *

There were eleven of them, and by morning I had most of their shapes.

Bram the weather-singer, who owned the caravan on paper and sang storms docile in his sleep. His wife, Petra, who kept the ledgers and, I suspected, kept Bram too, because a man who talks to the sky needs someone anchored to the ground. Two hired guards with mercenary eyes and no gift that I could find—which made them either safe or the most dangerous people in camp, and I hadn't decided which. A cook named Hesk whose only strangeness was that everything he made tasted like whatever you were homesick for, which he swore was seasoning and I swore was not.

And the boys.

The twins came out of their wagon at dawn like a matched pair of sparrows, all elbows and sharp collarbones, fourteen years old or nearly. Tam had a scar splitting one eyebrow. Rill didn't, and resented it. They fell into step beside me while I checked the axle of a wagon I had no business checking, and Rill said, "You're the bone man."

"I'm a carver."

"You dreamed us last week," Tam said. "Both of us. Same dream. A man on a road with a bag full of whispers, walking north with his head down like the sky owed him money."

"We bet each other you'd be ugly," Rill said. "Tam lost."

"They dream true," Bram said later, watching the boys race the dogs along the tree line. "Have since they were six. Their mother drowned the same winter the edicts started, and after that the dreams got—" He turned his hands over, searching for the word. "*Loud.* They see things coming. Not always. Not clearly. But when both of them dream the same dream on the same night, Petra and I pay attention." He glanced at me sideways. "They've dreamed you three times now. That's why I let you past the picket last night instead of putting a cudgel in your ribs."

"What do the dreams say?"

"That depends how much you like riddles." Bram smiled without much humor. "Rill says you're a door. Tam says you're a key. They argue about which."

I thought of my mother's letter folded against my chest. *Doorwarden.* I said nothing, and Bram, to his credit, let the silence stand.

* * *

Odile I met on the fourth day, and she was the reason I stayed.

She kept apart from the camp the way a wick keeps apart from the candle—necessary, central, and never touched. Her wagon was iron-banded and windowless, and she slept in it alone, and no one went in except Bram, once a day, to leave food at the step. She was small and brown and sixty if she was a day, with hands like knotted rope and eyes that had done a lot of arithmetic about other people's worth.

I caught her at dusk on the fifth evening, standing at the edge of camp with an iron lantern hanging from one fist, just looking at it. Inside the lantern, behind smoked glass, something glowed. Not like coals glow. Coals gutter and breathe. This burned steady, white-hearted, patient—a light that had decided to keep burning and saw no reason to discuss it.

Dragon-fire. I knew it the way you know your own name, though I'd never seen it. Some inheritance in the blood, some word my mother never taught me, rising up unasked.

"Don't stare at it," Odile said, without turning. "It notices."

"It's real," I said, and hated how young my voice sounded.

"It's *tired*." She raised the lantern slightly, and the light inside leaned toward her like a plant toward sun. "Seven years I've carried this coal. Do you know what dragon-fire eats? Not wood. Not oil. It eats *remembering*. It burns on what people know and refuse to say. Every mile north, it gets a little hungrier, because everyone back there is working so hard to forget." She looked at me then, and her eyes were old in a way that had nothing to do with years. "You carry dead things in that bag, carver. I can smell them listening. What do they burn on?"

"Grief," I said. "Grief and guilt. Mostly mine."

"Hm." Almost a laugh. "Cheap fuel. But fuel." She turned back to the lantern. "Bram says the twins dreamed you. Says you're going where we're going."

"The mountains?"

"The drake." She said the word carefully, the way you handle a blade by the flat. "You know about Vaskarath."

I didn't. But the name went into me like a hook finding flesh, and Odile watched it land, and nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed something.

"Everyone knows about the drake," she said. "That's the problem. Knowing is dangerous now. Come to my wagon tomorrow. If you're walking where I'm walking, you should know what I'm carrying and why."

* * *

I want to record honestly what those first days felt like, because honesty is the only thing I have left to carve with.

They felt like warmth. That's the shameful truth of it. Seven years I had been a man amputating himself piece by piece to survive—selling echoes too faint to matter, refusing a child her drowned brother, walking past hedge-witches being dragged to the square because meeting their eyes would mean dying with them. I had told myself that was wisdom. Traveling with the caravan, eating Hesk's homesick stew, listening to the twins argue about whether I was a door or a key, hearing Bram hum the rain gentle over the tents at midnight—it felt like being handed back an arm I'd cut off and forgotten I'd ever had.

And every hour of that warmth, some cold clerk in the back of my skull wrote the bill.

*Belonging costs. Belonging gets people found. Every person who learns your face becomes a confession waiting to be tortured out of them.*

I'd watched what the crown did to the connected. In Vell's Crossing they hadn't burned the hedge-witch alone. They'd burned her sister for harboring, her neighbor for knowing, and posted the names of three customers they were still hunting. Warmth was not comfort. Warmth was a list.

So I did what I do. I kept half of myself outside the firelight. I slept apart. I gave the guards false names for my hometown and practiced them until they were smooth. When Rill asked what my mother had been like, I said, "Quiet," which was true and revealed nothing, and the lie of omission sat in my stomach like a swallowed stone.

But at night, lying in the wagon bed with Maren's bones breathing their silt-smell beside me, I would hear the twins talking in their sleep across the camp—two voices falling into the same dream at the same moment, murmuring in unison like a choir of two—and Bram's low song holding the storm off the canvas, and the faint steady heart of Odile's lantern, and I would think:

*This is what it was. Before. This is what they took.*

I had been seven years old the last time I'd felt it—too young to name it, old enough to miss it forever. A country is not a map. A country is a campfire in the rain and everyone in it humming the same note without deciding to.

They had taken the note out of our mouths. And here, in a hollow in the northern hills, eleven people were humming it anyway, quietly, illegally, one verse at a time.

* * *

On the seventh night, Odile opened her wagon to me.

Inside it smelled of iron and hot stone. The lantern sat on a table bolted to the floor, surrounded by tools I didn't recognize—bone needles, silver tongs, a dish of ash that never seemed to spill. She set a stool across from the lantern and said, "Sit. Look at it properly. It likes being looked at by the right people."

The coal—if it was a coal—was the size of my thumbnail and burned without consuming anything. Up close I could feel it against my skin, a pressure like standing near a struck bell.

"A drake called Serevaneth gave it to me," Odile said. "Nineteen years ago, in the southern passes, when the crown's hunters cornered her. She was old and she was tired and she chose to spend her last strength making this instead of fighting." Her voice stayed level, but her rope-knotted hands tightened on her knees. "I was her flame-tender. I was supposed to get her over the border. I took a wrong pass in a snowstorm. Three days lost. By the time I found her again, the hunters had her." She looked at the coal. "She was still alive. She could have burned her way free—she had fire enough left for that, and none to spare for this. So I asked her for it. For the coal. Instead of for herself."

The silence stretched.

"She gave it," Odile said. "Of course she gave it. That's what dragons are. And then she died slow in the snow while I ran north with her last breath in an iron box, telling myself I was saving something." She finally looked up. "I have carried her death for nineteen years, carver, and fed it my remembering so it wouldn't go out, and I will carry it until there is a fire big enough to give it back. That is what I am. Now you know. And now you understand why I'm walking toward Vaskarath, the last of them, guttering in the caldera like a candle in wind."

"Why tell me?"

"Because Bram says the twins dreamed you three times, and because you flinched when I said the drake's name—flinched like a man hearing his own house called." She leaned forward. "You're not just running north, Isandro Iversen. I've known your face since the second day. Your mother sang at my wedding, did you know that? Before the erasure. Before any of it. Doorwarden's son, walking north with a bag of bones and a face full of secrets." She sat back. "Whatever you're carrying, it's heavier than my lantern. And I think the road is going to make you open it."

I wanted to deny it. I opened my mouth to deny it.

Across the camp, both twins screamed.

* * *

We reached their wagon as Bram was hauling them upright, shaking them, saying their names in the voice you use on drowning men. Tam's eyes were open and seeing nothing. Rill was speaking—not words, at first, just a sound like wind through a burnt forest—and then the words came, and both boys said them together, in unison, the way they said everything that mattered:

"*The candle in the mountain leans. The candle in the mountain leans. Wings like ash-cloth, breath like a thread—when the thread breaks, the door dies with it. Hurry. Hurry. Hurry.*"

Then they collapsed back onto their pallets, breathing hard, asleep before their heads touched the boards.

No one spoke. The whole camp had gathered, guards and cooks and all, drawn by the screaming, and now stood in the dark looking at each other over the sleeping boys.

Odile's lantern-light caught her face, and I saw that the arithmetic behind her eyes had finished, and the number was bad.

"How long," Bram said hoarsely, "have we got?"

Neither twin answered. They were already deep in whatever came after true dreams.

But I had Maren's bones against my chest, and my mother's letter, and a rite I hadn't dared read twice, and I knew—knew the way you know a name carved into your own body—that the memory I needed was still folded shut inside a dead woman's skull, and that the road ahead had just grown teeth.

"We ride at dawn," Odile said.
