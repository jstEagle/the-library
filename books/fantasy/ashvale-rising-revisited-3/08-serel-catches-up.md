## 8. Serel Catches Up

The road into Vell's Crossing smelled of wet ash and tallow, and Serel Iversen found it familiar in a way that irritated him.

He did not know why. That was the trouble with these roads—the crown sent him up and down the spine of the country, town to town, and every one of them snagged on something in him like a burr in wool. A crooked fencepost. A well with a rope gone green. The way the light came off the eastern hills at dusk, gold going gray at the edges. None of it meant anything. He had no memories before his anointing; the Order had made certain of that. They told him so plainly, the way they told him everything: *You were remade clean, Inquisitor. What you feel is residue. Burn it out.*

Residue. He had come to think of it as weather. It moved through him and it passed and it left nothing behind worth naming.

Except this town.

Vell's Crossing snagged worse than most.

He rode in at the tail of a gray afternoon, six riders behind him and the edict boards rattling against their saddle frames. The Great Winnowing. The words had been painted by a court scribe with a taste for flourishes, but Serel preferred them plain: *All gifted persons will present themselves for registration within nine days. Those who do not will burn.* Nine days was generous. In the southern provinces they'd been given five.

The square was already crowded when they arrived—not with the curious, though there were those too, but with people who had heard the riders coming and come anyway, the way people gather at a house fire. They stood in knots and watched him dismount, and their watching had a texture he knew. Not hatred. Hatred he could work with. This was something older and more tired: the look of people who had learned that looking away was what got their neighbors taken.

"Post the boards," he said.

His riders moved. Hammers rang against the market posts, and each strike silenced a little more of the crowd's murmur, until the only sound was paper flapping in the wind and somewhere, far off, a dog barking at nothing.

Serel stood in the middle of the square and let them look at him. He was good at this part. He had the face for it—long, severe, a jaw like a drawn line—and he wore the Order's black without ornament except the silver brand at his collar, the closed eye. He had been told once that he resembled someone. A magistrate in Corren's Ford had said it, squinting up at him from her knees: *You look like a man I buried.* He'd reported her for the remark. She'd burned on schedule. But the sentence had stayed with him the way the fenceposts stayed, the way the light on the hills stayed. Residue.

A woman near the well was crying without noise. She held a boy by the shoulder, gripping hard enough that the boy winced, and she wasn't looking at Serel at all. She was looking at his face the way you look at a door you thought was bricked over.

He turned from her and gave the orders that needed giving. House-to-house inquiry, beginning with the registered charm-sellers. Confiscation lists. The names of anyone who had bought bone-work in the last season. It was methodical work, and he was good at it, and being good at it was the only self he had ever been given, so he did not examine the small cold weight that settled under his ribs when the hammers stopped ringing.

* * *

They found the first charm-buyer within two hours.

Her name was Tessa Brandt, a cooper's widow, sixty if she was a day, and she wept the entire time the riders turned out her pantry, which contained, among the salt pork and the winter apples, a single carved knucklebone on a leather cord. She swore it was her mother's. She swore it was only a keepsake. She said the word *keepsake* four times, as if repetition could make it true.

Serel took the cord from her hands himself. He always did this part personally. It was not mercy—he had checked, early on, whether he was capable of mercy, and concluded the question was malformed—but procedure. Chain of custody. The charms went to the Order's pyre-censers, who burned them properly, because improperly burned bone-work could carry echoes for years, whispering in walls, keeping grief alive past its usefulness to the crown.

Grief past its usefulness. He didn't know where the phrase came from. It arrived in his head fully formed sometimes, wearing someone else's voice, a woman's voice, low and dry as autumn grass. He had never reported it. Reporting it would mean explaining it, and explaining it would mean admitting there was something in him the remaking had missed, and he was not certain what the Order did with inquisitors who were incompletely clean. He suspected he did not want to find out.

Tessa Brandt's charm was a knucklebone, sheep or calf, worn smooth at the edges from years of handling. Someone had cut a spiral into it, shallow, the lines uneven—a child's work, or a beginner's. He turned it in his fingers and felt nothing. Most of them were nothing. Ninety charms in a hundred were just bone with scratches, sold to the grieving by frauds and fools, and the crown's war was mostly a war against comfort.

But this one—

This one hummed.

Not sound. Something adjacent to sound, the way heat is adjacent to light. It sat in his palm and it *waited*, and when his thumb crossed the spiral a second time, the waiting broke open, and what came through was not a voice but a sensation: sawdust. Lamplight. The specific ache in the wrist of holding a small knife for hours. And underneath all of it, warm as a coal cupped in a hand, a feeling with no name attached—pride, maybe, or love, the kind that watches someone work and says nothing because saying would break the spell of the working.

Serel stood very still in the widow's kitchen.

"Inquisitor?" One of his riders, Halvard, young, eager, already reaching for the confiscation sack.

"It's nothing," Serel said. "Fraud-work. Burn it with the rest."

He handed it over. His hand did not want to open. He noted that, clinically, the way he noted the fenceposts and the light on the hills, and filed it under residue, and walked out of the widow's kitchen into the last of the daylight, and did not look back at Tessa Brandt, because looking back was how men like him started becoming men who couldn't do the work.

* * *

That night, in the Order's billet above the tollhouse, he could not sleep.

He sat at the table with the day's reports and wrote in his precise, upright hand: *Charm-trade active in Vell's Crossing. Source likely local carver, skilled, cautious—sells low-grade work only, nothing strong enough to register. Recommend surveillance of market days.* His pen paused over the word *cautious*. He had written it without thinking, and it was an odd word to choose. Sympathetic, almost. Admiring, even. He struck it out and wrote *evasive*, which was the correct word, and the page looked worse for it.

The lamp guttered. Outside, rain had begun, soft and steady, drumming on the tollhouse roof.

He found himself thinking about the knucklebone. About the feeling in it. Pride-or-love, watching someone work. He tried to locate the memory it had come from and got nothing, the usual nothing, the clean white nothing the Order had given him instead of a childhood. But the feeling itself remained, unmoored, floating loose in him like a splinter working toward the surface, and it hurt in a way he had no vocabulary for. It was not pain exactly. It was the shape of pain. It was the place where a memory should have been, aching like a socket after a tooth.

*A man I buried,* the magistrate had said.

He took out his knife—the short, heavy-bladed knife the Order issued for fieldwork, though he used it mostly for sharpening pencils and cutting apples—and laid it on the table, and looked at it for a long time without knowing why.

Then he slept, badly, and dreamed of lamplight and sawdust and a woman singing in a language he almost understood, and woke with wet eyes that he dried before the riders knocked.

* * *

Market day came gray and cold, and Vell's Crossing's gray market opened anyway, because hunger does not observe edicts. Serel walked it in plain clothes—no brand, no black, just a trader's oilskin and a face nobody wanted to meet twice. Halvard shadowed him at thirty paces, playing a drover badly.

The market ran along the canal in three rows of stalls, and it dealt in the small illegalities everyone pretended weren't ones: unlicensed cheese, untaxed wool, letters carried south for coin. And at the end of the second row, under a canvas awning patched with three different colors of thread, a table of bone.

The carver was not there. A placard said BACK AT NOON in a careful hand, and beside it lay the stock: charms in ranks, each tagged with a scrap of paper bearing a price and nothing else. No claims. No promises. *Echo-charms, mild. For comfort only.* Even the disclaimer was honest, which Serel found interesting. Fraudsters oversold. This one undersold, deliberately, keeping the goods dull enough to pass beneath notice. *Evasive*, he corrected himself again, and again the correction tasted wrong.

He picked up a charm. A rib sliver, polished, carved with a running knot pattern. Mild, as advertised—a breath of someone else's calm, gone in a moment. He set it down. Another: a bird's skull drilled for a cord, carrying, faintly, birdsong. Another: a finger-bone ringed with tiny marks like tally strokes, and inside it, so faint he nearly missed it, the rhythm of someone counting. Counting what? Steps? Days? He put it down more carefully than he needed to.

"You're up early."

The voice came from behind the stall, and Serel turned, and the world did something it had never done before.

It doubled.

The young man standing there with a satchel over his shoulder and sawdust on his sleeves—young, mid-twenties perhaps, dark-haired, guarded in the way of people who have practiced being unremarkable—was nobody. A stranger. Serel had never seen him. Serel was certain of this the way he was certain of the Order's doctrines, absolutely and completely and with a hollowness underneath, because at the same time, in some chamber of himself that had no doors the remaking could reach, he knew this boy's face the way he knew his own hands. Knew it thinner than it should be, hungrier, the jaw harder from years of clenching around secrets. Knew that the left eyebrow scarred itself in a fall from a workshop roof at age eleven, and that he'd cried more from the scolding than the stitches, and—

Serel did not know any of that. He knew none of it. There was no age eleven. There was no workshop. There was a white room and a voice saying *you are remade clean* and a name given to him like a tool: *Inquisitor.*

"You're the carver," Serel said. His own voice sounded distant to him, as if heard through water.

"I'm a trader." The young man's eyes flicked over the oilskin, the empty hands, cataloguing threat with a fluency that also felt horribly, impossibly familiar. "Charms are for comfort. Prices are on the tags. If you're looking for anything stronger, you've wasted a walk."

"And if I'm looking for the man who made these?"

"Then you're looking for a dead man. They say he drowned." A beat, perfectly measured. "Canal's right there. Happens all the time."

It was well done. Every note of it. The flatness, the deflection, the little joke to sand down the edge of the question. Serel had interrogated a hundred liars and this boy lied better than most trained agents, and the reason, he understood suddenly, with a certainty that arrived like a blade between the ribs, was that he had been *taught*. Taught patiently, over years, by someone who loved him enough to teach him how to survive, and—

There was no such person. There was no such teaching. Residue. Weather. It passes.

"The work is good," Serel said. He heard his own voice go quiet, go strange. "The spiral-cut especially. On the knucklebones. Where did you learn the spiral?"

Something moved behind the young man's eyes—fast, quickly mastered, but not fast enough. Recognition. Not of Serel's face, Serel realized; of the *question*. Of the word *spiral*, which meant something to him that it did not mean to the market, that it did not mean to the crown, that it meant—

"My father taught me," the carver said. Flat. Final. A door closing. "He's dead."

"Yes," Serel heard himself say. "I know."

And then neither of them spoke, and the canal lapped against its stones, and somewhere a gull screamed, and the two of them stood in the rain-smelling morning looking at each other across a table of bones, and Serel felt the hollow in him ache like a tooth, like a socket, like a room in a house he had never entered and yet somehow knew the dimensions of, down to the crack in the plaster, down to the wall cavity that was—

That was nothing. That was no cavity. There was no house.

Halvard appeared at the end of the row, conspicuous, anxious, and the spell—if it was a spell, if it was anything—broke. The carver's whole body shifted, subtle as weather, into a new posture: the merchant again, harmless, bored. "Buy something or move along," he said. "You're bad for trade."

Serel bought the bird's skull. Two coppers. The boy—*the boy*, the word rose in him unbidden and unbearable—made change without touching his hand.

At the mouth of the lane, Serel stopped. He did not turn around. He said, to the rain and the gulls and the boy behind him, in a voice pitched to carry no farther than the awning:

"Nine days. Then the burning starts. Whatever you're carrying, whoever you're hiding—" His throat closed on the next word and he had to force it out, and he did not know why the word cost what it cost. "—son."

He walked on. Behind him, silence. He did not hear the stall pack up, and he did not hear footsteps follow, and the not-hearing frightened him more than either would have.

* * *

That evening he wrote his report by lamplight, and his hand betrayed him.

*Source identified. Male, approx. 25, resident of Vell's Crossing, operates gray-market stall, canal-side. Carver of record for all confiscated charm-work. Subject is evasive, skilled, and almost certainly gifted.*

The pen hovered. Below that, in the same upright hand, unbidden, his fingers wrote:

*Subject has my father's hands.*

Serel stared at the line. He had no father. The Order had told him so: *You have no father, no mother, no before. You have duty.* He reached for the page to strike the sentence and found he could not. Instead he sat back, and the lamp hissed, and the rain came down on the tollhouse roof, and he thought about the knucklebone in the pyre-censer's fire, about pride-or-love going up in smoke, about a spiral cut by a beginner's hand that he had recognized the way you recognize your own signature.

He thought: *I am haunted by a life I never lived.*

He thought: *The boy called me a stranger, and lied, and the lie was kind, and I know—I know the way I know my own heartbeat—that he learned kindness from me.*

No. From no one. From no one at all.

He finished the report without the impossible line, sealed it, and gave it to the night rider for the southern road. Then he sat alone with his knife and the bird-skull charm he had bought for two coppers, turning it in his fingers, listening to the ghost of birdsong, and outside, far off in the dark hills, something answered the rain with a sound he had no name for either—a long, thin cry, like iron cooling, like a voice that remembered being a roar.

He did not report the sound.

He sat up all night instead, a hollow man in a lamplit room, guarding a feeling he could not source, while nine days burned down to eight.
