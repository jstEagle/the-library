## 9. The Twins Dream Fire

The twins stopped dreaming on the third night north of the Tollwater, and that was how Isandro knew something was wrong.

They were nine years old, Perrin and Cole, identical down to the chipped front tooth they'd both earned falling out of the same tree, and since the day he'd joined the caravan they had talked in their sleep the way other children mumbled about supper. Horses. Rivers. The weather-singer's stew, which even dreamed of was apparently disappointing. Their dreams leaked out of them like warmth from a kettle—small, harmless, true. That was what dreamers did, Odile had explained. They couldn't help it.

But for two nights now the boys had slept in silence, curled back to back in the wagon bed, faces tight as knots, and their mother Brava had started sitting up with them instead of sleeping.

Isandro noticed because he wasn't sleeping either. He rarely did anymore. Maren's bones rode wrapped in oilcloth in the bottom of his pack, and every time the wagon jolted he imagined her skull knocking against her own vertebrae, a dead woman being shaken awake by bad roads. He'd taken to walking beside the cart rather than riding in it.

"You look like a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like," Odile said, falling into step beside him. The iron lantern swung from her fist, and inside it the coal glowed its faint, stubborn orange—the last living coal of dragon-fire in Ashvale, or so she claimed. Seven days on the road together and she still held it like a woman carrying a sleeping infant through a war.

"I'm trying to decide how much to tell them," Isandro said.

"About the door?"

"About any of it." He kept his voice under the creak of the wheels. "Maren gave me the memory. She gave me the rite. But I haven't told them what I'm actually doing, walking north with a sack of bones and a lantern. If it goes badly—if anyone's caught near me—they should have been able to say they didn't know."

Odile snorted. "The Winnowing doesn't have a category for *didn't know*. You think the hedge-witch in Vell's Crossing knew anything worse than how to sweeten a goat's milk?" She lifted the lantern slightly, and the coal's light caught the hollows of her face. "We're all guilty of existing, carver. Telling them your business won't make it worse. It'll just mean you stop being a stranger."

He wanted to argue. He found, instead, that he wanted to not be a stranger more than he wanted to be safe, and that frightened him more than Serel Iversen's riders somewhere behind them on the road.

Before he could answer, Brava called from the wagon, and her voice had a crack in it.

"Perrin's waking."

* * *

Perrin woke screaming, which none of them were prepared for. A boy who dreamed of stew and horses didn't know how to scream properly; it came out wrong, rusty, like a hinge forced open after years. His brother woke a heartbeat later and screamed too—not from his own nightmare, Isandro realized, but from catching his twin's dream the way you catch a yawn. Whatever the boys saw, they saw it together.

Brava gathered them both against her chest on the wagon bench while the weather-singer—a lean, gray woman named Kest who smelled permanently of horse and rain—circled the caravan to stand watch without being asked. Odile set the lantern down between all of them like a candle at a deathbed.

"Tell me," Brava said, rocking Perrin. "Slowly. Tell your mother."

Perrin's eyes found Isandro first. Not his mother. Not Kest. The stranger with the satchel of whispering charms. There was a terrible certainty in a child's stare, and Isandro felt it land on him like a hand on his shoulder.

"There's a mountain," Perrin said. "With a hole in the top, smoking but not smoke. Gray. All gray. And there's—" His breath hitched. "There's an old, old thing inside it. Big as a barn. Bigger. It's lying down and it can't get up and its fire is going out."

Cole spoke into his brother's pause, finishing the sentence the way twins finish everything. "It's a dragon. It's the last one. It knows its name is Vaskarath and it's trying to remember why that matters."

Nobody moved. Somewhere behind them, a horse stamped. The coal in the lantern pulsed once—Odile swore later it hadn't—but Isandro saw it, saw the little flame bend toward the boys like a listener leaning in.

"It keeps saying *doorwarden*," Perrin whispered. "Over and over. It keeps saying, *if the door opens before I gutter, then—* and then I wake up." His chin trembled. "I don't want to wake up. When I'm asleep it isn't dying yet."

Brava looked around the circle of faces and said, flatly, "Someone explain to my sons why a dream made the lantern do that."

* * *

They argued by the fire that night, after the boys finally slept again—dreamlessly this time, or pretending to—and after Kest had walked the perimeter twice and reported nothing but cold stars.

"The answer's no," Kest said, before anyone else could speak. She sat with her boots off, rubbing a foot scarred by frostbite, and her voice carried the finality of a woman who had buried people. "The mountains are three weeks' travel north. Maybe four, with the wagons. The road past Harrow Fen is watched—we all saw the signal fires. And you're proposing we walk *toward* the thing a pair of dream-children saw, on faith."

"It wasn't just a dream," Odile said. "The coal answered them."

"Coals shift. Wind moves flames—"

"Kest." Odile's voice was quiet, and Isandro heard something in it he hadn't heard from her before: authority. She turned the lantern so the light caught her eyes. "Seven years I've carried this. Do you know what dragon-fire does? It doesn't burn like fire burns. It *remembers*. It holds what things were." Her thumb traced the iron handle. "If there's truly a drake left alive up there, then this coal came from its line. And if it dies—" She stopped. Looked at Isandro.

He took a breath and did the thing he'd been avoiding for a week. He told them everything.

The Doorwarden. His mother. The passage that opened only while dragon-fire still burned somewhere in the land. Maren's bones in his pack and her final memory folded into his skull like a letter he hadn't dared unfold. The rite. The name carved into bone. He told it plainly, the way he carved—no flourishes, no wasted strokes—and when he finished, the fire had burned down to coals and nobody had interrupted him except Cole, once, to whisper *doorwarden* to his brother like a password.

"So it's true," Brava said at last. "There's a way out."

"That's what I believed when I started," Isandro said. "Now I'm not sure the door leads out at all. But it doesn't matter tonight. What matters is what the boys heard. The door only opens while dragon-fire lives. One dragon left. Guttering." He let the word sit. "If Vaskarath dies before the way opens, it never opens. Not for us. Not for anyone. Ever."

"Then we go south faster," Kest said. "You want to escape? Escape. South past the border stones, cross the Silt, be in Tarrowmere by thaw. Let the dragon die and the door close and the whole cursed country stay exactly what it is—unnamed and quiet and safe to leave."

"Safe," Odile repeated softly.

"For the children," Kest snapped, and gestured at the wagon where the twins slept. "That's the arithmetic, isn't it? Four adults can gamble their lives. Those boys didn't consent to any of this. Every mile north is a mile closer to witch-hunters, and you're asking it because of a *feeling*—"

"Because of a coal that leaned toward them," Odile said. "And because of him." She nodded at Isandro. "Ask him. He's the one carrying the ghost."

They all looked at him. Isandro found he was looking at his own hands, at the knuckles scarred from seven years of careful, small, hidden work. Charms that whispered. Charms that never said enough. He had built an entire life out of selling people echoes and keeping the truth locked in his chest, and here at the end of that life, on the cold side of a dying fire, the arithmetic Kest wanted was the arithmetic he knew best.

"We could run south," he said slowly. "Some of us might make it. The twins might. And we'd live in Tarrowmere or beyond, and we'd hear about the burnings here, the way you hear about weather in another province. And every charm I ever carve would still be a whisper of the dead, because that's all this country will be. A beautiful place that died and can't say its own name."

"That's not an argument," Kest said. "That's a eulogy."

"It's the honest one." He raised his head. "I ran from Vell's Crossing. I've been running for seven years and one week extra since the edicts went up. Every time I ran, I told myself survival was the whole of virtue. It isn't. Survival is the *down payment* on virtue. What you buy with it—that's the rest." He spread his hands. "I don't know what's in that mountain. I don't know if the rite works or what the door opens onto. But I know the deadline is real, and deadlines don't care whether we're brave. The dragon is dying *now*. Tonight. While we sit here deciding whether hope is too expensive."

Silence. The fire popped. From the wagon came the small sound of Cole turning in his sleep, murmuring a word that might have been *Vaskarath* or might have been nothing.

Kest stared into the flames for a long time. Then she pulled her boots back on, one and then the other, deliberately, like a woman signing something.

"Harrow Fen is watched," she said. "So we don't take the road. We take the drover's cut along the fells—it adds days, but there are no toll bridges and no informers, because there's nothing to inform on but sheep." She looked up, jaw set. "I'll sing the weather kind for the crossing. First warm rain in Ashvale in seven years, and nobody will know to thank me." A pause. "Don't tell my horses I'm a fraud."

Brava laughed—one broken, wet note of it—and put her hand over her mouth.

"And the wagons?" Isandro asked.

"Sell them in Millbrook for whatever they'll bring. We go the last stretch on foot, light and fast." Kest stood, joints cracking, suddenly all business. "Which means everyone sleeps now. Especially you, carver. You look like something Maren dug up herself."

* * *

He didn't sleep. He tried. But an hour later he was still awake, sitting propped against the wagon wheel with Maren's bundle in his lap, when a small shape detached itself from the shadows and sat down beside him without asking.

Cole. Blanket around his shoulders, eyes too old for his face in the lantern light.

"I'm not supposed to be up," the boy said. "I'm telling you so you're not surprised when Mam scolds you."

"Noted. Thank you for the warning."

Cole studied the bundle in Isandro's lap with the frank curiosity of children. "Is that the ghost?"

"Maren. Yes."

"Does she talk in there?"

"Not anymore. She spent her voice on me. On the memory." He hesitated, then unwrapped a corner of the oilcloth—just enough to show a pale curve of bone, handled a thousand times, polished by reverence. "She gave everything she had left so I'd know the words."

Cole was quiet for a moment. Then, in the matter-of-fact tone that undid Isandro worse than any argument:

"In the dream, the dragon asked for somebody. Not the doorwarden. Somebody to *witness*. It said, *someone must remember what we were, or the fire is only heat.*" The boy shrugged. "I thought you should know. Perrin says it's rude to keep secrets that heavy."

"Your brother is very wise."

"He snores, though." Cole pulled the blanket tighter and stood, then paused. "Isandro? When we get to the mountain—is the dragon going to let us watch it die?"

Isandro thought of the vision filtered through a child's sleep: gray caldera, a vast body like a collapsed cathedral, a name being repeated like a prayer that had forgotten it was one.

"No," he said, because he had decided it in that moment, and some decisions you made aloud so they became real. "It's going to let us watch it *live*. One more hour. One more day. However long we buy it." He touched the oilcloth over Maren's bones. "The dead are owed passage, and the living are owed their names, and I'm done choosing between them."

Cole nodded gravely, as though this settled some private ledger, and padded back to the wagon.

Isandro sat alone with the lantern's ember-light and the ghost's silence and looked north, where the fells made black teeth against the stars. Somewhere out there, an old, old thing was guttering like a candle in wind, holding a door open with the last of its heat, waiting for a carver's son to stop running.

Three weeks, Kest had said. Maybe four.

He found himself hoping, for the first time in seven years, with his whole chest, that a dragon was stubborn.

Behind him, unnoticed in the wagon bed, the coal in Odile's lantern flared once—bright, brief, answering—and dimmed again, like a heart that had felt itself remembered.
