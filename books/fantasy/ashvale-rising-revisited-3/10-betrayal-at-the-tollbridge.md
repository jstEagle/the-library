## 10. Betrayal at the Tollbridge

The river at Tollbridge ran the color of old pewter, and Isandro hated it on sight.

Not for what it was—a wide, slow thing that carried barges of timber south toward the lowlands—but for what it meant. Rivers meant crossings. Crossings meant tolls, and tolls meant ledgers, and ledgers meant names written down by men who sold them. Seven years of hiding had taught him that the crown rarely found anyone by looking. It found them by reading.

"Two coppers a head," said the bridge-warden, a fat man with a wax-sealed chain and eyes that went over each of them twice. "Four for anything with hooves."

Halla, the weather-singer who passed for a horse trader, counted out coin from a purse she kept knotted to her belt like a second heart. Beside her wagon, the twins—Perrin and Tam, ten years old and identical down to the cowlicks—were pretending not to stare at the water. They were bad at pretending. Dreaming children never learned how to look bored.

Isandro stood at the rear of the little caravan with Maren's bones wrapped in oilcloth across his back and Odile's iron lantern hanging from his hand. The lantern was cold. It was always cold now—the coal inside slept unless Odile woke it—and he had caught himself more than once resting his palm against the metal just to feel something that wasn't fear.

"You're staring at the warden," Odile murmured beside him. She was small, gray-stubbled, with hands that looked like they'd been carved from the same wood as her lantern's handle.

"I'm staring at his ledger."

"Good instinct." She spat into the reeds. "Bad luck."

They crossed in single file. The planks boomed under Halla's wagon wheels, and the twins walked between Isandro and Odile as if the adults were fence posts they'd chosen to stand near. Perrin kept glancing up at him.

"You're sad," Perrin said. Not asking. The dreamers never asked; they reported.

"I'm careful," Isandro said. "It looks similar from the outside."

Tam, walking backward so he could face them both, said, "We dreamed about this bridge last night. There was fire on it."

"There's no fire on it now," Odile said.

"Not yet," Tam said, and turned forward again, satisfied, the way you'd close a book once you knew the ending.

Isandro felt the words settle into his chest like a swallowed stone. He thought about saying something comforting and understood, with the honesty that seven years alone had beaten into him, that he didn't have any. So instead he shifted Maren's bones higher on his shoulders and watched the far bank come closer, and told himself that deadlines were just hope wearing armor.

On the north side of the river stood a coaching inn called the Drowned Bell, all sagging eaves and peeling paint, where Halla negotiated space in the stable yard for the night. The caravan would rest until dawn, then take the high road east toward the mountains—toward the guttering candle-flame the twins had dreamed burning in the caldera, the last drake, the third key.

Isandro should have felt closer to the end of something. Instead, standing in the inn yard while Halla argued about hay prices, he felt the particular dread of a man who has been running long enough to know the sound of his own footsteps.

He heard them now. Hooves. More than two. Coming up the south road at a canter, unhurried, the way riders move when they already know where they're going.

* * *

He spotted the informer an hour later, and hated himself for how long it took.

One of the hired guards—Bramm, a thick-necked ex-soldier who'd joined the caravan three towns back, paid in silver and silence—kept positioning himself where he could see the road. Not watching for trouble. Watching *for* something. And when the riders finally showed themselves at the tree line—six of them, gray cloaks, the crowned-tower badge of the Winnowing offices stitched at the shoulder—Bramm did not shout a warning.

He raised a hand. Two fingers, touched to his brow. A salute.

Isandro was in the stable loft, checking the lashings on Maren's bones, when he saw it through the gap in the boards. His whole body went cold in a way that had nothing to do with evening air.

Six riders. One of them wore his father's face.

Serel Iversen sat his horse at the edge of the yard with the stillness of a man who had never once needed to hurry. Even at this distance, even after everything, Isandro's heart did the stupid, traitorous thing it always did—that lurch, that *father*, that flare of longing like a struck match before the mind could smother it. He's dead. He's dead seven years. That is a mask wearing a dead man's skin, and it is here for you.

And Bramm had waved it in.

Isandro dropped from the loft, landing soft, and crossed the yard at a walk because running would make it real. He found Halla first. She was at the well, and when she saw his face she stopped mid-sentence with the water bucket still swinging.

"How many?" she said. Weather-singers read storms; apparently they read people too.

"Six. Riders. Gray cloaks." He swallowed. "Bramm signaled them. We've been sold."

Something moved behind Halla's eyes—not surprise. Calculation. She set down the bucket with exaggerated care, the way you do when you want very much to throw it.

"The twins," she said.

"I know."

"They'll be taken alive. You understand that. Gifted children aren't burned—they're *registered*. There's a difference, and the difference is a cellar under the citadel and a life spent being cut open politely." She was already moving, untying the wagon's rear panel, pulling out saddlebags. "Odile takes the lantern and goes north with you. I'll take the children out the river side."

"Halla—"

"They're my cargo tonight," she said, in the voice she probably used on horses. "You're carrying bones and fire. I'm carrying the future. Go argue with someone who has time."

There was no time. The riders were dismounting at the yard gate, and Serel's voice carried across the gravel, mild and terrible: "In the name of the Crown's Grace, no one will be harmed who submits to examination. Please remain where you are."

Please. He'd always said please. Isandro remembered that from before—from the real father, the one who'd taught him to whittle and said please to servants and wept at his wife's funeral. The thing wearing his face had kept the manners and lost everything underneath them.

Then everything happened at once.

* * *

Halla did not run. That was the thing Isandro couldn't stop seeing afterward, the thing that woke him for nights: she did not run.

She walked to the center of the yard, planted herself between the riders and the stable, and began to sing.

It wasn't loud. That was the horror of it—it was almost gentle, a low rolling note like wind deciding which way to turn, and the weather above the inn answered. Clouds that had been sitting fat and harmless all evening suddenly had intent. The temperature fell so fast the horses screamed. Then the rain came sideways, then the hail, white and brutal, hammering the yard hard enough to raise bruises on the gravel, and every lantern on the inn's porch guttered and died except one.

Except the iron one, in Odile's hand, where a single coal woke glowing like a drowsy eye.

"Now," Halla shouted through the storm, without turning around. "North wall. *Go.*"

Isandro went. Odile grabbed his sleeve and hauled, and they ran along the stable's shadow with hail cracking off the shingles around them like small furious applause. Behind them he heard the riders' horses panicking, heard Serel's voice—still calm, still *please*—cutting through the din, giving orders Isandro couldn't parse and didn't let himself try to parse, because parsing them would mean stopping.

At the corner of the building, Isandro made the mistake of looking back.

He saw Halla on her knees now, arms spread, hair whipping in the gale she was spending herself to hold. He saw the twins being shoved through the stable's river-side door by a groom Halla must have bribed weeks ago—he saw Perrin look back, one instant, eyes finding Isandro's across the chaos with the awful certainty of a child who dreams true.

*"Fire,"* Perrin mouthed. Or maybe screamed. The storm ate it.

Then a rider closed the distance, and another, and Halla rose off her knees with blood running from her nose—weather-singing cost, always cost—and threw the storm's whole weight against six men at once. Lightning came down on the yard gate. The world went white, then blue, then dark.

When Isandro's sight returned, Halla was on the ground and not moving, and the rain was stopping, because there was no one left to sing it.

"Keep moving," Odile said, gripping his arm hard enough to bruise. Her voice cracked on the second word. "Isandro. *Keep moving.*"

He kept moving. That was the whole of his heroism that night: he put one foot in front of the other, north, away from the light of the yard, away from Halla's body and the twins' small registered futures, away from the man with his father's face who stood in the middle of the drowning storm and did not even get wet.

Serel looked up, once, across the distance. Through hail and dark and forty yards of screaming night, their eyes met. And Serel Iversen tilted his head—curious, unhurried, a scholar noting a citation—and did not pursue.

He didn't need to. He had what he'd come for. The rest was loose change.

* * *

They walked most of the night. Neither of them spoke until the inn was miles behind and the road had become a track and the track had become a deer path climbing into foothills black with pine.

Odile stopped first. She set the lantern on a boulder, and in its faint sleeping glow her face looked like something excavated.

"The twins," she said.

"I know."

"Do you? Because you haven't said a word since the yard, and I've been telling myself that's shock, but I'm starting to think it's arithmetic. You're counting what you saved. Bones. Lantern. Yourself." Her voice didn't rise. That was worse. "Three items. And you're not counting what got left on the other side of the ledger."

Isandro set Maren's bones down against the boulder with more care than he felt. His hands were shaking. "I ran."

"Yes."

"I always run. Seven years, and it turns out I'm not hiding, I'm just—running slowly. With stops." He laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged, nothing like laughter. "Halla died singing at them. She stood in the open and *spent herself*, and I went out a side door with the merchandise."

"Her merchandise," Odile said quietly. "That's what she called the children. That's what she'd call you, if she were here, before she hit you. You were her cargo too. Getting you out *was* the mission. You completing it is the only thing that makes her death worth anything."

"And the twins?"

Odile didn't answer. The silence stretched, and Isandro found that he preferred any answer to none, even a cruel one, because cruelty at least ended.

"We get them back," he said.

"That's not the mission."

"*We get them back.*" He heard his own voice change—drop into something low and unfamiliar, something with weight behind it. Fury, he realized. Not the hot kind that burns out. The cold kind that sets. Seven years of amputating pieces of himself so he'd be small enough to survive, and it had all been leading here, to this boulder, this lantern, this ledger with its missing lines. "Serel took them because they're leverage. Against me. Against the door. Which means they're alive, which means they can be *found*, which means—"

"Which means you stop running," Odile said. "Finally."

Above them, past the pine crowns, the mountains stood up against the stars—the caldera somewhere in that darkness, Vaskarath somewhere in that darkness, a dying flame and a buried door and a rite that wanted his own bone as its key. Somewhere south, in a cellar under a citadel, two boys who dreamed true were dreaming about all of it right now, and reporting it to each other in whispers, waiting for someone to come.

Isandro picked up Maren's bones. He slung them across his back where they belonged.

"Dawn's in four hours," he said. "We climb in the dark."

Odile lifted the lantern, and for one moment—whether it was the coal or her or grief, he never learned—the iron shell glowed warm as a heartbeat.

"Then let's give the storm something to talk about," she said.

They climbed.
