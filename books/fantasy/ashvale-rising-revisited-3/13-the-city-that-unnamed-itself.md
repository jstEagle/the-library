## 13. The City That Unnamed Itself

The capital had a name once. Everyone knew it the way you know your own hands—without thinking, without effort. Now when travelers asked what city lay ahead, people only said *the capital*, and their mouths did something strange around the space where the old word used to live, like tongues stepping over a missing stair.

Isandro felt it before he saw it: a pressure in his satchel, all the charms gone quiet at once, the way they'd gone quiet in Vell's Crossing the day the hedge-witch screamed. But this was different. This wasn't fear pressing down on the dead. This was something older, wider, woven into the ground itself.

"The whole valley's muffled," Odile said beside him on the road. She carried the iron lantern slung low against her hip, one hand cupped over its seam even though no light escaped. "Feel it? Like wool packed in your ears."

"I feel it." He flexed his carved forearm under its wrappings. The letters there had scabbed into ridges, half an alphabet, half a name, and they ached now with a deep bone-weather ache that had nothing to do with the healing. "The charms hate it here."

Maren's bones rode wrapped in oilcloth across his back, lashed tight. She hadn't spoken since the pass. Ghosts, it turned out, could be tired too.

They came over the last ridge at dusk, and the capital opened below them like a wound that had learned to smile. Ten thousand rooftops. Lantern-light in every window. The river cutting through it black and patient. And above it all, rising from the palace hill, a spire of white stone so clean it looked bleached—as if the whole city had been washed and scrubbed until nothing of itself remained.

"Corvale," Odile said, and then stopped, frowning. "That's not—it isn't called—" Her mouth worked. Her jaw tightened. "It doesn't have a name," she finished, and her voice came out flat and wrong, like a woman reading someone else's words.

"That's what I mean," Isandro said quietly. "Watch."

He tried it himself. He thought: *the city is called* — and reached for the word, and found a smooth blank wall where it should have been. Not forgotten. Not erased from memory the way Ashvale had been erased from maps. Something more surgical than that. He could remember that there had been a name. He could remember remembering it. But the word itself slid away from him like a fish through fingers, and when he tried to hold it, his tongue simply refused to shape the sounds.

"It's in our mouths," he said. "They put it in our mouths."

Odile stared at him. "Who?"

"The crown. When they unnamed the country, they didn't just burn the maps. They went into every throat in the kingdom and took the words out." He touched his own lips, almost expecting to find seams. "Everyone who ever lived here is walking around with a hole where their home used to be."

* * *

They entered the city at dawn, when the gates were crowded with carts and nobody looked twice at a horse trader's daughter and her quiet cousin with a wrapped bundle on his back. Odile had covered the lantern with a flour sack. Isandro had buried Maren's bones' presence as deep as he could—not that anyone could sense a ghost but him, but habits kept you alive.

Inside, the city was worse than the view promised. It wasn't poor. It wasn't broken. It was *polite*. People moved through the streets with the smooth efficiency of ants, buying bread, hauling water, greeting neighbors—and every conversation Isandro passed had the same strange architecture to it. No names for things. He heard a woman tell her son to look at the mountains, and the boy asked which ones, and the mother pointed instead of answering, and neither of them seemed to notice anything odd about that. He heard two merchants argue about a shipment from the coast, and neither could say the sea's name; they said *the water*, *the big water*, *out there*, gesturing eastward with increasing frustration that dissolved, mid-gesture, into placid acceptance, like a fever breaking.

"They don't know they're missing anything," Isandro murmured.

"Why would they?" Odile said. "You can't grieve a word if you can't remember it existed."

But he watched an old man standing in a doorway, watching the eastern sky where the mountains would be if the morning haze lifted, and the old man's mouth moved around nothing, over and over, like a man chewing on a stone he couldn't spit out.

At a fountain in a market square, children played a skipping game, chanting a rhyme. Isandro stopped dead.

*Step and turn and step again,*
*the door is shut, the fire is—*

The rhyme broke there. Every child, in unison, skipped the missing word and landed on the next line as if the gap were part of the song:

*—and we must never say its name.*

"What comes after 'the fire is'?" Isandro asked a girl with braids, crouching to her level.

She looked at him with the frank pity children reserve for slow adults. "Nothing. It's just where the song goes quiet."

"But it used to say something."

Her brow furrowed. For one heartbeat something flickered behind her eyes—a shadow of a shadow—and then it smoothed away and she laughed and skipped off, chanting the silence like it was the best part of the game.

Odile's hand found his arm. "Don't," she said. "I can see you winding up. Don't do anything here. Not yet."

"I'm not doing anything."

"You're breathing like you're about to."

* * *

They spent the day finding the way north. The Doorhouse ruins lay three days beyond the caldera, and the caldera lay beyond the high passes, and the passes began at the far side of this city—the crown had built its capital squarely across the only easy road, whether by accident or design Isandro didn't want to guess. They needed provisions. They needed to move through, not linger.

But the city slowed them anyway. Not the guards—there were fewer soldiers than Isandro expected, the Winnowing apparently needing less enforcement here where no one remembered enough to resist—but the streets themselves, which funneled and turned in ways that seemed almost deliberate, drawing strangers toward the great square at the heart of everything, the one crowned by the white spire.

By late afternoon they stood at the edge of that square without quite meaning to be there.

It was enormous, paved in pale stone, ringed by government buildings whose facades displayed reliefs: farmers harvesting, smiths hammering, ships launching. Every relief showed labor and plenty. None of them showed a single face looking up. And in the center rose the spire, and around the spire's base, carved in letters ten feet tall, ran a line of text that made Isandro's skin crawl precisely because it was so reasonable:

*WE ARE ONE PEOPLE. WE HAVE ALWAYS BEEN ONE PEOPLE.*

Below it, smaller: *Report forgetting. Report remembering. The crown heals all wounds.*

"People queue there," Odile said. A line of citizens snaked from a doorway at the spire's base—ordinary folk, holding little paper slips, waiting with the patience of the well-trained. "What are they reporting?"

"Forgetting, probably." Isandro's voice came out dry. "Or remembering. There's no way to know which is the crime this week."

A bell rang somewhere. The crowd in the square shifted, and a procession emerged from the western colonnade: a dozen gray-robed officials, and behind them, on a low platform wheeled by oxen, a family. A man, a woman, two children. Their wrists were bound with red cord. Their faces weren't terrified—they were *confused*, the particular confusion of people who have been told they've done something wrong and cannot imagine what.

A herald mounted the steps of the spire and unrolled a scroll.

"Citizens. This household of the Weald district has been found in violation of the Memory Statutes. The eldest daughter, aged nine, was observed by her teacher attempting to vocalize a prohibited phoneme cluster during recitation lessons."

The square went very quiet. Somewhere in Isandro's satchel, a charm—a soldier's charm, a dead man's patience—began to tremble.

"The household will undergo corrective silence," the herald continued, "in accordance with statute seven. Let this serve the common peace."

Guards stepped forward with instruments Isandro didn't recognize at first—thin silver rods, curved at the tip. When the first guard touched the nine-year-old's throat, the girl opened her mouth to scream, and no sound came out. Not muffled. *Removed.* The second guard touched the mother. The third, the father. Working methodically, like farriers.

The crowd watched. Nobody shouted. A few people looked away, then looked back, because looking away was also, apparently, something you could be reported for.

Odile's grip on Isandro's arm was iron. "We leave. Now. Through the north colonnade, before the crowd breaks and the guards start checking faces."

He let her pull him. He went. His legs obeyed. But something in his chest had caught fire, and it was not the good kind of fire, and it did not go out when they reached the shadows of the colonnade. It sat there under his ribs like a swallowed coal.

*Corrective silence.* They had done it to a child. A nine-year-old who'd tripped over a sound she couldn't help, some ancestral syllable rising up out of her blood, and they had taken her voice for it. Taken the whole family's voices. And ten thousand people had watched and memorized the lesson: *this is what happens to mouths that misbehave.*

"Odile." His voice sounded strange to him. "How much of the coal is left?"

She stopped walking. Turned. Looked at him with an expression he was learning to read—the flame-tender's arithmetic running behind her eyes, weighing fuel against need, warmth against distance. "Enough to keep it alive for weeks if I'm careful. Enough for one real working if I'm not. Why?"

"No reason yet."

"Liar."

"Yes," he agreed. "Liar."

* * *

They took a room above a chandler's shop near the north gate, paying too much for a bed and a lock. Maren's bones leaned in the corner, and around midnight she finally stirred, her voice surfacing through Isandro's skull like a bubble through dark water.

*This city,* she said. *I knew it when it had a name. I danced in that square, boy. Midsummer, the year before the burning. There were banners in every color the dyers could make, and the fountains ran wine after midnight, and everyone sang the old songs because nobody had thought to forbid them yet.* A pause, long and cold. *They built the spire on the dancing floor. Did you know that? They paved over the place where we were happiest and wrote WE ARE ONE PEOPLE on top of it.*

"I'm sorry," Isandro whispered into the dark.

*Don't be sorry. Be angry. Anger remembers better than grief.* The ghost's voice sharpened. *You've been carrying my bones toward a door for days now, quiet as a thief. But I've been carrying something longer than you've been alive, and I know what a person looks like when they're deciding. You're deciding.*

He lay on the narrow bed and stared at the ceiling and did not answer, because she was right.

He thought about the girl with braids and the skipping rhyme. He thought about the old man mouthing nothing at the eastern sky. He thought about his mother, who had spent seven years—or however many years—keeping a door hidden inside a cottage wall while an entire country forgot its own name, and how she must have walked through markets exactly like these, hearing the silence where the songs used to be, saying nothing, saying nothing, saying nothing, right up until they burned her for it.

Saying nothing hadn't saved her.

*There it is,* Maren said softly. *There's the look.*

"Tomorrow," Isandro said. "We provision tomorrow, and we're through the north gate by noon, and we don't stop until the passes."

*And tonight?*

He closed his eyes. "Tonight I'm going to sleep."

He didn't sleep.

* * *

Dawn came gray and thin. Odile was already awake, repacking the lantern's wrappings, and she took one look at his face and set down the flour sack very slowly.

"No," she said.

"You don't even know what I was going to say."

"I know that face. I've seen that face on people feeding a fire they shouldn't. You're going to do something stupid in that square."

"The rite needs the country's true name." He heard his own voice, calm and terrible, as if it belonged to someone else. "Maren gave me the memory. The words of the opening are threaded through the name—every line of the rite anchors to it. If I can't say the name, I can't open the door. And I can't say the name, Odile. I tried. It's not just forbidden. It's been *cut out*. Out of me, out of you, out of every throat in this kingdom."

"So we find another way. There's always another way."

"Is there? Show me. Because I've been looking since the wall cavity, and every road leads through this." He held up his scarred forearm, the half-carved alphabet ridged under its wrapping. "The keys are a ghost's memory, a name in my bones, and a dragon's breath. Two of those I have or nearly have. The third is dying on a mountain while we argue. And between me and all of it stands a city full of people who physically cannot say the word that opens everything."

Odile was silent. Outside, cart wheels rattled past on the cobbles; a vendor cried the price of early apples, carefully not naming the orchards they came from.

"If you speak it," she said finally, "they'll take you. You understand that. Not maybe. They'll take you, and they'll do to your throat what they did to that child, and then the last Doorwarden's son will die mute in a cell, and the door stays shut forever, and I'll have carried this coal across half the kingdom for nothing."

"Yes."

"And knowing that, you're still—"

"I'm still going to stand in that square," Isandro said, "and say it."

* * *

The square at midmorning was fuller than the day before—market day, stalls ringing the pale stone, the white spire throwing its long clean shadow across the crowd like a sundial measuring something other than time. Isandro left Maren's bones with Odile at the colonnade's edge, wrapped and hidden, and walked out alone into the light with his satchel heavy on his hip and his heart going like a drum in a procession.

He had planned it a hundred times in the night and the plan was simple, which meant it was doomed, which meant it was honest. He would climb the low steps of the spire's base. He would wait for the hush between market cries. And he would say the word.

He reached for it as he walked, testing, the way you test a broken tooth. *The country is called*—

The wall came down. Smooth. Total. His mind skated off it. His tongue lay inert.

But this time he did something different. This time he didn't reach for the word as a word. He reached for it as his mother had taught him, without meaning to teach him, on the nights she whittled by the fire and hummed the Doorwarden's lullaby: he reached for it as *bone-memory*. Not thought. Not language. Shape. The way a charm knows the dead—not by hearing them but by holding the shape their voice left behind.

His forearm throbbed under its wrappings. The half-carved letters woke one by one, aching, singing low.

*Oh,* he thought. *Oh, it's still in me. They cut it out of our mouths, but they couldn't cut it out of the bones. Bones remember what tongues are forbidden to.*

He climbed the steps. A guard near the spire's base glanced at him, decided he was a pilgrim or a fool, and looked away. The crowd churned below—hundreds of faces, thousands, none of them knowing why the air felt thick today, none of them knowing why the children's rhyme had a hole in it, none of them able to ask.

Isandro stood on the lowest step of the white spire, in the shadow of WE ARE ONE PEOPLE, and filled his lungs with the city's muffled air.

He spoke the name of Ashvale.

It came out of him like a blade leaving a body. One word, two syllables, old and round and utterly ordinary—and the square *cracked*. Not the stone. The silence. It broke the way ice breaks on a spring river, a single report running outward from his mouth in a widening ring, and Isandro saw it happen to faces: a fruit-seller freezing mid-cry, her lips shaping a word she hadn't said in seven years; a carter dropping his reins; a woman by the fountain clutching her neighbor's arm and sobbing a name—that was all, just a name, the country's name, over and over, weeping at the taste of it. All across the square, mouths opened. The enchantment didn't fall—it *tore*, and through the tear poured seven years of dammed-up sound, hundreds of voices saying it at once, raggedly, disbelievingly, the word rising off the pale stones like birds off a field.

For one ringing moment, the capital remembered itself.

Then the bells began.

Not the market bells—the spire bells, deep and fast, an alarm pattern. Guards boiled out of the colonnades. The herald's voice cracked across the square: *"SEIZE THE SPEAKER. REPORT REMEMBERING. REPORT REMEMBERING."* And the beautiful tearing sound began to close, the crowd's voices faltering as the enchantment knitted itself shut, word by word, face by face, the fruit-seller's mouth smoothing back into blankness mid-syllable, the weeping woman blinking at her own tears as if she couldn't source them.

Isandro stood on the steps as the first soldiers reached the square's edge, and he did the only thing left to do.

He said it again. Louder. Into the closing silence, into the bells, into the faces of the men running at him with silver rods—*Ashvale, Ashvale*—a hammer blow each time, each word cracking the healing silence open again in a smaller and smaller circle, buying the crowd seconds of remembering, spending himself like coin.

A rod swung for his throat. He ducked; it grazed his jaw, cold as well-water. Hands seized his arms, his satchel, his collar. From the colonnade he saw Odile break cover, Maren's bones clutched to her chest, shoving through the panicking crowd toward him, and he wanted to scream at her to run, to save the bones, to save the lantern, to save the door—

The last thing he saw before they pulled a hood over his head was the fruit-seller. Standing amid the chaos, tears streaming down her face, mouthing the name one final time before the silence sealed over her—holding it in her mouth like a coal, like a promise, like something smuggled.

Then the hood came down, and the world went dark, and the chains went on, and somewhere above him the bells kept ringing the alarm for a man who had committed the only crime that mattered.

He had said the word. Whatever they did to him now, ten thousand people had heard it.

The soldiers dragged him toward the spire, down stairs he couldn't see, into the white stone heart of the machine that had unnamed a country—and the last sound he heard, muffled through the hood, was Odile's voice in the crowd, screaming his name.
