## 14. Prison of Bone and Bureaucracy

The cell smelled of lye and old stone, and Serel Iversen had been awake for thirty-one hours when they finally brought the prisoner down.

He stood at the table in the interrogation room beneath the Vell's Crossing garrison and arranged his instruments with the care of a man setting a dinner service: a ledger, a quill, an inkpot, a taper of tallow candle. The instruments of his trade were not knives. Knives were for amateurs and sadists, and the crown had no use for either. The crown had paperwork.

"Name," he said, when the guards shoved the prisoner into the chair across from him.

The man was gaunt, ash-pale, wrists raw from the irons. He looked up, and Serel felt something shift behind his sternum—a sensation like a floorboard giving way in a house he'd never entered.

"You know my name," the prisoner said. His voice was rough as a whetstone. "You've known it since you rode into the square."

"I know what informants report." Serel opened the ledger to a fresh page. "Isandro Iversen. Charmsmith. Unregistered gift, category three—resonant objects. Confirmed seller of contraband bone-work in the gray market going back four years." He wrote as he spoke, the quill scratching in the quiet. "I am required by protocol to hear it from you regardless."

"Why?" Isandro's mouth twisted. "So I can watch you write it? So it becomes true on paper?"

"So it becomes *recorded*," Serel said. "There is a difference. Truth is what happened. Record is what the crown permits to have happened. My work lives in the space between."

Something flickered across the prisoner's face—not fear. Recognition, almost. As if Serel had said something familiar, something once said to him over a workbench strewn with shavings.

Serel ignored it. He ignored most things that flickered.

* * *

The interrogation proceeded according to form. That was the thing about the Winnowing that outsiders never understood—it was not chaos, it was *bureaucracy*. Every gifted person processed through the same eleven questions. Every confession entered into triplicate. Every execution scheduled fourteen days in advance so that the families could be billed for the wood.

Question one: nature of the gift. Question two: date of first manifestation. Question three: persons aware. Question four: objects produced, with inventory attached—

And there it stopped being form.

Because the inventory sheet lay between them on the table, and on it were listed the items recovered from Isandro's satchel when the riders took him at the capital's edge: forty-one charms, catalogued by Serel's own clerks. Sparrow bone. River-stone. A sliver of antler wrapped in copper wire. And item thirty-seven, described in the clerk's careful hand as *small carved disc, birch, worn smooth, inscription illegible*, which Serel now held under the candlelight and found was not illegible at all.

It was a list of prices. In a cramped, slanted hand he knew the way you know your own reflection—without ever having studied it. *Charm, luck, hare-bone, 3 coppers. Charm, sleep, dove, 5 coppers.* And at the bottom, half-finished, abandoned mid-word: *charm, fath—*

Fath. Father. A charm for a father, begun and never finished.

Serel set the disc down. His hands were steady. His hands were always steady; that was the first thing they'd told him he was good at, back when they found him on the road with no past and perfect penmanship and a face that made widows trust him.

"Item thirty-seven," he heard himself say. "Where did you acquire it?"

"I made it." Isandro's eyes came up, and there was something terrible in them—not hatred. Worse. A kind of patient grief, like a man watching ice crack over a pond he used to skate. "I make all of them. You knew that. You used to sit across from me while I worked and complain that my knife strokes were too deep."

"That is not—" Serel's quill paused above the paper. A drop of ink swelled at its tip and fell, blooming black on the record. "I have no memory of any such—"

"No," Isandro said softly. "You wouldn't."

* * *

They moved him to a proper cell after that, and Serel requested—citing protocol, citing thoroughness, citing nothing he could name—that the confiscated charms be stored in the evidence room adjacent to the prisoner's block rather than the central archive. There was no procedural reason for this. He told himself it was about security. The charms were resonant objects; proximity to their maker might induce confessions.

That was the official logic. He wrote it up in triplicate. The crown loved a justification with a signature on it.

What actually happened was this: on the third night, doing inventory by lamplight, Serel picked up item twelve—a charm of river-clay shaped like a sleeping bird—and heard singing.

Not from the charm. From somewhere behind his own eyes.

*A woman's voice, low, in a kitchen with steam on the windows, singing words that weren't words yet, a lullaby built out of half-remembered names:* doorwarden, doorwarden, keep the flame, *and a small boy's laughter underneath it, and flour on someone's hands—*

He put the charm down. The singing stopped. He stood in the evidence room for a long time, listening to his own heart, which sounded strangely like footsteps approaching a door.

In the morning he filed a report noting that item twelve showed no anomalous properties. It was the first lie he had ever recorded. He noted that too, in a margin so small only he would find it, and then sat looking at the note without understanding why his hand had shaken.

* * *

The interrogations continued. Protocol demanded eleven questions; Serel extended them to twenty, then thirty, citing depth of investigation. The truth was he kept finding reasons to come back.

On the sixth day he brought item nineteen—a fox-jaw charm—and laid it on the table between them without a word.

Isandro looked at it. Then at him. "Do you know what that one does?"

"It is catalogued as a minor resonance object."

"It holds a voice. A dead woman's. She used to sing to her goats." Isandro leaned forward, chains clinking. "Hold it to your ear. Go on. What are they going to do—arrest you?"

Serel should refuse. Procedure stated plainly that unvetted contact with resonant objects constituted a category-two hazard. Procedure had a great deal to say, and Serel had spent seven years letting procedure do his thinking for him, because procedure asked nothing of him except compliance, and compliance cost nothing, and a man with no memories could afford nothing more expensive.

He lifted the charm to his ear.

A goat bleated. Wind moved through dry grass. And beneath it, faint as breath on glass, a woman humming—off-key, unhurried, alive in the way the dead stay alive inside small carved things.

When he lowered it, Isandro was watching him with an expression Serel couldn't catalogue. "Well?" the prisoner asked.

"Livestock sounds," Serel said. "As catalogued."

"You're lying."

"I am *recording*," Serel said, and heard, distantly, how the sentence had changed shape since the first day. "There is a difference."

"There is," Isandro agreed quietly. "My mother taught me that. She said the crown doesn't burn people for magic. It burns them for remembering wrong." He tilted his head. "You'd have liked her. Everyone did. She had this way of making you feel like the rules were just stories everyone agreed to tell, and she knew better ones."

Something cracked. Not loudly. Quietly, the way ice goes—all at once, long after the sound has started.

Serel saw a workbench. Saw shavings curling off a knife blade, pale wood falling away in ribbons. Saw his own hands—younger, whole, unscarred—guiding smaller hands around the grip. *Too deep,* he heard himself say, warm with something he had no word for. *Your strokes are too deep, little wren. The bone remembers everything; you have to give it room to forget.*

He blinked. The interrogation room. The candle. The prisoner watching him with tears standing in his eyes and making no move to hide them.

"What did you call me?" Serel asked. His voice came out wrong. Smaller.

"Nothing," Isandro said. "I didn't say anything."

"You called me—" But the word was already gone, dissolved back into the white where his life before the crown was supposed to be. Seven years of blankness, officially explained as trauma from the border wars. Officially. He had never once questioned the explanation, because questioning was the first thing they'd trained out of him, or perhaps the last thing left to train out of him, because there had been so little else to work with.

"Little wren," Isandro said. "I called you little wren. Except I wasn't the wren, was I. You were. That's what she called you both."

*Both.*

Serel stood up so fast the chair went over backward. He stood with his palms flat on the table, breathing, the fox-jaw charm lying between them like a lit fuse.

"The interview is concluded," he said.

"Serel."

"That is *Inquisitor Iversen*—"

"It's *Father*," Isandro said, "and you know it. Somewhere under all that paper, you know it. You hesitated over my handwriting in the market square. You moved my charms next to my cell. You keep extending these interviews because some part of you would rather sit in a room with the son you can't remember than go upstairs and be the hollow man they built." His voice broke and he mastered it, the way Serel suddenly understood he'd learned to master it—from watching. From inheritance. "Ask me her name. Ask me anything. One question, Father. Off the record."

Serel's hand was on the ledger. Close it, said every protocol he owned. File the report. Request transfer. Request a memory audit—they did those, occasionally, for inquisitors who grew strange; he'd seen men come back from audits smiling and empty, emptier than before.

Or ask the question.

His quill hovered. Ink fell again, another black bloom on the record, another flaw in the document, another piece of evidence that Serel Iversen was coming apart along seams he hadn't known he had.

"One question," he said at last, and hated how much it cost, and how little. "Off the record."

Isandro exhaled, slow, like a man setting down a weight carried a long way. "Her name," Serel said. "The woman who sang. Tell me her name."

And Isandro smiled—the first smile Serel had seen from him, wrecked and radiant—and opened his mouth to answer.

Which was when the alarm bell began to ring.

* * *

It came up through the stones of the garrison, that bell, the way water comes through a cellar wall—everywhere at once, unstoppable, announcing ruin. Boots in the corridor. Shouting. The smell of smoke threading down the stairwell, thin and wrong, sweet almost, like—

Like ember-warmth. Like a coal spent all at once.

Serel was moving before he decided to move, body obeying protocols older than thought, and found himself standing between the open cell door and the prisoner, keys in hand, with no memory of having taken them from the guard.

Down the corridor, a guard lay groaning amid a bloom of spreading flame that burned without fuel, orange-gold, impossibly alive. And beyond him, silhouetted against the firelight, a woman with iron-grey hair stood holding an empty lantern, its door hanging open, its heart gone.

She looked at Serel. At the keys in his hand. At the son chained behind him.

"Well," Odile said. "Are you going to open that door, or do I need to find another way to spend everything I have?"

Behind him, Isandro had risen, chains taut, eyes wide—on the fire, on the empty lantern, on his father standing at the threshold of a cell with the means of escape in his hand and seven years of nothing where his soul should be.

"Father," Isandro said again, quieter now. "Please."

The bell kept ringing. Somewhere above, officers were shouting orders; somewhere below, the fire was eating the records, and Serel thought—with the first truly free thought of his manufactured life—that maybe it was right that the burning started with the paperwork.

He turned the key.

The lock clicked open like a word finally remembered.
