## 15. Odile Breaks the Lantern

The coal had been with Odile for eleven years, and she had never once let it out of arm's reach.

She'd slept with it strapped against her sternum, inside three layers of iron and wool, through winters that killed mules and summers that cracked riverbeds. She had starved rather than trade it. She had watched a man in the Saltmark offer her a house, a name, and a pardon for it, and she had spat at his boots and walked into the marsh with nothing but the lantern and the shirt she wore. Everyone who knew her knew the rule: the coal did not burn for anything. The coal was not for spending. The coal was a life, the last ember of a creature she had loved and failed, and keeping it alive was the only penance she had ever been permitted to carry.

She stood in the shadow of the gaol's outer wall and told herself all of this, the whole litany, the way you tell yourself the reasons a rope holds before you trust it with your weight.

Inside, they were preparing to burn Isandro Iversen.

* * *

She'd heard it from a guard who talked too much, the way guards do when they think the woman hauling ash barrels is nobody. Execution at dawn, he said, cheerful as a man discussing breakfast. The inquisitor himself had ordered it moved up — no waiting for the assizes, no paperwork. The one with the witch's name carved into his arm. The one who shouted a dead word in the square and cracked the silence like a plate.

*Ashvale.* She'd felt it from the market square, that ringing moment when the word went through the crowd like a fishhook through water. Everyone had felt it. Half of them hadn't known what they were feeling. They'd just stood there with their mouths open, tasting a name they couldn't keep, and then the soldiers came and the taste was gone and everyone went back to pretending their tongues had always been that empty.

Odile had not pretended. Odile had stood in the ash-barrel line with her heart hammering and thought: *he can say it. He carries the fire's memory and he can say it.*

And now they were going to burn him for it at dawn, in the same square where he'd cracked the silence, and they'd do it with pitch and oil and ordinary fire, the cheap kind, the kind that leaves nothing behind but a smell.

She set the lantern down on the frozen mud behind the gaol and looked at it.

The iron was old, patched twice, dented where she'd dropped it running from the Saltmark. Through the vents, the coal glowed its faint stubborn orange, no bigger than a walnut, no brighter than a dying hearth. Eleven years she'd fed it nothing, because it needed nothing — it was dragon-fire, and dragon-fire didn't eat fuel the way ordinary fire did. It ate meaning. It burned because a dragon had willed it to burn, and Vaskarath — she'd learned his name later, from a peddler who'd heard it from a dying shepherd — had willed it to burn a long, long time.

She could feel it the way she'd always felt it, a small warm hand pressing against her breastbone from the inside. *Still here. Still here. Still here.*

"Not for long," she said out loud, and her breath fogged, and the coal flickered as if it had heard.

* * *

She'd gone over the gaol's layout for two days, hauling ash, hauling water, hauling the bucket of night-soil nobody else wanted. Two gates. A yard with a watch fire — ordinary fire, tended by bored men. The cells below ground, six of them, iron-barred, reached by one stair. Isandro was in the last cell, the one they gave to prisoners they expected to die soon, because it was coldest and farthest from help.

The problem was never getting him out. The problem was that the moment he was out, the alarm would go up, and there were forty soldiers in the garrison and two of her and one of him, and he was still limping from the interrogation. She'd watched him being dragged back down the stair on the third day and had bitten through the inside of her own cheek to keep from doing something stupid.

So: a diversion. And there was exactly one thing in Ashvale that could pull forty soldiers out of a garrison at once, and she was holding it in her hands.

"There are other ways," she told the coal.

The coal glowed.

"There are. There's oil. There's the granary — I could fire the granary, it's thatch and dry grain, it'd burn half the town and nobody would ever know it was me."

The coal glowed, patient, the way Vaskarath had been patient at the end, when she'd stood in the wreck of the firedrake's roost with her hands full of nothing and her orders useless and the hunters coming up the valley, and the great head had turned to her — not in anger, never in anger — and breathed one last ember onto the ground at her feet. *Keep this,* the gesture had said. *Someone should.*

She'd spent eleven years deciding that *someone* meant her, and only her, and that keeping it meant never, ever letting go.

"You're a coward," she told herself. "You know that? Eleven years of calling it penance, and it's just a way of never having to give anything back."

The coal did not disagree. That was the worst of it. Dragon-fire was honest in a way she had never managed to be.

* * *

She went in at the change of the watch, when the yard was at its emptiest and the new sentries were still finding their feet. She'd spent her last coin on a guard's cloak off a drunk, and it fit her shoulders badly, and she walked with her chin down and her bucket of ash swinging, the way nobody looks twice at a woman doing the worst job in the building.

The stair down was guarded by one man, young, chewing something, bored past the point of vigilance. She'd planned a story — orders from the inquisitor, a message for the prisoner — but when she got close she saw his eyes were half-closed and his spear was leaning against the wall, and she simply walked past him and down, and he never moved.

The cells breathed cold and iron. She found Isandro at the far end, sitting with his back against the stone, and for one horrible second she thought they'd already killed him, because he was so still and so gray. Then his eyes opened.

"You shouldn't be here," he said. His voice was wrecked. One eye was swollen nearly shut. "Odile. You *shouldn't* be here."

"Probably not." She crouched at the bars. Up close she could see what the interrogation had done — the burn marks on his forearms over the carved letters, the way he held his left hand. Something in her chest went hard and hot. "Can you walk?"

"I can walk."

"Can you run?"

He almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, cracked at the edges. "I can run."

She took out the two iron picks she'd filed from a hinge over three days of ash-hauling and went to work on the lock. It was cheap work, garrison iron, and it surrendered in less than a minute, and then the door swung open and Isandro Iversen stood up out of the dark of his cell like a man rising from a grave he'd been lying in politely.

"They moved the execution up," he said. "Dawn. I heard them."

"I know. That's why it's tonight." She handed him the guard's cloak. "Listen to me. There's going to be a fire."

He looked at her, and then his gaze dropped to the lantern at her hip, and she watched him understand it — watched it land in his face like a stone in still water. He was a bone-charmer; he knew what things cost.

"No," he said.

"It's the only thing big enough."

"No. Odile. *No.* That's the last of it. That's — you told me what it is. That's eleven years. That's the drake's own—"

"And it's a walnut-sized coal in a tin lantern," she said, "and it was never going to rekindle a country. You told me that yourself. It takes the dragon's last breath and a Doorwarden and a name carved in bone, and I'm none of those things. All it's ever been is a nightlight I've been carrying so I could feel useful." She heard her own voice shake and hated it. "Let it be useful *once.*"

"Odile—"

"They're going to burn you at dawn." The words came out flat and hard. "In the square. I've watched them burn people, Isandro. I watched them do it in the Saltmark and I did nothing, because the coal wasn't for spending, because I had my rule, and I have regretted every single day of the last eleven years, and I am not doing it again. Not for you. Not for anyone. Now put the cloak on."

He put the cloak on.

* * *

The yard was the hard part. One sentry by the gate, two by the watch fire, and the garrison proper beyond, sleeping forty men who would wake to bells. Odile walked Isandro to the shadow of the woodshed — the guards' woodshed, stacked to the roof with split pine and pitch-pine kindling, because garrisons are always cold and never think about what they're stacking.

She knelt and opened the lantern.

The coal sat in its bed of ash, small and orange and alive, and eleven years pressed down on her hands all at once. She thought of the roost. She thought of the great wings folded like a burnt banner, of the eye the size of her head finding hers and holding it, unblinking, while the world's last firedrake decided to trust a human girl with the only piece of himself that would outlast him. She thought of every cold night the coal had gotten her through — not with heat, exactly, but with the fact of it. *Something true is still burning. Something true is still burning.*

"Thank you," she whispered to it. "For staying. I know you stayed."

Then she tipped it into the kindling, and the world's most precious fire did the most ordinary thing in the world: it caught.

That was the joke of dragon-fire, the thing no one believed until they saw it. It didn't roar. It didn't need oil or breath or bellows. It simply *agreed* to burn, and pine and pitch-pine agreed right back, and inside ten heartbeats the woodshed was a wall of orange, and inside twenty the heat was pushing her back across the yard, and the sentries were screaming, and the bells were going, and forty soldiers came out of the garrison like kicked ants.

"Go," she said, and grabbed Isandro's wrist, and ran.

* * *

They went over the muck-wall at the north end where the palisade had rotted, Isandro boosting her up and then hauling himself after with a sound he swallowed before it became a scream. Behind them the garrison was chaos — buckets, shouting, the watch fire scattered, everyone staring at the beautiful impossible blaze where the woodshed had been. Nobody looked at two figures dropping into the frozen ditch beyond the wall. Why would they? The fire had all their eyes. Fire always does.

They ran the first mile without speaking. Then the town was behind them and the road bent north toward the passes, and Isandro's leg gave out, and they stopped in the lee of a drystone wall, both of them bent double, breath smoking.

Odile looked back. The glow over Vell's Crossing was enormous, a second sunrise sitting on the wrong horizon. Somewhere in that light were the twins, in a cell somewhere, and she made herself look at it, made herself feel the whole weight of it — the coal spent, the twins not freed, the weather-singer dead in the mud at the tollbridge — because she refused to let this night be only a rescue. It was a payment against a debt, and the debt was still enormous.

"It's gone," Isandro said quietly. He was looking at her empty hands. "Odile, it's gone. Everything you carried."

She turned her palms over. They were still warm. They would be warm for an hour, maybe two — the last heat of the last drake, dissipating into the night air like everything else.

"No," she said. "Not everything."

He frowned at her.

"You're still carrying the memory," she said. "Maren's memory. The rite. The name on your arm. And somewhere in the high mountains there's an old dragon who hasn't quite finished dying." She pushed off the wall and stood, and her legs held, and her voice held, which surprised her. "The coal was a piece of the fire. You're carrying the fire's *story.* Turns out I've been keeping the wrong thing for eleven years. Or maybe," and here her voice did shake, just a little, "maybe I was keeping it until I could hand it to someone who'd know what to do with it."

Isandro looked at her for a long moment. The swollen eye, the carved arm hidden under the stolen cloak, the man who had shouted a forbidden word in a silent square because silence was the one thing he couldn't abide.

"The passes are three days' climb," he said. "We have no fire, no food, and they'll have riders on the north road by morning."

"Then we'd better not take the north road."

Something moved in his face — the ghost of the terrible cracked smile. "The weather-singer used to say there was a shepherd's track. Over the Shoulder, past the old cairns. Too steep for horses."

"Then it's too steep for horses," Odile said. "Come on. We're burning moonlight."

They climbed.

* * *

The high passes took them the way the sea takes swimmers — slowly, then all at once. By the second day the road was a rumor and the wind was a fact, and Odile learned what a person was without her coal. Lighter, she discovered. Emptier. Like a house after the funeral, when everyone says how well you're holding up. She caught herself reaching for the lantern at her hip a dozen times an hour and finding nothing, and each time the nothing said the same thing: *you spent it, you spent it, you spent it on a man you've known three weeks.*

And each time, she looked over at Isandro — limping, feverish, jaw set like a man carving his own name into the world whether the world liked it or not — and each time the nothing said something else, quieter: *you'd spend it again.*

On the third night, in the mouth of a cave above the snow line, Isandro's fever finally won. He went down onto the stone with his carved forearm clutched to his chest, and his skin was furnace-hot, and his lips were moving around words she couldn't catch — fragments of the rite, maybe, or names, or just the mind burning its own fuel to keep going.

Odile knelt over him and did the only thing left in her keeping.

She put her bare hands on the ruined arm, over the half-finished letters, and she thought about fire. Not the coal — the coal was ash on the wind over Vell's Crossing, and she'd made her peace with that, or close enough. She thought about what fire *was.* Vaskarath's fire had never been about heat. It had been about will. *Burn because I say burn. Endure because I say endure.* She was nobody's Doorwarden, but she had tended one true thing for eleven years, and tenderness, she was beginning to suspect, was also a kind of flame.

"Stay," she told him, the way she'd told the coal, every night, for a decade. "Still here. Still here. *Stay.*"

Outside, far to the north, past the cairns and the Shoulder and the whole white spine of the world, something flickered on the horizon.

At first she took it for a star. But stars don't pulse. Stars don't gutter, slow and labored, like a candle in wind — like a heart deciding, breath by breath, whether to keep going.

Odile stood in the cave mouth with her hands still warm and watched the light gutter, and understood what she was seeing.

The last drake was still alive.

And he was dying faster than they could climb.
