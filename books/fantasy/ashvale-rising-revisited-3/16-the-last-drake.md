## 16. The Last Drake

The mountain had been breathing long before we saw it.

Odile felt it first—she'd been carrying her lantern like a relic for so many years that she could read fire the way I read bone. She stopped on the switchback trail, wind tearing at her gray braid, and held the iron cage up as if it were a compass needle.

"It's pulling," she said. "The coal's leaning north. Toward the peak."

"Coal doesn't lean."

"This one does." Her voice was strange, stripped of its usual dryness. "It's been seven years since it moved at all. It knows where home is."

We climbed through the night and most of the next day, up trails that stopped being trails and became suggestions, through pine into scree into nothing but black rock and a sky so close I felt I could bruise my knuckles on it. Maren's bones rode against my back in their oilcloth bundle, light as kindling and heavy as a debt. My forearm throbbed under its wrappings—the half-finished alphabet carved into my own flesh, letters I still didn't fully understand, fever-scarred now instead of bleeding.

Somewhere below us, Serel was climbing too. I tried not to think about that. I failed at regular intervals.

* * *

We smelled the caldera before we crested the rim—a smell like a forge left cold for decades, like struck flint and old smoke soaked into stone. Odile went pale beneath her windburn.

"That's dragon," she whispered. "That's what my grandmother's stories smelled like."

I'd imagined this moment a hundred times since the twins' dream: a vast shape coiled in the crater's heart, wings spread against the stars, an eye like a banked furnace opening to regard us. What we found instead nearly broke me.

Vaskarath lay on his side in the bowl of the caldera like a ship run aground, and he was dying the way a candle dies—not all at once, but gutter by gutter. His scales, which must once have burned copper and bronze, had gone the color of old ash, dull and flaking. His wings were folded against his flank, and even folded I could see they were ruined—membranes thin as cloth left too long in the sun, translucent, stitched with holes where the fire had gone out of him and never come back. When he breathed—and he breathed perhaps once a minute, each breath a long laboring sound like a bellows full of gravel—his whole enormous body shuddered with the effort of it.

He was smaller than I'd expected. That was the cruelest part. The twins' dream had shown him mountainous, a horizon with teeth. The reality was a drake maybe forty feet from snout to tail-tip, starved down to the architecture of his own skeleton, so that what remained was mostly suggestion. A monument to something that used to be enormous.

Odile sank to her knees on the rim of the crater. The lantern swung from her hand, and inside it the coal—that single stubborn coal she'd kept alive through seven years of hiding, through the Winnowing, through the tollbridge and the cells and everything—glowed brighter than I had ever seen it. Straining toward him. Like a dog at the end of a rope.

"He's real," she said. "All this time I told myself I was keeping a coal alive for a country. I was keeping it alive for *him*." She looked up at me, and her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "Isandro. He's the last one. If he goes out—"

"I know."

"No, you don't. You know what the door needs. You don't know what the land needs. A country without its fire isn't a country. It's just territory with amnesia." She stood, wiped her face roughly with her sleeve, and started down the slope into the caldera, careless of the loose rock. "Come on. We're not letting him die alone in a hole."

* * *

Up close, Vaskarath smelled of cold iron and the far end of a long illness. His eye tracked us as we approached—one eye, amber gone muddy, the size of a cartwheel and clouded like a riverstone. It fixed on Odile first. On the lantern.

His nostrils flared. The breath that came out of him was warm, barely, the way a hearthstone stays warm hours after the fire dies.

"You," he said.

His voice didn't boom. It rasped, low and cracked, a voice worn down to the grain like mine after a week of silence. But it carried anyway, the way truth carries—it found the bones behind my ears and hummed there.

"You carry fire that was taken from one of us." The great eye shifted to Odile. "Whose?"

Odile's throat worked. "A drake called Seryth. Eleven years ago. The hunters cornered her in the Fenmarsh. I—I was supposed to help her escape. I kept her coal instead. I've kept it ever since."

"Seryth." Something moved through the ruined face—not quite a smile; dragons probably don't smile—but the eye softened the way embers soften. "She always flew ahead of the others. Too fast to guard her back." A pause, one laboring breath. "You have kept her well. Eleven years is a long vigil for a stranger."

"I wasn't a stranger. I just hadn't met her yet."

The eye turned to me then, and I understood why men in old stories went mad or holy when dragons looked at them. There was no cruelty in that gaze. There was something worse: complete attention. He looked at me the way I looked at bone when I carved—reading what was written there, what had been written by every year of the life that grew it.

"And you are the carver," he said. "The son of the Doorwarden. I can smell the rite on you. Half-cut and fevered." The nostril nearest me flared again. "And you carry dead woman's bones who are not finished leaving."

"Maren," I managed. "She remembers Ashvale. Before the erasure."

"Everyone remembers Ashvale." The words came out flat, and then the great head shifted, grinding against the rock, and there was something in his voice I hadn't heard before and took a moment to name. It was grief. "They remember it the way you remember a word on the tip of your tongue. They feel the shape of it and cannot say it. I remember it the other way. I remember it *whole*. Every road. Every harvest festival. Every name of every firedrake who ever lit the beacons from here to the coast." The eye closed, opened. "I am the last vessel of it. And vessels break, carver. I can feel myself going out. Not tonight. Perhaps not tomorrow. But soon, and when I go, everything I hold goes with me."

The wind moved across the caldera rim, and somewhere above us a stone clattered down the scree—small, probably nothing, probably the mountain settling. Probably.

"The twins dreamed you were bigger," I said, because I couldn't think of anything wise to say, and small true things seemed safer than large false ones.

"I was." No shame in it. Just fact. "Fire makes us large, carver. Take our fire and we shrink to fit what's left." He drew another of those terrible slow breaths. "You came for my last breath. The ghost told you the rite. The rite is true. One breath, given freely, will light the Heartfire—if it reaches the door while I still live. After I die, my breath is only breath."

"Then we should hurry."

"We should." The amber eye regarded me steadily. "But I did not survive eleven hundred years by giving away my final coin for free. Sit, carver. Both of you. I have a price, and it is not gold, and it is not service, and you will not like how small it sounds and how heavy it is."

* * *

We sat. Odile set the lantern between us, and the coal leaned toward the dragon like a compass finding north, and Vaskarath told us the price.

"Witness," he said.

"Witness?" Odile frowned. "You want us to watch you die?"

"I want someone to *carry* it." The great head lifted—an effort that made my chest ache to watch, tendons standing out like bridge cables under ash-gray hide. "Understand what I am, carver. I am not merely the last drake. I am the last *library*. Everything the erasure took from your people—the names, the borders, the songs, the reason the Doorhouse was built and who built it and why the door opens downward—all of it lives in me because dragons do not forget. It is our nature. We are made of memory the way you are made of water. And when I die, if no one has received it, it dies with me, and even if you relight the Heartfire, you will relight it over an empty land. Fire without history is just heat. Your people would wake remembering the word Ashvale and knowing nothing else. They would be born again into strangers."

The caldera was very quiet. Even the wind seemed to be listening.

"So I offer you my breath freely—freely, understand, that is the only currency the rite accepts. But first you must take the rest of me. The history. All of it. Someone must open themselves and let eleven hundred years pour through them and *keep* it. Carry it past my death. Be the vessel that survives the vessel." The eye fixed on me, and I realized with a lurch that he had known which of us it would be before we ever crested the rim. "It cannot be the flame-tender. She already carries a fire; two flames in one vessel burn the vessel out. It must be the carver. The one who already writes on his own bones. The one whose mother was Doorwarden, whose blood is bound to the door, whose craft is literally the preservation of voices in small hard things."

My mouth had gone dry. "How much history are we talking about?"

"All of it." For the first time, something almost like humor moved through the ruined voice. "Eleven hundred years, carver. Every king and every kitchen maid. The founding of the Doorhouse. The true name of the first Doorwarden—which, I will tell you now so you may dread it properly, is not the name your mother used. The reason the crown feared the door enough to erase a whole country rather than let it stand. Why the door opens downward. What waits in the buried heart besides fire." A breath. "And the faces. Carver, you will carry the faces of everyone who ever lived and died believing this land was worth naming. When you close your eyes for the rest of your life, you will see them. That is the price. Not pain. Not years. *Room*. You must make room in yourself for a country."

Odile was staring at me. "Isandro. You don't have to—"

"Yes, I do." I heard my own voice, steady, and was distantly surprised by it. "Maren gave me her memory and it nearly drowned me, and that was one woman's worth. This is—" I laughed, and it came out wrong. "This is a whole country's worth. He's asking me to become a library that walks."

"I am asking you to become what your mother was," Vaskarath said quietly. "Did you think Doorwarden meant only keeper of a door? A door is nothing, carver. Wood and iron. The Doorwardens were the land's rememberers. That is why only they could open it. The door answers to the one who holds what the door protects." He settled his chin back onto the stone, exhausted by the effort of speech. "Your mother knew. It is why she hid the kit in the wall and not the map alone. She was not hiding tools, carver. She was hiding an inheritance. And inheritances," the amber eye glinted, "are never given. Only accepted."

* * *

We made camp that night in the caldera's lee, in the crater's shadow, close enough to hear the dragon breathe—once a minute, then once every two minutes, each interval a little longer, a candle counting itself down.

I didn't sleep. I sat with my back against warm stone and turned it over, the way I turn a charm before carving: looking for the flaw, the crack, the reason to refuse.

Here was the flaw: I had spent seven years making myself smaller. Selling whispers, never voices. Refusing a child her brother's echo because hearing it might draw notice. Amputating pieces of myself one careful cut at a time, until the man remaining was mostly scar tissue and habit. And now the last dragon in Ashvale was offering to reverse the whole surgery—to fill me back up with everything I'd spent a lifetime avoiding. Every name. Every face. Every grief the country had been forced to forget.

Every grief *I'd* been forced to forget.

Because that was the thing I kept circling and wouldn't land on: the erasure hadn't only taken the country's name. It had taken mine. Iversen. Son of a Doorwarden. Brother to no one, cousin to no one, friend to no one—because friends required a self to offer, and I'd sold mine off in gray-market increments. The Winnowing hadn't needed to burn me. It had simply waited for me to finish the job myself.

Vaskarath was offering the opposite of the Winnowing. He was offering to make me enormous.

Across the ashes, Odile sat feeding splinters of dead scrub-wood to a tiny cookfire, keeping her lantern propped where the coal could see the dragon. She caught me watching.

"You're going to say yes," she said.

"I'm going to say yes."

"Good." She fed the fire another stick. "For what it's worth, I think your mother would have done it. And I think she'd have hated that you had to."

"That's the most comforting thing anyone's said to me in seven years."

Above us, the sky was doing something I'd never seen—no clouds, no moon, just stars in such density that the dark between them looked deliberate, designed, like the negative space in one of my mother's carvings. I thought about the letter in the wall cavity. *Keep the passage. Keep the memory. One is nothing without the other.* I'd read those words fifty times and only now, sitting under an obscene number of stars with a dying dragon breathing behind me, did I finally understand them.

My mother hadn't been a keeper of doors. She'd been a keeper of rooms—the room inside herself where a whole country could live.

I got up, walked to the great ash-colored head, and stood where the amber eye could find me.

"Yes," I said. "I accept."

The eye closed. Opened. And in it, for the first time, I saw the fire—not much, a spark, a pilot light, but *there*, patient as a seed.

"Then lie down, carver," Vaskarath said, "and open whatever you have been keeping shut. This will not be gentle. Memory with nowhere to go becomes weight, and I have eleven hundred years of it, and I have been holding it alone for a very long time." The ruined wing shifted, ash-cloth stirring in the wind. "When it is done, you will take my breath into the bone you have carved, and you will run for the Doorhouse, and you will not look back at what remains on this caldera floor. Swear it."

"Why does the swearing matter?"

"Because you will want to stay. Everyone wants to stay, at the end. And the breath must reach the door while my heart still beats." The eye dimmed, gathering itself. "Swear, Isandro Iversen. Son of Elara. Last Doorwarden of Ashvale."

My mother's name. Her *true* name—the one she hadn't used, the one the letter never spoke. Hearing it in the dragon's cracked voice did something to me that the fever hadn't, that the carving hadn't. It finished opening something.

"I swear."

"Then we begin," said Vaskarath, "with the founding of the Doorhouse—in the year the sea ate the western shore, when your ancestors came to my grandfather begging for a wall between the living and the dead—"

And he breathed out, and the breath was not air.

It was a city. It was a harbor full of masts. It was a woman singing in a language I had never heard and understood completely, and a boy whittling a whistle on a doorstep, and snow falling on a beacon tower, and a king's funeral, and a market day, and a flood, and a wedding, and a war, and a peace, and a child's name spoken over a cradle—

It poured into me like a river into a dry bed, and I felt myself filling, filling, becoming enormous, becoming a country, and the last thing I registered before the weight of it took me under was Odile's hand gripping mine, and the coal in the lantern flaring bright as dawn—

—and far below us, down the mountain, down the dark roads, torches. Many torches. Climbing.
