## 18. My Father at the Threshold

The orders came at dawn, written in the Inquisitor-General's own hand and sealed with the crowned fist of the regency: *the traitor Iversen is to be put to death at the Doorhouse ruins. No trial. No transport. Burn him where he stands.*

Serel read it three times on horseback while the column clattered behind him up the mountain road, and each time his eye snagged on the same detail, small and stupid: the name. *Iversen.* His own name. He had never asked why a man with no family record, no birth entry, no history before eleven years ago should carry the same surname as the bone-charmer he was hunting. Questions were not his trade. Obedience was.

Except lately obedience had developed a texture. A grain. Like running his thumb along wood and finding it rougher than he remembered.

He had filed the reports himself after Vell's Crossing—the interrogation of the charm-sellers, the sweep of the market—and every page of his own handwriting had felt faintly wrong to him, as if someone else had held the pen and done an adequate imitation. And there was the matter of the charm they'd taken off the twins before the twins escaped their cage, which should not have been possible, but boys who dream true are slippery, and Serel tried not to think about how quietly he'd arranged the locks.

The charm sat now in his breast pocket. A sliver of sheep bone, crudely carved, worth nothing by any assay. He had catalogued it as evidence. Evidence did not belong in a breast pocket, over the heart. He knew that. He had not moved it.

*Whittle away from yourself,* the carving on it said, in a hand he recognized the way you recognize your own face in a window at night—suddenly, wrongly, completely. *Thumb behind the blade. There. Good lad.*

Good lad.

Serel had stood in his quarters holding that scrap of bone for the better part of an hour, feeling something crack like river ice in spring, something enormous shifting beneath him, and he had reported for duty the next morning precisely on time because reporting for duty was the only thing he knew how to do when the ground opened.

Now the column crested the pass and the Doorhouse ruins spread below them in the caldera's shadow—a broken ring of scorched foundation stones, a threshold still somehow intact after seven years of erasure and fire, black against the snow. Two figures knelt there. One was thin and bloodstained and unmistakably the charmer. The other was a woman with an iron lantern hanging empty at her belt.

And beside the charmer, laid out with terrible care on the old threshold, a skeleton wrapped in travel cloth.

"Sir?" said Lieutenant Hask, reining up beside him. "Shall we take them alive? The General's orders specify—"

"I know what the orders specify." His voice came out flat and correct. "Form a line. Hold."

Hask hesitated. "Hold, sir?"

"Hold."

Down at the threshold, the charmer drew a knife and set it against his own forearm, and Serel's stomach dropped through seventeen years of nothing. He watched the boy—he could no longer see anything but a boy, bloodied and deliberate—begin to cut. Watched him speak words in an alphabet Serel's eye should not have recognized and did, instantly, wholly, the way you recognize a lullaby sung wrong but sung.

*Doorwarden's cant.* He knew Doorwarden's cant. He had kissed a woman awake with it murmured against her hair. He had—

The threshold lit.

Not flame. Something older than flame, a light like the memory of fire, gold shot through with green, rising out of the stone in a slow column, and the door that was not a door swung open *downward*, into the mountain's heart, into a depth full of warmth and vast slow breathing, and the light touched Serel's face—

—and the dam did not crack. It simply ceased to have ever been there.

*Snow on the cottage roof. Juniper smoke. A woman singing in cant, low, her hands shaping the melody the way other hands shape bread—"Hush now, the door knows our name, hush now, the fire remembers"—and she is pressing a whittling knife into small careful hands and saying, away from yourself, love, always away from yourself. Her name is Elke. Her name is Elke and she is the Doorwarden and she smells of cedar shavings and she died believing you would come home.*

*A boy at the kitchen table with his tongue between his teeth, carving a duck from pine, badly, gloriously. Isandro. Isandro laughing so hard at his own crooked duck that he snorts, and you laugh too, and your wife says the two of you are hopeless, hopeless, and the fire in the hearth leans toward her when she speaks as if it loves her, because it does, because everything living does.*

*Then the men in gray. Then the questions that go on for days. Then the table with straps. Then a voice explaining, patiently, kindly, that Serel Iversen died in the purge of the Doorhouse, that his records show he aided a fugitive, that what remains will be remade—and the pain, and then the merciful white nothing, and then waking with eleven years of clean empty room where a life had been, and a uniform that fit perfectly, and orders that always made sense until today.*

Serel did not remember dismounting. He was walking down through the snow toward the threshold with his helmet under his arm and no memory of taking it off, and Hask was shouting behind him—*"Sir! Sir, the line, sir!"*—and the world had narrowed to two figures by a burning door and a name rising in his throat like water finding a well.

Isandro saw him first. Of course he did; the boy had spent seven years learning to see hunters coming. Serel watched recognition move across his son's face—not surprise, not anymore. They had met in the cells. He had asked questions through a grille with a voice gone hoarse for reasons his report attributed to dust. Dust. He had sat three feet from his son and felt something straining against the inside of his skull like a bird against glass, and he had called it hatred, because hatred was a permitted explanation.

"It's open," Isandro said. His forearm was bleeding freely down onto the threshold stones, and he didn't seem to feel it. Behind him the door went down and down, warm light climbing out of it like something patient finally standing up. "It was never a way out. It goes to the Heartfire. It goes *down*, Father."

Father.

The word went through Serel the way the light had—no resistance at all. He had been hollow for eleven years and now he was being filled, fast, painfully, the way blood returns to a slept-on limb, all pins and needles and ache, and he found he was crying without any decision to do so, tears freezing on a face that wore his father's sternness and his mother's mouth and had worn them, all this time, for strangers.

"Elke," he said. It was the only word he trusted himself to carry.

Isandro's face broke. "You remember her."

"She sang the door to sleep." His voice was wrecked, unfamiliar, entirely his own. "Every night. She said the house was a living thing and doors were its eyelids. God—I used to think she was being fanciful. She was teaching you catechism and I thought—" He stopped. Steadied. Eleven years of discipline turned out to be good for exactly one thing: standing upright while the world ended. "She made me swear I'd never touch the craft. She said the crown would come for Doorwardens first, and if it ever did, I was to take you south and forget her name before they could take it from you. And I stayed to hold the passage. I stayed. That was the bargain and I broke it, because they took me anyway, and they took my memories of her out of my head like teeth."

Behind him, steel sang. Hask's line was forming up on the ridge, forty riders, torches being lit even though the sun was up—orders were orders, and the General had been explicit about burning.

"The woman," Serel said quickly. "Odile. Take her and go down. Now."

"Not without—"

"*Isandro.*" And there it was, the father-voice, the one that had once stopped a six-year-old mid-mischief across a whole room, surfacing from the wreckage unbidden. "I have eleven years of service and a rank and forty men who will hesitate exactly as long as I tell them to. That buys you minutes. Get her down the stairs."

Isandro stared at him. Seven years of running had taught that face never to believe rescue, and Serel ached for every lesson it had taken to teach it. But the boy—no, not a boy, a man now, a man carved by loss into something sharp and fine—turned and spoke low to Odile, and the woman shouldered the lantern and went to the threshold where the ghost-bones lay, and together they began to lower themselves into the light.

Serel turned to face his own line.

Hask met him halfway up the slope, visor up, confused. "Sir, the traitor—"

"The traitor is in the wind, Lieutenant." Calm. Correct. Every syllable a stone laid in a wall. "The Doorhouse site is unstable. We withdraw to the tollbridge and hold for the General's reinforcement. Those are my orders."

"Your orders say—"

"My orders," Serel said, very quietly, "are eleven years of lies wearing my dead friend's face, and I am finished carrying them. Withdraw the line, Hask. That is the last order I will give you as your superior, and I would take it as a personal kindness if you took it."

Something flickered behind Hask's eyes—the arithmetic every soldier does, weighing a mad order against a mad officer—and then, blessedly, the man's gaze slid past Serel's shoulder to the door standing open on the mountainside, pouring impossible light, and the arithmetic came out the way Serel needed it to. Some things you cannot burn on a general's say-so. Some things undo their own prohibitions just by being seen.

Hask saluted. Slowly, the line withdrew, torches guttering in the snow, though Serel marked the three riders at the far flank who wheeled instead and spurred east—toward the capital, toward the General. Messengers. So be it. Minutes bought were minutes.

He walked back down to the threshold alone.

The bones still lay there, half-crossed over into the light, and as Serel knelt beside them the air stirred and a voice came out of the doorway's glow—dry, amused, impossibly tired.

"So you're the father." Maren's ghost stood in the light with her hand resting on her own skull's crown like a traveler steadying herself on a gatepost. "Eleven years hollow. You poor oak. Does it hurt?"

"Yes," Serel said.

"Good. Pain means the sap's returned." She looked down into the depths, where Isandro and Odile were descending steps that had not existed seven years ago. "He'll need you further on, Warden's-husband. What sleeps at the bottom has one breath left in it, and the crown's riders are faster than grief. Can you run?"

Serel looked at the threshold—at the door his wife had kept, opening at last, going down into the buried heart of a country everyone had agreed to forget. Behind him, three riders carried his death warrant east. Ahead of him, in the warm dark, his son was walking toward a dragon with a bleeding arm and a stolen coal, and somewhere past him, at the end of everything, waited the choice Elke had died holding open.

He had served a crown that made him out of nothing.

He had a family that had made him first.

Serel Iversen set his helmet down on the threshold stone, gently, the way you set down a tool you will never pick up again—and stepped through after his son, down into the firelight, as the first horns of pursuit sounded on the northern pass.
