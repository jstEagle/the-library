## 22. Charms for the Living

The morning the first frost came to Vell's Crossing, Isandro Iversen opened his shutters and let the light in on purpose.

For seven years he had worked behind closed curtains, selling his charms from a blanket in the gray market with his sleeves pulled low and his voice pitched beneath hearing. Now the shopfront stood open to the street, wide as a confession. A sign hung above the door on iron hooks—*IVERSEN & DAUGHTER*, though there was no daughter yet, only a girl of nine who came every third day to learn—and beneath it, smaller, carved in letters deep enough to outlast weather:

*Maren's Memory.*

He had hung her name there himself, on a ladder, in front of half the street. His hands had not shaken. That was the strangest part. Seven years of hiding had taught his hands to be steady at exactly the moments they should tremble.

Inside, the shop smelled of cedar shavings and lamp oil and, faintly, of smoke that was not smoke—the ghost-warmth of dragon-fire that lived now in everything Ashvale touched. The fire ran in the land's veins again. You could feel it if you pressed your palm flat to the ground long enough: a slow pulse, patient as a heartbeat under blankets. The people of Vell's Crossing had stopped flinching at it. They had started, instead, leaving their windows unshuttered at night so the glow off the mountains could come in.

Isandro set out his knives. Six of them, ranked by size on oiled leather, each handle worn to the shape of his grip. Then he set out the bone—not the old stock, scavenged and anonymous, but the new supply that arrived monthly by cart from the south: antler shed by deer that grazed the caldera's rim, dense and faintly warm, carrying in its grain the memory of the fire that had remade the country.

He did not carve echoes anymore. He had not sold an echo of the dead since the Winnowing collapsed, and he never would again. What he carved now were promises.

A woman came in before the frost had melted off the sills—a cooper's widow named Hessa, broad-shouldered, eyes red-rimmed in the way of someone who had cried herself dry months ago and was only now learning what her face looked like without grief. She laid a strip of birch bark on his counter. On it, in a careful hand: *I will keep the shop. I will teach Tam the lathe. I will not sell.*

"My husband," she said. "Before the fever took him in the spring. He made me promise all three, and I mean to keep them, but—" Her jaw worked. "I want them somewhere that won't forget. Somewhere the forgetting can't get at them."

There it was. The word *forgetting*, spoken plainly in a shop in daylight, still landed like a struck bell. A year ago no one in Ashvale could have said it. A year ago no one could have said *Ashvale*. Isandro remembered the square in the capital, the silence cracking when he spoke the name aloud, soldiers converging, the ringing moment hanging over the city like a crack in ice. He remembered weeping strangers touching their own mouths afterward, saying the word over and over like tasting water after drought.

"I can do that," he said. "But understand what you're buying. These aren't memories. They're vows. The charm won't hold your husband—it'll hold *you*. Every time you're about to break one of those promises, you'll feel it. Like a splinter under the skin. Some people hate that."

Hessa looked at the birch bark a long moment. "Good," she said. "I'd rather be reminded than forgiven."

He chose a piece of antler the length of his thumb and began to work while she watched. This was the other thing that had changed: he carved in front of customers now. No curtain, no turned shoulder, no lie about what the knife was doing. The blade moved through the grain in the old alphabet—the Doorwarden's script, his mother's script, the letters that had once been outlawed along with every other true thing in the country—and the shavings curled away pale and sweet-smelling.

"Does it hurt them?" Hessa asked quietly. "The words. Going into the bone."

"They go willingly or not at all," Isandro said. "That's the whole craft. My mother taught me that much before I knew what she was teaching." He turned the antler, cut the curve of the second vow. "Bone remembers being alive. It wants a reason to remember."

When the last letter was cut, he breathed across the charm—not magic, just habit, just the old gesture—and felt the small warmth kindle in it, the way a coal takes. He wrapped it in linen and put it in Hessa's palm and closed her fingers over it.

"It'll warm when you keep them," he said. "And cool when you don't."

She held it against her sternum like something rescued from a fire. At the door she paused. "They say you carried a ghost's bones to the Doorhouse," she said. "That she walked through at last."

"She did."

"And your father?"

Isandro turned his knife in the light. "Still working on that one," he said, and Hessa had the grace to laugh, softly, and leave him to it.

* * *

The girl arrived at midday, as she always did, letting the shop bell announce her twice because she liked to ring it coming and going. Sefa. Nine years old, gap-toothed, the daughter of the ferryman, born two years after the erasure and therefore part of the first generation who would grow up knowing their country's name from the beginning.

"You're late," Isandro said, not looking up.

"The frost was on the boards. Papa said walk slow." She climbed onto the stool he kept for her at the second bench—the good bench, the one by the window—and pulled her practice piece from her pocket: a length of soft pine, already whittled into something that had started as a horse and was becoming, through persistence, a slightly different horse.

"Show me your grip."

She showed him. He adjusted her thumb without comment. For a while the only sounds were the shop sounds: knife through wood, the tick of the stove, the far-off clatter of the market rebuilding itself street by street. Vell's Crossing had burned twice during the Winnowing—once by hunters, once by its own people, burning what the hunters might use—and it was rising a third time now with mortar mixed with ash, which the masons swore made it stronger. Isandro believed them. Everything worth keeping in this country had been through fire at least once.

"Sefa." He waited until she looked up. "Today we start bone."

Her eyes went wide. "Real bone? Charms?"

"Real bone. But hear me first." He set down his knife and gave her the whole weight of his attention, the way his mother once had, standing in a kitchen that smelled of bread, telling a boy stories about doors and fires that he'd thought were fairy tales. "What I make isn't tricks. It isn't pretty carving. When I put a promise into bone, the promise stays. Longer than paper. Longer than stone, sometimes. And that means anyone who asks me to carve for them is asking me to hold something precious, and I have to be worthy of it, and so will you. Do you understand?"

Sefa nodded so hard her braid bounced. Then, because she was nine: "Can I do a promise for my papa? That I'll stop ringing the shop bell twice?"

Isandro laughed before he could stop himself. "Start with pine," he said. "Promises keep. Childhood doesn't."

But he gave her the smallest of the practice bones anyway—an antler tip, smooth as riverstone—and watched her hands go careful and reverent around it, and thought: *this is what inheritance is supposed to feel like.* Not a wall cavity full of secrets. Not a name you discover carved into your own flesh. Just an old man's knowledge passing into young hands in daylight, with the door open, with nothing to hide.

His forearm ached faintly where the old alphabet ran under the skin, scar-white now, finished at last in a cave while fever burned him. He touched it sometimes the way other men touched old battle wounds. It had been the worst night of his life and the truest. He would not trade it. He would also never ask it of anyone else—which was why Sefa would learn the letters on wood and shed antler, patiently, over years, until the day she chose the rest herself, if she ever did. Choice was the whole rite. It had taken him twenty-nine years and a dying dragon to understand that.

"Papa says you used to sell dead people's voices," Sefa said suddenly, sawing at her antler tip with more enthusiasm than technique. "In the gray market. Before."

"I did."

"Were they scary?"

Isandro considered the question honestly, because she deserved honesty; children could smell a softened story the way dogs smelled fear. "No," he said. "They were sad. Mostly they just wanted someone to carry them somewhere. That's most ghosts, I think. That's most people, maybe."

"Did you carry them?"

"One of them. The important one." He nodded at the sign outside, at the name above the door. "She's home now."

Sefa looked at the sign, then at him, and—being nine—asked the question adults had been too polite to ask all year: "Are you sad she's gone, or glad?"

"Both," Isandro said. "It turns out you're allowed both."

She nodded gravely, accepting this as one more strange rule of the adult world, and went back to her antler, and the shop filled again with the sound of small industry, and Isandro found that his chest, which had spent seven years learning to stay closed, had opened without his noticing and did not seem inclined to close again.

* * *

Near dusk, the bell rang once—properly, once—and Odile came in smelling of road dust and lantern oil, her gray braid frosted at the temples, her smile arriving a beat after her face the way it always did.

"You're back early," Isandro said. "The southern circuit runs another month."

"The southern circuit can miss me." She dropped onto the counter itself, having never once used a chair in his presence, and warmed her hands at his stove with the unconscious authority of a woman who had spent her life tending flames. "I heard something in Thornmere I wanted to tell you while it was fresh. There's a girl there—sixteen, maybe seventeen—who sings to wells. Finds water. Small gift, harmless, the kind we used to lose to the Winnowing without anyone even writing it down." She looked at him over her shoulder. "Her village didn't hide her, Isandro. They built her a little shrine. Put flowers around the well. There were *hunters'* children in the crowd, laying offerings."

The old alphabet prickled along Isandro's arm. "Hunters' children."

"Some of the hunters themselves are drifting north," Odile said. "Looking for work. Looking for forgiveness. Serel among them, some say." She said the name carefully, watching him.

Isandro picked up his knife and put it down again. His father. Half-restored, the crown's carving scraped out of him but the shape underneath still rough, still learning to be a man instead of an instrument. He came to Vell's Crossing some Sundays and stood across the street from the shop, not entering, not leaving, a hollowed figure trying to remember how fullness worked. Last month he had left a bundle on the step: his own old whittling knife, the one Isandro had learned on, recovered from whatever vault they'd kept it in. No note. There didn't need to be. The knife said everything the man couldn't yet.

"He asked me if he could try," Isandro said. "To be a father. To the man his son became. Those were almost his exact words."

"And what did you say?"

"I said the shop closes at sundown." Isandro turned the antler tip in his fingers—Sefa's first piece, abandoned on the bench, lopsided and earnest. "I didn't say yes. I didn't walk away either. I'm learning you can stand in a doorway a long time."

Odile snorted. "Doorwarden's son," she said. "Of course you can."

She stayed while he closed up—banking the stove, sweeping shavings, turning the sign. Outside, the frost was coming down again, and beyond it the mountains glowed faintly, steadily, the Heartfire breathing under the caldera like a sleeper who would not wake hungry. Somewhere up there, Vaskarath's ashes had become a hill that wildflowers owned now. Somewhere below the Doorhouse ruins, the buried heart of Ashvale beat on, and every person in the country carried its pulse without knowing, the way you carry your own name.

"Walk me to the ferry?" Odile said.

They went together through streets that smelled of bread and woodsmoke and thawing stone. At the corner by the well, a knot of children were playing a game Isandro had heard everywhere that autumn: one would shout a word and the others would shout it back, louder, *Ashvale, Ashvale*, as if rehearsing a rescue, as if making sure. Sefa was among them, her antler tip in her fist, shouting hardest of all.

At the landing, Odile hugged him briefly, fiercely, the way flame-tenders did everything. "Come south in spring," she said. "There are more gifts waking up every week. More names surfacing. Someone should teach them what you know."

"Someone is teaching them," Isandro said. "Slowly. On pine first."

He watched her boat cross the darkening water, lantern lit at the prow—one coal's descendant, still burning, still enough. Then he walked back up through the town he had once slipped through like a thief in his own life, past the rebuilt market, past the well, past his own lit window with Maren's name glowing above it in the firelight.

On the step, folded small and weighted with a pebble against the wind, lay a note in handwriting he was only beginning to know again—the same hand that had once guided his small ones around a knife, the same hand that had signed the edicts, the same hand, learning itself a third time.

*Sunday,* it said. *If the shop is open, I'll knock.*

Isandro stood a long moment in the cold, the paper warming in his grip like a charm keeping its promise. Then he went inside, set the note beside his knives, and reached for a fresh piece of bone.

He knew, without deciding it, what he would carve.
