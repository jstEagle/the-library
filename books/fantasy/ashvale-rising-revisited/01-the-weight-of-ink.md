## 1. The Weight of Ink

The ink was the first thing Freya Okafor learned to fear.

Not the Guild's enforcers with their iron-tipped staves. Not Master Scribe Corvane, whose voice could flatten a room like a dropped ledger. Not even the vault beneath the counting-house, where the soul-jars sat in their velvet cradles and hummed, faintly, like trapped bees. All of those were dangers you could see coming. But ink — good gall-ink, temple-grade, mixed with a drop of the client's blood when the trade required it — ink went where it was told and stayed there forever, and there was no power in Ashvale or beneath the mountain that could unwrite a line once it had dried.

Freya had been writing other people's lives away for eleven years.

She sat now in her alcove on the third floor of the Guildhall, in the hour before dawn, with three candles burning and the winter pressing its face against the frosted window like a beggar too polite to knock. The master ledger lay open before her, its pages soft as cloth from handling, each one ruled in red and black. Red for what was owed. Black for what was paid. And between them, in the narrow columns where the truth lived, the trades themselves: impossible things, bought and sold at prices no honest market would dare to name.

She dipped her pen. She did not write yet. First she read the night's entries back to herself, the way Corvane had taught her in her first year, when her hands still shook and she still asked questions.

*One widow, Maren Cottle, age fifty-one. Purchased: the last words of her husband, Aldous Cottle, deceased four years. Price: her memory of his face.*

The entry was already written — it had come up from the receiving desk at midnight, sealed with grey wax, the widow's thumbprint pressed into the cooling blood-wax beside her name. Freya's job was only to balance it. To find the column where Aldous Cottle's last words were held in escrow — they'd been sold by a debt-collector two winters past, a cruel little trade, the kind the Guild pretended to disapprove of while taking its tenth part — and transfer them across the page into Maren Cottle's keeping.

She found the entry. She drew the line. She wrote the date, the hour, the transfer complete, and signed her initials in the margin: *F.O.*, small and neat, the mark that meant *this is true, this is balanced, this is done.*

Somewhere across the city, in a narrow house above a chandler's shop, a woman was waking up with her husband's dying sentence warm in her ears again — *don't let the fire go out, Mare, mind the fire* — and no face left to hang it on. She would spend the rest of her life knowing exactly what he'd said and never quite what he'd looked like when he said it. She had agreed to this. She had signed. She had wept over the contract and then pressed her thumb into the wax anyway, because grief makes people generous with things they don't yet understand they own.

Freya blew on the wet ink until it dulled, and felt the familiar weight settle onto her chest, the one she had long ago stopped calling guilt and started calling simply *the work*.

* * *

By full light, the queue had formed outside the Guildhall doors, as it always did on balance days: the desperate, the greedy, and the merely curious, sorted by clerks at the threshold into three lines that never mixed. Freya saw them from her window — a dark ribbon of breath and huddled shoulders winding down Coinmaker's Row — and she thought, not for the first time, that Ashvale wore its hunger openly the way other cities wore their wealth.

The second trade of the day came up to her at midmorning, carried by a merchant named Osric Vane who smelled of cedar oil and expensive fear. He was a regular. The Guild liked regulars; regulars meant compound interest on intangibles.

"Master Vane." Freya gestured him into the chair across her desk. "You're early."

"I couldn't sleep." He set a wrapped parcel on the desk between them, and even through the cloth Freya could feel the particular cold that came off a living trade — the way a jarred soul felt cold, or a bottled year, or anything that had been taken out of a person before its time. "I want to amend my account."

"Amendments require Master Corvane's seal for anything above—"

"It's not an amendment." His voice cracked down the middle. He was forty, maybe, with the soft hands of a man who had others carry his ledgers. "It's a sale. I'm selling ten more years."

Freya kept her pen moving, transcribing his words as he said them, because the law of the Guild was that nothing existed until it was written. "Ten years of what, Master Vane?"

He looked at the window. At the wall. At the parcel.

"My daughter's laughter," he said. "Tobiah's daughter. She's nine. I sold five years of it when she was six — you'll have the entry — to cover the shipping losses after the storm year. It bought me back my warehouse. And it was—" his jaw worked, "—it was fine, actually. That's the terrible thing. She laughed less and I didn't miss it, because how do you miss a sound? A sound isn't like a face. You don't see the hole where a laugh used to be."

Freya knew the entry. She had balanced it herself, three years ago, in this same chair, from this same man. *Osric Vane sells five years of his daughter Tobiah's laughter; price deferred against warehouse deed.* She remembered thinking, that day, that the girl would grow up wondering why her father flinched sometimes when she giggled — why the sound seemed to pass through him like wind through a shuttered house.

"And now?" Freya asked, though she already knew. The parcel was shivering faintly. Things that had been taken from the living always shivered when they got close to being taken further.

"Now the warehouse needs a new roof, and the lenders won't wait, and—" He pushed the parcel toward her. Inside, she knew, was the mechanism of the trade: a hollow reed, wax-sealed, that had been held to the girl's throat while she slept. Ten years of laughter, condensed. The Guild's artificers had gotten very good at such harvests during the Quiet Centuries, when the gods slept and no one watched and everything a person was could be weighed, portioned, and sold.

"Master Vane," Freya said quietly, "she will be nineteen years old before she laughs freely again. Do you understand what that means? Not that she won't laugh. That every laugh between now and then will belong to someone else. If anyone ever buys it — and someone will, laughter keeps well — some stranger will feel your daughter's joy in their chest and she will feel nothing at all."

"I understand the terms." His eyes were wet and hard at once, the eyes of a man who had already grieved the decision and was only waiting for the paperwork. "I read the terms the first time. Write it down, scribe."

So Freya wrote it down. That was the whole of her life, distilled: *so Freya wrote it down.* She wrote the amount, the term, the deferral schedule, the penalty clauses. She called for the blood-ink because family trades required it — a drop of the seller's blood binding the sale of something the seller had no right to sell, which was the Guild's definition of patrimony. She watched Osric Vane open his own thumb with the little silver lancet and press three drops into the inkwell, and she watched the ink take the blood the way deep water takes a stone.

When he was gone, she sat for a long moment with her hand flat on the page, feeling the new entry dry under her palm like a scar forming.

There were forty-seven trades on her docket that day. A ferryman buying back his courage, sold in the famine years. A young soldier selling the memory of his mother's voice so he wouldn't hear it calling him home from the front. A woman — this one made Freya pause — attempting to purchase a single hour of her own childhood, price negotiable, payment in installments of her future sleep. The Guild allowed purchases of self as well as sales. It was considered generosity. The interest rates said otherwise.

Freya balanced all forty-seven. Her pen never hesitated. Her columns never wavered. By dusk her right hand ached in its permanent writer's curve, and the master ledger had grown fat with the day's truths, and somewhere in the city forty-seven people were slightly less than they had been at dawn, and the books were beautiful, and the books were balanced.

This was complicity, she knew. She had known it since her second year, when she'd tried to lose a page — just one page, a child's traded nightmares — and Corvane had appeared at her shoulder without a sound, the way he did, and said: *"The ledger does not forgive omissions, Scribe Okafor. Neither does the court beneath the mountain. Every entry you fail to write becomes a debt on your own line."*

Her own line. She had looked it up that night, alone, heart hammering. There it was, in the oldest section of the master book, in faded brown ink older than her mother's mother: *Okafor, Freya — bound to the Guild, debt inherited, term: indefinite.* She had never been told what the original debt was. She had never dared ask twice. Asking once, at fifteen, had earned her the only cold look Corvane had ever given her, and a week of latrine-cistern duty, and the understanding that some questions were themselves breaches of contract.

* * *

It was near midnight when the wrongness started.

The Guildhall emptied by degrees as the lamps guttered low — the receiving clerks first, then the artificers from the vault floor, then the guards, until only Freya remained in her alcove, finishing the day's reconciliation by candlelight because she refused to leave a column unbalanced overnight. Unbalanced pages festered. Corvane's phrase. They festered like wounds.

She was carrying the running totals forward — fourth month, ninth day, receipts of intangibles, sum entered — when her pen stopped.

There was an entry on the page that she had not written.

It sat in her column, in her spacing, ruled to her margins. The handwriting was hers. She would have sworn it on any altar standing: the slight forward slant of her *f*, the way her *k* kicked, the little hook she put on terminal *r*s that Corvane had scolded her for a hundred times and she had never cured. Every letter of it was hers.

And she had not written it.

*Fourth month, ninth day. One prayer, heard. One god, waking. Payment pending.*

Freya set the pen down very slowly, the way one sets down a blade one has discovered in one's own bed. She read the line again. Then a third time. Then she did the thing any sensible scribe would do: she checked the candle, certain she was dreaming; she checked the page number, certain she'd lost her place; she checked her own right hand, turning it over in the light as if the answer might be written on it.

The hand was just a hand. Ink-stained, aching, hers.

*One prayer, heard. One god, waking. Payment pending.*

The Quiet Centuries had lasted three hundred years. Every schoolchild in Ashvale knew the catechism: the gods had gone silent in the time of the great-grandmothers, gone wherever gods go, and in their silence the Guild had risen — because when heaven stops watching, commerce moves into the empty room. Every contract the Guild had ever sealed, every soul jarred and year bottled and name bought back, every impossible trade in every ledger Freya had ever balanced, all of it rested on one foundation: *the gods are not listening, and therefore no higher authority will ever call these debts into question.*

If a god woke —

Freya's mouth had gone dry. She looked at the entry again and noticed, for the first time, what was written beneath it in the margin, in the same impossible hand, smaller:

*Balance owed: everything.*

She should fetch Corvane. That was procedure. Anomalous entries were reported to the Master Scribe, sealed, investigated, expunged. She should stand up right now, walk the cold corridor to his chambers, and wake him with this page in her hands.

She didn't move. Because a thought had arrived, quiet and terrible, walking through her on soft feet: *if the handwriting is mine, then some part of me already knows what's coming. And some part of me wanted it written down.*

Outside, the wind changed. Freya felt it before she heard it — a pressure shift in the room, the candles leaning all together eastward though the windows were shut, the ink in her well trembling into rings. Down in the city, faint through stone and frost, a bell began to ring in the temple square. Not the Guild's bells. The old temple bell, cracked and silent for three centuries, the one the children said would only sound again when—

It rang a second time. A third.

And far off, muffled by miles of sleeping rooftops, Freya heard a crowd beginning to gather, and voices rising — not frightened voices, not yet. Voices doing something she hadn't heard an Ashvale crowd do in her entire careful, complicit, well-balanced life.

They were praying. Out loud. In the open street.

Freya looked down at the ledger, at the entry in her own hand, and picked up her pen with fingers that had finally, after eleven years, begun to shake.

*Payment pending,* the page said.

She wrote beneath it, in handwriting that was truly hers: *Gods help us all.*

And the ink, when it dried, was not black.
