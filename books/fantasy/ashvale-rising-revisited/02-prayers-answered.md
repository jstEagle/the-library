## 2. Prayers Answered

The morning the god answered, Freya Okafor was balancing a column of borrowed luck.

It was tedious work, as most of her work was. The Ashvale Guild's ledgers did not record grain or coin alone; they recorded the currency beneath currency. This particular folio tracked the luck-market in the Tanners' Quarter — who had sold a year's good fortune to cover a debt, who had bought a stranger's blessing at auction, who had run out of luck entirely and now walked through the world the way a man walks on a frozen river, listening for the crack. Freya ruled her lines straight, entered her sums true, and did not let herself think too hard about the widow on page forty who had traded the memory of her wedding day for her son's release from the debtors' cells. The Guild did not pay her to think. The Guild paid her to write, and her hand was the finest in the scriptorium, and everyone said so, and Freya had long ago learned that a compliment could be a leash as easily as a garland.

She was carrying the three down to the sum-line when the bells began.

Not the Guild bells — those were measured, deliberate, rung in codes she knew the way she knew her own pulse. These were the temple bells of Saint Verany's, and they were ringing wild, all of them at once, no pattern at all, the way bells ring when the ringer has dropped the rope and run.

Freya set down her pen. Around her, the other scribes looked up like a field of pale flowers turning toward an unexpected sun.

"Fire?" asked Merrit, the youngest, already half out of his chair.

"Fire doesn't ring all the bells," Freya said. "Fire rings two."

She was out of her chair before she finished saying it, and she did not entirely know why. Perhaps because the sound had gotten into her chest and made it impossible to sit still. Perhaps because some animal part of her, older than her training, older than the Guild, heard in that unruly clangor a note it had been waiting three hundred years to hear.

The scriptorium occupied the third floor of the Guild's counting-house, and its windows looked down over Chandler's Row to the market square. Freya reached the window in time to see the crowd.

Ashvale's market square held perhaps four hundred souls on a busy morning, and it held them all now, packed shoulder to shoulder around the old fountain — the fountain with the worn statue of a woman pouring water from a jug, the statue everyone called the Weeping Mother because three centuries of rain had carved grooves down her stone cheeks. The crowd was not fleeing. The crowd was kneeling.

They knelt in a spreading ring, like frost crossing a windowpane, one after another after another, and in the center of the ring stood a beggar child.

Freya knew the child by sight, as everyone in the Quarter did. A girl of perhaps seven, legs bent wrong below the knee, who sat every morning at the fountain's edge with her palms out and a tin cup, and who had sat there, in Freya's memory, for as long as Freya had bought her bread on market days. The child's name — the name she gave — was Sparrow. Her real name had probably been sold long ago; names were trade goods in Ashvale, and beggars' names went cheap.

Sparrow was standing.

She stood on straight legs. She stood on legs that had been bent since before anyone in the square could remember, and she was looking down at them with her mouth open, and her tin cup had fallen and rolled, and no one moved to pick it up, because every soul in that square was watching the light.

That was the only word for it. Light. It rose off the fountain like heat-shimmer, but it was morning-cool and silver, and it moved with intention, curling around the child's ankles, her knees, her hands, the way a cat winds around a beloved leg. And out of it — or through it — came a voice.

Freya heard it through the window, three stories up, over the ringing bells, over four hundred people. She would hear it for the rest of her life, in whatever life she had.

**WHO GAVE HER TO THE FOUNTAIN?**

The voice was not loud. That was the terrible thing about it. It was not loud at all. It was simply *everywhere*, the way the smell of rain is everywhere, the way grief is everywhere, and it passed through stone and timber and the ledgers on their shelves and the ink drying in their wells, and every person in Ashvale heard it in the voice of someone they had lost.

Freya heard it in her mother's voice. She did not know why. Her mother had been dead nine years and had never once, in life, spoken in capitals.

The crowd could not answer the question, of course. Who gives a beggar child to a fountain? No one. A child comes to a fountain the way water comes to a low place. But the question was not really a question. It was a god clearing its throat after three hundred years of silence, and what it said next was addressed to the child alone, and the child alone heard it, and whatever it said made Sparrow laugh — laugh, a bright cracked bell of a sound — and then the light went out like a candle pinched between wise fingers.

For a long moment the square was silent.

Then it was not.

The sound that came off the crowd was one Freya had no ledger-category for. Not cheering, exactly. It began as weeping and broke upward into joy in the same breath, four hundred people discovering at once that their knees still worked and that they wanted to use them for dancing. An old man threw his cap in the air and missed catching it and did not care. Two women who had feuded over a stall boundary for a decade embraced like sisters. Someone began to sing — a hymn, an old one, one of the ones the temples still kept out of habit, verses the Guild had long ago reclassified as *folklore* — and the whole square took it up, ragged and off-key and rising.

Freya stood at the window with her hands pressed flat against the sill and felt something crack open in her chest that she had not known was closed.

*They're answering,* she thought. *After three hundred years, they're answering.*

She had grown up, as everyone in Ashvale had grown up, on the story of the Quiet. The old gods had not died; the temples were careful to say so, and the Guild's own histories — which Freya had catalogued, and copied, and indexed — said so too. They had simply gone silent. One generation they spoke through their oracles and their storms and their small daily mercies, and the next they did not, and no one could say why, and after a while no one dared ask. The temples had become museums of prayer. The Guild had become — well. The Guild had become what filled the silence. When the gods stopped answering, people still needed impossible things. Years of life. Borrowed luck. The return of a dead husband's last words. And the Guild had learned, over three centuries, to provide them — at a price, under seal, in ink.

Every contract the Guild made was sealed in dragon-fire. That was the phrase, and Freya had written it ten thousand times without ever once asking what it meant, because asking questions about the seal was not in her job description, and because a small cold part of her had always suspected that the answer was a door she did not want to open.

*Every contract sealed under their silence,* she thought now, watching the crowd dance around the fountain. *And now the silence is ending.*

She did not yet know what that meant. She only knew that her hands, flat on the windowsill, had begun to shake.

* * *

By the time she got down to the square — and she did go down, telling herself it was to observe, to record, a scribe's duty, though some traitor part of her knew she went because she could not bear not to — the Guild's masters were already there.

They stood at the edge of the celebration the way herons stand at the edge of water: still, angled, pretending to be part of the landscape. Master Scribe Corvane was there, gray as slate in his high-collared coat, and with him three of the senior factors, and their faces were the second thing Freya would remember for the rest of her life, after the god's voice.

They were pale. Not startled-pale. Not the pallor of men surprised. The pallor of men who have seen a debt collector coming up the road and recognized the name on the writ.

Corvane was not watching the child. Corvane was watching the fountain — specifically, the statue of the Weeping Mother — and his lips were moving, very slightly, and Freya, who could read lips across a counting-room, caught the shape of the words and could not make sense of them.

*Not yet. Not yet. Not yet.*

Then he saw Freya, and his face closed like a ledger being shut, and he crossed the square to her through the dancing crowd with the unhurried tread of a man who owns the ground he walks on, which he did, or near enough.

"Okafor." Her surname only, never her given name; the Guild used surnames the way it used serial numbers. "You've seen this."

"Yes, Master Scribe."

"Then you've seen nothing. Do you understand me? The square saw a miracle. You saw a disturbance. Disturbances are recorded in the incident folios, in your own hand, before the sixth bell tonight. Cause: unknown. Effect: one beggar child, healed, no Guild interest. No Guild interest." He said it twice, and the second time he looked at her the way he looked at a column that refused to sum. "Do you know what happens to markets when people believe the old rules are coming back?"

"People stop trading," Freya said. "They wait for the gods to give freely."

Corvane's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "And what happens to a Guild built on trade, when the giving starts?"

It was not a question. He did not wait for an answer. He turned back toward the counting-house, and then paused, and said, without looking at her, in a tone so mild it took her a moment to understand it as a threat:

"Your hand is the finest in the scriptorium, Okafor. It would be a shame if it began writing things that weren't true."

He walked on. Freya stood in the square with the hymn still going around her and the joy still going around her, and she found, to her own quiet horror, that she was already composing the incident entry in her head. *Cause: unknown. Effect: one beggar child, healed, no Guild interest.* The lie was forming itself in her hand's own rhythm, the way a hymn forms itself in a congregation's throat, and she hated it, and she would write it anyway, and she knew that too.

That was the thing about complicity. It did not feel like a chain. It felt like a habit.

* * *

She wrote the false entry by lamplight that evening, in her own hand, in the incident folio, and her pen did not hesitate once, and she despised herself with a thoroughness that surprised her.

Then, because she could not help it — because the god's voice was still in her ears, her mother's voice, saying WHO GAVE HER TO THE FOUNTAIN — she went down into the archive.

The archive lived in the counting-house's cellar, behind three locks, and Freya was one of perhaps six people in Ashvale who could open all of them. It was not a grand place. It was dry, and orderly, and lit by oil lamps that never guttered — a small fact she had never examined, and which now, walking down the stairs, she examined and did not like. Nothing in the Guild's cellar was permitted to flicker. She understood that suddenly, in her bones, the way you understand a word you've read a thousand times: *nothing down here is permitted to change.*

The ledgers went back three hundred years. She had catalogued them all. She knew their spines the way a shepherd knows his sheep.

She did not know what she was looking for. She told herself she was looking for precedent — prior disturbances, prior silences, prior miracles — and she found some, small ones, a fever broken here, a storm turned aside there, all of them entered in a cramped old hand and all of them annotated in red ink with the same word: *Contained.*

It was the word *Contained* that led her, eventually, to the shelf at the back, the one that held the oldest folios, the ones bound in leather so dark it was nearly black, and it was there — because the archive keeps its own order and the archive wanted her to find it, she would think later — that she saw the ledger with the loose page.

The page was not loose because it was damaged. It was loose because it was not sewn in. It lay at the front of the folio like a letter slipped into a book, and the folio itself was dated — in the Guild's own dating, the old reckoning, the reckoning they had stopped using a century ago — to a year that made Freya's breath go strange.

The page was dated forty-one years before her birth.

She told herself it was a misdating. She told herself the old reckoning was unreliable. She stood in the lamp-steady cellar with the page in her hands and told herself all the reasonable things, and then she read it, and the reasonable things died quietly, one after another, like lamps going out.

The page was a contract. A standard form — she knew the form, she had written a thousand of them, the clauses had not changed in three centuries — and it was written in a hand she knew better than any hand in the world.

It was her own.

Not similar. Not a family hand, not a scriptorium hand, not the trained loop-and-line that all Ashvale scribes shared. *Her own.* The little hesitation she always made before the descender on a *g*. The way her *k*s leaned left, against all instruction. The tiny blot she always made after the word *sealed*, because her pen always lingered there, because some part of her had always flinched at that word, and the flinch had become ink.

*One soul, held in trust,* the contract read. *Debtor: the House of Okafor. Creditor: the court beneath the mountain. Term: until the fire dims.*

And at the bottom, in the signature line, in her own unmistakable hand, in ink that had gone brown with an age no living person could have given it:

*Freya Okafor, scribe.*

She stood in the cellar for a long time. The lamps did not flicker. Somewhere above her, muffled by three floors of stone and commerce, the city was still singing.

The gods were answering prayers again. Every contract sealed under their silence might be voided — or enforced. And in a ledger dated before her birth, in a hand that could only be hers, her name sat on a soul-debt like a bird on a wire, waiting.

Freya put the page back exactly as she had found it — she was a scribe, order lived in her hands even when her mind had come apart — climbed the stairs, locked the three locks, and went home through streets that still smelled of candle-smoke and hymns.

She did not sleep. Near dawn, she got up, lit her lamp, and took out the small private notebook she kept against all Guild regulation, and wrote one line in it, because a scribe's hand must do *something* with a truth or it will start doing things with lies:

*The gods are awake. My name is already in the books. I have to know what my family sold.*

She looked at the line for a long while. Then she blew out the lamp and sat in the dark, listening to Ashvale's bells ring the hour, and thought about the court beneath the mountain, and about a fire that dims, and about the words Corvane had mouthed at the fountain like a man bargaining with a tide.

*Not yet. Not yet.*

The next morning, she went down into the archive again, and this time she went looking for her grandmother.
