## 3. What Grandmother Sold

The archive beneath the Guildhall had a smell Freya had never managed to name — dust and tallow and something older, something like the inside of a sealed jar. She had worked down there for eleven years and still, some mornings, descending the spiral stair with her lamp held high, she felt she was walking into the throat of some patient creature that had swallowed the town's history whole and was digesting it slowly, shelf by shelf.

This morning she came down before the bell, before the other scribes, with a candle stub and a key she was not strictly entitled to carry.

The events in the market square had not left her. She had tried to lose them in work — there was always work; the Guild made certain of that — but the ledger kept pulling her back. She had opened it at dawn and read the entry again, the one dated forty-one years before her birth, written in her own unmistakable hand: the downstroke of the *f* in Okafor, the little hook she made on her capital F that no master had ever managed to drill out of her. *Freya Okafor. Held in trust. See: founding bargain, Ashvale line.*

See: founding bargain.

She had spent two hours telling herself it was a clerical error, a coincidence of penmanship, a joke some dead scribe had played across the centuries. Then she had told herself the archive would prove it was nothing. Then she had picked up her keys.

The founding records lived in the deepest gallery, past the tax rolls and the marriage bonds and the shelf of confessions the Guild kept "for the town's protection," which everyone understood to mean *for the Guild's.* The air grew colder as she went. Her candle flame leaned toward the dark as if being pulled.

The founding bargains were stored in iron cabinets, because the earliest contracts had been sealed in dragon-fire, and even centuries later, even faded to brown ghost-script, they carried a heat that made ordinary paper curl. Freya had catalogued these cabinets once, early in her service, and had been forbidden ever after to touch them. *Founding instruments are reconciled by the Master Scribe alone,* Corvane had told her, with the mild finality he used for things that were not, in fact, negotiable.

She touched them anyway.

The lock was old wax and older habit. Her key — a plain archive key, meant for the tax rolls — turned as if the lock had been waiting for her. That should have frightened her more than it did. She noted it, the way she noted everything, and set the feeling on a shelf in her mind to be dealt with later. It was getting to be a crowded shelf.

The cabinet held perhaps forty documents, each in a sleeve of oiled leather, each tagged with a family name she knew from Ashvale's oldest streets: Vess. Marrow. Dunlow. Okafor.

Her hand stopped over the last one without her permission.

*OKAFOR — ASHVALE LINE — SEE REVERSE.*

There was no reverse. There was only the sleeve, heavier than it should be, and inside it a single sheet of parchment gone the color of old tea, and beneath the parchment — she felt it before she saw it — a warmth. Faint. Flickering. Like a coal wrapped in wool.

She drew the parchment out into the candlelight.

The script at the top was not the Guild's. It was older, rounder, written by a hand that had learned its letters before the Guild standardized them, and it read:

*Bargain of the Famine Year. One soul, freely given, from the line of Okafor, to the court beneath the mountain. In exchange: grain that does not fail, water in the dry months, and the turning-aside of the sickness. Payment deferred until the dragon-fire dims. When the flame gutters, the debt comes due, and the soul is collected, with interest, at the court's discretion.*

Below it, a signature. Freya had seen her own name ten thousand times in ten thousand hands' worth of ledgers, but she had seen her grandmother's name exactly once — carved small on a marker in the paupers' row that her mother had never once visited, never once cleaned, never once spoken aloud in Freya's hearing.

*Sela Okafor.*

And below the signature, in the Guild's newer, colder hand, the words that made the candle flame shudder though there was no draft:

*Received and sealed. Witnessed under dragon-fire, the last of its kind. The court holds the collateral. The Guild holds the scribe.*

*The Guild holds the scribe.*

Freya read it four times. The words did not improve with repetition.

Her grandmother had not sold *a* soul. She had sold *the* soul — the line's soul, the family's soul, one soul passed down like a debt like an heirloom like a curse, deferred and deferred until the deferral ran out. And the deferral ran out when the dragon-fire dimmed. And last night, in the market square, an old god had answered a beggar child's prayer, and the Guild's masters had gone the color of tallow, and this morning Corvane had locked himself in his office with the crisis ledgers and had not come out.

*Payment deferred until the dragon-fire dims.*

The coal-warmth beneath the parchment pulsed once, softly, like something breathing in its sleep.

Freya sat down on the cold floor of the archive, in her good scribe's coat, and held the founding bargain of her family in both hands, and did not move for a long time.

* * *

She had been seven years old, maybe eight, the last time she heard the lullaby.

It was the year her mother took her to the coast — the only trip they ever took, and her mother had been strange the whole way, quiet in a way that wasn't her mother's usual quiet. Her mother's usual quiet was the quiet of a woman conserving herself, a banked fire. This was a different quiet. This was the quiet of someone standing in a doorway of a house she'd been born in, knowing she'd never come back.

There had been an old woman on the beach. Freya remembered her in pieces, the way you remember anything from that age: hands first, brown and cracked like riverbed clay; then a shawl the color of wet slate; then a voice, low and rough, singing something in a language Freya didn't know but her body did, some rhythm that made her chest ache before her mind caught up.

Her mother had gone stiff as a plank beside her.

"Who's that?" Freya had asked.

"No one," her mother said. And then, because her mother lied badly to her and knew it: "Someone I used to know."

The old woman had looked up then, across the wind, and her eyes had found Freya with terrible precision, the way a ledger line finds its sum. She had stopped singing. She had smiled — and Freya remembered the smile most of all, because it was not a happy smile and not a sad one either, but the smile of someone confirming something. Taking attendance. *There you are. Still on the page.*

"Go and play," her mother had said, gripping her shoulder hard enough to leave a mark, and Freya had gone, and when she looked back the beach was empty except for gulls, and her mother was standing very still with her face turned toward the sea, and she did not speak for the rest of the day.

That night, in the rented room, Freya had hummed the lullaby without knowing she was doing it, the way children carry things, and her mother had come across the room fast and knelt down and held her face in both hands and said, "Where did you hear that? *Where did you hear that?*"

"On the beach," Freya had said. "From the old lady."

Her mother had closed her eyes. And in the lamplight Freya had watched something happen in her mother's face that she was too young to name and has spent her whole life since learning the words for: grief, and guilt, and the particular exhaustion of a woman who has been carrying a debt she cannot explain to a child who is the debt.

"Never sing that where anyone can hear," her mother said. "Never. Do you understand me?"

Freya had understood the rule, if not the reason. She had kept the rule for twenty years. She had not sung it, had not hummed it, had buried it so deep that until this moment, kneeling on the archive floor with her grandmother's signature trembling in her hands, she had genuinely believed she'd forgotten it.

But the body keeps its own ledger. The body writes in the margin. And now the lullaby rose in her whole at once, complete, note for note, as if it had only been waiting — as if it had been *filed* — and she knelt there in the cold with her hand over her mouth and let it move through her without sound, because some rules outlive their reasons, and because the archive was listening. The archive was always listening.

* * *

When she could stand again, she did what she was made to do. She read the rest.

There was more on the reverse of the parchment — addenda, in the Guild's hand, accumulating across the decades like silt. She read them all, her scribe's eye catching what a grieving woman's eye would have missed, and what she caught made the grief go cold and hard and sharp-edged, which was, she would reflect later, perhaps the point of her training.

*Addendum, Year 12 of the Silence: the Guild acknowledges the court's claim and undertakes to hold the named line in close service until collection. The scribe of the line shall be maintained at Guild expense, in Guild housing, in Guild employ, and shall not be permitted to travel beyond the Ashvale boundary stones.*

*Addendum, Year 31: the Guild notes the death of Mara Okafor, mother of the current scribe, and the passage of the obligation to the daughter. The daughter is to be enrolled in the scriptorium at the earliest lawful age.*

*Addendum, Year 44: the Guild notes the current scribe's aptitude. She is to be kept close.*

*Kept close.*

Freya stood in the deepest gallery of the archive and looked back up the spiral stair, at the eleven years of her life stacked along its walls — the dormitory bed she'd been given at fourteen, the apprenticeship she'd been granted without applying, the promotions that had come so smoothly she'd thought herself talented, the housing, the stipend, the small kindnesses, the *we couldn't run this place without you, Freya* — and she saw it all now with the ledger's cold clarity, every line of it, an account kept in a book she'd never been shown.

She had thought she was the Guild's best scribe.

She was the Guild's *collateral.* She was the jar the soul came in. They had raised her the way you raise a debt — carefully, patiently, feeding it, keeping it warm, keeping it *close* — because the day the dragon-fire dimmed, the court would come to collect, and the Guild intended to be holding exactly what the contract required, in good condition, well-trained, grateful, and near.

Her mother had known. Her mother had taken her to the coast to see the old woman once, one time, before the silence closed over the whole subject — and then had spent the rest of her life never speaking of her, never visiting the pauper's marker, never singing, never humming, keeping her daughter inside the boundary stones and inside the Guild and as far from the mountain as a life in Ashvale allowed, and it hadn't mattered, none of it had mattered, because the contract didn't care about distance. The contract cared about *blood,* and blood could not be carried beyond a boundary stone a child could hop.

*She is to be kept close.*

Freya folded the founding bargain along its old creases and, after a moment's war with herself that lasted approximately half a second, slid it inside her coat, against her ribs, where the warmth of it lay against her heart like a small sleeping animal.

Then she heard the bell.

Not the morning bell. The other one — the single strike, the one that meant the Master Scribe required her. It rang through the archive's stone throat and came down the spiral stair and found her exactly where she stood, and she understood, with the certainty of a sum that balances, that Corvane had known she would find this. That the lock had turned too easily. That the addendum had been written in language plain enough for any scribe to read. That she had been *allowed* down here, this morning, the day after the god in the market square, because the books were about to be opened and the Guild wanted its collateral to understand, at last, precisely what she was worth.

The candle had burned down to her fingers. She pinched it out.

Above her, at the top of the stair, the Guildhall waited, and somewhere in it Master Scribe Corvane sat behind his desk with the crisis ledgers spread before him, and he had already written, she was sure of it, in his own cold hand, the entry that concerned her.

She climbed toward it, one hand pressed flat against the parchment over her heart, where her grandmother's soul — her soul — beat its slow, deferred warmth against her palm, like something knocking to be let out.

Like something that had been waiting forty-one years to come home.
