## 5. A Debt Named Tam

The trouble with selling your shadow, Tam Vess had learned over seven long years, was not the darkness. He'd expected the darkness. What he hadn't expected was the arithmetic.

A man without a shadow walked through Ashvale and cast nothing behind him. Candles didn't gutter when he passed. Dogs didn't startle. Children looked straight through him in the market lanes as though he were a smudge on the air, and he understood — slowly, in the way you understand a debt only when the interest comes due — that the Guild hadn't bought his shadow at all. They'd bought his *weight*. His proof of substance. The part of him the world agreed to see.

He'd sold it for forty crowns and a cure for his sister's lung-rot, and he'd have done it again, and that was the worst of it. That was what made him angry every morning when the sun rose and lay flat against the cobbles where his feet should have thrown it.

But this morning, the sun rose over a beggar child made whole in the market square, and the whole city was talking about it, and Tam Vess put on his good coat, the one without the smuggler's hidden seams, and went to collect.

* * *

The Ashvale Guild's counting house stood at the end of Ledger Row like a held breath: grey stone, narrow windows, a door of black oak banded in iron that had never once been forced because forcing it was beside the point. You didn't break into the Guild. You signed yourself into it.

Tam had signed once. He remembered the pen scratching, the ink cold as river water, the scribe across the desk — a young woman then, sharp-eyed, dark-skinned, her hair bound back so tight it looked like discipline made visible. She had read his contract aloud in a voice like a fair judge pronouncing sentence, and she had looked at him, just for a moment, with something that wasn't cruelty. Something worse. *Recognition.*

"You don't have to do this," she'd said quietly, before the witnesses arrived.

"I do, though," he'd said. "That's the whole point of it."

She'd nodded, once, and stamped the page, and his shadow had peeled off the floor like wet cloth and folded itself into a jar, and Freya Okafor had written the entry in a ledger that would outlive them both.

Now he pushed open the black door and stood in the counting hall with the morning light falling through the high windows and touching everything except him.

"Name," said the clerk at the receiving desk, an old man with ink-stained knuckles who did not look up.

"Vess. Tam Vess. I'm here about an account."

That got the old man's eyes up. Accounts were the one thing in Ashvale nobody came about casually. "Account number?"

"Don't need one." Tam set his hand flat on the desk, palm down, fingers spread. The clerk stared at the hand, then at the floor beneath it, where no shadow answered. "You'll find me under trades, seven years back. One shadow, adult, unblemished, sold under duress of illness in the family. Forty crowns paid out. I want to discuss redemption."

"The term is *repurchase*," the clerk said automatically, "and the schedule for repurchase of corporeal intangibles begins at ten times—"

"Times nothing." Tam leaned in. He kept his voice level, because he'd learned that in Ashvale the loud men got escorted out and the quiet ones got heard. "I've read the statutes, grandfather. All of them. Including the one they don't post on the wall."

The clerk's mouth opened. Closed. Somewhere behind the partition walls, pens scratched on, indifferent as rain.

And then the inner door opened, and she came out.

* * *

Freya Okafor had changed in seven years, but not the way people change. She'd been sharpened. Her coat was guild-grey, cut close, and she carried a ledger under one arm the way other women carried infants — with that automatic, protective curve of the elbow. When she saw him, something moved behind her eyes, quick as a fish under ice, and was gone.

"Mister Vess." Her voice was the same. Fair-judge voice. "You've come at a busy time."

"So I hear. A god healed a child yesterday." He let that sit in the hall, let the words carry the way she carried her ledger. "Funny thing. I prayed for seven years. Not for much. Just for my own shape back. Seems I picked the wrong century to be pious."

"Come through," she said. "Not the public counter. My office."

"That's new," he said, following her. "The Guild showing courtesy to debtors."

"The Guild shows courtesy to precedents," she said, without turning around, and Tam grinned at the back of her head because she'd always been honest in exactly the ways that cost her most.

Her office was small and ferociously ordered: shelves of ledgers in strict chronology, a single window looking out on the archive yard, a lamp trimmed so precisely it might have been a religious observance. She closed the door, set her ledger down, and finally looked at him properly.

"You're here about the shadow."

"I'm here about the shadow, and I'm here about the law, and I've brought both with me." He drew a folded paper from inside his good coat and laid it on her desk. "Market square, day before yesterday. An old god — the records call them the Quiet Ones, your own archives, third shelf, if the Guild hasn't burned that volume yet — answered a prayer publicly. Restored a dying child. No contract. No price. No seal."

Freya looked at the paper but didn't touch it. "Crowd testimony isn't a legal instrument."

"No," Tam agreed pleasantly. "It's better than one. It's a *fact*. And here's what follows from it, scribe, so follow my sums." He counted on his fingers, slow, theatrical, enjoying himself more than he'd enjoyed anything in years. "One: the gods are returning. Two: the contracts sealed during their silence were sealed under duress — the duress being that no higher power would intervene, which the Guild knew and profited from. Three: a contract made under duress is voidable at the injured party's petition. Four—" he tapped the paper, "—the gods themselves just demonstrated that prayer outranks contract. So I am petitioning. As an injured party. With divine precedent."

For a long moment Freya said nothing. The lamp hissed softly. Through the wall came the sound of the counting hall, the endless patient scratch of pens writing down the lives of the desperate.

"You've been reading," she said at last.

"I've been *waiting*," Tam said. "Reading filled the hours."

"And your sister?"

"Lung-rot took her two winters ago. The cure worked. That part of the trade was honest." His jaw tightened. "Don't do that. Don't look for the soft place in me to press. I came as a creditor, not a charity case."

"I wasn't—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. And Tam watched, with a strange sinking feeling, as the woman behind the ice-fish eyes assembled herself into the clerk. Spine straightening. Hands folding. Voice going smooth and terrible as poured wax.

"Mister Vess. Your account stands as written. Contract of sale, executed voluntarily, witnessed, sealed by dragon-fire, entered in the ledger of record. The Guild acknowledges no doctrine of duress arising from divine silence; the silence of the gods was the condition of all commerce, known to all parties, and cannot retroactively void instruments made in good faith." She recited it like a psalm. Like something she'd swallowed whole and could bring back up intact. "Repurchase remains available per schedule. Ten times the principal, plus accrued interest, payable within the fiscal year."

"Ten times." Tam laughed, and it came out ugly. "Forty crowns becomes four hundred. For my own *shadow*, Freya. You know what the Guild does with them? I've seen the vault. I bribed a porter. Rows of jars, all glowing faint, like a chapel of trapped moths. You're not even *using* them. You're just keeping them because keeping is owning and owning is power."

"We use them," Freya said, very quietly.

"What, as collateral? As decoration?"

"As *stability*." And there it was again, that flash beneath the ice — anger, or grief, or both wearing each other's clothes. "Every soul-debt, every impossible trade in this city rests on the certainty that the Guild honors its books. If I open your account and return your shadow on the strength of a street sermon, the books stop meaning anything. Every widow who traded honestly, every family that bargained in good faith — their holdings become worthless overnight. You'd crash the economy of the whole valley to get back a piece of your afternoon."

"A piece of my—" Tam slammed his palm on the desk, and the lamp jumped, and for one heartbeat the office was full of a shadow that belonged to the furniture and not to him. "It's not a piece. It's the *proof*. You wrote the entry. You know what's in the jar. Tell me what a soul looks like, scribe, since you keep them. Does mine glow? Is it comfortable? Does it dream about me?"

Freya flinched. Actually flinched, a small motion, quickly mastered — but Tam had spent seven years learning to read rooms without casting anything into them, and he caught it.

"You haven't seen the jars," he said slowly. "Have you. You write the entries and you've never once been allowed down to look."

"That's not relevant to your petition."

"It's the most relevant thing in this building." He leaned forward. "You hate this. I watched you hate it just now, in real time, behind your professional face. Seven years ago you told me I didn't have to sign. Nobody made you say that. So here's my question, Freya Okafor, and think carefully before your mouth answers with the Guild's voice: when the gods finish coming back — and they are coming back, everyone in this city can feel it, the way animals feel weather — what happens to every contract in these books? Because I'll tell you what happens. Either the gods void them, or the gods enforce them, and either way the Guild's masters are pale as milk this morning, and either way *you* are sitting at the desk when it lands."

Silence. The lamp hissed. Down in the hall, a bell rang twice — some account settled, some life amended.

"You should go," Freya said.

"Should I?"

"*Please* go." And the please cracked something in the middle of the word, just slightly, just enough. "If Corvane hears you've been in this office preaching insurrection over precedent, he won't escort you out, Vess. He'll add a line to your account. Breach of decorum. Contempt of instrument. He can attach wages, property, *years*. You have no idea how many things a man can owe."

"Oh, I have some idea." Tam straightened his good coat. "Here's what you don't understand about me, scribe. I spent seven years being careful, being polite, being *owed*. And yesterday I watched a god heal a child for free, in front of three hundred witnesses, and I thought: the world used to work like that. Before the ledgers. Before the seals. The world used to answer." He walked to the door, then stopped with his hand on it. "I'm not going away, Freya. I'm going to stand in the square every market-day with my paper and my sums, and I'm going to teach anyone who'll listen how to read their own contracts. There's more of us than there are of you. There always were. We just forgot how to count ourselves."

"Telling people the truth about their debts will get them attached," she said sharply. "Enslaved. Some of them will lose *years*, real years off their lives, do you understand—"

"They've already lost them!" It came out louder than he meant, ringing off the ordered shelves. "They lost them the moment they signed. All I'm doing is telling them the number." He pulled the door open. Paused. "You denied me today. By the letter. I watched you do it, and I watched it cost you, and I want you to know I noticed. That's not forgiveness. But it's a down payment."

He was three steps into the corridor when her voice caught him, low and fast, pitched for him alone:

"Third shelf. Archive annex. The Quiet Ones' covenant — the original, not the redacted copy in the public statutes. Read what the gods required in exchange for their silence." A breath. "And Vess — check the date on the famine bargain while you're there. The founding one. Check who *signed* for Ashvale."

He turned. She was already back at her desk, head bent over her ledger, the picture of a loyal clerk, pen moving, recording something. Denying him by the letter of the law she hated — and slipping him a key besides.

Tam Vess smiled for the first time in seven years, and it felt strange on his face, like wearing someone else's coat.

* * *

The archive annex smelled of dust and tallow and the particular cold that old paper generates, the way stone cellars stay cold in summer. Tam bribed the porter with two crowns and a lie about research into ancestral holdings, and found the third shelf, and found the covenant.

He read it standing, by the light of a borrowed taper, and the longer he read the stiller he became.

The gods had not gone silent. That was the first thing. The gods had been *bought* — or rather, their voices had been contracted away, sealed beneath the mountain by the founders of the Guild itself, in exchange for the dragon-fire that authenticated every instrument the Guild ever stamped. Divine silence, in perpetuity, as the consideration underlying all commerce. Every contract in Ashvale rested, at the bottom of its foundations, on a single ancient transaction: the gods' own muzzled mouths.

And beneath that clause, in the appendix, the famine record. The founding bargain. The one that had saved the valley from starvation three generations ago and purchased it with a human soul.

Tam read the signature line four times, because his eyes kept sliding off it the way water slides off oiled cloth.

It was not Okafor. It was not any name he knew from the poor rolls or the debtor lists.

*Corvane.*

An ancestor's hand, but the same name, the same line, the same family that now sat at the head of the Guild and sent clerks like Freya Okafor to deny men their shadows by the letter of a law built on a fraud.

"Well," Tam said softly, to the empty annex, to the jars he couldn't see, to the god somewhere above the mountain who had healed a child for free. "Well, well."

Outside, the afternoon light lay flat on the cobbles where his shadow should have been, and for the first time it didn't feel like absence. It felt like evidence.

He folded the knowledge into his good coat next to his paper of sums, and started walking toward the market square, where there were people to teach, and numbers to count, and a war to begin — one reader at a time.
