## 6. Dragon-Fire Gutters

The summons came at dawn, folded into a square of black paper that had no business existing.

Freya knew the Guild's stationery the way she knew her own heartbeat — cream stock for routine audits, grey for disciplinary notices, gold-edged for the masters' correspondence. Black paper appeared in the ledgers exactly once per century, and always in connection with the mountain. She turned it over twice before breaking the seal: a disc of red wax stamped with a coiled shape she had never seen outside of the oldest contract-templates, the one the scribes called *the sleeper*.

*The Court Beneath requests the presence of the Ledger-Keeper's hand. Come alone. Come before the flame asks twice.*

No signature. No seal from Corvane. The handwriting was not handwriting at all — every letter was identical to its brothers, as if cut from the same sheet and assembled into words. It looked like type. It looked like something a ledger would produce if a ledger could speak.

She read it four times standing in her narrow room above the counting-house, still in her nightclothes, the first grey light leaking through shutters she'd never bothered to oil. Then she dressed in her good coat — the scribe's coat, deep green wool with forty interior pockets, each sized to a specific instrument — and told no one where she was going.

That last part was instinct, and it troubled her. A loyal clerk would have reported the summons within the hour. A loyal clerk would have asked Corvane whether the Guild wished to send an escort, a counter-delegation, a gift. Freya did none of these things. She simply laced her boots and slipped out through the delivery gate while the night-watch dozed over their cups, and only on the pilgrim road, with Ashvale's rooftops falling away behind her, did she admit what the instinct meant:

She wanted to hear what the mountain said before anyone could tell her how to write it down.

* * *

The road to the mountain's mouth was older than the Guild, older than the famine, older — if the archive fragments were honest — than the city itself. Pilgrims had worn it into a smooth white groove through the grey hills, though no pilgrim had walked it in three hundred years. Now, this morning, Freya saw others on it: a widow in mourning-grey walking with the terrible steadiness of someone who has decided something; two merchants who kept glancing back toward Ashvale as if expecting to be arrested; a child, alone, barefoot, humming.

They did not speak to her. They did not speak to each other. But when they passed the boundary stones — squat pillars carved with the coiled sleeper — each of them touched the stone, briefly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you hoped would stay asleep.

Since the beggar child in the market square. Since the god that had opened its eye after three centuries of silence. Since Freya had found her own name written in her own hand on a page dated nineteen years before her birth.

The word had spread through Ashvale the way water spreads through chalk: invisibly, totally. *The gods are answering.* And everyone who had ever signed anything now walked with a small cold stone in their chest, asking the same question Freya had been unable to stop asking since she read that impossible page: *what happens to my debt?*

The mountain answered that question before she reached it.

She crested the last hill an hour past midmorning and stopped so abruptly that her satchel swung forward and struck her hip. The mountain's mouth — a cavern arch tall enough to swallow a cathedral, which in fact had swallowed one, its spire still visible fused into the rock like a fossil — was glowing.

Not lit. *Glowing*, the way a dying coal glows, in pulses, uneven, each pulse dimmer than the last. Deep inside the arch, far below the visible floor, something burned. Something had burned for three hundred years without fuel, without rest, without ever once being seen by the clerks whose entire economy depended on it.

Dragon-fire. The flame that sealed the contracts.

Every soul-trade, every year-of-life, every borrowed luck and sold laughter and purchased silence that passed through the Ashvale Guild was authenticated by that fire. When a contract was struck, a copy traveled down the pilgrim road to the court beneath the mountain, and the fire touched it, and the fire's acceptance was the law. Freya had processed eleven thousand entries in her nine years as Ledger-Keeper's hand, and she had never once thought about the fire itself. It was like thinking about gravity, or the color of ink. It simply was.

It was guttering.

She stood on the white road with the wind pulling at her coat and watched the pulses slow — bright, dim, bright, dimmer — and felt something in her understanding rearrange itself around a new and horrible arithmetic. If the fire died, nothing would authenticate. If nothing authenticated, then every contract in her ledgers became merely *paper*. Or worse — because the gods were waking, and waking gods did not recognize paper. Waking gods recognized *debt*. And debts, unsealed, unforgiven, unpaid, came due.

All of them. At once.

"No," she said aloud, to the mountain, to the arithmetic. "That's not how—" But she didn't even finish the sentence, because she was a ledger-keeper, and she knew exactly how it worked. Interest compounded. Defaults cascaded. She had watched a merchant's defaulted luck-debt swallow his neighbor's harvest-contract and then the neighbor's house and then the neighbor. One collapse, properly seeded, took everything with it.

Eleven thousand entries. Three centuries of them. All coming due together, if the flame went out.

The child who had passed her on the road was already at the boundary stones, humming, waiting. Waiting for the mountain to open. Freya realized her hands were shaking and pressed them flat against her coat until they remembered themselves, and walked down onto the threshold of the world underneath.

* * *

Inside the arch, the air changed.

It was warm — not comfortably, but the way a sickroom is warm, feverish, close. The light came from below, pulsing up through vents in the cavern floor like breath. And the sound: Freya had expected silence, or echo, or the drip of underground water. Instead there was a hum, vast and low, beneath the threshold of hearing, the kind of sound you feel in your teeth and your sternum and the backs of your eyes. It took her twenty paces to identify it, and when she did, she stopped again.

It was reading. Thousands upon thousands of voices, too many and too blended to separate, reciting. Contracts. Every contract ever accepted by the fire, being spoken forever by whatever dwelt down there, in the dark, keeping the books.

*A ledger,* she thought, with a vertigo that nearly buckled her knees. *The whole mountain is a ledger.*

"You come quickly, scribe."

The voice came from her left, out of a shadow that should not have held a person. The herald stepped into the fire-light, and Freya's first coherent thought was that it looked like something a scribe would draw if asked to draw a person from memory, badly, under pressure. Tall, yes. Robed, yes. But the proportions were wrong in ways she couldn't fix by looking harder — the hands too long, the face too smooth, the eyes set a half-degree too wide apart, as if the concept of *eyes* had been explained to whoever made it but the measurements had been lost.

It wore the coiled sleeper embroidered across its chest in thread the color of dried blood.

"I was summoned," Freya said. Her voice sounded very small against the hum.

"You were summoned." The herald inclined its head, and the gesture was almost right, almost courteous, which was somehow worse than if it had been wrong. "You are the hand of the Okafor line. The keeper's hand. We know your script." It smiled, or arranged its mouth into the shape scribes used for smiling. "We have read your grandmother's hand as well. For a long time."

Freya's heart performed a maneuver she felt in her throat. "My grandmother."

"Come and see why you were summoned." The herald turned and began to walk, and its gait was wrong too, gliding in a way that suggested legs were a courtesy rather than a necessity. "The flame asks twice, scribe. You arrived before the second asking. That is noted. Everything is noted. That is rather the problem."

* * *

The descent was measured in landings.

Each landing was a vault-gallery, and each gallery was lined with shelves, and each shelf held jars. Freya had known about the jars the way she'd known about the fire — abstractly, structurally, as line-items. *Soul, held in escrow. Soul, collateralized. Soul, transferred to deep storage.* She had written those words hundreds of times in her neat, disciplined, complicit hand.

Seeing them was different.

The jars were clay and glass, fist-sized to skull-sized, each one holding a light. Not fire-light. A softer radiance, pearl-colored, that moved when you looked at it the way a sleeper moves — slowly, restlessly, turning toward warmth that wasn't there. Some of the lights were bright. Some were dim. On the upper galleries, most were bright; the hum of recitation was strongest here, and Freya understood with a clerk's cold clarity that proximity to the flame kept them *read*, kept them current, kept them — she searched for the word and hated it — *serviced*.

"Upper galleries hold recent acquisitions," the herald said, conversational, terrible. "Trades from the last century. They are comfortable here. The fire is strong enough, still, to keep their terms."

"And lower?"

"Lower holds older terms." The herald did not look back. "Terms made under the old silence, when the gods slept and the fire was young. Those souls have been waiting a long time for their contracts to mean something."

They descended. Landing after landing. The jar-lights grew dimmer, and the hum changed key — lower, slower, the recitation stretching out like taffy, individual voices becoming audible now, and Freya caught fragments as they passed: a woman's voice reciting the terms of a bargain made in 214, promising her strength for her son's survival; a man's voice, flat with repetition, reciting a trade of his courage for his brother's freedom; a voice so old it barely sounded human anymore, reciting numbers, just numbers, some ancient accounting that had been its whole self for longer than Ashvale had existed.

Freya kept her eyes forward. It was the only way to keep walking.

At the ninth landing the herald stopped, and Freya nearly walked into it, and looked up — and there, framed in the shaft above them, visible all the way down through the aligned galleries like the spine of an open book, was the fire.

It hung in a chamber at the mountain's root, suspended over a pool of black glass, and it was the size of a barn and the color of a sunset having a seizure — gold and orange and a deep wounded red, pulsing, contracting, expanding, and every pulse was weaker. As Freya watched, it flickered. Actually flickered, the way a candle flickers in a draft, and the whole mountain shuddered, and every jar-light on every gallery dimmed in sympathy, and somewhere far above them a shelf cracked, and a single soul-light guttered and steadied and guttered again.

"The last dragon," the herald said. "Or rather, the last dragon's fire. The dragon itself sleeps beneath the flame. Has slept since the sealing. The fire is its breath, drawn out over three hundred years, spent authenticating your Guild's bargains." It turned those too-wide eyes on her. "Breath runs out, scribe. Even ours."

"How long?"

"That is why you were summoned." The herald gestured, and a section of the gallery wall — which Freya had taken for rock — slid aside, revealing a small chamber, a table, and on the table, a document. Black paper. The same impossible type-cut lettering as her summons. "Read."

She read.

*To the Keeper's Hand of the Ashvale Guild, greeting. The flame diminishes. When it fails, all terms authenticated by it fall due immediately and in full. Collection will be executed by the Court in the manner of the old times: directly, bodily, and without negotiation. There remain ninety-one days of breath. The Court extends this courtesy of notice once, as is traditional. The Court notes, further, that the Guild's master ledger contains certain entries of unusual age and unusual weight, and that among these is an entry bearing the bloodline of Okafor.*

Freya's eyes snagged on the last sentence and refused to move past it. *An entry bearing the bloodline of Okafor.* She had found the founding bargain herself, three nights ago, in the deep archive — her grandmother, Iyari Okafor, and the famine, and the deferred payment, and the phrase that had haunted her since: *to be collected when the fire dims.*

"When the fire dims," she whispered. "You wrote that. Two hundred years ago. You knew—"

"We knew the terms we were given." The herald's voice didn't change, but something behind it did, some vast patience shifting its weight. "Your grandmother did not sell cheaply, scribe. She bought a city's survival. The Court honored the deferral because the Court honors terms. But terms end." It tilted its smooth head. "You wish to know where her soul is kept."

Freya hadn't asked. Her mouth had gone dry and her hands had gone cold and she hadn't asked, but the herald had read the question anyway, off her face, off her bloodline, off whatever it was they read down here instead of ledgers.

"The deepest vault," the herald said. "Below the flame chamber. Closer to the dragon than any other holding." A pause, precisely calibrated, a merchant's pause. "The oldest and heaviest terms are kept nearest the fire, so that when collection comes, they are collected first. Your grandmother's soul sits closer to the dying flame than any other in this mountain. When the breath fails, hers is the first jar to go dark — and the first soul to be taken in full."

The hum filled the silence. Somewhere above, another shelf cracked.

"Ninety-one days," Freya said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her; she filed the steadiness away to examine later, the way she filed everything. "And you're telling me this because?"

"Because the Court keeps accurate records, scribe." The herald smiled its borrowed smile. "And because accurate records want *balancing*. Your Guild owes us a great deal. Your bloodline owes us a great deal. And you—" it leaned closer, and up close its eyes were not empty at all, they were full, full of a light that flickered in time with the dying fire, "—you have been finding entries in your own hand that you never wrote. Have you not."

It wasn't a question. Freya said nothing. Her heart was a drum counting down.

"The books are waking up," the herald said softly. "The gods return, the fire dies, and the ledgers remember what was written in them — including what was written falsely. Ninety-one days, Keeper's Hand. Come back before the flame asks twice. Or don't." It straightened. "Either way, we will see you soon. It is, after all, your family's account."

The wall closed. The audience was over. Freya climbed the nine landings with her eyes fixed on the dimming jar-lights, refusing to look at any of them, refusing to wonder which faint glow might be a woman who had once sung a lullaby her mother had never passed on — and at the top, stepping out of the fever-warm dark into the clean cold wind of the white road, she finally let herself think the thought that had been waiting for her the whole way down:

*Ninety-one days. And the deepest vault is closest to the flame. Which means the only way to reach her in time is to go deeper than the fire itself — and the Guild will never allow it, and the Court will only allow it at a price, and I have ninety-one days to learn what the price is before the mountain collects it from everyone at once.*

Behind her, far below, the dragon-fire pulsed — bright, dim, bright, dimmer —

and did not come back to bright.
