## 7. Ledger Ghosts

The candle had burned to a stub before Freya noticed the first of them.

She had been reconciling the solstice columns for three hours — grain futures against luck-futures, the Guild's eternal sleight of hand that made one look like the other — when her eye snagged on an entry in the margin of page 411. Small, neat, tucked into the gutter of the binding where auditors rarely looked.

*One shadow, held in trust. Interest: none. Note: the bearer wept.*

She knew that handwriting. Of course she did. It was her own — the particular way her *h* leaned, the flat-topped *d*, the little hook she put on terminal *r*s because Master Scribe Corvane had once told her a clerk's hand should be legible even to the dead. She had written that entry.

She had never written that entry.

Freya sat back in her chair and let the quill hover above the inkwell, dripping. She searched her memory the way she searched ledgers: systematically, column by column, year by year. Tam Vess and his shadow — that had been five years ago, the smuggler with the crooked grin who'd signed away his shade for safe passage through the Ashvale checkpoints, and she remembered the transaction perfectly, every clause, because it was the first contract that had ever made her feel sick while writing it. But there was no such note in the official record. She had checked a dozen times since. The margin note existed nowhere but here, on this page, in her hand, dated three days ago.

Three days ago she had been at the mountain's mouth, watching the last dragon's flame gutter like a dying hearth.

Her fingers were cold. She told herself it was the draft from the archive window, the same draft that always came down the stairwell at this hour, smelling of stone and old vellum. She turned the page.

There were more.

Not many. Seven, by her count, scattered across the last hundred pages like moths pressed between leaves. Each in her handwriting. Each dated within the past fortnight — since the god had answered the beggar child in the market square, since the crowd had sung and the Guild's masters had gone the color of tallow.

*One name, returned to its owner. No charge.*

*Ten years of laughter, refunded. The daughter remembers everything.*

*A widow's grief, lifted without payment. She lights two candles now instead of one.*

Freya read them twice, three times, her lips moving though no sound came. They were not transactions. They were — reversals. Little acts of mercy recorded in the language of commerce, as if someone were keeping a second set of books inside her own: a shadow ledger of debts forgiven, entries the Guild would burn a scribe alive for making.

And they were in her handwriting, which meant either she was writing them in her sleep —

— or something else was.

* * *

She did not sleep that night. She told herself it was diligence; the solstice reconciliation loomed, and Corvane had been explicit about the deadline. But diligence did not require her to sit with the ledger open under triple lamplight, watching the pages the way one watches a sleeping animal that might wake.

At some point past midnight, she began testing it. She wrote a harmless entry in her own book — *candle stock, depleted, replaced* — then compared the two hands side by side under the magnifying glass the archivists used for forgeries. Identical. Not similar. Identical, down to the hesitation blots, the places where her wrist tired and the strokes thickened. A forger could be caught by such things. This could not be caught by anything.

Then she tried an experiment that shamed her even as she performed it. She turned to a blank page near the end of the volume, wrote a question in her smallest hand — *who is writing these?* — and closed the ledger over it, weighting it with the brass scale-weight shaped like a mountain that every Guild desk kept, a small joke of the trade.

She waited an hour. The candle burned. Somewhere below, the night clerks turned pages, and the building breathed its dry paper breath.

When she opened the ledger again, beneath her question, in her handwriting, in ink still wet enough to smear:

*You already know.*

Freya stood up so fast her chair went over backward, and the crash brought a junior clerk to the door, wide-eyed, asking if the mistress wanted tea. She sent him away with a voice she was proud of — steady, bored, the voice of a woman balancing accounts — and then she stood alone in the lamplight with her heart hammering and the ledger lying open on the desk like a mouth.

*You already know.* She did know, or she suspected, and suspicion was worse. Since the gods had begun answering prayers, everything sealed in their silence had begun to loosen. Corvane said it himself, in his cold audit-voice: contracts are only as strong as the silence that enforces them. And what was a ledger, in the end, but a contract written down? What was ink but a bargain struck between memory and forgetting?

If the gods were waking, then perhaps the books were waking too. Perhaps the ledger had spent three centuries swallowing the truth the Guild fed it — every suppressed reversal, every mercy denied, every debt collected unjustly — and was now, in the loosening dark, spitting it back up. In her hand. Because she was the one who wrote the lies, and whatever lived in the pages had decided that the liar's hand was the honest vessel for the truth.

Or perhaps she was going mad. That possibility sat in the corner of the room all night, patient as a creditor. Madness would be simpler. Madness could be managed. You could not reconcile yourself against madness; there was no counter-entry.

Toward dawn she slept at her desk, cheek on the cold brass mountain, and dreamed of her grandmother — a woman she had never met, whose face she knew only from a single cracked portrait — standing in a vault full of jars, singing a lullaby Freya somehow knew the words to, though when she woke she could not remember a single line.

* * *

Corvane summoned her at midmorning, which meant he had heard about the overturned chair, or the sleepless lamp, or simply that the building itself reported to him the way everything in Ashvale eventually did.

His office sat at the top of the Guild tower, a room with no books in it — a fact that had unsettled Freya for years before she understood it. A master scribe who kept no records kept no evidence. Everything passed through him and lodged nowhere, like rain on oiled cloth.

"You look unwell," he said, without looking up from the document he was sealing.

"I've been working late."

"The solstice reconciliation." He pressed the seal into wax. "Yes. I trust the figures are behaving themselves."

It was the kind of sentence Corvane liked — deniable, faintly humorous, and sharp enough to draw blood if you pressed on it. Freya chose not to press. "They're nearly done."

"Good." He finally raised his eyes. They were pale, unhurried eyes, the eyes of a man reading a ledger he had memorized long ago. "There's a matter I want your opinion on. Purely theoretical." He slid a single sheet across the desk. "An auditor's hypothetical. Suppose an entry appeared in a ledger in a trusted hand — a hand beyond reproach — recording a transaction that hand never conducted. What would you conclude?"

Freya's stomach dropped through six floors of tower. She kept her face still, because she had learned stillness from him, and it was the only inheritance the Guild had ever given her for free.

"I'd conclude a forgery," she said. "And I'd check the ink age, the pressure of the strokes, the position of the page relative to the binding. Forgers are caught by physics, not by talent."

"And if the physics confirmed the true hand?"

"Then I'd conclude the writer wrote it and didn't remember." She paused. "Memory is also a ledger, Master Scribe. It has errors too."

Something moved behind his eyes — not warmth, nothing so useful as warmth, but interest, the way a merchant's eyes move when a price comes in lower than expected. "A poetic answer," he said. "Scribes shouldn't be poets. Poetry makes people believe things can be unwritten." He took the sheet back, folded it once, precisely. "Finish the reconciliation. And Freya — if you find any anomalies, you bring them to me. Not to the archive. Not to your friends. To me. Anomalies are like wounds. Left in the open, they fester."

"Yes, Master Scribe."

He returned to his sealing. As she reached the door, he added, without looking up: "Your grandmother was also meticulous. Did you know that? Before your time. Before mine, almost. The best hand the Guild ever employed." A pause, weighted like a coin on a scale. "Meticulous hands run in families. So do meticulous troubles."

Freya walked down six flights of stairs with her spine straight and her mind screaming.

He knew. Not about the ghost entries specifically — or maybe about those too, she couldn't tell anymore, couldn't separate what she knew from what the fear invented — but he knew *something*, and he had just told her, in his oblique ledger-language, that her family's history was a wound he intended to keep dressed. *Before your time. Before mine, almost.* Her grandmother had worked for the Guild. Her grandmother, whose soul sat in a jar beneath the mountain, whose bargain had bought Ashvale's survival, whose name her mother had refused to speak even on her deathbed.

The Guild kept her close. She had always known that — debt-bound, trained, promoted, housed within the tower walls. She had thought it was because she was valuable. Now, walking down through the floors of quiet pens and quieter clerks, she wondered if she had been kept the way one keeps a collateral asset: near, counted, and ready for collection.

* * *

That afternoon she went down into the deep archive, past the iron gate where the junior clerks weren't permitted, into the rows of shelving where the oldest volumes slept under dust sheets like furniture in a dead man's house. She told herself she was doing research for the reconciliation. She was getting very good at telling herself things.

She pulled the founding registers — the famine years, three centuries back, when Ashvale had starved and the court beneath the mountain had opened its terms. She had read them before, dutifully, as history. Now she read them as evidence.

And there, in the marginalia of the famine register, she found the eighth ghost entry.

Older than the others. The ink browned with age, the page foxed — but the hand was hers. Impossible, and yet: the leaning *h*, the flat-topped *d*, the hooked *r*. A hand three centuries old, waiting for her to be born to write it.

*Correction entered per original testimony, suppressed by order of M.S.C. — the famine was engineered. The bargain was fraud. The soul taken was not owed. See sealed annex, vault of jars, deepest shelf.*

Freya read it four times. The archive was silent around her, that particular archive-silence that felt less like absence of sound than presence of listening. Dust drifted through the lamplight. Somewhere far above, the city went about its day, buying and selling impossible things, every transaction resting on the assumption that the books told the truth.

*M.S.C.* Master Scribe Corvane. Or his predecessor. Or his predecessor's predecessor — the initials passed down like a title, like the office itself, a chain of men who had ordered truths suppressed and signed the orders with the same two letters, generation after generation, until the letters stopped meaning a person and started meaning a function. *Suppressed by order of M.S.C.* could mean any of them.

It could mean all of them.

Her hands were shaking. She set the register down carefully — clerks' habits outlive their courage — and stared at the entry until the words blurred. *The famine was engineered. The bargain was fraud. The soul taken was not owed.*

Her grandmother hadn't sold her soul. Someone had arranged the starvation, arranged the desperation, arranged the bargain — and then taken the soul anyway, and written it up in the books as a fair price paid. Three centuries of Ashvale's gratitude, built on an entry that was a lie. And the ledger — the ledger had known. Had swallowed the correction along with everything else, held it in the dark of its binding, and was only now, with the gods stirring and the dragon-fire dying, beginning to give it back.

In her hand. Always in her hand. Because the truth needed a witness, and the witness it had chosen was the woman who wrote the lies.

"Master Okafor?"

She spun. A junior archivist stood at the end of the row, holding a lantern, young enough that his face still showed everything. "Beg pardon. There's — there's a matter you should see. In the returns tray." He hesitated. "It's marked for you. Your seal."

Her seal. She hadn't sealed anything for the returns tray in weeks.

The tray sat by the archive stairs, a slatted wooden box where misfiled documents waited for re-shelving. On top lay a single folio sheet, folded, stamped with her mark in red wax — accurate to the last groove of the stamp she kept locked in her desk.

She opened it with fingers that refused to be steady.

One page. One entry, centered, in her handwriting, in ink so fresh it gleamed:

*One soul, returned. Price: the scribe.*

Below it, smaller, in the same hand, the date.

The date was the solstice. Nine days from now.

Freya stood in the archive's listening silence, the page trembling in her grip, and understood at last that she was not reading a ledger's ghosts. She was reading a bill of sale. Something in the books — the gods waking, the fire dying, the whole rotten architecture of debt groaning toward its reckoning — had priced her, weighed her, and found her exactly heavy enough to balance the scales.

Behind her, the junior archivist cleared his throat. "Is it bad news, mistress?"

Freya folded the page along its crease and slipped it into her coat, next to her heart, where a clerk keeps the one document she cannot file.

"No," she said, and heard how much it cost her to make the word come out level. "It's an invoice. And I intend to dispute it."
