## 9. Tam's Shadow Returns

The Guild vault had a smell Tam Vess remembered from the worst night of his life: cold tallow, old paper, and something sweeter underneath, like fruit going bad in a locked room. He'd smelled it once before, eleven years ago, when they'd taken his shadow off him at the counter and rolled it up like wet cloth. He'd sworn never to come back.

Sworn things didn't count for much in Ashvale anymore. The gods were answering prayers again, and every rule built on their silence was starting to creak like a floorboard under a fat man's weight.

Tam stood in the drainage culvert beneath Scribe's Row with water to his shins and a stolen key-ring warm against his chest, and told himself the same thing he'd been telling himself for three days: *you're doing this for you. Just you. Your shadow. Nothing else.*

He was a good liar. He'd had to be, to survive the Stacks. But lying to yourself was the one trade even the Guild couldn't broker, because there was no honest party left to hold the contract.

* * *

Getting into the vault proper was the easy part. That was the joke of it — the whole rotten edifice of impossible trade, years of life and borrowed luck and names pried out of desperate mouths, and its security came down to locks, and locks came down to keys, and keys came down to men like Tam, who'd spent a decade learning which guards drank on shift and which ones drank before it.

The hard part was what waited inside.

The vault wasn't a room so much as a throat — long, narrow, descending, lined on both sides with niches cut into the living stone. Each niche held a shelf, and each shelf held the Guild's inventory. He passed them slowly, holding his shuttered lantern through a crack in its slats, letting out just enough light to read by.

A jar of ten years' laughter, sealed with wax the color of a bruise. A folded coat that still breathed faintly, holding somebody's courage. A small lead box labeled in a clerk's neat hand: *one name, unused, condition fair.* And everywhere, everywhere, the paper — contracts, receipts, promissory notes, each one stamped with the seal of dragon-fire, each one a little piece of somebody that the Guild owned outright.

Tam's skin crawled. Not with fear, exactly. With recognition. This was where he lived now, in the world's accounting. A line item. Condition fair.

He found his niche on the left side, forty paces down, exactly where the drunk guard Odo had drawn it for him on the back of a tavern slate in exchange for two silver bits and a promise not to ask questions. There was no label. Shadows didn't get labels; they weren't valuable enough. They were just collateral, the way a poor man's teeth were collateral.

It sat on the shelf coiled like a sleeping snake, flat and dark and somehow *damp*-looking even in the dry air, and when Tam lifted it, it weighed nothing and everything at once. It moved against his hands. Not struggling. Recognizing.

"Oh," he said, stupidly, out loud, in the dark of the Guild's throat. "Oh, hello."

His voice cracked. Eleven years. Eleven years of walking through Ashvale feeling lopsided, feeling like a door with one hinge, feeling the sun pass over him without quite touching him the way it touched other men. People said you got used to it. People lied. You didn't get used to missing a piece of your own soul; you just got quieter about it.

He tucked the shadow inside his coat, against his chest, where it lay cold as river-stone and then, gradually, less cold. Warming. Like something waking up glad.

That was when he saw the jar beside the empty space on the shelf.

* * *

It shouldn't have been there. That was the first thing he noticed, before he even noticed the jar itself — the wrongness of it. His niche was collateral storage. Low-value. Nobody put anything precious next to a smuggler's shadow.

But there it was: a vessel of smoked glass, tall as a forearm, stoppered with black wax stamped twice — once with the dragon-fire seal, and once with a sigil Tam didn't know, a spiral like a snail shell or a curled fern. Inside, instead of the pale gold shimmer he'd seen in the laughter-jar, there was a light like the last coal in a banked fire. Dim. Dimmer than dim. Flickering in a rhythm that took him three full seconds to understand was a rhythm at all.

In and out. Slow. Labored.

Breathing.

There was a label on this one. Of course there was. The Guild labeled everything it loved, and it only loved what it could sell. Tam tilted his lantern and read the clerk's hand, fine and upright:

*One soul, aged, provenance: Okafor. Held in trust per founding bargain, ref. Ashvale Famine Relief, year 212. Payment deferred pending diminution of sealing flame. Do not disturb. Do not transfer. Do not open.*

Okafor.

Tam knew the name the way everyone in Ashvale knew it now — whispered in the markets since the beggar child had walked out of the square healed, since the word went round that the gods were back and the Guild's masters had faces like curdled milk. The scribe. The ledger-woman. The one who'd sat behind the counter with her ink-stained fingers folded and told him, in a voice like a closing door, that his shadow was forfeit per the terms of his contract, and no, there was no exception, and yes, she understood, and no, understanding changed nothing.

She'd been kind about it. That was the worst part. She'd looked at him with something that might have been grief, and then she'd denied him anyway, because the book said so, and she served the book.

And here, on her family's name, in a niche beside a smuggler's junk, was a soul breathing its last.

Tam stood very still. Water dripped somewhere far off. The coal-light in the jar pulsed — in, out, in, out — and each pulse seemed fainter than the last, like footsteps going away down a long hall.

*Do not disturb. Do not transfer.*

He thought: *not my problem.* He thought it firmly, the way you plant your feet in mud. He had his shadow. He had a boat arranged, a route south, a new name already picked out. He was a smuggler, not a hero; heroes in Ashvale ended up as entries, and he'd spent eleven years trying to climb *out* of the ledger, not deeper into it.

Then the light flickered, and for half a heartbeat — he would swear this later, swear it on whatever gods were listening again — the shape inside the glass leaned toward him. Not the light. The shape. Small and bent and old, an outline like a woman stooping under a burden, pressing her palms flat against the inside of the jar the way you press your palms to a window when someone you love is leaving.

Tam Vess had sold his shadow to feed his sister's children. He knew exactly what that pressed-palm gesture cost.

"Damn it," he said. "Damn it, damn it, damn it." Quietly. Because the vault heard everything, and everything in the vault was written down.

He didn't take the jar. He wanted to — his hands actually rose toward it before he caught himself — but the label had said *do not transfer*, and Tam had learned the hard way that the Guild's prohibitions weren't suggestions. Somewhere in those shelves was probably a contract that made moving the jar a violation, and violations triggered collections, and collections meant enforcers, and enforcers meant the descent papers the whole city was whispering about — the scribe Okafor being sent down the mountain with the master ledger, straight into the court beneath, and nobody sent down that road came back up it.

Wait.

Tam went cold all over, colder than the shadow against his chest.

*Payment deferred pending diminution of sealing flame.* Everyone knew the dragon-fire was dying. Everyone had seen the mountain's glow weakening these past months, like a hearth going out in a house full of guests. And if the flame died, the heralds had said, all unpaid debts would be collected at once — violently, the priests said, though the priests had also said the gods would never answer prayers, so maybe take their word for nothing.

Which meant this jar — this dimming, guttering, almost-gone soul — was on a clock. And the woman whose name was on it was being sent down the mountain carrying the master ledger, into the arms of the very court that held her grandmother, wearing Corvane's offer of debt-forgiveness like a gift.

Tam had been a smuggler too long to believe in gifts from rich men.

* * *

He got out the way he'd come in, through the culvert, wading the stinking channel with the shadow warming against his ribs and the image of the jar burned into the backs of his eyes. He didn't go to the docks. He didn't go south. He sat in the doorway of a shuttered dyehouse until dawn, wrapped in his own returned darkness — and oh, it was sweet, the way his body fit its shadow again, like a glove turned right side out — and he thought.

Here was the problem, laid out plain: he could warn her or he could run.

If he ran, he kept his shadow, his boat, his new name. If he warned her, he spent everything he'd just risked his life to steal, because a warning meant staying, meant writing to her, meant putting his mark on Guild-adjacent paper again — and Tam Vess's relationship with paper was the story of how he'd lost his shadow in the first place. He'd signed a bill of lading once without reading past the third line. The third line had contained the words *collateral may include intangibles.* He'd learned to read contracts after that. Learned the way you learn to hate a knife that's been in you.

And she'd denied him. That was the thing he kept circling back to, the stone in his shoe. She'd looked at him with those sad clever eyes and said *the terms are the terms,* and she'd been right, legally, and legality was exactly the problem, and she hadn't lifted one ink-stained finger off the page to help him.

So why warn her?

Because of the pressed palms. Because of the label that said *Okafor* on a soul the Guild kept in a smuggler's niche like contraband, hidden, unlisted, *unwritten* — because if the Guild was hiding it, the Guild was ashamed of it, and shame meant the bargain underneath was rotten, and rot always ran uphill toward the men who profited. Because he'd seen her face when she denied him, and it hadn't been the face of a woman who believed the terms were the terms. It had been the face of a woman memorizing the exact weight of what she'd done, the way he'd memorized the third line of a bill of lading.

She was a prisoner who didn't know she was in a cell. He'd been that prisoner. Somebody had once slipped him the knowledge — a dockhand named Merrin, dead now, who'd read his contract aloud to him in a tavern and watched his face fall apart — and Merrin had gained nothing from it except a debt Tam could never repay.

Truth outvalues contract. Merrin had said that too, drunk, laughing, tapping Tam's forehead. *They can seize everything you own, boy, but they can't seize what you know. That's why they make knowing expensive.*

Tam got up out of the dyehouse doorway as the sky went grey, and went to find paper.

* * *

He couldn't send her a letter. Letters were evidence; letters got people collected. He couldn't tell Odo, or any of the guards, or anyone who owed the Guild anything, which in Ashvale was nearly everyone. What he needed was a message that could sit in plain sight, in the Guild's own house, in the Guild's own language, and be invisible to everyone except the one woman fluent enough in ledgers to read what wasn't written there.

So he did the most dangerous thing he'd ever done, more dangerous than the culvert, more dangerous than the vault: he went to the Guild's public counting-house on Scribe's Row, in broad morning light, wearing his best stolen coat, and he asked to file an amendment.

Amendments were the small change of the trade — corrections, updates, minor reconciliations filed by clients too unimportant to warrant a meeting. They went into a tray. The tray went upstairs. A junior scribe logged them, and among the junior scribes, everyone knew, the senior scribe Okafor reviewed anything touching legacy accounts, because she was meticulous, because she was complicit, because she was *hers* — the Guild's creature, its best pen.

Tam wrote his amendment slowly, in a careful merchant's hand he'd practiced for exactly this kind of deception, on the counter's public slip. Around him the counting-house murmured: clerks, petitioners, a weeping man arguing about a deferred payment, the ordinary music of a business that traded in souls. His shadow lay flat and obedient at his feet, and he felt it there, felt it *his*, and the feeling steadied his hand.

The amendment read:

*RE: Account 212-O (legacy, famine relief). Client requests reconciliation of standing balance prior to solstice. Notes discrepancy: item listed as held in trust appears, upon inspection, to show depreciation beyond schedule. Value diminishing faster than contracted rate. Requests urgent audit of storage conditions. Client further notes that deferred payment clauses tied to sealing-flame output are, per standard practice, voidable at client's discretion should asset quality fall below recoverable threshold. Time-sensitive. Signed, T. Vess, factor for the estate of Maren Okafor.*

Every word of it was false. He was no factor. There was no estate. But every word of it was *shaped* true — shaped to catch a meticulous eye like a fishhook catches a lip. An account showing abnormal depreciation. Storage conditions in question. A soul depreciating faster than schedule — *dimming, scribe, your grandmother is dimming, look at your books, look at what they've hidden in the low-value shelves next to the trash.* And the hook beneath the bait, the part only she would feel snag her: *voidable at client's discretion.* The language of the gods' return, the precedent everyone was whispering about — answered prayers voiding old bargains. He was telling her: *the rules are changing. You can still use them. Use them fast.*

And the signature — *Maren Okafor* — that was the bell. He'd gotten the name off the jar's reference line and gambled it was the grandmother. If it was, the name alone would stop Freya Okafor's heart for a full second, and a stopped heart reads a second line, and a second line reads a third.

Third lines. He smiled grimly at the counter as he slid the slip into the amendments tray. Everything important lived in the third line.

The clerk on duty barely glanced at it. "Logged by close of day," she said, bored, stamping the tray. "Next."

Tam walked out onto Scribe's Row into the thin morning light, and his shadow poured off his heels ahead of him, long and dark and *present*, and he almost laughed at the strangeness of it — a man walking home richer than he'd ever been, having just bet the whole fortune on a woman who'd once told him no.

He gave her two days. Two days for the tray to climb, for the junior scribe to log it, for the meticulous one to pull account 212-O and find the niche number and go down into the vault herself and see what he'd seen.

And if she didn't come — if she was already on the pilgrim road, or if Corvane's leash was shorter than Tam guessed, or if she read the amendment and chose the book over the blood — then Tam would do the thing he'd sworn not to do. He'd go back into the throat of the Guild, past the sleeping guards, down to the low-value shelves, and he'd take the jar himself, and run south with a smuggler's cargo worth more than every ship in Ashvale harbor, pursued by every enforcer the Guild could loose.

He'd deny her the way she'd denied him: kindly, sadly, with full understanding, and no exceptions.

But looking up at the Guild towers against the brightening sky — dark-windowed, patient, hungry — Tam didn't think it would come to that. He'd seen her face across the counter. He'd seen a woman keeping a ledger of her own sins in perfect double-entry, waiting for someone to give her a reason to balance it.

Two days, he told his shadow, and his shadow, being his, said nothing, and stayed.

Up on the fourth floor of the counting-house, unseen, a slip of paper entered the amendments tray, bearing a name that had not appeared in the Guild's books for sixty years — and somewhere below the mountain, in a cathedral of jars lit by a dying fire, an old soul's flicker stuttered, and quickened, just slightly, like a sleeper hearing footsteps on the stairs.
