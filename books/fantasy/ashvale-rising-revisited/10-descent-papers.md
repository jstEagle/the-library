## 10. Descent Papers

The copying began at midnight, because that was when the Guild's house went quietest — when even Corvane's lamp behind his office door guttered low and the scribes' dormitory settled into the shallow breathing of people who dreamed in columns of figures.

Freya did not sleep. She had not truly slept since the mountain's mouth, since the herald's voice had wrapped around her name like a hand closing on her wrist. *Okafor.* The court beneath the mountain knew her bloodline the way a lock knows its key.

She sat at her own desk with a single candle shielded by her ink-stained palm, and before her lay three things: a plain notebook bought from a stationer who asked no questions, a fresh quill cut that afternoon, and her memory.

The master ledger itself sat thirty feet away and two floors down, in the vault behind triple seals, and she could not touch it. Tam's warning still lived in her ribs like a swallowed coal: *the jar beside my shadow is dimming, Freya. Whatever you're going to do, do it fast.* She had decoded his false entry in an hour — he'd hidden it in a shipping manifest for tallow, of all things, the numbers wobbling just enough to catch a clerk's eye — and now there was nothing left to do but the one thing no Guild scribe had ever dared.

She would copy it. All of it. From memory.

Fourteen years of entries. Fourteen years of trades in years and luck and names and last words, every figure she had ever balanced, every debt she had ever recorded in her small, exact hand. The Guild believed memory was safe because memory was owned — because a scribe bound by debt would never betray the ledger that held her leash. They had never imagined a scribe who understood that the ledger was not the truth. It was only the record of the truth, and records could be stolen twice: once by the powerful, and once again by anyone brave enough to write them back.

Freya dipped her quill.

*Entry the first,* she wrote, and her hand did not shake. That surprised her. Her hands shook at market stalls, at the temple, at the sound of her mother's name spoken aloud. But here, in the dark, doing the thing that would unmake her if she were caught, her hand was steady as a rule-line.

*Widow Anselm. One husband's final words, purchased at grief-rate, sealed under dragon-fire, term perpetual.*

She remembered the widow. Small woman, grey braid, hands folded like a closed book. She had wept quietly through the whole transaction, and Freya had recorded her tears in the margin as *client affectation, noted for rate adjustment.* Freya wrote that margin note out now too, word for word, and felt it land in her chest like a stone dropped down a well. There. Let the copy carry its shame along with its testimony. A ledger without the clerk's cruelty in it would be just another lie.

*Merchant Voss. Ten years of daughter's laughter, sold against future harvests.*

*Cooper Halden. Three decades of life, transferred to the Guild's second account, purpose unstated.*

Page after page. The candle burned down; she trimmed the wick with her thumbnail and kept writing. Somewhere past the third hour the work stopped feeling like theft and started feeling like something older — like the temple lessons she'd taken as a child, before the debt, when copying scripture had been an act of devotion. This was devotion too. Just to a different god. To the plain, stubborn god of *this happened.*

The unwritten entries were harder.

Those were the ones that had been appearing in her own handwriting since the gods came back — transactions she had never made, figures she had never set down, all in a hand indistinguishable from hers. *One soul, returned, price: the scribe.* She had spent nights telling herself she was mad, or cursed, or dreaming. But sitting here with the true entries flowing out of her, she noticed something she should have noticed weeks ago: the unwritten entries were *better* than her real ones. Cleaner. More honest about what the trades actually cost. Where the master ledger said *laughter, ten years, fair rate*, the ghost entries said *laughter, ten years, taken from a child who will not understand until she is grown why the house feels empty.*

They weren't corruptions. She was almost sure of it now. They were corrections — or arguments, at least. The ledger talking back. And if the ledger could talk back, then somewhere inside those triple-sealed vault doors was a witness that had been waiting three centuries for someone to listen.

She copied the ghost entries too. All of them, exactly as they had appeared, and marked each one in the margin: *not written by this hand.* If she died on the road below the mountain, let whoever found the notebook know the difference between her sins and the book's.

* * *

Dawn found her with forty pages done and her right hand cramping into a claw. She flexed it, blew out the candle, and sat listening to the house wake around her — boots on stairs, the kitchen fire being laid, the ordinary machinery of an institution built on souls grinding toward another day.

That was when she heard the step on the landing.

She knew it the way she knew her own heartbeat. Master Scribe Corvane walked softly for a man whose footsteps everyone feared, but softness had a texture, and she had catalogued it over fourteen years the way she catalogued everything. He stopped outside her door. He did not knock.

For a long moment there was only silence, and Freya sat perfectly still with the notebook face-down beneath a blank sheet of practice paper, her heart performing the slow, deliberate arithmetic of a woman deciding whether to be afraid.

Then the steps moved on, unhurried, descending.

He hadn't looked. Or he had looked and chosen blindness. With Corvane she would not know which until it was far too late to matter, and possibly not even then.

* * *

She finished the copying over three more nights, working in fragments — an hour before matins, twenty minutes while the tea steeped, a full stretch on the night Corvane dined with the Guild's masters and the house belonged to the junior clerks. By the fourth dawn the notebook held everything: fourteen years of the true ledger, the ghost entries, and — written last, in letters smaller than the rest, as though smallness could make them safer — the things that were not ledger-work at all.

Her grandmother's name: Maren Okafor. The famine year. The bargain deferred until dragon-fire dims. The jar in the deepest vault, closest to the dying flame. Tam's shadow, shelf nine, third row, already returned to him in secret — she had signed off on that theft herself, quietly, and slept better for it. And beneath all of it, one line she had rewritten four times before letting stand:

*The famine bargain was not my grandmother's greed. I believe this now. I intend to prove it below the mountain, or die holding the proof.*

There. Let it be a confession as well as a weapon. A copy of the master ledger was contraband worth killing for; a copy annotated with the copier's beliefs was a testimony, and testimonies were harder to bury. Corvane could burn a stolen book. It was much harder to burn an argument.

She sewed the notebook into the lining of her scribe's coat herself, unpicking the seam along the left breast with a sailmaker's stitch her mother had taught her, laying the pages flat against the space where a heart would sit if she still had one that worked properly, and closing the seam so fine that only a tailor looking for it would find it. Then she put the coat on and wore it to breakfast.

* * *

Corvane was waiting in the scriptorium.

Not lurking — Corvane did not lurk; he *presided*. He stood at the great window with his hands clasped behind his back, watching the morning fog burn off the rooftops of Ashvale, and when Freya entered he did not turn at once. He let her cross the whole room. He let her feel every step.

"Master Okafor." He used the honorific the way other men used shackles. "You leave tomorrow."

"At first light," she said. "The herald's terms allow five days. We're on the fourth."

"The terms allow what the terms allow." He turned then, and his eyes went over her the way they went over a page — top to bottom, margin to margin, missing nothing. She stood still and let him read her. It was the hardest discipline of her life. "You've been working late."

"Reconciling the descent accounts. You ordered the books clean before I go."

"I did." A faint smile, dry as old vellum. "And are they?"

"Cleaner than they've been in years."

Something moved behind his eyes — not suspicion exactly. Recognition. The look of a man who has caught a familiar scent and is deciding whether to name it. "You know," he said, conversationally, moving along the shelves, trailing one finger across the spines of ledgers going back a century, "when I took this post, thirty years ago, my predecessor gave me one piece of advice. He said: *the ledger does not remember, Corvane. Only the scribes remember. So keep the scribes small.*"

"And have you?"

"Mm?" He glanced back at her.

"Kept us small."

"I've kept you *precise*." He drew out a ledger at random, opened it, ran his thumb down a column, closed it. "Precision is a kind of smallness, I suppose. A very useful kind." He slid the volume home. "The court beneath the mountain will make you offers, Master Okafor. The herald will speak of inheritance, of blood, of destiny. They are very good at making a cage sound like a cradle. Your family has experience of their rhetoric."

"My family," Freya said carefully, "has experience of their contracts."

"One and the same, historically." He smiled again, and this time it reached his eyes, which was worse. "Bring the master ledger down safely. Deliver it into the herald's hands personally. Do that, and your debts are forgiven — every entry, every year, back to the day you were indentured. You'll walk out of Ashvale owing nothing to anyone."

*I'll walk out owing nothing because I'll be the payment,* she thought. *One soul, returned, price: the scribe.* The ghost entry sat against her left breast, stitched into her coat, and she wondered — not for the first time — whether the ledger had known about this meeting before either of them did.

"You're generous, Master Corvane."

"I'm an accountant." He gathered his papers from the lectern, squared their edges with two small taps. "Generosity is just a debt with better manners." At the door he paused, half-turned, and looked at her one last time — a long, level look that traveled, quite deliberately, from her face down to her left breast and back up again.

"Wear a warm coat on the road," he said. "The mountain is cold this time of year. Colder than it looks."

Then he was gone, his footsteps fading down the stair, and Freya stood alone in the scriptorium with her heart hammering against forty stolen pages.

He knew. He had to know. He had watched her copy it — or he had weighed the bulge of a seam, or read the sleeplessness in her face, or simply understood that a woman like her, cornered like this, would do exactly what she had done. And he had said nothing. Had sent her down anyway, carrying her weapon and her confession straight into the dark, because — because what? Because he wanted the copy made? Because a ledger in the court's hands served him better than a ledger in his vault?

Or because he was certain that whatever she carried, the mountain would take it from her, along with everything else.

Freya pressed her palm flat against her chest, over the hidden stitching, over the small hard rectangle of truth that beat there instead of a heart.

Tomorrow at first light, Tam would be waiting at the pilgrim gate with two mules, a smuggler's map of the old roads, and a wound that hadn't finished closing. Below the mountain, a dragon's flame was guttering toward its last breath, and in the deepest vault a jar held a lullaby her mother used to sing.

She took up her travel case. She checked the seals on her descent papers — her own name written there in Corvane's hand, granting passage, promising forgiveness, lying in perfect copperplate — and she blew out the lamps one by one, leaving the scriptorium dark behind her.

At the door she paused, and against every instinct fourteen years of service had drilled into her, she did not look back at the shelves where the master ledger slept.

Let it wonder where she'd gone.
