## 11. The Road Beneath

**Freya**

The pilgrim road to the mountain was built for penitents, and it treated us like penitents.

It began gently enough — a gravel track through the ash-fields north of Ashvale, where the soil was grey and fine as flour and nothing grew but the pale, stubborn moss the locals called forgiveness. By noon the road had begun to climb, switchbacking up through stands of black-pine that smelled of resin and old smoke, and by dusk it had narrowed to a ledge cut into the mountain's shoulder, wide enough for one traveler, or two who did not mind walking pressed close.

Tam Vess walked close.

He had insisted on taking the mountain side of the path — "so if you slip, you fall on me, and I'm worth less" — which was either chivalry or arithmetic, and with Tam it was impossible to say which. He carried his pack on one shoulder like a man carrying a grudge: lightly, permanently, ready to set it down or swing it at someone's head. His shadow moved when he moved, whole again, and I still caught myself staring at it the way you stare at a healed wound.

"You're doing the thing," he said without turning around.

"What thing?"

"The scribe thing. Where you look at me like I'm an entry you're trying to reconcile." He stepped over a fallen branch. "I've been reconciled, Freya. I balance. Mostly."

"I wasn't—"

"You were. It's all right. You've been looking at everything like that since we left the city. The road. The sky. Me." He glanced back, and in the slanting light his face was all angles and amusement over something that wasn't amused at all. "You're waiting for one of us to be a lie."

I said nothing, because he was right, and because the path had narrowed to a hand's width of crumbling stone and lying to him while watching my feet felt like too much dishonesty for one moment to hold.

The truth was this: I had spent my whole life believing that what was written was what was true. Ink made things real; ink made things binding; ink was the closest a mortal came to godhood. And now I walked toward the court beneath the mountain with a stolen copy of the master ledger sewn into my coat lining, forty pages of my own memory transcribed in my own hand, and I no longer knew whether I was carrying the truth or merely another version of it. The unwritten entries had been corrections — I believed that now, mostly, in daylight, when I let myself believe anything. But belief is a currency too, and I had been poor before.

The mountain grew ahead of us as the light failed, black against a bruised violet sky. At its summit, faint as a dying candle seen through smoked glass, the dragon-fire pulsed.

Once. Then nothing. Then once again.

Each pulse arrived slower than the last. I had started counting them out of habit — a scribe counts, it's what we do instead of praying — and the intervals had stretched from a hundred heartbeats to nearly three hundred since morning.

"How long?" Tam asked quietly. He'd noticed me counting. Of course he had.

"If the intervals keep lengthening the way they have been—" I did the arithmetic without wanting to. "Two days. Perhaps three. Before the flame goes out entirely."

"And then every debt comes due at once." He said it flatly, the way you recite a sentence you've been made to memorize. "Every soul the Guild ever bought. Every jar in that cathedral under the rock. Collected. Violently. All at the same moment."

"Yes."

"And Corvane wants that. He *engineered* that." Tam's voice didn't rise; it went quieter, which was worse. "The man sent you down here carrying his ledger like a lamb carrying its own slaughter."

"I know why he sent me."

"Do you? Because I've been turning it over, and there's a piece that doesn't sit right." He stopped walking. Below us the road dropped away into a valley full of evening mist, and somewhere in that mist, though we couldn't see them yet, were the shrine-towns where pilgrims had prayed for three centuries to gods who never answered — until last month, when one of them answered. "The enforcers. The Guild's own blades. If Corvane wants you to reach the court, why would he send men to stop you?"

I had no answer. I had noticed the same wrongness and filed it, the way I filed everything, in the part of my mind where unresolved entries go to wait.

We made camp that night in the shell of a wayside chapel, one of hundreds strung along the pilgrim road like beads on a rosary. Its roof had fallen in generations ago; the altar stone was furred with moss, and someone long dead had scratched a prayer into it in Old Ashvale script: *LET THE ACCOUNT BE SETTLED IN MERCY.* I ran my fingers over the letters while Tam built a small fire, shielding the flame with his body against the wind off the peak.

"Eat," he said, handing me dried fish and a heel of bread gone hard as slate. "You think better fed."

"I think fine."

"You think like someone reading her own death warrant looking for a clerical error." He tore his bread with his teeth. "Which, fair. But eat anyway."

I ate. Above the broken roof, the stars came out in their cold multitudes, and the dragon-fire pulsed against them, dimmer than it had been at noon. I counted the interval. Three hundred and forty heartbeats.

"Tam," I said. "Why are you here?"

He looked at me across the fire for a long moment. Wind moved through the pines below the chapel, a sound like the sea heard in a dream.

"Because you denied me," he said. "In your office, with the law open on the desk between us, you told me no. By the letter. You made me stand there and hear that my own shadow belonged to the Guild until the Guild said otherwise, and you said it kindly, which was the cruelest part." He turned something in his hands — a pebble, I realized; he was worrying a pebble between his fingers like a man counting prayer-beads. "And then, two weeks later, I find a false entry in a vault ledger in my own handwriting that I never wrote, telling me where my shadow was kept and where yours was going. Same hand that denied me. Same ink."

"The ledger wrote it. Not me."

"I know." He tossed the pebble into the fire. "That's the part I can't stop thinking about. Your ledgers have started telling the truth, Freya. Mine never did. So I figured — somebody ought to walk beside the one honest book in Ashvale and make sure it gets where it's going." He lay back against his pack, hands behind his head, and closed his eyes as if the conversation were over. "Also, you'll get yourself killed without me. But that's secondary."

I sat by the fire long after his breathing slowed into sleep, listening to the wind, counting the pulses of the dying fire above, and understanding — slowly, the way frost forms, all at once only after being gradual — that I had not been alone in years, and had not known it until now.

**Tam**

Here's what nobody tells you about getting your shadow back: you spend the first week flinching from it.

A shadow is a record. That's what they took from me, all those years ago, when I traded it for passage out of a debt I couldn't pay — not the dark shape on the ground, but the proof of me. A man without a shadow casts no evidence. Nothing he does can be traced back to him, which sounds like freedom and is actually the opposite: it means nothing he does belongs to him either. For six years I was a rumor wearing boots.

Now I cast evidence again, and I kept catching myself checking — glancing down at walls, at the firelight on the chapel stones — to see if I was still there.

I was. And next to me, asleep sitting up with the ledger-copy sewn against her ribs like a second heart, was the woman who'd put it back.

I hadn't told her the whole reason. The whole reason was embarrassing. The whole reason was that when she said *no* to me in that office — clean, legal, immovable, sorry, Mr. Vess, the contract stands — I had wanted to hate her, and instead I'd thought: *she hates this more than I do.* You can tell. Hate is loud; hers was quiet, which meant it was structural, which meant it was load-bearing. She was holding up a building she despised and calling it loyalty because the alternative was admitting she lived inside a cage with the door painted on.

I'd known that look my whole life. Every smuggler in the lowtown has it. We just don't pretend it's loyalty.

So when the false entry appeared — *TAM VESS, SHADOW, RETURNED, PRICE: WITNESS* — I understood it the way you understand a friend's handwriting. The ledger wanted a witness. Fine. I could witness.

I woke before dawn, because I always wake before dawn, old habit from years when sleeping past light meant losing cargo or skin. The valley below the chapel was white with mist, and the mist was moving wrong.

Mist moves with the wind. This was moving against it — flowing uphill along the road in a slow, deliberate sheet, the way water finds a channel. And beneath the hush of it I could hear, very faintly, the sound I'd spent six years learning to fear: the creak of leather, the whisper of a blade clearing a scabbard by inches, the particular silence of men trying not to be heard.

"Freya." I didn't whisper. Whispering wakes people gentler, but it also gives them time to argue. "Freya. Up. Now."

She came awake the way she did everything — instantly, completely, as if a bell had been struck inside her. One heartbeat she was asleep; the next her eyes were open and her hand was already at the satchel where she kept the copy of the ledger, pulling it against her chest.

"How many?" she breathed.

"At least four. Maybe more under the mist. Coming up the road." I was already kicking dirt over the fire, killing the light. "Guild pattern. They fanned out — one on the road, others in the trees. They don't want to kill us quick. They want the ledger and then the cliff, and they want it to look like pilgrims fell."

"They're supposed to be *ours*." Her voice had a crack in it, thin as ice. "Corvane's own—"

"Yeah. Think about that later." I pulled her toward the back of the chapel, where the wall had crumbled into a slope of tumbled stone leading up into the pines. "Road's done. We go high, through the trees, cut across the shoulder of the mountain. It'll cost us half a day."

"Half a day we don't have."

"Dead costs more."

She looked at me — the scribe-look, the reconciling-look — and then, for the first time since I'd met her, she threw it away. Something else came up underneath, something quicker and older and hungrier, and she nodded once and started climbing.

* * *

**Freya**

I had never run from anything in my life. Scribes do not run; scribes endure, scribes file, scribes wait for the error to surface and correct it in red ink. But I ran now, up through the black pines with Tam's breath ragged beside mine and the mist boiling up the slope behind us, and some animal part of my brain that had been silent for thirty-one years woke up and started giving orders that my clerk's mind obeyed with startled gratitude.

*Low branch. Duck. Loose stone — weight on the uphill edge. There — game trail, follow it.*

The enforcers came through the mist below us like ghosts with crossbows. I saw one clearly for a moment through the trunks: Guild grey, face masked, the ash-and-quill sigil stitched on his shoulder. I had signed his pay ledgers. I knew his name was probably in my books somewhere, his wages, his equipment, the cost of the bolts in his quiver, all of it entered in my neat hand. He raised his crossbow at the movement of Tam's pack through the trees, and I understood with terrible clarity that I was watching a line item come to collect us.

"Down!" Tam shoved me behind a granite outcrop as the bolt cracked past, splintering bark a hand's width from where my head had been.

They were herding us. I saw it even as my lungs burned — the climbers below cutting off the road, the ones above us on the ridge driving us downslope, funneling us toward the cliffs on the mountain's eastern shoulder where the path simply ended above a drop of two hundred feet into the cloud. It was well done. It was exactly how I would have planned it, if I planned murders, which until this week I had not believed anyone in Ashvale did.

"Why?" I gasped, as we scrambled sideways along the outcrop. "Corvane *wants* me at the court—"

"No." Tam's face was grey with effort and something worse than effort. "Think it through. Corvane wants the ledger delivered and you bound. But the rest of the Guild — the masters, the enforcer captains, everyone whose soul sits in a jar under that mountain — what do *they* want?"

I did the arithmetic. It took half a second and it was the worst sum of my life.

"If the descent fails... if I die on the road and the ledger is lost..."

"Then there's no renegotiation. No substitution. When the dragon dies, the debts get collected — but scattered, disputed, *contested*. The court can't enforce cleanly. Some souls slip free in the chaos. The masters are gambling that they're among them." He laughed, short and bitter, ducking a branch. "Corvane bet on order. His own people just bet on ruin. Welcome to the Guild, Freya. Even the knives have factions."

The trees ended.

There was no warning to it — one stride we were in pine-shadow, the next the world dropped away and we stood on a shelf of bare rock above a sea of cloud, the eastern face of the mountain falling sheer beneath us. Behind, the enforcers emerged from the treeline, five of them now, spreading out along the shelf with the unhurried confidence of men closing a ledger.

The leader pulled down his mask. I knew the face — Captain Dree Hale, twenty years in the Guild's service, a man whose daughter's tuition I had personally entered as a charitable disbursement. He looked at me almost apologetically.

"Scribe Okafor. Give the master the book and walk home. Nobody needs to be an entry today."

"The master sent me down," I said. "You're defying Corvane."

Hale's mouth twisted. "Corvane bet our souls on one roll of the dice. We're just... hedging." He raised his hand, and the crossbows came up. "Last kindness, scribe. The book."

My hand found the ledger through my coat, over my heart, where it had ridden for nine days like a second organ. Everything in me that had been trained, curated, owned, said: *give it to him, survive, negotiate, live to file another day.*

Everything in me that had woken up on this mountain said otherwise.

"Hale," I said, and my voice came out steady, which surprised us both. "Your daughter's tuition. Disbursement 4471, autumn ledger, marked charitable. It wasn't charity. It was collateral against your compliance. Check the marginal note. In red." I watched him flinch. "The Guild owns you the same way it owns me. The only question is whether we die as its entries or walk away as something else. I'm asking you — as the woman who wrote your numbers — put the bow down."

For one heartbeat, I thought it worked. His crossbow dipped a degree. Behind him, one of his men shifted uncertainly.

Then the man beside Hale — young, masked, unknown to me — fired.

Not at me.

At Tam, who had edged along the shelf during my speech, putting himself between me and the line of fire, the way he'd taken the mountain side of the path, the way he did everything — placing his cheaper life in front of mine.

I screamed his name. He turned — already turning, already seeing it — and the bolt took him high in the chest, punching through the collar of his coat, and he went down onto the rock with a sound that I will hear for the rest of my unrecorded life.

What happened after that happened fast, and I remember it in fragments, the way ledgers record disasters: in separate lines, without connective tissue.

Hale roaring *hold, HOLD, that wasn't the order—*

The young archer reaching for a second bolt and Hale's fist taking him across the jaw, the Guild tearing itself open in front of me—

Me, on my knees in Tam's blood, which was spreading across the pale stone shockingly fast, shockingly red, the first color on the whole grey mountain—

His hand finding mine, gripping hard, his other hand pressing cloth to the wound above his collarbone, blood pushing up between his fingers with every heartbeat.

"Still," he gritted, "still worth less than you. Told you. Arithmetic."

"Shut up." My voice broke on it. "Shut up, shut up—"

"Frey." His eyes found mine, and they were clear, terribly clear. "The book. Get the book to the fire. Whatever it costs. That's the price I paid — *witness* — so witness. Go."

Above us, through the thinning cloud, the dragon-fire pulsed.

Once.

And I got my arms under Tam Vess and lifted, this clerk, this scribe, this woman made of ink and obedience, and I carried him — stumbling, sobbing, stronger than I had ever been in my careful recorded life — up the pilgrim road toward the mouth of the mountain, while behind us the Guild's enforcers fought each other in the mist, and ahead of us the last fire in the world waited to see what we would feed it.
