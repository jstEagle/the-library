## 12. Court of Quiet Things

The mountain did not have a door. It had a throat.

Freya had imagined a gate, perhaps — iron-banded oak, a lock that wanted a key, something she could pick or bribe or burn. Instead the pilgrim road simply ended where the rock began, and the rock opened like a mouth that had been waiting a very long time to be fed. The air that breathed out of it was warm and smelled of hot copper and old smoke and, underneath everything, faintly of honey.

Tam leaned against the stone beside the entrance, one arm pressed to his ribs where the enforcer's blade had gone in below the shoulder. The bandage Freya had bound there was dark at its center, but the bleeding had slowed, and he could still walk, which was more than she'd dared hope an hour ago on the road when he'd gone gray as ash and she'd thought — for three terrible minutes — that she would carry him down alone or not at all.

"You don't have to come," she said. She said it again because he hadn't answered the first time.

"I heard you." Tam pushed himself upright. His shadow moved with him now, whole and black and obedient, and every time she glanced at it Freya felt a small fierce gladness she couldn't afford to feel yet. "You don't have to come either. That's the difference between us. I'm coming anyway."

"Why?"

"Because you denied me by the letter of a law you hated," he said, "and then you burned your own name out of your guild's book rather than let them use you. A man can follow worse reasons down a hole." He looked past her, into the dark. "Besides. If there's a vault down there, there's an accounting of my shadow. I want to see it with my own eyes before anything else happens."

*Before anything else happens.* As if they both didn't know what else might happen. The dragon-fire was dying. The herald's warning from the mountain's mouth still rang in Freya's ears: *when the flame goes out, all debts are collected at once.* They had perhaps two days. Maybe less. The flicker she'd seen through the crack in the world had been slower since — she could feel it somehow, the way you feel a storm coming in your teeth.

She touched the scribe's coat, where the copied ledger lay flat against her ribs. Forty-one pages in her own hand, written from memory over three sleepless nights while Corvane watched her do it and said nothing. The memory of his silence was heavier than the paper.

"Stay behind me," she said.

"In there?" Tam almost smiled. "I'll stay beside you. Behind you is where people get stabbed."

They went in together.

* * *

The passage descended in long spirals, cut so smoothly the walls felt polished, and the light came from nowhere — a gray-gold glow that lived in the stone itself, dimming and brightening in a slow rhythm, like breathing. Like a pulse. Freya counted the rhythm without meaning to; it was a scribe's habit, counting things, and it took her forty steps to understand what she was counting.

The light pulsed in time with the dragon-fire far above. Every swell of the flame in the mountain's throat sent a breath of gold down through the rock, and every falter sent a shiver of darkness after it. They were walking inside the body of a dying fire, and the whole mountain knew it.

"The jars," Tam said quietly.

She'd seen them too. They began sparse — one niche every hundred steps, a clay vessel no bigger than a wine jug, sealed with wax and dragon-fire, glowing faintly from within with a light that had nothing to do with the flame overhead. Each niche bore a small brass plate, and each plate bore a line of script, and Freya made herself read the first one because not reading it would be worse.

*Tam Vess. One shadow. Held in trust.*

"It's here," Tam said. He sounded strange — half vindicated, half hollow. "They kept the record even down here." He reached toward the jar and stopped, fingers trembling an inch from the wax seal. "It's warm."

"Don't," Freya said. "Not yet. We don't know what breaks what down here."

He withdrew his hand slowly, curling the fingers closed like a man putting away something precious. "You said *not yet.*"

"I said what I said."

They went deeper, and the niches grew closer together, and then they stopped being niches at all.

* * *

The court beneath the mountain was a cathedral.

That was the only word for it, and it was the wrong word, because cathedrals were built by hands and this had been grown — the vaulted ceiling arched up and up into darkness ribbed like the inside of a shell, and the pillars were not pillars but flowstone frozen mid-pour, and everywhere, everywhere, were the jars.

Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. They lined the walls in shelves carved floor to ceiling, they hung in nets from the vaults like lanterns, they stood in terraced rings around a central dais, each one glowing with its own small color — amber, green, a blue like deep ice, a red like embers under ash. The combined light should have been blinding, but it wasn't; it was soft, diffused, the way light is soft underwater. And above it all, visible through a shaft in the ceiling that rose beyond sight, the dragon-fire guttered — a vast orange heartbeat, stuttering now, each pulse weaker than the last, casting shadows that trembled across ten thousand jars like wind through wheat.

The sound was the worst part. Or the best. Freya couldn't decide.

From every jar came a voice — not words, not exactly. Murmurs. Fragments. A woman laughing at something remembered. A man repeating a name over and over, gentle, like a prayer. A child humming. Snatches of song in languages dead three centuries. It layered and wove until it became a single oceanic murmur, the sound of a city asleep, and standing in the middle of it Freya understood that she was standing inside the Guild's true treasury, and the treasury was full of people.

"They're all still *in there,*" Tam whispered. "I thought — I don't know what I thought. That souls were like coins. That they just sat."

"They're dreaming," Freya said. Her voice came out flat because if she let it do anything else she would not get through this room. "Or something like it. Look at the plates."

She read as she walked, because reading was the only tool she had. The brass lines ran in families: *held in trust. held in surety. held per contract of the famine year. held per contract of the plague year. held in perpetuity.* Some jars glowed bright and restless; some had dimmed to a coal-red ember-glimmer, and those were the oldest, and beside their plates someone had added small second inscriptions in fresh wax:

*dimming. review at collection.*

"Review at collection," Tam read aloud, and spat. "Like reviewing stock."

"Like reviewing stock," Freya agreed, and hated how steady her voice stayed. This was the thing the Guild had taught her that she could never unlearn: how to keep speaking in inventory while her chest filled with screaming.

They crossed the cathedral floor toward the central dais, and the murmuring changed around them. Not louder — *attentive*. The way a room of sleeping people shifts when someone new enters. Voices surfaced out of the wash, close to the path, curious: a woman saying *who is that, who is that,* a man singing three notes of a harvest song and stopping, a voice — old, cracked, amused — saying clearly, in the accent of the old Ashvale river-district, *that girl walks like a ledger.*

Freya stopped walking.

"What is it?" Tam said.

"Nothing." Everything. "Keep moving."

But the voices followed them now, softly, a tide coming in. *Okafor,* said one. *That's an Okafor walk,* said another. *Scribe-hands,* said a third. *Look at the way she reads the room, left shelf to right, top row down — that's a reader, that's one of ours—*

One of ours.

Her grandmother had walked this floor. Had been carried down it, or had walked it herself, sixty years ago, and these dreaming souls had watched her pass the way they were watching Freya pass now, and some of them had known her name.

At the base of the dais, the herald was waiting.

* * *

She had seen him once before, at the mountain's mouth, and had told herself she'd misremembered — that no living face could look like that. But memory had been kind. Up close, the herald was worse and stranger: tall past any natural proportion, robed in gray that moved like smoke, with a face that was smooth and unfinished, like wax warmed but not yet shaped, features suggested rather than committed. Only the eyes were finished, and they were ancient, and they were kind, which was somehow the most frightening thing about him.

"Scribe," he said. His voice resonated in the stone, in the jars, in Freya's own ribs. "You are expected. You have been expected for sixty years."

"We're not here on the Guild's business," Freya said. "Whatever Corvane told you—"

"He told us nothing. He tells us nothing. He has never once, in all his years, spoken truly to this court." The herald tilted the unfinished face, studying her. "You speak of him as though we are his. Scribe. Nothing down here belongs to the Guild. The Guild merely holds the *paper.*"

"Then give me my grandmother's soul," Freya said, "and keep the paper."

Something moved across the smooth face — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would go, like a crease forming in cloth. The herald raised one long hand, and the murmur of the cathedral hushed, jar by jar, ring by ring, until the silence rolled outward from the dais like a wave and the whole vast chamber held its breath.

Then the herald spoke, and his voice was quiet enough that only she and Tam could hear it, and it was worse than the loudness had been.

"Adaeze Okafor," he said. "Sold in the famine year, payment deferred against the dimming of the flame. Daughter of Nkiru, who refused to speak of her. Grandmother of the scribe who keeps the books." The ancient eyes did not blink. "You have her hands. When you write, your little finger lifts, exactly as hers did. She sat where you are standing and bargained for a city, and the city never learned her name, and she never once asked it to."

Freya's eyes were burning. She would not wipe them. Not here.

"You knew her," Tam said quietly.

"We hold her. Knowing is different." The herald turned, robes flowing like poured smoke, and gestured toward the innermost ring of jars — the ones closest to the shaft of guttering fire, where the light fell strongest and the heat of the dying dragon breathed down like a hearth. "Come. She is kept near the flame. The oldest contracts are kept nearest, so that when the fire dies, they die first. Your Guild calls this honor." The crease again, the almost-smile. "We call it scheduling."

* * *

The innermost ring was quieter than the rest of the cathedral. The jars here were older, their glow deeper and dimmer, and the voices inside them had gone soft and slow, like songs sung at the end of a long day. Freya walked the ring with her heart hammering against the copied ledger hidden against her ribs, reading plate after plate, names of the famine year, names of the plague year, and then—

A jar on a low shelf of black stone, apart from the others. Clay, not glass. Unadorned. No brass plate. Just a single line scratched into the wax seal itself, in a hurried hand, in the old river-district script:

*Adaeze Okafor. Paid in full. Ask her granddaughter why.*

Freya knelt.

The jar was dim — dimmer than any she'd passed, its inner light a thread of gold no brighter than a candle seen through a closed door. Sixty years of deferral, burning down alongside the dragon-fire above, and she could see now with terrible clarity what *review at collection* meant. Another season. Another month, maybe. And then the thread would go out, and whatever was dreaming inside it would go where dreams go when no one remembers them.

"Grandmother," she said. The word felt enormous in her mouth. She had never said it aloud in her life. Her mother had never allowed it — had gone silent as a shut book whenever the old woman surfaced in conversation, had turned her face away, and Freya had grown up thinking the silence was shame.

It had never been shame. It had been grief, and fear, and a daughter's fury at a bargain she'd been born too late to stop.

Inside the jar, the golden thread flickered.

And then, faintly — so faintly Freya first thought it was the murmur of the great hall bleeding through — came a sound. A melody. Three notes, four, rising and falling in a pattern her body knew before her mind caught up, because her mother used to sing it, at night, when she thought Freya was asleep: a lullaby in the old tongue, the one about the river carrying the lantern home, the one with no ending, the one her mother had always stopped singing halfway through as if the rest of it belonged to someone else.

*The river carries the lantern home,* the jar sang, barely audible, in a voice worn thin as paper. *The river carries the lantern home, and the dark cannot hold what the water remembers—*

Freya pressed both palms flat against the clay. It was warm. Of course it was warm. Everything down here was warm, everything was alive, nothing down here was a coin.

"She knows me," Freya said. Her voice broke on the second word and she let it. "Herald. She knows me."

"She has always known you." The herald stood above her, and for the first time the unfinished face looked almost sorrowful. "The deferred do not sleep, scribe. They wait. And the ones who sold themselves for others' sake wait the longest, and listen the closest, and hope the most cruelly." He paused. "She has listened to your ledgers through the stone for twenty years. She is very proud of your penmanship. She is not proud of what you have been made to write."

Tam made a rough sound — half laugh, half sob — and turned away to stand watch at the ring's edge, giving her privacy the only way a smuggler knew how: with his back to her and his eyes on the doors.

Freya knelt in the firelight with her grandmother's soul warm under her hands, and the lullaby wound down to its unfinished ending, the way it always did, the way it had in her mother's mouth, and the silence after it was the loudest thing in the mountain.

"Take it back," she whispered to the jar. "I'm here to take it back. Whatever it costs. I've already paid most of it — you'd be proud of the arithmetic."

The golden thread pulsed once, twice — quick, bright, like a heartbeat startled awake — and then settled, and Freya could have sworn she felt through the clay something that was not a word and not a song but was unmistakably, anciently, *yes.*

When she looked up, the herald was watching her with those finished, kind, terrible eyes.

"There is a term," he said, "that the court may offer you. There is a term that has waited sixty years to be spoken to an Okafor." He extended the long gray hand toward the dais, toward the deep places of the court, toward whatever waited beneath ten thousand dreaming jars. "Will you hear it, granddaughter of Adaeze? Will you hear what your family's debt has become?"

Above them, through the shaft, the dragon-fire guttered — and for one full second, went out.

The whole cathedral gasped. Ten thousand jars flared at once, a constellation crying out, and in the sudden dark Freya saw the herald's face clearly for the first time: not unfinished at all, but *eroded*, worn smooth by ages of offering terms that someone, someday, would finally accept.

The flame caught again. The light returned. The herald had not moved.

"Quickly now," he said gently. "While there is still fire to seal it."
