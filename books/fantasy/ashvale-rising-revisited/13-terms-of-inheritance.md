## 13. Terms of Inheritance

The Herald had carried souls for nine hundred years, and in all that time he had learned one truth above the others: no one comes to the mountain willingly. They come dragged, or trickled down through grief like water finding a crack, or they come because they have been told a lie so beautiful they mistake it for a door.

The scribe came because she was told the truth, and that was rarer still — and more dangerous.

He watched her from the shadow of the archway as she crossed the cathedral of jars, her boots whispering on stone worn smooth by centuries of bare feet and chained ones. The dragon's light fell through the shaft above them in long, failing pulses, each one dimmer than the last, and every pulse lit the jars along the walls like a city of lanterns going out one street at a time. Ten thousand souls. More. He had stopped counting at the millennium; counting was a kind of keeping, and keeping was the court's business, not his. His business was terms.

Beside her limped the smuggler, Tam Vess, one arm bound in scribe's linen gone brown with old blood, his face gray with pain he was refusing loudly to acknowledge. The Herald had read his entry once, centuries ago, when the boy sold his shadow for passage out of a famine winter: *Tam Vess, one shadow, price: survival.* It had been a clean transaction. Those were always the saddest.

But it was not the smuggler the Herald had come down from the threshold to meet.

"Freya Okafor," he said, and his voice moved through the cathedral the way the dragon's light did — touching everything, changing nothing, and yet everything felt it. "You have your grandmother's walk."

Freya stopped. She did not startle; he gave her credit for that. She turned slowly, and the jar-light caught the ink stains on her fingers, the ledger-shaped calluses on her right hand, the exhaustion carved under her eyes. A woman made entirely of record-keeping. He had known kings who were less legible than this clerk.

"You knew my grandmother."

"I held her." He stepped out of the archway, and let her see him — the tall, wrong proportions of him, the face like something remembered rather than seen, the eyes that reflected flame that had died before Ashvale existed. "When she came down. I carried her jar to the deepest vault myself. She asked me to tell whoever came after her that the lullaby was hers, and that she was sorry about the verses she'd forgotten."

Something moved across Freya Okafor's face — quick, involuntary, like a fish under ice. Grief, he thought. Or recognition. The two wore the same expression on the living.

"She forgot the verses?"

"Ninety years is a long time to hold a song with no mouth to sing it." He gestured, and the shadows along the eastern wall drew back like curtains, revealing the stair that spiraled down into the deep vaults. "Come. Both of you. The court will see you now."

"The court sees no one," Tam said hoarsely. "That's the whole point of a court beneath a mountain."

"The court sees debtors." The Herald began to descend, and the stairs accepted him the way water accepts a stone. "And you, Master Vess, are standing inside the largest collection of unpaid debts in the world. Walk carefully. Some of these jars can hear footsteps."

* * *

The deep vault was colder than the cathedral above, and quieter in a way that was not silence. Silence is empty. This was full — full of the sound ten thousand souls make when they are waiting, which is almost exactly the sound of snow falling on snow, if snow could be patient.

The grandmother's jar sat on a shelf of black basalt, alone, apart from the ranked thousands. It was smaller than Freya had imagined — the Herald watched her realize this, watched her hands rise slightly toward it and stop, the way hands stop before an altar or a stove. Inside, the soul glowed a faint, tired gold, guttering in time with the dragon-fire above, and as they approached, the glow flickered — and steadied — and flickered again, like a candle recognizing a draft.

Then, faintly, threading up through the cold air of the vault, came humming.

Three notes. Four. An old melody, half-remembered, sung by something with no throat.

Freya made a sound the Herald had heard only twice before in nine hundred years — small, broken, human — and pressed both ink-stained hands against the glass.

"Mama used to sing that," she whispered. "She said it was nonsense words. She said her mother never sang it right either."

"She didn't," the Herald agreed. "Your grandmother sold the verses too. Part of the bargain. The court keeps what is sold, even songs." He paused. "We can give them back. That is what I have come to offer."

Tam shifted behind her, wounded arm cradled, eyes narrow. "Nothing down here is given back free. I've read your contracts, Herald. Everything has a counterweight."

"Everything has a *price*," the Herald corrected gently. "A counterweight implies balance. Prices imply choice. The court deals in prices." He turned to Freya, and let his voice drop into the register he saved for the oldest bargains, the one that resonated in the marrow. "Freya Okafor. Scribe of the Ashvale Guild. Keeper of ledgers. Descendant of Adaeze Okafor, who bought a city's life with her own."

"That's not what the Guild's records say."

"No. The Guild's records say many things." He smiled, and it was not an unkind smile, but it was not a kind one either. "Shall I tell you what our records say? We keep accounts too, scribe. Older ones. Would you like to hear how your family's name first entered them?"

* * *

He told it standing beside the jar, his voice pitched low, while the grandmother's soul hummed its broken lullaby and the dragon-light above thinned another shade toward darkness.

"In the famine years, Ashvale was dying. You know this part — the Guild's histories confirm it, because it flatters their founders. What their histories do not say is that the famine was not weather. It was called. Called down by a Guild ancestor who wanted the granaries emptied and the people desperate, because desperate people sign anything." The Herald watched the words land on her, one by one, like stones dropped into still water. "Your grandmother went to the mountain's mouth with nothing but her voice and her bloodline. She did not sell her soul out of greed, Freya Okafor. She sold it out of arithmetic. One soul against ten thousand lives. She made the trade any honest ledger would demand."

"And the court took it."

"The court took it. And deferred payment until the fire that sealed the contract should dim — because the court is patient, and because your grandmother demanded one clause no one else in nine hundred years has thought to demand." He leaned closer. "She demanded that the debt pass down her bloodline as an *inheritance*, not a seizure. That when the day came, her descendant would be offered the choice. Refusal permitted. Always permitted. She paid extra for that clause, scribe. Do you understand what it cost her? She paid in verses. In memories. In the shape of her own laughter. So that you would never be taken — only ever *asked*."

Freya's hand was still on the glass. The soul inside had begun to pulse faster, gold light stuttering, and the Herald knew — they all knew — that it was burning low. Days, perhaps. Hours, if the dragon failed first.

"And if I refuse?" Her voice was steady. He admired that. He had heard kings fail to manage it.

"If you refuse, the soul burns out when the fire dies, and goes where burnt-out souls go, which is nowhere, which is worse than death, which is why the clause exists — so that someone would come, and be asked, and perhaps say yes." He spread his hands. "But if you accept — if you take your grandmother's debt into yourself — the court releases her. Whole. Verses restored. Laughter restored. Ninety years of captivity ended tonight, and she rises out of this mountain free, before the fire fails, before the great collection begins."

"And me."

"You remain." The word settled over the vault like frost. "Bound beneath the mountain. Keeper of the deep shelves. Your soul in a jar beside the place hers stood, warm, safe, unburnt — for as long as the court endures. Which, the gods being what they are these days, may be a very long time indeed."

* * *

They left her alone with it. That was the terms of the oldest bargains: the choice must be made alone, or it is not a choice, it is only obedience wearing choice's clothes.

The Herald withdrew to the archway with the smuggler, who glared at him with the particular fury of a man whose entire moral philosophy consists of hating exactly this kind of arrangement.

"You're enjoying this," Tam muttered.

"I am *witnessing* it. There is a difference."

"There really isn't, down here."

They watched her through the arch. She had not moved from the jar. Her forehead was nearly touching the glass now, and the soul within had pressed itself — there was no other word for it — against the inner curve, gold light spreading like a hand matching a hand through frosted panes. The humming had changed. It was no longer three notes and four. It was trying for the whole verse, stumbling, reaching, and the Herald felt something in his ancient chest that he had no ledger-entry for.

"She'll say yes," Tam said quietly. "Look at her. She's already saying it."

"Perhaps."

"Don't 'perhaps' me. You designed this. You've had nine hundred years to design it. The clause, the choice, the asking — you built a trap where the door out is shaped exactly like the person she loves most." His voice cracked on the last word. "That's not mercy. That's engineering."

The Herald considered the smuggler for a long moment. "You sold your shadow to survive a winter," he said. "Do you regret it?"

"Every day."

"And yet you'd do it again."

Tam said nothing. That was answer enough, and the Herald nodded, almost gently.

"That is inheritance, Master Vess. Not the debt. The *doing it again*. The court did not invent that. We merely built shelves for it." He turned back to the vault. "Now watch. And learn what your friend is truly made of."

Below, Freya Okafor lifted her head from the glass. She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand — a clerk's gesture, brisk, efficient, grieving in shorthand — and when she spoke, her voice carried up the stairwell clear and cold as well-water.

"What happens to my ledgers if I stay?"

The Herald blinked. Of all the questions — the soul, the freedom, the forever — she asked about the ledgers.

"They remain yours. The court does not confiscate. We only keep."

"Then here are my terms." She descended the stairs toward them, and the dragon-light caught her face, and the Herald saw that she was crying and smiling at once, and that neither expression was the one she would have chosen. "I'll assume the debt. My grandmother walks out of this mountain tonight, whole, with every verse. But I write the contract of my binding myself — in my own hand, in my own book, not yours. And I want to read the original famine bargain first. All of it. Every clause my grandmother paid for. Before I sign anything."

It was, the Herald admitted to himself, the most dangerous thing a debtor had ever said in this vault, and he had heard nine hundred years of debtors.

Because she wanted to read the original.

And the original — the true original, with Corvane's ancestor's seal at its foot and the fraud folded into its third clause like a knife sewn into a coat lining — was the one document in the court's keeping that the Guild's masters would burn the mountain itself to keep unread.

"Agreed," said the Herald, because the clause compelled him, and because some ancient, buried part of him — older than the court, older than the shelves — very much wanted to see what the scribe would find.

Above them, far up the mountain shaft, the dragon-fire guttered hard, threw the whole cathedral into darkness for one terrible heartbeat —

— and in that darkness, ten thousand jars flickered at once, and somewhere in the ranked thousands, a soul that was not yet due began, very softly, to scream.
