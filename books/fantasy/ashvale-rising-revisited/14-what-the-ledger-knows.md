## 14. What the Ledger Knows

The jars gave light the way dying coals give heat — grudgingly, in colors that hurt to name. Freya sat cross-legged on the cold floor of the vault's outer gallery, her copied pages spread around her in a careful fan, and read by the glow of a thousand stolen souls.

She had not slept. Sleep, down here, felt like a luxury purchased with someone else's coin.

Tam was dozing against the wall near the corridor mouth, his wounded side bound with strips of her scribe's coat, his breath coming shallow but steady. The enforcers' ambush on the pilgrim road had cost him blood Freya could not ledger away, and every hour she heard him breathe was an hour she did not have to write *deceased* beside a name she had come, against all her training, to care about.

So she read.

Her copy was imperfect — memory is a scribe's cruelest master, and she had worked from recall under Corvane's watching eye — but the entries were there, hundreds of them, thousands: the widow's last words, the merchant's daughter's laughter, Tam's shadow, the small cruelties and great ones that had built the Ashvale Guild brick by brick out of other people's lives.

And threaded through them, in handwriting that was hers and yet not hers, the unwritten entries.

She had found seven before the descent. Now, reading slowly, by soul-light, she found more. They appeared between the honest transactions like veins of ore in worthless stone — entries dated across three centuries, each one in her own looping hand, each one recording something the Guild's official books did not contain.

*One harvest, withheld. Ashvale fields blighted by Guild order, year of the Long Hunger.*

Freya stopped.

She read it again. Read it a third time, her lips moving silently, the way she'd been taught to verify a difficult passage — *read it thrice, girl, once for the eyes, once for the tongue, once for the truth.*

*One harvest, withheld. Ashvale fields blighted by Guild order.*

There was no famine. Not at first. There was a decision.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Scribes learned steadiness young — ink forgives nothing, and a trembling hand writes lies as neatly as truths. But beneath the steadiness something was opening, some sealed chamber in her chest cracking along lines she hadn't known were drawn there.

She kept reading.

*Grain stores diverted to Vess-adjacent trade routes. Profit recorded: forty-fold. Famine declared by proclamation, not by fact.*

Vess. Her eyes flicked to Tam, sleeping, oblivious, carrying a name that meant nothing and everything. No — different Vesses, surely. The city was full of Vesses. It meant nothing.

*Proclamation drafted by Aldous Corvane, Master Scribe. Purpose: manufacture scarcity sufficient to compel extraordinary bargain.*

*Extraordinary bargain secured: one soul, Okafor line, freely given in exchange for Ashvale's salvation. Payment deferred until dragon-fire dims.*

*Note: soul given freely. Fraud committed by buyer, not seller.*

*Note: fraud committed by buyer, not seller.*

The words repeated themselves in her skull like a bell struck too near her ear.

Her grandmother had not sold her soul out of greed. Had not bartered cheaply, or vainly, or with the cold ambition the Guild's histories implied — the histories Freya herself had catalogued, shelved, recited to apprentices. Her grandmother had stood in a blighted field that powerful men had *made* blighted, in a hunger they had *manufactured for profit*, and she had traded the only thing she owned that was worth anything to feed people who were being starved on purpose.

And then the Guild had spent three centuries letting everyone believe otherwise. Letting the Okafors carry the shame like an heirloom. Letting Freya's mother go silent and gray whenever the old woman's name surfaced. Letting Freya grow up believing her blood carried a stain instead of a wound.

The unwritten entries went on. They were meticulous. They were *hers* — her abbreviations, her marginal conventions, her habit of dating in the old temple style she'd never fully abandoned. Whoever or whatever had written them had reached into her hand and used it like a borrowed pen.

*Corvane line holds original fraud instrument. Fraud renewed annually at solstice sealing. Dragon-fire authenticates renewal without examining content. Fire does not read. Fire only burns.*

*Fire does not read. Fire only burns.*

That was why the dragon mattered. That was why Corvane needed the flame alive just long enough to complete his consolidation, and dead immediately after. The fire had authenticated three hundred years of lies simply because authentication was its nature and questioning was not.

Freya looked up at the nearest jar. Inside, something pale turned slowly, like milk stirred in dark water. A soul. Someone's grandmother, perhaps. Someone's mother. Someone who had signed something desperate in a desperate year because the alternative was worse, and who had been told ever after that desperation was the same as guilt.

"We were never the ones who owed," she said quietly. Her voice sounded strange in the vault, small and enormous at once. "We were the collateral."

Tam stirred. "You're talking to the jars now." His voice was rough with pain and sleep. "Corvane would say that's the first sign of ledger-madness."

"Corvane," Freya said, and the name came out of her like a splinter leaving flesh, "is the fourth generation of a fraud."

She gathered the pages — the unwritten ones, the corrections — and read them aloud to him while he woke properly, while he ate the last of the road-bread, while dawn-that-was-not-dawn filtered down through the mountain's throat in shades of ember. She read him the blighted harvest. The diverted grain. The manufactured famine. The freely-given soul and the fraudulent buyer.

Tam listened the way smugglers listen — still, watchful, weighing. When she finished he was silent a long moment.

"My family," he said finally. "During the Long Hunger. My great-great-grandmother's stories said the grain barges passed *through* Ashvale without stopping. Full barges. Everyone thought she was mad with hunger, saying such things about the Guild." He laughed, and there was no humor in it, only an old ache finding its shape at last. "She wasn't mad. She was *right*. Your ledger knew. Some part of your ledger always knew."

"The ledger doesn't lie," Freya said. "It's the only thing in Ashvale that never has. The Guild just decided which pages anyone got to see." She pressed her palm flat against the copied pages, against the truth in her own handwriting. "I spent my whole life thinking I was keeping accounts. I was keeping *prisoners*. Every entry I ever wrote was a wall around something true."

"And now?"

"Now I know what I inherited." She looked at the deepest gallery, where the guttering light of the dragon-fire pulsed faint and failing above her grandmother's jar. "Not debt. Not shame. *Memory.* That's the inheritance. That's what they couldn't put in a jar."

The rage came then — not the hot flash she might have expected, but something slower and vaster, rising through her like water filling a dry cistern. Three centuries of it. Every widow denied. Every child's laughter sold. Every soul dimming in glass while the Corvanes grew fat on interest. And underneath it all, the specific, personal fury of a woman who had given her life to the very institution that had ruined hers before she was born — who had balanced its books, defended its law, denied Tam Vess his shadow with her own mouth, all in service of a lie older than her grandmother's grave.

She wanted to burn something. She understood, suddenly and completely, what she would eventually burn, and why, and she let the knowledge sit in her like a coal settling into a grate.

But not yet. Not yet. First —

Footsteps on the stair. Measured, unhurried, familiar as her own heartbeat.

Tam was on his feet despite the wound, knife out, but Freya already knew. She had known that tread since her first day in the scriptorium. It was the sound of authority that never needed to raise its voice.

Master Scribe Corvane descended into the gallery of jars with a traveling case under one arm and the master ledger wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, and he smiled at her the way a man smiles at a sum that has finally come out even.

"There you are," he said. "Right where the books say you should be."

He set the case down. Began, calmly, to unpack instruments she recognized from the sealing rites — the silver stylus, the binding wax, the small sharp knife for signatures written in skin.

"I've come to settle our account, Freya. Yours, mine, and your grandmother's. All at once, if you'll hold still." He glanced at the pages scattered around her, and something flickered behind his eyes — not surprise. Recognition. "Ah. You've been reading the corrections. How like a scribe. Always checking another clerk's work."

"You knew," she said. "You've always known."

"Of course I knew." He said it the way he'd say *of course the sun rises*, and began to unroll the master ledger across the cold stone floor between them. "I'm the fourth generation of a fraud, Freya. It's the family business. And business—" he looked up at her, and his smile was almost kind, which was the worst thing yet— "has been very good."

Above them, far up the mountain's throat, the last dragon-fire guttered, flared, and began — unmistakably, irrevocably — to die.
