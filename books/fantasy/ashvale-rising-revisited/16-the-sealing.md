## 16. The Sealing

The herald's voice had barely finished naming the terms when the air behind us changed.

I felt it before I heard it — a pressure in the ears, like the moment before a mountain decides to be an avalanche. The cathedral of jars dimmed as something vast passed between the dragon-fire above and the light it shed. Then Corvane stepped out of the dark of the pilgrim road, and the court beneath the mountain went silent in a way I had never known silence could be.

He was not breathing hard. He had descended alone, an old man in scribe's grey, carrying nothing but the master ledger under his arm — the true one, the one I had copied and hidden against my heart. He looked at me the way he had always looked at me: not with hatred, not even with contempt. With arithmetic.

"You brought her," he said to the herald. "Good. That saves the Guild a retrieval fee."

"Master Scribe." The Herald's voice was ash and patience. "The court did not summon you."

"The court didn't have to." Corvane walked forward across the vault floor, his boots clicking on stone that had been worn smooth by centuries of kneeling debtors. "You offered her inheritance. I'm here to offer her something better. Finality."

Tam moved beside me. He had been leaning against a jar-shelf since we entered, gray-faced, one hand pressed over the wound in his side where the enforcer's blade had gone in on the road. I had bound it with my sash and half my shirt, and he had insisted on walking the last mile down into the dark because, he said, if he was going to die somewhere he'd rather it be somewhere interesting. Now he pushed off the shelf and stood between me and Corvane, swaying, stubborn as a fence post.

"You're supposed to be renegotiating," Tam said. "That's what she was told. Terms. A delegation."

"I am renegotiating." Corvane set the master ledger down on the central altar-stone with the care of a man laying a sleeping child in a cradle. "The terms are these: everything stays exactly as it is, except that the Okafor debt transfers from deferred to collected. Tonight. By rite."

My mouth went dry. "The dragon-fire is guttering. You can't seal a contract with a flame that's dying."

"No," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "Which is why the flame is dying."

The words dropped through the vault like a stone through deep water. I heard them land, and I heard everything they carried with them.

"You *dimmed* it," I said. My voice came out very small and very steady, which surprised me. "Three hundred years of deferred payments. Famine bargains. Widow-contracts. Children sold by the syllable. And you starved the fire so the whole account would come due at once — under *your* seal, at the moment of collection, when every soul-debt in Ashvale would have to pass through Guild hands to be settled—"

"When every soul-debt in Ashvale would have to pass through *one* pair of hands," Corvane corrected gently. "Mine. Yes. You were always quick, Freya. It's why I kept you close. Well. One of the reasons."

The herald had gone very still. Even the court — those pale, patient figures who drifted between the shelves like moths made of ledger-paper — had stopped drifting. They understood before I did. This was not their fraud. This was done *to* them, or around them, the way you might quietly reroute a river while the valley sleeps.

"The court's contracts predate the Guild," the herald said slowly. "You cannot collect debts you do not hold."

"I hold all of them." Corvane opened the master ledger. "Every contract ever sealed in dragon-fire has a mirror entry. Every mirror entry lives in this book. Your vault holds the souls; my book holds the *terms*. Burn the book and the souls go free — yes, I know what she's been telling you, she copies beautifully — but no one burns this book. Not the gods. Not the dragon. Not her." He glanced at me, almost fondly. "Especially not her. Because the final page requires a signature, and the only hand that can sign it is an Okafor's."

"That's why you sent me down here," I said. "Not to substitute my soul for the Guild's. To get me to *consent*. In writing. In front of witnesses. A voluntary assumption of the family debt, signed by the last Okafor — it makes the transfer legitimate. It makes the whole collection lawful."

"It makes it *clean*," Corvane said. "There's a difference. Lawful things survive scrutiny. Clean things survive gods."

Above us, the dragon-fire guttered hard — a long, shuddering flicker, like a candle in a dying man's fist — and for three full seconds the cathedral went nearly black. In that darkness I heard the jars. All of them. Thousands of souls, each one humming its own thin note, and underneath the hum, faint but unmistakable, a lullaby. My mother's lullaby. My grandmother's voice, worn down to almost nothing after sixty years in glass, still singing to whoever came near her shelf.

When the light steadied, Corvane had produced a pen.

It was a guild-pen, black lacquer, dragon-bone nib — the kind we used for entries that could never be amended. He uncapped the master ledger to the final page, and I saw what waited there. Not blank paper. Pre-written text, in ink that shimmered faintly, waiting only for a name:

*Debt assumed: one (1) soul, Okafor line, transferred voluntarily. Witnessed by the court beneath the mountain. Sealed in dragon-fire. Term: forever.*

Below it, a single line for signing.

"Freya." Corvane held out the pen. His voice softened into the register he used in the scriptorium, the kind teacher-voice that had made me love him once, years ago, when I was a temple orphan who thought precision was the same thing as goodness. "I know what you think of me now. It doesn't matter. Think of the widow who bought back her husband's last words. Think of the merchant's daughter who laughs again — you reconciled that account yourself, you told me it was your finest work. If the fire dies without order, all of it burns. Every reclaimed word, every returned laugh, every borrowed year of luck — chaos, and then nothing. Or—" he tilted the pen toward me, "—order. Forever. Signed."

And the terrible thing, the thing I will never tell Tam, is that for one long breath I considered it. Because he was right about the widow. He was right about the laughter. The ledger was monstrous, but it was also the only place in Ashvale where impossible things were *kept*, and I had spent my whole life believing that keeping was a form of mercy.

Then I heard the lullaby again, thinner now, and I remembered chapter and verse of what my grandmother had actually traded, and I took the pen.

Corvane smiled.

I did not sign. I wrote.

Fast, in my best scribe's hand, the amendment-line every clerk learns first and prays never to use: *Entry contested. Basis of contest: fraudulent provenance. See attached testimony.* And beneath it, the date — today's date — and beneath the date, the words I had copied from the unwritten entries that had been appearing in my own handwriting for weeks, the corrections the ledger itself had been trying to make since the gods woke: *The famine bargain was forged. The Okafor soul was taken, not sold. Provenance: Corvane line.*

The vault went cold.

"What," Corvane said quietly, "have you done."

"Reconciliation," I said. "You taught me yourself. An entry that doesn't match its source must be corrected or struck. Those are the rules."

"The rules are *mine*—"

"They're the *ledger's*." And I saw it happen — saw the truth of it move through the master book like fire through dry grass. The pages began to turn themselves. Slowly at first, then faster, thousands of entries riffling past, and everywhere my amendment touched, ink rose up off the page in beads, refusing, rewriting, correcting. The unwritten entries hadn't been the ledger going mad. They'd been the ledger going *honest*, the way a body goes honest at the end of a long illness, and now the whole diseased account was trying to purge itself at once.

Corvane slammed the book shut. The sound echoed like a tomb door.

Above us, the dragon opened one eye.

I had thought the fire was the dragon. It wasn't. The fire was a *seal* — a great slow burning sigil laid over the mountain's throat, and beneath it, coiled in the dark of the world's oldest vault, the last dragon had been sleeping inside its own diminishing breath for three hundred years. The eye that opened was the color of cooling bronze, and it was fixed on the altar-stone, on the closed ledger, on the smell of contested ink.

"It's waking," the herald breathed. "After three centuries — the seal is breaking from the *inside*—"

"Then we finish the rite *now*." Corvane's composure cracked clean down the middle, and underneath it was just an old man in a hurry. He grabbed my wrist — the pen-hand — and dragged it toward the signature line, shouting words in the old tongue, the contracting-words, the ones that make a promise into a chain. The dragon-fire flared overhead, guttering and surging, guttering and surging, and I felt the rite take hold of me like a current takes hold of a drowning woman. My name began to rise in my throat. Not the name I chose. The name in the books. The one that belonged to the debt.

And that was when Tam Vess threw himself across the vault floor toward my grandmother's shelf.

I screamed his name. He was gray as ash, leaving a dark smear on the stone behind him, and he shouldn't have been able to walk, let alone run, but Tam had spent his whole life stealing back things the powerful said belonged to them, and he knew — the way thieves know — that there is one moment in any vault when everything is unguarded, and it is the moment when everyone is looking at someone else.

He reached the shelf. He lifted the jar. My grandmother's soul turned inside the glass like a candle-flame in wind, and the lullaby rose, sudden and clear, sixty years of diminishment burning away in one bright note—

—and Tam hurled the jar down onto the altar-stone, beside the master ledger, and it shattered.

The sound was small. Glass on stone. That was all.

What came out of it was not small.

A soul released mid-rite cannot be re-contained — the herald had told us that much, back when the court still thought it was making us an offer. A soul released mid-rite becomes *witness*. It stands outside every contract, belonging to no account, and its testimony is binding on all parties present. That is the old law, older than the Guild, older than the mountain, and my grandmother — patient, defrauded, sixty years in glass — stood up out of her shards in a shape of soft gold light and looked at Corvane with the face from the one portrait my mother kept hidden, and she sang the lullaby *at* him.

The rite broke.

I felt it break — the current holding me snapped like a rotted rope, and Corvane staggered backward, the contracting-words dying in his throat, because you cannot finish a sealing when a free soul is testifying against its provenance. The master ledger flew open under its own agitation, pages tearing loose, entries lifting off the paper as sparks, and the whole cathedral of jars began to hum in answer — thousands of souls, feeling the law change in the air, pressing against their glass like minnows against ice.

"NO—" Corvane lunged for the book.

The dragon exhaled.

Not fire. Not yet. Just breath — a vast, slow displacement of air that smelled of iron and rain and the far side of the world, and it swept through the vault and took the loose pages with it, swirling them up the mountain shaft like a storm of white birds, and the master ledger — the true one, the irreplaceable one, the book that held the terms of every soul in Ashvale — tore free of the altar-stone, tumbling end over end, pages streaming, spiraling up toward the guttering seal and the open eye beyond it.

Corvane dove. I dove.

We hit the altar-stone together, two hands closing on the same falling book, and for one suspended moment the Master Scribe of the Ashvale Guild and the temple orphan he owned looked at each other across four hundred years of other people's signatures.

"Let go," he said. "Freya. Let go, and I'll forgive everything. Your debt, your mother's, all of it. I'll write you *free*."

"You don't understand," I said, and I was crying, and my grip didn't loosen. "There's nothing left to forgive. The entry's already corrected."

The dragon-fire surged.

And I pulled the ledger from his hands, and the mountain shook, and above us the seal that had held the world's oldest prisoner began — at last, after three hundred years of guttering — to burn.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way avalanches happen fast, each piece causing the next.

The freed soul — my grandmother, gold and singing — rose straight up the shaft, and every jar she passed cracked in her wake, hairline fractures spreading through six centuries of glass. The herald fell to its knees on the vault floor, and I will swear until I die that the sound it made was relief. Tam collapsed against the shelf, laughing and bleeding and saying something I couldn't hear over the roar. Corvane scrambled backward from the altar, screaming amendments, counter-entries, appeals to rules that were dissolving even as he named them.

And I stood at the base of the mountain shaft with the master ledger in both arms, heavier than any book should be, heavy with every soul it indexed, and above me the dragon leaned its head down out of the dark — vast, ancient, awake, its bronze eye reflecting ten thousand cracking jars and one woman holding the account of all their chains.

It looked at the ledger. It looked at me.

Somewhere in my chest, under the terror, an old certainty settled into place, calm as a balanced column of figures. There was only one payment left that could void everything — the book itself, fed to the fire that had sealed it. Everything the Guild had built would burn with it. Every record. Every rank. Every proof of who any of us had been.

Including mine.

The dragon opened its jaws, and the light coming up from its throat was the color of beginnings.

I lifted the ledger over my head, and jumped the last step forward, and gave Ashvale back its fire.
