## 17. Ashvale Rising

The ledger was heavier than it had any right to be.

Freya had carried it down the pilgrim road, through the ambush, through the dark of the mountain's throat, and at every step it had gained weight — not the weight of paper and ink, which was nothing, but the weight of everything written in it. Ten thousand bargains. Ten thousand souls. The widow's last words. The merchant's daughter's laughter. Tam's shadow, catalogued on page four hundred and twelve in her own careful hand. Her grandmother's name, entered ninety years ago by a scribe she had never met, in ink that still smelled faintly of ash.

She held it now at the dragon's threshold, where the heat came in waves that made the air itself shimmer like water, and Corvane's blood was still wet on the floor behind her.

He was not dead. She could hear him breathing — a wet, furious sound — where he had fallen against the dais when the rite collapsed. Tam lay nearer to her, one hand pressed to the wound in his side, his shadow pooled beneath him like spilled tar, whole again and clinging to him as if it never meant to leave. And above them all, filling the vaulted dark of the court beneath the mountain, the last dragon opened one eye the size of a cathedral door.

Its flame was dying. Freya could see it — the great guttering glow along its throat, the fire that had sealed every contract the Guild had ever written, flickering like a lamp out of oil. When it went out, everything unpaid would be collected at once, violently, and Ashvale would learn what ten thousand debts called in on a single night looked like.

Unless the debts ceased to exist.

"The ledger," Corvane rasped. He had dragged himself up onto one elbow, his fine coat torn, his composure cracked open like dropped crockery. "Scribe. You will give me the ledger. That is an order under seal. Your debt binds you. Your *name* binds you."

And there it was — the thing she had felt coming all her life without ever having words for it. Not Corvane's voice. The ledger's voice, speaking through him, because he was nothing but its most devoted servant. It did not want her soul, not really. It wanted the account balanced.

"I know," Freya said.

She turned to face the fire.

The herald stood between her and the dragon's threshold, tall and grey and patient, its features like something worn smooth by centuries of water. It had watched the rite collapse. It had watched Tam shatter the jar and let her grandmother's soul loose into a room full of hungry contracts. It had done nothing, because the court beneath the mountain did not act; it only enforced. Enforcement required terms. And terms required a document.

"You cannot void what is written," the herald said. Its voice was the sound of pages turning in an empty room. "The contracts are sealed in flame. The flame endures."

"The flame is dying," Freya said. "You told me so yourself. On the solstice, or the week after, or however long a dragon takes to gutter out. And when it dies, you collect everything at once." She lifted the ledger. It seemed to pull toward the fire, toward authentication, the way water pulls toward the sea. "Or —"

"Freya." Tam's voice was thin. Blood had soaked through his fingers. "Whatever you're thinking. Think faster."

"— or the flame burns the only thing that ever gave those contracts meaning." She looked up at the vast dim intelligence overhead, the slow-blinking eye, the throat-light pulsing weaker with each breath. "A contract is ink, herald. Ink and belief and fire. Take away any one and it's just words. I can't take away your belief. I can't take away the fire. But the ink —"

"The master ledger records the Guild's authority," the herald said. "Destroying it does not erase the debts. The debts exist in the souls themselves."

"No," Freya said, and here was the thing she had understood only in the last hour, holding her copied pages by jar-light while the truth uncoiled from the suppressed entries like smoke from a snuffed candle: "the debts exist in the *record*. That's what no one in three hundred years has dared to say out loud. A soul-debt isn't a thing you can hold. It's a sentence in a book that says it is. My grandmother didn't sell her soul — she signed a page that said she had. The page came first. The page always comes first. Every prayer answered, every god gone silent, every famine and every bargain struck in the dark — all of it happened because someone wrote it down and the fire said *yes*."

She walked toward the threshold. The heat pressed against her face like a living thing, curious.

Corvane was screaming now. She heard him moving, the scrape of his boots, and then Tam's voice, sharp: "No you don't," and the sound of bodies colliding, and Freya did not turn around. If she turned around she would stop. She knew that about herself the way she knew the weight of a well-made pen — completely, and without appeal.

Ten steps from the fire. The dragon's breath stirred her hair. This close, she could see the flame was not one fire but many — threads of light braided together, each thread a sealing, each seal a signature, three centuries of yes spoken in burning. The braid was unraveling. Threads went dark even as she watched, and each one that died took a contract with it into some half-state between kept and broken, and she understood suddenly why the herald had not moved to stop her.

*It wants this too,* she realized. *It is so tired.*

Five steps.

Her own name was in the ledger. That was the price no one had asked her to pay, and so it was the only honest one. Page nine hundred and six: *Okafor, Freya — indentured, temple scriptorium, debt assumed from mother, debt assumed from grandmother.* Everything she had done for eleven years was written there in her own improving hand. The first entry she'd ever copied. The thousands after. The unwritten entries that had appeared like frost, the corrections, the truth leaking through. All of it. Her apprenticeship, her competence, her complicity, her guilt. If the ledger burned, none of it had ever been *recorded*, and in Ashvale, in the economy of impossible things, what was not recorded had never happened.

She would burn her own history to free everyone else's future. There was no clause for that anywhere. No precedent. No god had thought to forbid it because no god had imagined a scribe who would choose oblivion over balance.

"Freya!" Tam's voice, raw. "Look at me!"

She looked. He had Corvane pinned beneath him, one knee on the master scribe's chest, and he was pale as milk and grinning through the pain like a man watching the best joke ever told. "You'll forget!" he called. "Everything! You won't remember doing this!"

"I know," she said.

"Then how will you know you were brave?"

The question went through her like a blade finding the seam in armor. And then, because she was her mother's daughter and her grandmother's granddaughter and a scribe to her bones, she gave the only answer a keeper of records could give:

"You'll remember for me."

She threw the ledger.

It did not fall. Ledgers do not fall when they are dying; they fly. The book opened in the air like a bird, pages fanning, and for one suspended moment Freya saw all ten thousand entries at once, ink lifting off the paper in black ribbons, names rising like startled starlings — and then it crossed the threshold and the last dragon inhaled.

The flame that had been guttering roared.

It came up out of the great throat like dawn, like the first fire of the world, gold and white and terrible, and it caught the ledger mid-flight and burned it in a single instant that somehow lasted long enough for Freya to feel every page go. She felt the widow get her husband's last words back. She felt the merchant's daughter laugh for the first time in ten years, somewhere far above in Ashvale, startling her father into tears over breakfast. She felt Tam's shadow settle fully into his body. She felt her mother's debt dissolve, and her own, and then — arriving last, like a guest who knows they are the reason for the party — she felt her grandmother.

Not the jar. The jar was shards on the floor. The soul itself, freed an hour ago by Tam's bloody hands and hovering unclaimed in the court's cold air, suddenly had nowhere to be bound to and everywhere to go. It rose.

They all rose.

Up through the mountain shaft the souls went, thousands of points of light streaming past Freya like embers loosed from a bonfire by a strong wind, upward toward the mouth of the mountain, toward the sky, toward whatever waits above the world for things that are finally unowned. The cathedral of jars lit up one by one and then went dark one by one, emptied from the inside, and the sound they made as the souls left was almost too much to bear — a note like a choir clearing its throat for a song it had forgotten it remembered.

And the gods' voices filled the dark.

They came from everywhere. From the stone. From the fire. From the mouths of the empty jars and the silence between heartbeats. Three centuries of unanswered prayers, all answered at once, not in words but in something older — a vast, warm attention settling over the court beneath the mountain like a hand over a frightened animal, and in that attention Freya heard her own name spoken, and her grandmother's, and Tam's, and even Corvane's, because gods returned to a world without ledgers have no one to punish and so must simply witness everyone.

The dragon closed its eye. The great flame settled low along its body, no longer a braid of bindings but simply fire — a hearth-fire the size of a mountain, burning because fire burns, warming the dark instead of sealing it.

Freya Okafor sat down on the scorched stone at the dragon's threshold and found that she could not remember her mother's face.

She could remember that she had loved her. The love was there, intact, a warm coal with no story attached. But the face — gone, like a page burned before she finished reading it. Her apprenticeship: gone. Corvane's orders: gone, though the man himself was still groaning on the floor, unmade of all authority, just an old liar in a ruined coat. Eleven years of her life had been written in that book, and the book was smoke, and the smoke was rising up the shaft with everything else.

Tam lowered himself down beside her, gasping, one arm clamped to his side. His shadow lay across her knees like a sleeping cat.

"So," he said. "Who are you, then?"

She thought about it. It was, she realized, the first genuinely open question anyone had ever asked her. Every previous answer had been an entry in a ledger. *Scribe. Debtor. Okafor.* All of it burned.

"I don't know yet," she said, and it felt like standing in a field after a flood — everything swept away, and under the mud, green things already starting.

Above them, faint as memory, the first of the souls reached the surface of the world. They felt it as a tremor of light through the mountain shaft, and then another, and the herald — the herald was on its knees, hands raised, face upturned, weeping something that was not grief.

"What happens to Ashvale now?" Freya asked the dark, or asked the gods, or asked the fire.

The answer came from everywhere and nowhere, in the voice of a lullaby she suddenly could not place and would spend the rest of her life trying to:

*Now it rises.*

* * *

They climbed for hours. Tam leaned on her; she leaned on the wall; the dragon's steady warmth followed them up the shaft like a lantern that asked nothing in return. Twice Tam tried to tell her about herself — "You denied my petition once, you know, you were merciless, it was magnificent" — and twice the stories slid off her like water off glass, and he saw it, and stopped, and simply took her hand instead, which was its own kind of record.

When they came out of the mountain's mouth, dawn was breaking over Ashvale, and the city below was screaming — but the scream had joy in it, somewhere underneath, the way a bell has music under the striking. Souls were visible in the morning air, drifting down into the streets, finding their old houses, their old bodies' descendants, their graves. People were waking up owing nothing and remembering everything except what it had cost them.

Freya stood at the edge of the threshold and felt the wind move through her — through her, not around her, nothing snagging, nothing binding, no hook anywhere in the world left to catch her on.

A woman no contract can hold. It should have felt like triumph. Mostly it felt like the first day of a very long walk.

Tam squeezed her hand. "Come on," he said. "Whoever you are — the city's going to want to meet her."

Below them, in the market square, an old god was bending down to lift a child, and every bell in Ashvale began, unprompted, to ring.
