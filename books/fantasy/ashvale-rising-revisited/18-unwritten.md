## 18. Unwritten

The last part of the climb, Tam did with his teeth.

His hands were ruined — split across the palm from the enforcers' blades on the road, torn again on the mountain's throat when he'd smashed the jar, and now scraped raw by the rock of the ascent shaft. His shadow clung to him the way a wet thing clings, heavier than it had ever been in his life, as if it had learned something about weight while sitting in a vault for eleven years. Above him Freya climbed one-handed, her other hand pressed flat against the scribe's coat where she'd carried nothing at all since the burning. He could see the shape of that emptiness under the fabric. She kept touching it anyway, the way you touch a tooth that isn't there anymore.

The dawn came down the shaft toward them before they reached it — a thin grey blade of light widening with every step, and then the sky opened over the mouth of the mountain like a hand unclenching, and Tam stood on holy ordinary ground and breathed air that smelled of pine and frost and nothing else.

No sulfur. No ink. For the first time in three days, no smell of burning paper.

He turned to look back once. The shaft behind them was dark and still, but not empty — he had seen what rose through it. He would see it every time he closed his eyes until he died: souls going up like embers in a chimney draft, hundreds of them, pale sparks spiraling on the heat of the dragon's breath, each one flickering past close enough to feel warmth against his cheek. One of them had paused. He was almost sure one of them had paused, at the height of his shoulder, for about the length of a heartbeat, and something in it had been singing a small descending melody, three notes, four notes, the kind of thing you hum to a child who won't sleep.

Freya hadn't looked back. She was looking east instead, where the road ran down out of the highlands toward Ashvale, and so Tam looked too.

The city lay in the valley like a coin at the bottom of a clear stream. And its towers were dark.

* * *

They came down off the mountain through the terraced fields, and Tam understood quickly that something had gone strange with the world, because the first farmstead they passed had its gate open and no one watching it, and the second had an old man standing in his own turnip rows weeping without any sound at all, just standing there among the green leaves with tears running into his beard, and when Tam asked him what was wrong the man said:

"I don't know. I know I lost something. I can't tell you what it was."

"Did the Guild hold your debt?" Freya asked gently.

"They held something." The old man wiped his face with the back of his wrist, leaving a streak of dirt. "I signed papers. I remember signing papers. Eleven of them, one every year, my mark at the bottom because I never learned my letters." He looked up at Freya, and Tam saw the question in him before he spoke it, the question that was going to be everywhere in Ashvale now, growing in every mouth like a weed. "What did I sell, miss? Do you know?"

"No," Freya said.

And Tam watched her say it and understood it wasn't quite a lie and wasn't quite the truth either, because she knew *kinds* of things people sold — laughter, last words, luck, names, years — but she didn't know this man's particular sale, couldn't know it, because the record of it had gone into the dragon's fire along with everything else, along with her grandmother's contract and Corvane's frauds and Tam's own signature, the one he'd made at nineteen with shaking hands and a smuggler's swagger, trading his shadow for a cargo that would keep his sister fed through the winter. All of it gone. Every account in Ashvale balanced by annihilation.

The old man thanked her anyway. That was the part Tam couldn't shake. He said *thank you, miss*, and touched his forehead, and went back to standing among his turnips, grieving something whose name he would never recover.

They walked on. Behind them the man began, very quietly, to sing — not well, just to fill the space where the knowing used to be.

* * *

Ashvale was holding a wake for itself.

That was how it struck Tam as they came in through the Salt Gate, past guards who weren't guarding, past the long market street where he'd spent his whole crooked life. Nobody was rioting. He had expected riots — expected fires, looting, the Guild's towers coming down stone by stone under the hands of the people they'd bled. Instead the city moved slow and quiet, like a house the morning after a funeral. Shopkeepers stood in their doorways. Women carried water and set it down and forgot why they'd come outside and stood looking at the middle distance. Children ran through the streets untouched by anyone, which was normal for children, except that adults watched them pass with expressions Tam had no word for, some mixture of envy and terror, as if the children were walking on the edge of a cliff.

In front of the Guild's counting-house, someone had laid flowers. Just a few stems of yarrow and late roses on the wide steps, arranged carefully, the way you'd arrange them on a grave. Tam stopped and stared.

"They're mourning it," he said. "Look at this. We burned the thing that ate thirty years of my life and they're leaving *flowers*."

"People mourn what they lose," Freya said. "Even when it was killing them."

"That's the saddest sentence I've ever heard."

"That's because you've never worked in a ledger office."

But she said it without any humor in it, and her eyes were fixed on the Guild's tallest tower — the Scriptorium, where she had lived and worked for nine years, where her narrow bed still stood in the clerks' dormitory and her ink-stained place still waited at the long table, except that none of it belonged to her now, because belonging was a thing that required records, and records were ash.

They walked to the Guild quarter together, though Tam couldn't have told you why. Habit, maybe. Or the need to see it confirmed.

The gates stood open. Inside, scribes wandered the courtyards in their grey coats like men who had woken from a fever. Some sat on benches doing nothing with their hands, which for scribes was the worst torture Tam could imagine. A boy of perhaps twelve recognized Freya from across the yard — came running up with his face full of hope and relief, shouting her name, and then pulled up short three steps away, and Tam watched the hope die in real time.

"You're Freya Okafor," the boy said. "You're — you taught me shorthand. You corrected my margins for two years. You're in the rolls, senior scribe third class, you have a seal and everything, I've *seen* the seal—"

"Show me," Freya said quietly. "Show me the roll."

They went inside, all three of them, through corridors Tam had no business being in and had been dragged through by enforcers often enough to know anyway. The archive room where Freya had spent her days stood with its doors open and its shelves bare — not looted, just emptied, because paper burns even when ledgers don't, and every scrap of the Guild's secondary records had gone up in sympathy with the master ledger, or been thrown into hearths by hands that couldn't explain why they needed to, or simply crumbled, depending on which of the returning gods' whims you believed. The boy searched the shelf where the personnel rolls should have been. Then the next shelf. Then the next, faster, pulling out trade tallies and shipping manifests and one water-damaged book of prayers, turning each over as if Freya's name might be hiding on the back.

There was nothing. There was nothing anywhere with her name in it. Tam knew before the search started that there wouldn't be, and he could see that Freya knew it too, and he watched her stand in that empty room with her ruined hands folded like a student waiting to be examined, letting the boy tear the shelves apart, because she needed to watch someone else discover it. Somebody had to. She couldn't do it alone.

"Your name was Okafor," the boy said finally, defeated, holding a manifest of tallow shipments as if it had personally betrayed him. "I remember that. I *remember* you."

"Then write it down," Freya said. "Before you forget. Paper's cheap now."

* * *

They got bread and hard cheese from a baker on Cooper's Lane who refused payment, waving them off with both hands — "everyone's refusing payment," he said, baffled and delighted and frightened, "nobody remembers what anything cost" — and took it up to the roof of the old granary where Tam had slept half his youth, back when he was nobody's debtor and everybody's problem. From there you could see the whole valley, the dark Guild towers, the market square where three centuries ago the gods had gone silent, and beyond it the mountain, its summit wearing a thin plume of smoke like an old man's breath on a cold morning.

The dragon-fire was still burning. Tam had checked three times since dawn, half-convinced the whole night had been a fever dream. It was burning, but differently now — steadier, lower, a hearth-flame instead of a forge. Sealing nothing. Promising nothing. Just burning, the way fire did before anybody thought to make it sign contracts.

"She doesn't remember me either," Freya said. She was sitting cross-legged with the bread untouched in her lap, looking at the mountain. "My mother. I went past our street. The house is still there. She was in the garden." A pause. "She looked at me the way you look at a stranger who's standing too close."

"Your name was Freya Okafor," Tam said. "Senior scribe, third class. You corrected a smuggler's grammar in a debt hearing once, in front of Master Corvane, because you couldn't stand to let bad syntax go past you even when correcting it hurt the man's case. Your grandmother sang a lullaby that starts low and goes down, three notes, then four. Your favorite color is that ugly brownish red, like dried blood, and you called it *oxblood* and defended it with citations. You denied my petition for the return of my shadow on the twelfth of Harvestmonth and you apologized with your eyes the entire time you were doing it."

Freya had gone very still.

"How do you—"

"I'm a criminal," Tam said. "We don't keep ledgers, love. We keep *memories*. It's the only vault nobody can burn." He tore the bread in half and put the bigger piece in her hands. "You have no rank, no debt, no record, no proof. Fine. Proofs are for people who expect to be contradicted. I remember you. That's worth more than a seal and a shelf, and unlike a shelf, it travels."

She looked down at the bread. Her hands were shaking, just slightly, and Tam pretended not to see, because she was the sort of person who needed not to be seen shaking.

"The gods will come for me," she said at last. "Returned gods always want a keeper. Who better than a woman who already knows how accounts work and has no institution left to serve?" She gave a short laugh with nothing in it. "They'll offer me my past back. My name in an archive somewhere. My grandmother's memory, whole. All I'd have to do is start keeping books again."

"And?"

"And I haven't decided," Freya said. "That's the terrible part. Some clerk-shaped hole in me is *aching* for it."

Tam chewed his cheese and looked at the dark towers and thought about the old man in the turnip field, singing to fill the hole.

"Then decide fast," he said. "Because a runner came through the Salt Gate an hour ago, riding like the hells with a god's sigil stitched on his coat, asking at every corner for a woman named Freya Okafor." He shrugged. "Nobody could help him. As far as Ashvale knows, no such woman has ever existed."

Freya went still again. Below them, faint on the wind, the baker's bell rang noon, and somewhere in the market a crowd was gathering around a beggar child who, according to the noise drifting up, had been healed of a withered arm that very morning — the second such healing in two days.

"So," Tam said, and stood, and felt his returned shadow stretch out long and faithful on the rooftop boards beside him, his at last, owed to no one. "Do we run from the messenger, or do we go hear what the gods want?"

Freya Okafor — who was no one, who had no records, whose whole history was a handful of memories in a smuggler's head — finished the bread, brushed the crumbs from her ruined hands, and stood up beside him.

"We go listen," she said. "And then, whatever they offer —"

"I know," Tam said, heading for the ladder. "You'll deny them by the letter of the law. You'll apologize with your eyes the whole time."

Below them, the messenger with the sigil turned onto Cooper's Lane, and began to ask his way upward.
