## 19. The Gods' Reckoning

The summons came the way the old gods did everything now: without warning, without paper, without a single line of ink.

Freya was mending a shutter in the schoolhouse — Tam's schoolhouse, though he refused to hear it called anything but hers — when the light changed. Not brightened. *Changed*, the way candlelight changes when someone else enters the room. The dust in the air hung still, each mote suspended like a held breath, and every sound in Ashvale below seemed to lean away from her window.

She set down the hammer. She had known this was coming since the night the mountain exhaled embers and souls and the smell of burning vellum. You do not unmake the accounting of three centuries without the accountants noticing.

"They're here," she said to the empty room.

The room agreed. The walls of the schoolhouse — salvaged Guild timber, scarred with the chalk-marks of children learning their letters — shimmered once, like water disturbed by a stone, and then she was no longer in the room at all.

* * *

There was no mountain this time. No cathedral of jars, no guttering dragon overhead. The gods had built her a place out of her own memory instead: the scriptorium of the Ashvale Guild as it had been on her first day, twenty years ago, when she was a girl of seventeen with ink-stained fingers and a debt she didn't yet understand she'd inherited. The long oak tables. The green-shaded lamps. The smell of iron-gall ink and beeswax and the particular dust that lived in old paper — a dust that was, she knew now, mostly the residue of burned names.

Everything was perfect. That was how she knew it was false. The real scriptorium had had a cracked flagstone by the third table that caught your toe if you hurried. This one forgave you. Nothing real forgave you.

They were seated at the head table where Master Corvane used to sit — but not Corvane, never Corvane again; his name was ash on the wind above the mountain and even the memory of him felt thinner here, worn smooth like a river stone. There were seven of them, or perhaps there were seventy, or perhaps there was only one seen seven ways. They had shapes the way heat has shape: visible only by what they bent. One wore the outline of an old woman spinning. One was a column of migrating birds. One was simply a door, standing open onto weather.

The middle one spoke, and its voice was the voice of every ledger Freya had ever balanced, read aloud at last by something that understood what it had been saying all along.

"Freya Okafor. Scribe. Daughter of Ashvale. Granddaughter of Adaeze Okafor, who is free." A pause, delicate as a turned page. "You are difficult to find. You have no records."

"I know," Freya said. Her voice sounded small in the perfect room. "I burned them."

"You burned *his* records. And your own, incidentally, which was either carelessness or sacrifice, and we have spent some considerable time debating which." The god that was a door opened slightly wider; through it she could see rain falling upward. "We are not the Guild, scribe. Do not mistake us for them. We do not deal in souls. We deal in *truth* — which is heavier, rarer, and far less fungible."

"Then why does this room look like his?"

"Because you built it. We merely furnished it with what you kept." The spinning-woman god let her wheel fall silent. "You remember everything, Freya Okafor. Every entry. Every fraud. Every name in every book you ever copied, including the ones you copied from memory in a dark road beneath the earth while a man bled beside you. That copy saved a city. Do you know what we call a mind like yours?"

Freya said nothing. Her heart was doing something complicated and embarrassing, because she did know, and she had always known, and she had spent twenty years pretending the knowing was pride rather than hunger.

"We call it a keeper," said the god.

* * *

They showed her what they offered, and they showed it honestly, which was somehow worse than if they'd lied.

The door-god stepped aside, and beyond it was not rain but the Guild archive as it had been before the fire — vast, ordered, alive. And moving through the shelves came figures made of light, and each figure was a record restoring itself: ledgers un-burning, pages reassembling from ash like birds flocking home to roost. She saw the tax rolls of Ashvale rebuild themselves year by year. She saw birth-records, marriage-records, the grain tallies of the famine years. She saw a page dated sixty years before her birth fill itself in with a careful, beautiful hand, and the hand was her grandmother's, and the entry was a recipe for millet porridge written in the margin of a shipping manifest, and Freya's throat closed around a sound she refused to make.

"Your grandmother's memory is intact," the gods said gently. "Souls are not jars, scribe. Souls are *records*. What burned in the mountain was the binding, not the woman. But a record needs a reader. Adaeze Okafor's soul rises toward forgetting, as all freed things do. Unless someone holds her pages open."

"And my past?" Freya asked. "My name? My rank? My debts?" She heard herself say *debts* with longing and hated it.

"Restored. Every archive in Ashvale will bear your name again: Freya Okafor, First Scribe of the Reformed Guild, architect of the Liberation of the Ledger. They will teach children about you. There will be a statue; we cannot prevent the statue, mortals insist on statues." A ripple through the bird-shaped god that might have been laughter. "And in exchange—"

"In exchange I keep accounts."

"In exchange you *keep*. Truth must be tended, Freya. It does not survive loose. Someone must hold the record of what was owed and what was forgiven, what was stolen and what returned, who suffered and who profited — or within a generation Ashvale will believe the famine was a natural thing, that Corvane's ancestors were heroes, that the jars were kindnesses. Forgetting is not neutral. Forgetting has winners. We need a keeper who knows that. We need—" the voices braided together now, all seven, or seventy, warm as lamplight, terrible as arithmetic, "—we need *you*."

The scriptorium softened around her, became something kinder: her mother's kitchen, then the schoolhouse, then the pilgrim road at dawn with Tam limping beside her. The offer wearing her own memories as clothing.

"You would be free," they said. "That is the difference. Corvane bound people to pay debts. We ask you only to remember them. No contract. No seal. Only purpose."

And Freya stood in the perfect false room with her grandmother's porridge recipe glowing on a shelf behind her, and she wanted it so badly her teeth ached.

* * *

Here is what she thought about, in the long silence the gods were courteous enough to grant her:

She thought about Tam Vess, who had sold his shadow for forty crowns and a boat, and who had come back for it not because he needed it — he'd learned to walk in the noon-dark well enough — but because it was *his*, and a man should be the sole author of his own silhouette. He had stood in her office with a god's answered prayer folded in his fist like a knife and demanded justice by the letter of a law he despised, because it was the only language the law respected. And when she denied him — she remembered denying him, the exact words, the clause numbers, the way his face went carefully blank — he had still, months later, taken a blade meant for her ribs and sent her a warning coded in a false ledger entry, risking the vault, the enforcers, everything, for a clerk who had once recited contract law at him like a wall.

Tam, who now sat in the front row of her school every morning, fifty years old and learning his letters, because he said a man who'd lost his shadow ought to at least own his signature.

She thought about the widow from her first chapter — no. From her first *week*, all those years ago, who had bought back her husband's last words at a price that took nine years to pay, and who had wept with gratitude, and Freya had recorded the transaction in a fair hand and gone home and slept soundly, and it had taken her until the unwritten entries began appearing to understand that the sleep was the complicity. The Guild hadn't needed chains. It had needed clerks who slept well.

She thought about the ledger itself — the master ledger, the true one, the one whose ghost-entries had appeared in her own handwriting before she ever wrote them. *One soul, returned, price: the scribe.* It had been recording corrections all along, truths the Guild suppressed, and in the end she had fed it to a dragon and been freed of every word she'd ever written. Unwritten. Unranked. Undebted. A woman no contract could bind, standing in a rebuilt city where nobody could quite recall what they'd owed her or she them — except Tam, who remembered for both of them, out loud, daily, usually at volume.

And she thought about what the gods had just shown her, and she finally saw it clearly, the way you see the trap in a fair contract only when you've already signed too many of them.

They were offering her the archives. The *archives*. Not truth — truth lives in bodies, in lullabies, in scars, in a smuggler's stubborn memory and a child's chalk letters. The archives are what power keeps. Every cage she had ever known had been built of records: the Guild's ledgers, the court's jars, the contracts sealed in dragon-fire. Even the gods' silence had been a kind of ledger — three centuries of unanswered prayers entered and balanced. And now the gods had returned and found the world's great book burned, and they were frightened. That was the thing under the warmth. That was the tremor in the lamplight voice. Gods who had been absent for three hundred years had come back to a world where the accounting was gone, and they could not bear it, because a god without a record of worship is only weather.

They didn't want a keeper of truth. They wanted a keeper of *themselves*.

"No," Freya said.

The perfect room went very still.

"The archives stay burned," she said, and her voice steadied as she went, the way it always did when she finally stopped balancing and started choosing. "My grandmother's memory doesn't live on your shelves. It lives in a lullaby my mother sang me, and I still have it, all of it, every note, and I'll sing it to whoever wants to learn it, and they'll sing it to their children, and none of it will be written down where the next Corvane can find it and price it. My name stays unwritten. Let them guess about the statue."

"Scribe—" began the door-god.

"You want to know what I learned under the mountain?" Freya took a step toward the head table, toward the shapes of heat, and she was not afraid anymore, and that surprised her more than anything. "I learned that a record is not the thing itself. The jar is not the soul. The ledger is not the debt. The archive is not the truth. Power always confuses the container with the contained — that's the whole trick, that's the oldest trick, and you're doing it right now with better lighting. You're offering me a cage with the bars made of my own good intentions, and I'm sure it's a very comfortable cage, and I watched a man build those his whole life and die when a dragon sneezed on his life's work."

The spinning-woman's wheel creaked to a stop. "Without a keeper, the truth will fray."

"Then it will fray in a thousand hands instead of sitting whole in one," Freya said. "That's not fraying. That's how living things work. You don't preserve a fire by putting it in a jar. Believe me. I've seen the jars."

Silence. The seven shapes regarded her, and she felt the weight of their attention the way she'd once felt the weight of the master ledger in her arms on the pilgrim road — heavy, but hers to carry or drop.

"You understand," the voices said at last, and there was something new in them, something that might have been respect or might have been the beginning of a much longer argument, "that we will ask again. Gods are patient. We have been dead for three centuries; we are very good at waiting. Someday, Freya Okafor, you will want something only a record can give."

"Probably," Freya admitted. "But I'll want it in front of witnesses, so you can't quietly enter it into a book."

And the bird-shaped god laughed — actually laughed, a sound like a whole flock taking wing at once — and the perfect room began, gratefully, to crack along its seams, the false flagstones splitting to show real mud underneath, the green lamps guttering into honest smoke.

"One gift, freely given, no record kept," the gods said as the scriptorium dissolved. "Because you declined beautifully, and even we keep no books on beauty. Look for what was never written down. Some things survived the fire because they were never in it."

"What does that mean?" Freya demanded. "What things? Whose—"

But the room was gone, and she was standing in the schoolhouse with the hammer still warm in her hand and evening coming down over ruined, rising Ashvale, and the words were already fading the way dream-words fade.

Except one. *Look for what was never written down.*

She stood there a long time, watching the light change, turning it over. Things never written down. Songs. Names spoken aloud. A lullaby her mother used to sing, which her mother had learned from a woman the family never spoke of, in a house that had stood near the mountain's shadow, before the famine, before the bargain, before any of it.

Freya set down the hammer slowly.

Her grandmother's jar was shattered; the soul had risen with the embers; she had mourned it as gone. But the gods had just told her — freely, without entering it anywhere — that some things had survived the fire precisely because no one had ever thought to burn them.

Things like a song.

She pulled her coat off its peg, checked that Tam's spare knife was still in the pocket where he insisted she keep it, and went down into the streets to find him, because if she was going to walk back into the emptied vaults beneath the mountain, she was not going to do it alone — and besides, someone had to remember, for both of them, exactly what she was about to go looking for.
