## 20. Grandmother's Lullaby

The vault was emptier than a held breath.

Freya stood at the top of the stair and let the silence come up to meet her — not the silence of stone, which was only the absence of sound, but the other kind, the kind that had weight and texture, the kind that pressed against the ears like water. For three days the court beneath the mountain had been emptying itself: souls rising in columns of ember-light through the great shaft, drawn upward by the dragon-fire that no longer sealed anything, that simply burned now, the way fire had burned before anyone thought to make it a witness. The heralds were gone. The jars stood open on their shelves, thousands of them, each one holding nothing but a faint ring of ash at the bottom, like the ghost of a cup of tea.

She had told Tam she needed to do this alone. He had argued — of course he had argued; arguing was what Tam did instead of breathing — and then he had looked at her face and stopped, and pressed his good hand briefly against her shoulder, and said, "I'll be at the mouth of the mountain. Sing loud enough and I'll hear it."

Now she descended.

Her boots made small sounds on the steps, and each one came back to her from the walls as if the vault were trying to remember how echoes worked. The shelves rose on either side of the narrow aisle, tier upon tier, receding into dark. Once there had been light here — jar-light, soul-light, the cold blue glow of ten thousand stolen years — and the court had called it beautiful. She remembered thinking so herself, standing here among them, hearing the lullaby rise faintly from a shelf near the deepest wall. She had thought it beautiful, and she would never forgive herself for that, or perhaps she would, because forgiveness was one of the things the burning had made cheap and plentiful, lying around in the ruins like coins no one wanted anymore.

She walked the aisle slowly, trailing her fingers along the shelf edges. Dust came away on her fingertips in soft gray streaks. Here and there a jar remained unbroken — the ones too high to reach when the rite collapsed, the ones whose souls had already risen before the glass could shatter. She looked inside one. Ash ring, and a smell like rain on hot stone, and nothing else. Whatever had been kept in these vessels had not been damaged by its keeping, exactly. But it had been *kept*, and keeping was its own violence, and now the keeping was over.

At the deepest wall, where the dragon-fire's warmth reached even now as a faint pulse through the rock, she found the place.

The grandmother's jar had shattered. Tam's thrown stone — or his boot heel, he'd never say which — had caught it square, and the shards lay scattered across a yard of floor, catching the dim red light and throwing it back in tiny pieces. Freya knelt among them. She did not touch them at first. She just knelt, the way her mother had once knelt at a grave that had no body in it, and understood, finally, why her mother had never spoken of the old woman. It wasn't shame. It was that grief with nowhere to put itself will hollow out a person's voice, word by word, until speaking the name costs more than silence ever could.

"Grandmother," she said.

The vault took the word and gave it back smaller.

* * *

She had brought things. She had planned this the way she planned everything, with lists, because lists were the last honest language she knew. In her satchel: a spade borrowed from the terraced gardens above, where the first crops since the famine-bargain broke were going into thawed ground. A length of linen. A clay bowl filled with mountain soil, because the vault floor was bare rock and something in her insisted that her grandmother should rest in earth, actual earth, the kind that grew things. And wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom, the notebook — plain paper, no seal, no watermark, bought from a stationer in ruined Ashvale who had asked no questions because he no longer knew how to ask any.

But before any of it, she gathered the shards.

They were worse than she expected. Not because they were sharp — she cut her thumb twice and barely noticed — but because of what was written on them. Of course there was writing on them. There had always been writing on everything in Ashvale; the Guild had inked its claims onto every surface the world offered, and the court beneath the mountain had been no different. Each shard bore a fragment of the contract: *the soul of Maren Okafor, given in surety* — *against the year of hunger* — *until the fire* — *dims*. Her grandmother's name broken across six pieces of glass. Freya sat back on her heels and looked at the name in pieces and felt something in her chest go very quiet and very hard.

They had written her grandmother down. That was the theft under all the other thefts. Not the keeping of the soul — the *writing* of it, the reduction of a woman who had sung and scolded and grown vegetables in bad soil and loved a granddaughter she would never meet into a line of terms in someone else's hand. Freya had spent her whole life believing that records were sacred, that ink was truth, that a thing properly entered in a ledger was a thing made safe. She had built her identity on it, entry by entry, in Corvane's scriptorium, and it had taken the master ledger burning in dragon-fire to show her the truth underneath: that a record is only a cage with good handwriting.

She folded the shards into the linen, carefully, the way you fold a shroud.

* * *

Digging in the vault floor was slow work. The rock resisted, then yielded grudgingly along an old seam, and Freya dug until her shoulders burned and her palms blistered and the hole was deep enough — not a proper grave, but deep enough for what it would hold. Twice she stopped and rested her forehead against the cool edge of the excavation and breathed, and both times she heard, or thought she heard, far above, the dragon exhale: a long slow sound like wind moving through a house at night, like something enormous turning over in its sleep and finding, for the first time in centuries, nothing to guard.

She lined the bottom of the grave with the mountain soil from the bowl. Then the bundle of shards. Then, after a moment's hesitation, she took the false ledger entry from her coat pocket — Tam's coded warning, the scrap of paper that had started her descent, that had saved her life twice over — and she almost buried that too, and then didn't. Some papers deserved to survive. That was the whole lesson, wasn't it? Not that records were evil. That records belonged in many hands, in plain notebooks, in schoolrooms, in the mouths of people telling each other what happened. Only the single, secret, sealed record was a cage. She put the scrap back in her pocket and covered the shards with soil, handful by handful, until the mound was done and her hands were filthy and there was dirt under her nails in a way no scribe's hands had ever been dirty before.

And then, because there was nothing left between her and the moment, she sang.

* * *

Her mother's version had been half-remembered, worn smooth like a river stone, and Freya's was smoother still — two generations of forgetting between the song and the throat singing it. But the melody came up out of her like water finding an old channel, and the words, most of them, assembled themselves in the dark:

*Sleep, my heart, the field is sown,*
*the debt is paid, the debt was never yours,*
*sleep, my heart, the fire is low*
*but low fires burn the longest,*
*and I will carry your name in my mouth*
*where no one can enter it in a book.*

She had never known where the lullaby came from. Her mother hadn't either. Now, kneeling in the emptied vault with dirt-blackened hands, Freya understood that it didn't matter. Songs like this one didn't have authors. They had carriers. The song had passed from her grandmother's mother to her grandmother to — presumably — the woman Maren Okafor had been before she sold herself to save a starving town, and then somehow, impossibly, past the jar and the vault and the three silent centuries, down to a girl in Ashvale who hummed it without knowing what she carried. Memory in the body. Not the book. The Guild had owned the name, the contract, the soul itself for a while there at the end — but it had never owned the song, because the song lived somewhere no scribe could reach.

Her voice cracked on the last verse. She let it crack. She was done with perfect penmanship.

When the final note faded, the vault was still again — and then it wasn't.

It began as warmth. Not the dragon's distant pulse through the rock, but something closer, something *present*, a warmth against her cheek like breath, like standing near an oven door, like being a child again and falling asleep against her mother's side while the evening market noise went on below the window. Freya went absolutely still. The warmth moved — slowly, deliberately, the way a hand moves when it means to be gentle — across her shoulder, down her arm, and settled over her dirt-caked hands where they rested on the fresh-turned mound.

And in that warmth, faint as heat-shimmer, she heard it: the lullaby, answered. Not in words. In melody only, four notes rising where hers had fallen, a harmony she had never learned and had always known, completing a phrase her version had lost somewhere in the generations. The missing line. The one about morning.

Then it lifted. She felt it go — a lightness passing up through her, out of her, unhurried, the way a bird leaves a branch without disturbing it. The warmth thinned, spread, rose toward the great shaft where the embers climbed, and Freya watched the air above the little grave shimmer once, twice, and go still.

"Grief is love with nowhere to put itself," she said aloud, to the empty shelves, to the dark, to no one. "I'm putting it here."

She stayed a long time after that. Long enough for her knees to ache and the dragon's pulse to change rhythm overhead, long enough that when she finally rose, brushing the dirt from her coat, she felt lighter in a way that frightened her slightly — as if she had set down not just the shards but some old weight she'd mistaken for her spine. She shouldered the satchel. She touched the notebook through the oilcloth, checking it was there, the way you touch a wound to confirm it's real.

At the foot of the stair she paused and looked back once. The little mound of mountain soil sat dark against the pale rock, the only new thing in a vault full of endings. Above it, impossibly, a thread of green — a moss spore, maybe, carried in on her boots, already finding purchase in the damp.

Things grew here now. That was the whole of the eulogy, and it was enough.

* * *

Tam was waiting at the mountain's mouth as promised, sitting on a flat stone with his bandaged side and his irrepressible face, and when he saw her expression he didn't ask how it went. He just nodded and fell into step beside her, and for the first mile neither of them spoke, which for Tam was practically a religious observance.

"You're different," he said finally, when the pilgrim road unrolled ahead of them, brown and ordinary and washed with morning light.

"I'm less," Freya said. "That's what different feels like."

He considered this. "Less is underrated. I've been less my whole life. Very freeing."

She laughed — actually laughed, a rough unused sound — and the road took them down toward the valley, toward the smoke of cooking fires rising from a town learning how to live without ledgers, toward whatever came next, which was nothing, which was everything, which was a blank page.

And it was there, on the road, with the mountain at her back and the sun in her eyes, that Freya Okafor stopped walking, because an idea had arrived — sudden, complete, and insistent as an unwritten entry forcing its way into her hand. Children in Ashvale couldn't write. Half the town couldn't read their own names, and the Guild had liked it that way, had *needed* it that way, and the Guild's towers might be dark but the ignorance was still standing, patient as stone, waiting for someone to build a new cage around it.

"Tam," she said. "How do you feel about learning your letters?"

He squinted at her. "Why?"

"Because," she said, already walking faster, "somebody has to write down what happened here — and this time, everybody writes."
