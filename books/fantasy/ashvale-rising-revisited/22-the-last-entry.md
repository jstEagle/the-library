## 22. The Last Entry

The notebook cost three coppers.

Freya Okafor turned it over in her hands in the market stall the way she had once turned over ledgers bound in calfskin and iron clasps — but this was only paper, cheap and soft-edged, sewn with plain thread. No guild seal on the cover. No chain to bolt it to a desk. No lock whose key lived in someone else's pocket.

"Writing book?" the stall-keeper asked. She was a young woman with flour on her forearms, one of the first students from the school's second year, back when the school had been a single table in the ruins of the counting-house and Freya had taught letters to anyone who would sit still long enough.

"A record," Freya said.

The word still carried weight in Ashvale. It always would. The stall-keeper weighed it carefully, the way people here now weighed all the old guild-vocabulary, testing each term for hidden hooks before she let it into her mouth.

"For debts?" she asked at last.

"No," Freya said. "The opposite."

* * *

She climbed the pilgrim road in the late afternoon, as she did every year on this day, though no one waited for her at the top and no law required her to come. The road itself had changed. Once it had been a corridor of dread — pilgrims shuffling up toward the mountain's mouth with their petitions clutched to their chests, hoping the court beneath would hear them out before the fire dimmed another degree. Now it was simply a road. Children raced down its switchbacks chasing a hoop. A man sat on the old waystone mending a fishing net, and he nodded to her without fear or reverence, which was, Freya thought, the finest monument anyone had ever raised.

The mountain's mouth stood open. It always stood open now; there was nothing left inside worth guarding, and the court of quiet things had gone quiet in an entirely different way — empty, echoing, its cathedral of jars reduced to glass shards and dust and the faint mineral smell of souls long since flown. Tam had offered, once, to lead an expedition down to clear the vaults properly, turn them into cellars or a shrine or anything that wasn't what they had been. Freya had gone down herself instead, alone, the year after the burning, and found that emptiness could be a kind of mercy. She had swept one small chamber clean with her own hands. That was enough. Some places you didn't repurpose. You just let them be finished.

Above her, wreathed in cloud, the dragon-fire burned.

It had steadied. That was the miracle no one had predicted and no god had explained: when the master ledger fed the flame, when every contract in Ashvale went up in a single conflagration of ink and obligation, the fire should have guttered out entirely. Instead it had drawn breath — a vast, slow, tidal breath — and settled into something new. Not the hungry white heat that sealed bargains. Something warmer. Amber, mostly, with veins of gold, pulsing like a heart that had finally been allowed to rest.

The scholars from the coast came every summer to study it. They wrote papers about residual thaumaturgic inertia and the thermodynamics of oathfire. Freya let them talk. She knew the truth, because she had felt it pass through her hands: the fire had never wanted to be a lock. It had been made a lock, pressed into service by the first scribes who realized that flame could authenticate a signature. Given nothing left to seal, it simply burned, the way a dog given nothing to guard will finally sleep in the sun.

She stood at the threshold a while, as she always did, and let the warmth touch her face.

"I'm going to write it down," she told the mountain. "All of it. As much as I remember."

The fire pulsed once, gently, like a nod.

It couldn't hear her. She was fairly sure it couldn't hear her. But some conversations you kept anyway, because the person you'd been having them with — the girl who had walked up this road with stolen pages sewn into her coat and a dead woman's lullaby caught in her throat — deserved to know how the story ended.

* * *

The school occupied what had been the Guild's Hall of Reckoning.

They had debated tearing the building down. Tam had argued for it with his arms crossed and his shadow — restored, whole, gloriously ordinary — stretched long across the rubble of the counting floor. "Burn it," he'd said. "Salt the ground. Let people build something that doesn't have ghosts in the walls."

But Freya had walked through the ruined hall and seen it differently. The great vaulted ceiling, cracked but standing. The rows of desks where clerks had once bent over other people's lives, recording trades in laughter and years and names. The light coming through the broken windows in long dusty shafts.

"They'll expect us to be afraid of it," she said. "So we'll fill it with children."

Now the Hall of Reckoning held forty students, ages six to sixty, and the desks had been sawn down to a height that suited the smallest of them, and the walls — where Corvane's ancestors had hung the framed charters of every impossible bargain — now held the students' own work. First attempts at letters. Family stories, written out in careful, wobbling hands. A charcoal drawing of the dragon-fire that little Maren Vess — Tam's granddaughter, gods help them all, the Vess stubbornness skipping a generation and landing twice — had made after her first trip up the pilgrim road.

Tam himself sat by the door most mornings, ostensibly keeping order, actually dozing, his shadow pooled around his chair like a faithful old hound. He was slower now. The wound he'd taken on the pilgrim road, the one meant for her, had never quite stopped aching in cold weather, and the winters had a way of finding him. But his eyes were still sharp, and when a merchant had come around two years ago trying to sign the school's students into "apprenticeship bonds" — paper contracts with suspiciously familiar phrasing — it was Tam who had torn the document in half before Freya even reached for it.

"We don't sign things here," he'd told the merchant, in the flat voice he saved for people who deserved it. "We *write* things. There's a difference. I can teach you the difference, if you've got the time. First lesson's free."

The merchant had not come back.

Freya unlocked the school door — an ordinary lock, with an ordinary key, hers alone, chosen freely — and lit the lamps, and set the day's slates out, and then, before any of it began, sat down at her own desk in the corner where the master ledger's stand had once stood, and opened the plain notebook to its last blank page.

* * *

She had learned, in the years since the burning, exactly what she had lost.

Not the guild rank — she didn't miss it. Not the debt — she had spent her whole life under that weight and the moment of its lifting had felt like surfacing from deep water, every breath since then a small astonishment. What she had lost was smaller and stranger: her own recorded past. Her apprenticeship papers. Her mother's name in the parish rolls. The date of her grandmother's sale, the exact terms, the precise wording of the famine bargain — all of it had lived in the master ledger, and all of it had burned, and so there was no document anywhere in the world that proved Freya Okafor had ever existed before the night the dragon-fire rose.

People remembered her, of course. Memory was the one archive the fire hadn't touched. Tam remembered. The students remembered. Old Widow Sarelle, who had bought back her husband's last words in chapter one of all this — who had sat across Freya's desk with her life savings in a handkerchief and purchased the sound of a dead man's voice — remembered, and told the story often, and always ended it the same way: "And that girl looked at me like she was already sorry. Like she knew what the books were doing to her."

But memory fades. Memory is a candle, not a lamp. And Freya had watched an entire city discover, in the ash-strewn weeks after the burning, how much of themselves they had outsourced to paper. Debts voided, yes — but also deeds unproven, marriages unrecorded, grandparents' faces going blurry because the only portrait had been a collateral asset seized by the Guild forty years ago. The freedom had been real. So had the loss. She refused, in her school, to pretend otherwise.

That was why she taught writing. Not contracts — never contracts; she had banned the word *sign* from the classroom and replaced it with *witness*, and the students laughed at her for it, and she let them. Writing your own history, in your own hand, kept by many hands — that was the lesson. Truth scattered among a hundred notebooks outlives any single ledger, however grand. Corvane had understood power as concentration: one book, one vault, one flame, one point of failure. Freya understood it as distribution now. You could burn her notebook. You could burn every notebook. But you would have to burn the whole city, memory by memory, person by person, and by then the story would already be somewhere else.

Still. There was one record only she could keep.

* * *

She wrote slowly. Her hand, once trained to the swift merciless efficiency of guild script, had loosened over the years into something rounder, more patient. She wrote the way she had learned to speak again after the mountain — plainly, without clauses nested in clauses, without escape hatches.

*Today is the seventeenth of Harvestmonth. The fire above the mountain burns steady. Maren wrote her name correctly for the first time and demanded a celebration, so we celebrated. Tam fell asleep during arithmetic again.*

Then, lower on the page, smaller:

*I went up the road today and told the mountain I would write everything down. I have been saying it for years. Today I mean to start.*

Because that was the truth she had circled for a decade: she had never written it down. The full account. The famine, and the fraud, and Corvane's ancestor forging her grandmother's consent; the court beneath the mountain with its cathedral of jars; the herald's voice like dust settling; Tam bleeding on the pilgrim road; the master ledger curling and blackening in the dragon's breath while souls rose past her like embers returning home. She had told it aloud a hundred times — to students, to scholars, to the occasional pilgrim who climbed the road looking for the woman from the stories. But telling was not the same as writing. Telling drifted. Writing stayed.

And she was not young anymore. She had earned the right to say so. Her hands ached in the mornings; her eyes needed better light than they used to. If the account was going to exist, it had to exist soon, in her own hand, before her own memory became the kind of archive that needed a keeper.

She had been afraid of it, she realized. That was the honest thing, the thing she would write if she was brave enough: she had been afraid that writing it down would make it a record again, and records, in her experience, became instruments. Every page she had ever entered had been used against someone. How could she put her grandmother there, in ink, in a book, and not feel the old machinery turning?

But that was the lesson of the plain notebook, wasn't it. The paper didn't make the cage. The terms did. A record kept freely, owned by no institution, answerable to no flame — that wasn't a contract. It was a gift. To whoever came after. To the girl Maren's age who would someday want to know what the silence under the mountain had sounded like.

To her grandmother, who had never gotten to be anything in the books except a line item.

*One soul,* the unwritten entries had said, in her own handwriting she'd never written. *Returned.* The ledger had known before she did. It had recorded the truth the Guild suppressed, correction by correction, until the corrections burned brighter than the original lie. She owed that book a strange debt — the only one she'd ever carry gladly. It had died so that she could live unrecorded. The least she could do was pick up the work it had started, and finish it in a plainer hand.

* * *

The students arrived with the morning bell from the temple — the old gods' temple, rebuilt small and warm, its priests elected now, its doors propped open with a brick because the congregation kept bringing offerings and the priests kept giving them away. The gods themselves remained a presence rather than a spectacle: answered prayers were rarer these days than in those first astonishing months, but they happened, quietly, a fever breaking here, rain arriving there, and the theology of it was still being argued about in the taverns with great enthusiasm and no resolution.

Freya taught the morning session — letters for the youngest, composition for the older ones — and gave her usual lecture when a boy named Aldric asked why they had to learn to write at all when the town crier could just tell everyone things.

"Because," Freya said, "a voice stops when the speaker stops. Ink keeps going. And one day there will be something true that powerful people want forgotten, and the only place it will be safe is in a hundred different hands, in a hundred different houses, where no one fire can reach it." She tapped the plain notebook. "I knew a book that held everything, and it held everything *badly*, because one person decided what went in it. Don't be one person deciding. Be a hundred people remembering."

Aldric considered this with the gravity of the ten-year-old. "Did you decide things? In your old job?"

"Yes," Freya said. "That's why I'm teaching you."

He wrote that down. She tried not to look too pleased about it.

At midday Tam limped in with bread and hard cheese and gossip, and they ate at the big table with whichever students hadn't run home, and he told her about the fish catch and the argument at the temple and the scholar from the coast who had offered him an absurd sum for "the smuggler's shadow, preserved, for study" — "Told him it wasn't preserved, it was *lived in*," Tam said, wiggling his fingers in the sunlight, casting a shadow that waved back — and for a while Freya forgot the notebook waiting in her desk drawer with its last page half-filled.

But when the students emptied out and the lamps were trimmed and Tam had gone home to his granddaughter's cooking, she took it out again.

* * *

Evening. The window of her small room above the school — she had never moved back into any house of her family's; there were none left to move into, and anyway this room suited her, one bed, one desk, one window facing the mountain — the evening light came amber through the glass, the same color as the fire on the peak, and Freya Okafor dipped her pen and began.

She wrote the famine first. The winter the crops failed and the Guild's granaries stayed locked behind terms no starving family could meet. She wrote her grandmother — Nana Ashe, who sang while she worked, who smelled of woodsmoke and river water, whose lullaby had traveled down a mountain and back up again inside a daughter's daughter. She wrote the bargain as the ledger had corrected it: not sold, *taken*. A fraud dressed as mercy, authored by a Corvane, witnessed by a flame that had no choice but to seal what it was shown. She wrote the court beneath the mountain, the jars, the hush. She wrote the herald's terrible gentleness. She wrote Tam's shadow and Tam's blood and Tam's laugh, which had survived both. She wrote the burning — the ledger opening in the dragon-fire like a bird releasing everything it had ever carried — and the souls rising, thousands of them, a reverse snowfall up the mountain shaft, and the sound the gods had made, which was not words at all but something like a chord finally resolving.

Her pen slowed near the end. Not from tiredness.

She wrote: *My mother never spoke of her mother, and I spent my childhood thinking that silence was shame. It was grief. Grief with nowhere to go, because the object of it was filed away in a vault, catalogued, interest-bearing. When I buried the jar-shards I understood at last that inheritance isn't what you're owed. It's what you carry. She is not in a book. She is in the way I sing. That is the whole of the estate, and I am rich.*

She sat back. The lamp flickered. Outside, far above, the fire on the mountain breathed its slow tide of amber, sealing nothing, owning nothing, simply burning, the way a heart burns when it is finally permitted to do only that.

One line remained. She had known what it would be for years — it had been waiting in her the way the lullaby had waited, patient, certain of its moment. Chapter one of her life had been a ledger entry: *Freya Okafor, apprentice scribe, bound by debt, value assessed.* This would be the closing frame, and it would be hers alone, in her own hand, in a book no flame authenticated and no institution claimed.

She wrote:

*Freya Okafor. Free. Remembered.*

She read it over once. Then she did the thing no guild scribe would ever have done, the thing that would have gotten her flogged in the Hall of Reckoning in another life: she closed the notebook without sealing it, without dating it into permanence, without locking it away. She left it open on the desk, face-up, where anyone who climbed her stairs could read it — an invitation, not an instrument. Tomorrow she would copy the line into Maren's notebook, and Tam's, and Widow Sarelle's, and Aldric's, and let a hundred hands carry it forward, imperfect and alive.

Below, in the darkened schoolroom, the students' notebooks sat stacked on their desks, hundreds of small histories in a hundred different hands, none of them binding anyone to anything.

Above, the fire burned steady.

And Freya Okafor blew out her lamp, and slept, unrecorded and utterly known, while the mountain kept watch over a city that had finally learned to keep its own accounts — in memory, in ink, in the bodies and voices of the living — and owed nothing to anyone but each other.
