## 1. The Ledger of Small Impossibilities

Rain is the honest weather of Greyharrow. It doesn't pretend to be anything else. It comes off the Grey Sea in long grey sheets, hammers the slate roofs, fills the gutters with a sound like applause for no one, and by midnight it has soaked every drunk, dockhand, and customs officer in the city to the same degree of misery. I have lived under this rain for eleven years, and I will tell you what I told the last person who asked me why I stay—and she only asked because she was dying of curiosity and bad claret: I stay because rain makes people look down. And people who look down do not look at me.

My name is Jonah Eastwick. You will not have heard it. That is not false modesty; it is professional accomplishment.

I am the gatekeeper and ledger-keeper of the Ashvale Guild, which trades—so the plaque on our door says—in *rare acquisitions*. What the plaque means, and what the ledgers say when they are feeling truthful, is that we trade in impossible things. Bottled echoes. Borrowed years. Last words, extracted warm from the mouths of the dying and sold to widowers who couldn't make it to the bedside in time. One locked door beneath the counting house that opens onto nowhere anyone can name, which we charge a shocking rate to never open.

The Guild has seven masters, forty-three sworn factors, an army of enforcers with matching boots, and exactly one man who knows what we actually own down to the last half-ounce of grave-dust. He is currently sitting in the counting house at the top of Salt Row with his sleeves rolled up, a pot of cold tea going bitter beside him, and four ledgers open in front of him like altars.

I balance accounts the way other men pray: nightly, kneeling, and with the quiet certainty that nobody upstairs is watching but doing it anyway.

* * *

The counting house of the Ashvale Guild was built by a man who understood that architecture is intimidation conducted in stone. The ceiling goes up so far that the chandeliers look like constellations belonging to some other, colder sky. The columns are black granite veined with white, and the factors who work beneath them speak in the hushed voice of men who suspect the building is listening. It is, in a sense. I keep its books.

My desk sits in an alcove behind the third column—the one with the hairline crack running floor to capital, which I have been meaning to report for six years and never have, because the crack is the only thing in this building older than the ledgers and I feel a kinship with it.

On this particular night—the ninth of Duskmonth, if you care, and nobody does—the work was ordinary, which is to say it was perfect. Entry by entry I worked through the autumn reconciliations. Two vials of bottled echo, purchased from the drowned choir of Saint Ferrow's, sold to a theatre manager who wanted his empty house to sound full. Debited: one echo of applause, mid-sized crowd, slight reverb. Credited: eighty crowns and the manager's undying gratitude, which I did not enter as an asset because gratitude depreciates faster than anything else the Guild handles.

A borrowed year. A fishmonger on Brine Street had purchased twelve months of somebody else's life—you never ask whose; there is a drawer for that question and the drawer stays shut—and he'd spent it badly, the way most do, on a woman who left him anyway. The year came back to us thin and used-up, and I entered its return with the small pang I always feel for years spent by the wrong people. There should be a charity for that. There isn't, because the Guild would buy it.

The locked door. Beneath the counting house, third sublevel past the wine stores, iron-banded oak older than any document we hold, and the entry for it reads—as it has read, in the same hand, for longer than my family has been able to write:

*Item: one (1) door, locked. Provenance: unknown. Status: sealed. Value: incalculable. Instruction: let be.*

*Let be.* Two words that have done more work than all the rest of the ledger combined. Every master who ever inherited the books inherited those two words, and every one of them obeyed, and I sometimes think that obedience is the only reason Greyharrow still stands. You learn things about a building when you keep its accounts. You learn that the sublevels are numbered wrong on purpose. You learn that the wine stores go deeper than the deed allows. You learn not to count the doors on the third sublevel twice, because the second count never agrees with the first.

I am content. I want that on the record, since I keep records. My lodgings above the gatehouse are small and dry and mine. My tea is adequate. My work is exact, and exactness is a kind of peace—I have never believed the people who say it isn't. When you know where everything is, nothing can frighten you. When every impossible thing in the world has a line, a number, and a status, the world holds still.

That was the shape of my night. Rain against the high windows. Cold tea. Four ledgers. The great black columns holding up their borrowed sky. And then I turned to the October folio, ran my finger down the margin to find my place, and stopped.

There was an entry I had not written.

* * *

Understand: I write every entry. In eleven years I have written every entry in the Ashvale ledgers, including the ones the masters dictated to me in whispers and the ones nobody dictated at all, which I found out myself and recorded because an unrecorded impossibility is not a safe impossibility—it is a loose tooth in the mouth of the world. My hand is in every book. I could pick my own script out of a thousand, the way a mother picks a cry from a nursery of them: narrow, upright, economical, the letters of a man who resents wasting ink.

This entry was in my hand.

That is the part I want you to sit with, because it took me several minutes to sit with it myself. Not similar to my hand. Not a fair imitation. Mine—the slight rightward lean, the way my *a* never quite closes, the little hitch in the descender of a *g* that my schoolmaster beat at me for two years and lost. If you had put the page in front of me blind, among fifty pages, I would have chosen it and sworn an oath on it.

*Entry 447:* *one (1) door, unlocked. Provenance: Ashvale. Status: open. Instruction: none.*

And below it, in the space where value is recorded, where I have written *incalculable* and *priceless* and once, disgracefully, *negotiable*—below all the vocabulary of my trade, in ink that matched the age of the page, that had dried into the paper the way only old ink dries—

*A door out of Ashvale.*

* * *

Here is the trouble with being a meticulous man: you cannot un-notice things.

A slovenly bookkeeper would have frowned, decided the light was playing tricks, and gone to bed. I checked. I checked the way I check everything, which is thoroughly, which is obsessively, which is to say I checked until checking became its own kind of horror. I fetched the October folio from two years prior—no such entry. Five years prior—no such entry. I held the page to the candle and looked at the ink under the flame's honest light, and the ink had sunk into the fibers of the paper, aged, foxed at the edges, decades old at minimum. The entry had always been there. I had read that folio perhaps two hundred times. It had never been there before.

You will say: then your memory is wrong. Fine. I invite you to believe that. I tried to believe it myself, standing in my alcove with the rain applauding nobody outside, and here is what stopped me: the folio's pagination. The entry sat between page 44 and page 45, and the pages around it had adjusted. Not torn, not inserted—a single leaf that carried entries 446 through 448, seamless, the chain-lines of the paper matching its neighbors perfectly. As though the book had grown a new page the way a tree grows a ring, from the inside, without disturbing the bark.

I know what you're thinking, because I thought it too: forged. Someone got into the counting house, someone with a grudge or a scheme, someone very good. But the counting house locks are mine. The keys are mine. The schedule of the watchmen is mine, drafted quarterly in my own hand. Nobody enters or leaves that building at night without passing my window in the gatehouse, and the window faces the only door, and the door had not opened. I would stake my reputation on it, and my reputation—which is to say, my invisibility—is the only thing I own.

So I did what a ledger-keeper does with an entry he cannot explain. I investigated the entry itself.

*Ashvale.* I said the word aloud in the empty hall and it made less sound than it should have. Try it yourself, if you can say it at all. It sat in my mouth like a coin from a country I'd never traded with. I searched the ledgers—every ledger—for the word. Nothing. Eleven years of bottling echoes and borrowing years and letting one door be, and the word *Ashvale* appeared exactly once in all my records: in the entry I had never written, in my own hand, describing a door out of somewhere that apparently needed a door *out* of it, which implies, if you follow the grammar of these things, that something might want back in.

Or out. I am careful with doors. It is my entire profession. And a door that has an *out* on one side has an *in* on the other, and both sides of a door are the same door, and I stood there in the lamplight hating that sentence for the truth of it.

The Guild plaque says *rare acquisitions*. The ledgers say otherwise. And now one ledger, in my own handwriting, in ink older than my grandfather, was telling me that somewhere there was a door whose provenance was a place that did not appear anywhere in the records of the world—or at least nowhere I kept books, and I kept the only books that mattered, which is a thought that had comforted me for eleven years and was beginning, that night, to feel like the arrogance of a man who has mistaken his cellar for the world.

* * *

I did not sleep. I want that recorded plainly, without romance: I sat at my desk until the candles burned down to their collars, and then I lit others, and I read Entry 447 perhaps a hundred times, and each time I read it, the dread in my chest changed shape slightly, the way a stone changes shape in a stream. First it was fear of forgery. Then fear of madness—of discovering that my own hand moved at night, that there was a Jonah Eastwick who wrote entries while the waking one slept, and that the two of us were not keeping the same books. And finally, worst, it settled into something quieter and more patient: the fear of a man who realizes the cage he lives in has a door in it, and the door is unlocked, and he cannot remember building the cage, and the lock is on the *outside*.

Near dawn the rain eased to the fine, exhausted drizzle that Greyharrow uses to apologize, and I heard the bell above the harbor gate ring once—wrong, half-strangled, the way it rings when someone grabs the rope rather than pulls it. A hand on the rope. A hand that then fell, or slid, or was too weak to hold.

I am the gatekeeper. Whatever comes to that gate at any hour is mine to meet. That is in my contract, written in my hand, dated eleven years ago.

I took the lamp, because habit is a kind of armor, and I went down the wet stairs to the gatehouse door, and I already knew—I cannot tell you how, except that ledger-keepers develop an ear for imbalance, for accounts that don't sum—that whatever rung that bell was going to unbalance everything I had spent my life making sum.

Through the iron grille, in the grey almost-light, someone was slumped against the gate. I could see blood, black in the drizzle, mixing with the rainwater in the gutters and running toward the sea the way everything in Greyharrow eventually runs toward the sea.

"Guild business," the shape at the gate whispered, and the voice was wrong—not sick, worse than sick, *thin*, like a voice heard through a wall, like a voice from a country away. "I need... the keeper of the ledger. Only him."

I am invisible. I am meticulous. I am content. Those three sentences have kept me alive and safe for eleven years, and I recited them to myself as I drew back the bolts, one by one, each scrape of iron louder than it has ever been.

The gate swung inward. The stranger fell toward me, and pressed something folded and cracked and old into my hands with the last strength he had, and said four words before the saying of anything at all left him forever:

"Ashvale remembers you, Eastwick."
