## 2. A Stranger at the Gate

Midnight in Greyharrow smells like three things: fish that have given up on their ambitions, tar, and the particular wet-wool misery of a city that has made peace with its rain. I know this because I have spent eleven years cataloguing what the night smells like, the way other men catalogue coins or sins. It is a small habit, and habits are the only luxury a gatekeeper can afford.

The Ashvale Guild's gatehouse is not a grand structure. People imagine the Guild—when they permit themselves to imagine it at all—as brass and marble, as counting houses with ceilings painted by men who were paid in borrowed years. And the counting house *is* all of that. But the gatehouse where I keep my vigil is a squat stone box with a stove that smokes when it sulks, a desk scarred by two centuries of clerks sharpening knives they shouldn't have been carrying, and one window that looks out on the cobbled lane between the harbor wall and our iron gates.

I was balancing the third ledger of the evening—the Ledger of Small Impossibilities, which sounds like poetry and is actually arithmetic with delusions of grandeur—when I heard the footsteps.

I should explain something about my hearing. When you spend your nights listening for the difference between a drunk singing to himself and a drunk singing to someone who isn't there, you develop an ear for wrongness. These footsteps were wrong in three ways. First, they staggered, but not like drink; they staggered like a man whose legs had received contradictory instructions from a body already negotiating its surrender. Second, they stopped twice, and both times the silence afterward had weight, the silence of a man leaning against a wall to argue himself into continuing. Third—and this is the part I still think about—they came from the direction of the sea.

Nobody comes from the sea at midnight. The tide was out. The flats were mud and old bones of boats. There is nothing in that direction except the drowned chapel, and nobody has gone to the drowned chapel in living memory, because until recently there was no reason to remember it existed.

I put down my pen. This, in itself, was unprecedented. In eleven years I have never once abandoned a ledger mid-entry. A ledger left open is a door left open, and I am, professionally speaking, a man who keeps doors shut.

The knock, when it came, was less a knock than a collapse against wood arranged vertically.

"Guild business," I called, in the flat voice I reserve for petitioners, smugglers, and the occasional lost nobleman who thinks impossible things can be purchased with charm. "Hours are dawn to dusk. Come back when the sun does."

"Please." The word came through the door like water through a cracked hull. "Please, keeper. I know what you are."

*What* I am, not *who*. That distinction matters. Nobody knows who Jonah Eastwick is. That is the entire architecture of my life: I am the man behind the ledger, the shadow behind the shadow, the clerk so thoroughly invisible that the Guild's own enforcers walk past me in corridors without their eyes catching. Corvane Hale, Guildmaster of the most powerful institution in Greyharrow, once introduced me to a foreign delegate as "our door," which I chose to take as a compliment.

But *what* I am—a keeper, a ledger-man, a holder of thresholds—that word, spoken through four inches of oak by a stranger bleeding on my step, landed somewhere beneath my ribs and stayed there.

I opened the door. I want it noted, for whatever record survives me, that every instinct I possess said *don't*, and I opened it anyway. Eleven years of careful invisibility, undone by the word *please* delivered in a voice like a man reading his own last will and testament.

He fell forward into the gatehouse before I could catch him, though I tried, and got a sleeve full of blood for my trouble. He was perhaps fifty, dressed in clothes that had once been fine and had since been through a war, a storm, and a determined attempt at disguise—in that order, if I read the salt stains right. His coat was grey wool, good weave, dyed over. Underneath, his shirt was soaked through from collar to hem, and not with seawater. Seawater, I'd have smelled.

"You're bleeding out on the doorstep of the most dangerous building in Greyharrow," I told him, easing him onto the bench by the stove. "There are perhaps six people in this city who would consider that a good idea, and five of them are dead."

"Six," he agreed, and laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing brought up red. "Then I'm the sixth. Good. Good that it's me."

"That's a strange thing to be glad about."

"It's a strange night to be alive in." He gripped my wrist with a hand cold as a ledger key. His eyes were the problem. Everything else about him I could file, categorize, reduce to entries: male, fiftyish, wounded, gentry-born or pretending, pursued. But his eyes looked at me the way a man looks at a country he thought had burned down and finds still standing. "You're Eastwick's boy."

"I'm the gatekeeper."

"You're Eastwick's boy," he repeated, with the stubbornness of the dying, "and you don't even know what that means yet. That's—" another laugh, wetter, worse, "—that's the whole joke, isn't it. They wiped us clean and left the *names*. Left the names sitting in ledgers like seeds in a drawer."

My father had been a Guild man. Dead fourteen years, a fall from the counting-house roof that everyone called an accident and nobody investigated, because investigating things is not what the Ashvale Guild pays for. I had never heard anyone say *Eastwick* aloud in connection with him. The name sat in my chest like a swallowed coal.

"Who are you?" I asked. "Who sent you?"

"No one sent me. That's rather the point." He fumbled inside his coat, and I confess my hand went to the knife under my desk—not to use, just to touch, the way a nervous man touches a talisman. What he drew out was not a weapon. It was paper, folded into eighths, soft at the creases from much handling, spotted dark with his blood where his fingers had pressed. "I carried this across the water. Do you know how far I carried it? You wouldn't. No one would. Distance doesn't work right, coming from—"

He stopped. Closed his mouth. Tried again, and I watched a man fight his own memory and lose ground.

"Coming from where?" I asked.

And here is the thing I will carry to whatever grave is allotted to me: I saw him remember, and then I saw him *not* remember, and the space between those two states lasted maybe two seconds and contained more horror than anything I have witnessed since. His face opened—there was a place, a green place, I could see it arrive behind his eyes like weather—and then it closed, smoothly, completely, the way a ledger closes when you press the cover down, and what remained was a man who knew he had forgotten something enormous and could no longer even grieve it properly, because grief requires a shape and the shape had been taken.

"There was—" he said. "There was a country. There was a—" His hand shook. "It's gone. It goes away while I'm holding it. Like water. Like—"

"Salt," I said, without knowing why.

His head snapped up. For a moment the look in his eyes was so fierce, so *alive*, that I forgot he was dying. "Say that again."

"Salt," I said. "You smell of it. Not the sea. Salt itself, like—"

"Like the flats after the tide," he whispered. "Like the chapel. Oh, you *do* remember. Some corner of you remembers, they couldn't get all of it, they never get all of it, that's why the bargain is failing—" He seized my hand and pressed the folded paper into it, folding my fingers closed over it with both of his own, and his grip had the desperate strength of a drowning man transferring his hold. "Take it. Take it and don't give it to Hale. Whatever he offers you, whatever he threatens—it's a map, keeper. It's the way back."

"The way back to where?"

"To Ashvale." He said the name and the stove flame guttered, and the lamp dimmed, and somewhere out in the lane a dog began to howl in a register I felt in my teeth. "Ashvale remembers you, Jonah Eastwick. It kept a room for you. It kept your mother's garden. It kept the bell you used to ring when you wanted supper. It has been keeping everything, all these years, holding it all in the dark, waiting for someone to come home—and the ones who sold it are afraid now, do you understand? The bargain is failing. The erasure is finishing. When it finishes, everyone who ever touched the name forgets themselves. Not the country. *Themselves.*"

I want to be able to report that I reacted with dignity. That I said something measured and professional, something a gatekeeper would say. What I actually said was:

"My mother didn't have a garden."

"She did," he said gently. "She grew white flowers along the south wall. You picked them for her until you were seven, and then you were taken across the water, and she stood at the gate and waved until you couldn't see her anymore, and then—" the terrible smoothness again, the closing, "—and then. You see? Even I'm losing it now. Even telling you, I lose it faster. That's how it works. Speaking it spends it."

The lamp steadied. The dog stopped. My hands, I noticed distantly, were shaking around the folded paper, and I could feel through the cloth the shape of it—lines, borders, a coastline I almost recognized.

"Who are you?" I asked him again, because it seemed important, suddenly, desperately important, to attach a name to this. "Your name. Give me your name and I'll enter it in the ledger. Anything entered in the Guild's books stays written. It's the one law even Corvane Hale honors."

Something moved across his face—gratitude, or amusement, or both wearing each other's coats. "A ledger," he murmured. "Of course. Of course you'd offer that. Write this, then: *a friend of the house, come home late.*"

"That's not a name."

"It's the truest one I have left." He sagged back against the wall. The color had left him entirely now; he was going grey the way the sea goes grey before weather. "Listen. Listen, there isn't time, and there's too much of it, both at once. The lock under the chapel wants blood and memory. Yours will open it—or half of it. Half is enough to start. Don't trust the Guild. Don't trust the Bureau. Trust—" he frowned, groping, "—trust the one who asks questions she already knows the answers to. She's coming. She's already on the water. She'll be insufferable. Let her."

"Who—"

"And keeper—" He caught my sleeve. His strength was going out of him like heat from a stone, but his eyes still held mine, and in them was the green place again, arriving and departing, arriving and departing, a country surfacing and diving. "When you see it—when you finally see it—don't look away. Everyone else did. Everyone else looked away, and that's half of why it worked. Promise me you won't look away."

"I promise," I said, because there was no other answer, and because I already suspected, with the cold certainty of a man watching arithmetic resolve, that I was going to break every promise I'd ever made before this was finished, and I might as well begin with a worthwhile one.

He smiled. "Good lad. Eastwick's boy." And he closed his eyes as if to sleep, which, in fairness, he did.

I knelt beside him to check his breathing and found none. I did the things one does—I pressed fingers to his throat, held a lamp near his lips, spoke his non-name aloud as though the ledger law might reach him retroactively. Nothing. The stove smoked. The rain continued its long argument with the roof. And I, Jonah Eastwick, invisible man, sat on the floor of my gatehouse next to a dead stranger, holding a map to a country that did not exist, feeling the first crack run up through the foundations of a life I had built very carefully out of not-being-noticed.

Then the body came apart.

I want to describe this accurately, because accuracy is the last professional virtue I have left. There was no violence in it. No light, no smoke, none of the theater that attends magic in the stories the harbormaster tells drunk. He simply became less solid, the way a sandcastle becomes less solid when the tide reaches it—not collapsing, exactly, but *relinquishing*. His outline softened. His hands went first, then the line of his jaw, and where he had been there was water—seawater, unmistakably, smelling of kelp and deep cold places—spreading across my flagstones, pooling against my boots, running in thin bright threads toward the drain.

In thirty seconds there was no man. In forty-five there was no stain. In a minute there was nothing in my gatehouse but me, a smoking stove, a lamp, and a folded piece of paper in my hand that was still warm, and still spotted with his blood, and still real.

I sat there for a long time. Long enough that the stove gave up sulking and died. Long enough that the rain changed its mind twice.

Here is what frightens me, and I set it down honestly: it is not the dissolving. Dissolving, I could file. Impossible things are literally my trade; the Guild ships bottled echoes to clients in Vell and rents out borrowed years to anxious heiresses. A man turning to seawater is merely an entry awaiting classification.

What frightens me is that when he said *your mother's garden*, some part of me—some locked cellar-deep part of me that I have never once audited—did not think *he's lying*. It thought *oh. There it is.*

White flowers. South wall. I can almost—

No. Almost is how they get you. Almost is the crack in the dam. I have spent eleven years being no one, and it has been the safest, quietest, smallest life imaginable, and I want it back. I want it back so badly my teeth ache.

I stood, crossed to my desk, and took out the false ledger—the one I keep bound in plain calfskin between two genuine volumes, the one with forty blank pages that have waited eleven years for a purpose. I unfolded the map halfway, enough to see the top edge: a coastline drawn in faded ink, and along it, in a cramped antique hand, a single word I did not need anyone to tell me how to read.

*Ashvale.*

Outside, far off toward the harbor, a ship's bell rang once—though no ship was due, and the fog was too thick for any sane pilot—and I had the distinct, unwelcome sensation of a door opening somewhere that I could not see, and of everything I had carefully shut now straining against its hinges.

I hid the map. I banked the stove. And I began, with hands that would not entirely steady, to write the entry:

*A friend of the house, come home late.*

The ink dried darker than it should have. I decided not to think about that either.

Tomorrow, Corvane Hale would summon me—I knew it the way one knows weather in one's knees—and he would ask gentle questions in his gentle voice, and I would have to lie to the Guildmaster of the Ashvale Guild for the first time in eleven years.

I found, examining myself in the dark window glass, that I was not afraid of that.

I was afraid of how much I was looking forward to it.
