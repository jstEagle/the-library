## 3. What the Guild Forbids

The rain had stopped by the time I climbed back to my rooms above the counting house, but Greyharrow's streets kept dripping anyway, the way this city does everything—reluctantly, and with an air of having been asked twice.

I carried the map the way you carry a stolen thing, which is to say badly. I'd wrapped it in oilcloth and shoved it inside my coat, where it sat against my ribs like a second heartbeat, and every step from the gatehouse to the stairs I was certain that everyone who passed me could hear it. The fishwife with her barrow. The lamplighter making his rounds at dawn. A cat on a windowsill gave me a look of such profound feline judgment that I nearly confessed on the spot.

Cats are the Bureau's finest recruits, I've always suspected.

My rooms were exactly as I'd left them: narrow, tidy, smelling of lamp oil and old paper. A bed I made each morning with hospital corners. A shelf of ledgers arranged by year, their spines flush as teeth. Eleven years in this guild, and I had built myself a life so orderly it could have been mistaken for a coffin with better lighting.

I locked the door. Then I locked it again, because the first lock felt optimistic.

* * *

Here is what you must understand about the Ashvale Guild, before you judge me too harshly for what I did next—and believe me, I judged myself plenty; I've always been my own most diligent auditor.

We trade in impossible things. Bottled echoes. Borrowed years. Luck by the dram. Grief, weighed and sold to collectors who find authentic sorrow fashionable. Every transaction is recorded in ledgers so complete that the Guild's own historians have been known to weep with gratitude, or possibly indigestion. And every ledger has a rule older than any of its keepers:

**We record what passes through our doors. We do not ask what stands behind them.**

It's a good rule. It's the reason the Guild has survived plagues, purges, three wars, and one particularly ugly incident involving a cardinal and a caged thunderstorm. We don't look behind doors. We keep the doors shut, we keep the books balanced, and in exchange, nobody comes asking us questions we can't survive answering.

So when a dying man pressed a map into my hands and whispered that *Ashvale remembers me*—when his body dissolved into salt water on my threshold like he'd never existed at all—I did the only thing my eleven years of training prepared me for.

I said nothing. And then I hid the evidence.

* * *

The false ledger lived on the third shelf, between the year I took over the night gate and the year Corvane Hale became Guildmaster. It looked, from the outside, like any other volume: leather gone soft at the corners, gilt lettering worn to a ghost. Inside, it held eleven years of entries so mundane they were practically a lullaby. Deliveries of candle-wax. Receipts for bottled fog. One memorable week when someone tried to sell us a haunted hat and I rejected it on grounds of insufficient documentation.

I pried up the false bottom—a trick my father taught me, though I've never let myself wonder why a ledger-keeper needs a false bottom in his own ledger—and laid the map inside.

It was still damp. Salt water had gotten into the folds somehow, though the stranger had been dry when he reached the gate, or near enough. The paper smelled of brine and something else, something green, like a hillside after rain. Like a place I had never been and yet, when I closed my eyes for half a second too long, could almost name.

I pressed the map flat. I closed the ledger. I put it back on the shelf, spine flush with its neighbors, and stepped away.

Then I stepped back and moved it two inches to the left, because if I were searching these shelves, I'd check the volumes nearest the gap first, and—

And there it was. That feeling again. The one from the gatehouse, when I'd read the entry that appeared in my own hand without my ever writing it: *One (1) door out of Ashvale.* The dread had a twin now, and its twin was exhilaration, and the two of them were going to be very poor roommates.

Because here is the thing about guilt and exhilaration: they feel identical in the chest. Same tightness. Same heat. For years I assumed that flutter under my sternum whenever I balanced a difficult column was pride in good work. Now I wondered if it had been something else all along—the part of me that had been waiting, patient as a spider, for a door to rattle in its frame.

I went down to make tea. My hands shook. I noted it, because noting things is what I do, and filed the observation under *pending.*

* * *

Corvane Hale summoned me at noon.

Not sent for—summoned. There's a difference in the Guild, and the difference is a bell. The counting house bell rang once, low and bronze, the tone reserved for matters the Guildmaster prefers not to put in writing, which in my experience meant either money or trouble, and since the Guild's money was the one thing in Greyharrow more boring than my ledgers, I assumed trouble.

His office occupied the top floor of the counting house, a room designed to make visitors feel like overdue accounts. Dark paneling. A desk of black oak older than the city charter. Windows that caught the harbor light and turned it the color of old brass. Corvane himself sat behind the desk with his hands folded, silver-haired, immaculate, wearing the expression of a man who has already forgiven you and is merely waiting for you to earn it.

"Jonah." He said my name the way he said everything—warmly, and with weight, as though words were coins he counted out carefully. "Sit."

I sat. The chair across from his desk had been chosen, I was certain, by someone with a grudge against spines.

"You worked the night gate last night."

"I did."

"A stranger came to you."

Here it was, then. Faster than I'd expected. Some traitor part of me—the part that had spent eleven years reading other people's tells—registered that my own pulse had jumped, and I made myself breathe the way I'd been taught: slow, even, boring.

"A drunk," I said. "He stumbled against the gate, mumbled something, moved on."

Corvane studied me. He had a way of studying people that made you feel itemized. "Moved on."

"Toward the harbor. They usually do."

"And he said nothing to you? Asked nothing?"

"He asked if I had the time." I shrugged, a masterpiece of indifference. "I told him midnight. He seemed disappointed."

Something flickered at the corner of Corvane's mouth—not quite a smile, but the memory of one. "You're a careful man, Jonah Eastwick. It's why I trust you with the ledgers. Careful men don't invent details." He leaned back. "Which makes it interesting that the enforcers found salt water on the gatehouse stones this morning. A great deal of it. In a pattern."

"In a pattern," I repeated.

"Like a man had stood there. And then, perhaps, hadn't." His eyes stayed pleasant. His voice stayed warm. None of it reached anywhere near his actual attention, which was fixed on me with the patience of a fisherman watching a line. "The Guild has rules about unrecorded arrivals, Jonah. You know them better than anyone. If something came to our gate last night—something that left no entry, no trace in your book—that would be an irregularity. And irregularities are rather my department."

"The tide reaches the gate at high water," I said. "Everyone knows that."

"Everyone knows that," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "And yet."

*And yet.* Two words, and they hung in the air between us like a blade deciding which way to fall. I sat in my uncomfortable chair and performed the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since dawn: how much did he know, how much did he suspect, and how much was theater? Because Corvane Hale did not summon ledger-keepers over salt water. Whatever had brought that dying man to my gate, whatever had written itself into my ledger in my own hand, it had rattled the Guildmaster enough that he wanted to look me in the eye while he asked.

And I—fool, coward, keeper of doors—found that some stubborn thing in my chest had already decided.

"I'll re-check the night ledger," I offered. "If anything came through, it would be recorded."

"It would be," Corvane said softly. "That's rather the point of you."

He let the silence sit. He was good at silence, Corvane. He'd had decades of practice making other people fill it. I watched him watch me, and I thought about the map beneath the false bottom two floors below, and I thought about a dead man's voice saying *Ashvale remembers me*, and I understood—with the strange clarity that arrives only when you're doing the worst thing you've done in years—that I was about to lie to the Guildmaster of the Ashvale Guild, in his own office, about a matter he clearly considered vital.

Eleven years without a single false entry. Eleven years of being the man whose numbers could be trusted absolutely, whose word was a fixture of the building, load-bearing. Men like me don't get to be invisible forever, I suppose. Eventually the invisibility becomes a kind of currency, and someone comes to spend it.

"Nothing came through the gate last night," I said.

My voice didn't shake. I remember being surprised by that. It came out level, almost bored, the voice of a man confirming a delivery of candle-wax, and I hated how good it sounded. Lying, it turns out, is a skill, and skills are just things you haven't practiced yet.

Corvane regarded me for a long moment. Then he smiled, and it was the warmest, most terrible thing I saw all day.

"Of course not," he said. "Silly of me to ask. You'd have recorded it." He rose, and I rose with him, and he crossed the room to clasp my shoulder with a hand that weighed more than it should have. "You know, Jonah, when your father held your post, he asked me a question once. Just one, near the end. He asked me what was behind the sealed door beneath the counting house." His grip tightened, friendly as a vice. "I told him the truth: that a good keeper doesn't need to know. He accepted that. I've always hoped you take after him."

"I balance the books," I said. "That's all I've ever done."

"Yes," said Corvane Hale. "Let's hope it stays that way."

* * *

I walked back down four flights of stairs with my heart going like a drum in a parade, and I want to be honest about what I felt, because honesty is the one luxury I apparently couldn't afford anywhere else that day.

I felt guilty. Of course I felt guilty. Guilt sat in my stomach like swallowed ballast, cold and permanent, and every stair seemed to echo with my father's name, which Corvane had produced from wherever men like him keep such things—some drawer of levers, labeled and oiled.

But underneath the guilt, braided through it so tightly I couldn't tell which strand was which, ran something bright and disgraceful and alive. Exhilaration. For eleven years I had been a fixture, a function, a well-kept column of figures, and in the space of one morning I had become a man with a secret—a man the Guildmaster himself had come to probe, a man worth lying to. The cage I'd built out of duty had never once felt like a cage until I heard the lock click from the outside.

I reached my rooms. I checked the false ledger. The map lay where I'd hidden it, damp and green-smelling and impossible, and I stood there holding it and understood that something had ended. The man who had walked up those stairs yesterday no longer existed, dissolved as thoroughly as the stranger on my doorstep, and whatever remained was going to be considerably more trouble.

I put the ledger back. Spine flush. Two inches left of center.

And that was when I noticed, pinned to the inside of my door with a plain iron nail, a card of heavy cream stock that had certainly not been there when I left for the counting house that morning. No seal. No signature. Just a single line in a small, precise hand:

*Bureau of Forgotten Places. Customs inspection, tomorrow, nine o'clock. Do be home.*

I read it three times. Then I did the only reasonable thing.

I made tea, sat down at my desk, opened a fresh ledger page, and began practicing the sentence *nothing came through the gate* until it stopped sounding like a lie.

Somewhere below, the counting house bell rang once—low, bronze, and, I could have sworn, amused.
