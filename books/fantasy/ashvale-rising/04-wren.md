## 4. Wren

The Crown's Bureau of Forgotten Places does not appear on any organizational chart of His Majesty's government, which is the first thing you should know about it and the second thing its employees are trained to deny. I have held my commission for six years, and in that time I have told customs officers, harbor masters, two archbishops, and one very persistent landlady that I work for the Office of Weights and Measures. It is a good lie because it bores people. Boredom is a door, and doors are my trade.

My name is Wren Calloway, and I hunt countries that do not exist.

That sentence sounds mad when said aloud, so I never say it aloud. I write it in reports sealed with black wax and delivered by courier to a building in Callowmere whose address changes whenever anyone tries to memorize it. The Bureau's mandate is simple: somewhere between three and seven times a century, something large gets subtracted from the world. Not destroyed—subtracted. A village here, an island there, once an entire duchy with eleven thousand souls and a celebrated cheese. The records go blank. The maps redraw themselves. The survivors—if there are survivors—wake up with new histories stitched into them so neatly you'd never find the seam unless you went looking with a lamp and a grudge.

I go looking with both.

* * *

Greyharrow greeted me with rain, which felt appropriate. Cities that trade in impossible things should at least have the decency to look guilty.

I came off the ferry from Sallow's Reach with one trunk, two forged letters of introduction, and a notebook in which I had copied out—by hand, twice, because I no longer trusted any archive not to eat its own contents—the sum total of what was known about Ashvale. Which was nothing. That was the point. That was the whole maddening, magnificent point.

Here is how you prove a country existed after every proof has been removed: you look for the holes. Erasure is not clean. It is a tide going out over sand, and tides leave marks. A road that ends in a hedge for no reason. A family name that appears in a parish register and then simply stops, mid-generation, as though the children had been born of air. Songs. Songs are the worst-erased things in the world, because songs live in mouths, and mouths are not archives. You can burn a library; you cannot burn a lullaby without burning the grandmother.

My grandmother sang me a lullaby about brass bells.

She died when I was nine, and I carried the tune for twenty years like a stone in my shoe before I finally sat down one winter and actually listened to the words. *Ring the bells of Ashvale green, where the river runs between.* Except there is no Ashvale. There has never been an Ashvale. I checked. I checked atlases, tax rolls, shipping manifests, gravestones, the heraldic rolls, the minutes of four parliaments. I checked in three languages and one dead script. No Ashvale. And yet the song had a place-name in it the way a coat has buttons, worn smooth with use, sung to babies who grew up and sang it to their babies, all across the western coast, all singing about a country that the world had agreed, unanimously and without discussion, did not exist.

Unanimous agreement is the loudest silence there is. I built a career on listening to it.

So: Greyharrow. Port city, fog-prone, prosperous in the way of places that don't ask too many questions about cargo. My sources—a retired excise man in Sallow's Reach who drank too much and remembered too well, and a smuggler who owed me a favor she deeply regretted—had both pointed me here independently. Something was stirring under Greyharrow. People were dreaming of hills they'd never seen. An old woman on Rope Walk had begun setting a second place at her table and weeping without knowing why. And the Ashvale Guild, richest and quietest institution on the coast, had recently doubled the guard on its counting house.

Guilds guard their vaults. They do not double their guards over dreams. Unless the dreams are coming from inside the vault.

* * *

The Bureau's method, when investigating an institution, is to walk in the front door carrying paperwork. Nobody guards against paperwork. Paperwork is the sheep's clothing of the intelligence profession.

My forged credentials identified me as Inspector Vess of the Office of Weights and Measures, conducting a routine audit of imported measures and standards. It is astonishing what a person can learn by asking merchants whether their bushels are honest. Everyone relaxes around weights and measures. Everyone, sooner or later, wants to complain about someone else's short measure, and complaints are just gossip wearing a uniform.

The Ashvale Guild's counting house stood on Ledger Square like a man pretending not to watch a fight. Grey granite, narrow windows, a door of black oak banded with iron that had gone green at the rivets. Above the lintel, carved in letters worn nearly smooth: *WE KEEP WHAT IS ENTRUSTED.* Guilds always carve promises over their doors. In my experience the larger the lettering, the smaller the truth.

Inside smelled of ink, candlewax, and cold stone. A clerk with spectacles and the posture of a folded umbrella took my letters, read them with agonizing slowness, and disappeared for the better part of a quarter hour—which told me he wasn't fetching approval, he was fetching instructions—and returned to lead me through corridors of shelved ledgers into a long room where the counting was done.

And there he was.

I knew him at once, though I'd never seen him, because I had done my homework and because some people are simply legible if you know how to read them. Jonah Eastwick, gatekeeper and ledger-keeper, per my notes: thirty-some years, unremarkable face, the kind of man whose job description is to be overlooked. He sat at a desk near the great iron-banded door at the room's far end, and he was doing sums, and he looked up when I entered with exactly the flat, polite attention of a man who expected to be bored.

Good, I thought. Bored men tell you everything.

"Inspector Vess," I said, presenting papers. "Weights and Measures. Routine audit. Your guild imports bottled goods—echoes, years, that sort of thing—and the Crown requires verification of standard volumes."

If you have never watched a professional liar decide whether you are one too, you have missed one of the finer spectator sports available to our species. What happened behind his eyes took perhaps a second and a half. First: the flicker—*bottled echoes, she knows the inventory*. Second: the calculation—*is this real or is this a test*. Third: the decision, which was neither yes nor no but a third thing, a door opened exactly one inch.

"Of course," he said. "We're always happy to cooperate with the Crown." And he rose, and pulled out a chair for me himself, and I noted that he placed it where he could see my hands and I could see his, which is either courtesy or mutual disarmament, depending on how paranoid you are.

I am quite paranoid.

* * *

We spent two hours on the world's most tedious audit. He produced inventories. I measured bottles. There were, genuinely, bottled echoes—little stoppered vials that hummed faintly when you held them to your ear, full of other people's conversations, other rooms' silences. There were borrowed years in wax-sealed jars, grayish and slow, like honey that had given up. I recorded volumes. I made small talk. I complained about the weather, the ferry, the state of the roads, and the price of everything, in that order, because complaint is a crowbar and everyone eventually takes hold of the other end.

He gave me nothing. He gave me nothing so smoothly, so completely, that it became its own kind of information. Men with clear consciences fidget. Men hiding something specific dodge in specific directions. This man simply... was not there. Every question landed on him like a coin dropped down a well, and I began to understand that I was not talking to a clerk. I was talking to a wall that had learned manners.

So I changed tactics. Walls are built to withstand pressure. They are not built to withstand precision.

"The guild handles unusual cargoes," I said, closing my ledger with a snap. "Which means unusual provenance. Tell me, Master Eastwick—where does one source a borrowed year? Is it like wine? Terroir? Do certain regions produce better ones?"

"A question for our factors," he said.

"Naturally. And your factors report to?"

"The Guildmaster."

"And the Guildmaster reports to?"

A pause. Half a heartbeat, no more. But I had been waiting for half a heartbeats all afternoon, and I caught it the way you catch a fish that has barely touched the fly.

"The Guildmaster," he said, "answers to the Guild."

"To the Guild," I repeated pleasantly. "Not to the Crown. Interesting. Most chartered institutions answer upward. Yours answers... inward?" I smiled. "You must forgive me. Weights and measures is a dull discipline, but it teaches you one thing above all others: everything must balance. Something comes in, something goes out. So I find myself wondering, Master Eastwick—your guild trades in echoes, years, memories, if rumor serves. What does Ashvale take in return?"

And there it was.

Not a flinch. Worse than a flinch. A stillness—the stillness of a man who has heard his own name spoken in a language he thought dead. Because I had said *Ashvale*. Not *the Ashvale Guild*. Ashvale. The country. The word itself, naked, out loud, in a room where I would have wagered my commission it hadn't been spoken in a hundred years.

He recovered beautifully. I want to give him that. Another half-second and he'd have been flawless. But I had already seen it, and he knew I'd seen it, and something passed between us across that desk that had nothing to do with bushels or bottled echoes—the cold, electric recognition of two professionals acknowledging each other's craft. He knew I knew. I knew he knew I knew. Any further pretense of boredom was now a costume we were both politely agreeing to keep wearing.

"Rumor," he said mildly, "serves very few useful purposes, Inspector."

"On the contrary," I said, standing, gathering my papers. "Rumor is the only history most people ever get. The official kind has a way of developing gaps." I let that sit for exactly as long as it needed to, then extended my hand. "Thank you for your cooperation. The Crown appreciates the guild's transparency."

He shook my hand. His grip was dry and careful, and he held it a fraction longer than the transaction required—not flirtation, not challenge. Message. *I know why you're here,* the extra half-second said. *And I did not give you away.*

Neither did I. There was a moment—I'll admit it—when I could have pressed. When I could have set my forged credentials on his desk and said, *shall we stop pretending?* Six years of instinct said the wall had a door in it and I had just found the seam. But walls with doors usually have men behind them, and men behind doors usually have orders, and I wanted this man curious, not cornered. Cornered men call for help. Curious men come looking.

I left my hook in the water. "If any irregularities occur during your ordinary business," I said at the threshold, "irregularities of record, of memory, of... provenance—the Office of Weights and Measures maintains a discreet channel for such reports. Ask for Vess."

"Irregularities," he repeated, in the tone of a man accepting a menu he did not intend to order from.

"We get them everywhere," I said. "Even here. Especially here, I suspect. Good day, Master Eastwick."

* * *

Outside, the rain had settled in for the evening, and Greyharrow's lamps were coming up along the quay in smudges of orange. I walked back toward my lodging the long way, past the harbor, and let myself think about what I'd actually learned, which was more than the audit showed and less than I wanted.

One: the word Ashvale was live ammunition in that building. Two: Eastwick was not merely staff—he was the lock the staff turned around, the man positioned between the guild's secrets and the world, and he had chosen, in the space of a handshake, not to feed me to whoever those clerks scurried off to consult. Three—and this was the part I kept turning over like a coin—he had been afraid. Not of me. I'm good at my work, but I am not frightening. He'd been afraid of the question itself, of the shape of it, as though I'd asked him something he had privately asked himself for years and never dared say aloud.

*What does Ashvale take in return?*

Everyone assumes erasure is something done to a place. Standing in that counting house, I'd had the opposite thought, sudden and cold: what if it was something the place agreed to? What if somewhere under that grey granite was a contract, and the contract was failing, and the man keeping the books had found an entry in his own hand that he never wrote?

I stopped at the rail overlooking the tidal flats below the harbor wall. The tide was going out, dragging the water off the mudflats in long silver sheets, and there—just visible in the last light, a dark tooth of masonry maybe half a mile offshore—stood the ruin the locals called the drowned chapel. My smuggler friend had mentioned it in passing, with a shrug: old church, swallows at high tide, nothing in it, been picked clean for a century.

Nothing in it. Mm. In my experience, "picked clean" is a phrase used exclusively by people who didn't know what they were looking at.

I wrote in my notebook by lamplight, sheltering under the eave of a chandlery:

*Eastwick, J. Gatekeeper/ledger-keeper. Competent liar—better than guild average, worse than he thinks. Reacted to "Ashvale" (the name, not the guild) with recognition + fear + suppression. Did not report me (probably). Knows something is wrong with the books. Hypothesis: he found the hole before I did.*

Then, underneath, because I am honest with myself even when it stings:

*Grandmother's song. Brass bells. If Ashvale is real, and if it was erased, and if the erasure is fraying—then the lullabies were right and every archive in the Empire is wrong, and I need to know which side of that line I'm standing on before someone makes me choose.*

I closed the book. Across the flats, the drowned chapel stood black against the dying light, patient as a held breath.

Behind me, on the wet cobbles, I heard footsteps stop. Not following me—waiting. I turned, hand already finding the little knife in my sleeve, and saw a guild clerk holding an oilcloth-wrapped parcel against the rain, a young man with rain running off his nose.

"Begging your pardon, miss—are you Inspector Vess? Only—" he glanced over his shoulder, lowered his voice. "Only Master Eastwick said to give you this, and to say there's been an irregularity. Of record." He thrust the parcel at me. "And he said—his words—to tell you the entry's dated tonight, and it's not in his hand."

I took the parcel. Inside, I already knew, would be paper. Possibly a map. Possibly the first true thing anyone in this city had handed me.

"Well," I said to the boy, to the rain, to the drowned chapel standing its long vigil in the darkening sea, "now we're getting somewhere."
