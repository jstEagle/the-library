## 5. Salt and Ink

There are three ways to follow a Bureau investigator through a rain-soaked port city without being seen, and I knew none of them, which is why I spent two hours getting soaked to the bone trailing Wren Calloway from the customs house to a chandlery to a tavern called the Drowned Rat—which, I will note for the record, was an insult to rats, who at least have the decency to drown quietly.

She moved like someone who had been followed before and had opinions about it. She doubled back twice. She stopped once in the mouth of an alley and stood so still that a fishmonger tried to sell her a haddock. And when she finally settled into a corner table at the Rat with her back to the wall and both exits in view, I understood that if she hadn't already spotted me, it was because she was letting me believe I hadn't been spotted.

I went in anyway. Call it curiosity. Call it the map folded against my ribs, warm as a second heartbeat. Call it eleven years of keeping doors shut finally curdling into something with teeth.

"You're better at this than most," she said without looking up from her cup. "Most people give up after the first doubling-back."

"I wasn't giving up. I was pacing myself."

That earned me a glance. Grey eyes, the color of a harbor before a storm decides whether to be polite about it. "Jonah Eastwick. Ledger-keeper. Gatekeeper." She said the titles the way a surgeon says *tumor*. "Sit down, Master Eastwick. You're dripping on the floor, and the floor here has enough problems."

I sat. The tavern keeper brought me something brown and hot that claimed to be tea the way a mule claims to be a racehorse. Wren waited until he'd gone, then leaned forward, and her voice dropped into a register I suspected she used on smugglers and worse.

"Here's what I know. Three days ago, a man died on your gatehouse step. He didn't leave a body. He left salt water and—" she tapped the table once— "something else. Something you didn't report. Which is interesting, because by every account I've gathered, Jonah Eastwick reports everything. Eleven years of flawless ledgers. A man who files complaints about his own complaints."

"Someone's been talking about me."

"Ledger-keepers are talked about. It's part of the charm of being invisible—you'd be amazed how much people say in front of furniture." She turned her cup a quarter-rotation, precise as a lock mechanism. "So either you've changed, or the dead man changed you. I'm betting on the second, because I found salt water on your threshold too, and salt water doesn't walk uphill on its own."

The map pressed against my ribs. I thought about Corvane Hale's gentle voice asking about irregularities, and the lie I'd told him sitting in my chest like a swallowed hook. I thought about how many ways this could go wrong, and arrived at the honest arithmetic: all of them.

"You're Bureau of Forgotten Places," I said. "You investigate things that don't exist. That must keep you busy."

"It keeps me underpaid." No smile. But something behind her eyes shifted—a door opening one inch onto a lit room. "My grandmother used to sing me a lullaby about a country of brass bells. When I grew up and looked for it—not the country, just the song—I couldn't find it. Not in any archive, any songbook, any grandmother. Other people's grandmothers sang other songs. Mine swore she'd never sung anything but sea shanties in her life." Her jaw tightened. "Somebody reached backward through time and stole a lullaby out of a dead woman's mouth, Master Eastwick. I don't care about your guild's ledgers. I care about that."

And there it was—the thing I'd been circling since the stranger died on my step. Because I had dreamed of brass bells my whole life. I had woken as a child crying for a home I couldn't name, and my father had held me and said *just dreams, Jonah, just dreams*, and his voice had cracked in a way that dreams don't crack voices.

"The dead man," I heard myself say, "said Ashvale remembers me."

Wren went very still. Stillness, I would learn, was her version of shouting.

* * *

We didn't talk further in the tavern. Too many ears, and hers were the least of them—since the stranger's death I'd caught the smell of the Guild's enforcers on the wind twice, that particular scent of oiled leather and old incense they wore like cologne. So we walked, separately, to the one place in Greyharrow where nobody watches anybody: the public baths off Tanner's Row, where steam makes everyone equally blind and equally naked and equally unwilling to discuss business.

We sat in the far corner of the cold plunge pool like two conspirators in a painting, and she unwrapped oilcloth from around a rectangle of vellum, and I took the folded map from inside my shirt, and we laid them side by side on the wet stone between us.

Hers was older. The vellum had gone the color of weak tea, and the ink had faded to ghost-gray, but the hand that drew it was confident—coastlines, soundings, a chapel marked with a careful cross. Mine was fresher, cruder, drawn in the dying stranger's shaking hand, but the lines—

The lines matched.

Where her map ended at the drowned chapel off Greyharrow's coast, mine began. Where mine showed a door—a door, drawn in obsessive detail, every hinge and rivet rendered like a portrait of a loved one—hers showed the approach: the tidal flats, the channel, the hours when the sea pulled back far enough to let the desperate through.

"A door out of Ashvale," Wren said quietly. "Under the chapel."

"Or a door into it. Depends which side you're standing on." I traced the stranger's inked hinges. "He died getting this to me. Whatever's behind it, he thought I was the one meant to open it."

"Why you?"

"I've been asking myself that. My best answer is that I'm nobody. Gatekeepers are chosen for forgettability. If the Guild ever needed to hide something in plain sight, they'd hide it in me."

"That's not an answer. That's a job description." She looked up, and the steam curled between us. "Ask the real question, Eastwick. Why does a door need a key that remembers?"

I didn't have an answer. But touching the map, I'd felt something—glimpses, half-formed. Green hills. Bells. A woman's voice calling a name that might have been mine. I kept that to myself, not out of strategy but out of something rawer. Some things you don't hand to a stranger in a bathhouse, even a stranger whose lullaby matches your dreams.

"So," she said. "The Guild wants whatever you're carrying. The Bureau wants me to file a report saying there's nothing here, which is what the Bureau always wants, because the Bureau's founding charter is a list of things the Crown prefers not to exist. And you want—" she studied me— "I genuinely don't know what you want. That's rare. Most people wear their wanting where I can read it."

"I want the ledgers to balance," I said. "That's the truth. All my life, everything's balanced. Entries in, entries out. And three days ago somebody wrote a line in my book that I never wrote, and now nothing balances, and I can't sleep, and I keep hearing bells." I heard my own voice do something it hadn't done in eleven years: shake. "I want to know why."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. Steam drifted. Somewhere a bath attendant argued with a plumber about the economics of hot water.

"All right," she said finally. "Here's my offer, and I'll only make it once. Alliance. Your access to the Guild's records, my access to the Bureau's. We find the chapel, we find the door, and we find out what happened to a country that was erased so thoroughly that even its lullabies were murdered." She extended her hand across the wet stone. "But no more lies, Eastwick. Not even small ones. Especially not small ones. Small lies are how big ones get in."

I looked at her hand. I thought about the false ledger hidden beneath my floorboards, and the lie I'd told Corvane Hale, and the omission I was committing right now by not mentioning either.

"I work alone," I said, which was a lie, because I had never worked at anything but alone, and alone was starting to feel less like armor and more like a cell. "But I'll work with you. Conditionally."

"Conditionally."

"I don't trust the Bureau. I don't trust the Guild. I trust ledgers, and you haven't shown me yours yet."

Something flickered across her face—annoyance, maybe, or respect, or the specific expression of a person recognizing their own disease in someone else. "Fair," she said. "I'll show you mine when you show me yours. Literally. Bring the false ledger tomorrow night. There's a place I know."

"And until then?"

"Until then, we pretend this conversation never happened. You go back to counting impossible things, and I go back to being a customs inspector nobody likes." She rose, water sheeting off her, and paused. "Eastwick. The bells you hear. What do they sound like?"

"Nineteen strikes," I said, before I could stop myself. "Always nineteen. Then silence, like the bell is waiting for something."

Wren's face did something complicated. "My grandmother's lullaby," she said, "had nineteen notes. My mother proved it once, marking them off. Swore she'd counted them a hundred times." She wrapped her oilcloth around nothing now, the maps back in our separate keeping, hers against her heart, mine against mine. "Get some sleep, ledger-keeper. Tomorrow we go looking for a chapel that the sea has been trying to forget for a hundred years."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I went home through streets running with rain, past the counting house with its windows dark, past the gatehouse where a stain of salt still ghosted the stones no matter how often I scrubbed it. In my quarters I pried up the floorboard and took out the false ledger, and by candlelight I did the thing I do best, the thing I am for: I made entries.

*Item: one map, acquired from a dead man. Provenance unknown.*
*Item: one alliance, conditional, with an agent of the Crown. Risk: total.*
*Item: nineteen bells, heard nightly since childhood. Previously filed under dreams. Reclassified.*

My pen hovered over the page. This was the moment, I understood dimly, where a life turns—not with trumpets, but with a line of ink in a book only you will ever read. Everything before this entry, I had been the Guild's man. Everything after—

After, I wrote: *Item: one question, unanswered. Why does a door need a key that remembers?*

And beneath it, in smaller script, because the candle was guttering and because some truths only get written when the light is failing:

*Note: her lullaby had nineteen notes.*

I blew out the candle and lay in the dark listening to the rain, and somewhere beyond the harbor, beyond the drowned chapel and the tidal flats and a hundred years of deliberate forgetting, I would have sworn I heard them. Nineteen strikes. Then silence.

Waiting.

Tomorrow, I thought. Tomorrow we go and ask the sea what it's been keeping.

And far off, faint as memory, the bells began again—as if they'd heard me, and were glad.
