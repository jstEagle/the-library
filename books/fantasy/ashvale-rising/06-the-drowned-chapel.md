## 6. The Drowned Chapel

The tide table said we had four hours. The sky said we had less.

I'd spent eleven years trusting ledgers over weather, and I trusted the tide table anyway, because a man has to believe in something, and arithmetic was the only thing that had never lied to me. But the clouds coming off the Greyharrow deep were the color of wet slate, and the wind had that particular salt-and-iron smell that old fishermen cross themselves against. Wren stood at the top of the tidal stairs with her hood up and her map half in one gloved hand, looking like a woman who had already decided the sea was going to disappoint her.

"You're smiling," she said.

"I'm not."

"Your mouth is doing the thing it does when you're about to be insufferable about numbers." She nodded at the flats below us, where the retreating water had peeled back to expose a causeway of black stone that no chart of Greyharrow had ever recorded. "That wasn't there yesterday."

"No," I agreed. "It was there eleven years ago, though. And probably two hundred years before that. It only shows at the lowest tides of the season, which is why nobody remembers it. People don't remember what they only see once a year. Memory needs repetition the way a fire needs feeding." I paused, hearing myself. "That's in the Guild's first-year primer. Door maintenance, chapter three."

"How comforting that your order of impossible things also does upkeep."

"Somebody has to sweep the thresholds. Miracles collect dust like anything else."

We went down. The causeway was slick with weed and older than anything else in Greyharrow except perhaps the harbor wall, and even the harbor wall had the decency to appear in municipal records. This stone appeared nowhere. My boots knew its rhythm anyway—left, right, skip, left—and by the third hundred paces I stopped being surprised that my feet remembered a road my mind had never walked. That was becoming the theme of my month.

Wren kept pace beside me without talking, which I had learned was her highest form of approval. When she did speak, it was low, almost lost under the wind.

"When we find it—the door. What do you think happens if you touch it?"

"I think nothing happens. Locks are locks. I've opened ten thousand of them with a key and none of them with a feeling."

"And if it's not that kind of lock?"

I didn't answer, because I'd read the stranger's map until the ink had worn soft under my thumb, and there was a line of script along the chapel's threshold on the parchment that I could now recite in my sleep: *By blood it knows you; by memory it keeps you.* The Guild primer had a chapter on that too. I'd always assumed it was allegorical, the way most of the primer was. Lately I'd been forced to consider how much of my professional education was actually a warning label.

* * *

The drowned chapel sat in the shallows like a ribcage picked clean, its spire snapped off a century or five ago, its walls furred with barnacles down to the waterline. At high tide the sea filled it like a cup. At low tide it drained through gaps in the floor, sighing, leaving pools in the hollows between the flagstones that caught the grey light and held it.

Inside, the air smelled of cold stone and old incense gone to salt. There were no pews. There was no altar. There was a floor of pale stone worn into a shallow bowl by centuries of water, and in the center of the bowl, set flush with the stone so you could have walked across it without noticing, was a door.

Not a doorframe. Not an archway. A door—a full door, oak and iron, lying flat as if the chapel had been built around a doorway someone had laid down to sleep. It was maybe eight feet long, banded in iron gone green-black, and carved across its face in letters that were neither quite runes nor quite script was the same line from the map. The wood was dry. Everything else in that chapel was drowning, and the door was dry, and that single wrong fact made the hair on my arms stand up like a startled cat's.

"Well," Wren said quietly. "That's not on any Bureau schematic."

"Bureau of Forgotten Places doesn't have schematics of things that refuse to be forgotten. This is the opposite. This is a thing that was made to be forgotten and is doing a very poor job of it." I crouched at the edge of the door, close enough to feel it—not warmth exactly, but the absence of cold, the way standing near another person makes the air feel occupied. "There's no keyhole."

"There's a seam." She knelt opposite me, tracing the line where the two halves would part. Her glove whispered over iron. "And look—" she pulled the glove off, bare fingers hovering, "—the carvings. They're worn everywhere except here. Like people touched this spot for years. Hundreds of years of hands."

"People don't touch a locked door for hundreds of years unless they're hoping."

"Hoping it opens?"

"Hoping it remembers them." I heard my own voice go strange. "Sorry. I don't know why I said that."

"Yes you do."

I did. That was the problem with Wren Calloway. She treated every lie I told myself as a clerical error, and she filed them all for later review.

She looked up at me across the door, rain beginning to tick against the broken roof above us, running down the walls in bright threads. "Jonah. If this thing is keyed to blood and memory, then touching it isn't opening it. Touching it is answering a question. And I don't think either of us knows yet what question."

"The map says blood and memory."

"The map says a lot of things. Maps are written by survivors." She flexed her bare hand. "I'll do it."

"You will not."

"It's my investigation—"

"It's my door." The words came out of me flat and certain, and they surprised both of us. I stared at the oak and iron and heard myself continue, calm as a man reading inventory: "Eleven years I've kept the Guild's doors. Every lock in the counting house answers to me. If something in this world is going to ask whether I belong to it, it can bloody well ask me first."

For a moment she just looked at me, rain dripping off her hood, and whatever she saw in my face made her sit back on her heels and give me the room. "All right," she said. "But I'm right here. Whatever it shows you—it lies, you pull back. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

It was the easiest promise I broke in eleven years, and I broke it within the minute.

* * *

I put my palm flat on the iron band.

Nothing happened, for the length of one heartbeat, which was long enough for me to feel foolish and start composing the wry remark I would make about allegorical locks. Then the door remembered me.

It didn't open. It *remembered*. The sensation came up through my arm like cold water rising in a well, and it carried images the way floodwater carries fences and furniture and whole lives—

Green hills. Not Greyharrow hills, which are bald and sheep-nibbled and apologetic. Hills so green they seemed lit from underneath, terraced with orchards, stitched with white paths. A city in a valley bowl with a river through it, and the city was full of bells—brass bells on every tower, brass bells strung across the streets on wires like laundry lines of sound, and when the wind came off the mountains all of them moved at once and the whole city rang like a struck glass. I knew the name of that sound. Every child in that city knew it. It was called the Morning Answer, and it happened at dawn, and you were supposed to stand still for it, and I never once managed to stand still because my friend Tam was always daring me to race him to the bellwright's stairs before the last echo died—

A kitchen. Low ceiling, copper pots, a woman with flour on her forearms singing something with a chorus I could almost reach. My mother. Not dead, not a story—a woman with flour on her forearms who sang off-key on purpose because my father hated it and secretly loved it. A man at the table with a ledger open in front of him, and he was teaching me entries, small careful figures, and he looked up at me across two hundred years and a drowned chapel and said, in a voice I had been dreaming my whole life without knowing it was a memory:

*"Numbers don't forget, Jonah. That's their mercy. That's their cruelty too. One day you'll have to choose which."*

I knew his hands. Gods help me, I knew his hands. I had spent my life believing I'd learned my meticulousness from some anonymous Guild master, some institution, some tradition. It was his. It was my father's handwriting living in mine, and I had been signing his name for eleven years without knowing it.

The bells again. The hills. A door in a hillside with a brass handle worn bright by generations of palms, and me, small, standing before it, and my father behind me saying, *"Remember this one, Jonah. Whatever they tell you after. Remember this one."*

And then—white. A tearing, like a page ripped out. The smell of burning ink. Hands that weren't hands closing over the whole country like a book snapping shut, and a voice, vast and tired and formal, reciting terms, and somewhere inside the recitation my own name being folded away like a letter into a drawer—

I came back to myself on my knees in six inches of seawater with Wren's hands gripping my shoulders and her voice saying my name in the tone people use for the nearly drowned.

"—Jonah. Jonah Eastwick. Look at me. You're in Greyharrow. You're on the coast. Breathe."

I breathed. It hurt, the way breathing hurts after crying, though I hadn't cried—I was almost sure I hadn't cried, except my face was wet and the rain hadn't started hard enough to account for it.

"The bells," I said. My voice came out sanded raw. "There were bells everywhere. Brass. They strung them across the streets. And the hills were—" I made a gesture that meant nothing and everything. "Green. Green like the color was invented there first and everywhere else got a copy."

Wren went very still. "Jonah. What did you see?"

"My childhood." I laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "My actual childhood. In Ashvale. I thought—" I pressed the heels of my hands against my eyes. "All my life I've had these dreams. A city that rings. A woman singing in a kitchen. I thought everyone had dreams like that. I thought they were just—the noise a mind makes. And they weren't dreams. They were *leaks*. Somebody erased a country out of my head and did such a thorough job that what was left over pooled in my sleep and I called it imagination."

Wren crouched in front of me, and I will say this for her: she didn't offer comfort first. She offered witness. "Tell me the rest."

So I told her. The kitchen. The flour. The man with the ledger—my father, though saying the word aloud felt like handling something hot. The door in the hillside. The instruction to remember. And the tearing at the end, the white, the formal tired voice reading terms.

When I finished, the rain had thickened, drumming on the broken roof, pooling in the chapel's stone bowl around the dry, patient door.

"Do you know what the worst part is?" I said. "It's not that they took it. It's that I was *content*. Eleven years, Wren. Balancing ledgers, keeping doors shut, invisible, safe, and content—and the whole time I was a man humming songs from a country that had been amputated out of my memory, grieving something I didn't know I'd lost. Grief needs a shape. Mine had been taken away and replaced with satisfaction." I looked at the door. "They didn't just steal Ashvale. They stole my mourning."

Wren was quiet a moment. Then, softly: "My grandmother used to sing a lullaby in a language no one could identify. Scholars, linguists—nothing matched. She said her own mother called it 'the old country's tongue,' and when my grandmother asked which old country, her mother would just stop talking and go stand by the window." She met my eyes. "Every record in the Bureau says no such country exists. But lullabies don't check archives, Jonah. They pass under the erasure, mother to daughter, below the watermark. Your bells survived in your sleep. My grandmother's song survived in hers. Whatever bargain buried this place, it couldn't get everything. Some things were written too deep."

"That's not comforting. That's horrifying. It means it's still in everyone. Sleeping. Under everything."

"It means," Wren said, "that it can be woken."

The word landed between us and neither of us picked it up for a moment. Rain. Sea-sigh through the chapel's wounds. The door lay between us, dry and silent, having shown me everything and given back nothing but ache.

I reached toward it again—couldn't help it—and this time I only laid two fingertips against the iron, light as checking whether a kettle had cooled. The door didn't flood me this time. It did something smaller and worse. Under my fingers, faint as a pulse through a wrist, I felt a rhythm. Slow. Patient. Counting.

"What is it?" Wren asked.

"It's counting," I said. "Like a ledger. Like something keeping time on a balance that's slowly going wrong." I pulled my hand back. "Wren, the entry I found in the Guild book—the one I never wrote, the door out of Ashvale—it wasn't a record of the past. Ledgers don't record the past. They record *state*. Debts outstanding. That entry is a live figure. Something is being counted down, or counted out, and this door is the account it's kept in."

Wren looked at the door for a long moment, and I watched her arrive at the same arithmetic I had, because her face did the thing it did when numbers turned ugly.

"If the balance is failing," she said slowly, "then someone on the other side of this door is keeping books too. And they've just felt your fingerprints on the lock."

Above us, thunder rolled in off the deep, and beneath it—so faint I might have invented it, so faint I would spend the next day failing to convince myself I had—something on the far side of the drowned chapel's impossible door began, very softly, to ring.

Brass bells. The Morning Answer. Out of season, out of hour, out of world.

Answering.
