## 7. Enforcers at the Counting House

I have always believed that a man's rooms tell the truth about him faster than his mouth ever could. Mine, for eleven years, had told a story of deliberate dullness: a narrow bed made with hospital corners, a shelf of ledgers arranged by year, a washstand, a single chair, a window overlooking the counting house yard where nothing interesting had ever happened. I had curated that room the way other men curate their reputations—carefully, and with an eye toward boredom.

So when I came up the back stairs that evening and found three of the Guild's enforcers turning my life inside out with the delicacy of men gutting fish, my first feeling was not fear. It was something closer to professional offense.

They had started with the bed. Of course they had. The mattress stood on its side against the wall, its ticking opened along one seam, straw strewn across the floorboards like the aftermath of a very small, very tidy riot. Brannock—the big one, the one with hands like ham hocks and a face like a closed ledger—had my washbasin in his grip and was peering into it as though the basin might be hiding secrets from him personally.

"Evening, Eastwick," he said, without turning around.

"Brannock." I set down my lantern. "You've missed a seam. Under the chair cushion. That's where everyone hides things."

He looked at me. The other two—Vess, small and quick, with eyes like a ferret doing arithmetic, and a new one whose name I didn't know and whose face suggested he'd been hired for his willingness to break things rather than his talent for finding them—looked at me too.

"You're not surprised," Vess said.

"I'm surprised you started with the mattress," I said. "It's the first place anyone looks. If I were hiding something, it would be somewhere clever."

"And if you're not hiding anything?"

"Then you're wasting a perfectly good evening, and so am I."

It was a good line. I want that noted for posterity, because it was also a lie delivered with a steadiness that would have made my younger self proud and my current self sick. Somewhere beneath the third floorboard from the window—the loose one, the one I'd pried up two nights ago with a chisel borrowed and never returned—lay a false ledger containing a folded map of a country that did not exist. And I stood there in my doorway making jokes about chair cushions while three men dismantled the only home I'd ever had.

The new one pulled my shelf of ledgers down and began rifling through them, checking dates against dates, looking for the gap. He was methodical. I hated him immediately and respected him almost as much.

"They'll be wanting you downstairs," Brannock said. "Master Hale's study. Now-ish."

"Now-ish," I repeated. "The Guild's summons vocabulary has really deteriorated."

"Eastwick." Brannock set the basin down. For a moment something that was almost kindness crossed his heavy face. "Don't make this harder than it is."

"It's already as hard as it is," I said. "That's what hard means."

* * *

Corvane Hale's study occupied the top floor of the counting house, and it was the only room in the building with a carpet thick enough to swallow footsteps. I had been summoned there perhaps six times in eleven years, and each time I had climbed those stairs rehearsing honesty the way an actor rehearses lines. Honesty was my armor. It was the whole architecture of my position: Jonah Eastwick, who kept the ledgers, who kept the doors, who kept his mouth shut, and who therefore could be trusted with everything because he wanted nothing.

I climbed the stairs rehearsing a lie instead, and the difference sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

The study was warm. A fire burned in the grate despite the season, and Corvane stood before it with his back to me, hands clasped behind him, watching flames do whatever it is flames do when no one honest is watching. He was not a tall man, but he had the particular stillness of someone who had never once needed to hurry, and his voice, when it came, was the voice of a man reading aloud from a book he had written himself.

"Jonah. Sit."

I sat. The chair was deep and hostile to straight spines. I suspected this was intentional.

"Do you know how old the Guild is?" he asked, still facing the fire.

"Four hundred and twelve years," I said. "Founded by nine partners under charter of—"

"Founded by nine partners," Corvane said, "under a bargain. Not a charter. The charter is the costume. The bargain is the body underneath." He turned. His face in the firelight was older than I remembered it being, and I realized with a small cold shock that I had been seeing him through the flattering lens of habit. "You balance our ledgers, Jonah. You know every entry. Bottled echoes. Borrowed years. The small impossibilities we trade in. Have you never wondered why the Guild deals in years? Why longevity is our oldest and most reliable commodity?"

"I assumed we bought low and sold high, like everything else."

"We don't sell years, Jonah. We keep them." He came and stood over me, and I made myself look up at him. "Nine founders. Nine men who looked at death the way a merchant looks at a bad quarter's accounts—as a problem to be solved, a loss to be avoided. And they found a way. Do you understand? They found a way to simply... decline it. To keep their ledgers open indefinitely."

Something moved in my memory. The portrait gallery on the second floor, the nine stern faces I passed every day, painted in styles from four different centuries. I had always assumed the Guild commissioned copies. New frames, old faces, tradition maintained.

"The founders are alive," I said.

"Seven of them," Corvane said. "Two grew tired. Tiredness is permitted. Death is not." He smiled, and there was no warmth in it at all. "But such a purchase does not come cheap, and it does not come from any merchant we could outbid. It came from a country."

The fire popped. Somewhere below us, in the counting house, a clerk laughed at something, and the sound floated up through the floorboards like a bubble through dark water.

"Ashvale," I said.

I hadn't meant to say it. The name came out of me the way blood comes from a cut—because the pressure was already there, because something had opened it. Corvane went very still, and in that stillness I understood that I had just told him something true about myself that I had not known until I said it.

"So you have heard the name," he said softly. "Interesting. We paid for our immortality with a country, Jonah. All of it. Its borders, its bells, its kings and its kitchen maids, its hills and its harbors. We gave it over entire, and in exchange the world forgot it had ever existed—and the seven of us forgot how to die." He crouched then, bringing his face level with mine, and his eyes were the pale grey of paper left too long in the sun. "Every man in this building walks past that gallery every day and never wonders why the founders' portraits span four hundred years. Every clerk trades in impossible things and never asks where the first impossibility was purchased. You want to know what the Guild is, Jonah? The Guild is the interest payments on Ashvale."

"The entry in my ledger," I said. My voice sounded far away to me. "A door out of Ashvale. I didn't write it."

"No," Corvane agreed. "You didn't. Which means either the ledger wrote itself—which has happened before, in the early years, and never meant anything good—or the bargain is coming due." He rose, crossed to his desk, and lifted a slim volume I recognized: the master ledger, the one I was never permitted to touch, the one whose entries appeared in no hand I could identify. "Look."

He turned it toward me. The page was blank except for a single line, written in ink that seemed to darken even as I watched:

*The door stands. The debt wakes.*

"That line appeared nine days ago," Corvane said. "The same night, I am told, that a stranger came to your gate bleeding salt water. The same night you reported nothing." He closed the ledger gently, the way one closes a coffin. "I'm going to ask you a question, Jonah, and I want you to consider very carefully whether the answer serves you. Where is the map?"

And here is the thing I will carry to whatever grave I eventually earn: some part of me, in that moment, wanted to hand it over. Eleven years of belonging pressed down on me like a hand on the back of my neck. Corvane had given me a place when I had none. He had taught me the ledgers, trusted me with the doors, let me become invisible in exactly the way I had needed to be invisible. Loyalty is not a thought; it is a weight, and mine had been accumulating for a decade.

But underneath the weight, another voice—the one that had spoken Ashvale's name unbidden—asked a simple question: *belonging to what?*

To a guild of gravediggers. To seven old men who had bought forever with a country and called it commerce. To a bargain that had erased lullabies, harbors, harvests, names—erased them so thoroughly that the erasure felt like the natural shape of the world. I thought of the stranger dying on my threshold, dissolving into salt water, spending his last breath to put a folded map into the hands of a gatekeeper. Of all the doors in all the world, that dying man had chosen mine. Perhaps coincidence. Perhaps the country remembering. But standing in that warm room with its hostile chair and its coffin-quiet ledger, I found I could no longer tell the difference between the Guild's trust and the Guild's leash.

"I don't have a map," I said.

Corvane watched me for a long moment. Outside, rain had begun again, ticking against the study windows like impatient fingers.

"Eleven years," he said at last, almost gently. "Eleven years without a single irregularity. Do you know why I chose you for the ledgers, Jonah? Your father served the Guild before you. A fine man. Careful. He asked questions too, near the end. About Ashvale." Something flickered across his face—grief, or its well-preserved imitation. "We retired him. You were nineteen. You thought he abandoned you. He thought he was protecting you. Both of you were wrong in ways that took years to arrange."

The floor tilted. Or I did. My father had walked out one autumn morning with a single bag and no explanation, and I had built my entire adult self in the negative space of that departure. *Careful, quiet, unquestioning.* Everything he wasn't, I had decided to be.

"He's dead," I said. It wasn't a question. Corvane's silence answered it anyway.

"The bargain requires maintenance, Jonah. Memories decay. The erasure must be renewed, patched, reinforced. Your father helped with the patching, and then he stopped helping, and men who stop helping—" Corvane spread his hands, an accountant's gesture, a shrug of pure arithmetic. "The point is this. The map cannot be allowed to exist. Whatever the stranger gave you, whatever the ledger is trying to tell us, the answer is the same as it has been for four hundred years: the door stays shut, the country stays forgotten, and the world stays safe. Because that is the other half of the bargain, Jonah, the part the founders negotiated hardest for. What sleeps behind Ashvale's erasure stays asleep. Open that door, and you don't free a country. You free what the country was used to bury."

"What was it?"

"That is above your station."

"Then give me a reason below my station."

Corvane smiled again, and this time there was something almost fond in it, which was somehow worse. "Safety," he said. "Every soul in Greyharrow, asleep tonight behind their doors, alive because seven old men were greedy enough to buy forever and careful enough to pay in a currency no one would miss. One country against the world, Jonah. That's not villainy. That's arithmetic." He returned to his desk and sat, folding his hands, and just like that he was the Guildmaster again, patient and reasonable and immovable as a wall of figures. "Surrender the map within three days, and this conversation becomes a misunderstanding. Keep it, and I will be required to treat you as an irregularity. You've seen what we do with irregularities. You file them."

I stood. My legs held, which surprised me.

"Three days," I said.

"Three days. And Jonah—" he picked up his pen, already dismissing me, already returning to the endless calm arithmetic of his existence, "—the floorboard by the window. Third one. You should know we found it Tuesday. The map we found tonight, in Vess's boot, where I instructed him to plant it during the search you watched so calmly. Yours is a copy now. A good one—I had it made from the original, which means I know precisely what you know, which means I know precisely where you'll go." He glanced up, and his pale eyes were terrible in their mildness. "Run if you like. But understand that from this moment, every door in Greyharrow opens for me and shuts for you. Even yours."

I walked out. Down the stairs, past the gallery where the seven undying founders watched from their four centuries of frames, past clerks who nodded to me as though it were any ordinary night, out into the rain-slicked yard where the world smelled of salt and tar and forgetting.

My quarters, when I returned to them, had been put back in perfect order. The mattress resewn. The ledgers reshelved. The floorboard nailed down tight—except that when I knelt and pressed the third board from the window, it lifted easily, silently, oiled for my convenience.

Inside lay the forged map, neat and mocking, drawn in a hand almost exactly like mine.

Almost.

Because whoever had copied it had made one error, small and human and beautiful: they had reproduced every line of the drowned coast, every depth-marking, every tide-note—except the last annotation in the margin, the one in my father's handwriting that I had found only that afternoon, the one that read, in a language I shouldn't have understood but did:

*What was bound can be bound again — but only by two who remember across the water.*

They hadn't copied it because they couldn't see it. The ink was visible only to someone the erasure had failed to reach completely. Only to someone born of Ashvale.

Only to me.

I sat back on my heels in my carefully rebuilt room, in my carefully rebuilt life, and I began, very quietly, to laugh—because Corvane Hale had just handed his enemy the one proof that mattered. They had taken the map and given me back a forgery, which meant the original was real, which meant the stranger was real, which meant Ashvale was real, and my father's hand was real, and the door was real.

Loyalty, I discovered, doesn't shatter. It curdles. It sits in you, souring by degrees, until one day you look down and find you've been carrying something else entirely.

Three days, he'd said.

I had a city to cross, a chapel to find, and a truth-seeker named Wren Calloway to convince that the deadline had just moved up.
