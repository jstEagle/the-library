## 8. The Bargain's Fine Print

My mother had three rules about books, and she broke all of them the day she died.

Rule one: never write in a book. Rule two: if you must write in a book, use pencil. Rule three: never, under any circumstances, encode anything important in marginalia, because marginalia is where important things go to be forgotten. She said this while writing something important in the margin of a book, in ink, which tells you everything you need to know about my mother and her relationship with her own rules.

The book was *A Natural History of Vanished Nations*, third edition, water-stained, purchased for four coppers from a stall that smelled of fish and regret. I'd carried it through six postings with the Bureau of Forgotten Places because it was the only thing of hers I hadn't burned, buried, or been forced to surrender. I'd read it cover to cover twice. I'd read the margins a dozen times more.

I'd never read them properly.

That's the thing about grief: it makes you a coward in specific directions. I could recite her lullabies—the ones about a country with brass bells that I'd always assumed were invented, because grandmothers invent things—but I couldn't look too hard at her handwriting without feeling like someone had opened a door in my chest onto a cold room. So for eleven years I'd skimmed her notes the way you skim a scar. Quickly. Sideways. With your breath held.

Last night, in a rented room above a chandler's shop in Greyharrow, with rain doing its best impression of a siege against the window, I finally held my breath long enough.

*The erasure is not destruction,* she had written, in the margin beside a chapter on the Principality of Vell, which also no longer existed and whose absence nobody mourned because nobody remembered it. *Destruction leaves ruins. Erasure leaves nothing—not even the knowledge that something is missing. Ashvale was not destroyed. It was filed.*

Filed. My mother, Crown investigator, mother of one inconvenient daughter, had used the word *filed* about an entire country, and then—because she was my mother—she had drawn a small, precise arrow pointing to the next line:

*A bargain has terms. Terms have fine print. Find the fine print and you find the seam.*

I sat on the bed with the book open on my knees and my heart doing something arrhythmic and unprofessional, and I turned pages with hands that wanted to shake and were forbidden to by standing orders I gave myself daily.

There was more. Of course there was more. She'd written in a cipher—a simple one, a substitution keyed to the first letters of the lullabies, which meant she'd hidden the key inside my childhood, inside songs she sang me while I fell asleep, which was either the most sentimental security system ever devised or the most ruthless, and knowing my mother it was both.

It took me until dawn to work through it. The candle guttered twice. The chandler's dog barked at the rain, at gulls, at the general injustice of existence. And I sat cross-legged like a child with a puzzle box, translating my dead mother's handwriting line by line, each decoded sentence landing somewhere between revelation and wound.

Here is what she knew. Here is what she died knowing, or possibly died because of knowing—I still haven't decided, and I suspect the deciding will matter later, when I have time for grief, which is to say never:

The erasure can be reversed. Not by force. Not by cleverness, though the Bureau would want it done cleverly, with warrants and formal language and a nice commemorative plaque. It can be reversed only if someone born of Ashvale opens the door back into it *willingly*—and here came the fine print, the clause my mother underlined twice and then boxed, as if boxing it could contain what it contained—

*willingly, knowing what it costs.*

She hadn't specified the cost. Either she didn't know, or she'd known and couldn't bring herself to write it down, or—and this possibility arrived around the fourth bell and stayed like an unwelcome guest—she'd known and had written it somewhere else, in some other book, in some other cipher, and I would find it only after it was already too late to matter.

Below the boxed clause, smaller, almost apologetic, she'd added:

*If you are reading this, Wren-love, then either I failed to keep you away from this work, or the work found you anyway. Both are likely. You have my stubbornness and your father's inability to leave a locked thing locked. Be careful who you tell. Be careful who you love. The ones who qualify are rarely free to pay.*

*The ones who qualify.* I'd read that phrase six times before its meaning finished assembling itself, and when it did, I put the book down very carefully on the blanket, stood up, walked to the window, watched the rain for a while, and thought about Jonah Eastwick.

* * *

Let me tell you how I interrogate people, since apparently we're being honest now, and honesty is a discipline like any other.

You don't ask about the thing. You ask about everything adjacent to the thing. You watch the hands, because trained liars control their faces and forget their fingers. You count the pauses—not the length of them, but where they fall. A man can lie fluently about his mother, his debts, his loyalties. What he cannot lie smoothly about is the shape of his own longing.

Jonah Eastwick lied to me beautifully, actually. That was the first thing that snagged. Customs inspection, my pretext, and he answered every question with the polished ease of a man who has been audited before—which, fair, he works for a guild that trades in bottled echoes, he's probably audited quarterly. But when I asked him, idly, testingly, whether he'd ever traveled, whether Greyharrow was home—he paused. Half a heartbeat. In the wrong place.

Not before the answer. Before the *word*. As if "home" required translation from a language he wasn't supposed to remember he spoke.

And his hands—this is the detail I keep returning to, the one I've turned over like a coin so many times the relief is wearing smooth—when I mentioned the drowned chapel off the coast, purely as local color, purely as gossip, his right hand moved. Just slightly. Toward his breast pocket. Where a ledger-keeper might keep a key, or a folded map, or a country.

So yes. When I decoded my mother's clause—*someone born of Ashvale, willingly, knowing the cost*—I did not need to wonder who qualified. I needed only to wonder how much it would cost him, and whether I could live with being the one who asked.

Because here's the arithmetic I couldn't escape, sitting in that grey morning light: I had spent two years chasing a rumor of a country, following a thread of erasure across half the continent, and every step of the way I'd told myself it was duty. Professional interest. The Bureau pays me to notice missing things. But the truth—the truth I'd been carrying the way you carry a stone in your boot, aware of it only when you stopped moving—was that the lullabies mattered to me. Grandmother's songs about brass bells and green hills and a river that ran backward on feast days. Songs nobody else's grandmother sang. Songs that shouldn't exist, sung by a woman who shouldn't know them, in a world that had supposedly forgotten.

Ashvale had leaked. Through songs, through dreams, through whatever channel grief and blood open in the walls of a bargain. And if it leaked into me—if some thin thread of it ran in my family like dye through cloth—then maybe, maybe, the door out of Ashvale wasn't sealed against everyone. Maybe the seal had hairline cracks, and people like my grandmother, people like me, were standing on them.

But cracks aren't doors. And I am not born of Ashvale. I checked. Believe me, I spent an ugly hour checking—going through my mother's papers, my grandmother's few surviving letters, looking for some hint that our family tree had roots on the wrong side of an erasure. Nothing. We were outsiders. Witnesses, perhaps. Kin to the leak, but not to the land.

Which left Jonah.

* * *

He came to the chandler's shop at midday, because I'd sent a note that said *the tide tables are wrong*—our code, established during exactly one previous secret meeting, which is not much of a code, but we were working with limited material and mutual distrust, which is how most codes start.

He looked terrible. Not dramatically terrible—no new bruises, no visible pursuit—but worn in the way of a man who hasn't slept and has spent the sleepless hours arguing with himself and losing. He sat across from me in the shop's back room among barrels of tallow and coils of rope, and he looked at my face and said, "You've found something."

"You look like you've found something too," I said. "Something with teeth."

"Corvane knows about the map." He said it flatly, the way you report weather. "Or suspects. He summoned me yesterday. Asked about irregularities. I lied to him." A pause, and then, quieter, with genuine wonder, as if reporting a medical symptom: "I lied to Corvane Hale. Eleven years I've kept his ledgers and I've never once—"

"Congratulations," I said. "First lies are like first falls from horses. The second dozen are easier."

"That's not comforting."

"It's not meant to be. It's meant to be true, which is rarer." I pulled the book from my satchel and set it on the barrel between us. "This belonged to my mother. She was Bureau, before me. She investigated the erasure twenty years ago and got further than anyone, and then she got careful, and then she got dead. Read the margins. The ones that look like nonsense. They're ciphered—keyed to the lullabies, if you must know, which you don't, but I'm telling you because apparently withholding things from you has gone badly for everyone lately."

He read. I watched him read, which is a vice of mine, and what I saw was this: confusion, then focus, then a stillness that spread over him like frost forming on a window. By the third page he wasn't breathing normally. By the fifth he had one hand flat on the barrel lid, pressing down, as if the book might otherwise float away.

"Someone born of Ashvale," he read aloud, barely voicing it. "*Willingly. Knowing what it costs.*" He looked up. His eyes were doing something complicated—hope and horror sharing a small space, elbowing each other. "Wren. Are you saying—"

"I'm saying the door beneath the chapel doesn't open for crowbars or warrants or cleverness. It opens for a native son who walks through it with his eyes open. And I'm saying—" and here my voice did something I hated, thinned and roughened, so I made it flat again, professional, bulletins and dispatches, "—that I checked my own family first. Thoroughly. I'm not from there. You are."

"How do you know I am?"

"Because of the pause before the word 'home.' Because of your hand going to your pocket when I mentioned the chapel. Because last night you told me, in the tone of a man describing a recurring dream, that you sometimes hear bells that aren't there. Brass bells, Jonah. Nineteen of them, you said. Who counts bells in a dream?"

"Everyone counts—" He stopped. Closed his mouth. Opened it again. "Nineteen," he said, differently now. Like a man tasting a word and finding it familiar. "The hour bells. They ring the hours in Ashvale, nineteen strokes at dusk, and the whole city stops to listen, and if you're a child you try to get all nineteen counted before they finish, and if you manage it you get—" He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "I get a honey cake. From the baker on Bellows Row. Her name was—" His hand trembled. "Her name was—"

"Gone," I said gently. "The names go first. That's how erasure works—it eats the proper nouns and leaves the grammar. You remember the cake and the street and the counting game, but not her. That's not madness, Jonah. That's filing."

He lowered his hand and looked at me, and I want to be honest about what that look did to me, because honesty is the discipline, even when it's unflattering. It undid me a little. There was a man across a barrel of tallow discovering that his entire interior life—every strange dream, every inexplicable homesickness, every night he'd woken at dusk hearing bells that Greyharrow's towers never rang—was not eccentricity but evidence. And instead of collapsing he went *still*, the way good investigators go still, the way my mother must have gone still, cataloguing the ruin of an assumption with steady hands.

"All right," he said. "Say I believe you. Say I'm born of Ashvale, whatever that means when the place doesn't exist. Your mother's note says *knowing what it costs*. Do you know? Does anyone know?"

"No."

"Then how can anyone consent?"

"That," I said, "is the question I've been chewing since dawn, and I'll thank you not to ask it in that reasonable tone, because I've been asking it in a much less reasonable tone all morning and it hasn't helped."

Something happened to his mouth then—not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the muscle memory of humor surfacing through dread. "You're offering me a door," he said, "with the price tag torn off."

"I'm offering you information. The decision is yours. That distinction matters to me." I took the book back, closed it, held it against my chest like a shield, which is exactly what it was. "I need you to understand something, though. Before you decide anything. I've spent two years on this. Two years chasing a country that officially does not exist, mocked in three bureaus, passed over twice for promotion, and I told myself it was duty because duty is respectable and obsession isn't. But it was never duty. My grandmother sang those lullabies. My mother died with this book in her effects and a hole in her investigation the size of a nation. This is personal for me, Jonah, and personal people make bad asks of other people. So I'm telling you plainly: I want that door open. I want it open so badly I can taste it. And the only person alive who can open it is sitting across from me smelling of lamp oil and guilt, and I need you to know that I hate this. I hate that the price lands on you. If there were another way, I'd have found it. I've looked. There isn't."

The silence afterward had texture. Rain on the roof. Tallow smell. Somewhere below us the chandler arguing cheerfully with a customer about wick quality, because the world is obscene like that; it goes on selling candles while you're dismantling someone's life.

Jonah looked at his hands for a long moment. Then he said, "When I touched the lock in the chapel—" and stopped, recalibrating. "I haven't told you what I saw."

"You saw Ashvale."

"I saw hills. Green, ridiculous hills, the kind of green you get in paintings to make people homesick for places they've never been. And a city with brass roofs, and bells, and a market where they sold—" his voice caught, steadied, "—bottled echoes. Real ones. Fresh ones. And I knew the streets. Wren, I *knew* them. Left at the fountain, past the fountain that isn't a fountain anymore because the city's gone, except it isn't gone, it's *filed*, and I stood in front of that lock and I grieved for a home I didn't know I'd lost, and it felt—" He shook his head. "It felt like coming home and being locked out at the same time. Which I suppose is precisely what it was."

"And?" I asked, because there was an *and*, I could hear it waiting behind his teeth.

"And part of me wants to open it so badly I can't think straight," he said quietly. "And part of me heard your mother's fine print and felt the trap in it. *Knowing what it costs.* Costs what, Wren? Everything? Who writes a bargain where the exit fee is everything? Who signs that?"

"Someone desperate," I said. "Or someone protecting something worse than the cost. Which brings me to the part I haven't said yet, and I need you to hear it before Corvane gets to you again, because he will, and men like Corvane Hale deliver bad news wrapped in comfort."

I leaned forward. Lowered my voice, though the chandler's argument had risen to a volume that would have covered a battle plan.

"My mother's notes end abruptly. Last entry, incomplete sentence, mid-thought. Investigators don't stop mid-sentence unless they're interrupted—or killed. And the last complete entry says the Guild's founders weren't just buyers in the original bargain. They were *draftsmen*. They wrote the terms. And my mother underlined one word in that entry, Jonah, and boxed it, the same way she boxed the cost clause, and the word was *reversible*—except she'd struck it through. Struck it through and written underneath, in a hand shakier than the rest:"

I recited it from memory, because I'd been reciting it since dawn, because some sentences you carry like a stone in your boot:

"*Unless the Guild would rather it stay shut. Ask why.*"

Jonah had gone very pale. "Ask why," he repeated. "Why would the Guild want the door to stay shut? They're the ones losing their immortality if the bargain fails. Corvane said—well. Corvane implied the erasure failing is their crisis."

"Yes. That's what he'd say. That's what a man says when the fine print favors him." I stood, slung my satchel, and looked down at this ledger-keeper who was, by blood and by clause, the only key in the world. "We need the original contract, Jonah. Not Corvane's version. The founders' draft, with every term intact—including the ones the Guild has spent generations making sure nobody reads. My mother died getting close to it. I intend to succeed where she—"

I didn't finish. Some sentences you don't finish out loud.

Jonah stood too, and for a moment we just looked at each other across the barrel, two people holding opposite ends of the same trap, and I saw him arrive at the same conclusion I had, the one neither of us would say yet because saying it would make it real:

Whatever the fine print cost, we were both already paying installments.

"The vault," he said at last. "Under the counting house. There's a door down there I've walked past for eleven years. It's always locked. It's the only entry in the ledgers I've never been allowed to read."

"Then tomorrow," I said, "you're going to show me the one door you were hired to keep shut."

Outside, the rain eased, and far off—impossibly, briefly, in a gap between Greyharrow's own dull bronze bells—I would have sworn I heard nineteen strokes, counting down to something.
