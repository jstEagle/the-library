## 9. Nineteen Bells

The enforcers came for us at the Saltmarket stairs, which was rude of them, because I had been having a genuinely pleasant evening.

Pleasant by my standards, anyway. Wren and I had spent two hours in the back room of a chandler's shop comparing our map halves by candlelight, and for two hours she had not accused me of anything. That is the closest thing to happiness I had known in eleven years of ledger-keeping.

Then a boy in Guild grey had looked at me through the window with eyes that were not a boy's eyes—flat as coins, patient as debt—and I had done the arithmetic every fugitive eventually does: *we are seen, we are slower than them, and the roofline is faster than the street.*

"Roofs," I said.

Wren didn't ask why. She was already moving. I have since learned that this is her most dangerous quality—not the Bureau training, not the questions like scalpels, but the fact that when I say *roofs* she simply goes, as though trusting me were the easiest thing in the world. It is not easy. I know exactly how much it costs her. She told me so herself, three days ago, in a voice like torn paper: "I don't trust you, Eastwick. I trust that you're bad at lying, which is nearly as useful."

So we went up. The chandler's roof first, then the rope-walk between warehouses, then the long slate spine of the tannery where the smell alone could stop pursuit—I hold no illusions about that; even enforcers have lungs. Behind us, boots on cobbles, four sets at least, moving with the unhurried rhythm of people who know the city will do their work for them. The Guild doesn't chase. The Guild waits. Greyharrow is a trap with streetlamps.

But Greyharrow has roofs too, and I grew up on these rooftops—or some version of me did, in a city I only remembered as dreams—and there is a particular knowledge a boy acquires scrambling over slates to escape his own thoughts, and it turns out that knowledge transfers across an erasure. My feet knew things my mind did not. Which tiles were loose. Which gutters would hold a man's weight. Where the gap between the tannery and the cooperage was four feet wide and crossable if you committed, and fatal if you hesitated.

"Eastwick." Wren's voice from behind, tight. "The gap."

"Commit," I said. "Don't look down. Look at me."

She looked at me. She jumped. And for one absurd half-second, hanging over four feet of empty air above a cooperage yard, with Guild enforcers closing below and the whole shape of my life collapsing around me, I thought: *she looked at me.*

Then we were both on the far side, breathless, and the moment was over, and we ran.

* * *

The bell tower of Saint Verity's-or-whoever-she-was rose out of the fog ahead of us like a stone finger raised in permanent admonition. Greyharrow has nineteen towers and I have climbed all of them in the course of my duties—the Guild insures bells, among its other impossible lines of business; there is a ledger entry for cracked bell-metal that I personally balanced in my third year—but this one, the old tower on Cindermount Lane, had been deconsecrated forty years ago and stood empty, its bell still hanging, its stairs still sound.

We went up through rot and pigeon filth, past the ringing chamber with its broken wheel, up into the belfry itself, where the great bell hung silent in the dark like a sleeping animal. Below us the fog swallowed the streets. Somewhere under it, lanterns moved. Patient. Unhurried.

"They'll wait us out," Wren said. She'd found the stairwell door and wedged it with a length of iron pipe from the ringing chamber. It wouldn't stop anyone. It would slow them enough to matter. "They have more night than we do."

"They have more everything than we do," I said. "That's rather their whole method."

She laughed. Short, unwilling, real. In the dark of the belfry, with the fog glowing faintly below us where the streetlamps drowned in it, her laugh sounded like something smuggled in from a better country.

We settled against the wall beneath the bell, shoulder to shoulder because the space demanded it and neither of us pretended otherwise. Her coat was soaked through from the fog. So was mine. The cold came up through the stones and sat with us like a third conspirator.

"You knew those roofs," she said after a while. Not a question. An observation, delivered the way she delivers all her observations—like a card laid face-up on a table.

"I knew them."

"You said 'commit' before I jumped. You knew the gap. You knew the tower was open." A pause. "You've never been up here, Jonah. I've read your file. Eleven years in the counting house. You don't climb."

"No," I agreed. "I don't climb."

"But your feet do."

And there it was, the question underneath the question, the one she'd been circling since the chapel, since the lock keyed to blood and memory, since I'd touched that threshold and seen green hills and brass bells and a woman's voice calling a name that might have been mine. She wanted to know what I'd seen. She'd earned the right to ask. She just hadn't yet earned the certainty that I'd answer.

I looked at the bell above us. Great dark thing, big as a whale's heart. In Ashvale—in the country that wasn't—there had been a bell like it. Nineteen of them. One for each district, rung at dawn and dusk and at hours that belonged to no calendar outside the walls.

"Wren," I said. "Do you want to hear about my dreams?"

* * *

Here is what I told her, in the cold and the dark, while the fog glowed below and the enforcers waited with the patience of the grave:

When I was small—before the Guild took me in, before Corvane Hale put a ledger in my hands and taught me that a balanced book is a quiet life—I dreamed of a city with brass bells. Every night. The same city. Green hills around it like cupped hands, and a harbor full of boats whose sails were dyed every color there is, and streets that rang. Not church bells. Something older. They rang at hours that made no sense, and everyone in the dream knew what the hours meant, and I never did, and that was the worst part—not being excluded from the city, but being included and unable to read it.

I told her about the woman who called me in from the water. I was always in the water in the dreams, or near it, and she'd stand on the sea-stairs and call a name—not Jonah, something else, something with a shape like a wave breaking backwards—and I'd wake up crying without knowing why, and the matron at the Guild orphanage would tell me I'd been dreaming of my mother, and I believed her for years, because it was the only explanation on offer.

I told her about the door. There was a door in the dreams, low and round-topped, set into a hillside, and it was locked, and I knew—I knew the way you know things in dreams—that the lock was waiting for me specifically. That it had been waiting a long time. That it was patient the way debts are patient.

"And then?" Wren asked quietly.

"And then nothing. Around my twelfth year they stopped. Just—stopped, like a shop closing. I told myself I'd grown out of them. Children dream strange things; adults balance ledgers." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Eleven years I kept books for the people who erased my home, and I told myself the dreams were indigestion."

"It wasn't indigestion."

"No. It was memory. It was Ashvale." Saying the name aloud felt like setting something fragile down on a high shelf. "The stranger said Ashvale remembers me. He had it backwards, or he had it exactly right, depending on how you look at it. I remember Ashvale. Badly. In pieces. Through the wrong end of a telescope. But I remember it, and that makes me either the key or the evidence, and the Guild wants both destroyed."

Wren was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had changed—lower, careful, the investigator's scalpel set aside for once.

"My grandmother sang lullabies in a language nobody in our family spoke. Four songs. My mother said they were from her father's side, some dead dialect, don't ask questions. I transcribed them when I was nineteen. I can still sing them." She paused. "Last week, in the archive, I found a fragment of an Ashvale nursery rhyme. Jonah—it was one of my grandmother's songs. Word for word. A country that was erased before my grandmother was born, and she was singing its lullabies like they were hers."

"The erasure leaks," I said. "Through songs. Through dreams. Through children on rooftops who know which tiles are loose." I shook my head. "It was never clean. It was never going to be clean. You can't unmake a country without leaving seams."

"Seams," she repeated, and I heard her filing the word away, and I loved her a little for it—for the way she took grief and turned it into methodology, because methodology was the only container she had for feeling that wouldn't crack.

Yes. Loved. Let's not make a ceremony of it. I'm a ledger-keeper; I know an entry when I make one. I had been carrying the figure for weeks without posting it, telling myself it was provisional, pending verification, subject to revision. Sitting in that belfry with her shoulder against mine and her breath making small ghosts in the freezing air, I posted it. No fanfare. Just the quiet click of a number becoming true.

* * *

The bells started at midnight.

Not our bell—ours stayed dumb and dark. But somewhere across the fog, one of Greyharrow's nineteen towers began to ring, and then another, and another, until all of them were going, the midnight peal rolling over the city like weather. It happens every night. I've heard it ten thousand times from my narrow bed above the counting house, and slept through it.

That night I sat bolt upright, heart slamming, because I understood the hours.

They weren't striking twelve. They were striking nineteen—one bell for each tower, each toll a different note, and the sequence of notes was a sentence, and the sentence was the same sentence every night, and I had slept through it ten thousand times because the erasure had taught my ears not to hear it. But sitting in a belfry, inside the sound instead of under it, with Ashvale's name fresh in my mouth—

"It's a message," I breathed. "Wren. The bells. Listen—they're not marking time, they're—"

"Jonah." Her hand closed on my arm. Hard. "Look down."

Below us, through the fog, the lanterns had stopped moving. All of them. Arranged now in a ring around Saint Verity's tower, motionless, patient as debt.

And the bells kept ringing, and the longer I listened the more certain I became, with the terrible certainty of a man reading his own handwriting in a ledger he never wrote, that the sequence of notes was not a message for the city at all.

It was a roll call. Nineteen names, one per bell, and when the last tower struck its last note, the silence afterward had a shape, and the shape was a pause, and the pause was waiting for someone to answer.

Every hair on my arms stood up. Because I knew the answer. I knew it the way my feet had known the rooftops. Some buried, dreaming piece of me had been answering that roll call every midnight of my life, and tonight, for the first time, I was awake to hear myself called.

"What is it?" Wren whispered. "What are they saying?"

I opened my mouth to tell her—to translate, to give her the sentence I could almost hold, the way you can almost hold a dream after waking—

And the great bell above our heads, silent for forty years, began to move.

A groan of ancient wood and rusted iron. The wheel turning. The rope trembling in its sheave though no hand touched it. Dust sifting down through the dark like snow falling upward.

The last note of the roll call hung in the air, unanswered, and our bell drew itself back to answer it, and I grabbed Wren and pulled her flat against the far wall as the first toll crashed down over us—

—and the note it struck was the name-shaped thing, the wave breaking backwards, the name the woman on the sea-stairs used to call.

My name. My other name. Rung out over Greyharrow at midnight by a bell that should not have been able to ring, in a language that officially did not exist, audible to every enforcer in the ring of lanterns below.

The silence after was absolute.

"Oh no," Wren said softly.

Down in the fog, every lantern began to move at once—upward, toward us, toward the tower stairs, fast now, no longer patient at all.

"They heard it," I said. "They heard *me.*"

Wren was already on her feet, hauling the iron pipe free from the door, and she looked back at me in the dark with the bell's echo still dying overhead, and whatever she saw in my face made her say, quickly, fiercely, before the world could get any worse:

"Whatever that name is—you keep it. Whatever else they take tonight, they don't get that."

And then the stairwell door burst open, and there was no more time for anything at all.
