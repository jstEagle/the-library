## 10. The Founder's Confession

There are three universal truths about guild vaults, and I had learned all of them the hard way before my twenty-fifth birthday, working for a Bureau that considered "forgotten places" a filing category rather than a nervous breakdown waiting to happen.

One: every vault believes itself unbreakable, which is precisely what makes it breakable. Two: the older the lock, the more it wants to be opened—locks are made by people, and people are made of secrets, and secrets rot from the inside out like teeth. Three: no one guards anything properly on the night of a festival.

Greyharrow's Lantern Night was in full swing when Jonah Eastwick and I slipped down the counting house's back stair, past a drunk junior clerk singing sea shanties to a mop, into the cold stone throat of the building. Above us, the city glowed with paper lanterns and cheap wine. Below us, the Guild kept its oldest lie.

Jonah carried a lantern with the wick trimmed so low it was barely a rumor of light. He moved like a man who had walked these stairs in his sleep for eleven years—which, I suspected, he had.

"You're certain about the rotation," I whispered.

"I wrote the rotation." His voice was flat as ledger paper. "For six years. Corvane trusts me with numbers. Numbers don't lie, so he assumes I don't either."

"And tonight?"

"Tonight he's upstairs drinking to the Guild's founding with men who think immortality is a pension plan." A pause. "Wren. If we find nothing down there—if this is just a strongroom with coin and title deeds—we walk away. You go back to your Bureau, I go back to my ledgers, and we pretend the last two weeks were a fever."

I looked at him in the guttering light. He meant it, or wanted to mean it, which is nearly the same thing and infinitely sadder.

"All right," I said, because lying to each other gently was becoming our love language, though neither of us had signed the paperwork on that yet.

* * *

The vault door was bronze, older than everything around it, and covered in script that predated Greyharrow's charter by centuries. Jonah pressed his palm to it the way you'd touch a sleeping animal.

"It knows me," he said quietly. "It's been knowing me since I took this post. I thought it was just... old metal being temperamental."

"Metal isn't temperamental."

"No," he said. "It isn't."

The lock was keyed to blood—I'd guessed that much from the chapel threshold—and memory, which was harder. But Jonah had been feeding this door fragments of himself for eleven years without realizing it: his name entered nightly in the gate log, his father's annotations carried against his chest, his childhood dreams recited to me in a bell tower while nineteen bells tried to shake them loose.

He cut his thumb on the edge of a coin—an old Ashvale mark, brass, worn smooth—and pressed it to the keyhole, and said, in a voice like a man confessing to a priest who is also his own reflection:

"I remember the bells. All nineteen. I remember green hills and a city that sang at dusk. Let me in."

The bronze exhaled. That's the only word for it—a long, mineral sigh, like something that had been holding its breath since before my grandmother learned her first lullaby. The door swung inward on darkness that smelled of dust and tallow and, faintly, impossibly, of salt water and summer.

We went down into the Guild's original sin.

* * *

The vault was smaller than I expected. Vaults usually are; enormity is for temples and tombs, but guilt fits in a closet. There were iron chests, deed boxes, a shelf of ledgers bound in leather gone black with age. And on a lectern in the center, under a glass case that had been polished obsessively by generations of guilty hands, lay a single document.

Jonah lifted the case. His hands were steady. Mine weren't, and I'm supposed to be the professional here.

The contract was written on vellum that should have crumbled centuries ago but hadn't, because some things refuse to age when they're too important—or too terrible—to be allowed to die. The ink was brown-black. I've seen enough old documents to know blood-ink when I see it, and this wasn't metaphorical.

Jonah read aloud, slowly, translating the old Ashvale script the way you'd translate a language you dreamed in but never spoke awake.

"'Let it be entered in the record of the Guild of Ashvale, this covenant being final and irrevocable, that the country of Ashvale, its lands, its waters, its nineteen bells and every soul born beneath them, does hereby surrender itself—'"

He stopped.

"Surrendered," I said. "Not sold."

"Not sold." His voice had gone strange. "Wren, every version of this history—the Guild's histories, the ones they taught initiates—it was a sale. A trade. Ashvale sold for longevity, for the founders' lives extended. A transaction. Clean. Commercial."

"Keep reading."

He kept reading. I watched his lips move and his face do something complicated.

"'—surrender itself willingly into concealment, that the thing which the founders could not kill might be bound beneath its hills, fed not by chains but by forgetting, starved of name and memory until such time as the covenant fails or is undone. In exchange, the signatories and their heirs shall know no death by years. The binding holds while the erasure holds. The erasure ends only when the door is opened from within by one born of Ashvale, who opens it knowingly, and freely.'"

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

"So the erasure isn't the crime," I said, working it through aloud because if I didn't say it, it would stay shapeless, and shapeless horrors breed faster. "The erasure is the *lock*. Every person who forgot Ashvale, every lullaby scrubbed clean, every record burned—not collateral damage. Mechanism. They turned an entire country into a prison cell and then erased the prison so the prisoner couldn't be found."

"And the founders bought immortality as payment for jailer's duty," Jonah said. He set the vellum down with exaggerated care, the way you handle something when you want very badly to throw it. "Except they weren't paying for their lives. They were paid *in* lives. For services rendered. Warden's wages."

"There's more. Read the marginalia."

He read the marginalia. And I watched the last comfortable assumption he had left die on his face.

"What does it say?" I asked, though I already knew from the way his jaw went tight. Jonah Eastwick has exactly two tells: he touches his left cufflink when he's lying, and he goes very still when the truth is worse than the lie.

"The binding is anchored to the door," he said. "Open the door, the anchor breaks. Open the door, and—" He swallowed. "'That which could not be killed shall rise with the tide of returning memory.'"

"We open the door, we free it."

"We open the door, we free it." He laughed, one dry note with no humor anywhere in it. "All this time I thought we were racing the enforcers to commit a rescue. We're not rescuing anyone. We're picking a lock on a cell because the cell looks cruel, and the thing inside has been listening to us pick for two weeks."

* * *

I did what I always do when the world rearranges itself: I got angry, because anger is just grief wearing armor, and armor lets you keep moving.

"Your father knew." I pulled the annotated pages from my satchel—Elias Eastwick's cramped handwriting in the margins of a stolen Guild history. "Look. 'The covenant is not what the Guild teaches.' 'Ask what sleeps beneath.' 'The price of waking.'" She'd been dead eleven years, my mother, and her marginalia had led me across half the coast chasing a country that officially didn't exist, and now it turned out she'd been leading me toward a cage with something starving inside it. "Everyone knew. Your father. My mother. The whole rotten generation, writing riddles in margins instead of *telling* anyone—"

"They couldn't tell anyone. Telling is remembering, and remembering feeds it—" Jonah stopped himself. Ran a hand over his face. "No. That's not it either. That's what the Guild would say. God, how do you know what's true anymore? Every fact I own came from the same institution that built this."

"Then let's establish facts we can verify." I spread the contract flat on the lectern, pinned the corners with coins. "Fact one: the erasure is failing. Your mystery entry, the map, the stranger who died at your gate—Ashvale is bleeding back into the world whether the Guild likes it or not."

"Fact two," Jonah said slowly, "if the erasure completes, the binding holds forever. Corvane doesn't want to erase Ashvale out of malice. He wants to finish the job before the lock rusts through."

"Fact three." I hated this one. I said it anyway, because I'd watched enough investigations die of wishful thinking to respect ugly facts. "If the erasure fails completely—if the door opens—the thing comes out. Whatever it is. Whatever the founders couldn't kill."

Jonah stood very still, looking at the contract, and I realized he wasn't reading it anymore. He was reading the space around it, the vault itself, the way you look at a room after learning someone died in it.

"My whole childhood," he said. "The bells, the hills, the city singing at dusk. I thought those were memories of home. What if they're memories of the *cell*? What if I grew up playing on the lid of a coffin?"

"Or," I said, "you grew up in a real place, a good place, that sacrificed itself to hold something down. Both can be true. Countries are like that. People are like that." I touched the vellum, the brown-black ink. "This says Ashvale surrendered *willingly*. Nobody sells themselves into a prison. But people volunteer for terrible duty all the time when the alternative is worse."

"That's a generous reading."

"I'm a generous person."

"You're a terrifying person," he said, and there was—briefly, flickeringly, like a lantern in wind—the ghost of the warmth from the bell tower. "You're standing in front of proof that our entire mission is a trap, and you're finding the charitable interpretation."

"Because the alternative interpretation is that we stop. We walk away, we let Corvane finish the erasure, we let Ashvale and everyone in it dissolve forever, and we live with that." I met his eyes. "Can you?"

He was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere above us, muffled through stone, the festival roared—laughter, a fiddle, someone badly misjudging a high note. Nineteen bells' worth of noise from a city that didn't know what it owed.

"No," he said finally. "But I can't unseal that door either. Not now. Not knowing what's anchored to it."

"Then we need a third option. There's always a third option. It's usually worse than the other two, but it exists."

"That's not comforting, Wren."

"I'm not in the comfort business. I'm in the forgotten-places business."

* * *

We copied the contract—he copied, I sketched the marginalia, both of us working fast now, because the vault had been open too long and doors that sigh closed eventually growl. And it was as I was sketching the final passage that I saw it: a line in the margin, in a different hand than the main text, older handwriting, hurried, almost gouged into the vellum.

*The binding was never tested at full strength. It was made in haste, on the last night, by frightened people. Ask what the founders were running FROM, not what they locked away.*

I showed Jonah. He read it twice.

"That's not founder's hand," he said. "That's—"

"That's someone who was there. Someone who disagreed." I rolled the copy tight, heart hammering in a rhythm that had nothing to do with fear and everything to do with the specific vertigo of a theory assembling itself. "Jonah. What if the founders didn't imprison something that attacked them? What if they imprisoned something that *frightened* them? Something that told the truth, maybe. Or offered them something they didn't want to admit they wanted."

"You're suggesting the monster might be innocent."

"I'm suggesting the victors wrote the contract, and the victors were buying immortality, and nobody buys immortality from a position of moral clarity." I slung my satchel. "We need the rest of the record. Correspondence. Founder's journals. Anything predating the covenant."

"There's one place," Jonah said, and the color drained out of his face even as he said it. "Corvane's private study. Top floor. Behind the portrait of the first nine founders."

"Of course it is. Why would it ever be somewhere sensible?"

"Wren." He caught my arm at the foot of the stairs, and up close his eyes were doing that thing where they looked like a man standing at the edge of deep water. "If we're wrong about this—if we open that door and it's exactly what the contract says it is—everything we've done, everyone who's helped us, it won't have been a rescue. It'll have been the worst mistake in the history of two countries."

"Then we make sure we're right." I put my hand over his. "And if we're wrong anyway, we'll be wrong together, having checked everything twice. That's the best anyone gets."

Above us, distantly, the festival bells began to ring out midnight—all nineteen, though Greyharrow only had twelve towers, and for a moment the sound hung wrong in the air, braided with something older, something that sang.

Jonah and I looked at each other.

Neither of us said what we were both thinking: *it knows we're coming.*

* * *

We made it as far as the second-floor landing before the lamps came on.

Not festival lanterns—guild lamps, white and merciless, arranged in the pattern Corvane Hale used for interrogations, which I recognized from Bureau training files I was never supposed to have read. He stood at the top of the stairs with four enforcers behind him, and he wasn't holding a weapon. He was holding a single sheet of paper, and he looked less angry than tired, which frightened me far more.

"Master Eastwick," he said. "Miss Calloway. You've been busy." He descended one step, unhurried. "I had hoped it wouldn't come to a conversation. But you've read the covenant now, haven't you? So let's skip past the part where I pretend you haven't, and get to the part that matters."

He smiled, and it was the smile of a man about to explain, patiently, why everything you believe is wrong.

"Because there's something in that contract you didn't read. Something the founders made sure was never written down."
