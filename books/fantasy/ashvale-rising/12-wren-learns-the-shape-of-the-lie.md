## 12. Wren Learns the Shape of the Lie

I have been lied to by professionals. Bureau interrogators with soft voices and hard eyes, smugglers swearing on their mothers' graves, one customs official in Vell who cried actual tears while denying he'd ever seen a shipment of bottled grief. I like to think I can tell a lie from the inside the way a tailor knows a seam—by running my thumb along it until I feel the place where the thread doesn't quite hold.

Jonah Eastwick lied to me with his whole face open.

That's what I kept telling myself, anyway, the morning after the vault, sitting in the rented room above the chandler's shop with my mother's notebook open on my knees and the rain doing its usual Greyharrow impression of a sky that has given up on itself. He'd shown me the contract. He'd read it aloud. He'd let me copy the clauses in my own careful hand, and when we'd finished he'd said, *that's everything*, and I'd believed him, because his voice hadn't done the thing voices do.

*That's everything.*

Three words. I'd written them in the margin of my notes like a scholar certifying a source. I want you to appreciate the irony. I am a woman who has spent nine years cataloguing the ways countries lie—borders that redraw themselves, archives that forget on purpose, whole coastlines struck from charts because someone powerful found them inconvenient—and I certified a lie in my own handwriting because a man with tired eyes and ink-stained fingers read a page to me in a steady voice.

The page itself was the problem. I'd copied it, yes, but I'd also noted its condition, because that's what I do, that's the whole of what I do: I notice what records *are*, not just what they say. And the vault page on the imprisonment clause—the one describing the thing the founders couldn't kill, the thing Ashvale had been surrendered to contain—had been whole. I remembered the bottom edge of it, the way the old vellum had gone soft and furred like the ear of a much-handled dog. I'd touched it. I remember touching it.

So when I woke and found the copy I'd made didn't sit right against my memory—when I checked my notes against the notebook and found a gap, a clause I'd *meant* to transcribe and had no record of transcribing—I told myself it was exhaustion. Grief. The ordinary erosion of a mind that had been running on tea and terror for a week.

Then I found the torn corner.

It was in my coat pocket. A triangle of old vellum, no bigger than a thumbnail, soft-furred on one edge. I turned it over in the grey light and read the fragment of ink on it, and the world went very quiet and very cold.

*—bound, not slain; the door must never—*

I sat with it for a long time. Rain on the window. The chandler downstairs arguing with his daughter about tallow. My own heartbeat, which had the decency to stay calm, because my heart has always been a bureaucrat even when the rest of me is on fire.

He'd torn it. In the vault, while I was copying the opening clauses, while my head was bent over the page and my attention was a lamp with a narrow shade—he had put his thumb under the corner of that page and torn, quiet as a man tearing bread, and then he'd read me everything *except* what he'd taken, and said *that's everything*, and I had written it down and believed him.

And the worst part—not the lie itself, I want to be clear, I have been lied to before and survived it, I have been lied to by institutions—the worst part was that I understood it. That was the unforgivable part. I could feel the shape of his reasoning the way you feel the shape of a key through cloth. He'd looked at the clause—*reopening the door frees it also*, or whatever the fragment's full sentence said, *the binding fails when the door opens*—and he'd looked at me, at this woman who had already given up her Bureau posting, her safe name, her entire careful life to chase a country out of a lullaby, and he'd decided I would still say yes. That I would walk into the door knowing what was behind it, and that the only way to make sure I said yes was to make sure I didn't know.

He'd protected the mission by protecting me from it. And underneath that, in the cellar of the thing, he'd protected something else: the possibility that if I knew, I'd stop him, and he couldn't bear to be stopped, because the door was his childhood, his brass bells, his green hills, and a man will forgive himself almost anything for the chance to go home.

I understood all of it. That's why it broke the way it did. You can survive being deceived by an enemy. It is much harder to survive being *read* by someone you love—being handled, gently, like a document with a dangerous page.

I put on my coat. I put the torn corner in my pocket, because I am a Bureau creature down to the bone and evidence goes in pockets, even when the evidence is the ruin of your own judgment. And I went to find him.

* * *

He was at the safehouse—the disused pilot's office above the old salt stores, where we'd been sleeping in shifts and pretending the sleeping arrangements were practical. He had the map halves out on the table and was annotating his father's notes, and when I came in he looked up with that expression he had, the one that made me furious now, the open face, the *I am only a ledger-keeper* face, and said, "You're wet. There's tea."

"Where's the rest of the page, Jonah."

I watched it land. I watched it move through him like weather—confusion first, then the flicker of calculation, then the terrible stillness of a man deciding whether to lie a second time to cover the first. I gave him credit for what happened next, and I have never taken that credit back, even in the worst weeks after: he didn't lie. His shoulders came down about half an inch, and he said, "You found the corner."

"I found the corner."

He looked at the table. At the map. At his own hands, which were ink-stained and shaking very slightly, and I hated him and ached for him in the same breath, which is, I have since learned, simply what it is to love someone honestly.

"The clause says the binding fails when the door opens," he said. "Whatever's down there—whatever the founders couldn't kill—it comes out with us. Or we come out with it. The contract's ambiguous about which."

"And you tore it out because—"

"Because you'd read it and stop. And you'd be *right* to stop. And I couldn't—" His voice did something I'd never heard it do, cracked down the middle like a spine breaking in a book. "I couldn't let you be right. Not about this. Not about the door."

I stood in the doorway of that cold room with rain running off my coat onto the floorboards, and I waited for the anger to come, because anger was the feeling I'd prepared for, anger was a tool I knew how to hold. It didn't come. What came instead was worse: a kind of vertigo, the floor of the last week tilting, every moment of trust re-weighting itself. The chapel. The bells. His hand finding mine in the dark of the tower while he told me about a city of brass bells and a mother singing in a language the world had forgotten. *That's everything.* Every time he'd told me the truth, I now had to ask: which truth, how much of it, what was torn out of this one too?

"Wren." He said my name the way you'd say a word in a language you were afraid of losing. "Say something."

"You should have told me."

"Yes."

"You should have let me decide."

"Yes." And then, because he was Jonah, because he was constitutionally incapable of leaving a ledger unbalanced: "I told myself I'd find a way around it. A rebinding. Something in my father's notes. I told myself the omission was temporary."

"You told yourself a story." I heard my own voice go flat and precise, the Bureau voice, the voice I used on smugglers, and I watched him flinch at the register of it. "That's what we do, people like us. We find a story with a hole in it the exact shape of the thing we can't face, and we live in it. I've spent nine years pulling other people out of those stories, Jonah. I am *not going to live in yours*."

"I know." Quiet. "I know. I'm sorry. Wren—"

"Don't." I held up a hand. "Don't say my name right now. I can't— if you say my name right now I'll forgive you, and I'm not done being angry yet, and if I forgive you before I'm done being angry I'll never know whether I forgave you or just surrendered."

That, at least, he understood. He was a man who kept accounts; he knew the difference between a debt forgiven and a debt abandoned. He nodded once, and stepped back from the door, and let me go.

I want it on the record that I did not slam the door. I closed it like a professional. I have regretted the professionalism of that door-close every day since, because it was the last controlled thing I did for some time, and I would have liked one more moment of control to keep.

* * *

The storm had come up the coast while I was inside, the way storms do in Greyharrow—suddenly, sideways, with the manners of a debt collector. Rain like flung gravel. The kind of dark that comes at three in the afternoon and stays. I walked anyway, because walking was the only thing I knew how to do with a heart that had just been torn like a vault page, and I told myself I was heading for the chandler's, or the Bureau's dead-drop, or anywhere, and I knew I was lying to myself, which was, under the circumstances, an irony I did not have the strength to appreciate.

Here is what I have learned about heartbreak, and I offer it as field notes, since field notes are the only prayers I know how to say: heartbreak makes you stupid in a very specific way. It makes you *legible*. A person in love who has just been betrayed walks through the world broadcasting—posture, pace, the set of the mouth, the way the eyes keep returning to a fixed middle distance where a memory is being re-examined. I have spotted that walk in a hundred interrogations. I have built cases on that walk.

And I did it, in the open, in a storm, on a street where I knew the Guild had eyes.

I was three streets from the salt stores when I felt it—the particular silence behind me that isn't silence at all but attention. I did the checks without breaking stride. Window glass: two shapes, grey cloaks, keeping pace on the far side. The mouth of Cooper's Lane: a third, waiting, too casual, a man standing in a downpour without a hood because his orders mattered more than his comfort.

I could have run. I want that noted too. There was a gap between the chandler's and the cooperage, a smuggler's cut I knew from the charts, and if I'd been whole I'd have taken it without slowing. But my feet were moving and my mind was still in that cold room, still hearing his voice crack, still doing the terrible arithmetic of *how much of the last week was true*, and by the time the arithmetic finished, Cooper's Lane was behind me and the man in the downpour had stepped into my path, and the two from across the street had crossed it, and the storm closed over the whole transaction like water over a dropped coin.

They didn't bind my hands roughly. That was the frightening part. They were gentle, almost courteous, the way you handle something valuable that has been misdelivered. One of them said, "The Guildmaster would like a word, miss," and the word *miss* in his mouth was the most threatening thing I'd heard all week.

They walked me toward the counting house through streets that were emptying fast, shutters banging, the storm tearing at the awnings. And as we went, I passed a lamp-post with a notice pasted to it, rain-blurred, and I read it out of pure professional habit, and my blood went cold for an entirely different reason.

It was a Guild broadsheet. A public notice. *By order of the Ashvale Guild: citizens are reminded that the festival of the Founders' Vigil begins at dusk. All persons are requested to remain indoors after nightfall and to report any unusual memories to their ward captain.*

*Unusual memories.*

They weren't just hunting us anymore. Corvane was moving to the final option—accelerating the erasure, sweeping the city's memory before we could reach the door. I didn't know yet how far he'd go, how much Greyharrow would lose before it was over. I knew only that the clock I'd been hearing for a week had just stopped being a metaphor.

And the last thing I thought, as they took me through the counting house doors, as the storm slammed them shut behind me, was not *I was a fool* and not *I hope he suffers*. It was this:

*He tore the page because he couldn't bear to lose the door. Corvane will burn the city because he can't bear to lose the Guild. And I am the only one of us who came into this with nothing to lose—and I just handed that advantage away by walking out alone.*

The doors closed. Somewhere above me, in the dark of the counting house, a ledger was being opened, and I had the sick, certain feeling that my name was already in it.
