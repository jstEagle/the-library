## 13. The Trade at Saltgate Pier

There is a particular kind of humiliation reserved for men who have spent eleven years being invisible and then discover that everyone was watching them the whole time.

I had been summoned by note — a heavy cream card, Guild seal in black wax, instructions written in Corvane's small precise hand: *Saltgate Pier. Midnight. Bring the map. Come alone.* The word *alone* had been underlined twice, which struck me as excessive. If I'd wanted to bring company, I'd have brought an army, not a truth-seeker with a bruised jaw and every reason to hate me.

Because Wren wasn't speaking to me. That was the arithmetic of my life now: I had traded her trust for what I told myself was strategy, and she had walked out into a storm to prove she didn't believe in my strategies, and within the hour the enforcers had taken her. So whatever happened at Saltgate Pier, I would be doing it alone, carrying two maps — one real, one forged — and one conscience that had been taking water for days.

The pier stretched out into Greyharrow's harbor like a finger pointing accusingly at the sea. Saltgate was the old fishing dock, mostly abandoned since the deepwater trade moved east: rotting pilings, a customs shed with its roof caved in, gulls arguing over something unspeakable on the far posts. Fog came off the water in slow gray sheets. Somewhere out past the breakwater, the drowned chapel sat under the tide, holding its door, holding my childhood, holding everything.

I walked the length of the pier with my boots knocking hollow on the planks and counted my assets the way I counted ledger entries. Asset one: I had memorized the map. Every line of it, every annotation in my father's cramped hand, every tidal notation. It lived behind my eyes now, inked into memory the way I used to ink entries into ledgers — permanently, and with a certain grim satisfaction. Asset two: the forgery. Three nights of work by lamplight, aging paper with tea and salt smoke, copying my father's hand until my own cramped in sympathy. It was good. It was very good. It was also, I now understood, the single most dangerous object in Greyharrow, because Corvane Hale had not built his career on trusting people like me.

Asset three: Wren Calloway, currently held somewhere in the counting house, which meant asset three was not actually an asset but a debt.

*Come alone,* the card said. Fine. I had come alone. But I had not come honest, and there is a difference between those two things that I was only beginning to learn, and learning it the hard way, the way I learned everything.

Corvane stood at the pier's end beneath a hooded lantern, and he had not come alone either. Four enforcers flanked him in the fog, gray-cloaked, faceless in the way the Guild trained them to be — not masked, merely unremarkable, men your eye slid off like rain off oilcloth. And between two of them, wrists bound in front of her, hair plastered dark against her cheek, stood Wren.

My stomach dropped through the pier and into the harbor.

"You brought her," I said. "You said—"

"I said *you* should come alone." Corvane's voice was mild, almost apologetic, which was how he sounded when he was about to do something unforgivable. "Nobody said anything about me. You've been alone too long, Jonah. It's made you clever in ways that worry me." He gestured, and the enforcers moved Wren forward a step. She looked at me, and her expression was the worst thing on that pier: not hatred, not fear. Assessment. She was measuring me, the way she'd measured me across the customs desk our first day, deciding what I was worth and whether I'd lie again.

"Is she hurt?" I asked.

"A bump on the head when she declined to come quietly. She declined very thoroughly — took two of my people down before they settled it." Something flickered across Corvane's face that might, in a better man, have been respect. "She's fine. She'll remain fine as long as this exchange goes simply. You know I don't enjoy mess."

That was true, at least. Corvane Hale had run the Ashvale Guild for thirty years without a drop of blood on the ledgers, which in our line of work was practically sainthood. He was a narrow man, dry as old vellum, with silver at his temples and reading glasses he wore pushed up on his forehead like a scholar pretending not to be a merchant. He had taught me double-entry bookkeeping. He had signed my apprenticeship papers. He had once stayed up all night with me when I had fever, years ago, spooning willow-bark tea into me and telling me stories about the founders that I now knew were lies.

"The map," he said, and held out his hand.

I took the folded packet from inside my coat. My heart was going like a drum in a parade, and I made myself breathe the way I breathed before balancing a difficult column: slow, even, boring. Boring was armor. Nobody watches a boring man.

"Before I hand this over," I said, "I want her released. Now. Not after. After is where promises go to die; I keep ledgers, Corvane, I know how 'after' gets recorded."

He smiled faintly. "You want terms in writing."

"I want them *before*, which is better than writing."

For a moment I thought he'd refuse. Then he nodded to the enforcers, and one of them drew a short knife and cut Wren's bindings. She rubbed her wrists, stepped back from them — not toward me, I noticed, and the distance of it went through me like a splinter — and stood at the pier rail with the fog curling around her, watching.

"There," Corvane said. "Now the map."

I gave it to him.

He unfolded it right there under the lantern, and I watched his eyes move across my father's annotations, the coastline of a country that officially did not exist, the little marked door beneath the drowned chapel. He read it the way I read ledgers — completely, hungrily, missing nothing — and I felt the sweat start cold between my shoulder blades, because if he missed nothing, then—

"This is half," he said quietly.

"It's what the stranger left me. Half a map. The other half—" I shrugged. "Maybe it dissolved with him. Maybe it never existed. Maps of impossible places tend to be incomplete. Ask anyone who's ever bought one."

Corvane looked up at me over the paper, and for a long moment the only sounds were the gulls and the water slapping the pilings and my own pulse filling my ears like surf.

"Jonah," he said gently. "I've known you since you were nineteen years old and couldn't look a customer in the eye. Do you truly believe I can't tell when you're holding something back?"

And that was when the second enforcer — the one standing nearest Wren, the tall one whose face I had been carefully not looking at — said, "Guildmaster. This half is wrong."

Everything happened very quickly after that, and I remember it in fragments, the way you remember a carriage accident.

The tall enforcer had taken the forged half — because of course there were two halves in the packet, the real one I'd surrendered and the false one I'd tucked beneath it, my little sleight of ledger, my masterpiece — and he'd held it beside the first, and my father's handwriting matched my father's handwriting, but the coastlines didn't match. The forgery showed the chapel door a mile north of where the true map placed it. A mile north was open water. A mile north was nothing.

"Wrong how?" Corvane said, though his voice had already changed, gone flat and cold, the voice from the counting house when the numbers didn't reconcile.

"Two different doors," the enforcer said. "Or one of these is drawn by someone who wants us walking into the sea."

Corvane turned to me slowly, folding both halves together with terrible neatness, and the look on his face was worse than anger. It was disappointment, the specific disappointment of a man who has just discovered a discrepancy in a ledger he trusted absolutely.

"Oh, Jonah," he said. "You memorized it."

I didn't answer. There wasn't anything useful to say. He was right, and we both knew it, and the confession sat between us on the wet planks like a third person.

"I wondered," he went on, almost conversationally, "why you asked for her release *before*. You never negotiate. Eleven years, and you've never once negotiated. It should have told me something." He sighed, and nodded, and said, "Take the map from him. All of it. And take him."

The enforcers moved. And here is the thing about four trained men closing on one unarmed clerk at the end of a pier: it should have been over in ten seconds.

It was not over in ten seconds, because Wren Calloway kicked the tall enforcer in the back of the knee, took the lantern off its hook, and put it through the face of the second one.

I want to be clear about what I saw, because for the rest of my life I intend to describe it accurately: she had been standing at the rail with her wrists freshly cut free, hands loose, weight easy, and I had thought — God help me, I had actually thought — *she's exhausted, she's beaten, I'll need to get her out of here somehow.* And then she moved, and it was like watching a door swing. The tall man went down onto one knee and she hit him across the temple with the lantern's iron handle as she pivoted, and the second man caught the burning lamp itself full in the jaw, oil and flame and glass, and he went backward off the pier into the harbor with a sound like a dropped sack of turnips.

"Down!" she shouted at me, and I went down, because when a woman who fights like that shouts, you obey; theology can wait.

The third enforcer got a hand on my collar and hauled me up, and I did the only thing my body knew how to do, which was nothing useful, so instead I did something my mind knew how to do: I grabbed the front of his cloak and pulled him off balance and said, directly into his face, in my best ledger-keeper's voice, "Your name is Merrick Voss and you owe the Guild nine years' wages you never claimed, entry forty-one, page three hundred, and I am the only man alive who remembers it."

He hesitated. One heartbeat of pure confusion — why does the clerk know that, what else does the clerk know — and one heartbeat was enough. Wren's elbow found the side of his head and he folded.

Which left the fourth enforcer, and Corvane, and me, and Wren, and a fire starting in the soaked timbers of the customs shed where the lantern had skidded, and the fog turning orange at the edges like the whole night was blushing at what it was witnessing.

The fourth enforcer had a blade out and was moving on Wren from behind while she was still turning from the third, and I did the second brave thing of my entire life: I threw myself sideways into his legs. We went down together in a tangle on the planks, and he was much stronger than me and much angrier, and I felt his fist against my ear and the world rang like the bell tower we'd sheltered in, all nineteen bells going off inside my skull. But he'd stopped moving toward Wren, and that was the whole of my plan, and it worked, and I have never been prouder of anything.

Wren finished it. Of course she did. Two strikes — I caught them upside down and half-blinded, a heel to the wrist, a palm to the throat — and the fourth man lay groaning and disarmed, and then she stood over me with her chest heaving and her knuckles bleeding and offered me her hand.

I took it. Her grip was strong and warm and furious.

"You lied to me," she said.

"Yes."

"And you got caught anyway."

"...Yes."

"And you came here planning to hand over a forgery to a man who has run a smuggling guild for thirty years, as if he wouldn't check."

When she put it that way. "In my defense," I said, spitting out blood — when had I started bleeding? probably the fist, possibly the deck — "in my defense, no. I don't have a defense. It was a stupid plan. It was the stupidest plan ever committed to paper, and I committed it to paper three times before I got the coastline right."

Something cracked at the corner of her mouth. Not forgiveness. Not yet. But the crack let a little light through, and light was more than I deserved.

"Jonah." Corvane's voice, behind us. We both turned.

He had not run. He had not called for reinforcements or drawn a weapon. He stood at the pier's end with the two map halves in his hand and the firelight on his face, and he looked — tired. Old, suddenly. Like a man holding a bill that had just come due after a very long deferral.

"You memorized it," he said again. "So burning the paper means nothing. Killing you means nothing — the memory dies with you into wherever dead clerks go, and the map with it, unless..." He stopped. He looked out at the black water, toward the drowned chapel, and I watched him arrive at the same conclusion I'd arrived at days ago, and I watched it frighten him. "Unless you use it."

"Give it up, Corvane," Wren said. "The bargain's failing. Your founders sold a country and bought themselves a few centuries, and the bill's come due, and you know it. Help us rebind it properly, or get out of the way."

"Rebind it." He laughed, and it was a horrible sound, dry and small. "Child, do you know what's under that chapel? Do you know what my predecessors—" He stopped himself, jaw working. Whatever the vault had shown Wren and me, Corvane had known it longer, carried it longer, and you could see the weight of it bending him like a mast in wind. "No. No, you don't understand, neither of you understands, and I don't have time to make you understand, because if that door opens wrong—"

"Then help us open it right," I said.

He looked at me for a long moment. Firelight and fog and the gulls screaming overhead like the world's worst chorus. And I swear to you, in that moment, some part of him wanted to say yes. Some part of him had been waiting thirty years for someone to say it to him.

But he was Corvane Hale, and Corvane Hale did not gamble, and so instead he said, "I'm sorry, Jonah," and threw the map halves into the harbor.

They hit the water and the ink bloomed off them instantly — Guild paper, treated paper, paper charmed to unwrite itself — and in three seconds there was nothing left but two pale ghosts of pulp sinking into the black.

It didn't matter. He knew it didn't matter. I knew it didn't matter. The map lived behind my eyes, and he could not burn that, and we both understood that the next move was his and it would be a bad one, and that was exactly why I'd brought the only person in Greyharrow who fought like a door swinging shut.

"Go," Wren said quietly.

We ran. Down the pier, past the burning shed, past the enforcer crawling toward the edge to fish his colleague out of the harbor, past the gulls rising in a shrieking gray cloud. Behind us Corvane did not pursue. I heard him call out, once, clear across the water:

"You'll need the founder's consent, Jonah! Ask your father's notes what consent cost him!"

And then the fog took the pier, and the fire, and him.

* * *

We didn't stop running until the terraces above Harrow Street, where the city climbed away from the harbor in crooked steps and the lamps burned yellow behind fogged glass, and ordinary people were sleeping ordinary lives that Corvane's Final Erasure hadn't started eating yet. Wren pulled me into the lee of a chimney stack and we stood there breathing like landed fish, steam rising off us, my ear throbbing in time with my heart.

"So," she said finally. "The forgery."

"The forgery."

"Which was always going to fail."

"The forgery," I admitted, "was always going to fail. It was never meant to succeed. It was meant to buy ninety seconds of confusion, and I was going to spend those ninety seconds getting you out." I looked at her. "Except you'd already gotten yourself out. Before I even arrived. Hadn't you? The bindings — you could have slipped them anytime."

She was quiet a moment. Fog beaded on her lashes.

"Since the counting house," she said. "Enforcer knots. My mother taught me knots before she taught me letters." She flexed her cut wrists, winced. "I stayed bound because I wanted to see what you'd do. Whether you'd come. Whether you'd lie to *him* the way you lied to me, or whether—" She stopped. Started again, more plainly, which was how Wren apologized: "Whether I mattered more than the mission. To you."

"And?"

"You threw yourself at a man with a knife. You. The man who alphabetizes his spice rack." A breath that was almost a laugh. "You're a terrible liar, Jonah Eastwick. You always have been. That's the whole problem with you, and it turns out it might be the whole point of you."

"I'm sorry," I said. "About the vault. About the torn page. About deciding for you what you could bear to know. It's the same sin Corvane commits, dressed in smaller clothes, and I won't do it again. Everything I know, you know. Starting now. Starting with what consent cost my father, because I think I'm about to find out, and I don't want to find out alone."

Wren studied me for a long moment, the way she'd studied me across the customs desk, except this time I had nothing hidden in my pockets and nothing hidden in my omissions, and whatever she was looking for, she found it.

"All right," she said. "Everything. Both directions. From here on."

"Both directions," I agreed.

She took my hand — the uninjured one — and laced her fingers through mine, matter-of-fact, as if we'd always done this, as if we'd simply misplaced the habit somewhere in the last week and were resuming it now. My heart did something unprofessional.

"Your father's notes," she said. "The founder's consent. Where do we start?"

I looked out past the rooftops, toward the harbor, toward the water that held a chapel and a door and a country, and I thought of the dying stranger's whisper — *Ashvale remembers you* — and of my father's handwriting, which I could reproduce stroke for stroke from memory now, and of the one thing I had never dared copy out because I'd never understood it until tonight.

"Page one," I said. "The dedication. He wrote it in a cipher I broke years ago and dismissed as sentiment. *For the one who opens the door, knowing what the opening costs — and pays it smiling.*" I swallowed. "Wren — I don't think my father opened the door. I think he tried. And I think we're about to learn exactly what it cost him."

Above us, far off across the sleeping city, a single bell began to ring — though no tower in Greyharrow rang at this hour, and the sound was coming from the sea.
