## 14. What My Father Knew

My father's handwriting was a thing I had spent eleven years pretending not to recognize.

It appeared in the margins of half the ledgers I kept—small corrections in a cramped, slanted script, *this entry is misdated by a day*, *the echo in jar forty-one has soured, note the smell*—and every time I saw it I told myself it was coincidence. That the Guild simply employed many men who wrote like that. That dead men did not annotate the books of the living.

I was very good at telling myself things. It was, after all, most of my job.

But now, sitting on the floor of my ransacked quarters with the false ledger open across my knees and the map of Ashvale spread beside me like a wound, I could no longer pretend. The annotations were everywhere once you knew to look. And they were not about echoes or borrowed years.

They were about the door.

*The lock is not a lock,* one read, pressed so close to the map's edge that the ink had bled into the parchment's grain. *It is a sentence. Read it as grammar and you will fail. Read it as grief and you may pass.*

I sat with that for a long while, listening to Greyharrow rain itself against my broken shutters. Somewhere below, the counting house bells rang the third hour, and I noticed—for the first time in my life—that there were nineteen of them, and that no ledger I had ever balanced explained why a guild that traded in impossible things would ring nineteen bells at an hour that officially did not exist.

Nineteen bells. The number had been following us for days. Wren had said it meant something. I had nodded along, because nodding along was also most of my job.

Now I turned to the back matter of the false ledger—the pages where bookkeepers hide what they cannot destroy—and found what my father had hidden there thirty years ago, folded into a page that looked, at a glance, like an inventory of candle stubs.

It was not an inventory of candle stubs.

* * *

Here is what I learned between the third bell and the fourth, alone in a room with its drawers pulled out like tongues, reading my father's hand by the light of a candle I probably should have been conserving:

The founders' contract—the one we'd stolen from the vault, the one that had turned our mission inside out like a glove—was only half a document.

Wren had read it and gone pale: *Ashvale surrendered itself to imprison something the founders couldn't kill.* A thing bound beneath the city of brass bells, sealed behind a door keyed to blood and memory, with an entire country erased from the world's mind to serve as the padlock. Every year the binding held, the world forgot a little more. Every year the forgetting deepened, the binding weakened. The bargain was failing by design, because a prison built out of forgetting must eventually forget its own prisoner.

Corvane's answer was to let it finish. Let the erasure complete. Let the door seal forever, even if it meant sealing the prisoner in with its own starvation until the binding rotted through from the inside anyway—a slow catastrophe dressed up as prudence.

Our answer, mine and Wren's, had been simpler and more terrible: open the door, rebind the thing properly, accept whatever the opening cost.

What neither of us had known—what the vault copy had not contained, because the vault copy was the founders' version, written by men who wanted their heirs to believe there were only two choices—was this, in my father's cramped and slanted script:

*A binding made in fear fails. A binding made in love holds. This is not sentiment; it is mechanics. The original founders bound the prisoner with terror and erasure, and terror and erasure decay. But memory freely shared does not decay. It compounds.*

And beneath that, underlined twice, the line that stopped my breath:

*The door can be opened by two who share memory across the erasure—one born inside it, one born outside it. Each carries what the other lost. Together they are the whole ledger. Such a pair can weave a new binding without freeing the prisoner and without starving it: feed it memory instead of oblivion. Give it names. Give it songs. A thing remembered is a thing held.*

I read it three times. Then a fourth, because the third time my eyes had blurred and I wanted to be sure I wasn't inventing hope out of candle smoke.

One from inside. One from outside.

I had been born in Ashvale, in a house on a hill above the brass-bell quarter, before the Guild carried me through the last closing door and let the world forget me. I was the inside.

Wren Calloway, investigator for the Bureau of Forgotten Places, granddaughter of a woman whose lullabies had named a country that officially never existed—she was the outside. She carried Ashvale in her blood without ever having stood on its soil, in songs she'd thought were nonsense, in her grandmother's habit of leaving offerings of salt water on windowsills "for the ones across."

We fit. Not metaphorically. Mechanically. My father had drawn a diagram.

* * *

I want to be honest about what I felt when I understood it, because honesty is supposed to be the theme of my life now, apparently, and I have been terrible at practicing it.

I did not feel noble.

I felt like a man who has spent years carrying a stone and is suddenly told the stone is a key. There is relief in that. There is also rage, because nobody told you, and your shoulders ache, and you dropped the stone in a river once in 943 and had to fish it out in winter.

My father had known. He had annotated ledgers for eleven years of my employment—corrections, warnings, little arrows pointing at entries I should question—and he had never once said, *Jonah, you were born in a country that doesn't exist, and you are half of a mechanism that can save it.* He had died—or left, or dissolved into salt water like the stranger at my gate, the two possibilities had begun to blur—without telling me what I was.

Or maybe he had told me. Maybe every correction in those margins had been a lesson shaped like bookkeeping. *Read it as grammar and you will fail. Read it as grief and you may pass.* Maybe that was not advice about locks. Maybe that was advice about fathers.

I closed the false ledger and put my forehead against it and let myself grieve for exactly as long as it took the candle to gutter, which is a precise unit of measurement and the only kind I trust.

Then I went to find Wren.

* * *

She was not hard to find, which worried me. After the storm of her walking out—after the torn vault page and the confrontation and the hour of silence in which I had assumed I'd lost her to honesty's usual consequences—I had expected her to be hiding well. Instead she was in the scriptorium of the old customs house, at a desk by the window, surrounded by Bureau papers, copying something in her quick, slashing hand.

"You're supposed to be in hiding," I said from the doorway.

"I'm supposed to be working." She didn't look up. "Hiding is just working somewhere with worse light."

"You were taken by enforcers."

"I was *detained* by enforcers, briefly, and then I picked the lock with a hairpin and a grudge, which is the entire Bureau training regimen summarized." Now she looked up, and her eyes did the thing they always did—the assessment, the weighing, the terrible accuracy. "You've found something."

"How can you possibly—"

"Because you're standing like a man holding a live coal and trying to decide whether to throw it or warm his hands." She pushed the papers aside. "Sit down, Jonah. Show me."

So I sat, and I showed her. My father's annotations. The grammar of locks. The mechanics of bindings. The diagram, which was genuinely beautiful in the way that only true things drawn carefully are beautiful: two circles overlapping, one labeled *within*, one labeled *without*, and in the overlap, in letters small enough to require leaning close, the word *both*.

Wren read it the way she read everything—fast, then slow, then fast again, hunting for the trick. I watched her hunt for it. I watched her fail to find it.

"This says we can rebind it," she said finally. "Not free it. Not starve it. Feed it."

"That's what it says."

"And the price—" Her finger stopped on the margin note. *A binding made in love holds.* "He means that literally, doesn't he. Your father. It's not poetry. It's load-bearing."

"My father did not write poetry," I said. "He wrote footnotes. If it's in the margin, it's structural."

She laughed—a short, startled sound, quickly strangled—and then pressed her knuckles to her mouth, and I realized she was doing arithmetic she didn't want to share yet. So I waited. Waiting for Wren to finish thinking is like waiting for a tide; arguing with it is how you drown.

"The overlap," she said at last. "The *both*. We don't just each carry half. We have to actually *share* it. Out loud. At the threshold. Memories from before the erasure matched against memories from after, woven together while the door stands open." She looked up. "You'll have to tell me everything, Jonah. All of it. The childhood you thought were dreams. The house on the hill. Whatever the brass bells sounded like. And I'll have to give him—" her voice caught slightly on the pronoun, the prisoner, the thing beneath the chapel that had once perhaps been a person or had eaten several— "the other side. Grandmother's lullabies. Salt water on windowsills. Everything the outside kept alive without knowing why."

"Yes."

"That's not a ritual. That's—" She exhaled. "That's just being known. Completely. By someone. While standing in front of a monster."

"When you say it like that it sounds bad."

"It *is* bad. It's the worst possible version of intimacy, Jonah, it's intimacy with witnesses, one of whom is an eldritch prisoner and the other of whom is your dead father's footnote." But she was smiling as she said it, that fierce crooked smile, and her hand had crossed the table to rest on the diagram, over the word *both*, as if she could hold it in place by pressure alone.

"There's a catch," I said, because there is always a catch, and I had decided that withholding catches was how I'd nearly lost her, and I was done with that. "Two catches. First: the rebinding requires the door to stand fully open during the weaving. Which means Corvane will feel it. Which means he'll come."

"We assumed he'd come."

"Second: my father's notes end here." I turned to the last page. "There's no instruction for what happens to the two who do the weaving. Whether we walk away. Whether the door takes a fee. Whether—" I stopped, because the next words were ridiculous and true. "Whether we're still ourselves afterward, or whether we become part of the binding. Part of the lock. Forever."

Wren was quiet for a moment. Rain ticked against the customs house glass.

"You know what I keep coming back to?" she said. "Everyone else who touched this problem got to choose between bad options that someone else designed. The founders designed fear. Corvane designed surrender. Even your father—he designed a mechanism, but he died before he had to stand in it." She tapped the diagram. "We get to design ours. Bad options, yes. But ours. Chosen with our eyes open, by two people who fit the lock."

"That's either hope or hubris."

"With teeth," she said. "Hope with teeth. It's the only kind worth having."

* * *

We worked until dawn, because hope with teeth still needs feeding.

Wren drafted the sequence of the weaving—she thinks in sequences the way I think in ledgers, and watching her work was like watching someone else balance impossible accounts. I transcribed my father's notes onto clean paper, twice, hiding one copy in the false ledger and one in the lining of my coat, because I had learned something about vaults and fires and enforcers in the past week: important things should exist in more than one place, and so should the people who carry them.

Somewhere around the fifth bell, she asked the question I'd been dreading.

"The memories you'll have to give him. The prisoner. What if some of them aren't good?"

I set down my pen. "What do you mean?"

"Ashvale surrendered itself, Jonah. An entire country agreed to be forgotten. Countries don't agree to that out of courage—they agree out of guilt, or grief, or shame. Whatever your people did to bind that thing the first time, it may not have been clean. When you open your childhood to the weaving, you might find things in it you don't want to know. Things your people chose to forget *on purpose*, and the erasure was mercy as much as prison." Her voice gentled. "Are you ready for that?"

I thought about the green hills. The brass bells. The house on the hill above the bell quarter, and a woman singing in it—my mother, presumably, though the memory was more warmth than face. I thought about the stranger at my gate, dissolving into salt water, whispering *Ashvale remembers you*.

"No," I said honestly. "But I'm done letting other people decide what I'm allowed to remember. Even my own fear."

Wren studied me for a long moment, and then she did something she had never done: she reached across the table and took my hand, ink-stained and steady, and held it.

"For the record," she said, "when we stand at that threshold and I have to tell a monster my grandmother's lullabies—if you laugh at my singing voice, the binding fails and we all die."

"Noted. I'll suppress all commentary."

"See that you do. Ledger-keeper."

Outside, the sky was going grey at the edges, the particular bruised grey of Greyharrow before a storm that means business. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, the counting house bells began to ring.

I counted them out of habit. Nineteen.

But they didn't stop at nineteen. They kept going—twenty, twenty-one, twenty-two—and across the city, dogs began to howl, and Wren went white, and we both understood at the same moment what extra bells meant.

Not a warning.

An alarm.

Someone had found the empty vault. Someone had found the forged map. And somewhere in the counting house, Corvane Hale had just learned that the heir he'd offered everything to had chosen otherwise—and the Final Erasure, which we had days to stop, had just begun to ring itself in early.
