## 15. The Guild Turns Out Its Lights

I have been in cities during sieges, plagues, and one truly memorable tax riot, and I can tell you that a city knows when it is dying. It hums differently. The fear gets into the cobblestones.

Greyharrow didn't know yet. That was the horror of it. On the morning after I walked out of Jonah Eastwick's life—after he admitted, with those careful ledger-keeper's eyes, that he'd torn the most important page out of the Guild vault and let me walk around half-blind—the city went about its business like any other morning. Fishwives argued over herring. Dockhands cursed a crate of glassware. A boy sold meat pies on Saltgate Row and cheated a sailor outrageously at dice while doing it.

And I sat in my rented room above a chandler's shop with my mother's marginalia spread across the bed, hating myself for two reasons in equal measure: because Jonah had lied to me, and because I understood exactly why he'd done it.

That's the trouble with being trained to detect lies. Eventually you get good enough to detect your own.

"He protected me," I said aloud to the empty room, which is never a good sign. "He decided what I could bear to know." My grandmother used to say men did that the way rivers did flooding—not out of malice, just gravity. I'd spent my whole career proving her wrong about men. Apparently I'd only proven her wrong about some of them.

The page he'd taken described the imprisonment clause. The thing beneath Ashvale. The reason the country hadn't simply been sold but *surrendered*—sealed away with itself as the lock. And every hour I spent reconstructing it from memory and my mother's cramped handwriting, I kept arriving at the same arithmetic: if the erasure finished, the thing stayed locked forever and everyone who ever touched Ashvale's name became nobody. If we opened the door, we might free something the founders couldn't kill.

Jonah had chosen to carry that alone. I chose to be furious about it for precisely as long as it took me to notice the light through my window had gone strange.

Not storm-strange. Not fog. Strange like a candle burning down—Greyharrow's morning light had a gutter to it, a flicker at the edges, as if someone were pinching the wick of the world.

I got up so fast I knocked my mother's papers to the floor and didn't stop to gather them. In eleven years with the Bureau of Forgotten Places I had learned one iron law: when forgetting starts, you don't tidy up first.

* * *

The chandler's wife, Marta Ostwick, was in the shop below, wrapping tallow candles in brown paper, humming. She'd hummed the same three bars every morning since I arrived—a fisherman's tune, she'd told me, that her mother sang hauling nets.

"Marta," I said. "What's that song?"

She looked up, smiling, paper rustling. "Beg pardon?"

"The song. You're humming it right now."

Her hands kept moving. Her smile stayed where it was, fixed and pleasant, and behind it her eyes went carefully, terribly blank—the look of a woman reaching into a drawer and finding it empty. "Am I? I don't—I don't recall that I am."

"You are. Three notes, then down. Your mother sang it hauling nets."

"My mother," Marta said slowly, "was a laundress."

She wasn't. I'd heard the story twice, complete with the nets and the storm off Vell Point and the father lost at sea. I watched the story leave her face in real time—not dramatically, not with gasps and clutching, but the way frost leaves a window. Just gone. She frowned faintly at the candles in her hands as if unsure how they'd gotten there, then smiled at me again, a customer, a stranger, which is what I now was.

"Can I help you, miss?"

Outside, the lamplighter was making his rounds at nine in the morning, lighting lamps against a daylight that no longer seemed to want them.

* * *

Here is what the Final Erasure actually looks like from the street, since I doubt anyone else will ever write it down:

It doesn't come like a wave. It comes like an accountant.

It takes the smallest things first. Names, then faces, then the reasons you crossed a room. I watched a man on Cooper's Lane stop mid-stride and stand there turning his hat in his hands, because he had been walking somewhere and the somewhere had been subtracted. I watched a woman call a child's name and get silence, and then call a different name, and then no name at all, just a shape of sound with nothing in it. The child didn't cry. That was the worst part. The child looked up at its mother with polite attention, the way you regard a stranger speaking a foreign language.

And everywhere, the lights. Greyharrow's lamps burning at noon, guttering, dimming, street by street, as if the city were being read by candlelight on its way out.

I ran. I'm not proud of the running—it was undignified, elbows-out, Bureau training reduced to its animal core—but I ran toward the counting house, because if Corvane Hale had started this early, then either he'd found the forged map Jonah slipped him, or he'd realized the forgery, or he simply hadn't cared. Any of the three meant the same thing: the clock we thought we had until the next dark moon had been thrown out the window, and the only people who knew how to stop the erasure were a man I'd sworn off four hours ago and me, and one of us had lied, and the other one was currently sprinting past a fishmonger who had forgotten what fish were.

The fishmonger was standing in his stall holding a mackerel at arm's length, examining it with scholarly bafflement. I want that recorded somewhere. When they tell this story—and Wren Calloway intends for someone to tell it—they always skip the mackerel.

* * *

The counting house doors stood open.

That alone should have emptied the street. The Ashvale Guild did not leave its doors open; the Guild treated an open door the way other institutions treated heresy. But both leaves were flung wide, and inside, the great hall where I'd once endured a customs inspection conducted entirely in silences was full of Guild clerks standing at their desks, perfectly still, holding their pens over ledgers they had stopped understanding.

One of them, a young man with ink to his elbows, turned to me as I passed and asked, with genuine courtesy, "Miss, do you work here?"

"No."

"Oh." He considered this. "Do I?"

I didn't answer. There was no kind answer. Behind him, another clerk was reading her own ledger entries with the furrowed brow of someone deciphering a dead language—which, in a sense, she was, because every entry named a thing the world was learning to forget, and the words were going hollow even as she read them.

Corvane's office door was closed. Through it I could hear voices—one Corvane's, low and strained, and the other one wrong. Not loud. Wrong the way a bell is wrong when the crack has finally gone all the way through. I put my ear to the wood, which is unprofessional and would have gotten me dismissed from the Bureau, and listened.

"—the terms are not negotiable," the wrong voice said. "They were never negotiable. You bought continuance, Master Hale. Continuance has a price schedule."

"The bargain said generations. It said *generations*—"

"And the generations are spent. You may thank your clerk and his map for accelerating the audit." Something that was almost a chuckle moved through the door and made the hair on my arms stand up. "You've begun the Final Erasure. Good. Finish it. Or would you prefer I finish it for you? I have been hungry a very long time, and your founders' signatures bind you to feed me either way."

I stepped back from the door. My hands were shaking, and I made them stop, because shaking hands are a luxury and I had maybe hours.

Because here was the thing I now knew that Jonah didn't, the thing the torn page had held: the imprisoned thing wasn't waiting to be freed. It was waiting to be *fed*. The erasure wasn't a side effect of the bargain failing. It was the bargain working. Every name Greyharrow forgot was a meal, delivered piping hot through a lock the Guild had built and paid for with a whole country.

And Corvane Hale—proud, patient, immortality-adjacent Corvane—had just opened the kitchen door himself.

* * *

I found Jonah where I knew I'd find him, because I am a professional and professionals don't run to the obvious place, they merely arrive there faster than amateurs.

He was in the gatehouse, the little stone room where he'd spent eleven years being invisible, and he was tearing it apart—not ransacking, *searching*, drawers emptied onto the floor, ledgers stacked in towers, his father's annotations spread across the desk in a fan of yellowed pages. He looked up when I came in, and whatever was on my face stopped him mid-motion.

"Wren." Just my name. He said it carefully, like setting down something fragile, and I understood it was an apology offered in advance for everything he'd say next.

"Don't," I said. "Don't apologize yet. Listen first, because I only have time to say this once and you only have time to hear it once." I told him. The voice behind the door. The price schedule. The feeding. The way the erasure wasn't failure but function. He went gray around the mouth in the places I'd predicted and white in the places I hadn't.

"The forged half," he said finally. "Corvane has the forged half. Which means when he tries to use it—"

"It fails. And when it fails, he won't renegotiate. He'll escalate." I picked up one of his father's pages, the one with the annotation I'd memorized and he'd shown me before everything went wrong between us: *two who share memory across the erasure.* "Jonah. How long do we have?"

He looked at the window. Beyond it, Greyharrow's harbor lay under a sky the color of old pewter, and along the quay the streetlamps burned in broad daylight, and one by one, block by block, they were going out—not blown out, not extinguished, but *dimming*, the flames shrinking to embers to memory to nothing, and each lamp that died took a street's worth of names with it.

"Look at the far shore," he said quietly.

I looked. Across the water, the hills beyond Greyharrow—the ones I'd catalogued on arrival, three ridges, a ruined tower, a stand of pines—were smoothing out. Softening. Like a sketch someone was rubbing out with their thumb. By tomorrow there would be no hills there at all, and no one in the city would remember there had ever been anything there, and the sea road would curve for no reason anyone could name.

"That's the horizon going," Jonah said. "When it reaches us—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. We'd both seen Marta Ostwick's face. We'd both seen the child with no name.

"The chapel," I said. "Low tide."

"Low tide is at moonrise. Six hours." He was already moving, sweeping his father's pages into a leather satchel, and I noticed his hands were steady now, steadier than mine, and I hated him a little for it and loved him considerably more than a little, and neither of those facts was going to survive the next six hours unless we both did.

"Jonah." He paused, satchel over his shoulder, framed in the gatehouse doorway with the dying city behind him. "On the rooftops, in the bell tower—you told me things you believed were dreams. The brass bells. The green hills. Your mother's voice." My throat tightened. I made myself say the rest. "You'll need all of it. Every dream. Because the rebinding runs on shared memory, and mine are all from outside, and yours are the only ones from in."

"I know," he said. "I've known since the night I touched the threshold." And then, because he was still Jonah, still the man who hid truths inside false ledgers: "I should have told you about the clause. I told myself I was protecting you. That was half true. The other half was that I couldn't bear to watch you recalculate our odds and arrive at zero."

"Our odds aren't zero."

"No," he agreed. "They're worse. Zero is a number. What we have is a hope with teeth." He almost smiled. "Your phrase."

"Move," I said, and he moved.

* * *

We went out through the Guild's back ways, and that was when I understood how bad it truly was, because the back ways were empty. Not quiet—empty. The Guild's servants, its porters, its knife-eared enforcers, all of them simply absent, and in the scullery a pot of porridge burned on a fire no one remembered to tend. The Guild had traded in impossible things for two hundred years, and now the impossibility had come for its staff, and the building stood around its own emptiness like a coat with no one in it.

In the courtyard we nearly ran into the last of them: three enforcers, standing in a loose triangle around a fourth who sat on the cobbles, weeping, asking them who he was. And the enforcers—hard men, scarred men, men I'd fled across rooftops from—stood there helplessly, because none of them could remember either.

One of them saw us. Recognition flickered across his face and then, horribly, began to fade even as he reached for it, his hand rising, pointing, his mouth shaping the word *stop* and losing the word and then the shape and then the wanting-to-stop-us at all. He lowered his hand. He looked at us the way you look at weather.

We walked past four armed men of the Ashvale Guild, and they let us go, because forgetting had disarmed them more thoroughly than any blade could have.

"They're not all gone," Jonah murmured as we cleared the gates. "The erasure moves street by street. Lamp by lamp. It's following the ley of the bargain—old Guild ground first, then outward."

"Then we go outward. Toward the water." I checked the sky. Pewter, bruising darker at the seaward edge, and a wind coming up that tasted of storm. Of course. A tempest on the flats, racing an apocalypse. The Bureau would have called this a textbook recovery operation. The Bureau had never once left its offices.

"Six hours to low tide," Jonah said.

"Then we have six hours to cross a city that's forgetting itself, reach a drowned chapel, break a blood-and-memory lock, and rebind something the founders couldn't kill." I pulled my collar up against the wind. "I've had worse assignments. I can't, offhand, name one, but I'm told nostalgia improves with practice."

He laughed—short, startled, real—and for one moment, standing in a street where the lamps were dying and the names were going with them, it felt like the old days, the good days, the two days in Greyharrow when we'd been allies instead of casualties.

Then the clock struck.

Not the harbor clock. Not any clock I knew. It came from everywhere and nowhere, nineteen slow bronze strokes rolling across the city, and with each stroke another block of Greyharrow went dark—not lamp-dark, *memory*-dark, windows going blind, doors standing open on rooms no one owned anymore. Nineteen bells. The number Jonah had whispered to me in the bell tower with his childhood spilling out of him like light through a cracked door.

"What is nineteen?" I asked, though my stomach already knew it was bad.

"The count," Jonah said. His face had gone the color of the sea. "My father's notes. Nineteen bells is the last measure. When the nineteenth finishes ringing—" He swallowed. "It isn't a deadline, Wren. It's a countdown. And it just started."

Far off across the water, through the thickening storm, I could see the drowned chapel's reef breaking the swell—and beyond it, faint as breath on glass, hills that were not quite there, holding on.

Nineteen bells. Eighteen left.

We ran.
