## 18. Rebinding

I have faced down Bureau interrogators, forged my way into three sealed archives, and once talked a customs officer out of confiscating my mother's notebook by convincing him it was a book of recipes. None of it prepared me for standing in a drowned chapel with the sea pressing against the walls like a held breath, watching Jonah Eastwick decide whether to save the world or himself.

The bound thing beneath the threshold had made its offer. I'd heard it—not in sound, exactly, but in the way you hear your own name called from another room. *All of it back, keeper. Every year. Every name. Your childhood whole and warm again.* The water around us had gone still as glass, and in that glass I could see Jonah's face doing something terrible: wanting.

"Jonah." I said his name the way you'd throw a rope to someone going under.

He looked at me. And here is the thing about Jonah Eastwick that took me far too long to understand: he is not a brave man. He told me so himself, on the rooftops, with nineteen bells shaking the night apart around us. He is a careful man, a precise man, a man who has spent eleven years making himself small enough to fit through the cracks of the world unnoticed. Bravery was never in his inventory.

But he chose anyway.

"No," he said—to the thing, to Corvane, to the boy he had been in Ashvale before any of us knew there was an Ashvale. "No. I'm done trading."

* * *

Let me go back, because the moment deserves its proper weight, and because if I am to be the one who carries this story—as I now understand I must be—then I will carry all of it, even the parts that make my hands shake even now.

Corvane Hale arrived at the threshold minutes after we did, soaked to the bone, his immaculate Guildmaster's coat hanging off him like a flag of surrender. He came down the chapel steps with a lantern that guttered in the salt wind, and when he saw us standing before the door—the door out of Ashvale, the door that had been locked for three generations—he did not order the enforcers behind him to seize us.

He dismissed them.

"Go," he said, and they went, because whatever else Corvane Hale was, he was a man whose voice had been obeyed for forty years, and obedience dies hard. Then he stood there in the rising water, an old man in a ruined coat, and said the most honest sentence I ever heard from him:

"I've read the original contract four hundred times. Did you know that? Four hundred times, looking for a loophole. There isn't one. There never was."

"The rebinding," Jonah said. "My father's annotations—"

"—are correct," Corvane finished. "Which is why I'm here. Not to stop you." He laughed, and it was a horrible sound, dry as old paper. "To help. God help us all. To help."

The bound thing stirred beneath the threshold. I felt it the way you feel weather changing—in the teeth, in the old scar on my knee, in the pressure behind my eyes. It had been starving down there for three generations, fed only on the dregs of the founders' bargain, and it spoke to us in borrowed voices. My grandmother's lullaby, once. The tune every child in Greyharrow hums without knowing where they learned it. That was the cruelest thing it did, I think—using the songs the erasure left behind like bones washed up on a shore.

It offered Jonah everything. It offered me nothing, because I was not its keeper; I was only the outsider, the other half of the lock. One born inside the erasure, one born outside it—that was what my mother's marginalia said, what Jonah's father's annotations confirmed. Two memories braided across the wound. That was the thread. That was the binding.

And bindings, the contract made clear, require mortar.

* * *

We began with names.

That sounds small. It was not small. In a place where memory itself was the battlefield, a name spoken truly is a weapon, a key, and a prayer all at once. We stood at the threshold with the black water lapping at our knees and the door—a door of dark wood banded in brass, older than any wood should be—glowing faintly with the light of a country that no longer existed.

"My name is Wren Calloway," I said. "My grandmother sang me a song about green hills and brass bells, and she cried when she sang it, and she never knew why. I found her lullabies in a Bureau archive with the country's name cut out of every verse, and the cuts were clean, and the cleanest cuts are the ones that frighten me most."

Jonah's hand found mine. His palm was cold and steady.

"My name is Jonah Eastwick," he said. "I was born in Ashvale, in a house on Bellmaker's Row, and my mother hung sheets on the line on Thursdays and let me believe the wind did all the work. I remember the smell of bread from the ovens under the clock tower. I remember my father teaching me letters using ledger entries, because he was a guildsman even then, even before—" His voice caught. "Even before he understood what the Guild had done. I thought those memories were dreams. I built my whole life on top of them like a man builds a house over a well, and I never once looked down."

The door brightened. The thing beneath us made a sound—not a word, not yet, but interest. Hunger turning its attention toward us like a great eye.

"Again," I whispered. "Closer to the seam."

Because that was the craft of it, once we understood the shape: the erasure was a seam in the world, badly sewn, and every true memory spoken across it—one from inside, one from outside, each answering the other—was a stitch. My mother's marginalia had been explicit about this, in her cramped sideways handwriting: *A binding is not a wall. A binding is a weaving. What is woven can hold without crushing.*

So we wove.

"You sang that song wrong," Jonah said suddenly, and laughed—a wet, broken laugh. "Grandmother Hartwell. She wasn't my grandmother, but everyone in Ashvale called her that. You sang it the way the exiles' children sing it, with the third verse dropped. The third verse is about the sea taking something back."

"The sea takes back what was given," I recited, and my skin prickled, because I had never known the words until they were in my mouth. "The sea keeps what was loved."

"That's it. That's the verse." He pressed my hand hard enough to hurt. "How do you know that?"

"I don't know. I just know it." And that was the strangest grace of that night: the memories were not only ours. They lived in songs and scars and superstitions, in the way Greyharrow sailors refuse to whistle at low tide, in the brass bell on the customs house that rings once a year on a date no one can explain. The erasure had cut the country out of the records, but it could not cut it out of the people entirely. People are not ledgers. We keep what we love in places no contract can reach.

Stitch by stitch, the seam began to close. I could feel it—not see it, exactly, but feel it, the way you feel a headache lifting. The pressure behind my eyes eased. The water around our knees warmed by a degree, two degrees, impossible and undeniable.

And the thing below us grew quieter. Not gone. Quieter. Fed, at last, on something other than neglect.

* * *

Then Corvane stepped forward, and everything changed.

"The mortar," he said. His voice was steady. His hands were not. "Your father's notes are specific, Eastwick. The binding needs mortar. Memory given freely, knowing it will not come back. Knowing it is spent." He looked at Jonah. "You're the keeper. If you give yours, you'll have nothing left to stand inside Ashvale with. You'll open the door onto your own home and not recognize a single street."

"I know," Jonah said quietly. "I've known since the vault."

"And you were going to tell her?" Corvane glanced at me, and there was something almost gentle in it. "Or were you going to carry that alone too? Like the imprisonment clause?"

"Corvane," I warned.

"No. Let him answer." But Jonah didn't flinch. He just looked at me with those careful, ledger-keeper's eyes and said, "I was going to find a way around it first. And then I was going to tell you. I've learned my lesson about half-truths, Wren. Mostly."

"Mostly," I repeated, and despite everything—the drowning chapel, the starving god beneath us, the end of the world arriving street by street in Greyharrow above—I almost smiled. "We'll work on it."

"The mortar doesn't have to be yours," Corvane said.

And then he told us what he had come to the chapel already knowing he would do.

He was not a young man. He had spent sixty years keeping the bargain, feeding the erasure its increments, telling himself every morning that it was necessary, that safety was worth any silence, that the founders had been wise and he was merely their faithful steward. Four hundred readings of the contract had finally shown him the truth he'd been avoiding: that he was not the steward of a necessary evil but the jailer of a mistake, and that the mistake was growing hungry, and that hunger always, eventually, comes up the stairs.

"I gave this bargain the best years of my life," he said. "My wife left me because I couldn't tell her what I did all day. My daughter thinks I'm a tax collector. I have memorized a country I helped erase, and I am the last living soul who knows all nineteen verses of the founding charter by heart." He smiled, and for one moment I saw the man he might have been in a kinder life. "Let it be good for something. Let me be the mortar."

"You don't have to—" Jonah started.

"Yes, I do. That's rather the point of freely given." Corvane rolled up his sleeves, which was such an absurdly bureaucratic gesture for the edge of oblivion that I nearly laughed, and I think that was intentional. He was giving us permission to survive this lightly. Even now, even then. "Besides, Eastwick. Someone has to stay behind and balance the books."

He put his hands flat against the door.

What followed, I will set down as plainly as I can, because plainness is all I have to offer him now.

Memory does not leave dramatically. It leaves the way tide leaves a shore—so gradually you don't notice the waterline moving until the sand is bare. Corvane began to speak, and as he spoke, he unwove himself. The founding charter, all nineteen verses, recited in a clear strong voice while his face grew younger and vaguer at once, like a portrait being repainted over. The names of the founders. The terms of the bargain. The taste of the wine served at his induction, forty years ago. His wedding day—I heard him describe it, the rain, the borrowed hall, his bride's blue shoes—and then I watched him pause, mid-sentence, and frown slightly, as though reaching for a word on a high shelf.

"What was I saying?" he asked pleasantly.

"The wedding," Jonah said, his voice wrecked. "Yours."

"Was I married?" Corvane considered this. "I think perhaps I was. How strange. It feels like a story someone told me." He kept his hands on the door. He kept talking. The charter. The seals. The counter-seals. The things that must never be forgotten, poured into the seam like molten lead into a lock's guts, hardening around the stitches we had made, holding them fast.

The door blazed. The water around us turned gold. And the thing beneath the threshold—for the first time in three generations—was quiet. Not sleeping. Bound. Held in a weaving of shared memory and spent memory, comfortable at last, the way a scar is quiet.

When Corvane finally stopped speaking, he was an old man who could not remember being old. He looked at his hands in the golden water with mild curiosity, then at us.

"Are you the archivists?" he asked. "I seem to have lost my lantern."

"You did," Jonah said. He took off his own coat and put it around Corvane's shoulders. "We'll take you home."

"Kind of you." Corvane smiled at the blazing door with the vague pleasure of a man looking at a nice fire. "Lovely door. Is it new?"

And that was the price. That was the mortar. He gave everything, including the knowing that he had given anything, and the last thing he surrendered was the grief of surrendering.

I am crying as I write this. I want that recorded too. Wren Calloway, investigator for the Bureau of Forgotten Places, cries at the fall of Corvane Hale, and does not care who knows it.

* * *

The door opened.

Not burst, not swung wide in a rush of trumpets—it opened the way a house opens when someone inside has been waiting up for you: a crack of warm light, then the smell of bread, then the sound.

Bells. Brass bells, hundreds of them, ringing across green hills in a pattern I had known my whole life without knowing it, the pattern from the third verse, the pattern my grandmother cried to. The threshold filled with light the color of late afternoon, and beyond it—beyond it was a city on a hill, terraced and bright, with laundry on the lines and smoke from the ovens under the clock tower and people in the streets who turned as one to look at the door that had been shut for three generations.

They had kept the bells oiled. All this time, in a country that did not exist, someone had kept the bells oiled.

Jonah stood at the threshold and made a sound I will never repeat to anyone, because some griefs and joys are private even when witnessed. His home. Whole. Waiting. Every street he'd built his life on top of, opened at last, the well not a grave but a spring.

"Come on, then," he said, and his hand was shaking in mine. "Before I lose my nerve. I'm told I'm not brave."

"You're not," I agreed. "You're something better. You're stubborn."

We stepped through together.

The grass of Ashvale was real under my boots. The air tasted of bread and rain and something floral I had no name for. An old woman at the head of the crowd pressed her hand to her mouth and said, "Hartwell's boy," in a voice like a bell itself, and Jonah made that sound again.

Behind us, the door stayed open. Warm wind flowed both ways through it, carrying the smell of Greyharrow's salt into Ashvale and Ashvale's bells out to the sea. It should not have stayed open. Bindings close. Doors seal. That is their nature.

But this one didn't.

And as I stood there in the country that remembered, holding the hand of the man who had traded his whole safe invisible life for this, I felt the truth settle over me like a coat: the door was open because it was meant to stay open, and meant to stay open meant guarded, and guarded meant—

"Jonah," I said slowly. "Why is the door still open?"

He turned. And I saw in his face that he already knew—or suspected—or was, in that stubborn, unbrave, extraordinary way of his, beginning to count the cost of a ledger entry he hadn't written yet.

"Because," he said, "someone has to keep it."
