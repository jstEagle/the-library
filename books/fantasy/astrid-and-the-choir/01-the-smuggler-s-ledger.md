## 1. The Smuggler's Ledger

The ledger lived in August Blackwood's head, and it never balanced.

She kept the accounts anyway, out of habit, out of the same stubbornness that kept her counting steps on the long climb to the Kettle Pass — four hundred and eleven from the shepherd's cairn to the wind-scoured saddle, four hundred and twelve if the snow was soft. *Two bolts of Thornfell wool, promised to the dyer at Harrow's Ford. One crate of southern wine for the innkeeper at Millbrook, who paid in coin that was more honest than he was. One parcel, unopened, unasked-about, from a man in the lowlands who had paid triple the going rate and asked no questions either.*

That last one was the problem. August had learned, in seven years of walking the high roads, that the parcels nobody asked about were the ones that came back to bite you. The wine was just wine. The wool was just wool. But a man who paid triple and said *don't look inside* was a man who understood exactly what he was buying: not her mule, not her knowledge of the passes, but her silence.

She had a great deal of silence to sell. It was, in fact, the only thing Ysolde had left her.

August shifted the pack on her shoulders and paused at the saddle of the pass to let the wind have its say. It always did, up here. Thornfell's wind was an old gossip, full of voices it had stolen from the valleys below — a dog barking in Millbrook, a woman calling a child home, the far-off groan of the sawmills. Once, when she was younger and stupider, she had tried to answer it. She remembered the way the wind had carried her voice for three miles, remembered Ysolde's face when she'd come back to the cottage flushed and laughing, and remembered the sound of the door bolting from the inside.

*You do not spend the Gift on trifles,* Ysolde had said through the wood. *You do not spend it at all, if you can help it. Every working is a signature. Every signature is a name written in a book that other people read.*

August had been nineteen and had thought the book was a metaphor.

She was twenty-six now, and she knew better. Somewhere in the capital there were clerks who did nothing but read signatures — in the residue of workings, in the traces left on the air like footprints in snow. They had titles and stipends and, she'd heard, a new name since the winter: Inquisitors. She had never met one. She intended to die without meeting one.

She came down the southern face of the pass as the light went long and copper, and by full dark she had reached the waystation — a shepherd's hut, dry-walled and turf-roofed, that she'd bought from a dead man's estate and kept alive through the simple expedient of never being in it. The parcel rode in her pack. She set the pack down inside the door and then, for a long moment, stood with her hand on the canvas, not opening it.

The trouble was that she could feel it. Not the contents — she'd warded herself against that, a small dull working, the magical equivalent of pulling a hood up — but the *weight* of it. Some things carried weight the way a bell carried sound. This parcel hummed against her senses like a struck tuning fork, and the note it held was one she knew in her bones, the way you knew your own mother's voice.

It was Gift. Wrapped in sacking and waxed canvas, packed probably among dye-stuffs or dried fish, there was something — or someone's something — that rang with the same frequency as the thing coiled behind August's own sternum.

*Don't open it,* said seven years of survival. *Weigh it, carry it, deliver it, forget it.*

But she had counted the steps up the pass, and she had counted the years since the cottage, and she had counted every night she'd lain awake wondering if Ysolde was alive, and the arithmetic of her life had come out to a number she could not bear to look at directly. She was so tired of not looking at things.

August cut the wax, unwrapped the canvas, and folded back the sacking.

A boy looked up at her from inside the parcel.

Not a boy, quite — young man, seventeen maybe, all elbows and terror, curled into a space no larger than a wine crate, his knees to his chin, his eyes so wide that in the lamplight they seemed to be all pupil. He had a musician's hands, long-fingered and callused at the tips, and they were shaking. There was a cloth gag in his mouth. When he saw her face, he made a sound against it — not a scream, worse than a scream, the small hopeless noise of someone who has been in the dark so long that light itself has become a threat.

August's hands went cold. She reached in and pulled the gag down, gently, the way you'd gentle a snared bird.

"Water first," she said, because her voice would shake otherwise. "Then you tell me who packed you, and I'll decide whether to be angry with them or grateful."

He drank from her flask like a man who had forgotten water existed. Then he said, in a voice sanded raw: "You're not them. You're not — they said a guide. They said a guide over the passes, they didn't say—" His breath caught. "They said you'd know. They said you'd *know* what I am."

August closed her eyes. The ledger in her head, which had never once balanced, tipped over entirely.

"I know what you are," she said.

* * *

His name was Tam Voss, and he was a singer — a real one, the kind the lowland temples used to train, though the temples had stopped training anyone like him the moment the edicts started. His Gift was in his voice. He could hold a note and make it do things notes shouldn't do: carry a lullaby through a stone wall, calm a spooked horse at forty paces, once — he told her this in a whisper, ashamed of it, the way you'd confess a crime — stop a fist in mid-swing by singing the anger out of the arm that threw it.

"They found out in Millbrook," he said. He was wrapped in her spare blanket now, sitting as close to the little stove as the stove would allow. "My — the woman I sang for. Her husband heard. He went to the reeve. Not because he hated me. Because he was *frightened*, and they've taught everyone that frightened people should report things." Tam stared at the fire. "They came at dusk. I went out the wash-house window with nothing but my voice and the clothes I was wearing, and a man I'd never met put me in a box and paid a smuggler to move me north, and the smuggler put me in a *smaller* box, and now I'm here."

"Here is the worst place you could be," August said, before she could stop herself.

Tam looked at her. He had the particular stillness of the very frightened, the stillness of a rabbit deciding which way to run, and August recognized it because she saw it in her own reflection every morning.

"Why?" he said. "Because of the soldiers?"

August went very still. "What soldiers?"

"The ones behind me." Tam's voice dropped. "There were three of them on the lowland road, in grey cloaks without any markings. They stopped the carter I was with. They didn't search anything. One of them just stood there with his eyes shut, like a man listening to music, and then they let the carter go and started asking about the passes. Which guide. Which route." He swallowed. "That was four days ago."

Four days. August did the arithmetic without wanting to. Four days at a carter's pace, with a detour to throw off pursuit, meant they could be a day behind her. Maybe less. Maybe they had already reached Millbrook Cross and were asking the innkeeper who paid in dishonest coin, and the innkeeper — who liked her, who genuinely liked her — would tell them what he knew, because frightened people report things.

She should have opened the parcel on the far side of the pass. She should have opened it nowhere at all. Every choice she'd made in the last day was a signature, and somewhere behind her, someone was reading.

"Right," August said, and heard her own voice go flat and practical, the smuggler's voice, the voice she'd built over the voice that wanted to scream. "Here's what happens now. Tomorrow, before first light, we go up. Not the Kettle — the Kettle's the obvious route and obvious routes are where grey cloaks wait. We take the Shepherd's Error, which is not a route so much as a rumor with scree on it. It's ugly and it's slow and it will hurt, and at the top of it there's a valley that doesn't exist on any crown map, and at the bottom of that valley—" She stopped. She had been about to say *safety*, and she did not know what was at the bottom of that valley, because she had never been desperate enough to go there.

"At the bottom of that valley," she said instead, "there are people."

"What people?"

"People like us." The words came out of her like a splinter coming out of a wound — painful, and leaving her strangely lighter. Seven years she had not said *us*. Seven years she had kept herself a singular, sealed, unremarkable woman who moved wool and wine over mountains. "I don't know them. I know of them. Ysolde—" The name caught. She hadn't said her mentor's name aloud since the night she'd fled, and it came out rusty, like a gate hinge in winter. "Someone told me about them once. A long time ago. I never wanted to find them."

"Why not?"

Because finding them would mean admitting what she was. Because belonging to a thing meant the thing could be taken from you, and she had already had one family taken, and she had done the taking herself. Because Ysolde had taught her that the only safe number was one.

"Because I'm a coward," August said, "and I've been very well paid for it."

Tam almost smiled. It was a terrible, trembling thing, but it was a smile. "I sang at a wedding once," he said, "where the groom's mother stood up in the middle of the feast and said, *love is just fear that's found somewhere to live.* Everyone laughed. I never understood it until now."

August fed the stove another split of pine and did not answer, because she was thinking about the ledger again, and how the entries had changed. *One boy, one voice, one debt she had not agreed to and could not put down.* Somewhere in her chest, behind the coiled and sleeping Gift, something that had been frozen for seven years shifted its weight.

* * *

They were in the saddle of the Shepherd's Error when the wind changed its gossip.

August heard it first as wrongness — the wind's stolen voices had gone quiet, all of them at once, the way birds go quiet before a hawk. She stopped so abruptly that Tam walked into her back. Below them, maybe half a mile down the switchbacks, where the trail bent around a shoulder of black rock, there were men. Four of them. Grey cloaks, no markings, and one of them — the one in front — had his eyes shut and his face tilted up toward the mountain like a man tasting the air.

"Down," August breathed, and pulled Tam into the shadow of the cairn.

"They've found the trail," Tam whispered. "How—"

"Because I opened the parcel." Her own voice was ice in her ears. "Because I used a warding on the hut. Because I've been leaving signatures on this mountain for seven years like a dog marking territory, and I told myself it didn't count because nobody was reading." She pressed her back against the stone and made herself breathe. Below, the men had stopped at the switchback's turn. The one with the closed eyes was turning his head slowly, slowly, a compass needle finding north. "Tam. Listen to me. When I say run, you run up. Don't look back. Don't wait for me. There's a cleft in the rock two hundred paces above us, on the left — you can't miss it, it looks like a door. You get in it and you stay there until dark."

"What are you going to do?"

What was she going to do. The ledger tipped again, and this time she let it fall all the way open. *One boy. One voice. One life, entered against her own.*

"Buy you the two hundred paces," August said.

She stood up out of the shadow before she could think better of it — thinking was a luxury, and she had spent her whole life on luxuries of that kind — and she did the thing she had not done in seven years, the thing Ysolde had bolted the door to stop her doing, the thing that every cold and careful day since had been built to prevent.

She spent the Gift.

She did not fling fire; she had no fire. What she had was what she had always had, the thing Ysolde had called *small and strange and worse than useless* — the Gift of turning, of persuading, of asking the world to be briefly other than it was. She asked the wind to carry her voice down the switchbacks in someone else's voice, a woman's voice, a voice crying for help from the scree field on the far side of the valley. She asked the loose stone on the lower trail to shift, just so, a whisper of sound like boots on gravel going the wrong way. She asked the light to bend, a little, a hand's-breadth, around the cairn where Tam crouched.

Small workings. Three of them, quick as heartbeats. And she felt each one leave her like a coin leaving a purse, and she felt them *ring* — she felt the signature go out of her into the world like a bell struck in a cathedral, unmistakable, unrepeatable, a name written in a book that other people read.

The man with the closed eyes opened them.

He did not look at the scree field. He did not look at the false footsteps. He looked up — straight up, through half a mile of cold thin air, past the switchbacks and the cairn and the bending light — and he looked at August.

He smiled.

It was, she would remember afterward, the worst thing about it: he smiled at her the way you smile at a name you've been hoping to find. He said something to the others, and they turned together, unhurried, and began to climb.

"Run," August said, and Tam ran.

She ran too, up and up, the scree sliding under her boots, her lungs full of knives, the four grey figures below her moving with the terrible patience of people who did not need to hurry because they knew something she didn't. The cleft in the rock came — a black door in the mountainside, exactly as promised — and she shoved Tam through it and followed, and then, in the dark, with the wind's gossip gone utterly silent and her own Gift still ringing in her bones like a struck bell that would not stop, August Blackwood understood, with a clarity that felt almost like relief, that the ledger had finally come due.

The last thing she heard, before she pulled the darkness of the cleft around them both and began to climb toward a valley that did not exist, was the wind finding its voice again — and in it, faint and stolen and unmistakable, a stranger's voice saying her name.

*August Blackwood.*

They knew who she was. And they were not hurrying, because they already knew where she was going.
