## 2. Proclamation at Millbrook Cross

The hammering woke her before the light did.

August Blackwood lay in the narrow bed above the cooper's old shop, listening to iron on oak, iron on oak, a rhythm too deliberate for any honest carpenter. Three doors down, then two, then one. She knew that cadence from her years on the passes — the sound of men doing a job they meant to finish before anyone could argue.

She was out of bed and at the window with frost still in her hair when the first grey light showed her what they were nailing to the doors of Millbrook Cross.

Proclamations. A dozen of them, white as shrouds against the black timber, each one stamped with the crowned seal she'd spent half her life learning not to look at directly. Soldiers moved door to door in pairs, efficient and unhurried, the way men move when they are certain no one will stop them. Behind them walked a clerk with ink-stained fingers and a ledger under his arm, reading aloud at every threshold in a voice pitched to carry.

*By order of His Majesty, be it known throughout the realm: the Gift is a plague upon the body politic. Its bearers are infected vessels. They shall be identified, registered, and culled.*

*Culled.* The word went through her like a fishhook through soft flesh. Not *punished*. Not even *executed*. Culled — the word you used for sick lambs, for blighted trees, for things that had never been persons at all.

August stood very still at the window, breath fogging the glass, and did the arithmetic she had been doing all night since the soldiers' campfires appeared on the south road: how long to pack, which roads were watched, whether the deep ways over Thornfell would take her weight in early winter. She was good at arithmetic. It was the other thing — the thing Ysolde had called *looking at what is* — that she could not make herself do yet.

Because down in the square, old Marta Hedley had come out of the bakery in her flour-dusted apron, and she was reading the proclamation nailed to her own door.

* * *

Marta Hedley had lived in Millbrook Cross longer than August had been alive. She had pulled August out of the millpond when August was seven and foolish and showing off for the Tanner boys, had smacked her twice on the back until the water came up, then wrapped her in a blanket smelling of yeast and cinnamon and said, *Well, that was stupid, wasn't it,* and given her a bun anyway. She had never once asked why August sometimes made the bread rise wrong in her presence, or why the hedgerow flowers turned their heads after her like sunflowers after the sun. Millbrook Cross was a village that understood some questions were better left unasked, and Marta was its soul in that respect.

Now Marta stood in the square in her apron, reading, and August watched her shoulders change.

It was a small thing. A settling, like a house shifting on its foundations. But August had spent eleven years reading bodies on mountain trails — knowing from the set of a shoulder which man would try something, from the angle of a hip which mule would bolt — and she read Marta's shoulders now and felt sickness open in her stomach like a wound.

Because Marta had the Gift. Everyone had always known, and no one had ever said. She kept the bakery ovens at a perfect even heat by something that wasn't skill alone; her loaves never burned, her pastry never cracked, and once, when the fever came through and took three children, Marta had sat all night in the Hedley kitchen with her hands hovering over small sweating brows, and the fever had broken by morning, and everyone had agreed loudly that it was the will of the Saints and a good deal of willow bark.

That was the bargain Millbrook Cross had lived by for forty years: we see nothing, you keep us whole. It was a bargain written nowhere and kept everywhere, the kind of covenant that holds villages together better than law.

The clerk reached Marta's door and began to read again, louder, as though she were deaf rather than frozen. And Marta — Marta looked up from the paper and across the square, straight at the crowd gathering at the edges of it, at the faces she had fed for four decades, and August saw her waiting. Saw her actually wait, one heartbeat, two, for someone — anyone — to say *no*. To say *she's ours*. To say anything at all.

Nobody said anything.

The baker's son-in-law looked at his boots. Widow Crane drew her shawl tighter and studied the church eaves. Tammas Pike, who owed Marta more than he'd ever admit, who'd eaten free at her counter through two bad harvests, developed a sudden fascination with the mud on his shoes. Forty years of unasked questions curdled, all at once, into forty years of convenient silence.

A sergeant took Marta's arm. Gently, even — that was the worst part, the gentleness, the careful way he steered her toward the cart as though she were elderly and frail rather than sixty and furious — and Marta went, because there were six soldiers and one of her, and because her son-in-law would not meet her eyes.

At the cart's tailboard she stopped. Turned. Looked out over the square one last time, and August, pressed back from the window with her heart slamming, would swear until her dying day that Marta's gaze found hers through the glass, through the grey morning, through everything — and that the old woman did not look betrayed. She looked *sorry*. Sorry for August, sorry for the village, sorry for the whole rotten arrangement of the world.

Then the cart rolled south, and Millbrook Cross began, immediately and loudly, to go about its business.

* * *

They talked about it all day, of course. That was the horror of it — how fast the talking started, how relieved everyone sounded. In the taproom of the Drowned Lamb, where August went at noon because a smuggler who suddenly stops buying ale draws more notice than one who keeps her habits, the talk ran warm and quick as meltwater.

"It's a plague, that's what the edict says, plague," said Tammas Pike, who had eaten those free loaves. "Plague's nobody's fault. You can't blame a body for catching a sickness."

"Can't you?" said Widow Crane. "My cousin in Harrowdale says they burn them in Harrowdale. Says it's cleaner."

"Cleaner," repeated the smith, and spat.

"I'm only saying what I heard."

August sat in the corner with a pot of small beer and let the words wash over her, cataloguing faces the way she catalogued trail hazards: which ones watched her too closely, which ones avoided looking at her altogether. Old Fenner the ferryman caught her eye once, held it a moment too long, and looked away first. He'd carried her contraband twice. He knew what she was — or half-knew, which was worse, because half-knowledge makes men inventive.

Eleven years she had lived here. Eleven years since she'd run from Ysolde's cottage in the high fells with a satchel, a scarred left palm, and a hatred so pure she'd thought it would keep her warm forever. Eleven years of smuggling brandy and untaxed wool and occasionally, quietly, healing a child or turning a flood, and telling herself she was no witch, just a woman with a knack, just a trick of the blood, nothing the crown need ever trouble itself over.

And in one grey morning, the crown had troubled itself over everyone.

Here was the thing August could not stop circling, the stone in her shoe all day: she had seen this coming. Not the edict itself, perhaps, but the weather of it. Three months ago a tinker had shown her a broadsheet from the capital, woodcut of a burning woman and verse beneath about cleansing fires, and August had read it twice and bought nothing and told herself the capital loved its spectacles. Two months ago the tithe-collectors had begun asking at every market for *names*, friendly-like, names of odd folk, uncanny folk, and August had heard herself give none and had still not packed so much as a spare shirt. A month ago Ysolde's letters had stopped coming — the little quarterly notes, unsigned, disguised as invoices from a wool factor, the only thread between them since the running-away — and August had told herself the old woman was simply dead at last, or done with her, and had drunk enough to sleep and called it grief and let it lie.

She had watched the flood come for a season and built no ark, because building an ark meant admitting the water was real, and admitting the water was real meant admitting that Ysolde had been right about everything, and August would sooner have drowned.

* * *

Dark came early that time of year. By full dark the village had shuttered itself, and August climbed the stairs to her rooms above the cooper's shop and did not light a lamp.

Her kit stood ready by habit — smuggler's discipline, Ysolde's discipline really, *pack as if tonight is the night, girl, because someday it will be* — but the room held one thing the kit could not hold, and she knelt at the floorboards under the bed and lifted the loose plank with fingers that shook only a little.

The letters.

Forty-one of them, tied in bundles by year, each one in Ysolde's cramped sideways hand, each one disguised as trade correspondence. *Regarding the autumn fleece, hold back the dark lots, they fetch better in the north country.* Which meant: trust your instincts about people, the gift shows truest in the dark. *Do not send payment for the spring order; the account stands settled.* Which meant: stop paying for what I taught you with guilt; the debt was mine to pay, not yours. Eleven years of a love neither of them had ever managed to say in plain words, ciphered into wool prices, and August had hoarded them like a dragon and read them maybe ten times and answered none.

The last bundle held only three letters, and the last of those was eight months old, and its final line had frightened her so badly she'd burned nothing else for weeks afterward, unable to touch flame near paper:

*If the roads close, girl, remember the monastery at Candlemere keeps a candle lit for wanderers. Old debts are honored there. Go north, and do not grieve me until there is a body to grieve.*

She had read that line a hundred times and understood none of it. Candlemere was a ruin; everyone knew the monastery had been abandoned a century past. What candle? What debts? What had the old woman gotten herself into?

Tonight, sitting on the cold floor with the letters spread around her like a hand of losing cards, August finally admitted what the line was. It was a map. It was Ysolde reaching forward through eight silent months to leave a door open behind her, the way she'd done everything — sideways, ciphered, never saying the plain thing even at the end. *Go north.* And August had read it and felt only anger, because going north meant following, meant forgiving, meant kneeling at the feet of the woman who had worked her half to death and praised her like a mule and never once—

The knock came at the street door, soft, twice.

August went cold from scalp to sole. She gathered the letters with both arms, all forty-one, an armful of paper worth more than the kit and the mule and eleven years of work combined, and stood in the dark listening. The knock came again. Then a voice, low, Fenner's voice: "Miss Blackwood. It's Dan Fenner. Alone. I'd take it kindly if you'd hear me out."

She stood a long moment. Then she crossed to the window shutter and put her eye to the crack, and there was indeed only Fenner, hat in hands, hunched against the cold, glancing up and down the empty lane.

She opened the door a hand's width. "Quickly, then."

"They took Marta to the crossroads gaol," Fenner said, without preamble, keeping his voice under the wind. "Cart goes south at dawn with the assize men. And there's a list, miss. Clerk's got a list, and I seen it over his shoulder while he was wetting his pen." He swallowed. "Your name's on it. Second page. *Blackwood, A., hedge-craft suspected.* Suspected." He almost laughed, horribly. "They don't even know. They just wrote you down because you keep to yourself and your mule doesn't spook proper."

"Who else knows you've told me?"

"No one. And no one will. You pulled my boy from the ice on the Fenn, miss, winter before last, and I never said a word to a living soul, and I'm not saying one now." He set his hat back on his head, and for a moment the old ferryman looked at her with something raw and steady in his face. "South road's watched. North road's watched. But you know the deep ways better than any of them, and I've a skiff at the weir, if a woman wanted to be on the far side of the Fenn before light."

"Fenner—"

"Don't thank me. Just don't get took." He touched his brim and was gone into the dark, footsteps fading fast, a man eager to be able to say honestly that he'd seen nothing.

August closed the door and leaned against it, and for a moment she allowed herself to feel all of it at once — the loss of Marta going south in a cart, the loss of Fenner's kindness, the loss of the whole quiet covenant of the village, the forty-year bargain broken in a single morning by a piece of paper and a hammer. Home didn't shatter like glass. That was the thing no one told you. It shattered like ice on a spring river, all at once and silently, and afterward you couldn't point to the moment it broke, only float in the pieces and know the crossing was gone.

Then she knelt, and struck flint to the cold hearth, and fed the letters to the fire one at all except the last.

One at a time, that is — all except the last. She burned the wool-prices and the guilt-forgivings and the gruff ciphered tenderness of eleven years, and the fire ate them greedily, and she watched every page go because it seemed like cowardice not to. When only the final letter remained, the one with Candlemere in it, she held it over the flames a long moment — and then folded it instead, small as a coin, and pushed it deep into the lining of her coat, over her heart, where a smuggler keeps the things she cannot afford to lose and cannot afford to be found carrying.

Let the crown find ash in her hearth. Let them find a cold room and a gone woman. But the one letter, the map, the open door — that she would carry north, into the mountains, into whatever Ysolde had tangled herself in.

She packed by feel in the dark, the way she'd been taught: rope, flint, dried meat, the good knife, the pass-ledger committed to memory and the paper copy burned with the rest. She left the mule loose in the paddock with the gate open — a kindness to the animal and a false trail for the soldiers both — and went out the back and down through the sleeping village, past the bakery with its cold ovens and its proclamation nailed white on the door, past the crossroads gaol where a single lantern burned and, behind its small barred window, an old woman's shadow sat unbowed.

August stopped there, just for a moment, in the lee of the wall. "I'll remember," she whispered, not knowing if the Gift worked through walls, not caring. "Whatever I can do, Marta. I'll remember."

The shadow did not move. But as August turned away, the lantern flame in the gaol window stood up straight and tall, burning bright and unwavering against the wind, like a held note, like an answer — and stayed that way until she was out of sight.

The weir lay a mile north. Fenner's skiff waited in the reeds, bailed and ready, and beyond the river the fells rose black against the stars, and beyond the fells the deep roads climbed into Thornfell, into the teeth of winter, toward a ruined monastery where an old debt might or might not be honored.

August stepped into the boat and pushed off, and did not look back at the village. Looking back was for people who believed they might return.

Behind her, Millbrook Cross slept on, dreaming its comfortable dreams, having sold its oldest neighbor for a piece of paper and called it safety. Ahead, somewhere in the dark of the high passes, something was already moving — she could feel it the way she felt weather in her scars, a pressure, a watching, a patience — as though the mountains themselves had marked her crossing the water, and were counting.

The current took the skiff. August bent to the oars, and rowed north.
