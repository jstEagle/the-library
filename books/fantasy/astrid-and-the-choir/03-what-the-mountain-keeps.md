## 3. What the Mountain Keeps

The blizzard came down the Thornfell passes like a debt collector.

August had named it that in her head somewhere past the second ridge, because there was no other way to think about a wall of white that wanted something from her and would not leave until it was paid. It took her warmth first, then her sense of direction, then — by the time the light went gray and shapeless around midday, if it even was midday — her certainty that she had ever known where she was going at all.

She had climbed out of Millbrook with nothing but a satchel, Ysolde's letters burned to ash in her hearth, and one unburned page folded against her heart like a splinter she couldn't dig out. *Candlemere*, the page said, in Ysolde's cramped hand, and beneath it a string of directions written as riddles: *where the river forgets its name; under the bell that no longer rings; follow the cold star down.* August had read it forty times on the road north and understood none of it, and so she did what smugglers do when maps fail them: she trusted the mountain instead of the map.

The mountain, in return, tried to kill her.

It wasn't personal. That was one of the first lessons Thornfell taught anyone who worked the passes — the mountain didn't hate you, didn't want you, didn't notice you at all. It simply kept what fell into it. She'd found things over the years, hauling contraband through the high country: a rusted lantern, a boot with a foot still in it, once an entire mule frozen upright as if it had merely paused to consider the view and been kept. The mountain kept what it caught, and it caught everything eventually, and today it was working hard to catch her.

Snow came sideways, then upward, then in great slow spirals that seemed to have opinions about her course. The wind screamed through the pass with a note almost human, almost words, and August pulled her scarf over her mouth and nose and thought about the soldiers behind her.

Three days back, maybe four — time had gone soft since Millbrook — she'd seen riders on the southern road, six of them, moving fast for men in mail. They hadn't seen her. But they were following the same logic she was: hunted things run for the mountains, so hunt the mountains. The proclamation had been nailed to every door in every village from here to the coast, and August could recite it now in her sleep, in the voice of the crier who'd read it at Millbrook Cross while Tam's neighbor — no, not Tam's, *her* neighbor, old Marta Hale who'd sold her eggs and gossip in equal measure — was dragged past with her wrists bound and her mouth still insisting, right up until they gagged her, that there had been some mistake.

*The Gift is a plague. Its bearers will be culled.*

Plague. As if magic were something you caught. As if you could burn the sick and be well again.

August stumbled, caught herself on a hand of rock buried under snow, and kept moving. Her fingers had stopped hurting, which was bad. Pain meant blood still bothered to visit. She counted her steps the way her mentor had taught her — *count, girl, counting is a rope and you hold the end of it* — though Ysolde had never specified what happened when you reached the other end of the rope.

Ysolde. Even now, three years after the woman walked out of August's life without a goodbye, the name arrived like weather. August had spent those three years telling herself a story about it: that Ysolde had grown tired of teaching, that hedge-witches wandered, that it was nothing. Then the letters, then the burning, then the page she'd saved, and the story had curdled into something worse — that Ysolde had left because leaving was safer than staying, and that August had never mattered enough to be taken along or warned or anything but abandoned.

She hated how much of her strength came from being angry about it. Anger was warm. Anger got you up the next ridge.

The shrine appeared out of the white all at once, the way the mountain gave up its secrets — no warning, just suddenly *there*, close enough that she nearly walked into its wall. A low stone structure hunched against the cliff face, older than any kingdom, its roof furred with ice. Travelers' shrines dotted the high passes; smugglers used them as waypoints, thieves as caches, pilgrims as proof that someone, sometime, had believed the mountains could be asked for mercy rather than merely endured.

August pushed through the door — half off its leather hinges — and the wind cut off like a slammed door behind her.

Inside: dark, dry-ish, and small. A niche in the far wall held a figure worn featureless by centuries of drafts, some god whose name the mountain had also kept. Someone had left offerings recently — a stub of candle, a twist of dried meat, a copper coin green with age. August stared at the candle with a longing that felt obscene.

She should keep moving. Every hour in one place was an hour the riders gained. But her legs had begun shaking in a way that had nothing to do with fear, and her hands — she looked at them, flexed them, watched them obey slowly, like servants woken from deep sleep. Frost had whitened the tips of two fingers.

"Right," she said aloud, and her voice sounded strange in the small dark, the first voice but hers in days. "Not today."

She got the candle lit with hands she had to press flat against her chest first to wake them. The flame threw her shadow huge against the walls, and she sat beneath it, back to the stone, and let herself shake.

That was when she heard the dogs.

Or not dogs — she corrected herself a heartbeat later, breath stopping — horns. Far off, down the pass, thin as thread through the storm. And beneath the horns, voices. Shouting. Not calling to each other in the way of lost travelers. Calling *at* someone.

August pinched out the candle.

She moved to the door on legs that protested every step and peered through the gap where the leather hinge had rotted away. White chaos. And in it, downslope, perhaps two hundred yards, figures — dark shapes swimming through the murk, and one shape smaller than the others, running badly, stumbling, rising, running again.

They'd cornered someone. They were herding them — up the pass, toward the cliff edge, toward—

Toward the shrine.

August's body made the decision before her mind finished arguing. She stepped back from the door, looked at the tiny room, the one exit, the featureless god in its niche, and swore with a fluency that would have made her old crew proud.

Hiding was impossible. Fleeing was impossible. Which left the thing she had sworn off since Millbrook, the thing that had put her on this road at all, the thing that got Marta Hale dragged away in front of everyone who'd ever bought her eggs.

Magic.

"Just a little," she whispered to the god, who offered no opinion. "Just enough."

She knelt and pressed both palms flat to the floor stones and reached down, the way Ysolde had taught her — not grabbing at power but *listening* for it, the way you listen for water under rock. The mountain's Gift ran deep here, old and slow and patient, a vast cold attention that had been watching the world turn long before kings. Hedge-witch magic wasn't fireballs. It was asking politely. It was borrowing.

*I need the snow to lie thick,* she asked it. *I need the drifts to rise. I need them blind.*

The mountain considered. The mountain, being a mountain, took its time. And then the storm outside changed — August felt it more than saw it, a shift in the wind's grammar, the snowfall thickening from a curtain into a wall. Through the gap in the door, the dark shapes below seemed to lose each other, their shouts turning confused, their formation dissolving into scattered, cursing silhouettes.

The fleeing figure scrambled upslope, closer now, and August saw it clearly for one moment between gusts: young, slight, hood blown back, dark hair plastered to a pale face — and carrying, absurdly, a lute case strapped across their back like a shield.

A singer. They were hunting a singer.

The figure tripped on the shrine's threshold and half-fell through the doorway, landing hard on the stones, and August had a knife at their throat before either of them had decided anything.

"Don't," August said. "Don't scream, don't move, don't—"

"I wasn't going to scream." The voice was young, hoarse, and — incongruously — amused. Dark eyes blinked up at her through dripping hair. "Screaming is how they find you. I've been very carefully not screaming for six days." A pause. The eyes flicked to August's hands, still pressed flat against the floor, still faintly *glowing* — she saw the moment the stranger noticed, saw the frost-light of borrowed mountain-power fade from her own knuckles, and watched the stranger's expression do something complicated. "Oh," they said softly. "You're one too."

The word landed like a blow. *One.* Not a smuggler, not a witch-for-hire, not a person with a name and a trade and a life — just *one*, the way plague victims are ones.

"I'm not—" August started, and stopped, because outside the horn blew again, much closer, and both of them froze.

"They'll sweep the shrine," the stranger whispered. "They always check the shrines. Old habit — people hide in holy places." They were already pushing themselves up, scanning the little room with the practical eye of someone who had done this before, many times, and August realized with a lurch that this bedraggled singer was better at being hunted than she was.

"There's nowhere to go," August hissed.

"There's always somewhere to go. You just have to know which way the mountain keeps it." The stranger grinned — actually grinned, teeth chattering, knife-adjacent, snow-blind — and pulled a small wooden whistle from inside their coat. "Do you trust me?"

"No."

"Good instinct. Do it anyway." And they blew the whistle, one clear note that cut through the storm like a blade through silk.

Nothing happened. The horn sounded again, fifty yards, thirty. August drew the knife fully free and turned to face the door, because if she was going to die in a shrine she'd die facing outward, and she heard the stranger say, conversationally, "You might want to stand away from the floor," and then the floor opened.

Not all of it. One slab, perfectly fitted, silently counterweighted, swung down and aside like a yawn, revealing steps descending into darkness — and out of that darkness rose hands. Many hands. Reaching up, steady and sure, and a voice, low and rough:

"In. Both of you. *Now.*"

August looked at the stranger. The stranger looked at August, eyebrows raised, and said, "Told you," and dropped through the hole like a stone into a well.

The door of the shrine burst open behind her. Wind, snow, torchlight — and a soldier's silhouette filling the frame, mail rimed white, sword already drawn.

August jumped.

* * *

Tam Voss had been singing for the Choir since he was fourteen, and in eight years he had learned that fear had a pitch. Terror was a high, thin note, unsustainable; panic was a jagged interval that made listeners flinch. The trick — the whole trick, the thing Brannoc had hammered into him in the tunnels under Candlemere until Tam dreamed in harmony — was to hold your fear at a pitch you could sustain. Low. Steady. Long.

He sustained it now, falling through the dark with a stranger above him and soldiers above her, and hit the packed-earth floor of the tunnel in a roll that Wren had drilled into him a hundred times. Above, the hatch sighed shut. Boots thundered overhead. A voice shouted something about tracks, about the storm, about searching the treeline, and then the storm ate even that, and there was only the dark, and breathing, and the smell of wet wool and lamp oil.

"Lamp," Tam said, and Lark struck light.

In the glow, he got his first real look at the stranger. Woman, twenties, built like someone who carried heavy things for a living — broad shoulders, scarred knuckles, a smuggler's frame if Tam had ever seen one. She stood with her back to the tunnel wall, knife still out, weight balanced, cataloguing exits. Her eyes went from face to face around the lamplight — Wren with her crossbow, Lark with the lamp, big Hollis who'd lifted the hatch, quiet Ivet with her hands already glowing faintly, ready — and Tam watched her arrive at the correct conclusion with impressive speed.

"The Choir," he said. "You've heard of us, or you haven't. Either way—"

"You've been following me." Her voice was flat. Dangerous-flat, the flatness of someone deciding whether violence was efficient. "Since Millbrook. I caught it twice — movement on my backtrail, gone before I could confirm. I told myself it was deer."

"It was us being bad at our job," Lark admitted. "Sorry."

"Why?" The woman's knife didn't waver. "If you're crown, say so now and save us both the theater. If you're bounty-men, same. If you're something else, I want to know what, and I want to know why a hunted woman interests you enough to shadow her through a blizzard."

"Because you hid a Gift-bearer from soldiers," Tam said, "and then you used your own Gift to cover his escape, and then you stayed in Millbrook another week anyway, because you're either the bravest fool on this side of the Thornfells or you had nowhere else to go. And then the proclamation came, and you burned your letters, and you took the deep roads north alone, in winter, toward Candlemere." He shrugged. "We don't collect people, friend. We collect exactly this."

Her knife-point dipped, maybe an inch. "You watched me burn my letters?"

"We watch everyone we hope to help. It feels awful. You get used to it, or you don't." He extended a hand. "Tam Voss. Singer, scout, occasional rescuee. This is Wren and Lark — twins, don't ask — Hollis, who could carry you and the mountain both, and Ivet, who's a better healer than she looks, which is saying something because she looks like a healer."

The woman didn't take his hand. But she sheathed the knife, which in Tam's experience was most of a handshake. "August Blackwood," she said. "Smuggler. And I'm not one of you. Whatever you think I am."

"Sure," said Wren, from behind her crossbow, in a tone that suggested she'd heard that particular song before and knew all the verses.

"She's bleeding," Ivet said quietly. "Frostbite, two fingers, left hand. And she's been walking on an ankle that's been wrong since the second day. We can argue in the refuge. Brannoc will want her warm first."

"Brannoc," August repeated. Something flickered across her face — Tam filed it away, the way he filed everything, names especially. "And Candlemere is real. The monastery. There are actually people under it."

"There are actually people under it," Tam agreed. "About thirty of us. Singers mostly, and healers, and a farmer who insists he's not a magician even though his beans come up in January. It's not much. It's warm, it's hidden, and nobody there will look at you the way you looked at yourself just now, when I said 'one.'" He held her gaze. "You flinched. I saw it. Come not-flinch with us for a while."

For a long moment she said nothing. The lamp guttered. Somewhere far above, the storm went on trying to collect its debt from the empty shrine.

Then August Blackwood laughed — short, disbelieving, aimed mostly at herself — and followed them down into the dark.

* * *

They walked for hours. The tunnels under Thornfell, Tam explained as they went, were older than the monastery above them — miners' works, then hermits' cells, then smugglers' runs, layer on layer like the writing on a palimpsest. The Choir had spent twenty years learning every passage, bracing the weak ones, hiding the rest. "The mountain keeps what falls into it," he said. "So we fell in on purpose."

August said little. She saved her questions, Tam noticed, the way she probably saved coin — carefully, spending only when necessary. But she watched everything: the timber shoring, the air-shafts disguised as animal burrows, the chalk marks that meant nothing to an outsider and everything to the Choir. Once, passing a junction, she stopped dead and touched a mark on the wall — a spiral with a line through it, half worn away — and her face did something complicated and private.

"You know that sign," Tam said.

"My mentor used it." Her voice came out careful, level, a loaded cart on a bad road. "Years ago. On everything. I thought it was hers."

"Maybe it was. Maybe she learned it somewhere." He kept his voice light, easy, the way you talked a spooked animal forward. "Lots of things travel further than the people who make them."

They descended a final stair, and the air changed — warmer, damper, smelling of woodsmoke and bread and, underneath it, the mineral breath of deep stone. The passage opened, and Candlemere unfolded below them.

It had been a monastery once, Tam knew, though hardly anyone alive remembered what order: a great vaulted undercroft where monks had sung the hours, pillared halls, cells carved into the living rock, a spring-fed cistern that gave sweet water. The monks were dust; the vaults remained. Now laundry lines crossed the nave where altars had been. Cookfires glowed in iron braziers where censers had swung. Children — two of them, born in the refuge, who had never seen an open sky — chased each other between the pillars, and someone was playing a fiddle badly, and someone else was laughing at them, and the whole vast space hummed with the ordinary music of people refusing, collectively and cheerfully, to be extinct.

August stopped at the top of the steps and stared.

Tam watched her take it in — the fires, the children, the laundry, the sheer unhidden humanity of it — and watched something in her face crack along lines she'd probably thought were load-bearing. Three weeks alone in the cold, he guessed. Three weeks of flinching at her own reflection. And now this.

"Welcome to Candlemere," he said.

She didn't answer. She was looking at a woman across the hall — gray-haired, broad, stirring an enormous pot — who had looked up and was already coming toward them with the unhurried certainty of someone who had fed thirty fugitives every day for years and did not intend to stop for one more.

"You're Blackwood," the woman said, arriving. "Ivet sent word ahead. Sit. Eat. The ankle first, then the fingers, then questions — yours and ours, in whatever order survival requires." She steered August toward a bench by the fire with two hands on her shoulders, gentle and absolutely non-negotiable, and August — August, who had faced down soldiers and storms and a knife fight in a shrine — went.

Tam hung back by the stairs, lute case still strapped across his back, and allowed himself, finally, to let his fear drop from its sustained note. In the quiet that followed, he became aware of a small presence at his elbow: Osk, twelve years old, barefoot as always, staring at the newcomer with an expression Tam had learned to dread.

"What is it?" Tam asked quietly.

Osk didn't answer at first. His eyes had gone somewhere else — somewhere far south, behind the eyes of a man in a palace, in the cellar of himself where a king had lived buried for thirty years. When he spoke, his voice was thin and strange.

"She's the one," he said. "The king keeps dreaming about her teacher. And now the Regent knows she's here."

Across the hall, oblivious, August Blackwood sat down by the fire and began, at last, to thaw.
