## 4. The Choir of Candlemere

August woke to the smell of bread and the sound of someone singing badly.

She lay still for a long moment, cataloguing. Wool blanket, coarse but clean. A pallet in a stone alcove, the walls sweating cold. Her boots had been removed and set side by side at the foot of it — a courtesy or a warning, she hadn't decided which — and her pack was gone from where she remembered dropping it.

The singing drifted down a corridor beyond the alcove's mouth, off-key and cheerful, a drinking song by the sound of it, though the words kept dissolving into humming whenever the singer lost the thread. Which was often.

Her head still ached from the blizzard, and beneath the ache sat the deeper bruise of what had happened at the shrine: Tam Voss's hand on her arm, snow in his lashes, saying *you're not alone* as if it were a simple fact and not an act of war against everything the crown had spent a year teaching her to believe.

She got up quietly, pulled on her boots, and followed the smell of bread.

* * *

The refuge beneath the ruined monastery of Candlemere was larger than she'd expected and stranger. She climbed out of the sleeping alcove into a vaulted undercroft where pillars marched away into shadow, their capitals carved with saints whose faces had been chiseled off long ago — smooth-eyed, anonymous, patient. Lanterns hung on wires strung pillar to pillar, maybe two dozen of them, and beneath each one something lived: a bedroll behind a curtain, a shelf of books, a cookfire built into a broken font, a garden of pale mushrooms cultivated in a trough of grave-dirt. A whole village folded into the bones of a dead church.

And people. More than she'd expected. A dozen, perhaps more, moving through the lantern-light with the unhurried economy of those who have learned to be quiet in a loud world. A woman with grey braids was grinding something in a mortar. Two young men were hauling water up a stairwell, arguing about rope knots. A girl no older than fifteen sat cross-legged on a pillar's base with her eyes closed and her fingers pressed flat against the stone, and when August passed close enough, the girl opened one eye and said, "You walk like you're counting exits."

"I am."

"Good," said the girl, and closed her eye again.

The singing led her to the kitchen — a corner of the undercroft where a great hearth had been built into what must once have been the monastery's refectory wall — and there she found Tam Voss, sleeves rolled, flour to his elbows, attempting to shape loaves while he sang. He was worse at both than he'd been at anything in the blizzard, and he grinned when he saw her standing at the edge of the firelight.

"You're awake. Good. Sit. Eat." He nodded at a bench. "Bread's almost — don't look at me like that, it's almost ready."

"Where are my things?"

"Safe. Nobody here steals." He paused, wiping his hands. "Well. Lark steals, but only from Brannoc, and only to annoy him. It's a whole thing. You'll see."

August didn't sit. "I want my pack. Then I want directions to the surface, and then I'm leaving."

Tam looked at her for a moment, and whatever he saw in her face made him set down the dough and wipe his hands properly, slowly, buying time the way careful men do. "Leaving for where?"

"South. Away."

"It's a hundred miles of hunted country between here and anywhere," he said gently. "Your face is on proclamations now. I've seen them. *The Blackwood woman, hedge-trained, dangerous.* They gave you a description and everything. Quite flattering, actually — they make you sound taller."

"Then I'll cut my hair and be shorter."

"And go where?" He spread his hands, flour drifting. "August. Every road south ends at a pyre right now. I know you don't owe us anything. I know we dragged you half-frozen out of a shrine and you've had no say in any of it. But stay three days. Eat. Rest. Listen. If after three days you still want to walk into the lowlands alone, I'll personally carry your pack to the pass and wave you goodbye."

She wanted to say no. She heard herself say nothing at all, which was worse, because Tam smiled like a man who has just won a bet with himself, and went back to his bread.

* * *

Brannoc found her an hour later, or perhaps she found him; it was hard to tell who was seeking whom in that place.

He was a big man gone soft at the middle, with a beard going iron-grey and hands like a stonemason's, scarred across every knuckle. He sat at a table near the mushroom garden with a ledger open before him, doing sums in pencil, and he did not look up when August stopped across from him. He finished his column first. Then he closed the ledger, folded his hands atop it, and gave her the full weight of his attention, which was considerable.

"August Blackwood," he said. "Welcome to Candlemere. I'm Brannoc. I run this place, such as it runs."

"You knew my name before I told you."

"Tam told us. Also the proclamations." He said it without apology. "I like to know exactly who I'm feeding. It's nothing personal. It's survival."

"What is this place?"

"A hole in the world." He gestured at the undercroft, the lanterns, the chipped saints. "Candlemere monastery burned two hundred years ago. The crown wrote it off. The villagers above think it's haunted, which suits us fine — we do our best to earn the reputation. There are forty-one of us at last count. Smugglers, singers, a couple of runaway priests, Sef over there—" he nodded toward the woman with the grey braids, who raised her mortar in salute without looking up— "who patches everyone together, and whoever else the mountains spit at our door. We've been hiding Gift-bearers for six years. Since long before it was fashionable."

"Hiding them how?"

"The way anyone hides anything. Carefully, and with good bookkeeping." He tapped the ledger. "Every person who comes through gets fed, healed if they need it, taught what they want to learn, and moved on or settled in, whichever keeps them alive. That's all this is. No grand purpose. We're not revolutionaries. We're a cellar full of people the world would rather burn."

"That's not what Tam said."

"No?" Brannoc's eyebrows rose slightly. "What did Tam say?"

"He said you'd been shadowing me since Millbrook. Watching me. Following me through the passes." She leaned forward. "That's not a cellar full of refugees, that's a network. Networks have purposes."

For a moment something moved behind Brannoc's eyes — weariness, or grief, or the particular exhaustion of a man who has been waiting years to have a conversation he dreads. He glanced around the undercroft, at the lanterns, at Sef grinding her herbs, at the twins hauling water, and when he spoke again his voice was lower.

"Not here. Come with me."

He led her through a curtain at the far end of the undercroft, down a stair that had been cut into living rock rather than built, and into a small round chamber where a single candle burned on a stone table. The walls were black with old smoke. Someone had drawn a circle on the floor in white chalk, old and renewed so many times it had become a groove.

"This is the chapel proper," Brannoc said. "Or what's left of it. The rest of the monastery burned; this room was already underground, so it just... stayed. The Choir meets here."

"The Choir."

"That's what we call ourselves. The ones who stayed." He looked at the candle instead of at her. "There are eleven of us who know. The others — the ones in the undercroft — most of them don't. They think this is a waystation. A safe house. And it is. But underneath that, there's something else, and I'm showing you because Tam vouched for you, and because you used magic in front of crown soldiers to save a man you'd never met, and people who do that tend to need to know things eventually whether they want to or not."

August's arms prickled. "Know what?"

Brannoc took a breath, let it out slow. "That the king is not a tyrant."

She laughed. She couldn't help it — a short, sharp bark of disbelief that echoed strangely in the smoke-black chamber. "I've read the edicts. I've watched his soldiers drag my neighbors out of their homes. I watched a woman I've known since I was six get taken because her grandson sneezed wrong. Whatever sits on that throne, it isn't a prisoner."

"No," Brannoc agreed. "It isn't."

The laugh died in her throat.

"There's a boy here," Brannoc said quietly. "Osk. Sixteen years old. Came to us two winters back, half-starved, ears bleeding, screaming about dreams that weren't his. He hears the king. Not rumors, not politics — the actual mind of King Aldous, every night, whether he wants to or not. And what he hears—" Brannoc's jaw worked. "What he hears is a man screaming inside his own skull. A man buried alive in himself for thirty years while something else wears his face and signs his name."

The candle flame bent, though there was no draft.

"You want me to believe that," August said. Her voice came out flat. "You want me to believe the man who burned Millbrook Cross is a victim."

"I want you to listen to Osk," Brannoc said. "That's all. Listen, and then decide. That's the only thing I'll ever ask of anyone here. Listen first, decide second." He finally met her eyes. "Ysolde Vane taught you better than that, I'd wager. Even if she never taught you much else."

The name hit her like a slap. August went very still. "How do you know that name?"

"Because Ysolde Vane stood in this room thirty years ago," Brannoc said, "and helped bind a king. And she's the reason any of us know the truth at all."

* * *

He didn't tell her more. He said, with the maddening patience of a man managing a skittish animal, that some things were Osk's to tell, and led her back up into the lantern-light, and left her at the hearth with a bowl of stew she couldn't eat.

She sat with it instead, turning it over. *Ysolde.* Five years of silence, five years of wondering what she'd done wrong, why her mentor had vanished in the night without a word, leaving behind a half-trained apprentice and a hut that smelled of ash. And now a stranger with a ledger was throwing the name around like a coin, claiming Ysolde had stood in this room, had bound a king, had—

No. It was a test. It had to be. Some trap baited with the one word that would stop her running. Crown agents were cleverer these days; Maren Vale's kind, the ones who read your letters before they burned them.

Except. Except that Tam had pulled her out of a blizzard at cost to himself. Except that nobody here had asked her to prove anything, sign anything, swear anything. Except that the girl on the pillar had counted exits too, and grinned about it.

"Stew's getting cold," said a voice at her elbow.

Sef had materialized beside her, bowl in her own hands, grey braids swinging. She sat down uninvited, close enough to be friendly, far enough to be polite, and began eating with the thoroughness of a woman who had buried too many people to waste meals.

"You're the healer," August said.

"I'm the everything-medical, yes. Bones, births, fevers, hearts. Hearts are the worst." Sef pointed her spoon at August's untouched bowl. "Eat. Grief digests poorly on an empty stomach."

"I'm not grieving."

"Mm." Sef ate another spoonful. "You've got the look, though. The one they all get, the new ones. Like a dog that's been kicked at every house it ever tried to love, sitting at the gate trying to decide." She nodded around the undercroft, at the lanterns, the curtains, the arguing water-haulers. "They all sat where you're sitting. Wren sat there. Lark. Tam. Me. Deciding whether we were worth the risk. Take your time. The gate's not going anywhere."

"And if I decide it's a trap?"

"Then you'll leave, and we'll grieve you briefly and get on with it." Sef shrugged. "That's the other thing they all learn eventually. This place doesn't need you. That's what makes it safe. Need is what the crown uses against people — needs names, addresses, mothers. We just... offer. What you do with the offering is yours."

August looked at her. "You talk like someone who's practiced this speech."

"I've given it eleven times." Sef stood, collecting her bowl. "You'd be the twelfth. The stew really is better warm, though. Just so you know."

* * *

She found Osk by accident, or he found her.

It was deep-night quiet in the undercroft, the lanterns turned low, most of the refuge asleep behind its curtains. August had given up on sleep herself and taken to walking the pillars, counting exits again, learning the place the only way she trusted — with her feet. She was passing the mushroom garden when she heard breathing that wasn't quite right, quick and shallow, and found a boy curled against a pillar base with his hands clamped over his ears and tears cutting tracks through the grime on his face.

He was maybe sixteen, thin as a winter branch, dark hair lank with sweat. His lips were moving. Counting, maybe. Or praying. Or both.

August crouched down in front of him, slow, the way you approach a hurt animal. "Osk."

His eyes opened. They were unfocused, looking through her at something miles and years away. "He's dreaming again," the boy whispered. "He always dreams the same part. The door. There's a door and he can't remember which side of it he's on."

"Whose dream?"

"The king's." Osk's eyes focused, finally, and what was in them made her chest hurt — not fear exactly, but the bone-deep exhaustion of someone who has been carrying something far too heavy for far too long. "You're the new one. August. Tam's blizzard-woman." A wet, cracked almost-laugh. "Sorry. Bad greeting. He's — it's a bad night. Thirty years ago tonight, give or take. He counts it. He counts everything. Days, breaths, the number of times he's begged."

August should have walked away. Every instinct honed in five hard years of smuggling said walk away, this is madness, this is a boy having a fit and a cult with a story to sell. She heard Ysolde's voice in her memory instead — *evidence first, opinion second, little bird* — and stayed crouched.

"Tell me what you hear," she said.

Osk stared at her. Then, slowly, he unclenched one hand from his ear and held it out, palm up, an offering or a plea.

So she took it.

The world dropped away.

Cold. Cold like a cellar, like a coffin, like the bottom of a well with the lid shut. Darkness that wasn't darkness but *absence*, a man-shaped hole in the world where a man used to be. And in the hole, a voice — thin as paper, worn as river stone, patient the way only the utterly trapped are patient:

*Day ten thousand nine hundred and fifty-eight. I dreamed of her again. The witch. I dreamed she was weeping. I cannot remember if I hated her. I cannot remember my mother's face. I cannot remember my own. But I remember her tears, and I count them, I count them, I—*

August tore her hand free and sat down hard on the flagstones.

Osk was watching her with terrible gentleness. "Now you know," he said simply.

"That's not possible." Her voice shook. "That's not — bindings like that, they take a circle of masters, they take *years*, no hedge-witch could—"

"Ysolde Vane wasn't alone," Osk said. "And she wasn't a hedge-witch then. She was twenty-three years old, and they told her it was the only way to save the kingdom, and she believed them." He drew his knees up, wrapped his arms around them, made himself small. "She helped build the prison. Then she spent the rest of her life trying to find the key. That's what Brannoc means. That's the heresy. The king didn't make the purges. Something wearing him made them. Something that's been eating him alive for thirty years while the whole country salutes."

August sat on the cold stones of a dead church, in a hidden village beneath a haunted ruin, and felt the ground tilt under everything she knew.

The edicts. The proclamations. The woman from Millbrook Cross being dragged away by soldiers with the king's seal on their breasts. All of it reassembling itself around a different center, a colder one. If it was true — if even a piece of it was true — then the hunt wasn't policy. It was appetite. It was a thing feeding, wearing a crown, wearing a king, wearing a country like a coat.

And Ysolde. Ysolde who had vanished, who had left her, who had — oh, *little bird*, had she run from the Regent's agents, or toward something? Had she left August behind to protect her, or because there was no longer time to teach?

"She's alive," August said. It came out before she could stop it, raw and childish. "Isn't she. That's what you're not saying."

Osk's face did something complicated. Pity, mostly. "I don't hear her," he said softly. "I only hear him. I'm sorry."

August looked down at her own hands — smuggler's hands, hedge-witch's hands, hands that had lit a lantern with a word in front of crown soldiers and doomed her old life forever. Somewhere above her, snow was falling on the ruins of Candlemere, and somewhere south of that, a kingdom slept under a tyrant who was also a prisoner, and somewhere in the middle of it all was a woman who had once promised to teach her everything and taught her only how to be alone.

Three days, Tam had said. Stay three days, and then decide.

She thought about the chalk circle in the smoke-black chapel, renewed so many times it had become a groove. Eleven people who knew. Twelve, now, if she stayed. A gate that wasn't going anywhere, and a bowl of stew she should have eaten warm.

"All right," she said, to Osk, to the pillar, to herself. "Three days."

Osk smiled — tired, but real. "That's how it starts," he said. "With everybody."

Above them, faint through fathoms of stone, the monastery bell that hadn't rung in two hundred years gave a single soft toll, and neither of them mentioned it, because some things you agree not to notice until you're ready.
