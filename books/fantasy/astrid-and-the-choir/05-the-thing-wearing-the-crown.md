## 5. The Thing Wearing the Crown

Osk had learned to sleep in layers.

The first layer was the ordinary kind — the body's small surrender, breath slowing, the warmth of the blanket Sef had woven from mountain wool. The second was harder: a deliberate dimming, like shuttering a lamp room by room, so that the far-off murmur of the king's dreaming would fade to almost nothing. And beneath both lay the third layer, the one he never spoke of, where he kept himself pressed flat and silent and very small, the way a hare goes still when the hawk's shadow crosses the grass.

Because the king did not only dream. Sometimes — worse than dreaming — he *listened back*.

Tonight Osk had managed only the first two layers. He lay in his narrow cell in the undercroft of Candlemere, staring at the ceiling where centuries of candle smoke had stained the stone the color of old honey, and he waited for the tide of another man's sleep to come in over him.

It always came at the same hour. Midnight, or near it, when the palace in the capital was dark and the guards outside the royal bedchamber changed, and the thing wearing the crown let its vessel sink at last into exhausted slumber. Osk had named the hours of it the way sailors name tides. He knew the dreams by their flavors: the long gray corridors, the throne room flooded with light that cast no shadows, the endless signing of documents in a hand that was not quite the king's own.

He had told the Choir about the corridors and the documents. He had never told them about the listening.

The tide came in.

It began, as it always began, with cold. Not the cold of Candlemere's stone — a different cold, older, the cold of deep water and deeper cellars. Then the sensation of lying on a bed far too large for him, in a body far too large for him, hands scarred with thirty years of a life he had never lived, staring up at a canopy embroidered with the sigil of a kingdom he had never wanted.

And beneath him — beneath the king, beneath the bed, beneath the floor of the world — something *turned over* in its sleep.

Osk had no better word for it. It was like feeling a whale pass under a boat: not seen, but known through the soles of the feet, through the water itself, an immensity displacing everything around it. The Regent. That was what the king's buried mind called it, in the one clear corner of itself it had managed to keep all these years — a corner no bigger than a monk's cell, walled with memory, lit by a single stubborn flame. *The Regent rules until the true king comes of age.* That was the lie it had written into the binding, elegant as a signature. But the true king had been fifteen years old when they bound him, and he had never come of age at all, because a man cannot come of age inside his own skull.

Osk drifted down through the layers of the dream, hating every fathom of it, unable to stop.

The corridors first. Always the corridors. They were gray and windowless and went on farther than any palace had a right to go, and the king walked them because walking was the only thing left to him. His name — his true name, Aldous, which he repeated to himself each morning like a prayer before the day drowned it out — echoed off walls that did not echo anything else. Thirty years of footsteps. Thirty years of documents signed by a hand that moved while he watched from behind his own eyes, screaming without sound, until even the screaming wore smooth and quiet as river stone.

But tonight the dream was different.

Tonight there was a door in the corridor that Osk had never seen before.

It stood where no door should be — mid-corridor, freestanding, oak and iron, utterly plain — and the king stopped before it the way a man stops before a grave. Osk felt the king's heart, felt it actually beat inside the borrowed dream, quick and frightened and absurdly human. Because the king knew this door. The king had built it himself, once, in the first year, out of nothing but will and terror: a door against the inside of his own mind, locked from his side, behind which he had hidden the only things the Regent must never find.

The king reached out. His hand — an old man's hand now, though he was only five-and-forty; captivity ages faster than time — trembled above the iron latch.

*Don't,* Osk thought, with the useless urgency of a dreamer. *Don't open it, it'll see—*

The latch lifted.

Inside there was no treasure chamber, no armory of secrets. There was a single memory, kept green and alive through three decades of gray: a young woman standing in a frozen chapel, snow melting on her shoulders, her hands raised and shaking, her face wet with tears. She was speaking words in a language Osk did not know, and light moved around her fingers like water around stones, and she was weeping as she worked — weeping as though the binding were killing her too, as though she were burying something beloved along with the king.

Behind her, half-seen in the chapel's dark, others waited. Hooded figures. A circle. Their faces were turned away, but one of them — the tallest — held a lantern, and the lantern-light caught the edge of a jaw Osk somehow recognized, though he could not have said why.

*Ysolde,* the king's memory said, the way you say the name of the person who killed you and saved your life in the same act. *Her name was Ysolde.*

Osk woke.

Not gently. He came up out of the king's dream like a diver breaching, gasping, drenched in sweat despite the cold cell, his hands clawed into the blanket. For a moment he could not remember where the ceiling was, whether he lay in Candlemere or in the gray corridors, whether his hands were his own small chapped hands or the king's scarred ones. He lay still until his breathing remembered itself.

Then he got up, because there was someone he had to wake, and it was not going to be easy, and he had put it off too long already.

* * *

August Blackwood slept badly in Candlemere, which surprised her. She had expected the mountain silence to be a balm after Millbrook's fear. Instead the silence pressed on her like a hand, full of things unsaid — Brannoc's careful questions, Tam's watchful kindness, the way the whole Choir moved around her as if she were a stray fire they hadn't decided whether to feed or smother.

So when the knocking came at her door — soft, urgent, wrong for the hour — she was awake before the third knock, knife already in her hand out of smuggler's habit.

"It's Osk." The boy's voice through the wood was thin. "I need to show you something. I'm sorry. I need to show you now."

She opened the door on a fourteen-year-old swaying with exhaustion, eyes red-rimmed, holding a stub of candle that guttered in the undercroft draft. Behind him, drawn by the same instinct that drew moths and mourners, Tam had appeared in the corridor with a lamp, and beyond Tam, woken by some subtler alarm, Sef.

"The king," Osk said. "He dreamed about her tonight. About Ysolde."

The name went through August like a blade finding the gap in armor. She kept her face still — smuggler's habit again — but she heard her own pulse.

"Come in," she said. "All of you, if you're standing there. Come in."

They crowded into the small cell: Osk on the edge of her cot, shivering; Tam and Sef against the wall; Brannoc arriving last, summoned by Tam's signal, filling the doorway with his broad unhurried presence and his eyes already narrowed. August lit her own lamp and turned to the boy.

"Start from the beginning. Slowly."

"I hear him when he sleeps." Osk's hands twisted together. "I've heard him since I was nine. I never asked to — it just — the Gift doesn't ask, does it? You know it doesn't." He looked at her with sudden fierce appeal, and she nodded, because it didn't, because hers had arrived at thirteen like a fever and no one had asked her either. "Most nights it's just — corridors. Papers. A man walking forever inside his own head. But tonight there was a door. He keeps a door, in the deepest part, where he hides things from the Regent, and tonight he opened it, and—" The boy swallowed. "There's a woman in the memory. Young. Binding him. In a chapel made of ice, or frozen — snow on her shoulders. She's crying while she does it. And he knows her name."

"Say it," August said quietly.

"Ysolde." Osk's voice cracked on it. "He said her name was Ysolde."

The lamp-flame leaned in the draft. No one spoke. August became aware of Brannoc watching her with the particular attention of a man reading weather.

"You've spoken of her," Brannoc said. "Your mentor. The one who vanished."

"My mentor." August set the knife down on the table with exaggerated care, because her hands wanted to do something less controlled. "Ysolde Vane taught me everything I know of the Gift for six years, and then one morning her cottage was empty and her kettle was still warm, and I spent eleven years deciding what that meant about me." She looked at Osk. "Show me. You said you needed to show me. Show me the memory."

"It doesn't work like showing, exactly," Osk said miserably. "It's more like — drowning. I take you under and we both come up when we come up. It can hurt. Last time I tried it with Wren she couldn't speak properly for a day."

"I've been hurt before," August said. "Go on."

Brannoc stirred. "August—"

"She taught me," August said, not looking at him. "Whatever is in that man's head, part of it belongs to me. Sit down if you like, Brannoc. Or hold my hand. Either way, we're doing this."

A pause. Then, unexpectedly, Tam laughed — a low, warm sound — and dragged a stool to the cot's side and sat, and took August's hand without asking, the way singers take hands before a hard harmony. "For the coming-up," Tam said. "It helps to have something to climb."

Sef lit more candles. Brannoc, after a moment, lowered himself onto the floor beside the cot like a man settling in for a long night, which, August thought, was exactly what he was.

"All right," she said to Osk. "Drown me."

* * *

Falling felt like falling. That was the mercy of it — no transition, no threshold, just the sudden vertigo of a world replaced. August stood in a gray corridor that stretched in both directions past reason, and the air tasted of dust and candle smoke and something underneath both, something vast and patient, the smell of a cellar where something enormous has been sleeping.

Beside her, translucent as breath on glass, stood the king.

He was not what she had expected. She had expected a tyrant — the Choir's heresy notwithstanding, she had spent eleven years building a tyrant out of proclamations and burned letters and a neighbor dragged from his doorway. What stood before her was a gaunt, stooped man in a nightshirt, barefoot on stone, with the ruined face of someone who has been screaming silently for so long that the scream has become his resting expression. He was looking at the door.

"He can't see us," Osk said, and his voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the boy's consciousness spread thin through the dream like oil on water. "We're just weather to him. Small weather. Watch."

The king opened the door, and the memory unfolded around them like a flower pressed too long in a book.

The frozen chapel. August knew it instantly, knew it the way you know a place from a story told so often it has become a homeland — the vaulted roof glittering with frost, the altar like a block of ice, the windows throwing colored light that seemed to come from somewhere colder than the sun. Snow drifted through a broken pane and did not melt.

And there, at the center, hands raised and shaking, tears cutting clean lines down a face August had last seen disappearing down a road eleven years ago —

"Ysolde." Her own voice, in the dream, sounded like a child's.

Younger Ysolde — twenty-five, perhaps, twenty-six, a stranger and yet not, the same sharp jaw, the same impatience even in grief — stood over a boy of fifteen who knelt bound at the altar steps, and she was weaving light around him in patterns August knew down to her bones. Because she had *seen* those patterns. On parchment. In the margins of her mentor's letters, in the diagrams Ysolde had drilled into her across six winters: the interlocking circles, the seven-fold knot, the anchor-spokes radiating from a central seal.

"That's her work," August whispered. "That's — Osk, that's her binding style. The seven-fold knot. The way the anchors cross at the third ring — she used to make me practice that crossing until my hands bled. She said a sloppy third ring was how bindings slipped." Her voice was climbing, cracking. "This isn't a memory of Ysolde visiting the king. This is Ysolde *binding* him. She was there. She did this."

"Not alone," Osk murmured, and turned the dream, gently, toward the chapel's shadowed edges — where hooded figures stood in a circle, six of them, seven, their faces deliberately averted, and among them a tall shape holding a lantern whose light caught nothing it should have caught.

The Regent turned over beneath the floor of the world.

August felt it the way Osk had described: through the soles of her feet, through the water of the dream, an immensity shifting its weight. And in the memory-Ysolde's face, August saw the exact moment the young witch felt it too — saw her falter, saw the light stutter around her fingers, saw her look down at the kneeling boy with an expression August had spent eleven years trying to decode and understood now in a single stroke: not cruelty, not madness. Horror. The horror of a woman halfway through a thing she can no longer stop, who has realized the price is larger than she was told, and who finishes anyway because the alternative is worse.

*Forgive me,* the young Ysolde mouthed — to the boy, to the chapel, to no one. *Forgive me. There's no other way. Forgive me.*

The memory closed like a book. The door swung shut. The gray corridors rushed back in, and beneath them, rising, rising, something vast becoming aware that small weather had blown through its house—

* * *

August surfaced gasping, Tam's hand crushing hers, four voices calling her name from a great distance. She came back to the cell by inches: candlelight, wool blanket, Osk's terrified face hovering over her, the taste of blood where she'd bitten her lip.

"He listened," Osk was saying, backing away. "At the end, it noticed us, it noticed, I'm sorry, I thought we had longer—"

"Stop." August sat up. Her whole body shook, but her voice, when she found it, was level. "Tell me what it noticed. All of it."

"It felt you recognize the binding." Osk's eyes were huge. "It's felt that before, I think. From her. When Ysolde—"

"When Ysolde spent thirty years trying to undo what she'd done." August said it flatly, and watched the word land in the room like a dropped stone. She looked around at them — Tam still gripping her hand, Sef pale by the candles, Brannoc risen from the floor with his jaw set like a man hearing a battle horn. "You understand what this means. She didn't abandon me. She didn't vanish. She ran — or she was taken — or she's dead — but whatever happened, it happened because she bound the king when she was barely older than I am now, and then spent the rest of her life trying to unbind him before the thing she helped chain consumed her too." Her voice finally broke, and she let it. "Everything I believed about her was wrong. Everything."

The vertigo of it was physical — the room tilting, eleven years of grief revealed as a misreading, a letter read upside down. She pressed her palms to her eyes.

When she lowered them, Brannoc was crouched in front of her, and his expression had changed — the wariness gone, or at least set aside, replaced by something rawer.

"If Ysolde bound him," Brannoc said slowly, "then Ysolde may have left the means to unbind him. Notes. A rite. Anything." He exhaled. "August. Did she leave you letters?"

August thought of the fire in her hearth at Millbrook. Of pages curling, ink flaring gold, eleven years of a mentor's voice going up in smoke because a proclamation on a door had made her afraid of her own history.

"Some," she said. "I burned most of them when the edict came. But Ysolde taught me paranoia before she taught me magic." She met Brannoc's eyes, and felt the strange new thing taking root in her chest — purpose, terrible and bright. "She wouldn't have burned everything. And neither, it turns out, would I. There are letters I hid where no soldier would think to look, and one of them I never dared read, because she sealed it with a knot I recognized and told me once — laughing, gods, she was *laughing* — 'the day you can undo my third-ring crossing, little owl, you may open it.'"

Silence. Somewhere above them, wind moved over the monastery ruins, sifting snow through broken glass.

"What was in the letter?" Tam asked softly.

"I don't know." August stood, and her legs held, and her hands had stopped shaking. "But I know a man buried alive beneath a thing called the Regent, and I know my teacher put him there crying, and I know she spent her life trying to take it back." She looked at Osk — at the boy who carried a king's nightmares and had come to her door shaking with the weight of them. "Get some sleep, all of you. Tomorrow I'm going to teach myself to undo a third-ring crossing."

She paused at the door, and the hook of it pulled tight:

"And then," she said, "someone is going to tell me exactly what the Regent felt, when it noticed me recognizing its cage."
