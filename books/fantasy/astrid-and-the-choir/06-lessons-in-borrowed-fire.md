## 6. Lessons in Borrowed Fire

The training hall beneath Candlemere had been a wine cellar once, back when monks still climbed the mountain to sing. August could see the ghost of it in the stone: the iron rings where casks had hung, the shallow channel cut into the floor for drainage, now dry and furred with moss. Brannoc had cleared a space in the middle and drawn a circle of white chalk that looked, to her eye, entirely too neat.

"Again," he said.

"I did it."

"You did it *alone*." He said the word the way other men said *heresy*. "That's not what I asked."

August stood in the circle with her palms still tingling, a thin ribbon of light guttering between them like a candle flame in a draft. It was hers. She had pulled it up out of herself the way Ysolde had taught her — breath first, then the image, then the grip, hard and clean, like lifting a stone out of deep water. Ten years of solitary discipline lived in that little flame. Ten years of cold mornings and silent practice and a mentor who had said, over and over, *your power is your own, August; share it with no one, trust it to no one, and it will keep you alive.*

"Again," Brannoc said, "with Wren."

Wren was sitting cross-legged at the edge of the chalk circle, sharpening a knife against a whetstone with the patient rhythm of someone who had done it ten thousand times. She didn't look up. "She hates this," Wren said.

"She does," Brannoc agreed.

"I don't hate it," August said, hating it.

The twins were the hardest part of Candlemere to understand. Wren and Lark moved through the monastery's halls like two halves of one thought, finishing each other's sentences, sharing dreams, sharing — as far as August could tell — a single current of power that passed between them so easily it might have been breath. When they worked together, their magic didn't add. It *multiplied*. And they wanted her to do the same, wanted her to open whatever door Ysolde had nailed shut inside her, and every instinct she owned stood at that door with an axe.

"Ysolde taught you to be a fortress," Brannoc said. He lowered himself onto a wine barrel with the groan of a man twice his age, though August suspected he was barely fifty. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, gray-shot beard, hands like shovels, and eyes that missed nothing. "I'm asking you to be a bridge."

"A bridge gets walked on."

"A fortress gets besieged." He nodded at her flame. "How long can you hold that?"

"As long as I need to."

"And tomorrow? And in winter, when you're half-starved and running? A lone Gift is a well, girl. It runs dry. Ours is a river." He spread his broad hands. "The Choir doesn't pool magic because we're sentimental. We pool it because pooling is how twelve hunted people survived twenty years under a mountain while the crown burned everyone we loved above it."

August closed her fist and the flame died. The dark rushed back in, comfortable, familiar. "My mentor—"

"Your mentor," Brannoc said, "isn't here."

The words landed harder than he could have known. He watched her face and something shifted in his expression — regret, maybe, or recognition — but he didn't take it back. That was the thing about Brannoc: he would cut you to the bone and then stand there holding the knife, honest about the wound he'd made.

"We'll stop for today," he said.

"Good," said August, and left before either of them could see her hands shaking.

* * *

She found Lark in the bell tower, which wasn't a bell tower anymore because the bell had been taken down and melted, decades ago, for its bronze. What remained was a stone cylinder open to the sky, and Lark sat in the gap where the bell had hung, legs dangling over a hundred feet of nothing, singing very quietly to the wind.

August climbed the stairs because it was that or the dormitory, and the dormitory meant people.

"You sound like a cat being washed," she said.

Lark grinned without turning around. "It's a calling song. You use it when you've lost someone in fog or forest. They hear it in their bones even if their ears are full of storm." She scooted sideways, making room. "Brannoc chewed you raw again?"

"He wants me to hand my power to strangers."

"To family," Lark said, "but yes, essentially." The wind came up the tower shaft and riffled her hair, red as rust. Below them Candlemere fell away in terraces — the ruined cloister, the collapsed chapel roof, the pine forest marching down the mountainside into cloud. Somewhere under all that stone, Sef was grinding herbs and Osk was having one of his bad days, and Tam was teaching the younger ones harmonies old enough to predate the kingdom. "Can I tell you something about the first time I linked with Wren? Properly, I mean. Not the twin-thing we were born with."

August leaned on the parapet. "You'll tell me whether I ask or not."

"I was fourteen. We'd been cornered by a tithe-collector with a fire-Gift, a real soldier's Gift, ugly and fast. Wren took a burn across the arm shielding me, and I — I panicked. I grabbed her power the way you'd grab a drowning person. Just reached in and *took*." Lark's grin faded into something older. "And it was the most frightening thing that ever happened to me, because for about three seconds I wasn't Lark anymore. I was both of us. I could feel her fear and her fury and the fact that she'd been afraid for me since we were children and never once said it. Three seconds, and I knew my sister better than fifteen years of living with her had managed."

"That sounds awful."

"It was awful," Lark agreed cheerfully. "And then it was the truest thing I'd ever felt. Both of those are allowed to be real at once. Ysolde never told you that, I'd wager."

August said nothing. The wind filled the silence.

Below, a figure came out of the treeline onto the cloister path — small, quick, wrapped in a patched cloak, moving with the jerky urgency of someone pursued by something no one else could see. Osk. He stopped in the middle of the ruined cloister and pressed both hands flat against his ears, though there was nothing to hear, and August felt the sight of it go through her like a fishhook.

"There he goes again," Lark said softly. "Third time today. He says the king is dreaming about snow. All that snow, and a woman crying." She glanced at August. "Brannoc thinks you should talk to him. Says you've got the steadiest head of the new ones."

"The new ones. There are three new ones and one of them is a smuggler."

"A smuggler with a strange Gift and a dead mentor and nowhere else to go." Lark slid off the parapet ledge, landing light as a bird. "That's the entire membership requirement, August. Welcome to the Choir."

* * *

The supply train came north from Hallowford on the third week of her training, and Brannoc called a meeting in the refectory that ended with August volunteering before she fully understood why.

"It's grain, salt pork, lamp oil, and winter uniforms," Brannoc said, tapping a charcoal map of the pass road. "Crown stores, headed to the garrison at Thornfell Keep. Which means it's food and warmth bought with the money they used to pay the men who dragged Marta Hale off her own doorstep." He looked around the table. "We don't need much. Oil, salt pork, the good wool. We hit them at the narrows where the road hugs the cliff, take what we can carry, and scatter the rest to the valley farms. Small hands, quick work. August, Wren, Tam, and me."

"Why me?" August asked.

"Because your Gift doesn't look like ours." Brannoc's eyes were steady on hers. "Every bearer the crown has catalogued throws fire or moves water or reads minds, and the Inquisition has hunters trained to spot exactly those shapes. But what you did at the shrine — what you did hiding that boy from the soldiers — I've never seen its like. Your power doesn't announce itself. That makes you valuable. That makes you *safe*, if you keep it quiet."

Keep it quiet. Ysolde's whole catechism in a single phrase. August heard herself say, "When do we leave?" and hated how eager it sounded, and went to pack anyway.

They went up through the deep roads in the grey hour before dawn, Brannoc leading with a smuggler's certainty that August grudgingly respected — he knew the mountain's back paths nearly as well as she did, and where he didn't, he trusted her to, and the trusting sat strangely warm in her chest. Tam hummed as she walked, some walking-song that kept their feet quiet on scree. Wren ranged ahead, a shadow among shadows, knife already in hand.

The narrows were everything the map promised: road cut into the cliff face, a forty-foot drop to a frozen streambed, the supply wagons strung out single file with six soldiers and a bored sergeant. Easy. It should have been easy.

It went wrong in the way things go wrong — all at once, and because of a detail nobody thought mattered.

The detail was a dog. A camp-follower cur trotting behind the last wagon, and it caught Wren's scent in the rocks above the road and began to bark.

What happened next happened in pieces. The sergeant shouting. Soldiers turning, one loosing an arrow blind into the cliffside that cracked off stone a hand's width from Tam's head. Brannoc rising up out of the rocks like the mountain itself standing, throwing a wall of force across the road that flipped the lead wagon onto its side with a scream of splintering wood. And Wren — Wren dropping down onto the rearmost wagon to cut the traces, quick and silent, except the driver wasn't cowering like he was supposed to, the driver had a loaded crossbow under the bench, and August saw it come up, saw the bolt seated, saw Wren turn toward the sound a half-second too slow—

There was no decision. Later, going over it a thousand times, August would search for the decision and never find one. There was only the reach — the thing Ysolde had spent ten years teaching her never to do — reaching out and *in*, seizing the driver's arm mid-draw, not his body but the heat of him, the animal furnace under his skin, pulling it up and out and *shaping* it, and the crossbow bolt came loose and the man folded over his own arm screaming about frost, frost in his bones in high summer's cousin, deep autumn, and Wren rolled clear off the wagon as the bolt buried itself in the bench where her chest had been.

Then the world caught up. Brannoc roared something; Tam's voice rose in a chord that made the soldiers' knees buckle and their courage die; and they ran, all four of them, up and over the shoulder of the mountain with what they could carry, leaving the wrecked wagons and the scattered grain and the sergeant's curses behind.

They didn't stop until the trees closed over them and the garrison bells were a faint outrage far below. Then Wren rounded on August, and August braced for anger.

Instead Wren stared at her with an expression August couldn't read, breathing hard, a scratch of blood on her cheekbone. "What *was* that?"

"What?"

"What you did. To him." Wren's voice was low and fast. "He wasn't hurt. Not really. His arm went cold and he dropped. You didn't burn him or break him or — you just *took the fight out of him*. I've seen Gift-work my whole life. That's not any shape I know."

"It's mine," August said. "It's just mine."

"No," said Brannoc quietly.

He had come up behind them, and his face in the grey light under the pines was grim in a way that had nothing to do with the botched raid. He was looking at August the way a man looks at a door he thought was painted on a wall and has just discovered opens.

"No," he said again. "It isn't just yours. I've seen that signature before. Thirty years ago. On the night the king changed."
