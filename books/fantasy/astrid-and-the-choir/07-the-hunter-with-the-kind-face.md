## 7. The Hunter with the Kind Face

The snow on the supply-train road had not yet finished falling when Maren Vale knelt in it and pressed her bare palm to the scorched earth.

Her gloves were in her saddlebag. She always took them off for this part, though her superiors would have called it sentimentality and her subordinates called it madness. The cold bit into her skin with small honest teeth, and she let it, because a hunter who could not feel what she walked through had no business deciding who lived at the end of it.

"Signature's fresh," said Corporal Denny behind her, breath steaming. "Half a day, maybe less."

Maren did not answer at once. She was listening — not with her ears but with the trained stillness she had spent eleven years perfecting, the stillness that let the residue of the Gift rise up out of burned ground like heat off a summer stone. There it was: a trembling in the air above the wrecked wagons, a taste of iron and frost and something stranger underneath, something that did not belong to any of the Choir's known singers.

She had catalogued their signatures the way a lepidopterist catalogs wings. Brannoc of Candlemere, if that was even his true name, burned like peat — slow, deep, stubborn. The healer Sef left traces of chamomile and copper. The twins Wren and Lark were nearly indistinguishable, a doubled note like a bell struck twice. Tam Voss sang even when he cast, leaving trails that hummed faintly if you knew how to listen.

This was none of them.

This was older. Wilder. It had the flavor of a hedge-witch's craft — root-magic, hearth-magic, the kind learned from a grandmother or a stolen book rather than a choir — but it had been *aimed* with a precision no hedge-witch should possess. And beneath the precision ran something else, something that made the fine hairs on Maren's arms lift inside her coat: a signature threaded through with grief. Magic done in sorrow had a texture all its own. She had felt it perhaps four times in eleven years, and every time, the caster had been someone protecting another person at cost to themselves.

"Corporal," she said, rising and pulling her gloves back on. "How many dead?"

"Four crown soldiers. Six drivers." He hesitated. "None of ours beyond the four."

"And the survivors?"

"The drivers who ran. We've got two in the camp. They say the attackers came out of the storm like ghosts. One of them—" he fumbled for the words "—one says a woman pulled a burning beam off a man with her bare hands. Hands that weren't burned."

Maren looked down the road, north, toward the passes. Toward Thornfell.

"Someone new has joined them," she said quietly. "Or someone old has come home."

* * *

They made Millbrook Cross by nightfall of the second day, riding hard through country that grew poorer and more beautiful the higher they climbed. Maren had been here before — three years ago, chasing rumors of a root-doctor who cured fevers, whom she had found and questioned and ultimately let go, because the woman's magic extended no further than easing childbirth and setting bones, and because Maren's commission, whatever else it demanded, did not require her to hunt grandmothers.

That leniency had cost her a reprimand. She kept the reprimand folded in the bottom of her dispatch case like a talisman.

Millbrook Cross smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and fear. Fear had a smell; Maren had learned it early in her career, and it never stopped grieving her. It sat on a village the way fog sat on a river, and the people moved through it hunched, not meeting one another's eyes, doors closing softly as she passed — softly, always softly, because everyone understood now that loudness drew attention.

The edict was nailed to the meetinghouse door, its edges already curling with damp. THE GIFT IS A PLAGUE, it read, in the crown's crisp official hand. BEARERS WILL BE CULLED FOR THE SAFETY OF THE REALM. REPORT ALL SIGNS OF SORCERY TO YOUR LOCAL WARDEN. INFORMANTS WILL BE REWARDED.

*Culled.* The word choice had been deliberate. Not purged, not punished. Culled — as one culls a herd, as one culls diseased stock. Someone in the Office of Inquiry had a poet's ear and a butcher's heart, and Maren had long since stopped trying to learn which.

She posted her soldiers at the well and went alone, as she always did, to the house of the headman.

He was an old man named Garrick Voss — no relation to Tam, though the name gave her a small unwelcome pang — with hands like tree roots and eyes that had once been kind and were now simply careful. He received her in his kitchen, offered her tea he clearly could not afford, and answered her questions with the flat courtesy of a man walking on lake ice.

"Inquisitor Vale," he said. "You were here before. Three winters back."

"I was."

"You let Mother Anselm go."

"I did."

He poured the tea. His hands did not shake, which told her he had decided either that she was safe or that shaking would do no good. "Then ask what you've come to ask."

"August Blackwood," Maren said. "Born here. Left — when? Five years ago? Six?"

Something flickered across the old man's face. Grief, quickly shuttered. "Six years this past thaw."

"She was seen at the supply raid. A woman matching her description, using magic of a kind consistent with her training." Maren set her cup down untouched. "Master Garrick, I am going to be honest with you, because I have found honesty costs me less in the end than lies. If August Blackwood is taken by anyone other than me, she will be taken badly. There are hunters in the south who burn first and file reports after. I am not one of them. But I will find her, whether you help me or not. The only question is how much of this village burns with her when I do."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"She was a good girl," Garrick said at last, to his tea. "Helped her neighbors. Never took coin she couldn't earn. When the fever came through, she sat up nights with the dying so their families could sleep." He looked up, and his careful eyes were wet and furious. "Is that the plague, Inquisitor? Is that what we're culling?"

"No," Maren said. "It isn't. And I'm sorry for what's coming, because I can't stop it. I can only choose how it arrives." She stood. "Where did she live?"

* * *

August Blackwood's cottage stood at the edge of the village where the fields gave up and the forest began, and it had been empty six years and yet somehow not empty at all. The neighbors had kept it — Maren saw it in the swept path, the mended fence, the stack of cut wood under the lean-to that someone refreshed each autumn. Villages did not tend the homes of monsters.

Inside, dust lay thick on everything except the hearthstone, which was clean. Someone still came. Someone still, after six years, swept the hearth of a hunted woman's house, in case she ever came home.

Maren stood in the middle of the small room and felt the terrible decency of it settle over her like snowfall.

On the table, in the corner where the light fell worst, she found the ashes.

Someone had burned letters there — recently, within the month, by the state of the ash. A hurried burning, in a basket or a bowl, then scattered. But fire is an imperfect servant, and Maren had spent eleven years learning its failures. She knelt, sifted gently with a knife's tip, and found what she knew she would find: fragments. Corners of pages the flames had missed, curled but legible. A scrap reading *...the binding holds but the binder weakens...*. Another: *...if I fail, tell her nothing, keep her small and safe...*.

And near the wall, half-tucked beneath a loose floorboard the burners had missed entirely, a single unburned page.

Maren drew it out with two fingers and read it standing, because her knees had gone unreliable.

It was written in a strong, slanted hand, the ink faded but the words enduring:

*Astrid — if you are reading this, then I could not come back, and I am sorry, and you must not look for me. What I helped make cannot be unmade by running. Go to Candlemere. Under the monastery. They will hate what I was, but they will shelter what you are. Trust the singer before you trust the leader. And Astrid — the king is not the enemy. Whatever they tell you, whatever you see — the king is not the enemy.*

Ysolde.

Maren had heard the name whispered in the archives — Ysolde the Hedge-Witch, Ysolde who vanished thirty years ago, Ysolde whose binding-work appeared in forbidden texts that Inquisitors were required to memorize and forbidden to acknowledge. Every trainee learned her signature the way Maren had learned the Choir's. It was the signature of the binding that held the king.

And here it was, in a letter to her apprentice, pointing to Candlemere.

Maren read the page three times. Then she sat down slowly on a stool that some loving neighbor had polished smooth, and held the paper in both hands, and thought about what she was holding.

If she reported this, the southern companies would move on Candlemere within the fortnight. They would burn the monastery, and the ruins beneath it, and everyone hiding there. They would burn August Blackwood. They might, if the wind turned wrong, burn Millbrook Cross for having harbored her.

If she did not report it—

"Inquisitor?" Corporal Denny stood in the doorway, snow melting on his shoulders. "Anything?"

Maren looked at the page. At the clean hearthstone. At the swept path leading away into the trees, kept clear for six years by people who loved a witch.

"Ashes," she said. "Old letters, burned years ago. Nothing current." She tucked the page into her coat, against her heart, where no one would search. "We ride south in the morning. The trail goes cold at the pass — winter's closed it."

Denny frowned. "The raiders went north."

"Winter closes all roads eventually, Corporal. That's what winter is *for*." She pulled on her gloves, and her voice was steady and her hands were not. "Come. We'll question the outlying farms and be gone by week's end. These people have suffered enough."

Outside, the snow had begun again, softening the road north, filling hoofprints, erasing everything. Maren stood in it a long moment, feeling the letter press against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and understood with perfect clarity that she had just become two people: the Inquisitor who would file a report full of nothing, and the woman carrying a dead mentor's last plea toward a hidden door beneath a ruined monastery.

She did not yet know which of them would win.

But as she mounted her horse and turned its head away from the mountains, away from Candlemere, away from the truth she was carrying like contraband — she caught herself looking north one last time, at the white peaks going blue with evening, and thinking:

*Trust the singer before you trust the leader.*

She wondered, with a hunter's instinct she could not kill and a compassion she had never wanted to, who else was already listening.
