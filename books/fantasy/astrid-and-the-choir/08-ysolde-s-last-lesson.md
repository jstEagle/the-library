## 8. Ysolde's Last Lesson

The letters had survived the fire because August had never truly meant to burn them.

She told herself, in the weeks after Millbrook Cross, that the ash in her hearth was all of it — every page, every looping line of Ysolde's cramped, slanting hand. But when she'd knelt at the grate with the bundle in her arms and the proclamation nailed to her door behind her, her hands had betrayed her. She had fed the fire a handful of pages, watched them curl and blacken, and then — some animal instinct she still didn't have a name for — she had snatched the rest back out of the light and buried them in the false bottom of her ledger chest, beneath twenty years of smuggler's arithmetic.

Now, in the grey quiet of Candlemere's under-halls, with the mountain humming its low stone note around her and Osk's nightmares echoing faintly through the walls like weather, August laid what remained on Sef's worktable and began to read.

There were eleven letters. She had counted them a hundred times over the years without ever reading past the first lines, because the first lines were always the same: *August, my heart, forgive me.* And August had never been able to forgive her, so she had never been able to go further. Reading on would have felt like granting something Ysolde hadn't earned.

But that was before. Before the edict. Before the shrine and the blizzard and Tam Voss pulling her out of the snow by both wrists. Before Brannoc had stood in the lamplight of the refectory and said, in his flat gravel voice, *the king is not a tyrant, girl, he's a hostage* — and before Osk had taken her hand with his small cold fingers and shown her the thing wearing Aldous of Vane like a coat.

Before she had seen the binding.

She knew knots. Her whole life in the passes had been knots — cargo knots, rescue knots, the quick-release hitch that saved a mule from a crevasse. And when Osk had opened the king's mind to her, when she had looked down into that cellar of a man where something ancient sat wearing his crown, she had seen the shape of the chains holding him: sevenfold, woven, each strand anchored to a season and a stone. It was a style. A signature. The way a farrier's hammer-strikes lived in every shoe he made.

It was Ysolde's work. She had known it the way you know your own mother's footsteps on a stair.

So now she read.

*August, my heart, forgive me. I did a terrible thing when I was younger than you are now, and I have spent my life paying for it, and I am a coward, because I could not pay for it in front of you.*

The second letter was older — the ink browner, the paper soft as cloth. In it, Ysolde wrote of a winter long ago, of a young king newly crowned and beloved, and of a circle of magicians summoned to the capital to perform a great working. They had been told it was a warding. A protection for the realm. Seven of them, the finest of their generation, brought into a frozen chapel beneath the palace and asked to weave a binding around a darkness the court mages claimed had taken root in the king's dreams.

*We believed them,* Ysolde wrote. *Gods forgive us, we believed them. We thought we were saving him. It was only after — after the chapel doors sealed and the cold came up through the floor like a tide — that I understood we had not bound the darkness in the king. We had bound the king inside the darkness.*

August stopped. Her thumb had gone white pressing against the table's edge.

Around her, Candlemere went about its evening. Somewhere above, Wren was arguing with Lark about ration weights; the sound came down through the stone as a faint, domestic murmur, like birds in an eave. Sef moved among her jars at the far end of the room, grinding something that smelled of bitter resin, and gave August a single glance — the healer's glance, cataloguing, kind — and said nothing. They had learned, the Choir, to leave August alone with her silences. Mostly.

She turned the page.

*His name was Aldous. He laughed at my bad poetry. He gave me his cloak in the chapel because I was shivering, and then we closed our hands around him and sang him into a prison, and he looked at me — through the Regent's first rising, through all of it — he looked at me and he was not angry. That is the worst of it, August. Thirty years I have carried it, and the worst of it is that he was not angry. He was afraid, and then he was sorry for US.*

The word *Regent* appeared for the first time in the third letter, written smaller than the rest, as if Ysolde had been afraid of it even on paper. The entity had a name of its own, one so old the ink seemed to flinch from it; Ysolde had refused to write it. *Names are handles,* she had scrawled in the margin. *It taught me that. Never give it a handle.* The Regent, then. The court's polite fiction. The thing that had spoken through the young king's mouth within a day of the binding, thanked the seven magicians graciously for their service, and sent them home with purses of gold and a memory-wipe so thorough that six of the seven never questioned anything again.

Six of the seven.

*I broke,* the fourth letter said. *I woke one morning three years later with his laugh in my ears and no memory of where I'd heard it, and I wept until noon over a man whose face I couldn't recall, and I went to the archives, and I dug, and I dug, and what I found under the pretty official histories made me sick for a month. So I went back. Under pretense of pilgrimage. I got into the chapel. I saw what we had made.*

And here the handwriting changed — grew tighter, faster, the loops sharpening into something almost like claws. August recognized the shift. She had seen it once before, in Ysolde's last year at the cottage above Millbrook, when the old woman had worked late into the nights on something she wouldn't name, muttering over diagrams August had been forbidden to touch.

*I tried a small unbinding. One thread of seven. I thought — one thread, carefully, gently, like drawing a splinter. The Regent noticed me before I finished the second verse. It came up through the king's mouth and it spoke to me in HIS voice and it said: little witch, I remember your hands. Then it showed me what it would do — to the kingdom, to the Gifted, to anyone who had ever loved me — if I cut so much as one more thread. And it showed me what it would do to ME. There are worse things than death, August. It has thirty years of practice imagining them.*

August became aware, distantly, that her breath had gone ragged. Sef had drifted closer without seeming to move, and now set a cup of something hot at her elbow — willowbark and honey, the healer's answer to everything — and retreated again without a word. August wrapped her hands around the cup and did not drink it. The warmth helped anyway.

The fifth letter was the one that explained everything, and it explained nothing, and August read it four times.

*The unbinding rite exists. I built it, over twenty years, from the bones of the binding itself — every knot wants its own loosening, that is the first law of the craft, and this knot is no different, only larger and crueler. But hear me, my heart, hear me and do not hate me further: the rite cannot be cast by me. I am one of the binders; my hands are part of the knot. To loose it I would have to unmake myself, thread by thread, and the Regent would feel every thread go and would know exactly where to strike while I came apart. No. The rite needs someone OUTSIDE the weaving. Someone with the binder's craft in their blood or training. Someone the Regent has never seen.*

*I trained you for this, August. Do you understand? Every drill you hated, every lesson in silence and patience and hiding your light — I was not teaching you smuggler's tricks. I was building the one tool the Regent has no grip on. You are the splinter it cannot find.*

*And that is why I left. Not because I tired of you. Never that. Because the Regent found me. Last autumn, in the market at Harrow Ford, a king's clerk smiled at me too long, and I saw the shadow behind his eyes, and I knew my thirty years of hiding were over. If it takes me, it takes everything I know — including you. So I ran north, alone, toward the one place it cannot easily look, carrying the last pieces of the rite in my head, and I left you the letters because I am a coward twice over: I could not say goodbye to your face.*

*Find the singers, August. The rite needs a song — a living harmony to hold the threads apart while they're loosed, voices braided tight enough that the Regent cannot slither between the notes. I met such singers once, years ago, in the mountains under the old monastery at Candlemere. If any survive, they will know the half of it already. They dream of the king. One of their children hears him.*

August put the letter down very carefully, as though it might bruise.

Osk. *One of their children hears him.* The boy who couldn't sleep for the weight of another man's dreams, who flinched at bells, who had taken August's hand in the dark and shown her a prison with a king in it — Ysolde had known about him. Had perhaps, in her wandering years, sat in these same halls, in this same lamplight, and planned all of this decades before August was born into it.

She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. Grief rose in her the way floodwater rises in a well-shaft — silently, from below, filling spaces she hadn't known were empty. All those years she had hated Ysolde. All those mornings in the cottage, watching the old woman stare north through the window with that terrible fixed attention, August had read it as regret, as longing for a life without a burdensome apprentice. She had built her whole wounded self around that interpretation. *She left because I was not enough. She left because I was too much. She left because—*

Because the alternative — that Ysolde had run to save her, had spent her entire remaining life constructing a weapon and hiding it in plain sight as a grumpy hedge-witch teaching a stubborn girl to keep her head down — that alternative was unbearable, and so August had never once let herself think it.

Until now.

She thought it now. It went through her like a blade going through wet canvas, slow and total, and what bled out of the wound was not blood but every bitter story she had told herself for eleven years.

* * *

She did not come out of the workroom for two days.

The Choir, being the Choir, handled it the way they handled everything: by orbiting her quietly. Sef left food. Lark left a joke, written on a scrap and slid under the door, which was so bad that August laughed despite herself — a short, cracked sound that surprised them both. Wren sat outside the door for an hour on the second night, saying nothing, just being a warm presence on the other side of the wood, and August found that this helped more than words would have.

It was Brannoc who finally came in.

He didn't knock. He filled the doorway the way he filled every doorway, big and grey-bearded and immovable, and he looked at the letters spread across the table, and at August sitting among them like a survivor among wreckage, and he pulled up the second stool and sat.

"Ysolde," he said.

"You knew her." August's voice came out flat. "In the fifth letter. She says she met singers here."

"Thirty years ago. She was younger than you are now, the second time we met — after the binding, though we didn't know that's what it was. She came looking for us with a stolen archive page and a wild story, and Tam's grandmother listened to the whole thing and then sang a verse of it, and Ysolde cried like the world was ending." Brannoc's hands lay open on his knees. "We've kept the song since. Half-remembered. Waiting."

"For what?"

"For someone who could finish it." He looked at her then, directly, with those pale eyes that missed nothing. "You've read what the rite costs."

August's jaw tightened. "She doesn't say. Not plainly. There's a passage in the seventh letter—" She stopped. Started again. "She writes around it. Like she's trying to spare me even on paper."

"She was sparing you," Brannoc said. "That was her whole art, in the end. Hiding things to keep them safe." He leaned forward, elbows on knees. "I'll not lie to you, girl, because you'll need your wits about you and lies rot wits. An unbinding of that size needs an anchor. Something to hold the shape of the knot while it's loosed, so it doesn't simply tear and take the king and half the capital with it. The anchor has to be a caster. A strong one. Close enough to the binding to resonate with it."

"And?"

"And the anchor burns," Brannoc said simply. "The rite draws the Gift through the anchor like water through a sieve. All of it. What you are, poured out entirely, to buy what he is, freed." He held her gaze. "Ysolde couldn't pay it — not because she feared death, but because her death mid-rite would free nothing and hand the Regent a corpse full of secrets. So she built it for someone else. She built it for you, and she prayed every day of her life that you'd never have to use it."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere far above, wind moved through the ruined monastery, and the mountain hummed its note.

August looked down at her hands. Hands that had carried contraband over ice. Hands that had pulled a stranger from a crown soldier's grasp with a whisper of borrowed heat. Hands Ysolde had shaped, deliberately, patiently, across a thousand hated lessons, into the exact instrument this moment required.

She had spent eleven years believing she'd been abandoned. The truth was worse and better in equal measure: she had been *chosen*. Chosen the way you choose a keystone, knowing what the arch will ask of it.

"I'm not angry at her anymore," August said, and heard how strange her own voice sounded. "That's the mad thing, Brannoc. Eleven years of anger, and now I read her handwriting and there's just — nothing left to be angry with. Only her. Only an old woman running north with a weapon in her head, trying to get it somewhere it could be used before the thing in the crown caught her."

Brannoc nodded slowly. "Grief does that. Turns to purpose, given time and a sharp enough reason." He rose, joints cracking, and paused at the door. "The vote comes soon. The Choir will decide whether we attempt this at all — kill the king or free him, and either road runs through blood. When it does, I'd have you speak. Not as Ysolde's ghost. As yourself."

After he left, August sat a long time with the eleventh letter, the newest one, the one dated barely two years past, the one she had saved for last because some part of her had known it would hurt most.

*August, my heart. If you are reading this, then either the Regent has found me, or my legs have finally failed, and either way the rite must pass to you entire. Everything is in these pages. The verses. The anchors. The price. I have tried to write it so that you might choose NOT to pay it — I want that on the record, in ink, in my own hand: you owe him nothing, you owe this kingdom nothing, and any life you spend should be your own to spend or keep.*

*But if you do go — if you stand in that frozen chapel where I stood as a foolish girl in a borrowed cloak — then know that I have loved you every day since the day they put you in my arms, and that love is the only spell I ever cast cleanly, without cost, without regret. Whatever you decide, decide it free. That is my last lesson, and my hardest: the craft gives nothing freely, but the choosing is yours, always yours, and no power in this world — not crowns, not entities, not old women's guilt — may take it from you.*

*Forgive me. Or don't. Come find me, or don't. But live, August. Live loudly enough to spite us both.*

August folded the letter along its worn creases and held it against her sternum for a long moment, the way you hold something warm in deep cold.

Then she cleared the table, took fresh paper, and began to write — lists, diagrams, questions for Brannoc, the skeleton of a plan. Outside, she could hear the Choir gathering in the refectory for the evening meal, their voices rising in the old grace they sang before bread, and underneath the melody, faint and persistent as a pulse through stone, she fancied she could hear the mountain itself listening.

Three days later, Tam Voss came to her at dawn, face pale as tallow, and said the words that ended the waiting:

"There's a rider coming down the pass. Crown green. And August — she's asking for you by name."
