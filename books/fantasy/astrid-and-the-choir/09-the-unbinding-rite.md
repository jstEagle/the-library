## 9. The Unbinding Rite

The letters had stopped making sense the way a river stops making sense when you are drowning in it — every word still legible, every sentence still Ysolde's voice in August's skull, and yet none of it adding up to anything she could hold.

She sat in the scriptorium of Candlemere's undercroft with six pages spread before her on the scarred oak table, weighted at the corners with candlesticks, a chipped mortar, Wren's abandoned deck of fortune-cards. Three days she had been reading. Three days of deciphering her mentor's cramped hand, written in two inks because Ysolde had run out of brown halfway through and refused to stop for so trivial a thing as ink. That was like her too. Everything about these letters was like her, and that was the wound of it: this was not the writing of a woman who had fled in fear. This was the writing of a woman building something, carefully, over years, and hiding it even from the girl sleeping one thin wall away.

*The binding is not a knot,* Ysolde had written, in the violet ink, the hand shakier here. *A knot can be cut. It is a chord — three notes struck together and sustained for thirty years by the thing's own hunger. To silence it you must unstrike each note in turn, and you must be, in some true sense, the same instrument.*

August read the passage again. Then again. Then she pushed back from the table and went to find Brannoc, because there were things in these pages that could not sit alone in her chest a single hour longer.

She found him in the refectory, which the Choir had converted into something between a war room and a kitchen. Maps of the southern passes were pinned to one wall; a pot of barley soup argued gently with itself over the fire; Sef was grinding willow bark at the far bench while Lark tried to teach Osk to fold a paper boat and failing magnificently, mostly because Osk kept looking at the middle distance where, everyone now knew, the king's dreams lived.

Brannoc looked up when she came in. He was a big man gone quietly grey, with the stillness of someone who had learned to measure his movements the way other men measured their words. "You've finished," he said. Not a question. He'd seen her face.

"I've finished." Her voice came out strange and flat, and she had to start again. "It needs three things. Only three."

The room changed. Sef set down her pestle. Lark stopped folding. Even Osk came back from wherever he'd been.

"Say them," Brannoc said.

"A bloodline or an apprentice of the binder." August swallowed. "That part is me. Ysolde helped make the binding — I told you what the letters said, but now I understand *how*. She wasn't just present. She was one of the three notes." Saying it aloud still felt like swallowing glass. The woman who had raised her, taught her, vanished without a goodbye — one of the architects of thirty years of buried king. "The second is a song. A specific one, sung by voices bound to each other, which—" she gestured vaguely at all of them, at the Choir, whose whole existence was harmony, whose name was not an accident, "—which is why Ysolde wrote to Candlemere. Why she knew this place. She built the rite *for* you. For whoever would come after her."

"And the third?" Brannoc asked. His voice was very level, and August had spent enough months among these people to know what that levelness cost him.

"The third is a price," she said. "Freely chosen. Named aloud at the moment of casting, and paid in full before the last note fades. The rite doesn't work otherwise. Ysolde writes it three times, in different ways, as if she couldn't believe it herself. The price must be chosen freely, and it must be great enough that the caster feels its absence forever." She looked down at her own hands. "Magic answers to balance. You cannot unmake a thirty-year binding without giving up something equal to it."

Silence. The soup muttered. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the ruined monastery and dislodged a stone, and the small clatter of it seemed enormous.

Then Brannoc said, in the tone of a man setting a single coin on a table: "Then we pay a life. We pay the king's."

* * *

He did not shout. That was the terrible thing about Brannoc — he never shouted, not even now, when August could see the old grief rise in him like floodwater finding its mark. He stood at the head of the refectory table that evening with the whole Choir gathered around it, nineteen faces in the candlelight, and he spoke the way he did everything: precisely, patiently, like a man laying stones.

"I have listened," he said. "I want that understood first. August brought us Ysolde's rite, and I have listened to it, and I have found nothing false in it. The magic will work. I believe her. I believe the letters. I believe our king is a prisoner and the Regent is the jailer wearing his face — I believed it the night Osk first screamed himself awake, though I hated believing it, and I hate it still." He laid both hands flat on the table. "And none of it changes my answer."

"Brannoc—" Tam began.

"Let me finish. Thirty years." His voice did not break, but something in it bent, the way timber bends before it goes. "Thirty years of purges, quiet at first and then not quiet. My village was called Harrow's End. There is nothing there now but a chimney and a well, and I have been to look. The soldiers came in the king's name. The proclamations bore the king's seal. Every mother in the ground from Millbrook to the coast went into it with that seal ringing in her ears, and whether the man inside the crown is weeping or screaming or dead a hundred times over, I do not care, because *his hands did it*. His signature. His authority." He straightened. "Here is my arithmetic, and I'll stand by it in front of any god. One life, freely given, against thirty years of slaughter ended tonight. One old man in a gilded cell against every Gift-bearer yet unborn who will hang from a scaffold if the Regent rules another decade. If the rite demands a price equal to the binding, then let the price be the thing bound. Let Aldous die, and the entity die with him, root and branch. That is not cruelty. That is the gentlest mathematics anyone has offered this kingdom in thirty years."

Nobody spoke. August watched the faces around the table and saw that he had moved them. Sef was nodding slowly. Two of the older singers, Hesta and Corvin, had the hard, wet-eyed look of people hearing their own grief spoken aloud by someone braver.

"And I say it with love," Brannoc added, quieter, turning to August. "I want you to hear that. What you're offering — your mentor's rite, your mentor's sin to undo — it's the most generous thing I've ever heard of. But generosity is not the same as rightness, and mercy that costs the world another thirty years is just a slower cruelty with better manners."

"The price doesn't have to be a death," August said.

"It has to be *equal*, by your own telling."

"Equal isn't identical." She rose. Her legs felt like borrowed things. "Ysolde was explicit — the price is chosen, and it is paid in the coin of the caster. Not in blood. In *loss*. Listen to me, all of you." She looked around the table, meeting eyes: Tam's steady dark gaze, Lark's sharp one, Sef's tired kindness, Osk's frightened twelve-year-old face. "The binding was made by three casters striking one chord. Ysolde, and two others whose names I haven't found yet — she calls them only 'the others' and writes about them like wounds. To unbind, the same chord must sound, and the caster who strikes it must give up something *of the same nature as the binding itself*. Do you understand what the binding is made of?" Her voice shook and she let it. "Power. Gift. Ysolde poured her own magic into that knot thirty years ago — that's in the letters, plain as daylight once you know to look. She didn't flee the purge. She fled the *cost*. She spent her whole strength on the binding and spent thirty more years trying to find a way to undo it without—"

"Without paying it again," Sef said softly.

"Without paying it again." August nodded. "And she never found one. Because there isn't one. The unbinding takes the caster's Gift entire. That's the price. That's the only price the rite names as sufficient — everything, given freely, forever." She heard the words leave her and was surprised they sounded calm. Inside, some part of her was already grieving, had been grieving since the third day of reading, quietly, in a corner of herself she'd locked and walked away from. "I'm her apprentice. Bloodline or apprentice — the rite allows either, and I am the only one left. So the price falls to me, and I am telling you I choose it. My Gift, for his freedom. All of it. I've done the arithmetic too, Brannoc, and mine says no one has to die."

For a moment the table wavered toward her — she saw it in Lark's face, in Tam's, in the way Osk leaned forward like a plant toward light. And then Brannoc shook his head, slow and sorrowful, and the moment broke.

"You'd blind yourself," he said. "Permanently. You'd cut out the thing that makes you *you* — I've watched you work, August, I've seen what your power is to you, it's closer than your heartbeat — and for what? For a man we have never met, whose guilt or innocence we take on faith from a boy's nightmares? And what does it buy? You pull the Regent out of Aldous, and then what? An old man with thirty empty years, a kingdom that remembers only his edicts, an entity that — what, dies? Are we certain? Or does it go looking for another crown?" He spread his hands. "Kill the vessel and the parasite dies with it. Free the vessel and maybe you free them both, and maybe you loose it on the world again, and you'll have paid your soul for the privilege. I cannot vote for that gamble. I won't."

"The Regent dies with the binding," August said. "Ysolde is clear—"

"Ysolde is a woman who ran away," Brannoc said, and the room flinched. "Forgive me. But she bound the king and then she vanished, and her great corrective rite requires a sacrifice so vast that no one sane would pay it, and you are asking nineteen hunted people to bet everything on her certainty. I buried my certainty in Harrow's End. I don't have any left to spend."

* * *

The argument did not end that night. It did not end for four days.

It moved through Candlemere like weather. At meals the Choir split along invisible seams — Hesta and Corvin and the twins' cousin Merrit siding with Brannoc, drawn to the clean finality of his answer; Tam and Lark and Sef drifting toward August, not out of certainty, most of them admitted, but out of an unwillingness to build salvation on a grave. Wren refused to speak of it at all and dealt fortunes nobody asked for, and every card she turned seemed to come up the Tower, and eventually Sef took the deck away from her.

Osk did not vote. When the question finally reached him — gently, because he was twelve and already carried more than any of them — he looked at the floor and said, "He dreams about apples. The king. Little green ones from a tree in his mother's garden, and he can't remember how they taste anymore, and neither can I, because they're his dreams, not mine." Then he went to the chapel alcove and sat with the paper boats until supper, and no one pressed him, and August thought: *there is the whole argument, settled by a child who didn't mean to settle it.* You could kill the dreamer. But you would still be killing the apples.

On the fourth evening Brannoc came to her in the scriptorium. He stood in the doorway a long time, filling it, before he entered and sat across the table from her among the scattered letters.

"I was at Harrow's End when they came," he said, unprompted, to the candle flame. "I was in the hills. I watched from the ridge. Eleven houses. It took the soldiers less than an hour, and they sang on the march out — not cruelly, that's the thing people never believe. They sang because they believed they were cleansing something. Because a seal on a proclamation is easier to trust than your own eyes." He looked up. "I have spent fifteen years keeping these people alive, August. That is the whole of me now. Whatever I was before is in that well. So when you ask me to gamble them on mercy — understand that you are not asking a hard man for a soft thing. You are asking a frightened one."

"I know," August said.

"Do you?" He rubbed his face with both hands, and for a moment he looked every year of his age. "Your rite. The song. Could the Choir sing it? Truly?"

"They could. It's built for voices like yours. Bound to each other." She hesitated. "It's built for a family, Brannoc. I think that's what Ysolde meant Candlemere to be. I think that's what she was apologizing to, in the letters. Over and over. She writes 'forgive the choir' in the margin of the last page like it's a person."

Brannoc was silent a long while. The candle burned between them, and outside, far above, the mountain wind made its endless argument with the ruins.

"If we do this," he said at last, heavily, "we do it my way. Full vote. Everyone. No proxies, no absences, and no one shamed for their answer — not ever, not by anyone. And if the vote goes to the unbinding, then I will lead the singing myself, and I will carry that old man's freedom like a stone in my teeth, and I will never tell you whether it chokes me. That is the best mercy I have in me. Take it or leave it."

"That's not nothing," August said. "That's everything."

"It's less than it looks." He rose, and paused at the door, not turning around. "Tomorrow, then. The vote. Sleep, if you can."

When he was gone she sat among Ysolde's letters until the candles guttered, and read one last passage, the one she had read forty times and would read forty more:

*They will tell you the price is too high. The price is always too high. Pay it anyway, and pay it gladly, and do not tell them beforehand what it is, because love makes cowards of the people who ought to help you carry it.*

* * *

The vote was held at dawn, in the chapel proper, up in the ruin, because Brannoc said that whatever they decided deserved sky over it. Nineteen of them stood among the fallen pillars with frost on their shoulders and their breath rising like incense, and Brannoc explained the choice once, plainly, without argument — kill the king, or free him — and called the roll.

Fourteen names were spoken before the count mattered. Seven and seven.

Then Osk.

The boy stood with his fists in his pockets, looking smaller than anyone had ever seen him, and the whole mountain seemed to lean in. He looked at Brannoc. He looked at August. And then he said, in a voice that cracked down the middle:

"He wants his apples back."

Lark made a sound that was half laugh, half sob. Osk stepped over to stand beside August, ears red in the cold, and did not look at anyone.

Seven and eight. One voice left. Brannoc closed his eyes, and August watched him stand there at the head of his shattered, chosen family with fifteen years of graves on his back, and she watched him open his mouth to cast the deciding vote either way — and that was when Tam, scanning the eastern ridgeline out of a singer's habit of always knowing where an audience might be, went suddenly still and said:

"There's smoke coming over Thornfell pass. A lot of it. And it's moving fast."
