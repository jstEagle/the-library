## 10. What Osk Hears in the Dark

The king dreamed of snow the night it first spoke back.

Osk knew it was snow because he could feel it — not on his skin, but in that strange interior place where the dreams arrived, the cellar of himself where he had learned to sit and listen. The dream-snow fell upward, which was wrong, and each flake was a small white tooth, which was worse. Osk had learned, over three years of this unwanted gift, to sort the king's dreams from ordinary nightmares by their wrongness. Ordinary nightmares belonged to people. These belonged to whatever wore the man like a coat.

*You have good ears,* said the voice.

Osk had been sitting cross-legged on his pallet in the third undercroft of Candlemere, past midnight, doing what Brannoc called "the listening discipline" — opening the connection just wide enough to map it, never wide enough to drown. It was a practice like holding one's breath underwater. He had been getting better at it. He had gotten so much better at it.

He came out of the trance so fast he cracked his head on the low ceiling and sat there in the dark with his heart slamming, waiting for the voice to continue.

It did not. Not that night.

But he had heard it, and hearing went both ways, and somewhere beneath the palace in the capital, in the frozen chapel or wherever the thing actually lived, something ancient and patient had turned its attention — slow as a lighthouse beam — toward a boy in a mountain who had been eavesdropping for three years.

* * *

He told no one.

That was the shame of it, and the shame came quickly, arriving before the fear even finished unpacking. He lay awake in the dark of the cell he shared with Lark and rehearsed the sentence he would say at breakfast: *The Regent spoke to me last night.* Four words, more or less. Simple words. He practiced them against the ceiling until dawn greyed the arrow-slit windows, and then at breakfast he ate his porridge and said nothing, because Wren made a joke about Sef's lopsided bandaging, and Tam was humming the harmonies for the listening discipline, and everyone was laughing, and the truth sat in Osk's chest like a swallowed stone.

What would he even say? That the entity that had eaten a kingdom had noticed him the way a man notices a fly walking on his wrist? That he, Osk of nowhere, sixteen years old, whose gift was nothing but listening — no fire, no healing, no songs that could crack stone — had attracted the attention of the oldest intelligence in the world?

They would send him away. That was the thought underneath all the other thoughts, the one he never let come fully into the light. The Choir kept him because he was useful. His listening had found three hunted families safe passage; his listening had warned them of the soldiers on the western road before the scouts did. Useful. But a boy through whom the enemy could speak? A boy who had been touched, marked, possibly already turned? Brannoc was kind, but Brannoc had buried a wife and two children under the king's edicts, and Brannoc's kindness had edges you could bleed on. Sef would want to look inside him. Wren and Lark would watch him differently forever. And August — August, who had come to them half-frozen and furious and was slowly, grudgingly becoming his favorite of all of them — August knew more about Ysolde's binding than anyone, and she would look at him and see exactly what he was beginning to see when he caught his reflection in the rain barrel:

A door. A door left open too long.

* * *

It spoke again the second night, and the third, and by the end of the week it had stopped bothering with the king's dreams at all.

That was how Osk understood, finally, the shape of what was happening. For three years, listening to the king had been like pressing his ear to a wall — muffled, layered, the Regent's presence only a pressure behind everything, a cold weight like deep water. Now the wall was gone. Now there was a room, and a chair, and something sitting in it, waiting for him.

*I've watched you grow,* it said, on the seventh night, while Osk sat pretending to sleep. *You were such a small thing when you first found me. Frightened. Your sister had been gone — what, a year? You listened for her every night. You never found her, but you found me instead, and I gave you something to hold in the dark. Didn't I?*

"Shut up," Osk whispered into his pillow.

*Your friends are asleep twelve feet above you,* the Regent said, conversational, unhurried. It never hurried. Hurrying was for creatures with something to fear. *Wren snores. Lark sleeps like the dead and pretends otherwise in the morning. The healer checks on the new mother twice a night because she lost a patient in the spring and hasn't forgiven herself. I know them, Osk. I have known them longer than you have. Kings keep very complete records.*

"Stop it."

*The girl who came to you in the snow — August Blackwood. Ysolde's apprentice. Do you know what Ysolde did to me? What she's planning? You do know. You've heard her letters in August's mind when she reads them late at night, haven't you? You hear everything. That's the trouble with doors: they let things in, and they let things out.*

Osk pulled the blanket over his head, which was childish and useless and he did it anyway. The voice didn't need his ears. It lived in the same cellar he did now, the one he'd spent three years furnishing without knowing who would eventually move in.

*I could take your sister back,* it said.

Everything in Osk went still.

*Yes. I know where she is. The purges were thorough but I am more thorough than any purge. She is alive, Osk. Working in a dyer's yard in Marrowfield, with cropped hair and a new name, and she thinks about you every day, usually at dusk. Shall I tell you what she thinks?*

"Don't." His voice broke on it. He hated that it broke. "Don't you touch her. Don't you say her name."

*Then we understand each other,* the Regent said, gently, the way you'd speak to a child you'd already decided to hurt later, at your convenience. *Sleep well, little listener.*

* * *

The offers began after that, and they were never crude. That was the horror of it. The Regent did not promise him fireballs or thrones, not at first. It promised information. Where the crown's next sweep would fall. Which pass would close with the thaw. Whether Inquisitor Vale had found the burned letters — and Osk, listening involuntarily, realized with a sick lurch that the Regent knew about Maren Vale, knew about the unburned page, knew things about their danger that the Choir itself did not know, and every night he held that knowledge in his chest like a coal and told no one.

Because telling meant confessing. And confessing meant admitting the contact had gone on for weeks. And admitting the contact had gone on for weeks meant admitting every night he'd lain awake and said nothing while the thing pried at the seams of him, learning the shape of the hole where Mira used to be, learning the geography of his guilt the way water learns a coastline.

Shame, August had told him once, in one of her rare moments of unprompted gentleness — she'd caught him crying behind the cistern, months ago, after a dream about Mira — shame is a hook. It only works if you swallow it. Spit it out and show someone and it's just a hook, small and stupid, easy to pull free.

Good advice. He thought about it every night, lying in the dark with the Regent's voice pouring honey and ice into the cellar of him, and every morning he woke up and swallowed it again.

* * *

The visions started on the night of the new moon, when there was no light to soften anything.

The Regent had grown tired, apparently, of conversation. Or perhaps this was simply the next course of the meal. Osk closed his eyes and saw Candlemere as the entity saw it — or wanted him to see it, he couldn't tell anymore, and that inability was its own kind of wound. He saw the monastery ruins from above, moonless, beautiful, the hidden stair down into the undercrofts exposed like a throat. He saw crown soldiers descending it in the grey hour before dawn, moving with the terrible competence of men who had done this many times. He heard Tam singing and then heard Tam stop singing, mid-note, and the silence where the note should have continued was the worst sound Osk had ever heard.

He saw Brannoc kneeling in the great hall of the undercroft with his hands bound, and the vision lingered there, lovingly, on Brannoc's face — not pain, exactly. Something worse. The expression of a man doing arithmetic, counting the members of his family, again, and watching the number go to zero.

He saw Wren and Lark trying to reach each other across a burning refectory and failing, and failing, and failing. The vision replayed it. The Regent had a connoisseur's relationship with failure.

And he saw August — he saw her standing in front of the others with her hands raised and nothing coming out of them, nothing at all, her power gone or taken or never enough, and the Regent's amusement rolled through the vision like weather, vast and indifferent. *This is what you're protecting,* it said. *A choir with no song. A witch who will be powerless at the moment it matters. Ask her why, little listener. Ask her what the rite costs. She knows. She's known since she read the third letter, and she hasn't told them either.*

Osk opened his eyes.

For a long moment he lay there, drenched, shaking, cataloguing what was true and what was poison. That was the discipline, wasn't it — the listening discipline turned inward, separating the signal from the flood. Soldiers: maybe true, maybe threat. The deaths: maybe prophecy, mostly likely theater. But August — the price, the third letter, the cost of the rite —

That part rang true. He had heard fragments of it himself, drifting up out of August's focused mind when she read by candlelight three cells away and forgot, as she always forgot, that the boy who heard everything was sleeping down the hall. Fragments: *the caster's Gift entire.* He had thought he'd misunderstood. He had hoped.

She was going to pay herself. And she hadn't told them.

And now the Regent knew he knew, and had shown him deliberately, sewing distrust between them before Osk could even decide whom to trust with his own rot.

"It doesn't work," Osk whispered to the dark. "I'm not going to turn on them."

*Of course you aren't,* the Regent said, with something horribly like affection. *You misunderstand me entirely. I don't want you to betray anyone. Betrayal is loud. I only need you to keep being ashamed. Shame is such a quiet thing, Osk. It seals mouths better than any spell I know. Just keep swallowing the hook. Keep it exactly where it is.*

And through him — he felt it happen, felt it like a fisherman feeling the line go taut — the Regent looked out through the cellar of him, up through three years of accumulated intimacy, through every careful mapping of the listening paths, through the geography Osk himself had drawn in his mind of the mountain's veins and hollows, the hidden stair, the warding-song boundaries, the exact depth and bearing of the third undercroft where the Choir slept.

It had never needed to search. It had simply needed a boy who loved his sister and hated himself, and to wait.

Somewhere far away, in the direction of the capital, something finished taking note, the way a ledger closes.

* * *

In the morning, Osk sat across from August at breakfast. She pushed him the honey jar without being asked, the way she always did, and complained about Brannoc's training regimen, the way she always did, and laughed at something Tam said — actually laughed, an unguarded bark of a laugh she seemed almost surprised by — and Osk looked at her thin, scarred, stubborn face and thought: *she's going to give them everything, and I'm going to be the reason it isn't enough.*

"You look awful," August said, narrowing her eyes at him. "Bad dreams?"

"No," Osk said.

The lie came out smooth and easy, worn smooth by weeks of use, and he smiled while he said it, and no one at the table noticed anything at all except, perhaps, the way the candle between them guttered once, though there was no draft, though the doors were shut, though the mountain above them had never been quieter.

Down in the cellar of himself, very softly, the Regent began to hum.
