## 11. Ash Wednesday Market

The town was called Hollowmere, and it smelled of woodsmoke and tallow and the particular sourness of fear that had lived in a place too long.

August saw it from the ridge an hour before they reached the gates: a gray smudge of rooftops cupped in a frozen valley, smoke rising thin and straight in the windless cold. Around it, the fields lay white and empty, stubble poking through snow like the stubble on a shaved head. Somewhere beyond those roofs, in a market square, people would be buying bread and lamp oil and rope, haggling over pennies, complaining about the price of salt — all of it under the shadow of a proclamation nailed to every door in the kingdom.

*The Gift is a plague. Bearers will be culled.*

She had burned her mentor's letters rather than let them be found. She had watched Tam Voss nearly freeze to death on a mountainside. She had learned, in the weeks since Candlemere, what it meant to walk through the world with something coiled behind her ribs that the crown now called disease. And still, looking down at Hollowmere, her first thought was not *danger*. It was *bread*. Real bread, wheat bread, with a crust — not the hard black rye Brannoc baked in the monastery's old ovens, dense enough to drive nails.

"Everyone's thinking it," Lark said beside her. The twin had come up the ridge silent as falling snow; August hadn't heard her approach at all. "Don't pretend you're above it."

"I'm thinking about components," August said.

"You're thinking about the honey cakes they sell at winter markets. I can tell. You have the face."

"What face?"

"The face Osk makes when he smells Sef's feverfew tea." Lark grinned, then sobered as quickly as she'd brightened — she did that, flipped moods like coins. "Brannoc wants you in the front group. He says your smuggler's face is the best we've got."

"My smuggler's face."

"You spent years passing contraband through crown checkpoints without blinking. That's a face worth more than any of our papers." Lark patted her arm. "Just don't use any magic. Not a spark. Inquisitors can taste it in the air for days afterward, Maren Vale's especially. Or so Wren heard from a drover who heard it from a man who sold horses to the palace guard, which is how we know everything we know."

The plan was simple, because simple plans were the only ones Brannoc trusted. Four of them would go into Hollowmere: August, Lark, Tam, and Sef. They would split into pairs, buy what the rite required — grave-soil from unconsecrated ground, ash from a fire that had burned a whole night, three silver pennies minted before the old king's reign, beeswax blessed by no one, and a dozen smaller things on Ysolde's list that August had copied out in code — and they would be back through the north gate by midday, before the market filled and the guards grew bored. Brannoc, Wren, and Osk would wait with the cart two miles out, in a stand of black pines, ready to run if anyone came.

Osk had argued to come along. Osk always argued to come along, and lately there had been something frantic underneath the arguing, something August couldn't name but kept catching at the edge of her eye — the boy flinching at nothing, staring into fires too long, going quiet in the middle of sentences. When Brannoc refused him this morning, Osk hadn't sulked. He'd looked *relieved*, and that frightened August more than any argument would have.

"He wanted to be told no," she said to Tam on the road down, quietly, when Lark had run ahead.

Tam pulled his scarf up over his chin. His voice, when he spoke, was muffled and wry. "I know. I sang next to him last night. Something's got its fingers in him, August. Something cold." He glanced sideways at her. "He won't say what."

"Should we make him?"

"Have you ever made a boy that age tell you anything?"

August thought of herself at fifteen, lying to Ysolde about where she'd been, what she'd touched, what she'd read. "No," she admitted. "But I don't like carrying secrets I can't see the shape of."

"That's the thing about secrets." Tam's eyes crinkled above the scarf. "You never do. You just learn to sing around them."

* * *

Hollowmere's gate was a single arch guarded by two soldiers who were warmer than their post deserved and knew it. One held out a hand for papers; the other held out a hand for coins, and both transactions went smoothly, because August had spent half her life learning exactly how much grease a wheel needed.

Inside, the town closed around her like a fist.

She had forgotten how loud towns were. Candlemere was silence and stone-sleep and the small sounds of nine people trying not to be found. Hollowmere was dogs and cartwheels and a smith hammering somewhere and children shrieking down an alley and a crier at the square's edge reading the proclamation yet again, his voice flat with repetition: *—bearers of the so-called Gift are declared carriers of plague, to be reported, seized, and culled by order of his majesty King Aldous, whose mercy is infinite—*

*Whose mercy is infinite.* August walked past the crier with her basket on her arm and her eyes down and thought: *if you only knew whose mouth that mercy comes out of.* She thought of Osk's drawings, the ones he did in his sleep — a throne room rendered over and over in charcoal, and on the throne a shape that was a man and also very much not a man, crowned in something that moved.

Lark peeled off toward the chandler's for the wax. Sef, wrapped in a widow's grays, went to the herbalist for the dried things on the list. August took Tam with her to find the sexton of the old church, because grave-soil was the one component that couldn't be bought honestly and she preferred to handle dishonesty herself.

The church of Hollowmere stood at the square's far end, squat and slate-roofed, its bell tower leaning like a tired old man. Its graveyard had been full for a hundred years, which meant the sexton kept a second yard outside the walls for the unbaptized and the unclaimed and the suicides — unconsecrated ground, exactly what Ysolde's list demanded. August found the man in his shed, a leathery creature named Doss who smelled of pipe smoke, and laid out her story with the smoothness of long practice: she was buying soil for her sister's memorial planting, poor girl died unbaptized, family shame, coin attached, no questions wanted.

Doss took the coin. Doss asked one question anyway, because there was always one.

"You're not from here."

"No."

"Strange time to travel." He jerked his chin toward the window, toward the square, where the crier had stopped and a knot of people had gathered around something. "They posted new lists yesterday. Names. Folk taken in the purges down south. Half the town came to look, hoping not to see someone they knew." He tamped his pipe, unhurried. "My brother's granddaughter was taken at Millbrook Cross. Good girl. Quiet. Nobody even knew she had the — you know. Until someone wrote it down and sent it in." He looked at August with red-rimmed, unhurried eyes. "Someone always writes it down."

August's mouth had gone dry. "I'm sorry for your loss."

"Mm." He filled a sack with dark soil from the corner heap and tied it off. "There's a woman asking after travelers at the inn. Crown woman, but polite about it. Buys folk ale instead of breaking their fingers. My advice, stranger, is to finish your business and be gone before she finishes hers." He handed her the sack. "For your sister. Grow something stubborn on it."

Outside, the knot by the square had thickened. August shouldered the soil sack under her cloak, found Tam, and steered him toward the chandler's with her heart beating a rhythm she didn't like.

"There's an inquisitor here," she murmured. "Asking questions. Politely."

Tam's step didn't change, but his hand found her elbow, steadying. "Then we shop faster."

* * *

They almost made it.

The chandler's shop was warm and dim and smelled gloriously of honey, and Lark had already negotiated the wax down to a fair price and was deep in flirtatious haggling with the chandler's son when August stepped inside — and the bell above the door rang, and a woman came in out of the cold behind her.

August knew her before she turned around. Knew her by the way the room rearranged itself around her presence, the way the chandler's son straightened and Lark went very still by the counter. Knew her from Wren's whispered descriptions and from the drovers' gossip and finally, terribly, from her own instincts, which had been screaming since the gate.

Maren Vale, Inquisitor of the Second Chair, hung her fur-trimmed cloak on the peg by the door like any traveler, and asked the chandler's son for a pound of good candles, and thanked him when he fetched them, and paid exactly what was asked.

Then she turned, and her eyes — pale gray, kind, appalled at themselves for having to look at anything unkindly — passed over August's face.

And stopped.

August watched it happen. Watched recognition move through the woman like weather: first the flicker of *I know you*, then the search — *the road, the passes, a smuggler's ledger, a face seen across a checkpoint fire two winters ago* — and then the arrival, unmistakable, of the truth. August Blackwood. Bearer. Wanted. Standing four feet away holding a sack of grave-dirt and a basket of contraband components, with a singer and a witch's twin at her back.

Every plan August had ever made emptied out of her head. There was nothing left but the animal arithmetic: door behind the inquisitor, window too narrow, Lark's knife under her cloak, Tam's voice that could crack a man's courage like ice on a pond. Three against one, in a crowded shop, in a town full of soldiers.

Maren Vale's hand did not go to the sword at her hip.

It went, instead, to the counter, and gripped it, and the knuckles went white, and August saw — clearly, undeniably — that the woman was afraid. Not of August. Of something else. Of the arithmetic running behind her own pale eyes.

"You're a long way from home," Maren said quietly. Her voice was low, pitched for August alone beneath the chandler's cheerful chatter. "Blackwood. From the Thornfell side. I had your file once. I gave it away." A breath. "Why are you here?"

August's tongue felt like lead. Every answer was a trap. *Buying candles* was a lie; *you know why I'm here* was a confession. So she did the only thing her smuggler's training offered: she told a truth that wasn't the dangerous one.

"Shopping," she said. "Same as you."

Something moved at the corner of Maren's mouth. Not quite a smile. "I burned a village in the fall," she said, still barely above a whisper, still gripping the counter as though it were the rail of a ship in high seas. "Ordered to. Eleven households. There was a child's shoe in the lane afterward, and I have been unable—" She stopped. Closed her eyes for one heartbeat. Opened them. "Is it true? What the singers say. That he's not — that the king isn't—"

"Your Majesty's mercy is infinite," August said softly. "So the crier says."

"Don't." The word cracked. Maren Vale, Inquisitor of the Second Chair, terror of three provinces, looked suddenly and completely exhausted, a woman who had been carrying something heavy up a long hill for years and had just realized the hill didn't end. "I've read every account of the king's dreams. I've interviewed his physicians. I know what the palace doesn't say aloud. And I burned eleven households anyway, because the order had my name on it and I—" Her jaw tightened. "Tell me it's true. Tell me there's something under this that isn't just madness wearing a crown. Because if there isn't, then I am exactly what they say I am, and I would rather die than be that."

The shop was very warm. The candle-smell was thick as honey. Behind Maren, Lark's hand had slipped inside her cloak, and Tam had gone still in the way that meant he was gathering a note, a weapon-note, a note that could stop a heart.

And August — God help her — looked at this woman, this butcher with the kind face, and felt the thing she had no right to feel.

Sympathy.

It flickered up in her like a struck match, dangerous and brief, and she hated it, and she could not put it out. Because she knew what it was to carry orders with your name on them. She knew what it was to burn letters and call it necessity. She knew the exact weight of a guilt you couldn't set down, and she saw it sitting on Maren Vale's shoulders like a yoke, and some traitorous part of her wanted to take it off her.

"There is something under it," August heard herself say. "Bigger than you. Bigger than him. And it's been eating this kingdom for thirty years while everyone hunted shadows."

Maren's breath caught. For a moment — one suspended, absurd moment — the two women stood in a candle shop in a dying town, enemy and quarry, and between them hung a truth neither could afford to hold.

Then boots rang on the cobbles outside. Two soldiers, passing. Maren's spine straightened like a drawn bow, and the inquisitor came back into her face, armor over wound.

"Go," she said, so quietly it was almost not words. "Out the back. Now. Before I remember what I am."

August went. At the door she paused, one heartbeat, and said the thing she would turn over for nights afterward:

"When you're done remembering — Candlemere. Ask for the Choir."

Then the cold took her, and the alley swallowed her, and they ran.

* * *

They didn't speak until the pines closed around the cart, until Brannoc's gray eyes had counted them twice and found all limbs attached, until the horses were moving and Hollowmere was a smudge of smoke behind them.

Then Lark exploded. "You *invited* her! You invited the Inquisitor of the Second Chair to the only home we have left!"

"She hesitated."

"Hesitation is not conversion! Hesitation is the pause before the knife goes in!" Lark was pacing the cart bed, wild-haired, furious. "Wren, tell her. Tell her what hesitation means."

Wren, driving, said mildly, "It means she hasn't decided."

"*That's worse!*"

"It might be," Wren conceded, "or it might be the most important thing that's happened to us since the proclamation. We'll know which soon enough."

Brannoc said nothing for a long mile. August sat with the soil sack in her lap, feeling the weight of it, feeling everything — the match-flare of sympathy she couldn't extinguish, the memory of a child's shoe in a burned lane, the sound of a strong woman's voice cracking on *tell me it's true.*

"Brannoc," she said finally. "Say something."

He looked at her, and his face was the face of a man doing arithmetic he despised. "You gave her Candlemere," he said. "Our sanctuary. Our families. Everything." A muscle worked in his jaw. "If she brings soldiers, we die. If she comes alone—" He exhaled, long and slow, breath smoking in the cold. "Then we gain the one thing the rite needs that gold can't buy. A way into the capital. Through the palace's forgotten doors, past every checkpoint, past every eye that watches for us." He shook his head. "You gambled our lives on a stranger's conscience, August."

"I gambled on what I saw in her face."

"And I buried my family because of faces like hers." But he said it without heat, wearily, the argument already fought and lost inside himself. He turned forward again. "We'll know within the week. If riders come, we run. If she comes alone—" his hands tightened on the reins, "—God help us, we listen."

The cart rolled north through the pines. August looked back once at the fading smoke of Hollowmere, and thought of Maren Vale standing in the candle-warmth, gripping the counter like a drowning woman grips a spar, and heard again the crack in that terrible, decent voice:

*Before I remember what I am.*

Behind them, in the town, a bell began to ring — slow, deliberate, alarm-strokes. Whether for them or for something else, the wind carried no answer.

August faced north, and prayed to no one in particular that doubt, once lit, burned hotter than duty.
