## 12. The Vote at Candlemere

The candles had been burning since dusk, and by the time Brannoc called the vote they had burned down to stubs, their wax pooling on the refectory table like the fat of some slaughtered animal. He found himself staring at the pools instead of the faces. It was easier. The faces were the problem.

Twelve of them sat in the old monastery's refectory, beneath a vaulted ceiling that had once heard monks argue theology and now heard smugglers, healers, hedge-witches, and one boy who could not stop hearing the king's dreams argue about murder. The argument had gone on for six hours. Brannoc had let it go on because every hour of arguing was an hour in which no one had to decide.

"The rite needs three things," August said again, for perhaps the fourth time that night. She stood at the far end of the table, and the candlelight made her look younger than she was and harder than she'd been when she'd stumbled half-frozen into Candlemere two months ago. "Ysolde's letters are clear. The binder's line or apprentice — that's me. A song only this Choir can sing — that's Tam and the singers. And a price, freely chosen." She paused. "I've told you what I think the price is. I'll pay it."

"You *think*," said Wren. "You don't know. That's the whole rotten heart of it. You're asking us to march to the capital on a guess from a dead woman's letters."

"She's not dead," Lark said quietly.

"She vanished. Same thing, as far as the rite is concerned."

Brannoc let his gaze travel down the table. Sef sat with her hands folded, her healer's stillness about her, though he knew her hands were not still inside. Tam had pushed back from the table entirely, arms crossed, jaw set — the stubbornness of a man who had already decided and was waiting for everyone else to catch up. The twins flanked Osk like a pair of gateposts, which was what they always did when they were frightened for him, though neither would admit it.

And Osk. Osk sat with his chin nearly on the table, thirteen years old and gray-faced with exhaustion, because the king's dreams had been getting worse and everyone in the room knew it and no one wanted to say it aloud.

"Say it plainly, then," Brannoc said. "Both roads. Plainly."

Wren took it up like a woman handed a blade she'd been sharpening all evening. "Road one. We kill him. We get close enough — and Maren Vale's defection gets us close enough — and we put a knife in Aldous Vane's heart, and whatever is wearing him dies with the body. No rite. No song. No price we can't calculate. One death against thirty years of purges. Against—" her voice caught, steadied— "against everything the crown has done. It's clean. It's *proportionate*."

"It isn't clean," Sef said. "It's just simple."

"Simple is a mercy, these days."

"And road two?" Brannoc asked, though he knew.

August answered. "We attempt the unbinding. We free the man and destroy the thing. If it works, the Regent is gone entire — not passed on, not fled into some other vessel, but *gone*, because Ysolde built the rite to unmake it. And the king lives, and we have a witness, and maybe — maybe — we have a kingdom that doesn't hunt us anymore."

"*Maybe*," Wren said. "If it works. If the price is what you guess. If the Regent doesn't tear us apart in the singing — and it will try, August, you know it will try, Osk has *told* us what it does—"

"I know what it does," Osk said.

Everyone stopped. The boy almost never spoke in council. His voice came out cracked, unused, like a door opening in an empty house.

"I know what it does because it talks to me now." He lifted his head. His eyes were red-rimmed and terribly steady. "Every night. It knows my name. It shows me things." He looked at Brannoc, and Brannoc felt something in his chest turn over, because the boy was apologizing with his eyes before he said a word. "It showed me this room. It knows where we are. I didn't tell it. I never told it. But it *listens through me*, and I couldn't stop it, and I'm sorry, I'm sorry—"

Lark had her arm around him before the second apology. Wren had gone white. Tam swore softly and fluently, a smuggler's litany.

Brannoc stood very still. So. The Regent knew where Candlemere was. Had known, perhaps, for weeks. Every plan they had made in this room, every route and supply cache and contingency, might already be ash in the entity's mouth.

He should have felt panic. What he felt instead was a strange, cold clarity, like the first breath after falling through ice.

"Then there's no road where we stay here," he said. "Whatever we choose, we choose it tonight, and we move within the week. It knows where we sleep. It will send Vale's hunters, or worse than Vale's hunters." He looked around the table. "So. The vote. Kill, or unbind. In favor of killing the king: raise your hand."

Wren's hand went up first, fast, furious. Then Lark's, slowly, her eyes on Osk. Then two of the older ones — Marta, who had watched her brother burn in the Millbrook cullings, and Fen, whose silence all evening had been its own kind of speech. Five, counting Wren's raised chin and Lark's lowered eyes.

"In favor of the unbinding."

August's hand. Tam's, without hesitation, without looking at anyone. Sef's, gently, like setting down something fragile. Osk's, trembling, held high as if he could throw the vote that way. Hesper, the oldest of them, who had been a nun at Candlemere before it burned the first time. Six.

Six to five. And the twelfth hand —

Brannoc realized, with a lurch, that every eye had come to him. That they had been coming to him for some time. That he was the twelfth hand, and everyone in the room knew it, and he had spent six hours letting them argue so that he would not have to feel the moment arrive.

His family had died under the king's seal. Not metaphorically. Not in the abstract way people say *the crown did this*. Specifically: soldiers with the royal stag on their banners had come to Harrow's Ford in the spring of the year his daughter turned seven, because a tinker passing through had been overheard humming a charm to keep milk sweet, and the Inquisition did not tolerate maybes. They had burned the tinker on the green. And because Brannoc's wife had shouted — only shouted, only words, only grief — they had taken her and the girl too, and he had come home to a cold hearth and a neighbor who wouldn't meet his eye, and he had spent eleven years since then making sure that no hearth he kept would ever be cold enough for the crown to notice.

He had built the Choir out of that grief. He had fed it, housed it, led it, lied to it about being safe. And now a boy with a stolen voice in his head was asking him to spend his grief on mercy, and every scar he owned said *no*, and—

And Ysolde's letters said the Regent could not be killed while it wore its vessel. Wren's clean knife would slide between the king's ribs and the thing would simply step out of the cooling body and into the world, unbound, unhoused, hungry. August had shown them the passage. Brannoc had read it four times. *The binding is the cage; break the cage and you loose the wolf.* Killing the king was not road one. There was no road one. There was only the unbinding, done well, or the unbinding done badly, or the end of everything done by accident.

But if he said that — if he voted with his head instead of his hate — then he voted to walk into the capital carrying the man who had signed his family's death warrant, and to sing that man free, and to call it justice. He voted to stand in front of Aldous Vane and not put his hands around his throat. He did not know if he could do it. He genuinely, honestly did not know.

"Brannoc," Sef said softly. "However you vote, it's allowed."

That was the worst kindness anyone had offered him in years.

He thought of his daughter's laugh, which he could barely remember, and his wife's anger, which he remembered perfectly, and how she had died for shouting, and how shouting was all he wanted to do right now, and how none of it — not one hour of it, not one grave of it — would be answered by a knife in a sleeping man's chest. Grief wanted the knife. Grief had always wanted the knife. But grief was not the only thing he had. He had also built this table. He had gathered these twelve people and taught them, without ever saying it, that they were allowed to want more than revenge.

"Mercy isn't a feeling," he heard himself say. His voice came out rough, like something dragged over stone. "I learned that late. It's a thing you do with your hands when your heart's telling you to do otherwise. Ysolde's letters are clear — kill the vessel and the Regent walks free. Wren's right that the rite is a gamble. But it's the only gamble that ends anything." He closed his eyes. Opened them. "I vote for the unbinding."

The sound that went through the room was not relief. It was more complicated than relief — a kind of collective exhale that carried grief in it, because half of them had wanted permission to kill and had been denied it, and the other half had wanted company in hope and had received it, and both halves understood that Brannoc had just paid something none of them could see.

Wren stared at him. "You lost them," she said. "Your family. To *him*. To his seal, his soldiers, his—"

"Yes."

"And you're voting to save him?"

"I'm voting to kill the thing that made him do it." Brannoc picked up one of the candle stubs and turned it in his fingers, watching the flame gutter. "Or I'm lying to myself, and I don't know which, and I won't know until I'm standing in front of him. Either way, the vote stands."

Nobody applauded. Thank the silent saints for that. Tam nodded once, slow, like a man closing a ledger. Marta got up and left the room without a word, and Fen followed, and Brannoc let them go, because a five-to-seven split needed room to breathe or it would fester.

August stayed last, as the others drifted out. She stood across the table from him, and for a moment neither spoke.

"You hated yourself just now," she said. Not a question.

"Every day since Harrow's Ford. Tonight I just did it in public."

"That's not what I meant." She leaned forward. "You think voting for mercy betrayed them. Your wife. Your girl. It didn't. It's the only thing that *answers* them. Everything else is just noise the Regent makes."

He wanted to tell her she was wrong. He wanted it so badly his teeth ached. Instead he said, "Go pack your kit. We leave in three days. Funeral procession — singers and mourners. Tam's idea."

She went. The candles guttered out one by one behind her, and Brannoc sat alone in the dark refectory of a ruined monastery, twelve seats and one man, doing the arithmetic of what he had just agreed to carry.

Three days later, they dressed themselves in mourning black and practiced walking slowly.

* * *

The valley below Candlemere wore its snow like a shroud, and Brannoc stood at the monastery's broken gate at dawn on the day of departure, counting heads and hating the counting, because counting heads was how you learned what you were about to lose.

August came first, her kit on her shoulder, Ysolde's letters wrapped in oilcloth against her chest. Then Tam, tuning a small traveling harp with numb fingers. Then Sef with her herb baskets, then Osk, pale and silent, flanked by the twins, who were staying behind to hold Candlemere with Hesper and the others — someone had to keep a door open for whoever came home.

Brannoc checked the cart harness, checked the false coffin they'd bought from a cooper in the lowlands, checked the mourning bands. A funeral procession of singers, hired to wail a rich merchant's son down to the capital. It was good cover. It was terrible cover. Everyone in the party knew it, and nobody said it, and that was the last thing they all agreed on.

Osk stopped beside him at the gate. The boy was looking back up at the monastery, at the thin thread of chimney smoke rising from the refuge that had been the only safe place any of them had known.

"If it talks to me on the road," Osk said, "I'll tell you. Right away. Whatever it offers."

Brannoc put a hand on the boy's shoulder. It felt fragile as a bird's.

"I know you will," he said, and found, to his surprise, that he meant it — that somewhere in the night's wreckage, forgiveness had gotten there ahead of him. "That's why it loses, in the end. Things like that can't understand being told the truth."

They went down into the valley, seven mourners and an empty coffin, and behind them Candlemere's smoke thinned and vanished, and ahead the road ran north through the frozen passes toward the capital, toward the king, toward the thing wearing him — and Brannoc marched at the front with his grief strapped to his back like a second coffin, hating himself, hating the mercy, marching anyway.
