## 13. The Road to the Capital

They buried no one on the morning they left Candlemere, and that was the point.

August stood at the monastery's broken gate in the grey before dawn, watching Brannoc arrange the procession with a quartermaster's fussy precision. Four mourners, veiled. A bier bearing a coffin that weighed almost nothing, its interior packed not with a body but with the rite's components wrapped in grave-linen: salt from the drowned pans, ash of yew, the iron nail Ysolde had specified in her letters, and the bundle of letters themselves, because August could not bring herself to leave them behind even now, even knowing them by heart. Funeral processions were sacred traffic. Even an Inquisitor's writ paused at the edge of a grave.

"Sing low and sing sad," Tam murmured, adjusting her veil. She was the only one of them who looked natural in mourning gray; grief sat on her like a garment cut to measure. "Nobody stops a choir going to a burial. They avert their eyes. Grief is the one thing this kingdom still respects."

"It doesn't respect it," Osk said quietly. "It fears it." He walked with his hands shoved deep in his sleeves these days, August had noticed, as if he needed containing. Since the vote, since whatever he would not tell them, he had stopped sleeping whole nights through. She had heard him pacing the cells below at the hour when even the mountain seemed to hold its breath.

Brannoc shouldered the bier's front pole as if it were heavy, which was theater, which was practice for the road. Under the coffin's false floor they had hidden the rest: blades, coin, dried meat, and Sef's healer's kit, because Sef had refused — flatly, in the voice she used when argument was already over — to let them go without a healer through passes where the cold killed as efficiently as any Inquisitor.

"You'll turn back at the river valleys," Brannoc said to her now, in the tone of a man re-litigating a lost cause one final time.

"I'll turn back where the roads split at Marrow Bridge," Sef agreed, "if everyone's alive to turn back."

August took her place at the rear of the little procession, where she could watch their backtrail. It was a smuggler's habit and she was glad of it now; glad of anything that gave her hands and eyes a job while her heart did its own grim arithmetic. Behind them Candlemere's ruin wore its winter shroud of ivy and snow, and below it the Choir — those who remained, Wren and Lark and the others, the wounded, the too-young, the stubborn keepers of the refuge — stood in the gatehouse shadow and did not wave, because waving was memorable, and being forgotten was the whole of their armor.

Lark raised one hand anyway, small and quick, and then made it into a fist, and held it up against the pale sky.

August held up hers the same way, and turned north.

* * *

The Thornfell passes in deep winter were a country that did not want them.

August led them by the deep roads — the smugglers' ways, goat tracks and old drovers' cuts and one stretch of Roman-grade paving that predates the crown and most of its gods, running beneath an avenue of dead elms whose branches clicked together like counting bones. She taught the others the grammar of the mountains as they went. Walk on the north side of slopes where the snow holds. Sleep in the lee of stone, never wood, because wood means people and people mean report. Burn nothing that smokes. Speak under wind.

Tam learned it fastest, having spent years singing in taverns where reading a room's danger was survival. Brannoc learned it grudgingly, the way soldiers learn a civilian's craft, muttering that hiding was dishonorable and then hiding very well. Osk simply absorbed it, silent and watchful, and that worried August more than open fear would have. He looked at the horizon the way other men look at a creditor.

On the fourth day, in the high col above the Ashen Vale, they sang for the first time.

It was Tam's idea, framed as rehearsal — the rite's song had to live in four throats before it could live in the chapel, and voices rusted without use. But August understood, watching Tam choose the moment, that there was more in it than practice. They were high above the world, ringed by peaks, utterly alone, and Tam waited until the wind dropped and then began, low, almost a hum, the first line of a melody none of them had been taught but all of them somehow knew.

The Choir's song. The one Ysolde had written into her letters in fragments, the one the singers had reconstructed note by note over twenty years of hiding. It was not a beautiful melody, exactly. It was a *true* one — it moved the way water moves under ice, inevitable, patient, gathering itself toward a single enormous consonance. When August joined, her hedge-witch power rose in her throat unbidden, and for three or four bars she felt the song and her Gift interlock like two hands.

Then the bar came where her voice should have yielded, and she yielded, and the harmony closed over the gap she left as if she'd never been missing, and she understood something about the Choir she had not understood in all her weeks among them. They were not powerful together. They were *unlost* together.

Osk wept openly by the third verse and pretended he wasn't, and nobody let him see that they noticed.

They sang the song through twice, and then Tam cut it off mid-phrase — "That's enough. It shouldn't be worn smooth before we need it" — and they went down out of the col in silence, and August carried the last chord inside her chest all afternoon like a coal banked for morning.

* * *

They saw the hunters on the sixth day.

August spotted them first, because spotting was her trade: three riders on the valley floor below, moving along the frozen river in that particular rhythm she had learned to dread — not traveling, * quartering*. Sweeping the ground on either side of the track. Soldiers searched; these men *read*, bending in their saddles toward the snow, and she knew what they were reading, because she had left it herself: the faint wrongness in a footprint where a body lighter than it should be crosses drift, the scuffed lichen on a handhold. And behind the three riders, at a distance calculated to be invisible from below, a fourth figure on a gray mare, riding with her reins knotted and her hands bare despite the cold.

Maren Vale.

"She's found us," Osk said. He hadn't turned to look. He said it facing forward, in a voice like a struck bowl, and August felt the hair rise on her arms. Lately he knew things. Lately there had been a smell of hot metal about him at odd hours, like a forge banked somewhere just out of sight.

"Not found," August said, and was proud of how level her voice came out. "Followed. There's a difference. Found means she knows where we're going. Followed means she only knows where we've been."

"And if she follows us all the way to the capital?" Brannoc asked.

August watched the gray mare pick its way down a shale chute with a sureness that spoke of long acquaintance. "Then she follows us to the capital," she said. "Keep walking. Mourners don't run."

But that night, camped in a hollow beneath a leaning slab of granite, she took first watch and spent it doing sums she didn't like. The procession disguise bought them days, not safety. A funeral party traveling toward the capital in winter, taking smugglers' paths instead of roads — Maren Vale was too clever to be fooled for long. Clever enough to count four veiled figures and wonder why a burial detail included a woman built like a siege engine and a boy who flinched at nothing visible. Sooner or later the disguise would become the trap: four hunted magicians, announced by their own coffin, with nowhere to scatter on ice-covered ground.

She woke Brannoc at the change of watch and told him so, and he listened with his chin on his fists, firelight making one bronze plane of his face, and at the end he said only: "Marrow Bridge."

"Yes," August said. "Marrow Bridge."

* * *

Marrow Bridge crossed the Kell in a single stone span the old empire had thrown across a gorge so deep the river was less a sound than a pressure, a constant sub-audible grinding far below. The road forked at its southern end: west into the hill country where Sef would leave them, north down the long descents toward the capital plains. It was the last easy place to say goodbye, and the last hard place to be ambushed, and both of these facts were true at once, and only one of them mattered in the end.

They came to the bridge at midmorning, under a sky the color of dishwater, and August knew before they reached it. Twenty years of reading country had taught her how a landscape looks when it has people in it who don't want to be seen — a certain absence of birds, a stillness in the scrub. The bridge's southern approach was very still indeed.

She caught Brannoc's sleeve. He followed her glance, read the same silence, and did the bravest and simplest thing she had ever seen him do: he kept walking. Kept the procession's pace, slow and mournful, the bier swaying between him and Tam, and pitched his voice low for her alone.

"Crossing order?"

"Sef turns west before the bridge. If it's a trap, they'll want the bridge, so the ground west is ours. Tam takes Osk across — fast, no dignity, dignity's a luxury now. You and I are the wall."

"And you're certain?"

"No," August said. "But certainty's a luxury too."

Sef understood without a word spoken. That was Sef all over; she had spent a lifetime reading what people needed before they knew to ask it. She slipped westward off the track with her kit and her quiet face, meaning to circle the gorge rim and rejoin them beyond — and she would have made it, August would swear afterward on anything holy or unholy, she would have made it clean, except that the ambush sprang a half-breath early because one soldier's nerve broke, and the crossbow bolt meant for Tam's back found the open ground where Sef was running instead.

The world went very simple after that.

August threw her veil back and put her hands out at the treeline and did the thing she had sworn on Millbrook's ashes never to do on open ground: she burned. Not the soldiers — she was past caring, some cold distant part of her noted, whether she was past caring or not, but her Gift had always had her mother's conscience — she burned the *ground*, the deep snowpack along the approach, and it slumped and slid and took the treeline's footing with it, and three crossbows went down into a white avalanche of their own ambush.

Brannoc roared — actually roared, like something out of the old stories — and charged the bridge, because four soldiers were already on it, coming from the northern end, and Tam and Osk were in the open middle of the span with nowhere to be.

And Sef.

Sef was sitting propped against a boulder with a black-fletched shaft standing in her chest below the collarbone, doing the worst arithmetic of her life, and August reached her in ten strides and dropped to her knees and knew with a healer's daughter's certainty exactly what she was looking at. Some wounds are arguments. This was not an argument.

"Go," Sef said.

"Don't be—"

"August." Sef's voice was perfectly calm, the calm of a woman who had set more fractures than she'd had hot dinners and knew a hopeless one by feel. Her hands, steady, pressed August's away from the shaft. "The bridge. Tam can't sing with a spear in her. Go and be my hands."

There was a thing August learned about herself on the frozen ground below Marrow Bridge, and the thing was this: obedience could be love. She kissed Sef on the forehead — tasted snow and iron — and ran.

What happened on the bridge happened fast and ugly. Brannoc met the four soldiers at the span's throat and became, briefly, the reason the old empire had built its walls so thick. August came up behind him and put her Gift into the bridge's ancient iron rail bolts, and the rail sang and sagged, and two soldiers decided very sensibly that no warrant was worth this and went over the side into the gorge's grinding dark rather than stay. Tam had Osk by the hood, hauling him low behind the coffin — and August saw, in the corner of her eye, Osk standing *up*, standing straight, one arm flung out toward the northern end of the bridge where reinforcements were spilling from the rocks, and she saw the air in front of his palm ripple like heat over a forge, and she saw the soldiers stop as if they had struck glass.

She had no time to wonder what it had cost him. The southern approach was collapsing in slow thunder; the northern end held Brannoc's fury; and between them the procession-that-was-no-longer-mourning ran, coffin and all, across the last forty feet of the Marrow Bridge with the Kell's grind rising around them like applause from something vast and indifferent.

They did not stop on the far side. Stopping was death. They drove north along the descent for a full hour, hearts slamming, until the gorge was a scar behind them and the capital plains opened gray and endless below, and only then did Brannoc halt them in the shadow of a waystone and say, in a voice scraped down to gravel:

"We go back for her at dusk. Quietly."

August looked at the old man who had voted mercy with his family's graves still fresh, and hated what she had to say to him, and said it anyway.

"No. We both know what dusk will find. What we go back for at dusk is a grave we dig ourselves."

Osk sat down on the snow then, suddenly, like a puppet with cut strings, and put his face in his hands. Tam crouched beside him. Brannoc stared north at the plains, at the smudge of the capital on its far horizon, jaw working around something that would not come.

And August stood at the waystone with the rite's components on her back and Sef's blood likely still under her fingernails though she could see none, and understood that this was the price beginning — not the price the rite would ask of her at the end, in the frozen chapel, but the toll collected along the road, in installments, paid in the currency of names.

Behind them, far to the south, a gray mare picked its way across ruined snow toward a broken bridge, and a hunter with a kind face knelt down beside what they had left there.

August did not know that yet. But she felt it, the way you feel a door open somewhere in a house at night — a change in the pressure of the world — and she turned her face north, toward the capital, toward the thing wearing the crown, and started walking again, because stopping was death, and the dead of Marrow Bridge deserved better than to have died for nothing.
