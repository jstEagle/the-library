## 14. Interlude: The King in the Cellar of Himself

There is a cellar in him, and he lives there.

He knows this the way a stone knows water — not as thought but as condition, as the long cold fact of being worn. The cellar has no door he can find. It has walls that are not walls, made of his own forgetting, and a ceiling so low it is really the underside of his skull, and sometimes — this is the worst of it, worse than the dark, worse than the hunger that never quite reaches him because something else does the eating now — sometimes he hears footsteps overhead. Not feet. *Intentions*, walking. Vast and unhurried, pacing the rooms of his body the way a landlord paces a house he means to keep.

He used to have a name. He is nearly sure of it. It had a shape like a bell, or maybe like a river — Aldous, Aldous, he turns the sound over in the dark like a coin rubbed smooth of its face, and half the time he cannot tell whether it is his name or only a word he found somewhere down here and decided to carry, the way a beggar carries a cup that may once have belonged to someone else.

Thirty years. He counts them by dreams that are not his own.

That is how he keeps the calendar, in the cellar of himself. The thing upstairs dreams, and the dreams leak through the floor like light under a door, and each dream is a season, and each season is a year, and he lies beneath them all and watches the leak-light move across his ceiling and thinks: *another one gone.* Thirty winters of someone else's weather. Thirty springs in which something wearing his hands signed decrees, wore his face to councils, kissed his wife's forehead with his mouth while he screamed from underneath — no. Not screamed. He did scream, at first. He remembers screaming the way you remember a country you were born in and left too young: vividly, wrongly, in colors that were probably never real.

The screaming stopped being useful. That was the first lesson the cellar taught him. The second was that memory is a room you can be locked out of.

He has lost his mother's voice. He knows he had one — a mother, a voice — because there is a hollow in him shaped exactly like her, the way a glove holds the absence of a hand. He has lost the taste of bread. He has lost, and this he discovered only last winter, which was perhaps his fortieth winter or his twentieth, he genuinely cannot say — he has lost his own face. He went looking for it, the way you go looking for a tool you swear you set down just here, just a moment ago, and found instead a smooth blank place where the mirror-memory should be. He knows he is tall. He knows his hair went grey at the temples early, because Ysolde said so, once, laughing. But the face itself — the thing other people saw when they loved him or feared him or knelt to him — that is gone. The Regent wears it now, daily, expertly, and has worn it so long that the original has been handled out of existence, like a coin passed through ten thousand hands.

* * *

He clings to one memory. He has learned to be a miser of memories, hoarding the few coins the Regent's dreaming does not steal, and of all his small hoard, one piece is gold.

A young woman. A witch. Ysolde.

She was weeping when she bound him.

This is the strangest thing, the thing he returns to on the long nights when the leak-light of the Regent's dreams runs red with conquest or black with cullings, the nights when he wants to stop existing altogether and cannot even do that, because the Regent needs the cellar tenant alive — this is the strangest thing: she was crying. Her tears fell on his hands where they lay palm-up on the chapel floor, frozen stone beneath him, candle-smoke above, and each tear that struck his skin burned cold, colder than the stone, and he understood — even then, even in the first terrible minute of what was being done to him — he understood that she did not want to be doing it.

"Why," he had asked her. His voice worked then. His voice was *his* then. "Why me? Why weep for me?"

And Ysolde — young, so young, younger than his own daughter would have been if he had ever been allowed a daughter, with frost in her dark hair and her sleeves singed from some working done in haste — Ysolde had looked at him with an expression he has spent thirty years trying to name and has finally, recently, settled on: it was the look of a surgeon who loves the patient.

"Because it will hold better if I mean it kindly," she said. "Old things feed on hatred. Hatred gives them grip. So I will bind you grieving, Majesty, and my grief will be iron, and I am sorry, I am so sorry, but the alternative is the end of everything you have ever ruled."

"The alternative," he said, "is you killing me."

"Yes." She wiped her face with the back of her wrist, leaving a smear of ash across her cheekbone like a warpaint no army ever earned. "Yes, that is the alternative. And I find I cannot."

"You could save yourself the sorrow," he told her, and meant it as mercy, meaning: *kill me, girl, spare us both.* "One clean stroke. Kings die every day. Kingdoms survive it."

"Not this kingdom. Not with *it* already inside." She knelt then, close enough that he could see her eyes were the brown of autumn water, and she took his hands in hers, and began. "Hold still. Hold *yourself*. Whatever happens, whatever it says through your mouth afterward — hold yourself. Somewhere in you there is a man. Keep him. Keep him the way you'd keep an ember in wet straw. Feed him one memory a day if you must. One is enough. One is enough to stay a man."

Then she sang, and the world closed over him like water, and he sank, and sank, and woke in the cellar.

* * *

One memory a day. He has kept the discipline the way monks keep vigils, the way prisoners scratch marks into walls. Some days the Regent's dreaming floods everything and he loses whole seasons to another mind's ambitions; some days the leak-light shows him executions and he tries not to know the faces, fails, adds them to a ledger of griefs so long it has become less a ledger than a landscape. But every day — every day he can find the edges of — he goes to the Ysolde memory and feeds the ember.

He has worn the memory thin with handling. He knows this. He knows that the frost in her hair may be invented by now, that the ash-smear on her cheek may have migrated from some other face entirely, that the exact words may have shifted in their beds the way words do after thirty years of turning over. It does not matter. A beggar's cup is still a cup. What matters is that it happened — that once, at the center of the worst moment of his life, a human being chose grief over convenience and kindness over hatred, and bound him *weeping*, and told him to keep himself.

So he keeps himself.

Some days keeping himself looks like nothing. Looks like lying in the dark reciting the names of rivers he once rode beside — the Wether, the Sorrel, the little unnamed burn behind his childhood nurse's cottage whose water tasted of iron and moss. Some days it looks like rage, and the rage is dangerous, because the Regent drinks rage through the floorboards, fattens on it, and he has learned to pour his fury out slowly, diluted, in homeopathic doses, a king rationing his own wrath like bread in a siege. Some days it looks like this: choosing, deliberately, absurdly, to remember the taste of bread. He cannot retrieve the taste anymore. But he can remember that he once had it, and that remembering-that-remembering is itself a kind of possession, a deed of title to a self the thief has not fully confiscated.

And lately — this is new, this is the strange weather of this particular winter — lately there is a boy.

* * *

The boy comes to him in the leak-light. Small, sharp-eared, frightened, listening without wanting to listen, the way August— the way people once listened at keyholes to rooms they feared. No. Not August. Where did that name come from? There is a name moving toward him through the floors of the world, he can feel it the way the cellar feels frost coming down through the earth in deep winter — a name with a smell of pine smoke and smuggler's roads attached to it, a name the Regent has begun to mutter in its sleep. August. He does not know why he knows it. Things drift down through the ceiling. He catches what he can.

But the boy — Osk, the boy is called Osk, the boy's fear tastes of salt and sister-grief — the boy listens, and for the first time in thirty years, the king in the cellar is *heard*.

It is almost more than he can bear. He had forgotten what it was to be perceived. For three decades his existence has been a secret kept from everyone including himself, a prisoner walled up so thoroughly that even his jailer forgets to count him among the living except as furniture, as the shell, as the inconvenient meat the crown must be fitted to each morning. And now a boy's attention presses against the ceiling of the cellar like a face pressed to frosted glass, and Aldous does the only thing he can think to do, the thing Ysolde taught him without knowing she taught it:

He keeps himself. Loudly.

He gathers every scrap of ember he owns — the rivers, the bread, the bell-shaped name, the weeping witch — and he holds them up toward the crack in the ceiling like a lantern, like a signal fire, like a man on a raft waving a shirt at a ship too far away, and he thinks as hard as thinking can be done in a place like this: *I am here. I am a man. My name is Aldous. Tell her. Tell whoever is coming. Tell them the binding can be undone — she built it to be undone, I felt it, I felt the mercy in the architecture, there is a seam, there has always been a seam —*

Whether any of it crosses, he cannot know. The boy flinches away sometimes, frightened, and the light closes, and the cellar is the cellar again. But other nights the attention returns, and stays longer, and once — once — Aldous is almost certain he felt an answer. Not words. Something older than words. The sensation of a hand laid flat against the far side of a wall.

*Someone is coming,* the hand seemed to say.

Or perhaps he invented it. Thirty years is long enough to invent anything, even hope. Especially hope. Hope is the easiest thing in the world to hallucinate when you are starving, and he has been starving for a manhood, for a marriage, for a reign, for a face.

* * *

Tonight the leak-light runs strange.

The Regent upstairs is agitated — he can feel it in the floorboards, in the way the intentions pace and repace, quick, quick, quick, a landlord who has heard glass break somewhere in the house. Its dreams come fast and jagged: a bridge, splashed red; a town market and a face it hates, a young woman's face, dark-haired, watchful, carrying herself like someone who has learned to walk past soldiers; a chapel, frozen, waiting; and threaded through all of it, sung low and constant like a pulse beneath the fever — a song. Voices. Many voices, braided, stubborn, singing something the Regent clearly fears, because fear makes the leak-light run hot and the hot light sears the cellar floor and for the first time in years the king in the cellar has to shield his eyes.

They are coming. The singers are coming. The girl named August is coming. And the Regent knows, and is afraid, and its fear falls through the ceiling like warm rain, and Aldous kneels — he has knees again in the dream-logic of the cellar, tonight he has a body made of memory, tall, grey at the temples, a face he still cannot see but can feel, jaw and brow and mouth, a ghost of a face fitting itself over the ghost of a man — Aldous kneels in the falling warmth of his enemy's terror and does the discipline. One memory. Fed to the ember.

Ysolde, weeping. Frost in her hair. Ash on her cheek. *Keep yourself.*

"I have kept myself," he says aloud, and his voice works, tonight his voice is his, bell-shaped, river-named, a king's voice saying a plain man's sentence in a cellar with no door. "Do you hear me, girl, wherever you are, old and sorry and still alive somewhere in the world — I have kept myself. Come and see. Bring your seam-rippers. Bring whoever inherited your weeping. I will hold still. I have held still for thirty years, and I will hold until the stone itself forgets its cold."

Above him, the pacing stops.

The Regent has gone very still, the way a beast goes still when it scents the wind change, and its stillness is worse than its pacing, and through the floor comes a pressure like a great eye lowering itself to a keyhole. Looking down. Looking, for the first time in decades, *down*, into the cellar, into the tenant space, wondering — perhaps for the first time — whether the furniture remembers.

Aldous lifts his head. He cannot see the eye. He looks at it anyway.

"Come down whenever you like," says the king in the cellar of himself, and smiles with a face made of remembered light. "You'll find the house empty. I gave the man away years ago. He's walking north."

And far above, faint as frost forming, faint as a tear striking frozen stone, he begins — for the first time in thirty years — to hear singing.
