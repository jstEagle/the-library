## 15. Maren Vale's Crisis of Faith

The order came at dawn, written in the king's own hand, which was the first thing wrong with it.

Maren Vale had read a hundred commissions in her nine years as Inquisitor — warrants for arrest, writs of seizure, sentences of quiet removal — and they all bore the same dry bureaucratic cadence, the same seal pressed in grey wax by some clerk in the capital who would never smell the smoke of what he ordered. This one was different. The script was cramped and slanting, the ink blotted twice, as though the hand that wrote it had been shaking. *Suspected bearers confirmed at Hollowmere. Cull the village. Leave nothing that breathes.*

*Leave nothing that breathes.* Not even the goats. Not even the dog that would come barking at the horses' hooves.

She sat on the edge of her camp cot with the paper trembling in her fingers and told herself it was forgery. It had to be forgery. Someone in the chain of command had gone mad, or someone wanted her disgraced, or someone was testing her loyalty. Any of those explanations were kinder than the truth, which was that the seal was genuine, the cipher matched, and the courier who'd brought it had saluted her with the flat-eyed blankness of a man who had delivered such orders before and would deliver them again until he died or the country ran out of villages.

"Captain," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "Break camp. We ride for Hollowmere."

Outside, the frost lay on the pines like a shroud laid over sleeping men. Her company mounted up — forty riders, torches in oilcloth, the casks of lamp-oil lashed behind the supply mules — and Maren looked at their faces one by one as she always did before an operation. Sergeant Corwin, gray at the temples, who hummed hymns when he thought no one listened. Young Pell, seventeen, who still flinched at loud noises and lied about it. Dara Oskell, who kept a pressed flower from her mother's garden inside her gauntlet and had never once told anyone why.

These were not monsters. She had built her whole career on the certainty that they were not monsters, that the crown's work could be done with mercy, that a hunter with a kind face made all the difference between justice and butchery. She had said as much to novices, to magistrates, to herself in the dark hours when the faces of the taken came back to her. *We are not the plague,* she told them. *We are the cure. And a cure administered gently is still a cure.*

She believed it the way you believe in a floor beneath your feet: absolutely, right up until it isn't there.

Hollowmere was four hours' ride through the high forest, a village Maren knew slightly — known for its wool, its cider, its shrine to some minor harvest saint whose name she'd forgotten. She had passed through it twice on other commissions. A woman there had once given her bread and honey without being asked, because Maren's horse had thrown a shoe and the farrier lived two lanes over. She could not remember the woman's face. That frightened her more than anything else about the day: that she had eaten this village's bread and could not remember the face of the woman who gave it.

They crested the last ridge at midmorning, and Hollowmere lay below them in its bowl of white fields, smoking gently from a dozen chimneys, utterly unaware. Maren raised her hand and the column halted in the trees.

"Captain Corwin," she said. "Ride down alone. Tell them the crown requires shelter for a passing company. Get them gathered in the square. No one runs, no one dies. We do this by the book."

Corwin looked at her a moment too long. "By the book," he repeated, and something in his voice suggested he had already read the book she meant, or guessed at it, and wished he hadn't.

* * *

It went by the book, which was the horror of it.

The villagers came to the square the way villagers always did, confused and courteous, bringing stools for the soldiers because that was what you did when the crown visited — you offered hospitality, because refusal was a tax on your soul. Maren stood on the mounting block before the well and read the commission aloud, and she heard her own voice go strange on the words, flat and distant, like someone else speaking through her mouth.

*Suspected bearers confirmed at Hollowmere.*

Confirmed by whom? She had asked the courier. He had stared through her. There were no reports. There was no evidence. There was only the word *suspected*, which in nine years had been enough, because she had trusted the machinery above her the way the villagers now trusted her — because the alternative was unbearable.

A child started crying somewhere in the crowd, a small hiccupping sound, and a mother hushed it, and Maren found her eyes going to the sound and refusing to leave it.

"The crown has determined," she heard herself say, "that the Gift is a contagion—"

"You're Maren Vale." An old man near the front, wool-capped, hands like tree roots. "My granddaughter has your broadsheet. The one about how bearers deserve dignity in custody. She keeps it folded in her prayer book." He wasn't accusing her. That was the worst part. He was *reminding* her, holding her own words up like a mirror, waiting for the woman in the broadsheet to appear and make this make sense.

Maren looked at him. She looked at the square — at the baker with flour still on his forearms, at the girl of perhaps twelve standing too close to her brother, at the farrier whose forge fire was still burning in his shop because no one had told him today would be different from any other day. Forty soldiers. Two hundred souls. Oil casks stacked by the well like wedding gifts.

And she understood, finally, with the slow sick clarity of a bone setting wrong, what she had been feeding all these years. Not justice. Not safety. Not the gentle cure she'd imagined herself administering. She had been the kind face on the door of the slaughterhouse, and her compassion had done nothing — *nothing* — except make the cattle walk in calmly, grateful, offering stools.

There was no evidence because evidence didn't matter. There were no reports because reports weren't needed. Somewhere above her, whatever wore the crown had decided Hollowmere should burn, and every kind thing Maren had ever done had merely oiled the machine that would do it.

"Captain Corwin," she said.

He came to attention beside the block. His face was gray. He had read the order too; she saw it in him now, had maybe seen it for years and said nothing, because she was his captain and captains carry the weight so the sergeants don't have to.

"Torches," she said.

And then, before the word could finish leaving her mouth, before forty hands could move toward forty oilcloths, Maren Vale reached into her coat, drew out the commission with the king's shaking hand and the grey wax seal, held it up so the whole square could see it —

— and set it alight from Corporal Pell's nearest torch.

The paper burned fast. She held it until the flame kissed her gloves, then let the ashes fall into the snow between her boots. The square went silent enough that she could hear the farrier's forge hissing two streets away, and the child had stopped crying, and everyone — soldiers and villagers alike — was looking at her as though she had just spoken in tongues.

"Inquisitor?" Corwin said quietly.

"The order is void," Maren said. Her voice cracked on the second word and she let it. Let them hear it crack. "By my authority, which I hereby resign. Go home. All of you. Ride back to the garrison and report that I burned the commission and defected, and let them send whoever they like to hang me for it."

Nobody moved. Then Dara Oskell dismounted, walked to the well, and began unbuckling the oil casks with steady, methodical hands, dumping lamp-oil into the snow where it would freeze and be useless by nightfall. One by one, the others followed. Not all of them — six riders turned and fled for the treeline, and Maren watched them go without hatred, because they were doing exactly what she should have done nine years ago — but most of them stayed, and stayed, and stayed.

The old man with the tree-root hands took off his wool cap.

"Maren Vale," he said again, and this time it sounded like a blessing, or a sentence, or both.

* * *

She left Hollowmere before dark, because staying would only bring the next company down on them faster, and she rode north with three volunteers — Corwin, Oskell, and young Pell, who had not flinched once all day — and a single piece of intelligence she had carried for weeks without understanding why she couldn't put it down.

Candlemere.

The burned letters from August Blackwood's village — the unburned page naming the ruined monastery — had been sitting in her dispatch case since autumn like a stone in a boot. She had meant to act on it. She had drafted the operation twice. Each time, she had found reasons to delay: weather, manpower, the need for confirmation. Now she understood the reasons had been her conscience buying time, though she hadn't known what for.

She also understood something else, something she had pieced together from fragments across a year of hunting: the signature from the supply raid, the strange collaborative weave the Choir used, the boy who heard things, the way the crown's orders had grown stranger and crueler in the past season, as though whatever sat the throne had begun to hurry. The Choir wasn't just hiding in Candlemere. They knew something. They were planning something. And if half of what Maren had begun to suspect about the king himself was true — and she hated herself for how much of it rang true, all those orders in that shaking hand, all those eyes gone distant at council — then the hunted magicians in the mountains might be the only people in the kingdom trying to save it.

She was not riding to arrest them. She was not even sure she was riding to be forgiven. She was riding to ask a question she had no right to ask, of people she had spent a year trying to destroy:

*Can he be saved? The king. The man under the crown. Is there anything left under there worth saving?*

Because if there wasn't, then everything she had ever done had been in service of a corpse wearing a crown, and if there was — if there was — then she had nearly burned a village full of children to protect the thing that had killed him thirty years ago.

Snow began falling as they climbed into the Thornfells, soft and thick, erasing the road behind them. Corwin hummed his hymn. Pell asked no questions, which Maren suspected was its own form of loyalty. On the third night, huddled under a rock overhang with the horses stamping and steaming, Maren took out the charred page with the word *Candlemere* on it and held it over the fire, and did not burn it, and slept.

Two days later, half-frozen and leading a limping horse, she came around the shoulder of the mountain and saw the monastery ruins above her, black against the white, and figures moving among the broken arches — watchful, armed, unmistakably aware of her long before she reached them.

She raised both empty hands, dismounted, and knelt in the snow.

"I'm not here for a fight," she called, and her breath tore white in the cold. "I'm Maren Vale. I burned my commission five days ago. I've come to ask you something, and after that you can do whatever you like with me."

Silence, long and cold. Then movement on the wall — a big man with a gray beard, a bow half-raised, and beside him a slim figure in a smuggler's leathers whom Maren recognized instantly from a hundred sketches and one burned page.

August Blackwood looked down at her for a long moment, and Maren braced for hatred, and deserved it.

Instead August said, quietly, to the big man: "Let her in. If she wanted us dead, we'd be dead already." A pause. "And someone get her a blanket before she freezes. She looks like she's been carrying something heavy for a very long time."

Maren knelt in the snow and, to her own astonishment, began to laugh — or weep; in the cold it was impossible to tell the difference — as the gates of Candlemere opened above her.
