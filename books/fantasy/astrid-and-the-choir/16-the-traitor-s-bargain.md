## 16. The Traitor's Bargain

The dreams had changed.

Osk knew the difference the way a sailor knows weather by the ache in his teeth. For years the king's dreams had come to him like water through a cracked wall — seeping, formless, mostly grief. He had learned to sleep with a knot of wool in his fists and Tam's lullabies humming in his ears, and still the grief got through, because grief was patient and he was not.

But lately there was something on the other side of the wall. Something pressing its mouth to the crack and *listening back*.

He woke in the dark of Candlemere's undercroft with his heart going like a trapped bird and his sister's name already half out of his throat. Not Maren — his other sister, the one who'd been taken, the one whose name he only said inside himself now, the way you keep a coal banked so it doesn't go out. *Ilse.* Ilse, who was eleven when the soldiers came to Vell's Hollow, who had waved at him from the cart with her whole arm because she thought being taken for testing meant she was special. Ilse, who had stopped waving when she understood.

He sat up. The undercroft was warm with sleeping bodies, the low orange glow of banked coals, Sef's empty cot that no one had moved since the bridge. Someone — Wren, probably — had draped a blanket over his shoulders while he slept. He pulled it tighter and did not let himself cry, because crying made noise, and noise made the thing in the dark turn its attention toward him like a great blind face.

*I know where your sister is,* said the voice in the wall of the world.

Osk went very still.

It had been speaking to him for six nights now. At first he'd told himself it was the king bleeding through, the poor buried man reaching for any hand. But the king's voice was small and human and drowned. This voice was not drowned. This voice stood on dry land and looked down at the water.

*You hear me,* it said, not as sound but as certainty, the way you know your own name. *You have always heard me. That is why I have come to you and not to the singers, not to the witch, not to the old soldier with his graves. You are the door, Osk. Doors are honest things.*

"I'm not," he whispered into the dark. "I'm not anything."

*Everything is something to me. Even doors.*

* * *

He didn't eat at morning meal. He sat with his bowl of barley porridge untouched, watching August across the fire. She was mending a strap on her pack, her hands moving with the unconscious competence of someone raised on the road, and she caught him looking and raised an eyebrow — that quick, wry expression that had become, over these weeks, the closest thing Osk had to a feeling of safety.

"You look like you slept in a ditch," she said.

"Close enough."

She set down the strap. August was good at that — setting things down when they mattered. Brannoc would have pressed, Sef would have healed, Tam would have sung, but August just waited, and the waiting was somehow easier to fall into than any question.

"It's getting louder," Osk said finally. "The king. Or — not the king."

August's hands went still entirely. Around them the undercroft went on: Lark arguing with Wren about rope lengths, Brannoc's low voice counting stores with the quartermaster's habit he'd never shed. But between August and Osk a small cold room opened up, and they both stepped into it.

"Since when?"

"Six nights." His voice came out smaller than he wanted. "At first I thought it was him. It isn't. It talks like—" He searched for it. "Like someone reading my thoughts aloud to me. Politely. Like it's doing me a kindness."

August reached across and took his wrist, not gently but firmly, the way you hold someone on a ledge. "Has it asked you for anything?"

And there it was. The whole shape of it, sitting in his chest like swallowed ice. He could tell her now. He could say *it offered me Ilse* right here over the porridge fire, and August would do what August did, which was carry things.

But the bargain wasn't struck yet. And some rotten, exhausted corner of him wanted to hear the price again first — wanted to stand in the marketplace of himself one more time before selling.

"Not yet," he lied.

August held his eyes a moment longer. Then she squeezed his wrist once and let go, and picked up her strap, and the moment closed — but not all the way. He could feel it staying open behind him like a door he'd left unlatched.

* * *

That night the Regent came with gifts.

Osk had taken to sleeping apart now — he told them it was so his thrashing wouldn't wake the others, which was even true. He climbed to the scriptorium above the undercroft, the ruined monastery's one intact room, where moonlight fell through a broken roof in long silver blades and the frost on the windows made lace of the world below. He wrapped himself in two blankets and waited, hating himself for waiting.

*There he is,* the Regent said, warm as a hearth. *My door.*

"I'm not your anything."

*Of course. Forgive me. I forget how the young love their independence.* A pause, and then, softly, images: a kitchen with a blue door. A woman stirring something, flour on her forearms. *Shall we speak of Ilse?*

Osk's breath stopped. "You don't know her."

*I know every soul the purges touched. I ordered them, after all — or rather, the crown did, and the crown and I wear the same skin.* The image sharpened: Ilse older now, fourteen maybe, taller, her hair braided the way their mother used to braid it. She was carrying water buckets in a stone yard. Alive. Alive, alive, alive. *She is in the custody at Greywater Keep. They test the bearers there. Most fail the tests and are sent to the mills. She has not failed yet. She is clever, your sister. She hides it well. She learned that from watching you hide your hearing.*

"Stop." His voice cracked. "Stop showing me."

*Why? It is the truest kindness anyone has shown you in years. Everyone else wants things from you, Osk. The Choir wants your ears. The witch wants your door. The king wants a witness to his suffering. I am the only one offering to give.* The presence leaned closer, and it did not touch him, and that was worse — it simply filled the room the way cold fills a room. *Here is my offer, and I will make it only once, because unlike your friends I do not deal in second chances.*

The frost on the window crawled. In the silver moonlight the patterns resolved, briefly, horribly, into a map: mountains, a road south, a chapel beneath a city. Their plan, drawn in ice.

*They mean to unbind me. You have known this since the bridge, when you dreamed their route into my ear without meaning to. I ask nothing dramatic. No knives, no poison, no betrayal of bodies. Only this: when the song begins, you will stop listening. Close the door. Let the singers sing to a silence. The rite needs a hearer on this side, little door — that is the secret your witch has not yet understood. Ysolde built it that way, bless her frightened heart. A binding needs a witness within. Without one, the song is only a song.*

"That's not true," Osk whispered. "August said—"

*August says many things. She is brave and grieving and wrong about the architecture of her own inheritance.* The voice softened further, became almost tender, and that tenderness was the most frightening thing Osk had ever felt. *Say yes, and Ilse walks out of Greywater before spring. I will put her on a road east with gold in her pocket and no name that anyone can trace. She will grow old. She will braid her own daughter's hair. All of that exists, Osk, folded up inside a single word from you.*

"And if I say no?"

Silence. Then, gently, the way you'd tell a child about winter:

*Then I will show you what Greywater does with the ones who fail. I will show you every night, in perfect detail, until the rite or I end one of us. You cannot close your ears to me anymore than I could close mine to thirty years of that man's screaming. We are married now, you and I. Choose what kind of marriage it will be.*

Osk sat in the moonlight for a long time. Somewhere below, faintly, Tam was singing the evening round, the one about the river that forgets its name, and the melody rose through the floorboards like warmth rising from a hearth in a house he was standing outside of.

"Yes," he said.

The relief that flooded him was obscene — warm, sweet, total, like fever breaking. The Regent's presence withdrew with something that felt horribly like a smile.

*Wise door. Sleep now. I will show her to you each night as proof of good faith. Consider it a courtship gift.*

And it did. Every night after, Ilse in her stone yard, Ilse eating, Ilse alive — and every night Osk lay in the scriptorium drowning in gratitude and self-loathing in equal measure, telling himself he'd saved her, telling himself the Choir would understand, knowing they wouldn't, knowing *he* didn't.

* * *

On the fourth night, August found him.

He heard her boots on the stair — she walked heel-first, quietly, a smuggler's walk — and he didn't have time to arrange his face. She came into the moonlight carrying two cups of something hot and sat down beside him without asking, shoulder to shoulder, and handed him one. Chamomile with honey, the way Sef used to make it. His eyes burned.

"You've been sleeping up here five nights," she said. "Brannoc thinks it's grief. Tam thinks it's the dreams. I think it's neither, because you flinch when people hum."

The cup trembled in his hands. "August—"

"I'm going to tell you something," she said, looking out at the frost-laced dark, "and then I'm going to shut up. When I ran from Ysolde, I did something I've never told anyone. There was a man on the road who recognized my teacher's mark on my kit, and he was going to sell me to the crown, and I could have run but instead I burned his ledgers with him standing next to them. He lived. But I took his livelihood and his trust in one night, and I told myself for three years it was survival, and it was, and it also wasn't. It was fear wearing survival's coat." She turned to look at him then, and her eyes in the moonlight were steady and terribly kind. "Whatever it offered you — and it offered you something, because that's what it does, it offered Ysolde the same thing once, I read it in her letters — whatever it is, you haven't done it yet. Saying yes in the dark isn't the same as doing the deed. There's still a gap between those two things, and I'm standing in it, and I'm not moving."

Osk broke.

It was not graceful. It was fifteen years old and sobbing into chamomile, and August held the cup so it wouldn't spill and then held him, one-armed, awkward, absolute. And the words came out of him in pieces — *the map in the frost, the door, the witness, Ilse, Ilse at Greywater, I said yes, I said yes, I said yes* — and August never once tightened her arms in judgment or loosened them in horror. She just listened the way she did everything: completely.

When he was spent, hoarse and hollowed, she was quiet a long moment.

"The witness," she said slowly. "It said the rite needs a witness within. A hearer on this side."

"Ysolde built it that way. It said—"

"It would know. It watched her build it." August set down both cups and stood, and Osk watched something move across her face — not despair, nothing so simple. Recalculation. The smuggler, replotting the route. "Osk. Look at me. Did it ever actually lie to you? About Ilse, about the rite — did anything it showed you contradict something you know is true?"

"No," he admitted. "That's the worst part."

"Good." And she smiled, and it was not a comforting smile; it was the smile of someone who has just found a hole in a fence. "Because a liar's best trick is telling the truth about the wrong things. If the rite needs a witness — fine. Then the witness is part of the architecture, and architecture can be rebuilt. Ysolde left us letters, Osk. Thirty years of letters. If there's another way to be the door — if the door can be opened from our side instead of yours—" She stopped herself, shook her head. "No. Not tonight. Tonight you're going to drink your tea, and tomorrow we wake Brannoc and Tam, and you tell them exactly what you told me. All of it. And then we get to work."

"They'll hate me. Brannoc lost—"

"Brannoc voted for mercy knowing what mercy costs. He'll survive one scared boy." She crouched, took his face in both hands, and her voice dropped to something fierce and quiet. "Listen to me. What you did tonight — confessing — that wasn't weakness. That was the first blow anyone's struck against that thing since Ysolde bound it. It planned everything around your silence. You just took its plan away."

Below them, faintly, Tam's evening round ended. The last note hung in the frozen air.

"One more thing," August said. "This Ilse it shows you. Alive, safe, growing older. Ask yourself what a creature that has worn a liar's crown for thirty years understands about keeping promises." She squeezed his hands once, hard. "We're going to get your sister, Osk. But we're going to do it the way that leaves everyone breathing — including you."

She gathered the cups and started down the stairs, then paused, silhouetted against the moonlight.

"Get some sleep. Tomorrow we fight."

And Osk lay back among the blankets in the ruined scriptorium, beneath the broken roof and the indifferent stars, and for the first time in six nights the voice in the walls of the world said nothing at all — and he understood, with a clarity that felt almost like peace, that it had gone quiet because it was listening.

Let it listen, he thought, closing his eyes. Let it hear the whole Choir singing tomorrow.

The door, it turned out, swung both ways.
