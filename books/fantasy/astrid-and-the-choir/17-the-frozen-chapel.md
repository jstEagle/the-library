## 17. The Frozen Chapel

The forgotten ways of the palace smelled of old stone and older secrets — dust, candle-tallow gone to wax-sweat in the dark, and beneath it all a faint mineral coldness like the breath of a well that had never seen sun. August kept her hand on Maren Vale's shoulder as they walked, because the Inquisitor moved through these passages with the certainty of someone who had spent years memorizing them for exactly this purpose, though she had not known it until three days ago.

Behind August came Tam, humming under her breath — not the rite's song, not yet, but something small and steady to keep the fear from pooling in their boots. Osk walked last, and August did not look at him. She had not been able to look at him since his confession on the road, two nights past, when he had told them all in a voice like a cracked bell: *It knows where we're going. It has always known. I gave it everything.*

*Then why are we still walking toward it?* Brannoc had asked, and no one had answered, because there was only one answer. The chapel was the only place the unbinding could be done. The Regent could not stop them coming without revealing how it knew — and so it would simply be there when they arrived, patient as winter.

"Here." Maren's voice was low, stripped of its courtroom music. Her lantern swung left, illuminating a seam in the wall so fine August would have taken it for a shadow. "This door hasn't opened in forty years, by the palace's own records. The records are wrong. My predecessor's predecessor used this way to move bearers quietly out of the city during the last purge. He wrote it down in cipher, and I found the cipher in a box of confiscated letters, and I have thought about that every day since."

"You were already doubting," August said.

"I was already *curious*," Maren corrected. "Doubt came later, wearing curiosity's clothes." She pressed her palm flat against the stone and pushed, and the seam widened with a grinding whisper, exhaling air so cold it bit.

Beyond lay stairs going down, and the cold came up the stairs like water finding its level.

* * *

They descended for what felt like an hour. The walls changed around them — dressed palace stone giving way to rougher work, then to masonry so old its mortar had crumbled to grit, then to something else entirely: blocks of pale granite carved with lines of script that predated any alphabet August knew, worn nearly smooth by hands or weather or time itself. Ysolde had never spoken of this place directly, but August remembered now, with a lurch, the diagrams in the burned letters — circles within circles, and at the center a small square room drawn with obsessive precision, annotated in her mentor's cramped hand: *the chapel below, where it was done, where it can be undone.*

Tam's humming stopped. In the silence August heard what Tam had heard: nothing. No drip of water, no skitter of rats, no wind. The mountain above the capital held its breath.

"The cold isn't natural," Sef would have said. Sef was dead on a bridge in the Greywater passes, and August carried the memory of her falling like a stone in her chest, heavy and smooth and impossible to set down. "Cold is just heat leaving. Something down here is *drinking*."

Osk made a small sound. August turned despite herself.

He stood with both hands pressed over his ears, eyes wide and fixed on the dark ahead. "It's singing," he whispered. "Not — not words. It's doing what Tam does. Holding a note. Holding it for thirty years."

"Holding what note?" Brannoc asked, and his voice was gravel over deep water.

Osk lowered his hands slowly. "A *bound* one."

No one spoke after that. They went down.

* * *

The chapel opened before them like a held breath released.

It was not large. A square chamber perhaps forty feet to a side, ceiling lost in darkness above, and every surface — floor, walls, the pillars that marched along each side — covered in the binding script, thousands upon thousands of characters cut into the granite and filled with something that still gleamed faintly silver after thirty years. August's skin prickled with recognition. She had traced those letterforms in Ysolde's margins, practiced them in frost on window glass, dreamed them. This was her mentor's handwriting, scaled up until it became architecture, until it became a prison.

At the center of the floor, a circle of bare stone perhaps ten feet across — the only clean space in the room. Inside it, a low altar, and on the altar, a crown.

Not the crown. Not gold and jewels. This was iron, plain and black, small enough to hold in two hands, the kind of thing a smith might make in an afternoon and regret for a lifetime. But August felt its weight from across the room, a pressure behind her eyes, a hook set deep in some part of her she hadn't known she had.

"That's the anchor," Maren said quietly. "The regalia the king wears is theater. This is the true crown. It never leaves this room. Every coronation, every oath, every law signed in the king's name — it all flows down here first, and the thing below answers."

"How do you know that?" Tam asked sharply.

Maren was silent a moment. "Because I've read the interrogation records of every bearer the crown ever took," she said finally. "And the ones who survived the questioning all said the same strange thing, in different words. That when they were broken, they didn't feel the king watching them. They felt something *older*, and very tired, and very hungry. I thought it was delirium." She looked at the iron crown with an expression August had never expected to see on that composed face: naked grief. "I was wrong about a great many things."

Brannoc stepped forward, boots ringing on the script-carved stone, and August caught his arm. "Wait. Look at the circle. The clean ground. Ysolde's notes say the caster stands inside it, and the singers stand at the four—"

"I know where the singers stand," Tam said. Her voice had gone very steady, the steadiness of someone stepping off a cliff on purpose. "I've known since the vote. I've been composing the descant in my head for six weeks." She managed something that was almost a smile. "Sef was supposed to take the third harmony. I'll carry it. She'd have wanted it carried badly rather than not at all."

August laughed — a short, broken sound she couldn't help — and Tam squeezed her hand once, hard, and let go.

They began to arrange themselves. Maren took the stairway door, lantern raised, sword drawn — she had refused to give up the blade even after burning her commission, saying that a defector's promise was worth more if she could keep them alive long enough to honor it. Osk sat against the eastern pillar, knees drawn up, because his task was to listen, and listening required him to open the door in his mind that he had spent his whole life trying to nail shut. Tam moved to the northern point of the circle and began, softly, to warm her voice — scales climbing like steps into cold air.

Brannoc stood at the southern point, facing the door, and August understood without being told. He had voted for mercy knowing it might cost everything; now he planted himself between that mercy and whatever came for it, because that was the only penance he believed in. His family had died behind a door much like this one, in a village called Harrow's Bend, while he was away selling smuggled wool. He had told August this once, drunk, in the tunnels under Candlemere, and had never mentioned it again. But he had built his whole life since into a door held shut.

"August." His voice was rough. "When it starts — whatever happens — you finish it. You hear me? Whatever you see, whatever it shows you, whatever it *sounds* like it's offering — you don't stop singing, you don't stop cutting your palm, you don't stop. Promise me."

"I promise."

"It's not enough." For the first time since she'd known him, Brannoc's composure cracked, and underneath it was not anger but terror, plain as a wound. "I've buried everyone I ever — " He stopped. Started again. "You're the last lesson she gets to teach, girl. Don't waste it."

August's throat closed. She nodded, and it was enough, and it wasn't, and there was no time for either.

She stepped into the circle.

The cold rose up through her boots like a tide meeting a swimmer. The iron crown sat on its altar three feet away, and up close she could see that the black metal was not smooth — it was covered in the same script, but these characters were *moving*, crawling slowly across the surface like insects disturbed under bark, and she understood that the Regent had been reading them for thirty years, and rewriting them, and waiting.

She drew Ysolde's knife — the bone-handled knife that had been the first thing August ever stole, back when stealing from her mentor was a game — and opened her palm along the old line, and let her blood fall on the altar's edge.

The script on the walls lit silver.

And the Regent stopped pretending to sleep.

* * *

It did not roar. That was the worst of it. August had braced herself for a roar, for a shriek, for the earth-shaking fury of a thing betrayed. Instead the temperature dropped so far so fast that her breath froze mid-air and fell as glittering dust, and the darkness between the pillars grew *thick*, and a voice that was not a voice said, everywhere at once, gently:

*You came. I confess, I wondered if you would. So few people keep appointments with their own funerals.*

"Begin," August said, and her voice came out steady, which surprised her, and she would remember later that it was Brannoc's presence at her back that steadied it.

Tam began to sing.

The song was old — older than the chapel, older than the kingdom, a round that the Choir had inherited from hunted generations of bearers who had passed it hand to hand like embers in a blackout. Tam's voice alone was thin in the vast cold, a single thread of warmth. Then Osk, eyes shut, tears freezing on his cheeks, added the second line, his boy's voice cracking and holding anyway. Then August, who could not sing, who had never sung, found the third part rising out of her blood as if Ysolde had hidden it there years ago and it had only been waiting for the door to open.

The silver script blazed. The moving characters on the iron crown slowed, stuttered, began to be *overwritten*.

And the Regent began to fight.

It did not touch their bodies. It went for the soft places. August felt the chapel dissolve around her and suddenly she was nine years old, standing in Ysolde's doorway in the rain, and Ysolde was turning away, and the door was closing, and the voice poured through the gap like smoke: *she left you, she chose to leave you, and now you will die here among strangers for her sins—*

*I know,* August sang, and kept singing. *I know, and I'm here anyway.* The lie dissolved. The song continued.

Across the circle she saw Tam stagger, saw the singer's face go white as her own fear took her — drowning, always drowning, the river at Millbrook, the sister she couldn't save — and then Tam planted her feet like a woman in a gale and *pushed the note higher*, and August could have wept at the sheer stubborn beauty of it.

Then the door banged open.

Not the stairway door — the far door, the one none of them had entered by, the one that should have opened onto collapsed corridors and forty years of rubble. Through it came men in the king's colors, six of them, eight, crossbows level, faces blank in the way of faces that were not entirely their own, and behind them the darkness itself seemed to lean forward with interest.

"No," Maren breathed. "They weren't due to rotate the guard until—" She was already moving, planting herself in the doorway, commission-burned hands raised, voice ringing with the full authority of an office she had destroyed: *"In the name of the Crown Council, stand down—"*

The crossbows fired. Maren twisted, took a bolt through the meat of her shoulder, and did not fall, and August would think about that later, about the Inquisitor bleeding in a doorway and refusing the ground.

But the soldiers were a diversion, and August knew it one heartbeat too late.

The Regent came out of the dark behind Brannoc like winter coming through a window left unlatched.

There was no shape to it — or rather, there were too many shapes, a flickering suggestion of antlers, of a crowned head, of long gray fingers, of something winged and jointed wrongly, never settling, never whole. It did not walk so much as *arrive*, again and again, closer each time. And Brannoc — Brannoc, who had voted for mercy with his dead family's names in his mouth — turned from the southern point of the circle and put himself in its path with his arms spread wide, and shouted the only word he had left:

"*Sing!*"

The Regent passed through him.

It was not violence, exactly. It was subtraction. Where the entity touched him, Brannoc simply ceased — color first, then warmth, then the great stubborn engine of his heart. August saw him arch, saw his mouth open on a sound that was not a scream but a final exhalation, saw frost bloom across his beard and his open eyes. He stayed standing. Even dying, he stayed standing, a wall of a man propped against the dark, and the dark broke around him like a river around a bridge-stone, buying them seconds, buying Tam's voice time to find the fourth harmony, buying August the three heartbeats she needed to press her bleeding palm flat against the iron crown and speak the first word of the unbinding.

The script on the walls flared white.

The Regent recoiled from Brannoc's frozen body with something that might, in a lesser creature, have been surprise. The blank-faced soldiers faltered. Maren slid down the doorframe, clutching her shoulder, alive. Osk lifted his head, and his eyes had gone silver as the script, and through his mouth — his young, frightened, borrowed mouth — the entity spoke its first true words in thirty years:

*"You cannot unmake what was willingly made, little witch. Your teacher learned that. Ask her why she ran. Ask her what it cost her. Ask her what it will cost y—*

"Ysolde's debt is paid," August said, and cut the second line into the crown with the knife, and began the third verse of the song.

The chapel shook. Somewhere far above, thirty years of frozen silence began, at last, to crack.

And the Regent turned all of its endless attention — every year of patience, every stolen dream, every buried king — onto August Blackwood, and began, with terrible tenderness, to sing *back*.
