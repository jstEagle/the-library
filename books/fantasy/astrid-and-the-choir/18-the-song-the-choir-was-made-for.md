## 18. The Song the Choir Was Made For

Tam Voss had sung in worse places than a frozen chapel beneath the capital. He had sung in a smuggler's hold with bilge water sloshing over his boots; he had sung for coin in a tavern where the crowd threw things when they didn't like the key. But he had never sung anywhere that hated him back.

The chapel breathed cold. Not the ordinary cold of stone and depth — this was a living cold, an attentive cold, the kind that pressed against your eardrums like something listening through them. The vaulted ceiling above was furred with frost that caught what little light remained: three guttering candles August had wedged into a broken censer, and the pale blue glow of Osk's Gift, which flickered around him like breath on glass. Somewhere beyond the great doors Brannoc was holding the line alone, and every few minutes the doors shuddered on their hinges like the ribs of something enormous trying to inhale.

Tam stood at the center of the crossing, where the floor tiles still showed a faded compass rose, and arranged his singers around him the way he'd done a thousand times in Candlemere's undercroft — Sef's place empty and aching at his left, Wren at his right with her hands shaking too badly to hide it, Lark pressed close to her twin as if proximity were armor. Maren Vale knelt near the door with her sword drawn and her face gray, a hunter turned guard dog, watching the hinges instead of the singers because she could not bear to watch the song.

And August stood opposite Tam across the compass rose, sleeves pushed up, chalk lines already drawn on the frozen stone in Ysolde's looping hand, waiting for the only thing she couldn't do herself.

"From the top," Tam said. "No matter what happens. From the top."

Wren laughed — a thin, cracked sound with no humor in it. "You say that like we'll get a second chance."

"We always get a second chance," Tam said. "That's what a refrain *is*."

He raised his hands. The candles leaned toward him like flowers toward sun.

He began.

The opening of the rite-song was not impressive. That was the first thing Ysolde's notation had taught them, all those weeks of deciphering by candlelight in the undercroft while the snow came down over Candlemere like a curtain drawn against the world: the song did not begin with power. It began small, almost humble, a rising third and a falling fifth, a melody so plain it might have been a lullaby, or a work-song, or the tune a mother hums over a cradle without knowing she's humming at all. Tam sang it alone first, unaccompanied, his voice thin in the vast dark, and he felt absurd doing it — a man singing a nursery tune into the teeth of a god.

Then Wren came in underneath him, low and steady, and Lark found the third, and the plain little melody suddenly had roots, had soil, had *depth*, and Tam felt the chapel change around them the way a room changes when a fire is lit. The frost on the ceiling dimmed. The cold pulled back a half-step, like a listener leaning away.

Osk's voice joined last, high and wavering, and where it touched the harmony the air went strange and sweet, the way it always did when the boy sang — as though somewhere very far away, someone else were hearing this exact song and remembering it.

That was the point. That had always been the point. The song was old — older than the binding, older than the Regent, older, maybe, than the crown itself. It was a song about unmaking, but gently, the way you unmake a knot rather than cut it. It had been made for exactly this: for a voice to reach down into a buried man and remind him of his own name.

For thirty seconds, it was working.

Tam felt it working. He felt the song go out through the stone and down through whatever lay beneath the stone, and he felt something answer — not the Regent, but the other one, the small one, the king, turning over in the cellar of himself like a man hearing his childhood street called out in a foreign city.

Then the doors stopped shuddering.

The silence beyond them was worse than the pounding. Tam kept singing, kept his eyes on the score in his mind, but every hair on his body stood up, because the silence meant the Regent had stopped trying to get *in* the ordinary way.

It had decided to come in through the song.

* * *

It started with Wren.

She was holding the drone, the long low note that anchored everything, and Tam heard it waver — just once, just slightly, the way a note wavers when a singer's throat tightens. Then again. Then she was looking at nothing, her lips still shaping the syllables, her eyes gone wide and wet, and Tam knew before she said it, knew the way you know weather coming.

"Wren." He didn't stop singing. You never stopped singing; stopping was death, stopping was letting the harmony collapse into the silence where the thing lived. "Stay with me. Eyes on me."

"He's here," Wren whispered, and her drone slid flat. "Tam, he's — I'm in the kitchen, I'm eight years old, and the soldiers are coming up the road, and Mama is telling me to run and I can't remember why I should run, why would I run, they're only—"

"Lark." Tam pitched the instruction under the melody, a conductor's whisper threaded through the verse. "Take her line. Don't fight her fear. Sing *around* it."

Lark moved closer to her sister until their shoulders touched, and lifted her voice into the space Wren's was abandoning, and for a moment the harmony held on three voices instead of four, thinner, more exposed, a table with a leg kicked out from under it. Wren's mouth kept moving but no sound came. She was somewhere else entirely, standing in a kitchen thirty years gone, and the Regent was pouring the memory into her like tar, slow and patient, showing her the moment that had broken her life and offering, with terrible gentleness, to let her live inside it forever.

"No," Tam sang, and folded the word into the melody where a rest should have been, a word that wasn't in the score but belonged there anyway, the way folk songs grow words the way rivers grow banks. "No. Come home. We're here. We're *here*."

Wren blinked. Her eyes found his. Her voice came back into the drone like a swimmer surfacing, ragged and gasping, and the four-part harmony closed over the gap and healed.

The cold in the chapel deepened by a degree. Something out there in the dark had registered the failure, and was interested now. Truly interested.

* * *

It went for Lark next, and then for Osk, and it did not bother being subtle anymore.

Lark got the fire. That was hers, the fear she carried folded small at the bottom of her chest the way everyone carries their worst thing — the border village burning when she was eleven, the sky the color of a wound, her own voice screaming names that no longer had anyone to answer to them. The Regent gave it back to her whole, in the middle of the second movement, and her descant shattered upward into a scream that was almost the right note and almost killed the harmony entirely. Tam grabbed it — reached out with his own line and caught hers the way you catch a falling child, taking the weight into his own throat, bending his melody to carry both parts until Lark could breathe again. His voice tore a little doing it. He let it tear. A voice was a tool, and tools were for using.

Osk got something worse, because Osk had been talking to the thing for weeks and it had a whole garden of fears planted in him already. It showed him his sister. It showed her alive, it showed her in a cell with bread and water and her own name, and it showed him the door of the cell, and it put its voice — that voice, the reasonable, weary, grandfatherly voice Tam had heard once through Osk's nightmares and never forgotten — right into the middle of the boy's high clear line:

*All you have to do is stop singing.*

Osk's voice died.

Three singers now. The song staggered. Tam felt the whole structure sway like a bridge under too much weight, felt the Regent pour itself into the gap where the fourth voice had been, filling the harmony with rot, wearing Osk's absence like a mask. For one horrible moment the song was still going but it wasn't *theirs* anymore; the thing below had learned the tune in thirty seconds of listening, the way it had learned everything, crowns and kingdoms and the shapes of human hearts, and it was singing along.

So Tam did the only thing he knew how to do, the thing he had been doing since he was six years old and his mother first put him in a choir row and told him: *your job is not to be the best voice, boy. Your job is to be the voice that doesn't stop.*

He changed the key.

Not subtly. Not artfully. He wrenched the entire song sideways, up a minor third, mid-phrase, the musical equivalent of pulling a rug, and the Regent — which had learned the song in the old key, which had committed its vast attention to the old key — stumbled. Its borrowed harmony collapsed into dissonance. And in that dissonance, in that ugly grinding wrongness, there was no room for it, because the song was no longer the song it had learned. It was a new song. It was, in fact, the same song, but sung by people who refused to stand still, and that was a different creature altogether.

"That's it," Tam gasped between phrases, blood-warm joy rising through the terror like light through water. "That's it, you beautiful bastards — move! Keep moving! Never sing it the same way twice!"

They understood. Wren understood, dragging her drone up into the new key with her eyes squeezed shut and tears freezing on her cheeks. Lark understood, climbing after her, laughing now, actually laughing, a wild cracked sound woven into the descant. Even Maren looked up from the door. And Osk —

Osk was still silent. Still staring at the place where his sister wasn't. The Regent's bargain hung around him like a noose, and Tam could see the boy weighing it, really weighing it, the way a drowning man weighs the anchor chained to his ankle.

Tam could not leave his line to go to him. If he left the line, the song fell, and if the song fell, everything fell — Brannoc's sacrifice behind the door, August's chalk circles, Sef dead on a bridge in the mountains, all of it traded for a nursery tune in a frozen room.

So he did what conductors do. He looked at Osk, and he sang *at* him, poured the melody directly at the boy's bowed head, and he thought — not words, nothing so clean, but the shape of words, the shape of a truth he'd carried since Millbrook Cross since the blizzard since the first night a stranger dragged him out of the snow:

*We are not the ones who left you. We are the ones who stayed.*

Osk's chin came up.

His voice came back on the high part, the part that floated above the harmony like a bird above a field, and it was ragged and it was cracking and it was the most beautiful sound Tam had ever made or heard, because it was chosen. Every note of it was chosen, snatched out of the Regent's fist one at a time, paid for, owned. The four voices locked together, climbed together, and the song stopped being a song about unbinding and became, for a few bars, simply a song — the thing songs are, the reason humans ever made them at all: proof that somebody was not alone in the dark.

The frost on the ceiling began to fall. Not melt — *fall*, shaken loose, drifting down through the candlelight like snow falling upward in reverse, and beneath it the compass rose on the floor began to glow with August's chalk lines waking one by one.

* * *

The Regent stopped playing fair.

Tam felt the change come the way you feel a storm change direction — a pressure shift, a wrongness in the ears. The memories stopped. The whispers stopped. Instead the cold gathered itself into the center of the chapel, into the space just past the doors, and became a presence, vast and patient and *attentive*, and it spoke to all of them at once, not in words but in certainty:

*I have heard every song ever sung. I unmade the choir that wrote this one. Their bones are the foundation of this palace. What do you imagine you are to me?*

And it was true, and it was awful, and Tam felt it land in each of them — felt Wren's voice falter, felt Lark's grip on the melody slip, felt Osk sway like a reed. They were four frightened people in a hole in the ground singing a dead woman's music against a thing that had eaten centuries.

But Tam Voss had spent his whole life among people the world had decided were nothing. Smugglers and hedge-witches and burned children and boys who heard kings dreaming. He knew exactly what they were to the world. He also knew what they were to each other, and he knew — with the stubborn, unreasonable, completely indefensible certainty that had gotten him through every bad night of his life — that the Regent was wrong about the most important thing.

"You've heard every song," Tam said aloud, still singing, weaving the words into the refrain so the melody never broke. "But you've never *sung* one. Not once. Not in all your centuries. Because you can't. Because a song needs more than one voice, and you have never in your existence been anything but alone."

The presence recoiled. Actually recoiled, a flinch of pure cold, and Tam laughed into the rising harmony, laughed with a torn throat and freezing lungs, because he had found it. The bruise. The one soft place in all that ancient armor.

"Alone in a crown," he sang. "Alone in a kingdom. Alone in a man's skull for thirty years with nobody to sing to. We know what you are. We've *had* members like you."

The song surged. All four voices, plus August's spoken cadence rising from the chalk circle, plus — faintly, impossibly, from somewhere far below and far within, a fifth voice, thin and rusty and thirty years out of practice, a king in the cellar of himself opening his mouth and joining the refrain he'd been made to forget.

The compass rose blazed. The great doors boomed once, twice — Brannoc, still holding, somehow still holding — and the song went up like a signal fire, and the Regent screamed, and the scream was the beginning of the end, and Tam Voss sang straight into the heart of it.

He did not see the doors give way behind them. He did not see what came through.

But he heard Maren Vale rise to her feet, and he heard her say, very quietly, "Oh — oh, no—"
