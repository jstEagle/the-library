## 19. The Price

The chapel breathed cold.

August had thought she knew cold before — the Thornfell passes in deepwinter, the wind off the Millbrook millpond, the way frost crept through a cracked window and silvered the inside of the glass. She had known nothing. This cold came up through the floor like the chapel itself was exhaling, a cold that had been waiting thirty years for someone to walk into it, and it pressed against her skin not like absence of warmth but like attention. Like being *noticed* by something that had been sleeping with its eyes open.

The frozen chapel beneath the capital was not large. That was the first thing that surprised her, when Maren's lantern finally found the far wall. After the palace's forgotten ways — staircases that descended into nothing, corridors bricked shut on the maps and open in the stone, the long dark throat of the old processional passage — August had expected a cathedral. Instead: a room no bigger than the Candlemere refectory, a vaulted ceiling lost in shadow above, walls of black granite furred with ice that caught the lantern light and scattered it into a hundred small false stars. An altar of the same black stone. And carved into the floor before it, a circle within a circle within a circle, the lines worn shallow by three decades of nothing happening inside them.

Ysolde's lines. August knew her teacher's hand the way she knew her own heartbeat — the slight asymmetry of the inner ring, the way each sigil was drawn with a single unbroken stroke, beginning and ending at the same point, as if the circle had never been drawn at all but had simply always been there, waiting to be noticed. She had traced these same shapes a thousand times in the frost on Ysolde's windowsill. She had never known what she was tracing.

*You were teaching me the whole time*, she thought. *You were making me memorize my own grave.*

The Regent was waiting. It had known since the bridge — since Sef died on the ice with an arrow through her throat and Tam's song still caught between her teeth — and it had done nothing with the knowing except wait, the way a spider waits, the way winter waits. It sat in the king the way a hand sits in a glove. King Aldous knelt at the center of the circles, and he was not the man from the proclamations, not the stern gilded face on every wall in the kingdom. He was thin. He was *old* in a way that had nothing to do with years, and his eyes — when they found August across the chapel — were the eyes of a man looking out of a window at a world he had been told no longer existed.

He was weeping. He had been weeping, August realized, since they entered. Silently. The tears had frozen on his cheeks and glittered when he moved.

"Ysolde's girl," the Regent said, and the voice was the king's voice, and it was not. It came from everywhere the ice was. "I wondered which of her strays would come. She always collected the broken ones."

"August." Brannoc's hand closed on her shoulder, heavy and warm and shaking. He was bleeding from the door — from holding the door, from the thing that had come through the door wearing the shapes of the Choir's dead — and he stood between her and the king like a wall that had decided, after thirty years of hating this king, to die for him instead. "August. The song first. Then you."

Tam was already moving. Her voice, when it rose, was small in the great cold dark — a single thread of melody, the first phrase of the rite-song that the Choir had been singing to each other in fragments for years without knowing what it was for, a lullaby and a lockpick, a thing Ysolde had taught a smuggler's daughter in a kitchen in Millbrook twenty years before August was born. Tam's thread was joined by Osk's, thin and wavering, and then by the others — the singers who had come down the mountain and across the kingdom carrying a song like a coal carried in cupped hands.

The circles lit.

Not with fire. With *remembering* — the ice on the walls flaring soft gold along Ysolde's lines, the sigils waking one by one like windows being opened in a dark house, and August felt the chapel recognize the song, felt the binding underneath everything shudder like a great beast turning in its sleep.

And the Regent began to scream.

Not aloud. Inside. It poured itself into their minds the way cold water pours into a boot — every fear they owned, every grief, every private shame dragged up into the light and magnified. August saw the Choir stagger. She saw Tam's voice crack and hold, crack and hold, stubborn as a fence post in a flood. She saw Osk on his knees with his hands over his ears, singing anyway, singing through his tears, and she understood with a lurch that the Regent was showing him his sister, showing him what it would do to her, and that Osk was singing *anyway*.

She walked to the center of the circles.

The song was a door. She had understood that much from Ysolde's letters, from the pages she had read a hundred times by candlelight while the Choir slept around her. The song was a door, and the circles were the frame, and the binder's line was the key. *The apprentice stands where the binder stood. The apprentice speaks the words the binder spoke. And the rite takes its price from the hand that turns the key.*

She had thought, all this way, all this winter, that the price was a life. Brannoc had argued for it — had stood in the candlelight at Candlemere with his dead family behind his eyes and argued that the only fair price was the king's, an eye for a kingdom of eyes. And August had argued for salvation without knowing, until this moment, standing on Ysolde's lines with the song rising around her like water, what salvation would cost.

She knew now.

It was in the letters. It had been in the letters all along, in the passage she had read as metaphor, as poetry, as the rambling of a woman going half-mad with guilt: *the rite does not take what you offer, child. It takes what you are. A binding of this depth is woven from a life's whole thread, and to unweave it you must give the thread back — all of it, the Gift entire, the thing you are underneath the thing you seem. That is why I ran. That is why I could not finish it. I told myself I fled the crown's hunters. I fled the arithmetic.*

August stood where Ysolde had stood thirty years ago, and understood, at last, with a clarity that felt like falling, why her mentor had vanished in the night without a goodbye. Not cowardice. Not abandonment. *Arithmetic.* Ysolde had stood here at nineteen years old and woven a king into a prison, and she had spent her whole life knowing that the only way out was through herself, and she had not been able to make herself walk through the door. She had run instead. She had run for forty years, and taught a smuggler's daughter her circles in the frost, and loved her in the wordless sideways way of the guilty, and fled again when the end came near.

*She was making me the key,* August thought. *She was too afraid to turn it herself, so she made me. God forgive her. God forgive me, because I am going to turn it.*

The Regent felt her understanding. She felt it turn its attention on her fully, the way a searchlight turns, and the voice came again, and this time it was gentle, and that was the most horrible thing yet.

"Girl. Smuggler. Thief of roads. I have worn this crown for thirty years and the kingdom has never been quieter. No plague-wars. No succession bloodbath. I keep the peace the way a tomb keeps a body. You will unmake all of it for a man who knelt in his cellar of himself while my hunters burned your kind in the villages." A pause, silk over stone. "And you will pay for the privilege with the only thing you have ever loved. Your little fire. Your hedge-witch spark. You will be *nothing*, August Blackwood. You will be what the others are. Is that not what you have feared all your life — that without the Gift you are only a smuggler's daughter with dead eyes and a ledger heart?"

The song held. Tam's voice, Osk's voice, the voices of the dead and the nearly-dead, holding the harmony together through sheer stubborn love, and inside that harmony August stood very still and did her arithmetic.

It was true. She had feared it. All winter, all her life, she had feared the smallness of herself without the fire — the way Ysolde's lessons had been the only place she had ever felt *large*. And the Regent offered her that fear like a merchant offering coin, and August looked at it, and looked at the kneeling king with his frozen tears, and looked at Brannoc standing wounded between her and the dark, and Tam singing through her cracked voice, and Osk singing through his sister's ghost.

"No," she said. "You've got it wrong. You think the Gift is the thing I love. It isn't. It's the thing I *hid behind*."

She knelt at the center of the circles, where the king knelt, where Ysolde had knelt, and she took the king's frozen hands in hers, and she began to speak.

The words were Ysolde's words. She had carried them from Millbrook without knowing — every lesson, every frost-circle, every half-remembered rhyme had been the rite broken into pieces small enough for a child to swallow, and now the pieces came together in her mouth like a key remembering it was a key. The circles blazed. The song climbed. And August felt the rite reach into her — not like a knife, not like a theft, but like a *tide going out*, slow and vast and gentle, drawing back along the channels of her the thing that had always been there, the warmth in her palms, the whisper under her skin, the hedge-witch spark she had carried since she was six years old and Ysolde had first caught her hands and said *oh, you're one of mine*.

She had a moment — one long, wide, golden moment — to feel all of it at once. Every spell she had ever cast. The soldier she had hidden in the millpond's reflection. The frost she had walked through unburnt. Wren, falling from the supply train, and August's power going out and catching her like a hand out of the sky. The whole thread of her, the thing she was underneath the thing she seemed.

*It's all right*, she told it. *You can go. You were never mine. You were only borrowed.*

The tide went out.

And the binding — thirty years deep, woven by a frightened girl and fed on a kingdom — came apart.

It did not shatter. It *unraveled*, thread by golden thread, and the Regent screamed — truly screamed this time, aloud, through the king's mouth and through the ice and through the walls of the world, a sound like a cathedral tearing — and it tore. Out of the king. Out of the circles. Out of August, who felt it leave her too, the cold thing that had ridden the binding's edges for thirty years, the attention she had felt since she entered the chapel — and it went, shrieking, thinner and thinner, a scream stretched to a thread stretched to a whisper stretched to nothing.

The ice on the walls fell all at once, a hundred small false stars coming down.

Silence. Real silence, the first in thirty years.

King Aldous opened his eyes. They were only eyes now — a man's eyes, old and wet and astonished, with nothing behind them but a man. He looked at August. He opened his mouth. What came out was not a decree, not a thank-you, not a king's voice at all, but a whisper, cracked and small and thirty years out of practice:

"It's quiet."

And August Blackwood, kneeling in a ruined circle in a frozen chapel, reached for her Gift the way she had reached for it ten thousand times — the way she reached for her own heartbeat — and found nothing there.

No panic. That was the strange thing. No grief, not yet, not in that moment. She felt the absence the way an amputee feels weather: a fact, a change in the air, a door that was now a wall. She was a smuggler's daughter with dead eyes and a ledger heart, and she was alive, and the king was breathing, and the Regent was gone, and Tam's song was ending, note by note, the way a fire goes down.

Brannoc's hand found her shoulder again. He was crying without any attempt to hide it, this hard man who had voted for mercy and hated himself for it, and he said her name like a question.

"I'm all right," August said. And found, testing the words the way she'd test thin ice, that they were true. "I'm all right. Help him up. He's been on his knees for thirty years — it's someone else's turn to kneel."

She rose. The chapel was dark and ordinary and full of weeping singers, and somewhere above them the palace of the thing that had worn the crown was waking to a world with no Regent in it, and August Blackwood — Gift-less, unbroken, lighter than she had been since she was six years old — turned toward the long climb back into the light.

The kingdom, she thought, had no idea what was coming next.

Neither did she. But for the first time in her life, that felt less like fear than like a road.
