## 20. A King on His Knees

The first thing Aldous knew was cold.

Not the cold of the cellar — he had known that cold for thirty years, the cold of stone and silence and the thing that wore his voice — but a different cold, a clean one, the cold of air moving. Air that moved meant wind. Wind meant sky. He could not remember the last time he had felt wind.

He was on his knees.

That seemed right. He had been kneeling for three decades; it would have been strange to stand at once. His knees ached against frozen flagstones, and his hands were pressed flat before him as if in prayer, though he had long ago stopped believing anyone was listening. The chapel around him was dark except for a scatter of candle stubs guttering in iron sconces, and the walls — he could see them now, truly see them — were carved with runes half-erased by frost, the binding-script of a rite that had just finished unmaking itself. The last of it flaked from the stone like ash and drifted down over his shoulders, settling in his hair, and Aldous knelt in the falling dust of his own prison and did not know how to be a man.

Because the silence was gone.

For thirty years there had been no silence. There had been the Regent, coiled through the marrow of him, humming its endless hunger, thinking with his mouth and ruling with his hands while he screamed behind glass so far inside himself that even screaming became a kind of habit rather than a hope. He had forgotten the sound of his own thoughts uninterrupted. He had forgotten what it was to reach for a memory and find it whole. Now the silence rushed in like water into a broken hull, vast and terrible and *his*, and it was so much larger than he remembered that it frightened him more than the entity ever had.

*Who am I,* he thought, and the thought was only his own, and he wept.

It was not a kingly weeping. It was the ugly, gasping weeping of a man whose body has been someone else's house for thirty years and who has just been given back the keys. His shoulders shook. His breath came in hitches that hurt his chest. Somewhere far away, a door opened, and footsteps crossed the chapel — hesitant, human, uneven — and stopped a few feet from where he knelt.

"Your Majesty."

A woman's voice. Young. Wary. Not afraid, exactly — or afraid, but of something other than him, which was itself remarkable. No one had spoken to Aldous without fear in thirty years. They had spoken to the Regent wearing his face, and the Regent had taught the world to bow.

"I'm not," he managed. His voice was a ruin, rusted from disuse, hoarse with thirty years of disuse. "I'm not — whatever you think I am. I don't—" He lifted his head. The candlelight swam. A young woman stood over him, dark-haired, travel-worn, her hands hanging empty at her sides in a way that struck him as profoundly deliberate, the way a soldier stands when they want you to know their weapon is sheathed. Her eyes were red-rimmed and dry now, the crying already done, and there was something missing in her — some brightness, some hum — that he could not name but felt the absence of, the way you feel a tooth gone from your jaw.

"You're free," she said simply. "The binding's broken. The thing is gone."

"The thing." He laughed, or something near laughter, cracked and awful. "Do you know what it did? Do you know—"

"No," she said. "But I know what it took from me. So start there. Start with what it took from *you*."

And that — that simple, brutal kindness — undid him entirely. He bowed forward until his forehead nearly touched the stones, and the tears came again, and the woman stood there and let him, because she understood, somehow, that this too was part of the unbinding: a king learning to grieve in public for the first time in thirty years.

When he could speak again, he asked the only question left in him.

"Who have I harmed?"

The woman was quiet a long moment. "Everything," she said finally. "Everyone. Thirty years of edicts. Purges. Hunts. My mentor vanished because of it. My friend Sef died on a bridge two days ago covering our retreat from your soldiers." She said it without cruelty, which was worse than cruelty. "But it wasn't you. I watched it leave you. I paid for that watching. So I need you to understand — I'm not the one you answer to. They are." She gestured vaguely, at the chapel, the mountain above, the world beyond. "All of them."

Aldous knelt in the ruin of the binding and understood that mercy had arrived, and that it was the most terrible thing he had ever been given.

* * *

They brought him up out of the chapel in stages, because his legs would not hold him at first. The boy — Osk, the woman said, the one who had heard his dreams all these years, who had heard them and never once looked away — helped carry him up the forgotten stairways with an arm slung under his shoulder, and Aldous felt the boy trembling and said nothing, because he recognized the trembling. It was the shaking of someone who has touched the Regent's mind and lived. Later, he would learn what it had cost Osk. Later, he would kneel to the boy as he had never knelt to any throne.

But first there was the surface. First there was the sky.

They came out through a postern gate into the grey hour before dawn, and Aldous stopped dead in the snow and looked up, and the sky went on forever, and he made a sound that was half laugh and half sob and pressed his bare hand against the falling flakes until his fingers burned.

"It snows," he said stupidly.

"It snows," August agreed. August — that was her name. August Blackwood. She had told him on the stairs, in the flat tone of someone reciting facts to keep from feeling them. "Every winter. For thirty years."

Thirty years. He tried to hold the number and it slid through him like water through fingers. He had been twenty-six when Ysolde came to him with her apology already on her lips and her rite already half-built. Twenty-six, and newly crowned, and so proud of the peace he was going to make. And then the entity — the Regent, the Choir called it, an old word for an old horror — had come to him in the shape of counsel, and Ysolde had bound it into him rather than let it loose upon the world, and had spent the rest of her life trying to undo the arithmetic of that choice.

"Ysolde," he said, testing the name. "She's alive?"

August's face did something complicated. "We'll see," she said. "There's someone coming to Candlemere. Someone old. We'll see."

* * *

They gave him a room in the monastery above — not a cell, they were careful about that, not a cell, though every room in Candlemere had once been a cell of one kind or another — and Aldous sat on the edge of a narrow bed in borrowed clothes and discovered that he did not know how to be alone.

The silence kept rushing in. Every time it did, he flinched, waiting for the Regent to fill it, waiting for the hum, the hunger, the cold intelligence threading through his thoughts like wire through wax. It did not come. It was not going to come. August had paid — he learned this from Tam, the singer, who brought him broth and stayed to talk because the singer could not bear silence either — August had paid with her Gift entire, the whole of her power, freely chosen, and the rite had taken it and unraveled the binding and cast the Regent out into whatever darkness waits for things that are cast out.

"She can't do magic anymore," Tam said, watching him over the steam of the broth. "None. She says it's like losing a limb she'd grown used to. She says—" the singer smiled, small and sad, "—she says it's worth it. She says it like she means it, which is the worst part, because then you can't argue."

"And you believe her?"

"I believe August," Tam said. "That's different, and better."

When the singer left, Aldous sat with the cooling bowl in his hands and tried to pray, out of habit, and found he had no one to pray to, and found that this too was a kind of freedom, and wept again, briefly, quietly, like a man learning to bleed without an audience.

* * *

On the third day, they brought him the lists.

Brannoc brought them — the big man with the grief-carved face who had voted for mercy and hated himself for it, who looked at Aldous the way a man looks at a fire that killed his family and is nonetheless being asked to warm his hands. He dropped the ledgers on the table between them without ceremony.

"You wanted to know who you harmed," Brannoc said. "Here. Thirty years of crown records. Every purge order signed with your hand. Every village burned. Every bearer taken. The Regent used your seal, your voice, your face — but the ink is yours, Majesty, and the world doesn't care whose will moved the pen. Read them. All of them. That's not punishment. That's just arithmetic."

"How many?" Aldous asked.

Brannoc's jaw worked. "We're still counting."

Aldous read. He read for hours, by candlelight, while the Choir came and went around him — the twins, Wren and Lark, who watched him with open hostility and undisguised curiosity in equal measure; Sef's empty chair, which no one moved and no one sat in; Maren Vale, the former Inquisitor, who had burned her commission and now sat in corners sharpening pens instead of knives, drafting the architecture of a kingdom that might not need hunters. He read until his eyes blurred, and then he read more, and when he came to the record of Millbrook Cross — the first purge, the neighbor dragged from her door, the edict nailed to every lintel calling the Gift a plague — he closed the book and put his head down on the table among the dead people's names and stayed there a long time.

August found him like that.

She came in quietly — she moved quietly now, he noticed, as if listening for a power that wasn't there anymore — and stood across the table from him, and waited until he raised his head.

"I keep trying to find the line," he said. His voice was steadier now, day by day, the rust wearing off it. "The line between what the Regent did and what I allowed. There isn't one. That's the truth of it. I was weak, and it was strong, and everything after is written in my name."

"The line doesn't matter," August said. "What matters is what you do standing on this side of it."

"And what's that? What does a man do, August Blackwood, when the kingdom he ruined learns his face was a mask? When they learn the tyrant was a prisoner? Do they forgive? Should they?"

"No," she said, without hesitation. "Forgiveness isn't owed. It isn't mine to give and it isn't yours to take. But—" she pulled out the chair opposite him, the chair that was not Sef's chair, and sat, and leaned forward, and her eyes held his with a ferocity that had nothing of magic in it and needed none, "—there's a difference between forgiveness and use. Nobody has to forgive you. But you're alive, and you know where all the bodies are buried, because you signed the orders. You know the vaults, the codes, the names of every Inquisitor still loyal to the old regime. You're the most dangerous man in the kingdom to the people who'd put the Regent's heirs on the throne — if there are heirs — and the most useful man alive to the people trying to build something better. If you choose to be."

"Or you could kill me," Aldous said. "It would be simpler."

"It would," August agreed. "Brannoc argued for it. Twice. He lost the vote twice, and the second time he voted against himself, and I've never seen a man pay so much for a single act of mercy. Don't waste it. That's all I ask. Don't you dare waste it."

Aldous looked at her — this young woman who had walked into his prison carrying her own sacrifice like a lantern, who had lost her Gift and her mentor and her friend and her home and had come out the other side still standing, still choosing, still *asking* things of him as if he were capable of answering — and he did the thing he had not done since he was a crowned boy of twenty-six.

He knelt.

Not to her — he made that clear, rising off the chair and going down onto the cold flags with his fists braced against the floor, his head bowed, his voice rough. "Not to you. To them. To Sef, whose chair that is. To the ones in the ledgers. To everyone who suffered while I slept in my own skull. I can't undo it. I can't even properly mourn it yet — mourning takes a self, and mine is thirty years old and doesn't fit. But I swear this, here, where the binding was cut: I will spend what's left of my life paying the debt. Not begging forgiveness. Paying. There's a difference, and I think you're the one person in this room who knows it."

August was silent. Then, awkwardly — and it was awkward, terribly, unbearably awkward, mercy always is in the moment after it lands, both parties standing in the wreckage wondering what hands are for — she reached out and gripped his forearm, the way smugglers greet each other on mountain roads, the way the Choir greeted new members, the way family does when words fail.

"Get up," she said gruffly. "Kneeling's done. Work starts tomorrow."

* * *

Work started tomorrow.

It started with a council — a real one, not the Regent's theater of nodding chancellors — gathered in the monastery's refectory around a scarred oak table: Brannoc and August and Maren Vale, the twins, Tam, Osk sitting close to the king as if guarding him from himself, and Aldous at the end, in plain wool, no crown, no seal, his hands folded like a student's.

"There will have to be a reckoning," Maren was saying, tapping her papers. "Inquisitors in the field, garrison commanders, the chancellors who profited. Some will claim they were bewitched. Some actually were. Sorting one from the other will take years."

"Then we begin," Aldous said, "by telling the truth. All of it. What I was. What was done to me and what was done in my name. Let the kingdom judge me openly rather than whispering about it forever. If I am to be useful, I must first be *known*."

"That proclamation will tear the country apart," Brannoc growled.

"It may," Aldous agreed. "But lies have torn it apart for thirty years, and look at the seams."

Osk spoke then, barely above a whisper, the boy who had heard the king's dreams for half his life. "There's something else. Something I've been afraid to say." Every head turned. The boy's hands were white-knuckled in his lap. "The Regent spoke to me. Before the end. It... learned things through me. Candlemere. Your route. And it said — it said it wasn't the only one. That it had *siblings*. Old things, older bindings, sleeping under other thrones." He looked up, terrified, at the faces around the table. "I hear them sometimes now. Faint. Like songs through walls. One of them is awake."

The refectory went very quiet. Snow ticked against the shutters. Aldous felt the old terror rise in him — the hum, the hunger, the wire through the wax — and mastered it, because mastery was the only thing he had left to offer.

Then August Blackwood, Gift-less, unbroken, pulled the ledger of the dead toward her and said, "Then we'd better finish counting fast. Because it sounds like we're going to need a bigger choir."

Outside, the snow kept falling on Candlemere, soft and endless, burying the old year and all its horrors — and somewhere far to the east, beneath another throne, something ancient turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to wake.
