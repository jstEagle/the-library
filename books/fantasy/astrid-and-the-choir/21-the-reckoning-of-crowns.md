## 21. The Reckoning of Crowns

**August**

The throne room of Vael Amaran smelled of dust and old incense and, faintly, of the cold that had lived in its foundations for thirty years. August stood at the foot of the dais with snow still melting in her hair, and she could not stop looking at the king.

He was smaller than she had imagined. Smaller than the coins in her pocket, smaller than the stern face stamped on every proclamation that had ever nailed a neighbor to a door. King Aldous sat on the steps below his own throne because he could not bear to sit upon it — Tam had told her that, quietly, on the road back — and his hands were folded in his lap like a man waiting for a confession he already knew by heart.

The great hall was not full. That was the strangest part. After everything — the chapel, the song, Brannoc's body carried out through the frozen dark, the Regent's scream going out of the world like a nail drawn from wood — there were perhaps forty people in the room. Survivors of the palace guard who had thrown down their arms when the sky above the capital had shuddered. Courtiers too frightened or too curious to flee. Maren Vale, standing apart from everyone with her commission burned to ash somewhere behind her. And the Choir: Tam hoarse as a crow, Wren with Lark's arm around her shoulders, Osk pale as candlewax and unable to look at anyone.

And August, who had come down out of the mountains carrying nothing but a smuggler's kit and an absence where her Gift used to be.

"Say it again," Aldous said. His voice was rough, like a door long unopened. "I have been silent for thirty years. I want to hear it said aloud."

August glanced at Maren. Maren gave her the smallest nod — *go on* — and August stepped forward onto the cold marble.

"The thing is gone," she said. "The Regent. Whatever it was. It's dead, or as near to dead as anything gets. You're free."

Aldous closed his eyes. For a moment August thought he might weep again — he seemed to do little else — but instead he bowed his head forward until his forehead nearly touched his folded hands, and held there, breathing, the way Sef used to breathe over the wounded. Counting. Anchoring himself in a body that was finally only his own.

When he looked up, his eyes were wet and terribly clear.

"Then I have work to do," he said, "and thirty years of it."

* * *

**Maren**

Maren Vale had spent eleven years learning how to read a crowd for fear. She knew the way guilt pooled in the corners of a market square; she knew the particular silence of people watching a neighbor be taken away, silence shaped exactly like relief. She had told herself, all those years, that what she did was mercy — that a clean culling was kinder than mob violence, that her gentle voice and kind face made the crown's cruelty survivable.

She had burned her commission in a guardhouse brazier three weeks ago and felt nothing break, which was itself a kind of revelation. What broke instead was everything else, slowly, the way ice breaks on a river in spring: one crack, then another, then the whole surface giving way all at once beneath her.

Now she stood in the throne room of the man whose orders she had executed, listening to him say, "I have work to do," and she thought: *so do I, and mine begins with telling him what was done in his name.*

"Your Majesty." Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. Every head turned. She felt August Blackwood's eyes on her back, and Tam Voss's, and did not let herself flinch. "Before this room decides anything about you or the kingdom, someone must tell you what the last thirty years contained. The purges. The edicts. The lists." She swallowed. "I kept some of those lists. I would rather you heard it from me than from anyone who enjoyed it."

Aldous studied her for a long moment. Up close, without the Regent behind them, his eyes were ordinary eyes — tired, lined, human. He looked, Maren thought, like every father she had ever watched beg for a Gifted child's life and been forced to refuse.

"You are the Inquisitor Vale," he said. "The one they sang about in the chapel corridors. The hunter with the kind face."

"Yes."

"They said you let a smuggler go in Ash Wednesday Market."

"I did." Maren lifted her chin. "It was the first honest thing I did in eleven years."

Something moved across the king's face that was almost — almost — a smile. It looked unfamiliar on him, like a coat cut for another man.

"Then tell me," Aldous said. "All of it. Do not spare me. I have been spared enough for one lifetime."

So Maren told him. She told him about the first purge, the winter the edicts went up on every door from Millbrook to the coast. About the villages burned on suspicion alone. About the children taken to the Inquisition's houses and never written about again. She spoke for the better part of an hour, and the throne room stayed so quiet she could hear the snow ticking against the high windows, and when she finished, King Aldous rose from the steps of his own dais and walked down to stand before her, and the whole hall tensed —

— and the King of Vaelmar knelt.

Not to her, she understood at once. Not to any of them. He knelt on the cold marble in front of the assembled survivors of his own reign, and he pressed both palms flat to the floor, and he said, in a voice that cracked down the middle:

"I cannot undo this. I know that no apology can weigh against it. But I will spend whatever years remain to me trying, and I will begin by giving back everything my captivity took." He raised his head. "Starting today, I abdicate."

The word went through the hall like a stone through still water.

"There will be no more kings of Vaelmar wearing this crown alone," Aldous said. "There will be a council. Chosen from every province, every trade, every faith — and from the Gifted, who are not a plague and never were. They will hold the regency of this realm together, and I will serve them if they'll have me, as a clerk, as a witness, as a penitent. Whatever they ask." He looked around the hall, and his gaze found August, and Tam, and Osk, and lingered there. "Whatever they ask."

* * *

**August**

They argued for six hours.

August had expected it — had, in fact, counted on it, because Ysolde had taught her once that any decision reached too easily was a decision no one had examined — but the shape of the argument surprised her. It was not Gifted against ungifted. It was not even north against south. It was the old question, the one that had split the Choir at Candlemere, wearing new clothes: *what do you do with a wound this deep — cut it open, or let it scar?*

The courtiers wanted order. Of course they did; order was the air they breathed. Some wanted a regency with Aldous as figurehead, a king restored but leashed. The soldiers wanted a strong hand, preferably someone with a name they recognized. And a handful — fewer than August expected, but more than zero — wanted vengeance dressed as justice: trials, executions, the Inquisition's houses opened and everyone inside them answerable for what had happened there.

It was Tam, voice worn down to gravel, who cut through it.

"You want to burn the Inquisition's houses?" Tam rasped. "Fine. Burn them. But then what grows in the ashes? Fear grows in ashes. We've had thirty years of fear. I'd like to try something else."

"And what's that?" snapped Lord Casserly, a hawk-faced man whose estates bordered Thornfell and who had, August happened to know, sold grain to the crown armies at generous rates.

"A council," Tam said. "Like he said. Real seats, real votes. Farmers and smiths and singers. And yes — us. The ones you hunted. Not as a favor. Because we're the only people in this kingdom who know what the crown looks like from underneath."

Silence. Then Wren, of all people, stepped forward — Wren, who had not spoken above a whisper since the bridge, since Sef.

"My sister died covering our retreat," Wren said, and her voice did not shake, which somehow made it worse. "Sef. A healer. She healed crown soldiers once, before the edicts, and she never stopped believing some of them were decent. If she were here, she'd vote for the council. So I'm voting for her." She looked at Casserly without blinking. "That's not mercy. That's arithmetic. Mercy would be burning your house down. I'm choosing not to."

August felt something loosen in her chest that had been tight since the frozen chapel. *Justice without vengeance,* she thought. *Sef's arithmetic.* It wasn't forgiveness — Wren's eyes said forgiveness was a country she hadn't emigrated to yet — but it was a door, propped open, with light coming through.

The vote, when it finally came, was not close. It was not unanimous either, and August found she preferred it that way. Unanimity smelled like the Regent.

* * *

**Maren**

They asked her to speak for the Gifted on the council.

Maren had imagined many ends to her career — a blade, a pyre, a trial of her own — and none of them had been this: standing in a drafty council chamber three days later, before twelve faces, being asked to advocate for the very people she had spent a decade hunting.

"I'm not worthy of it," she said plainly. "You should know that before you decide. There are families in this city who will spit when they hear my name. There should be."

An old woman among the twelve — a weaver from the river districts, Maren learned later, whose granddaughter had been taken in the second purge — leaned forward. "We know who you are, Inquisitor Vale. We also know you burned your commission and walked into the mountains to find the people you'd been hunting. Why?"

Maren considered lying. She had always been good at lies that sounded like kindness. But the room was full of people who had survived thirty years of institutional cruelty precisely by smelling that kind of lie at a hundred paces.

"Because I saw what my compassion was feeding," she said. "I told myself gentleness made the cullings humane. It only made them efficient. When I finally understood that, I couldn't un-know it. So I came looking for the truth of the king, because if he could be saved, then maybe the whole rotten logic of the purges — that the Gift is a plague, that bearers are beyond saving — was wrong at the root. And it was wrong. I helped prove it was wrong." She spread her hands. "I don't ask you to forgive me. I ask you to use me. I know how the Inquisition thinks. I know where the lists are buried, which officers will resist a council and which can be won over, and which of the taken children might still be alive in the records I kept. Give me the work and I will do it until I die, and I will never ask to be loved for it."

The weaver woman looked around the table. One by one, the others nodded — slowly, some grudgingly, one or two with tears running freely down their faces.

"Then it's settled," the old woman said. "First Advocate for the Gifted. Gods help you, girl. The work will hate you before it loves you."

"I know," Maren said. "I'm counting on it."

* * *

**August**

Osk found her on the palace roof that evening, where she'd gone to watch the sun go down over a city she had entered as a fugitive and would leave as something she didn't yet have a name for.

He sat down beside her without asking, the way the twins did, the way the whole Choir had learned to do with each other over one hard winter. For a while neither of them spoke. Below, the city was lighting its lamps, and somewhere a bell was ringing — not an alarm, just the evening bell, ordinary as bread.

"They voted to forgive me," Osk said at last. "At Candlemere, before we left. Brannoc—" His voice caught on Brannoc's name; it caught on it for all of them now, a stone in the throat of every sentence. "Brannoc voted last. He said—" Osk stopped, started again. "He said shame is a weapon the enemy forges for you, and the only way to break it is to hand it back to the people you trust and let them hold it a while."

"He'd know," August said softly. "He carried his a long way."

They were quiet again. The lamps multiplied below, street by street, until the whole bowl of the city glimmered like a hearth seen from a mountain pass.

"I'm staying," Osk said. "Here. With the council work. They need someone who can—" He gestured vaguely at his own head, at the sense that had been a curse and was becoming, piece by piece, a profession. "Listen. The records of the taken children, the ones Maren kept — someone has to read them properly. Someone who can hear what's underneath the words. And the Choir voted to keep Candlemere going. As a school, eventually. A refuge. Someone has to be the bridge between here and there."

August looked at him. Sixteen years old, hollow-eyed, carrying more than any child should — and sitting straighter than he had all winter.

"You don't owe them that," she said. "Any of them. Nobody here thinks you do."

"I know." Osk smiled, small and crooked, the first real smile she'd seen on him since the chapel. "That's why I get to choose it. That's the whole point, isn't it? The Regent took everyone's choices. Giving them back is the job now." He bumped her shoulder with his, awkward and warm. "Besides. Somebody has to teach the new ones. And I hear the best teacher at Candlemere lost her magic and got better anyway."

August laughed — really laughed, for the first time since the frozen chapel, and the sound startled a rook off the eaves.

"Flatterer," she said. "Tam taught you that line."

"Tam taught me the harmony. The line's mine."

Below them the evening bell finished ringing, and the city went on with its night, and far to the north, under a ruined monastery, a door stood open in the dark, waiting.

* * *

**Maren**

On the morning the council was formally sworn, Maren stood at the back of the hall and watched August Blackwood decline a seat.

It caused a small uproar. Lord Casserly had argued for it — *the woman who freed the king, a living symbol* — and half the chamber had agreed, and August had stood in front of them all in her patched smuggler's coat and said, "Symbols freeze. Teachers move around. Ask me again when Candlemere doesn't need me," and sat back down to scattered, bewildered applause.

Afterward, in the colonnade, Maren caught up with her.

"You could have had anything," Maren said. "Power. Title. A pardon signed in gold ink."

"I had a family waiting in the mountains." August shrugged. "Turns out that was the anything."

They walked a while in the thin spring sunlight. Maren realized she was hesitating over words, which was unlike her, and finally understood why: she was afraid of the answer.

"The records," she said. "The taken children. Some of the entries are marked with a sigil I've never been able to trace. An old binding-style. Loops within loops. Your mentor's, I think — Ysolde's. Which means some of those children may have been hidden, not destroyed. Hidden well enough that only someone who knew her hand could find them."

August had stopped walking. The color had gone out of her face and come back again, twice, in the space of a breath.

"How many?" she asked.

Maren had the number ready; she had checked it three times that morning, unable to sleep.

"Forty-one," she said. "And August — the oldest entry is dated the same year your mentor disappeared."

Far to the north, under a mountain, a door stood open. August Blackwood looked south toward Candlemere, and north toward the road, and Maren watched her understand that the reckoning of crowns was finished — and hers, at last, was only beginning.
