## 22. August and the Choir

The thaw came to Candlemere the way forgiveness came — slowly, and then all at once.

August stood in the monastery's upper yard, where the snow had rotted into gray slush and the first green shoots were pushing up through the cracks in the flagstones like prisoners testing a door. The ruined arches of the old cloister caught the morning light and threw it back gold. Somewhere below, muffled by three floors of stone, Tam was singing — one of the work-songs the Choir had invented over the winter, half hymn and half complaint, about how Sef had hidden the good needles again and if Wren had taken them there would be consequences.

August smiled at that. She smiled a great deal these days, she had noticed. It was an odd thing to notice about oneself, like discovering a new freckle. For years her face had been a smuggler's face — watchful, economical, giving nothing away. Now it leaked all over the place.

She turned her hands over in the pale light. They were just hands. Callused from rope-work, scarred across the left palm where a crown soldier's knife had caught her in the pass two winters ago, ordinary as bread. She had spent the first weeks after the chapel staring at them, waiting for the Gift to come back the way a man who has lost a tooth keeps probing the gap. It did not come back. It was not coming back. The unbinding had taken it entire, as Ysolde's letters had warned in that cramped, furious hand — *the rite drinks the caster, girl, it drinks the caster down to the root* — and August had known, and had paid anyway, and would pay it again.

What surprised her was how little she missed it.

Not at first, of course. At first it had been like going deaf — reaching constantly for a world of sound that was no longer there. She had felt the silence of the Gift everywhere: in the hearth-fire that was only fire, in the mountain stone that was only stone, in the space between people that was now, truly, empty. She had grieved it privately, in the hour before dawn, so no one would see.

But grief, she had learned, was a thing you walked through, not a thing you camped in. Ysolde had taught her that too, though she'd taught it by vanishing rather than by saying it.

"August." Lark's voice, from the cloister stair. "You're wanted. Osk's got the new ones up on the wall and he's teaching them the breathing wrong again."

"I taught him the breathing."

"Yes, and it's wrong, and he's teaching it wronger." Lark grinned. She and Wren had filled out over the winter, the twins, the hunted sharpness in their faces softening into something that was almost — August searched for the word — almost carefree. Not quite. None of them would ever be quite that. But closer than they had any right to be.

"Coming," August said, and followed her down.

* * *

The new ones had come with the thaw, as the new ones always would now.

That was the other thing the spring had brought. Word traveled, the way water traveled, finding every crack. *There is a place under Candlemere. There is a place where they don't cull you. There is a place where a woman with no power at all will teach you what you are.* They came down out of the passes in ones and twos — a miller's daughter whose hands made flour rise without yeast, a drover's boy who dreamed other people's cattle home, a grey-faced old soldier who had hidden his Gift through three purges and wept the first time anyone said the word *magic* to him without spitting.

Twelve of them now, besides the Choir proper. August kept the ledger herself, in a smuggler's hand, in a smuggler's book: names, gifts, fears, what they'd left behind. She had learned from Brannoc that survival was mostly bookkeeping.

Osk stood on the wall-walk with five of the new ones arranged in a crooked line, all of them squinting against the wind, all of them holding their hands out flat the way he'd been shown. He was sixteen now, or thereabouts; he'd stopped counting, he said, because the Regent had stolen the years and he didn't want them back. He had filled out too. The shadows under his eyes were still there — some mornings they were bad, when the dreams came — but the flinch was gone. The flinch of a boy who had carried a god's whispers alone, who had betrayed his family's hiding place and confessed it before it could kill them, who had been forgiven so thoroughly it had nearly broken him worse than the betrayal.

He was teaching them wrong, as Lark had said. He was telling them to push the breath down into their bellies and hold it like a stone, when what August had taught him — what Ysolde had taught August, in another life, in a kitchen that smelled of rosemary and burnt sugar — was to let the breath fall, not push. To make a cradle of it, not a fist.

August watched for a moment. She could have corrected him from the wall's foot, called up, saved herself the climb.

She climbed.

"Right," she said, when she reached them, and five frightened faces turned to her with the particular hunger of the newly hunted — the look of people who had been told all their lives that what they were was a plague, and had come here on a rumor and a prayer to find out if it was a lie. "Everyone breathe out. All the way out. Until you're empty."

They breathed. The wind came off the mountain smelling of wet stone and, faintly, impossibly, of some early flower that had no business blooming this high yet.

"Now don't pull the next breath," August said. "Let it fall into you. Like rain into a dry field. You're not taking. You're being given to."

She watched Osk's face change as he heard it — watched the correction land, watched him understand not just the words but the why of them, the difference between a fist and a cradle. He adjusted. The miller's daughter adjusted. The old soldier's hands, held out flat in the wind, began very slightly to tremble, the way hands trembled when something long-clenched finally let go.

"There," August said quietly. "That's the whole secret, really. Everything else is bookkeeping."

She stayed on the wall until the lesson ran itself, and then went down to find Brannoc, because there was always something to find Brannoc about.

* * *

Brannoc was in the refectory, arguing with a map.

He had aged, that winter. Everyone said so behind his back, and no one said it to his face, because Brannoc would have made them do breathing exercises until they apologized. The lines around his eyes had deepened into something geological. But his hands were steady on the map of the southern roads, and his voice, when he looked up at August, was the voice of a man who had cast a deciding vote for mercy with his family's graves still fresh, and had never once asked to be forgiven for the years it had taken him to get there.

"The council's envoy comes up the pass in a week," he said. "Maren's letter says they want to formalize the sanctuary. Charters, boundaries, the whole rigmarole." His mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "A monastery for magicians, with a government charter. Ysolde would have laughed herself sick."

"Ysolde would have read the charter twice and found the loophole."

"Ysolde," Brannoc said, and stopped.

The silence between them was an old one now, worn smooth like a stair-stone. They did not speak of Ysolde often. There had been nothing to speak of — a woman fled into the mountains thirty years ago, mad or dying or both, and no word since. August had written letters and sent them by every trader and pilgrim she could find, addressed to nothing, to everywhere: *Ysolde. Candlemere. Come home.* It was the kind of thing you did knowing it was useless, the way you planted a tree knowing you might not sit in its shade.

"The charter's good," August said. "Maren won't bring us a bad one. She's earned that trust."

"She has." Brannoc rolled the map up with the brisk violence of a man who did not like being caught feeling things. "The twins want to go down for the envoy. Tam's writing a new song for it. Osk—"

"Osk wants to stand on the wall and breathe correctly."

Brannoc huffed. It was as close to a laugh as he came before noon. Then his face went still, and he said, in the careful voice he used perhaps twice a year, "You know this place doesn't need you to be its mother, August. It needs you to be its teacher. Those are different jobs. One of them will eat you alive."

August looked at him. The fire cracked in the great hearth, ordinary fire, only fire, warming the room because someone had laid it and tended it and that was enough.

"I know," she said. And then, because he had earned the truth: "I'm learning the difference. Slowly."

"Good." He turned back to the map, and then, without looking up: "The stew needs stirring. Sef's herbs are in the crock by the—"

"I know where Sef keeps her herbs, Brannoc. I've only been doing this for a year."

"Doing what?"

"Running your household."

"I run this household."

"Of course you do," August said, and went to stir the stew.

* * *

She thought about power a great deal, that spring. What it was. Where it lived.

She had believed, once — Ysolde's teaching again, or Ysolde's bitterness, it was hard to separate the two — that the Gift was the only real thing about a magician, and everything else was scaffolding around it. Take away the Gift and what remained was a husk. A person-shaped absence.

But she had watched Tam sing the rite's song in the frozen chapel with her voice tearing and her fear like a live thing in her throat, and Tam had no Gift at all — none, never had, that was the whole point of the Choir's singers, that harmony was a magic anyone could make if they were stubborn enough and loved each other enough. She had watched Brannoc hold a door against the Regent's terror with nothing but his body and his hatred and his terrible, costly choice. She had watched Osk, Gifted past all bearing, nearly destroyed by the simple fact that no one had ever taught him what his power was *for* — and saved, in the end, not by magic but by confession. By telling the truth to someone who stayed.

Power lived in the connections. That was what the Choir had known all along, in their ragged, collaborative, infuriating way. The Gift was just one thread. Pull it out and the weave held.

So she taught. Not magic — she couldn't, and it didn't matter. She taught the breathing, and the ledger-keeping, and the history the crown had burned. She taught the new ones how to hide and how to stop hiding, and when each was right. She taught the miller's daughter that her gift for rising was not a plague but a bread. She sat with the old soldier through the nights when three buried purges came up out of him like floodwater, and she held his hands — just hands, ordinary hands — until the shaking stopped.

And she gathered them. That was the word Brannoc had used, half-accusing, and he had been right, and she had stopped apologizing for it. Someone had to be the door. Someone had to stand in the gateway of this broken, holy place and open it, over and over, every time the wind brought another frightened soul down the pass. It was not a power. It was a practice. It was, she suspected, the only practice that had ever mattered.

On the evening the envoy's banners were first sighted from the wall — a week early, the roads must have been clear — August was in the yard teaching the drover's boy to fall without hurting himself, which was the first and last thing any hedge-witch needed to know. She heard the horn, and the shouting, and she sent the boy up to the wall to watch, and she stood alone in the slanting gold light with her empty, ordinary hands at her sides.

And that was when she saw, far down the switchback trail, a single figure coming up on foot. Not with the envoy. Ahead of it, or apart from it. Small, bent, moving with the lurching, halting gait of someone who had walked a very long way on legs that had forgotten how.

August's heart did something complicated in her chest.

She knew the walk. She had watched it recede down a village lane eleven years ago, and had hated it, and had grieved it, and had spent a year deciphering letters that explained it and forgave it and could not bring it back. She knew the shawl, or what was left of it — a rag of grey wool that might once have been the one Ysolde wore to market. She knew the way the figure stopped every ten steps to look at the mountain, mouth open, as though the mountain were speaking.

Half-mad. The traders' rumors had said half-mad, when they said anything at all. Old, and half-mad, and alive.

August walked down through the yard. Past the arches where the early flowers were blooming out of season and out of place, because Candlemere had always been a place where the impossible put down roots. Through the gate that Brannoc had rebuilt and the twins had reinforced and Osk had, in a moment of uncharacteristic whimsy, hung with bells. Down the switchback trail, her boots loud on the stone, the wind at her back pushing her forward as though the mountain itself had opinions.

Ysolde saw her when they were twenty paces apart. Stopped. Squinted.

The years had carved her down to wire and weather. Her hair was white as the chapel frost. Her eyes — those fierce, impatient, brilliant eyes that August had both loved and fled — were clouded, wandering, fixed on something over August's shoulder that was not there. She was muttering. August caught fragments: *the root, the root drinks, girl, I told him, I told them all* — the old lesson, the old guilt, worn smooth by thirty years of repetition into a rosary.

August's hands ached to reach out. She kept them at her sides. You did not grab a wild thing, even one you loved. Even one you had spent eleven years learning to forgive.

"Ysolde," she said.

The old woman's muttering stopped. Her clouded eyes dragged themselves, slowly, with visible effort, back from whatever distance they had been gazing across, and found August's face. And something moved in them — not recognition, not yet, but the *approach* of recognition, like a ship sighting land through fog.

"You," Ysolde said. Her voice was a ruin of the voice August remembered, cracked and sanded down. "You're the one who's been writing. The letters. All the letters." She frowned, and her head tilted, birdlike. "I burned them, at first. Then I read them. Then I burned them again. Then I—" She stopped. Her hand came up, trembling, and hovered near August's cheek without touching, the way you'd test a fire. "You paid it," she whispered. "You paid the whole price. You foolish, brave, *stupid* girl. You paid it and it worked."

"It worked," August said.

"And you stayed here." Ysolde's eyes went past her, up the trail, to the ruined arches gold in the evening light, to the bells on the gate, to the thin thread of Tam's song rising from somewhere below. "You stayed, with no power, with nothing, and you—" The old woman's face did something August had never seen it do in all the years of her hard, solitary, secretive teaching. It crumpled. "You built the thing I couldn't. You built the *choir*."

"You built the song," August said. "We only learned to sing it together."

For a long moment neither of them moved. The wind came down the pass, smelling of stone and snowmelt and that impossible early flower. Below them, faintly, the envoy's horns sounded on the far side of the mountain, and neither of them turned to look.

Then Ysolde's legs — old, half-mad, eleven hundred miles tired — simply quit, the way old legs do, all at once and without negotiation. And August was there, arms open, catching her, taking her whole weight without magic, without anything but muscle and will and the fierce, unremarkable strength of a body that had decided, a long time ago, what it was for.

She did not say *welcome*. She did not say *I forgive you*. She did not say any of the ten thousand things that had waited eleven years to be said, and would keep, and would be said in their own time, over stew, over years, over the long slow work of two women learning each other again.

She just held the door open — held it the way she had learned to hold it, with her whole self — and walked her mentor home.

Behind them, up the switchback, the first bells of the envoy began to ring. And ahead, in the gate of Candlemere, the whole impossible family of them had gathered — Brannoc with his steady hands, Tam with her ruined-and-remade voice, the twins shoulder to shoulder, Osk breathing correctly, all the new ones crowded behind — and every single one of them was watching August come up the trail with the old woman in her arms, and every single one of them stepped aside to let her through.

The door, as always, was open. August carried Ysolde across the threshold, into the gold light and the smell of stew and the sound of a song that had been waiting thirty years for all its voices — and the bells rang on, and the mountain stood witness, and spring, having come at last to Candlemere, showed no sign at all of leaving.
