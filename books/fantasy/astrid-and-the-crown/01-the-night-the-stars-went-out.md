## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

I was carving a memorial star for a dead shepherd when the new voice arrived.

That's not as strange as it sounds. I carve memorials for a living — or that's what the people of Thornfell valley believe, anyway. They bring me their grief in the form of knucklebones and rib bones and the occasional whole skull, and I render it into something small enough to hold: a bird, a boat, a star. What they don't know is that when my knife touches bone, I hear the person who owned it. Not echoes. Not memories. Voices. Whole, chattering, opinionated voices of the dead, lodged in the marrow like flies in amber.

I've heard them since I was six years old, when I picked up my grandmother's finger bone off the hearthstone and she told me, in no uncertain terms, to put it back where I found it. I have kept the gift secret for thirty years since, because in Thornfell, a man who hears the dead is either a saint or a witch, and saints get pilgrims and witches get rope. I prefer neither. I prefer tea, solitude, and a steady commission of grieving widows who pay in cheese.

So on the night everything ended, I was doing what I always did: listening to old Tomas the shepherd complain about his wife's cooking while I shaped his femur into a seven-pointed star, because he'd asked for one before he died and dead men are surprisingly persistent about their last requests.

*Make it eight points,* said Tomas.

"Seven," I muttered around my knife. "Seven's traditional."

*Eight. I counted sheep by eights. Drove Marta mad.*

"You drove everyone mad. It's seven."

And then, underneath Tomas's voice, under the low murmur of the forty-odd other souls I carry in my workshop — the drawer of relics I've never been able to fully quiet, a chorus I've learned to sleep through the way you sleep through a river — came a voice I had never heard before.

It was young. That was the first thing. Young and frightened and *new*, which should have been impossible, because every bone in my workshop had been there for months at least, and I knew them all like neighbors.

*Please,* it said. *Please, someone. I need passage. I need out. Please.*

I set down my knife.

Now, I want to be clear about something: I am not a brave man. I want that on the record, carved somewhere, dated. When the plague came through when I was nineteen, I stayed in my workshop and carved little protective charms for the families who asked and did not once go down to help bury anyone. When the tax collectors ride through, I suddenly remember urgent carving business. My mother used to say I had a talent for being elsewhere, and she meant it as an insult, and she was right.

But that voice was wrong in a way I couldn't ignore. The dead in my keeping sound like the dead — like things that have finished, that have settled, that are only waiting to be let go or carried on. This voice sounded like a living person somehow folded up inside death's pocket. Like someone shouting from inside a locked chest.

*Where are you?* I asked. Yes, I talk back to them. Out loud, even, when I'm alone, which is most of the time. You'd talk to yourself too, if your self were this crowded.

Silence from the new voice. Then, fainter, as if it were moving away from me at speed: *The stones. The border stones. He'll find me if— please, carver, if you can hear me—*

And that was when every star above Thornfell went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded over. *Out*, all at once, like a hand snuffing a hundred candles in a single sweep. I was standing at my window with a cup of gone-cold tea, and I watched the sky above the peaks turn from its ordinary salt-and-pepper scatter to a flat, absolute black, and I felt the silence of it in my teeth.

You don't notice, most nights, how much light the stars give. Then they're gone and the mountains aren't mountains anymore, they're just absence shaped like mountains, and the snowfields that usually glow faintly blue went dark as wet slate.

Behind me, in the drawer, the dead went quiet too. All of them. Even Tomas. Forty voices, silenced mid-complaint, and the silence they left was worse than any noise, because I realized with a lurch that they weren't gone — I could still feel them, pressed against the inside of my skull like faces against a window — but they were *listening*. All of them. Listening to the same thing I was about to hear.

The wards broke.

I felt it before I heard it, a high thin note through the soles of my feet, up through the floorboards, into the bones of my ankles — and I mean that literally; my bones *sang*, which has happened exactly twice in my life, both times before earthquakes. Then came the sound itself, rolling down from the passes: a crack like the sky splitting along a seam, followed by a long descending shimmer, glass raining down a staircase made of glass, going on and on and on.

Every child in Thornfell learns what the border wards are before they learn their letters. Old magic, older than the kingdom, strung across the mountain passes like a net of light — invisible on ordinary days, but every midwinter the priests bless them and you can see them for one night, a faint silver webbing hung between peak and peak, holding out everything on the other side. Holding in everything on this side, too, though nobody likes to say what. The wards keep the dark things dark. The wards keep the old hatreds buried with the old kings. The wards are why we sleep well.

They shattered like a dropped wineglass, and I stood in my workshop listening to the pieces fall for what felt like an hour.

Then the screaming started, down in the village. Small and human and very far away, and getting closer, because doors were opening, lamps were lighting, and soon the whole valley would be awake and asking questions, and the first question would be *who did this*, and the second would be *who isn't here to answer it*.

I am, as established, not a brave man. But I have spent thirty years learning exactly one skill: knowing when a thing is about to become my problem.

This was already my problem. Because of the voice. Because of what it had said. *The border stones.* Whatever had broken the wards hadn't broken them from outside. Something had been *inside* the stones — anchored there, hidden there, begging for passage out — and now it was out, or loose, or running, and the dead in my drawer were all whispering the same word over and over like a tide coming in:

*Anchor. Anchor. Anchor. Anchor.*

"Tomas," I said. My voice cracked on it. "Tomas, what's an anchor?"

He surfaced slowly, reluctantly, the way he'd done everything in life. *The wards need a heart, carver. Everyone knows that. Stone and starlight and a beating soul to tie it to. Why do you think the old kings—*

He stopped. Dead men can stop themselves, when they decide a truth is too heavy to hand over.

"Why do you think the old kings *what*?"

*Ask her,* said a different voice, one of the older ones, a woman who'd been a ferry-keeper two hundred years ago and never lost her habit of knowing more than she said. *When you find her. And you will find her, boy. She's already calling to you.*

"I don't—" I started.

The sky answered instead. Below the blacked-out stars, near the eastern pass where the wards had sung loudest, a single point of light flared — not a star, nothing so calm — a white-hot streak falling out of the darkness like a spark off a grindstone, trailing embers, dropping fast toward the high country north of the valley.

Toward the smuggler's road.

Toward, if I knew my geography, and I did, because smugglers' roads are the only geography worth knowing in Thornfell — the old mine trails, the ones that cross the border without touching a checkpoint.

Something fell out of the broken sky onto the road that people use to leave the country without being seen leaving it.

I'm not a brave man. Let me also say I'm not a fool, or at least I wasn't, right up until that moment. Because here is what a sensible man does: he bars his door, he banks his fire, he waits for morning and the king's soldiers and the priests with their censers, and he lets the crown handle its own catastrophes. A sensible man does not pack.

I packed.

I don't entirely know why. I've had years since to think about it, and the honest answer is that the voice was still in my head — fainter now, miles away, but there, a thread of *please, please, please* pulled taut through the dark — and that I have carried the dead for thirty years without once being able to help a single one of them, and some rotten splinter of me heard a voice that might still be alive and thought: *not this one. Not this time.*

Also, less nobly: whatever broke the wards was going to be found, and questioned, and probably killed, and if it had been hiding in the border stones then it knew things about the wards that the crown would want, and I did not want to be the man whose bones started singing the answers while royal interrogators were in the room. Self-interest and conscience, walking hand in hand down the mountain. That's usually how it goes.

I took the knives first. All nine of them, wrapped in oilcloth — the long paring knife, the hooked burin, the little saw-toothed thing my mother left me that I've never had the heart to use. Bone-carving tools, yes. Also, in a pinch, weapons, though I'd sooner fight with a spoon. I took the good cloak, the one that doesn't announce *hermit* quite so loudly. I took dried fish, hard bread, a skin of the plum brandy I pretend is for medicinal purposes. I took the drawer.

Yes. The drawer. Forty relics, forty voices, wrapped in wool and loaded into a satchel like a bag of angry cats. Leaving them behind wasn't an option. It has never been an option. When I walk away from carved bone, the voices follow me anyway, thinner and more desperate, like dogs chained to a post watching me go. Better to carry them. They complain about the packing. They're complaining now, actually—

*You forgot the star,* said Tomas. *My star. Eight points.*

"It's seven points and it stays on the bench."

*I died for this valley.*

"You died of a bad heart while cheating at cards. Get in the satchel."

Outside, the night was wrong in every direction. No stars. No moon — and I stood there a moment realizing the moon was gone too, which I hadn't noticed, which tells you how thoroughly the sky had been murdered. The village below was a scatter of lights and noise, bells ringing badly, someone's dog losing its mind. And up the slopes, the snowfields lay black and lightless, and the high pass gaped like a missing tooth.

And the thread of that voice pulled north. Fainter with every minute. *Please. Please. Please.*

I looked at my workshop — twenty years of shavings and half-finished commissions and the chair worn to the shape of me — and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding. If I walked out that door toward the pass, I was walking toward whatever had fallen out of the broken sky, on the worst night in the history of the kingdom, carrying a bag full of dead people, with no plan beyond *north* and *help* and an unexamined conviction that these two things belonged together.

A sensible man banks his fire.

I banked my fire. Then I picked up the satchel, and the dead settled against my back with a weight that had nothing to do with bone, and I stepped out into the starless dark, and my boots crunched on snow that gave back no light at all.

The trail north climbs through pine shadow for two hours before it breaks above the treeline. I did most of those hours in silence, except for the dead arguing about whether I was an idiot — consensus forming, unfortunately — and except for the moments when I stopped to listen to the thread of the voice, checking it like a pulse.

It was weakening. Or it was hiding. Both, maybe. And threaded through the fear of it, when I listened close, was something else — something that didn't fit a fugitive, didn't fit a monster, didn't fit anything I had a shelf for. Underneath the *please, please, please*, so faint I might have invented it, the voice was singing. A few notes, over and over, in a language I didn't know and my bones clearly did, because every time she sang them, my marrow hummed the harmony.

The dead had gone quiet again. All of them. Listening.

"What?" I demanded of the satchel, somewhere on the switchbacks with the valley lights dying below me. "What is it? Somebody say something."

Long silence. Then the ferry-keeper, gentle, which frightened me more than shouting would have:

*Boy. Whatever falls out of that sky tonight — be careful how much of yourself you offer it. Souls like that don't take gifts. They take anchors.*

"I'm not anchoring anything," I said. "I'm delivering a message and coming home."

Nobody laughed. That's how I knew they didn't believe me either.

Near midnight, cresting the ridge above the old mine trails, I stopped, because the thread of the voice had changed. The fear was still in it. But underneath the fear, for the first time, was something worse:

Confusion. A hollow, echoing confusion, like a house with the furniture carried out. And beneath that, small and dazed, words that turned my blood to well water:

*Who am I? Who was singing? Why can't I remember who was singing?*

I stood on the ridge in the starless dark, forty dead souls holding their breath in my satchel, and looked down at the black ribbon of the smuggler's road, where something was lying in the snow that glowed faintly, stubbornly, like an ember refusing to die.

It had a shape. The shape, when I finally dared to look properly, was a girl.

"Well," I said to no one, to everyone, to the empty sky. "There goes the quiet life."

I started down.
