## 2. A Girl Without a Name

The smuggler's road out of Thornfell is called the Shepherd's Path, which tells you everything you need to know about smugglers' honesty. There are no shepherds. There never were. The name was invented by some long-dead fence who understood that a road called the Sheep-Rustler's Shortcut tends to attract the wrong sort of attention, and the wrong sort of attention, in my line of work, tends to arrive wearing a crown's colors.

I had been on the Path for two days when the sky finished its business of going out.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I had left. The stars did not fall. They did not dim. They went out the way a candle goes out when someone pinches the wick — all at once, all of them, in every direction, as if the whole night had been a painted ceiling and someone had finally pulled the cloth away to show the bare black stone behind it.

I had seen the wards shatter two nights before. I had felt them shatter, which is worse, because I feel things in my bones that other people get to feel in their imaginations. It had been like standing next to a cathedral window when it breaks — a sound you don't hear so much as inherit. Then the border beacons had guttered, the mountain passes had gone strangely, terribly open, and every dead soul I carry had started talking at once.

You'd think that would have been the worst night of my life. It wasn't. The worst night was the one I'm telling you about now, because on that night I met her.

* * *

Let me explain something about my bones, since you'll need it and since I'd rather explain it on my own terms than have you hear it from someone who'd use the word *witch*.

Every bone-carver learns the trade the same way. You take the femur of a sheep, say, or the antler of a red deer, and you carve. Sigils, mostly. Little prayers in old Thornic. The bone remembers being alive, and if you cut the right way, it remembers out loud. That's the honest version of the craft, the one they teach apprentices in the lowland towns. It makes charms that keep milk sweet and wardstones that keep wolves honest.

The dishonest version — the version my mother taught me, in a workshop above a mountain pass, with the door barred and the shutters closed — is that bone remembers everything. Not just sheep and deer. People. And not just the dead, either. The dying. The long-gone. The ones nobody mourned and the ones everybody mourned until mourning wore a hole in the world and the dead fell through it, still talking, still wanting, still looking for someone with ears soft enough to hear.

I have forty-one voices in my bones. I counted once, the way a man counts his debts. Some of them are family. Some of them are strangers whose bones I bought at market because I couldn't bear to leave them in a spice barrel. One of them is a highwayman who has been apologizing to me for eleven years for a thing he did to a toll-keeper, and I have stopped telling him the toll-keeper isn't my problem, because the apology is the only thing keeping him together, and I am not so cruel as to take a man's last possession.

The point is: I hear the dead. All the time. Forever. And on the third night after the stars went out, forty-one voices went quiet all at once — which had never happened, not once in my life — and then a forty-second voice said, very clearly, very close:

*She's on the road. She's on the road and she's broken.*

I stopped walking. The Shepherd's Path runs along a shelf of rock above the Vell River, and below me the water was making its usual argument with the stones, and above me there was nothing at all where the sky should have had opinions.

"Who's broken?" I said. I talk to them. I've stopped being embarrassed about it. A man who hears voices and refuses to answer is either a saint or a fool, and I have never once been mistaken for a saint.

*The anchor,* said the voice. It was a woman's voice, old, with a lowland burr I almost recognized. *The anchor of the wards. She's walking toward the ford and she doesn't know what she is.*

"Anchors," I said, "are made of stone and prayer and the sweat of a hundred carvers. They are not *walking*."

*This one is.*

And then, because the dead are never satisfied with being cryptic, all forty-one of the others came back at once, and every one of them said the same word in the same breath, which has also never happened, and which made my knees consider resigning:

*Hers. Hers. Hers. Hers.*

* * *

I found her at the ford.

I want to tell you I crept up on her like a hunter, all shadow and skill. The truth is I slipped on wet rock, swore in a manner my mother would have charged me for, and came around the boulder flailing like a heron in a windstorm, and she still didn't run. That should have told me something. A girl alone on a smuggler's road, in a country that was coming apart at its borders, who doesn't run from a flailing stranger — either she's very brave, or she's got nothing left to run *to*, and I've learned to fear the second thing more than the first.

She was sitting on a flat stone at the water's edge with her boots off and her feet in the river, which was madness, because it was the cold season and the Vell comes down off the icefields. She wasn't shivering. That should have told me something too.

She was maybe twenty. Dark hair, chopped short with a knife by someone in a hurry — herself, I'd have guessed, by the unevenness of it. A wool cloak of good make gone to rags at the hem. And in her lap, held the way a child holds a thing it's been told never to put down, a broken wardstone the size of a fist.

I knew wardstones. I had carved a hundred of them, small ones, for doorways and wells and the little shrines shepherds build where the passes narrow. A wardstone hums. That's the sign it's working — you feel it in your teeth, a faint wrongness, like a struck bell that never finishes ringing. A broken wardstone is silent.

This one was not silent. This one was screaming, quietly, at a pitch only I could hear, and every bone in my body — the living ones and the forty-one sets of dead ones I carry — answered it like a pack of dogs answering a whistle.

*Hers,* said the dead. *Hers. Hers.*

"Evening," I said, because apparently I am constitutionally incapable of approaching a situation like a normal person.

She looked up. Her eyes were — I want to say *wrong*, but that's not fair to her. They were ordinary eyes, grey, tired, red-rimmed. What was wrong was what was behind them. You've seen the look. It's the look of a house where the furniture's all there but someone has carried out the family. She looked at me the way you look at a door when you can't remember whether you locked it.

"Evening," she said. Her voice was rough, like it hadn't been used in days. "Is this — am I still in Thornfell?"

"You're on the Shepherd's Path, a mile above the Vell ford. So yes. Barely."

"Is that north or south?"

"That depends on where you're going."

"I don't know." She said it without drama, without the wobble people put into that sentence when they want you to ask questions. She said it the way you'd say *it's raining*. "I've been walking for — I think two days. Maybe three. I keep expecting to remember where I'm going and I don't. I know what a river is. I know what bread is. I know I should be afraid of the dark right now and I can't remember why, which seems like the kind of thing a person ought to remember." She looked up at the starless sky. "I know the stars are gone. I know that's wrong. I know it's wrong the way you know you've lost something — not what it is, just that there's a shape where it used to be."

I should have walked away.

I want that on the record, wherever records go. I had spent thirty-four years building a life out of smallness — a workshop, a trade, a careful habit of never knowing anything about anybody that could get anybody hanged. And here was a girl on a dark road holding a screaming stone, and every dead soul in my body was chanting *hers* like a congregation that had found its relic, and the smart move, the move that kept me alive for three decades, was to point her south, wish her luck, and never think of her again.

But.

Here is the thing about guilt: it doesn't consult the smart move. Two nights earlier, when the wards shattered, I had been in my workshop, and I had heard — through the stone, through the bones, through whatever highway the dead use — a voice begging. Not the forty-one. A new voice. Young. It had said, *let me through, please, please, I can't stay, please let me through,* and I had sat there with my hands over my ears like a child in a thunderstorm and done nothing, because doing something is how bone-carvers end up in the kind of stories that get carved into other people's warning charms.

And now here was a voice on a road, and it was not begging anymore. It was just empty. And I knew — the way I knew the highwayman's apology by heart — that this was the same voice.

"What's your name?" I asked.

She was quiet a moment. The river argued with the stones. Somewhere far off, something that might have been a wolf or might have been the border coming down made a long low sound.

"I keep reaching for it," she said finally. "It's like — there's a shelf, and I know the shelf is there, and I reach, and my hand goes through. I had a name. I'm sure I had a name. Everyone has a name." She laughed, a small terrible sound. "I've been calling myself *girl*. In my head. *Girl, you should eat something. Girl, you should find a road.* It's very practical. I recommend it."

And that was when I lied to her.

I've turned it over since, a hundred times, and I still don't fully know which part was mercy and which part was cowardice, and I suspect the two had gotten so tangled by then that no one will ever separate them, least of all me. But I looked at her — at this girl with no name, no past, no direction, holding the anchor-stone of a collapsed border in her bare hands, in a kingdom that was about to tear itself apart looking for exactly such a girl — and I thought: if I tell her the truth, the truth will eat her. And if the hunters find her first, the truth will eat her faster.

So I said, "You might be a pilgrim."

She blinked. "A pilgrim."

"There are shrines in the high passes. Old ones. People walk to them when they've lost something. Sometimes what they've lost is themselves." I crouched down, carefully, the way you crouch near a strange dog. "There was a sickness, years back, in some of the valley towns. Fever that took memories. Took names, faces, whole years. Most people got them back. Some walked to the shrines to ask the old gods for help." I shrugged, a masterpiece of casual craft. "You might be one of those. Walking to a shrine."

"Was I?" she said. "Do you know?"

"I don't know you at all," I said, and that much was true, and it was the last fully true thing I'd say to her for a long time. "But I'm headed south, and there's a shrine town on the way. Halvard. If you've got nowhere else to be—"

"I have nowhere," she said, "at all," and she said it like she was checking the words for holes, and finding none.

"Then walk with me as far as Halvard. I'm Cyrus. I'm a carver. Bone and antler, mostly. Charms, keepsakes. Nothing interesting."

She looked at my hands. I don't know what she saw. Carvers' hands are distinctive — scarred, nicked, one thumbnail permanently blackened from a chisel slip years ago. Maybe she saw a craftsman. Maybe she saw something else and was too empty to be afraid of it.

"Astrid," she said suddenly.

I went very still. "That's your name?"

"No." She frowned at the river. "It's — I don't know. It was just there. Like it was sitting on the shelf after all, at the front, the way you'd leave out the one thing you need most." She said it again, testing it. "Astrid. It feels like mine. Or it feels like something that was put in my pocket. I can't tell the difference anymore."

*Hers,* whispered the dead, gently now, the way you'd speak near a sleeper. *Her name. Her true name. Careful, carver. Careful what you carry.*

* * *

We walked south that night and the next day, because the dark was no darker than the day now — the sun still rose, thin and watery, but the stars were gone from the nights, and everyone on the road had the same pinched look of people waiting for the other shoe. Refugees, mostly, streaming down from the passes with carts and goats and grandmothers, all of them telling the same story: the beacons went out, the wards came down, and for the first time in living memory the borders of Thornfell were just — geography. Nothing more.

She was good company, which I hadn't expected. She asked questions the way a child does — not to make conversation, but because she genuinely didn't know what rain was called before she saw it, what a mill was, why the dead brambles along the path had been cut back in patterns. Once she stopped dead in the road and stared at a shrine post — one of the little wayside markers, a deer antler carved with the Thornic sign for *pass safely* — and she said, very quietly, "I know that mark. I don't know how I know that mark. It's in my hands. If you gave me a knife I think my hands would know what to do."

"Lots of people's hands know things their heads have lost," I said. "It's why we carve. The hands remember the prayer even when the mouth forgets."

She looked at me then, a long look, and I got the distinct feeling I was being measured by someone with no ruler of her own, borrowing mine.

"You talk like the stone," she said.

My stomach dropped through the shelf of the world. "The stone?"

"The one I've been carrying." She dug it out of her cloak and held it up, and up close I could see it was no shepherd's charm — it was old work, masterwork, the kind of carving they stopped making two hundred years ago because the last carvers who could do it died without apprentices. And it was humming against my bones like a struck bell that had finally decided, after two hundred years, to finish ringing.

"It hums," she said. "Did you know that? Not with the ears. Somewhere else. And when it hums, I almost — I almost remember something. A door. A very large door. And someone saying *it has to be a living soul, there's no other way, forgive me, forgive me*—" She stopped. Her hand had closed around the stone hard enough to whiten her knuckles. "Do you hear it too?"

Every dead voice in my body had gone silent again. Forty-one souls, holding their breath.

"No," I said.

It was the worst lie I have ever told, and I have told lies to customs men, to captains, to a dying woman about her son. She looked at me, and I watched her decide to believe me — not because she believed me, but because believing me was the only door available, and she was so tired of standing in the dark.

"All right," she said. "Cyrus the carver. Take me to your shrine town."

* * *

That night we camped in a hollow under a leaning rowan, and she slept the sleep of the truly emptied out, and I sat up with my back against the rowan and let the dead talk, because they were going to talk whether I let them or not.

*She's the anchor,* said the lowland woman's voice, the one who'd spoken first. *The border wards of Thornfell were never anchored to stone, carver. Stone wears out. Stone can be unmade. The old kings anchored them to a living soul, a soul given to the wards at birth, bound so deep she'd never feel the chains. And that girl broke the binding, and the wards came down, and the stars — the stars were never stars, carver, they were the wards, seen from underneath—*

"That's not possible," I whispered.

*You carry forty-one dead men in your bones and you're telling me what's not possible.*

I looked at her, asleep by the cold fire with the broken stone in her fist even in sleep, and I thought about the voice that had begged to be let through, two nights ago in my workshop. *Please. I can't stay. Please.*

She hadn't been begging to be let into the wards.

She'd been begging to be let *out*.

And I had sat with my hands over my ears, and the wards had come down, and the stars had gone out, and somewhere in a palace a king or a prince or a thing worse than either was already sending hunters onto the roads with sketches of a girl's face.

*What will you do, carver?* the dead asked, all of them, softly, in the tone of men who already know the answer.

I pulled my cloak tighter and did not answer, because the answer had already happened. It had happened at the ford, when I'd said *walk with me.* It had happened when I lied about the pilgrims and the fever. I had already chosen, the way you choose in a dream, without ever feeling the moment of decision — and now I was simply a man walking south with the most dangerous person in Thornfell, lying to her for her own good, and hoping to every god whose name I'd ever carved that my own good and her own good turned out to be the same direction.

Near dawn, she stirred and spoke without opening her eyes.

"Cyrus. When we get to Halvard — will I be me again?"

I watched the starless sky go grey over the eastern peaks, and I lied one more time, gently, the way you'd set a broken bone.

"Yes," I said. "I think you will."

Below us, far to the south, a bell began to ring in some valley town — alarm, alarm, alarm — and the dead in my bones all turned their faces toward it at once, like a field of flowers toward a sun that wasn't there anymore.
