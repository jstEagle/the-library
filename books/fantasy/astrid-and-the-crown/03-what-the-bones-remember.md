## 3. What the Bones Remember

The man who claimed to hear the dead snored like a landslide.

I lay on the far side of the smuggler's hut's cold hearth, watching his chest rise and fall in the grey hour before dawn, and I thought: *this is my savior. This hollow-cheeked man with sawdust in his hair who talks to ghosts and sleeps like a man with no enemies.* Either the world had gotten much stranger than I remembered — which was a low bar, given that I remembered nothing — or he was lying to me.

I had considered both possibilities for two days now, turning them over like river stones.

Here is what I knew about myself: my name might be Astrid, or it might not; Cyrus had offered it hesitantly, the way you offer a coat to a stranger you're not sure deserves one. Here is what else I knew: I could walk for hours without tiring, I could read a trail of broken twigs like a letter from an old friend, and when a wolf had circled our fire the first night, my body had known exactly what to do before my mind caught up. Whoever I had been, she had been competent. She had also been erased, scrubbed clean as a butcher's block, and someone had gone to considerable trouble to do it.

That last part was the problem. You can lose your keys. You can lose your way in a forest. But memories don't just wander off. Someone had taken mine, deliberately, the way you'd take a knife out of a child's hand.

And the only person who claimed to know anything about it was currently drooling onto his own sleeve.

I sat up. The hut we'd sheltered in belonged to no one anymore — a charcoal burner's place, abandoned so recently there were still cups on the shelf, still a bundle of dried yarrow hanging by the door. Everyone in this valley had fled when the stars went out. I didn't blame them. I had seen the sky go black, seen the border wards shatter into lightless shards, and I had felt something in my chest tear open in sympathy, though I couldn't have said why. It was like hearing a stranger scream and finding blood on your own hands afterward.

Cyrus's pack sat against the wall, half open. His carving knives were wrapped in oilcloth, and beside them, nested like eggs, were the things he made: small figures of bone and antler, hares and birds and one long-coiled serpent, each of them polished smooth as river ice.

He had told me, on the road, in his dry unimpressed voice, that the dead spoke through his bones. That they had been speaking since he was a boy. That one of them had begged him, the night the stars went out, to find me.

*Find her,* he'd said the voice told him. *She carries what fell.*

It sounded like madness. It sounded, worse, like the kind of story a clever smuggler tells a lost girl to make her pliable. And yet.

And yet the broken wardstone I'd been clutching when he found me — the one piece of my former life I owned — hummed whenever he came near. Not metaphorically. It hummed against my palms like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing, and it went quiet when he walked away. Whatever he was, the stone believed him.

I am not, whatever else may be said of me, a patient person. I could feel that about myself the way you can feel a missing tooth with your tongue. So I did the thing I had been itching to do since yesterday evening, when he'd unwrapped his knives to sharpen them and I'd seen the little carved figures gleam.

I took the serpent.

It fit in my palm like it had been made for it. Bone, or antler, worn soft at the edges from handling. He'd said once, offhandedly, that carving quieted the voices — gave them somewhere to sit besides inside him. Which meant every figure in that pack was a house full of dead people, if you believed him.

There was one way to find out.

I pressed my thumb into the serpent's spine and waited to feel foolish.

For three heartbeats, nothing. The hut creaked. Cyrus snored. Somewhere outside, a bird sang the tentative first notes of a dawn it wasn't sure would come — because how do you sing to a sun that rises under a starless sky? Then the humming started, low, felt in the meat of my hand rather than heard, and the world tilted sideways.

Not darkness. Something older than darkness. A room I had never seen, lit by candles whose flames burned perfectly still, as if the air itself were afraid to move. Stone floors. A dais. And a voice — my voice, unmistakably mine, pitched lower than I'd ever heard it, speaking words in a language I did not know and yet understood the way you understand a blow to the face:

*"...and the crown shall pass to the vessel, and the vessel shall kneel, and what is poured out cannot be poured back."*

My own mouth saying it. My own throat shaping syllables that tasted of iron and candle smoke. In the vision-memory-echo — I have no better word for it, and I resent that — I watched my hands, pale and steady, set something down on stone. A knife? A crown? The image slid away like water through fingers, and I was left gripping a bone serpent in a cold hut while my whole body shook.

"Put that down."

Cyrus was awake. I don't know how long he'd been awake; his eyes in the pre-dawn grey were sharp as his knives. He hadn't moved otherwise, which somehow made it worse.

"You said the dead speak through these," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "You failed to mention they speak *through me*."

"They don't." He sat up slowly, the way you move around a wounded animal. "They speak through the carving. To anyone holding it, sometimes. Most people get nothing but a shiver."

"I got a throne room." I heard my own voice crack on the word and hated it. "I got *myself*, Cyrus. My voice, saying words in a language I've never learned, standing in a room I've never seen, and I understood every word." I looked down at the serpent. Its coiled body seemed to watch me. "So either I'm haunted, or—"

"Or the memory isn't gone," he said quietly. "It's *stored*. Somewhere the taking couldn't reach."

The hut was very silent after that. Outside, the bird had stopped singing, as if it too were waiting.

"Tell me what you know," I said. "All of it. Not the road version. The real one."

He rubbed his jaw. Sawdust drifted from his hair. For a moment he looked less like a madman and more like a tired craftsman being asked to explain why his workshop was on fire.

"The night the stars went out, a new voice came to me. They always introduce themselves eventually — the dead are polite that way, in their fashion. This one didn't. It just screamed one thing over and over: *she carries what fell.*" He nodded at the shard of wardstone, which I kept in my shirt now, against my skin. It had gone warm the moment I touched the serpent, and it hadn't cooled. "Then the wards broke, and every star above Thornfell went dark at once, and I did the arithmetic any sensible man does. Wards that old aren't anchored to stones alone. Stones hold the shape. Something living holds the weight."

"And you think that's me."

"I think the stone in your pocket has been humming at me since the moment I found you, and it never hums at anyone, and I've carried it—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "I think you already know what I think."

I did. That was the worst of it. Some part of me, some deep cellar of myself the thieves had missed, had known since the first night. When the sky went black, I hadn't been frightened the way the others were frightened. I'd been *guilty*. Guilt without a crime attached, like a bill arriving for a debt you can't remember owing.

I got up and walked to the window. The valley below was drowned in fog, the kind that swallows roads and makes every tree a gallows. Somewhere out there were hunters — Cyrus had mentioned them once, briefly, the way you mention weather — looking for someone. Looking, possibly, for me.

"Cyrus." I kept my eyes on the fog. "If someone took my memories — all of them, everything, even my own name until you handed it to me like charity — that's not theft. Theft is opportunistic. This took planning. Rituals. Time." My reflection in the warped glass looked like a stranger wearing my face, which, I suppose, was accurate. "Whoever did it wanted me to be nobody. Why would anyone want that?"

He didn't answer right away. When I turned, he was looking at the serpent in my hand with an expression I couldn't read — grief, maybe, or its close cousin, recognition.

"Because somebody is dangerous," he said, "when they remember who they are."

* * *

We left the hut before full light. Cyrus said roads were safest when the fog was thickest; the hunters' dogs lost scent in damp, and the dead, he added with the flat tone he used for his most unsettling remarks, were quieter in fog too. Packed tight in the air, he said, like bees in winter.

I carried the serpent. He noticed and said nothing, which was its own kind of permission.

By midmorning the fog burned off into a sky the color of dirty pewter, and we came down into a valley where a shrine stood. Or what had been a shrine. It was old — older than the charcoal burner's hut, older than anything I could name, which admittedly was a short list — a ring of leaning stones around a sunken basin, all of it furred with moss the grey-green of old bruises.

"We'll water the horses and move on," Cyrus said, and then, seeing my face: "What."

"Bring me another one."

"Astrid—"

"*Another one.*" I heard my own voice harden into something that surprised us both. "You want me to walk across a country full of people hunting me, carrying a hole where my life should be, and you want me to do it blind? Every carving you make is a door, you said. Well, I'm going to start opening them. Because whoever emptied me missed a few rooms, Cyrus, and I intend to search every single one before they come back to finish the job."

He looked at me for a long moment. There was something in his face I couldn't parse then — guilt, I'd learn later, the specific guilt of a man who knows more than he says — but what he did was reach into his pack and take out the hare. Small, long-eared, worn nearly featureless by years of thumbs.

"This one's oldest," he said. "It's held more voices than any of them. If you're going to go digging, dig here." He set it in my palm and closed my fingers over it. "But hear me first. What you touch in there, you can't un-touch. Memory doesn't care whether you're ready. Are you ready?"

No. Obviously no. I was a woman with no past standing in a ruined shrine under a starless sky, asking a bone-carver to let the dead talk to me. Readiness was a luxury for people with names they trusted.

"Yes," I said.

The hare was different from the serpent. Where the serpent had shown me a single frozen scene, the hare opened like a floodgate — and what came through was not a room but a *feeling*, vast and specific: terror, running, my lungs burning, branches whipping my arms, and underneath the terror a resolve so hard and bright it cut. I was running *toward* something, not away. I was running toward a wall of light — the wards, I knew it the way you know your own heartbeat — and I had a knife in my hand and words in my mouth in that same iron-tasting language, and I was going to—

The vision tore. Just ripped away, mid-stride, like a page torn from a book, and behind the tearing I felt — I *felt* — the edges of the cut. Smooth. Deliberate. Surgical. Someone had taken this memory and cauterized the stump, and the cauterization had a signature, a flavor, a cold precise patience that I recognized the way you recognize a handwriting.

Someone had done this carefully. Lovingly, almost. Which meant they'd had time with me. Days, weeks. Which meant I had trusted them, or been helpless, or both.

I came back to myself kneeling in the moss with the hare clenched so hard its ears bent, and Cyrus's hand on my shoulder, and my own breath sobbing in and out of me.

"Astrid." His voice was careful. "What did you see?"

"Running." I made myself say it. Made myself look at him. "I was running toward the wards with a knife, and I was *glad*, Cyrus. Whatever I was doing, I chose it. And then—" I swallowed. "And then someone cut it out of me. Mid-stride. Like they couldn't let me finish remembering what I'd done."

The silence stretched. Above us the pewter sky pressed down like a lid.

"That's not how erasure works," Cyrus said at last, slowly, the way he spoke when the dead were feeding him lines. "Taking a memory leaves a wound. Everyone knows that — it's why it's forbidden, why the carvers' guilds won't teach it. But you—" He turned my wrist over, gently, studying the skin as if memory might be visible in the veins. "You should be a ruin. Hollowed out. Instead you're... intact. Sealed. Like someone locked the doors instead of burning the house."

"Why does that matter?"

"Because burning a house is hatred," he said. "Locking the doors is protection. Somebody sealed you up to keep something *in* — or to keep something *out*." His eyes went, unwillingly, to the horizon, where the mountains stood black against the starless sky. "Or both."

I looked down at the hare, at the serpent, at my own hands — competent, capable, belonging to a woman who ran toward walls of light with knives and felt glad. I thought about the guilt that had bloomed in my chest the night the stars died, guilt with no crime attached.

Maybe the crime was still attached. Maybe it was just waiting for me to catch up to it.

"We should keep moving," Cyrus said quietly. "Veyra's hunters will have reached the valley by now. There'll be sketches. Postings." He paused, and I watched him decide to say the next part, watched the cost of it cross his face. "Astrid — if they're looking for who I think they're looking for, your face may be on those postings. We need to know who you are before they tell us."

I pocketed the hare next to the wardstone, where it hummed softly against my skin like a second heartbeat.

"Then walk fast," I said, "carver. Before I remember something that gets us both killed."

He almost smiled. Almost. And we went down out of the shrine valley together, toward the roads and the hunters and the posting-sketches waiting in the next town like mirrors I wasn't ready to look into — carrying between us a dead woman's future, a live girl's past, and the growing, terrible suspicion that the person who had erased me might have been the kindest person I ever knew.
