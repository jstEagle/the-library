## 4. The Hunter's Toll

The dead woke me before the horns did.

That was new. Usually it went the other way — horns first, then the dead grumbling awake like old men in a chapel pew, complaining about the cold, about the living, about me specifically. But that morning there was a voice at the edge of my hearing, thin and urgent as a wire pulled taut, and I lay in my bedroll in the loft of Marta Ostrow's barn and listened to it say the same word over and over.

*Run.*

I'd been ignoring that particular instruction for thirty-one years. It had never once been right. But the voice had a quality I'd learned to trust, the way you learn to trust a certain note of creak in a floorboard — not the sound itself, but what it means is coming up the stairs.

Then the horns started, down in the valley, and I decided maybe the floorboard knew something after all.

* * *

Thornfell Valley in the third week of the dark was a study in how quickly people can become strangers to themselves. The stars had been gone twenty days. Twenty nights of sky like a lid screwed down over the world, and the border wards — those old, half-forgotten threads of light that had hummed along the mountain passes since before anyone's grandmother's grandmother — shattered into nothing on the same night. Nobody had seen them break. You don't see a thing like that; you feel it, the way you feel a tooth crack, sudden and wrong and then just part of your mouth.

The valley had spent those twenty days doing what valleys do: blaming each other. The miller said the wards were the crown's business and the crown had let them rot. The priest said it was punishment, though he declined to specify for what, which struck me as theologically convenient. Old Halvard's kin said it was the work of the lowland lords, and the lowland traders said it was the mountain clans, and everyone agreed it was somehow also the fault of smugglers, which meant me, or people shaped like me, which was close enough for a mob.

So when the horns came up the valley road that morning, bright and royal and insufferably confident, half of Thornfell ran out to greet them like they were deliverance.

I stayed in the hayloft and watched through a knothole, because I have survived this long by being the man who stays in the hayloft.

They came up the valley road in column — forty riders, maybe fifty, in the grey-and-silver of the Crown Guard, with a supply train behind them and a banner that looked, in the starless morning gloom, like a smudge of ash on a pole. At their head rode a woman on a dun mare, and even from the loft I could read her the way I read bone: straight spine, still hands, eyes moving over the village the way an appraiser moves over a room full of other people's heirlooms.

I didn't know her name yet. I would soon. The dead, bless their gossiping hearts, told me within the hour.

*Veyra,* they whispered, from a soldier who'd died at some border skirmish years back and whose cousin was riding in that very column. *Captain Veyra. The king's own hound. She doesn't miss.*

Wonderful. I love a competent antagonist. It keeps things interesting right up until it kills you.

* * *

Astrid — though she wasn't Astrid yet, she was still *the girl*, because I hadn't given her a name and neither had she — was in the barn's back stall, mucking out after the goats with the grim competence of someone who has clearly done worse chores under worse circumstances and remembers none of them. That was the arrangement we'd made, she and I, in the nine days since I'd found her on the smuggler's road clutching a broken wardstone like it was the last warm thing in the world: she worked, she kept her hood up, she asked no questions I couldn't answer, and I lied to her steadily and with great craft.

She was better company than most of the dead, if I'm honest. Quieter. She had a way of looking at you when you talked, like she was trying to catch the lie midair, and mostly failing, and knowing she was failing, and deciding to let it go anyway. There was either enormous patience in that girl or enormous exhaustion, and I suspected they were the same thing wearing different coats.

"Cyrus." Her voice came low from the stall. "Riders."

"I know. Stay here. Hood up. If anyone comes in, you're Marta's niece from the coast, simple-minded since a fever, and you don't speak unless spoken to, and then only about goats."

"That's a lot of detail," she said. "It sounds rehearsed."

"It is rehearsed. I've been Marta's imaginary nephew-in-law for six years. It's the longest relationship of my life." I pulled my coat on. "Stay."

She stayed. That should have been my first warning that something was about to go badly wrong — the girl without a name, obeying instructions. In my experience, obedience is the universe clearing its throat.

* * *

Down in the square, Captain Veyra did not give a speech. I want that recorded somewhere, because it impressed me more than any speech could have. She posted four soldiers at the crossroads, two at the shrine, one at the well, and then she stood in front of the gathered villagers — most of Thornfell, come out in their good coats to gawk — and held up a sheet of paper.

"This woman," she said. Her voice carried the way a blade carries light — not loud, just impossible to look away from. "She is the daughter of the late King Eirik. She went missing the night the wards fell. The crown believes she was taken against her will by parties unknown, possibly injured, possibly confused. Anyone harboring her, or having seen her, will be compensated generously. Anyone concealing her—" she paused, and let the pause do the work of a paragraph, "—will be treated as a traitor. And you all know what happens to traitors these days. Or rather, you've noticed what hasn't happened to them lately, because the law has stopped reaching this far into the mountains. I'm here to change that."

Then she nailed the paper to the notice post beside the shrine, and another to the mill, and another to the tavern door, and turned her horse around, and that was it. No threats beyond the necessary ones. No theater. A soldier who understood that fear works best when you let people fill in the details themselves.

I filled in the details myself, later, standing in front of the notice by the mill while pretending to examine it with the idle curiosity of a man who cannot read.

The sketch was charcoal on good paper, done by someone who knew faces. Not a flattering portrait — whoever drew it had resisted the court painter's instinct to make royalty beautiful and instead made her *true*, which is far more dangerous. The set of the jaw. The slight asymmetry of the brows, left higher than right, as if permanently mid-question. The eyes drawn with a specific kind of hollow attention, the look of someone watching a door they expect bad news to come through.

I had watched that face eat porridge across a campfire for nine mornings. I had watched it flinch from the voices in my carved relics and then lean closer anyway. I had watched it, three nights ago, in the dark of Marta's barn, turn toward me with an expression of such naked, furious longing for her own name that I had to leave the barn and stand in the cold until my hands stopped shaking.

The caption beneath the sketch read: *PRINCESS ASTRID SOLVEIG VASSKA. REWARD: 2,000 CROWNS.*

Two thousand crowns. I stood there doing arithmetic I didn't want to do. Two thousand crowns was a workshop in the lowlands with a southern window. It was ten years of not sleeping in haylofts. It was my mother's trade restored to respectability, my name cleared of the smuggling charges I'd never actually beaten so much as outrun. It was, in short, everything I had ever pretended not to want, offered in exchange for pointing at a barn.

And the dead were listening. They're always listening. That's the trouble with carrying forty voices in your marrow — you are never alone with a decision, and every one of them had an opinion.

*Take it,* hissed the tax collector, who in life had sold out his own brother over a debt of eleven crowns and saw no reason the afterlife should improve his character. *You'll hang someday anyway. Dying rich is the only winning available to men like us.*

*Don't,* said the midwife, quieter, the voice I liked best of all of them, the one that had taught me most of what I know about holding steady. *Boy. Look at her hands in that drawing. Look at how the artist drew her hands.*

I looked. Clenched. One fist closed around something small — the artist had rendered it as a smudge, but I knew its shape. Round. Cracked. Humming.

The wardstone. She still carried it. Of course she did. It was the only piece of herself anyone had ever handed her.

"Trouble reading?" said a voice behind me, wry and hard as a whetstone.

I turned. Captain Veyra stood three feet away, helmet off, studying me with the unhurried attention of someone who has already catalogued everything worth cataloguing and is now checking for surprises. Up close she was younger than her voice suggested and older than her face — thirty-odd years worn like armor that fit.

"Some," I said, and gave her the smile I keep for customs officers. "My letters swim. Always have. I was admiring the artwork."

"The artwork is accurate," she said. "I made sure of it. I interviewed her seamstress, her fencing master, and a chambermaid who cried the whole time. Do you know what all three agreed on?"

"That she had excellent posture?"

"That she was kind," Veyra said, "in a way that made people underestimate her. The fencing master said she fought like someone apologizing for hitting you." She tilted her head. "You're the carver. Iversen. My scouts asked about you in three villages before this one. Bone-carver, sells charms to the grieving, keeps to himself, no family, no fixed address. The kind of man who sees everything and reports nothing."

"That's a slanderous amount of accuracy for a scout."

"Mm." She looked past me, at the notice, at the valley, at the smoke rising thin from chimneys under a sky with no stars in it even at noon — the sun came, but pale, like it was embarrassed. "Twenty days of dark, Iversen. Do you know what that does to a country? Lords are raising levies. Priests are burning each other. On the coast they've started drowning 'ward-breakers' — whoever's nearest and looks guilty. The king is dying, his brother is ruling, and the only person who might hold what's left together is missing." Her eyes came back to mine, and they were not unkind, which frightened me more than cruelty would have. "I don't much care about the reward. I care that whatever broke the wards broke them from inside, and I think she knows how. Maybe she even did it. Either way, she's the only thread I can pull, and I intend to pull it before this country tears itself apart along seams that have been waiting two hundred years to rip."

There it was. Not a hunt — an autopsy. She wanted the princess the way a surgeon wants the body: to find out what killed the light.

"You sound like you almost admire her," I said.

"I admire competence. She vanished from a locked tower on the worst night in a century, past wards that ate grown men, leaving no trail but a broken anchor-stone that half the royal arcanists swear couldn't be broken." Something moved behind her eyes — not doubt exactly, but its first cousin. "Whoever she is, she's not what the sketches say. Which means the sketches aren't enough. Which means I need people to talk." She stepped closer, and dropped her voice to something almost conversational. "So. Carver. You hear things in your line of work. Grieving people tell carvers what they won't tell priests. Has anything reached those clever ears of yours? A girl. Alone. No name, no history. Found on a road, maybe. Confused."

Every voice in my bones went silent at once. Forty dead, holding their breath. Even the tax collector.

And I understood, in that moment, the shape of the choice, and I hated its shape, because it wasn't a choice between safety and danger. It was a choice between two kinds of betrayal. Betray her — hand her to a woman who, whatever her reasons, would drag a hollowed-out girl through a burning country as a specimen — and live comfortably, and hear her voice in my bones forever alongside the others, asking me why. Or betray myself — my caution, my rules, the whole careful architecture of a life built on never being anyone's reason to burn a barn — and run, now, today, with a girl who didn't know her own name tied to my fate like a second shadow.

"There was a girl," I heard myself say, "on the east road. Nine days back. Travelers mentioned her at the tavern — found wandering near the old pass, half-frozen, remembering nothing. They took her toward Lowmark, I heard. Looking for family."

It was a good lie. Specific enough to satisfy, vague enough to send her the wrong direction, wrapped in the credible cowardice of a small man eager to be helpful and gone. Veyra studied me for a long moment, and I felt the examination go all the way through, the way her eyes had gone through the village, through the valley, through everything.

"Lowmark," she repeated.

"East road," I confirmed. "Half-frozen. Nothing in her head but fog, they said."

"Thank you, carver." She put her helmet back on, and the face disappeared behind steel, and she was only a soldier again. "For what it's worth — and it isn't worth much — I hope you're lying to me. The kingdom's short on honest men. It's shorter on liars with a spine." She swung onto her horse. "If she passes through your valley again, remember: I don't want her hurt. Whatever my orders say. Remember that instead of the poster."

She rode out. The column followed. The horns went away down the valley, taking the royal morning with them, and the villagers dispersed in a buzz, and I stood by the mill with my heart going like a smith's hammer and forty ghosts exhaling at once.

*You lied beautifully,* said the tax collector, almost proud.

*You lied at all,* said the midwife, which was the point.

* * *

I climbed to the barn slowly, buying time with every step, running through what I'd say, how I'd say it, the shape of the departure — we ride tonight, no, now, no, dusk, when the roads empty — assembling the next lie to tell the girl, because that was the currency between us, lies, carefully minted, always in her interest, or so I told myself.

She was waiting in the stall, hood down, wardstone in her open palm, and she was looking at it with an expression I hadn't seen before. Not confusion. Recognition, almost — the way you look at a word on the tip of your tongue.

"They came for me," she said. Not a question.

"Astrid—"

"No." She looked up, and her eyes had that hollow attention from the sketch, sharpened now to a point. "Don't. I saw your face from the loft gap when you read the notice, Cyrus. I've spent nine days learning your face, because it's the only face I have. You went white as bone." She closed her fist around the stone. "Princess. Two thousand crowns. So. Are you going to sell me, or are you going to lie to me again? Because I've gotten quite good at telling the difference, and I'd honestly rather know."

Outside, far down the valley, faint as memory, the horns sounded once more — turning east, toward Lowmark, toward the lie I'd fed them.

Toward the truth I'd have to stop feeding her, starting now, or lose her entirely.

I opened my mouth, and for the first time in nine days, I had no idea what was going to come out of it.
