## 5. Roads Only Smugglers Know

*Cyrus*

The entrance to the Vaskr mines was hidden behind a waterfall, because of course it was. Smugglers have never met a cliché they didn't like. My mother used to say the best hiding places are the ones everyone's heard of and nobody believes, and she'd have known — she spent eleven years moving salt and secrets through these hills before the mountain decided it wanted her back.

I stood at the lip of the falls with the spray soaking through my coat and listened to the dead argue about whether I should go in.

*Flooded,* said the miller, who had drowned in a millpond in 912 and considered himself an authority on all things wet. *The lower galleries are under water, boy. I can feel it in the stone.*

*The upper galleries are dry,* said the old woman whose voice I'd carried longest — sixty years now, a grandmother named Sef who died before I was born and who had appointed herself my conscience, my cook's timer, and my chief nag. *The upper galleries go under Thornfell itself. Where else would you take her, genius? Over the open road with the king's hunters snapping at your heels?*

*You could just leave her,* said a third voice, newer, colder. It had followed us for two days now, and I didn't like it. New voices were rarely friendly. *You could walk away and let the dark sort her out.*

"Nobody asked you," I said, and then remembered the girl was standing three feet behind me, watching me argue with a waterfall.

"You do that a lot," Astrid said. Not a question. In two days she had learned the shape of my strangeness the way you learn the shape of furniture in a dark room — by bruising yourself on it.

"The dead are chatty," I said. "It's rude to interrupt them mid-argument."

"Which one won?"

"None of them. They never do." I tested the first handhold above the waterline. Wet, but sound. "We go in through the upper adit. Stay behind me, keep one hand on the wall, and if I tell you to run, you run and you don't look back."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you'll be the first person in history to out-stubborn a dead woman named Sef, and I'll want to watch."

Almost — *almost* — she smiled. It was a thin, cold thing, the smile of someone who had opened her own skull that morning and found the shelves empty. But it was there, and I found I'd been rationing hope without noticing, and here it was, spent on a smile in the spray of a waterfall.

We climbed.

* * *

The mines swallowed the last grey light within fifty paces, and after that there was only my lantern and the sound of water moving somewhere below us like a slow animal. The dark in the Vaskr mines was not ordinary dark. The stars had gone out four nights ago, and whatever the stars had been holding up — call it the sky, call it the roof of the world — had come down a little closer since. Dark used to be just an absence. Now it had weight. Now it leaned.

And the dead got louder.

I don't know how else to explain it to someone who's never heard them. Living people think the dead are quiet, that death is a door closing. It's not. It's a door opening onto a room where everyone is talking at once and has been talking for centuries and no one has ever once said anything worth hearing. I have spent my whole life turning the volume down — carving bone, which gives the voices somewhere else to go, somewhere to pool like water into a cup. Without my knives in my hands, without the little anchors I'd left scattered across three counties, the voices came up through the stone of the mine and into the soles of my feet and I could do nothing about it.

*Cold down here,* said the miller. *Cold and wet and the water remembers.*

*Keep him moving,* said Sef. *The girl keeps him moving.*

*The girl, the girl,* said the cold new voice. *Look at her. Look at what's under her skin. You're carrying a lit lantern through a powder store, carver.*

I put my hand flat against the tunnel wall and pretended to be catching my breath. The stone was full of old deaths — miners, mostly, men who'd gone into the mountain in the days when Thornfell's silver was rich, and their voices had soaked into the rock like tallow into cloth. They didn't know me. They didn't know anything anymore. They just repeated the last hour of their lives, over and over, the way a clock repeats the hour it broke on.

Behind me, Astrid's breathing was steady. That was the thing I kept having to relearn about her: she was afraid — anyone would be, hollowed out and hunted — but her fear sat in her like a passenger, not a driver. She walked into the starless dark of a flooded mine with her chin up and her hand on the wall and her weight balanced like she'd been born in tunnels.

Which, I supposed, was exactly the problem.

"How far?" she asked.

"To the first junction, half a mile. Then down the eastern gallery, and we come up in a shepherd's bothy above the tree line." I lifted the lantern. "Assuming the eastern gallery isn't underwater."

"Assuming."

"Smuggling is mostly assuming. The rest is running."

We went on. The tunnel narrowed, and the ceiling came down to meet us, and the dark leaned harder, and the dead got louder, and I did what I always do, which is to say I endured it and made jokes about it to no one who could hear.

* * *

The junction was a cathedral of bad news.

The eastern gallery was flooded — not to the ceiling, but to the chest, black water lying flat and still as glass, and out of that glass the cold came up like a hand. I stood at the edge and held the lantern high and did arithmetic I didn't like. The water ran maybe three hundred paces to where the gallery climbed. Three hundred paces, chest-deep, in water cold enough to stop a heart, in the dark, with the dead screaming.

"We could go back," Astrid said. "Try the western—"

"The western gallery is blocked. It came down in the spring, Sef says." I paused. "Sef is the—"

"The dead grandmother. I know. You talk about her like she's your mother."

"She was somebody's mother. Just not mine." I crouched and put my palm in the water. It was worse than I'd thought. "There's a ledge. Along the left wall, about a foot wide, maybe eighteen inches above the waterline. If we hug the wall we can edge along it. It'll take an hour. My arms will hate me. But it's dry, mostly."

"Mostly."

"I told you. Assuming."

She looked at the black water for a long moment. Then she said, quietly, almost to herself: "I've done this before."

"You've done—"

"I don't remember it." Her voice had gone strange and flat. "But my hands know. When I look at the ledge, my hands already know the grip." She held them up, turned them over, as if they belonged to a stranger. "Cyrus. What if whoever I was, I was someone who did things like this? What if I was someone bad?"

The cold new voice, down in the water, laughed.

I should have lied to her. I'd been lying to her since the night I found her — telling her she might be a merchant's daughter, a scholar, anyone, anything, because the truth was a hook with the king's seal on it and I couldn't bear to watch her swallow it. But lying in the dark beside that water felt different. Felt like the water itself.

"I think," I said, "that whoever you were, your hands learned to hold on. That's not a bad thing to have been."

It wasn't an answer. She knew it wasn't an answer. She let it be one anyway, which was its own kind of mercy, and we started along the ledge.

* * *

*Astrid*

There is a version of fear that feels like falling, and I had been living in it for four days. And then there is the other kind — the fear that feels like arriving. Like your body has been waiting in an empty house for you and finally hears your step on the stair.

The mine was the second kind, and it terrified me more.

Because the ledge was narrow and the water was black and the lantern-light only reached six feet into a dark that went on forever, and my feet knew exactly where to go. My weight knew where to sit. When the ledge crumbled under my left heel — a sound like a biscuit breaking, absurdly small in that vastness — my hand shot out and found a seam in the rock I hadn't seen, and my body swung close to the wall and rode it out, and Cyrus didn't even notice, because I made no sound, because I had learned somewhere, in some life I couldn't remember, that sound is what kills you in the dark.

Who teaches a girl that? What kind of life?

The dark got deeper as we went. I don't mean the lantern got dimmer. I mean the dark itself got *thicker*, like we were wading into it, and I began to hear things in it that I was fairly sure Cyrus heard too, because his jaw had gone tight in a way I was learning to read. Voices. Not words. The shape of words, the way you hear the shape of a conversation through a wall — the rise and fall of it, the anger, the grief. The mountain was full of them. The mountain was a throat, and we were walking down it.

And underneath the voices, something else. Something that felt — and I know how this sounds — something that felt like it *knew me*. Like the dark was a crowd and somewhere in it was a face turned toward me, patient, waiting for me to come to it.

"Talk," I said.

Cyrus glanced back. "What?"

"Talk. About anything. Your mother. The mines. I don't care. Just — keep talking." My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "The quiet is doing something. I can feel it listening."

For a moment he looked at me with an expression I couldn't name. Then he said, "My mother once smuggled a live bear through these tunnels."

"A bear."

"A dancing bear. Belonged to a fete-master in Lowmark who'd run afoul of a tax collector. The bear was collateral. My mother was paid to move the bear. She said it was the worst client she ever had — it complained the whole way and ate half the cargo." His voice was steady, easy, a rope thrown across black water. "She left the bear with a hermit above the tree line and told the fete-master it had run off. The hermit wrote her a letter every year for a decade. Signed it with a paw print."

"Did the bear dance?"

"On the ferry across the Vaskr, once. Sank the ferry."

Despite everything, I laughed. It came out of me like something startled, and the dark flinched from it, and I thought: *good. Be flinched. I am still a person who laughs at bear jokes. Whatever they took out of me, they didn't get that.*

We were maybe two hundred paces along when the ledge ended.

Not gradually. It simply stopped, a bite taken out of the wall where the rock had sheared away, and beyond the gap there was only the black water and, after the lantern's light reached across it, the far wall — ten feet away. Ten feet of nothing.

Cyrus set the lantern down and looked at the gap and I watched him do the arithmetic again, and this time the sum came out wrong.

"It was here," he said. "The ledge was continuous. Sef walked this route in—" he stopped. "Sef walked this route. Before the collapse."

"The collapse that blocked the western gallery?"

"The same."

"So the mountain's still falling apart." I looked at the water. Flat, patient, cold. "And the stars going out did this?"

"Everything's connected. The wards, the sky, the stone. Pull one thread—" He crouched at the edge of the gap, testing the wall above the waterline. There were handholds, maybe. A traverse, the kind of thing a climber would call easy and I would call a eulogy. "I can make it. I've done worse."

"And me?"

He was quiet a moment too long, and in that quiet I heard the answer he was too kind to say: *I don't know if you can make it. I don't know anything about you. I found you in a ditch four days ago and I've been lying to you ever since and now I'm going to watch you try to cross ten feet of black water in the dark.*

"I'll make it," I said.

"You don't know that."

"I know my hands." I held them up again. They were steady. They were *always* steady, and it was the steadiest and most frightening thing about me. "Trust me. You keep saying you're the one who hears the dead. Fine. But right now I'm the one who hears the mountain."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, and started across.

He was good. I'll say that for him — he was better than good, moving along the wall with the economy of someone who'd spent a life doing this, and for eight feet it was going to work, and then the mountain decided otherwise.

I heard it before I understood it: a sound like a knuckle cracking, deep in the rock above him. The seam he was holding. The whole face of the wall above the waterline had been waiting, the way rock waits, patient as the dark, for one more ounce of weight.

The seam let go.

He didn't fall so much as the wall stopped being a wall, and he went with it, and the lantern went with him, and the world went black, and I heard the water take him with a sound like a door closing.

I have no memory of deciding to move.

That is the honest truth and I will carve it on my own stone someday: there was no decision. There was a gap of nothing — the same nothing that lives where my memories should be — and on the far side of it I was in the water.

The cold was total. It didn't hurt; it went past hurt into something else, a white silence, and in that silence I found his coat, and his coat found my fist, and my other hand found — I don't know what it found. A hold. In black water, in absolute dark, my hand went out and found a hold in the rock that my eyes had never seen, as if the mountain had told me where it was. As if I had asked.

I hauled. He was heavier than he looked and the water wanted him and the dark wanted both of us, and my shoulders burned with a strength that felt borrowed from someone else, someone who had done this, who had *trained* for this, and I hauled him up onto the far ledge and then myself, and we lay there in the black, gasping, two drowned animals on a shelf of stone.

The lantern was gone. So was the dark's patience — the voices in the rock had risen to a roar, wordless, enormous, a sea of the dead all around us and no light and no walls, and I felt Cyrus shaking beside me and I didn't think it was the cold.

"Cyrus." My voice came out cracked. "Cyrus, talk to me."

"Here," he managed. "Here. I'm — the lantern—"

"Forget the lantern. Listen to me." I found his collar in the dark and gripped it, because it was the only true thing in the world. "You told me the dead are loud. So be loud back. Talk. Bear jokes. Anything. Because I can't see, and I can't hear what you hear, and I am *this close* to losing my mind, so talk, damn you."

And he laughed — a broken, gasping, half-drowned laugh — and said, "The bear. Did I tell you about the bear?"

"You told me about the bear."

"Good. Good. Because there's a second bear story, and it's worse, and it's the only thing I know that's louder than the dead."

So he talked. In the black, on the ledge, with the dead roaring around us like a flood that had gotten into the walls of the world, he talked, and his voice was a rope and I held it, and slowly — so slowly — the roar settled back into its old shape, the shape of a conversation through a wall, and the mountain closed its mouth.

After a long time he said, quietly, in a voice with all the jokes gone out of it: "You went in after me. Into that water. In the dark."

"You were drowning."

"I know what I was. I mean—" He stopped. Started again. "Nobody goes in after anyone. Not into that. Not for a stranger they've known four days. You didn't even hesitate. You moved like—" A breath. "You moved like someone the dark is afraid of."

I lay on the cold stone and looked up into a blackness that had no top, and somewhere in it, I knew, was the thing that knew my name — the thing that had been waiting for me since the stars went out.

"Then let's hope," I said, "that whatever I was, it stays on my side."

We came up out of the mine at dawn, or what passes for dawn now — a grey light with no sun behind it, a sky like the inside of a closed fist. The shepherd's bothy stood where Sef had promised, and in its doorway, folded and left where someone had wanted it found, was a piece of paper weighted with a stone.

Cyrus picked it up. I watched his face as he read it, and I watched the blood leave it.

"What is it?"

He handed it to me without a word. The sketch was fine work, done by someone who had seen its subject up close: a young woman, sharp-eyed, chin lifted. Underneath, in a soldier's block letters:

*BY ORDER OF THE CROWN — 500 CROWNS — THE PRINCESS ASTRID, LAST OF THE OLD LINE. ALIVE.*

The face in the drawing was mine.
