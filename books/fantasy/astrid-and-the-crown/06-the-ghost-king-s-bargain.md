## 6. The Ghost-King's Bargain

The shrine found us the way trouble always finds you on a smuggler's road: by looking, at first, like shelter.

We'd been walking since before dawn through a country that had forgotten how to be a country. No stars, no wards, and no sun worth mentioning — just a low grey lid of cloud pressing down on the hills like a hand on a lid of a pot that someone, somewhere, was very determined to keep boiling. Cyrus walked ahead with his collar up and his hands in his pockets, and every so often his head would tilt, just slightly, the way it did when the dead were saying something he didn't want to hear.

"You're doing the thing," I said.

"What thing."

"The tilt. Like a dog hearing a whistle nobody else can hear. It's been going on for an hour and you keep not telling me what they're saying."

He didn't break stride. "They're saying a lot of things. Most of them are complaints. The dead complain the way rivers run — constantly, and downhill." A pause. His jaw worked. "One of them keeps saying *shrine*. Over and over. Like a name being called across a courtyard."

"Whose shrine?"

"That's the part I don't like." He glanced back at me, and in the flat light his eyes looked like wet slate. "It's not one of my usual voices. My usual voices are small dead. Farmers. Smugglers. A woman who won't stop describing her recipe for eel pie. This one is—" He stopped, visibly reorganizing his face into something less honest, which for Cyrus was a full-body effort. "Loud. Old. It doesn't talk *to* me. It talks *past* me. Like I'm furniture."

"I'm sure that's very insulting to the furniture."

"Astrid." He said my name — the fake one, the one he'd given me on the road when I woke up hollow with nothing but a broken wardstone in my fist and a voice in my bones screaming that I had been somebody important enough to erase. He'd told me I might be a merchant's daughter. Fleeing a marriage. It was a kind lie, and we both knew it was a lie, and we both pretended otherwise because pretending was cheaper than the truth. "If there's a shrine up ahead, we should go around it."

"And if it has a roof?"

"...Roofs have value."

That was how, an hour later, I stood in the doorway of a ruin that had no business still standing, listening to a dead king offer me everything I'd lost.

* * *

The shrine sat in a fold of the hills like a coin dropped in grass and forgotten. It had been grand once — you could tell from the bones of it, the way the columns leaned with dignity rather than collapse, the way the carvings along the lintel had been worn smooth not by weather but by centuries of hands touching them in hope or fear. Now moss owned the floors and something had nested in the hollow of a fallen saint's head. The roof held over maybe a third of the building, which in this country counted as luxury.

Cyrus stopped dead three steps inside.

I felt it too, though I couldn't have explained how. The air changed texture. Thicker. Warmer. Like stepping from a cold street into a room where a fire had recently gone out.

"Don't," he said quietly.

"I haven't done anything."

"You're about to. You get a look on your face when you're about to do something. I've known you four days and I already know the look."

I did, in fact, have the look. Because at the far end of the shrine, where the altar should have been, there was a man sitting on the steps as though he'd been waiting for us specifically and had all the time in the world to do it.

He was dressed in the fashion of a picture in a history book: high-collared coat gone grey at the edges, boots that had outlasted their maker, a crown that wasn't a crown at all but a circlet of black iron worn smooth as river stone. His face was handsome the way old portraits are handsome — correct, composed, and entirely without warmth. And he was translucent at the edges, the way smoke is translucent, except that smoke doesn't sit with its hands folded and its ankles crossed.

"Cyrus Iversen," the ghost said, and his voice filled the whole ruin without raising itself, the way cold fills a house. "Bone-carver of Thornfell. Son of Maren. Carrier of forty-one voices, though I count only thirty-nine remaining. You've been spending yourself faster than usual."

I heard Cyrus's breath catch. Not at the words. At being *counted*.

"Who are you?" I asked.

The ghost turned his attention to me, and I want to describe what that felt like, properly, because it matters. It felt like being read. Not looked at — *read*, the way you'd read a ledger, line by line, checking for errors. Somewhere behind my ribs, the broken wardstone hummed against my chest where it hung on its cord, and the hum had a shape I almost recognized.

"I have been called many things," the ghost said. "King is the one that stuck. You may use it."

"We're leaving," Cyrus said.

"He can't hear you unless you let him," the ghost said mildly, to me, as though Cyrus were wind rattling a shutter. "Interesting. She has walls. Someone built them well."

"Who *built* them?" The question came out of me fast and hungry, and I hated how fast and how hungry. Four days of being a person with no inside. Four days of reaching for a memory and finding smooth blank wall, over and over, like knocking on doors in a house where every room has been bricked shut. Four days of watching Cyrus watch me with guilt he thought he was hiding. "You know something about what was done to me."

"I know everything about what was done to you." The ghost unfolded himself from the altar steps and stood, and standing, he was taller than any man has a right to be, and the shadows in the shrine leaned toward him like plants toward a window. "I watched it done. I have watched a great many things done in this kingdom, princess. Waiting is largely what death is for."

"*Princess.*"

"Ah." Something moved across his face that on a living man would have been a smile. On him it was more like weather. "She doesn't know. Oh, this is *delicious*. Carver — you told her she was a merchant's daughter?"

"The road's dangerous for women traveling alone," Cyrus said, flat and hard. "A story keeps questions down."

"It does. Though hers was never a story anyone could outrun." The ghost drifted closer — not walked, *drifted*, his boots never quite committing to the floor — and stopped an arm's length away, close enough that the wardstone hummed so hard I could feel my teeth buzzing. "Shall I tell her? Or would you rather keep lying to her? I'm told lies are easier to carry downhill. Like complaints."

"Cyrus." My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "What is he talking about?"

The silence went on long enough that I heard the wind working at the broken roof tiles. When Cyrus finally spoke, he wouldn't look at me. "The dead say you're the anchor. Of the border wards. The ones that fell."

"The wards fell the night I— the night I woke up. With nothing."

"Yes."

"So whatever I was, I was tied to them. Tied to the wards that kept—" I gestured vaguely upward, at the ceiling, at the whole starless sky. "*That.* From happening."

"The wards kept a great deal from happening," the ghost said. "And now nothing is keeping it. Which brings me, conveniently, to business." He clasped his hands behind his back and began, horribly, to pace, a king inspecting a ruin that happened to be his own afterlife. "Here is what I offer, girl-without-a-name. Your memories exist. They were taken, not destroyed — carving a soul is like carving bone; the shavings must go somewhere. I have them. Every year of your life. Every name. Every reason. I can return them to you piece by piece, at whatever pace your mind can bear, beginning whenever you say the word."

The hunger that went through me then — I have no clean way to describe it. Imagine thirst. Now imagine that you didn't know you were thirsty until someone said the word *water*, and suddenly your whole body is one long scream for it. That was me, standing in a ruined shrine, wanting to say the word so badly my mouth actually watered.

"And the price," Cyrus said. His voice had gone very quiet, which I was learning meant very dangerous. "There's always a price. Dead men don't do charity."

"No charity," the ghost agreed pleasantly. "The price is paid later. Unspecified. At a time of my choosing."

"That's not a price, that's a debt with no number on it."

"All the best debts are unnumbered, carver. Numbered debts can be paid off and forgotten. Mine stay... motivating." The ghost turned back to me, and his attention settled on me again, that ledger-reading weight. "Consider your position honestly. You are hunted — yes, hunted, don't look at the carver, he knows and hasn't told you why. You are nameless in a country sliding toward ruin. Every day the sky stays dark, the borders rot further, and men with sketches of your face move closer. Your memories are the only map you have to who you are and what you're carrying. And the man offering them to you asks only one thing now, and one thing later." He spread his hands. "Refuse, if you like. Walk back into the dark and stay a stranger to yourself. Many people manage entire lives that way. Most of them die puzzled."

"*Don't,*" Cyrus said.

I looked at him. Really looked. And here is the thing I want to set down honestly, because it took me years to forgive myself for it: some part of me registered, in that moment, that Cyrus was afraid *for* me — and some other part of me, a colder part, a part I didn't know I had, registered that his fear was useful. That a frightened man tells you where the edges are. That I could stand here and weigh his terror against my hunger like a merchant weighing coin.

Four days old, and already I had a part of me that did arithmetic with people.

"One fragment," I said.

"Astrid—"

"One," I said to the ghost. "You said piece by piece. So give me one piece. Now. And the rest stays yours until I decide otherwise."

The ghost inclined his head, gracious as a chess player accepting an opening. "A fair bargain. Better than fair. Most take the whole loaf." He raised one hand, and the shadows in the shrine gathered around it like moths. "Though I'll warn you, princess — memory is not a gift you receive. It's a door you walk through. Where it opens, you follow. Ready yourself."

"I've been ready since I woke up with nothing."

"Then look at me," the ghost said, "and remember."

* * *

I want to write down exactly what the memory gave me, while it's still sharp, because I suspect it will dull — the ghost warned me of that too, in his way, saying that returned things fade faster than kept things, like borrowed clothes.

It opened with gold.

Not the warm gold of candlelight. The hard, ceremonial gold of a throne room at midwinter — banners hanging dead-still in air that smelled of pine boughs and tallow and, underneath it, faintly, of iron. I was standing in the memory the way you stand in your own body, except I was also watching from somewhere above my own shoulder, seeing myself at nineteen — younger than I am now, older than I felt — in a white gown, walking a carpet of black cloth down the center of a hall lined with people who were not watching me.

They were watching the throne.

No. Watching the *space* before the throne, where an old woman stood in robes stitched with constellations, holding a knife that caught the light like a splinter of ice.

I knew her. In the memory I knew her the way you know your own hands. High Priestess Ysolde. Keeper of the Wards. And I knew — the knowledge arriving whole, the way memory does, without reasoning toward it — that the hall was soaked. Not with blood spilled in violence. With blood spilled in *ceremony*, generation upon generation of it, sunk so deep into the stones that the floor drank torchlight instead of reflecting it, and everyone standing on it had agreed, in the polite unspoken way of courts, never to mention it.

I was walking toward the knife.

And the terrible thing — the thing I keep circling like a tongue probing a broken tooth — is that in the memory, I wasn't afraid. I was *resigned*. I knew the prayers. I'd memorized them as a child, the way other children memorized songs. *Blood for the wards, life for the light, the crown gives and the crown takes away.* I knew that princesses of the old line were anchors, and anchors were renewed, and renewal was a word that meant exactly what it meant, dressed up in vestments. I remembered my mother's face in the crowd, pale and proud and *relieved*, and I remembered thinking, with a clarity that still frightens me: *she's glad it's me and not her.*

The knife came up. The hall held its breath—

—and the memory tore.

Not ended. *Tore*, like cloth ripped sideways, and through the rip I saw — or felt, or knew — the other thing. The thing underneath the ceremony. My own hands, later, in the dark, pressed against the base of a wardstone the size of a standing man, singing words I hadn't been allowed to learn, feeling the great deep roots of the border magic shudder under my palms like a mast in a storm. Feeling them *crack*. Choosing it. Doing it anyway.

I broke the wards.

I broke the wards, and the stars went out, and somewhere in the tearing dark between those two images — the knife rising, the stone cracking — the memory slammed shut like a door in wind, and I was on my knees on the mossy floor of a ruined shrine with Cyrus's hands on my shoulders and my own voice hoarse from screaming, though I had no memory of screaming.

* * *

For a while neither of us spoke. The ghost had withdrawn to the altar steps and sat watching us with the patience of furniture, which somehow made it worse.

"They'll come back," he said eventually, conversationally. "The pieces. They always come back. The first one is the worst — it has to introduce itself to a mind that's been swept clean. Later ones settle in like guests who've visited before." He rose. "You'll want the second soon. Within days, I think. The mind doesn't like half-opened doors; it rattles them."

"I didn't agree to a second."

"You agreed to a price paid later. The later is approaching faster than you'd like. Everything approaches faster than you'd like, these days." He drifted past me toward the broken doorway, and where he passed, the moss blackened slightly, frost blooming and dying in his wake. At the threshold he paused. "Carver. A courtesy, since you've been such gracious furniture. The hunters reached the valley two nights ago. They carry sketches. The captain is named Veyra, and she is better than the men she serves. You have perhaps three days. Spend them well."

Then the doorway was empty, and the shrine was just a shrine, cold and mossy and ordinary, and the only proof anything had happened was the taste of blood in my mouth that wasn't mine, and the weight of the wardstone against my sternum, humming a note I now recognized.

It was humming the first bar of the anchor-prayer. *Blood for the wards.*

I had sung it myself, once, to bring the sky down.

Cyrus crouched in front of me, and his face was doing several things at once, none of them successfully hidden. Fear. Pity. And underneath both, carefully buried, guilt — the specific guilt of a man who has been lying to someone and has just watched the lie collapse in real time.

"Astrid." He hesitated. "What did you see?"

I looked at this man who had lied to me to keep me safe, who carried thirty-nine dead voices in his bones and was paying for each one with pieces of himself I was only beginning to guess at, who had fed me and taught me the smuggler's roads and called me *merchant's daughter* in a voice gentle enough that I'd almost believed it.

I saw a throne room soaked in blood. I saw a knife. I saw my own hands on a breaking stone.

And I lied.

"A room," I said. "Gold. People in fine clothes. Nothing that makes sense." I let my voice shake, which took no acting at all. "Cyrus, I don't — I can't remember any of it clearly. It's like trying to hold water."

He studied me for a long moment. I watched him decide to believe me, and I understood, with a sick lurch, that he was choosing to believe me not because he did, but because the alternative was a conversation neither of us could survive on four days of trust and an empty stomach.

"Three days," he said instead, standing, offering me his hand. "Before the hunters catch our trail. We need to move."

I took his hand. His grip was warm and calloused and honest, and I used its honesty to haul myself upright and walk out of the shrine beside him, into the grey starless afternoon, wearing my secret like a stone sewn into my coat.

Behind us, faint as a bell heard through water, the shrine hummed the anchor-prayer.

And far off, at the edge of hearing — his hearing, not mine — Cyrus tilted his head, and said nothing, and lengthened his stride.
