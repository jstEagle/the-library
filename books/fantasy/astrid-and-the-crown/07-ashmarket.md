## 7. Ashmarket

The dead had opinions about Ashmarket, and none of them were kind.

*Too many gates,* said the miller who'd ridden in my bones since the Karst pass. *Too few ways out.* He'd been murdered by his own brother over a waterwheel, which made him an expert on places where trust went to die.

I kept my hood up and my hands in my pockets, where my fingers could worry the small bone star I'd carved on the road — a habit now, like breathing. Beside me, Astrid walked with the careful steps of someone pretending the ground was ordinary. She'd gotten better at it. That frightened me more than the hunters did.

"Tell me again," she said, low, "why we're walking into a city full of people who want answers about the sky."

"Because the fence who can get us papers is here, and papers are the only answer that matters." I nodded toward the walls rising out of the valley haze — Ashmarket, so named because some ancestor with a poet's soul had burned the original town for the iron in its ashes. "Old Sela knew my mother. If anyone remembers how to make a person unremembered, it's her."

"You keep saying *knew*. Past tense does a lot of work in that sentence."

"So does *if*."

The gates were open, which was itself a bad sign. Cities that expect trouble close their gates; cities that have already given up open them and let the trouble walk in wearing whatever face it likes. The guards weren't checking papers. They were checking faces — scanning the crowd with the flat, hungry look of men hunting one particular stranger among ten thousand.

I steered us wide of them, into the river of refugees flowing up the Ash Road. The city had swallowed half the borderlands in three weeks, and it showed: tents in the gutters, cooking fires in doorways, children asleep against cart wheels while their parents stared at a sky that gave them nothing back. No stars. No moon worth naming. Just a low grey ceiling like dirty wool, pressing down until you could feel it on your shoulders.

People don't do well without stars. I'd known that abstractly my whole life, the way you know fire burns. But walking through Ashmarket was watching the knowledge become flesh. The dark didn't just frighten them. It *offended* them. It was a theft, and everyone in this crowd had been robbed personally, and everyone was looking around for the thief.

"They're going to turn on each other soon," Astrid said quietly.

"The dead say the same thing. Well. They say worse."

"What do they—"

"*There,*" hissed the miller, sharp enough that I flinched. "*The woman by the shrine post. Grey cloak. She's been at the corner since we entered. Watching.*"

I didn't look. Looking is how you confirm what you already know. Instead I took Astrid's elbow and turned us down a tanner's lane, all shadow and stink, and put two corners between us and the shrine post before I spoke.

"Don't ask questions yet. Walk faster."

"Cyrus—"

"Grey cloak. Shrine post. Been there too long." I heard her breath change, the small hitch of fear being swallowed and filed away. She was getting good at that too. I hated being the reason she needed the skill.

Sela's fence-house sat behind a chandler's shop on Candlemakers' Row, marked — if you knew how to read it — by a carved lintel: a wren with a broken wing. My mother had cut that bird when she was younger than Astrid. Sela had never replaced it, not through two fires and one flood, because in her trade a promise carved in bone was worth more than one written in ink.

I knocked the pattern: three quick, two slow, one long drag of the knuckle. A beggar's knock, a smuggler's knock, a knock that said *I'm nobody, but I'm somebody's nobody.*

The shutters stayed closed. The street stayed quiet.

Then the door opened six inches, and a voice like dry paper said, "Wren's boy."

"Sela. You remember me."

"I remember your mother. You I remember as a debt she never paid." But the door opened wider, and we slipped inside, into air thick with tallow smoke and the smell of old secrets kept in oilcloth.

* * *

Sela had gone from plump to hollow in the years since I'd seen her, but her eyes hadn't changed — small, quick, and lit from somewhere cold. She looked at Astrid exactly once, then at me, and I watched her do the arithmetic every fence in the world does automatically: *what is this worth, and what will it cost me.*

"You're bringing me a problem," she said.

"I'm asking you to lose two people. Papers, routes, names that don't echo."

"For her?" A jerk of her chin at Astrid. "Or for you?"

"For both of us equally. We're a matched set."

Astrid spoke up, and I'll give her credit — she didn't flinch under Sela's stare. "We can pay."

"With what? Coin's dying in this city. Everyone knows coin's next after the stars." Sela settled onto a stool, bones cracking like green wood. "You know what people are saying in the markets? That the crown sold the sky. That the king's brother made a bargain and the price was every star over Thornfell. They're saying it quietly now. They won't be saying it quietly much longer."

My stomach did something slow and unpleasant. Because they were wrong, and wrong in a way that pointed directly at the girl sitting beside me, and crowds don't need to be right to be lethal. They just need a direction.

"How long to make the papers?" I asked.

"A week. Maybe less if the roads stay closed and I'm bored." Sela leaned forward. "But hear me, Wren's boy. There's a sketch going around. Bought its way into every guardhouse and every tavern board in this city yesterday. A girl's face. And the reward isn't coin."

I felt Astrid go still beside me. Not frozen — still, deliberate, the stillness of someone choosing not to run.

"What's the reward?" she asked.

"That's the strange part. Silver, title, land — the usual. But posted underneath, in the prince's own seal, a line no one understands: *Bring her whole.*" Sela's mouth twisted. "Whole. Like she's a shipment. Like she's a jar that mustn't break."

*Bring her whole.* The words went through me like a fishhook. Aldric didn't want her dead — or not only dead. He wanted her *intact*, and I thought of the ghost-king in his ruined shrine, trading memories back piece by piece, and I wondered — not for the first time — whose hand moved which puppet.

"Show her the sketch," Sela said, watching me. "She should see what the whole city's about to see."

He drew it out himself — I did, from my coat, because I'd already bought one off a crier an hour before we reached the gate. I'd told myself I wouldn't show her unless she asked. Cowardice wears many coats.

Astrid took the paper and looked at her own face for a long time.

"It's good," she said finally. "Whoever drew it knew me." Her thumb traced the jawline, the set of the eyes. "Before. Someone who saw me *before*."

And then, because the universe has a sense of humor like a boot heel, someone hammered on the door.

Three knocks. Two slow. One long drag.

Sela's pattern. Sela's *warning* pattern, the one that meant *your knock brought company.*

The old woman was already moving, snuffing candles, herding us toward the back room with the economy of someone who'd survived two fires by knowing when to stop arguing. "Cellar," she breathed. "Through the coal chute. Don't come back here. Whatever happens, don't come back."

"Sela—"

"You were your mother's debt," she said, pushing me down the ladder into the dark. "Now we're even. Go."

The trapdoor shut above us, and through the floorboards came the sound of the front door opening, and a voice I knew — clear, cold, carrying the particular patience of someone who has never once needed to raise it.

"Madam Sela. Captain Veyra, Royal Third Hunters. May we speak about the wren?"

* * *

The cellar was black and smelled of coal dust and old brine. Astrid's hand found my sleeve in the dark — found it fast, unerringly, another of those reflexes she couldn't explain and I tried not to think about.

"Above us," she whispered. "That's her. The captain from the posters' talk. Veyra."

"I know."

"She's talking to Sela right now. About a wren. About—" I heard her swallow. "Cyrus. Your mother's mark. She knows the mark. Which means she knows about your mother, which means—"

"Which means the hunters have been reading my family longer than I've been reading theirs." Above us, boots crossed the floorboards. Two sets. Then a third, heavier, stopping directly overhead. "Quiet now. The dead are listening, and they're better at it than we are."

The dead were indeed listening. The miller murmured: *Four upstairs. One at the coal chute outside. Young one, nervous, keeps shifting his weight.* And the drowned woman from the ferry crossing added, in her endless underwater voice: *They have a dog.*

"A dog," I breathed.

"What?"

"Nothing. Wonderful news, actually."

Above us, Veyra's voice came again, muffled but distinct, in that tone that makes interrogation sound like hospitality: "—only asking because the wren appears in an old case file, madam. A carver named Iversen. Mother deceased, son unaccounted for. The sketches match his build. So you understand my difficulty. Either you're sheltering him, or someone taught him your knock, and either way I'd very much like to sit down and finish this conversation."

Silence. Sela's voice, too low to parse, doing what fences do — buying seconds with words.

Then Astrid's grip tightened on my sleeve to the point of pain.

"Cyrus," she whispered, barely air. "Look."

I couldn't see anything. Then I realized what she meant: the thin line of grey light under the cellar's street-door — the coal chute's far end, where it opened into the alley — was flickering. Orange. Growing.

Smoke. Or firelight. Or torches, many of them, moving up the alley.

And beneath the boots overhead, beneath Veyra's patient voice, another sound was building — distant, then less distant, then undeniable: a crowd. Hundreds of voices, thousands, rolling up from the market squares like a tide coming in over gravel. And threaded through the roar, chanted in ragged unison, words that made the hair on my arms stand up:

*Give us back the sky. Give us back the sky. GIVE US BACK THE SKY.*

*It's starting,* said the miller, almost gleeful, because the dead love a riot the way the living love a festival. *The granary square first. They're burning the tax rolls. Oh, this is going to spread.*

The boots overhead stopped. Even Veyra's patience had limits, and a city tearing itself apart outranked one fence and one ghost of a carver. Doors slammed. Boots thundered down the stairs — not to the cellar, past it, out the front, shouting orders: *To the granary! Form ranks! Protect the record halls!* Because that was Veyra, through and through — even hunting you, she'd stop to save the archives first.

The pressure above us lifted. And in the sudden quiet, Astrid turned to me. I couldn't see her face, but I could feel her thinking, that fierce machinery grinding behind her empty past.

"The chute," she said. "The guard at the chute — the nervous one. He'll have run to the fire. They all run to fires. That's what soldiers do."

"And the dog?"

"Dogs run to their handlers." A beat. "Probably."

"*Probably* is doing a lot of work in that sentence," I said, and heard her laugh — short, startled, real — for the first time since the night the stars went out. It did something to my chest I decided not to examine.

We climbed the chute in the dark, shoulders scraping coal dust, toward a line of orange light and a wall of screaming. At the top, Astrid paused, silhouetted against the alley's chaos — and for one strange moment the crowd noise seemed to bend around her, like a river around a stone, and I thought: *they're chanting at the sky, and she's the reason the sky is empty, and someday this exact crowd will figure that out.*

Then she hauled herself up and out, and reached back for my hand, and there wasn't time for prophecy.

Ashmarket burned its anger block by block, and we ran through the heart of it — past the granary square where tax rolls curled black in a bonfire, past a shrine to the stars where someone had smashed the altar and was weeping into the wreckage, past guards forming lines that bent and broke like the wards themselves. Nobody looked at us. Everybody was looking up, at the blank ceiling of the world, screaming at it to give something back.

At the river gate — unwatched, abandoned, its guards long gone to the fires — we slipped out of Ashmarket the way smoke leaves a house.

Only when the city was a glow behind us did Astrid stop, hands on knees, breathing hard, and look back.

"Sela bought us that," she said. "Whatever it costs her."

"Yes."

"And Veyra knows your name now. Your mother's name. The wren." She straightened, and in the light of the burning city her face was grim and steady. "She'll find the workshop, Cyrus. She'll find the mountain. Everything behind us is a trail now, and we're the ones leaving it."

"I know," I said.

Because I did know. And because, in my bones, faint as a heartbeat under wool, the dead were telling me something new — not warnings tonight, not gossip, but one word, repeated, from voices I'd never heard before. Voices from the old capital, far to the east, waking up after centuries of silence.

*Come home,* they said. *Come home. Come home.*

And beside me, Astrid shivered — and asked, very quietly, why I'd stopped walking.
