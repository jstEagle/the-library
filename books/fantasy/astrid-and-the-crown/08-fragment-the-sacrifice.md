## 8. Fragment: The Sacrifice

The second fragment came for me in a stable, which felt about right. My life had developed a pattern: every revelation arrived somewhere undignified.

We'd stopped at a waystation outside a village called Brindlemere — if the leaning sign was to be believed, and the sign had clearly been drinking. Cyrus negotiated with the stablemaster over hay and coin while I pretended to be his sister's quiet friend, which was the story we were running that week. Last week I'd been his cousin. Before that, his wife, an experiment we had both agreed never to speak of again after I laughed so hard at the word *husband* that a farmer's dog started barking.

I was mucking out nothing in particular, keeping my hands busy because idle hands gave the hollow too much room, when I touched the rail.

Not carved. Just wood, old and sweat-darkened, with a horse's teeth marks near the end. And underneath my palm, the world went white.

* * *

I am fourteen, and the throne room is cold in a way stone shouldn't be.

They've dressed me in white, which is funny, because everyone knows you bury people in white here. Mother chose the dress herself. She's smoothing the hem now, on her knees before me like a servant, like a penitent, and her hands are shaking and her face is doing something complicated that I will spend years trying not to remember.

"It doesn't hurt," she says. "That's what the bone-singers promise. The wards take you gently. You'll be asleep before you know you're—"

"Before I know I'm what, Mother? Say it. Say the word."

She doesn't say it. She never says it. In all the years of preparation — the special diet, the isolation, the tutors who taught me ward-lore instead of history because I wouldn't need history — no one ever once said the word *sacrifice*. They said *renewal*. They said *offering*. They said *honor*, said it so often the word wore smooth as a river stone.

Midwinter comes. The stars are bright as knife-points. I stand on the dais where coronations happen, wearing white, and Uncle Aldric reads from a scroll in a voice like warmed honey, and the high carver brings out the wardstone, and it is the size of a heart and humming like a hive.

And I understand, finally, standing there with the whole court watching, that they have spent fourteen years fattening me for this. That my mother held me at night and sang to me and was, all the while, singing a lamb toward slaughter. That she believed it was mercy. I can see it in her face — she believes it even now, even as I look at her, even as some animal part of me screams through my eyes at her — she believes this is the kindest thing anyone has ever done for the kingdom.

The wardstone touches my sternum.

It doesn't hurt. That's the worst part. It's warm. It's warm like sunlight, like a bath, like falling asleep in summer grass, and it begins to drink, and I feel myself going down into that warmth like going under water, and the last thing I think is: *no.*

Not *help.* Not *Mother.* Not any of the things a good dying princess thinks.

Just: *no.*

* * *

I came back to myself kneeling in straw with a pitchfork clattering somewhere behind me and Cyrus's hand on my shoulder saying my name — the fake one, the week's name, *Maren*, *Maren* — and I realized two things with terrible clarity.

First: I had been meant to die. Not might have been, not could have been. Was. Scheduled. Dressed for it. Sung to sleep for it by the woman whose lullabies I apparently still missed.

Second: I hadn't died. Which meant someone had stopped it, or I had stopped it, or —

No. That wasn't right either. The memory ended at *no*, but the fragment didn't feel finished. It felt like a door opened onto a landing, with more stairs above. There was more. I could feel the shape of the missing part the way you feel a missing tooth with your tongue.

"Astrid." Not Maren. He'd dropped the cover name, which meant I looked bad enough to warrant it. "You're white as bone. Sit down before you fall down."

"I'm fine." I sat down. On a hay bale, in a stable, in a body that belonged to a dead girl who had gotten better at lying since she was fourteen.

Cyrus crouched in front of me, and I watched him decide whether to ask. He had a face built for deciding things slowly — heavy-browed, patient, the kind of face that made you want to confess just to fill the silence. His gift made it worse. I'd seen him listen to the dead the way other men listened to weather, and I knew he could probably hear whatever the stable's old bones remembered, and I found myself fiercely glad horses didn't die dramatically enough to leave articulate ghosts.

"The fragment?" he asked.

"How did you know?"

"Because you touched the rail and then tried to be somewhere else entirely. I've carried enough voices to know the look." He paused. "Do you want to tell me?"

Here is what I should have said: *I was to be sacrificed at midwinter. The wards were fed with me. My mother dressed me in my burial clothes and called it honor.*

Here is what I actually said: "Nothing important. Court stuff. A birthday."

He looked at me for a long moment. I held his gaze the way I'd learned to hold it — steadily, warmly, with exactly the right amount of wounded openness. I was getting frighteningly good at this. Fourteen years of being raised as an offering had taught me how to be looked at without being seen.

"Right," he said finally, and stood, and let it go, and I hated him a little for being kind about it, and hated myself considerably more.

* * *

That night we slept in the stable loft because the waystation's rooms were full of refugees fleeing south, and refugees snored, and Cyrus claimed the loft smelled better, which told me everything about the rooms.

I lay awake listening to him breathe and turning the fragment over and over like a coin I couldn't spend.

Because here was the thing I hadn't said, the thing I was holding under my tongue like a hot stone:

The wards hadn't failed on their own.

I had broken them.

I didn't have the memory of it yet — that was still up those stairs, in the locked room above the landing — but I could do arithmetic now, and the sums were ugly. The wards fell the same night I ran. The wards were anchored to a living soul, and Cyrus's bones whispered that the soul was mine. A girl scheduled for sacrifice at midwinter does not simply wander off. Either someone stole her — or she tore the anchor out herself and ran, dragging the sky dark behind her like a torn curtain.

Every starless night since. Every refugee on the road. Every village whispering about curses and kings and the end of the world. Every farmer staring up at a black ceiling where the stars used to be, wondering what they'd done wrong.

Me. Probably me. Almost certainly me.

And the truly poisonous part — the part that curdled as I lay there — was that I understood why. I could feel the ghost of the reasoning in my own bones, the way you feel an old break when weather comes. *They were going to kill me and call it kindness, and no one, not one person in that whole shining court, said stop.* Of course I'd run. Any sane creature runs from the altar.

But the wards. Did I know, when I broke them? Had I known what tearing loose would do to the sky, to the borders, to the thousand small magics that kept Thornfell's edges whole? Or had I just pulled, blind and desperate, and let the consequences land on everyone but me?

I thought of the flooded tunnels, of Astrid — of *I* — hauling Cyrus out of black water on reflexes I couldn't explain. Reflexes of a girl trained for something. Trained for sacrifice, maybe. Trained to be useful right up until the moment of use.

"You're thinking loudly," Cyrus said from across the loft.

"I'm asleep."

"You're the loudest sleeper I've ever heard." A pause. Hay rustled. "You don't have to tell me the fragments. I want you to know that. They're yours. Whatever they are."

*Whatever they are.* If only he knew how much weight that *whatever* was carrying.

"They're yours too, though, aren't they?" I said, because cruelty at 2 a.m. is easier than honesty. "You're the one carrying the dead. You're the one who heard the wards scream. If I'm the reason the stars went out—"

"If," he said. "You don't know that."

"Don't I?"

"No," he said, and his voice had that flat certainty I was learning to distrust. "You know a shape. Shapes lie. I've spent thirty years listening to the dead tell stories about themselves, and half of them get their own deaths wrong." He rolled over. I heard the hay settle. "Get some sleep, Astrid."

My real name, in the dark, where no one could hear. He'd started doing that a few days ago — using it only when we were alone, like it was something fragile he was keeping safe for me. It should have moved me. It did move me. That was the problem. Every kindness he handed me made the secret heavier.

Because he thought he was smuggling an innocent. A victim. A princess stolen from her own life, and Cyrus Iversen, who had spent his whole existence hiding his gift from people who would burn him for it, had risked his neck out of guilt and decency for a girl he believed deserved saving.

What happened to that belief when he learned the stars went out because I pulled them down?

* * *

Three days later, at a shrine so ruined even the ghosts had left, I found the second fragment waiting — or it found me. I touched the base of a shattered statue, some forgotten god with its face worn smooth, and the white came again.

This time the stairs went higher.

*I am seventeen, five nights before midwinter, and I have a knife I stole from a guard who was kind to me, which I regret, because I liked him.*

*I am in the ward-vault beneath the chapel, where they brought me for the final attunement, and the carver — old Sorrel, with her ink-stained fingers — has just told me, gently, the way you tell a child about weather, that after midwinter I will not hurt anymore. Ever. Because I will not be.*

*And I am smiling at her. I have been taught to smile beautifully. And inside, behind the smile, I am doing something I have been secretly practicing for two years, in the dark, in the hours when they think I'm sleeping: I am reaching into the ward-thread with everything I am.*

*They made me the anchor. They spent my whole childhood weaving me into it, teaching me its language, tuning me like an instrument. They never once considered what an instrument does if it refuses the song.*

*I pull.*

*The sound is not a sound. The vault goes white — different white than the warmth of the sacrifice-dream, a hard white, a breaking white — and Sorrel is screaming, and somewhere far above me I feel the border-wards shatter like frost off a window, all along the mountains, all at once, and the stars go out like snuffed candles, one by one by one, and I am running.*

*I am running and I am laughing and I am crying and I do not know which of the three is true, and behind me the kingdom I was born to save falls into the dark I made.*

*Good, says some voice in me that I don't recognize. Let it.*

*Let it burn.*

* * *

I surfaced gasping against the statue's base, and Cyrus was three feet away sharpening a knife, and he looked up, and I smiled at him.

I smiled at him the way I'd smiled at Sorrel, and I despised myself for it, and I did it anyway.

"Fragment?" he asked.

"Mm. More court stuff." I flexed my fingers. They weren't shaking. I'd trained them not to. "Birthdays are dull."

He studied me a moment longer than I liked. Then he went back to his knife, and I sat with my back against the ruined god and stared at the starless sky through the broken roof, and I did the arithmetic again, slower this time.

Five nights before midwinter. That's when I'd run. Which meant midwinter was coming again — the wheel turning, the anniversary arriving — and if the wards needed renewing at midwinter, and the wards were broken, and the crown wanted me found...

They didn't want me rescued. I'd suspected it since the hunters' sketches, since Veyra's too-careful questions in Ashmarket. But now I knew the shape of it. A broken ward needs a new anchor. An uncle who arranged one sacrifice would arrange another. And this time there'd be no warning, no kindly carver, no stolen knife — because this time I wouldn't see it coming. This time I didn't even know who I was.

Except I was starting to.

And there it was, the choice that sat in my chest like a swallowed coal: tell him, or don't.

Tell Cyrus that the girl he was protecting was the disaster he was protecting her from. Watch the guilt in his eyes turn to something else. Lose the one person in the world who knew my name.

Or carry it alone. Keep smiling. Keep letting him believe in a version of me that had maybe never existed — because that was the other thing the fragment had shown me, wasn't it, the thing I kept circling like a tongue around the missing tooth:

*Let it burn,* said a voice I didn't recognize.

Whose voice was that? Mine? Or something else that had been riding me even then?

"Cyrus," I said.

He looked up.

"Which way to Halvard from here?" I asked. "The bone-singer. How much farther?"

"Four days, if the roads hold. Five if they don't." He tilted his head. "Why?"

"Because," I said, and the lie came out smooth as river stone, "I want my memories back. All of them. Before midwinter."

He nodded slowly, and something in his face — relief, maybe, or hope — made the coal in my chest burn hotter.

He thought I wanted my past so I could be whole.

He had no idea I wanted it so I could find out what else I'd destroyed — and whether, given the choice again, I'd do it twice.
