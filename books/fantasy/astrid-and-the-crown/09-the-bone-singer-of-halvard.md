## 9. The Bone-Singer of Halvard

Halvard smelled like a place that had given up on perfume and settled for woodsmoke and wet wool. The town sat in a bowl of hills where the river slowed to think, and its streets were narrow enough that two strangers couldn't pass without negotiating. In the old days, before the stars went out and the world started eating itself, Halvard had been famous for three things: eel pies, a bell tower that leaned like a drunk uncle, and the bone-singer.

The first two were still there. I was counting on the third.

"Remind me," Astrid said, walking half a step behind me the way she'd learned to, the way that said *I trust you to pick the road but I reserve the right to judge it*, "what exactly a bone-singer does. And why we're trusting her with my head."

"She sings to bones. Reads them. Some say she can mend what's carved into them, or uncarve it." I ducked under a laundry line strung between two houses, because the alternative was wearing a stranger's wet stockings as a hat. "Your memories weren't just taken, Astrid. They were cut. Carved away. And carving leaves a mark, even in flesh. If anyone can read the mark, it's her."

"And if she can't?"

"Then we've walked a long way for eel pies."

She made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh. She'd been making more of those lately — not-quite-laughs, almost-smiles, the ghost of a person assembling herself from spare parts. I tried not to think about how much I'd come to look forward to them. Thinking about it felt like picking up a coin off a dead man's eyes.

The dead, for their part, had been quiet since the shrine. Too quiet. After the ghost-king's bargain — after Astrid took that first fragment and came out of it white as tallow, whispering about a throne room and blood — the voices in my bones had drawn back like a crowd making room for something important. I didn't like it. A quiet grave is either at peace or about to open.

The bone-singer's house was the last one before the river took over, a crooked little thing with a door painted the blue of a sky none of us had seen in weeks. No sign, no bell. Just a wind chime made of finger bones, which in Halvard apparently counted as a *welcome* rather than a *warning*.

I knocked. The door opened before my knuckles finished the job.

The woman in the doorway was old the way rivers are old — not frail, just patient. Her eyes were the problem. Not blind the way a blindfold is blind; blind the way a stone is blind, milky and settled, aimed slightly past my left shoulder as though something more interesting were standing there.

"You knock," she said, "like a man who's been taught doors are dangerous."

"Most doors are."

"Mm." She turned her head, and I felt the weight of her attention pass over Astrid like a hand over a flame. "And you brought me a girl with a hole in her where a life should be. Come in, then. Mind the cat. She bites, and she's earned the right."

The inside of the house was every bone-carver's nightmare and every bone-carver's dream. Shelves of carved pieces ran floor to ceiling — not the crude charms peddlers sold at markets, but work so fine it looked grown rather than made. Birds with spread wings no bigger than a thumbnail. A procession of dancers circling a rib. And everywhere, hanging from rafters and doorknobs and the cat's basket, wind chimes. Dozens of them. The house never stopped singing.

The cat was a gray lump of malice on the hearthstone who looked at Astrid, looked at me, and decided with visible effort not to bite either of us. I respected that kind of restraint.

"You're Iversen," the old woman said. It wasn't a question.

"You know me?"

"I knew your mother." She lowered herself into a chair by the fire, joints cracking like green wood. "She came through here twice. The second time she was carrying you, and you screamed the whole visit, and she said, *He hears the chimes, Marta. He hears what's in them.*" Her blind eyes found me with unsettling accuracy. "Sit down, boy. You look like a man carrying furniture."

Astrid glanced at me — a quick, sharp glance, the kind she'd been giving me more often since the blizzard, since I'd told her the truth about knowing her name from the first night. We were past the worst of that wound but not past the scar. She sat on the bench by the wall. I sat where the fire could see me.

"I'm Marta," the old woman said. "And you're here for her, not for you. That's the right order. But I'll tell you now what I tell everyone: I don't fix people. I read them. What they do with the reading is their own affair." Her head turned toward Astrid. "Give me your hand, girl."

Astrid gave it. Marta's fingers walked over her wrist, her palm, the fine bones of her fingers, with the unhurried certainty of a woman counting coins she already knew the total of. Her lips moved. I caught fragments of it — not words, exactly. A melody with grammar. The bone-song.

I'd heard my mother hum it once, when she thought I was asleep. I'd never heard anyone else do it and live to keep the hearing.

"Hm," Marta said, which was not encouraging.

"Hm?" Astrid said.

"Your bones are quiet. Not silent — quiet. Like a house where someone's gone through and packed everything away in boxes and put the boxes in a cellar and bricked up the door." Her thumb pressed the inside of Astrid's wrist. "But the brickwork's fresh in places. Cracked in others. Someone was in a hurry at the end." She tilted her head. "You broke something, girl. Before they took your memories, you broke something, and the taking was punishment, or the taking was mercy, and I'd wager my cat the carver wasn't sure which."

I felt Astrid go still beside me. I'd felt her go still like that twice before — once at the shrine, once on the mountain road when I'd said her name for the first time. It was the stillness of a woman standing on ice and hearing it talk.

"Can you open the door?" Astrid asked quietly. "The bricked-up one."

"Perhaps. Perhaps not. Perhaps I open it and what's behind it doesn't fit back in the house." Marta released her hand. "Memory isn't a wound, child. A wound wants to close. A memory wants to be *carried*. If I sing the brick loose, everything comes through at once, in no order, and you'll have to carry it from the first breath. Some people break under that. Some people break *open*, which is different, and better, but costs more." She sighed. "I'd need time. Days. And quiet. And Halvard has neither, lately. Have you looked at the sky?"

We had. Everyone had. The starless dark had been spreading for weeks now, a stain with an appetite, and the towns on the edge of it had started doing the things frightened towns do: hoarding, praying, blaming. Halvard's blame had settled, as blame usually does, on whoever was nearest and least able to argue.

"Three days," Marta said, before either of us asked. "I'll sing to her for three days. In the meantime—" and here her head swung back to me, and her blind eyes did the thing blind eyes do when they see far too much, "—in the meantime, you'll sit for me, carver. You'll give me your arm and you'll hold still, and you'll not like what I find."

* * *

I want it on the record that I offered my arm out of politeness. A man doesn't come into a bone-singer's house, eat her bread, and refuse her the one thing she asks. That's not manners; that's suicide with extra steps.

She took my wrist in both hands and went quiet for a long time. The fire ticked. The chimes sang their small bone chorus. The cat relocated to Astrid's lap, having apparently decided she was the least likely to retaliate.

Then Marta's hands started to shake.

It was a small thing at first, a tremor like a string plucked too hard. But it spread, up her wrists, into her shoulders, and her breathing went ragged, and I realized with a cold slide in my gut that the bone-singer of Halvard was frightened.

"Boy," she said. "How many?"

"How many what?"

"How many voices are you carrying?"

I didn't answer. There wasn't an answer that wasn't a confession.

"Because I count—" her fingers pressed down, hard, "—I count *layers*. Do you understand what I'm telling you? A man who carries one dead voice wears it like a coat. A man who carries ten wears a house. You—" she let go of my wrist as though it had burned her, "—you're not wearing them, boy. You're *built* of them. There's more of them in you than there is of you."

"That's dramatic," I said. My voice came out steady, which I counted as the day's one victory. "I feel fine."

"You feel *quiet*," she said. "That's what I'm telling you. Tell me I'm wrong. Tell me you still remember your mother's face."

The fire ticked. The chimes sang. I opened my mouth to say *of course*, and found the shelf where that memory lived, and found it — I want to be honest here, even now, even in this telling — I found it stocked with facts. Brown hair. A humming voice. Hands that carved. The word *mother* written on the outside of the box in my own handwriting.

But the face. When I reached for the face, I reached through it, like a man grabbing for a rope and closing his fist on fog.

"I remember she hummed," I said.

Marta closed her eyes. "And your name. Your whole name. Say it."

"Cyrus Iversen."

"Say your mother's."

Silence. The particular silence of a man standing in a room where a wall used to be.

"That's not the same as forgetting," I said finally. "That's just — the dead take up room. It's a big house. Things shift."

"Things don't shift, boy. Things get *displaced*." Marta leaned forward, and for the first time she looked her age, and older, like the last ring of a very old tree. "Every voice you take in takes a room. And the house doesn't grow. So the house starts eating its own walls. First the rooms you don't use — old songs, old faces, the name of the street you grew up on. Then the rooms you *do* use." She tapped my wrist, once, gently, the way you'd tap a wall to find the stud. "You're not dying fast, carver. You're dying the way a candle dies in a draft — so slow you call it burning. But the wax is going. There's less of you every year, and what's left is getting thinner, and one day — I can't say when, five years, ten, twenty — you'll sit down to carve and find there's no one left at the table to hold the knife."

Astrid's hand had stopped moving on the cat. She was looking at me with an expression I hadn't seen on her before, or maybe I had and it had been on a face I couldn't quite recall — my mother's, maybe, the day she'd caught me talking to the grave behind our house and hadn't asked me to stop.

"Why?" Astrid asked. Not to Marta. To me. "Why do you carry them? All of them. Why not let them go?"

And there it was. The question I'd spent thirty years not answering, asked by a girl with a hole in her head, in a house full of singing bones, while an old blind woman watched me with the patience of someone who already knew.

"Because someone has to," I said. "The dead don't get a door out of this world unless someone holds it open. My mother taught me that. She carried voices too. Fewer. She was careful." I laughed, and it came out wrong, thin as the fog where my mother's face should be. "I was careful too, once. Then the wards fell and the border dead came through in a flood, and I — I couldn't choose. You can't choose. They're all screaming and they're all someone's daughter, and so you take them, and you take them, and you tell yourself the house is big enough."

"The house is not big enough," Marta said.

"No," I said. "The house is not big enough."

* * *

They gave me the small back room, which had a bed, a window, and a view of the river going about its business with more sense than any of us. Astrid came in without knocking, which by then was a right she'd earned, and sat on the floor with her back against the bedframe, the way she did when she had something to say and wanted the dark to say it to.

"Three days," she said. "She'll sing to me for three days. And then I'll know." Her voice did the thing it did when it was carrying more than it wanted to — went flat, careful, like a woman walking on ice she'd already fallen through once. "I'll know what I did. What I broke. Why they took my head apart."

"Probably."

"And you'll know it too. Because I'll tell you. Because after the blizzard, after you told me the truth, that's the arrangement. No more silence." She was quiet a moment. "But you've been silent about yourself my whole — my whole *whatever this is*. You never said you were dying."

"I'm not dying. I'm thinning. There's a difference. One's a tragedy and one's a budget problem."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

"Cyrus." She said my name the way she'd learned to say it — carefully, like a word in a language she was still translating. "What she described. Forgetting your mother's face. Forgetting your own—" She stopped. Started again. "You've spent your whole life making room for the dead. Who makes room for you?"

I looked at the ceiling. The rafters were hung with chimes, and in the dark they sang so softly it was almost a whisper, almost the sound the dead make when they're not screaming, when they're just *there*, keeping a man company on the long walk toward the wall he built himself.

"No one," I said. "That was the arrangement. That was always the arrangement. I hold the door for them, and when I go through it, I go through it empty, and that's a fair trade. That's a *good* trade. I never wanted—" and I stopped, because the next word was *more*, and I had never once in my life let myself say that word aloud, because wanting more is how you end up grieving, and I had seen what grief did to the dead, and I had decided at fifteen that I would skip that particular department entirely.

"You never wanted," Astrid finished for me, "what you couldn't keep."

"Something like that."

"Well." I heard her shift, settling her shoulders against the bedframe, making herself comfortable the way soldiers do, the way people do when they've decided a fight is happening whether they like it or not. "That's the stupidest arrangement I've ever heard, and I once arranged a marriage I can't remember. So here's the new one. Three days, Marta sings to me, and I get my head back — all of it, the good and the ugly. And when I know what I did, you're going to sit down, and I'm going to read it to you. Every piece. Because someone has to hold the door for *you*, Cyrus, and I've decided it's me, and I don't recall asking your permission."

I should have argued. A wiser man would have argued — pointed out that she might not like what she remembered, that she might not like *me*, that the arrangement of my life had kept exactly one person safe from the flood and I'd grown fond of the arrangement.

Instead I lay in the dark in a house full of singing bones and listened to her breathe, and thought: *this is what it feels like, then. This is what the ones I carry lost. This is the room the dead remember.*

On the second morning, Marta's singing stopped mid-verse.

I came out of the back room with my knives already in my hand, because a bone-singer going silent is a sound like a heart stopping. She was standing at her front window, hands pressed to the frame, her blind face aimed at the eastern hills where the starless dark had been sitting like a patient beast for a week.

"It moved," she said. "The dark. It's not spreading anymore. It's *coming*. And girl—" her head turned toward Astrid, and her voice dropped to something I hadn't heard from her yet, something old and afraid, "—whatever's behind your bricked-up door, it knows you're about to open it. Something is coming to Halvard to stop it. I can feel it in the floor. In the *bones of the town*."

Outside, the eel-pie sellers were packing up their stalls. Somewhere down the street, a child was crying, and the leaning bell tower began to ring — not the hour, not a warning, just ringing, the way a town rings when it has nothing else to say.

Astrid looked at me. Whatever was in her face, it wasn't fear.

"Then we'd better open the door first," she said.
