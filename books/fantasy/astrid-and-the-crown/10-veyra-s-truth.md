## 10. Veyra's Truth

The cell smelled of wet straw and old lies, which was, in my experience, the standard perfume of every interrogation room in the kingdom. I had sat in a hundred of them. I had sat on both sides of the table. The chair never got more comfortable either way.

The smuggler's name was Doss. I knew that because his partner had given it up in under a minute, the way a rotten tooth gives up its socket — with a sound of relief and a little blood. Doss himself was made of sterner stuff, which is to say he was made of greed, and greed holds its shape longer than courage does.

"Captain Veyra," he said, when I came in with the lamp. "Heard you were in the valley. Heard the stars went out over Thornfell and the whole border came down like a dropped plate."

"Heard a lot, have you?"

"I hear things." He shrugged against his chains. "It's a living."

"It's a hanging, more often." I set the lamp on the table between us and sat, letting the silence do the first hour of work for me. Silence is the best interrogator I ever employed. Never asked for wages, never got bored, never believed its own cleverness.

Doss lasted eleven minutes. It would have been a personal record for him if I'd been keeping one, and I was.

* * *

Let me tell you what I knew when I rode down out of the hills with six of my best and two prisoners in the wagon. I knew the princess Astrid had vanished from Glasshall three nights before the stars went out. I knew the king — her father, may he rest, though the gods know he didn't — had been dead eight months, and that his brother Aldric held the regency with the comfortable grip of a man who has decided the chair is his. I knew my orders had come sealed with Aldric's mark, not the council's, and I had told myself that meant nothing, because a soldier who starts asking why about her orders ends up asking why about everything, and then she's no use to anyone.

I told myself a lot of things that winter. I was very good at it. It's a soldier's second trade.

The orders said *recover the princess alive*. Those words, in that order. I had read them a dozen times on the road, by lamplight, by the grey light of a starless dawn, by the green flicker of the ward-fires that were all the sky would give us now. *Alive.* It was the one mercy in the whole business, I thought. Whatever else the regent wanted — and regents always want — at least the girl would come home breathing.

Doss disabused me of that somewhere between the eleventh minute and the twelfth.

* * *

"It wasn't a rescue," he said. His voice had gone flat, the way voices do when a man stops performing and starts confessing. "The man who hired us — the one with the signet — he wasn't paying for her back. He was paying for her gone."

"Speak plainly."

"I am speaking plainly, Captain, and I'd thank you not to interrupt, because I've been building up to this for eleven minutes and my nerve is a finite resource." He licked his lips. "The princess was to die on the road. Made to look like bandits, or like the border collapse got her, or like she drowned in her own bath for all I cared — the how was mine to choose. The *that* was his. Alive was never in the contract. The word on the paper was *recovery*, and the man with the signet laughed when I asked what we were recovering her *to*, and he said, 'The kingdom's peace,' like it was a joke we were both in on."

I kept my face still. Twenty years of practice. Inside, something shifted its weight, the way ice does on a lake before it decides.

"Describe the signet."

"Black boar on a field of ash. I only saw it once, when he paid the first half." Doss's eyes flicked to mine and away. "You know whose it is, Captain. Everyone knows whose it is. I'm just the fool who said it out loud in a room with you."

The black boar of Aldric. I had carried that seal on orders I'd sworn to. I had slept under it.

"Who was the man?"

"A clerk. Grey, soft, hands like a man who's never held anything heavier than a pen. He counted out crown silver like it offended him." Doss paused. "There was a second payment promised. Half on delivery of proof. Proof being—" he made a small, apologetic gesture with his chained hands, "—her head, Captain. Or her ring, if the head was inconvenient. The clerk said the ring would do. Said the regent wasn't sentimental."

I stood up. I don't remember deciding to. My body made the choice the way a horse throws a rider — some instinct older than discipline deciding the ground itself had turned false.

"Captain," Doss said, and for the first time the smuggler sounded almost kind. "You didn't know. I could tell the moment you walked in. You've got the face of a woman who read her orders and believed them." He shrugged. "That used to be me. Believing's the expensive habit. Everything else is free."

I took the lamp and left him with the dark. It seemed fair. He'd given me his.

* * *

I did not sleep. I sat in the command tent with the second prisoner's confession spread out beside Doss's, and I read them both until the words wore grooves in me. The second man had known less, but what he knew agreed: the contract, the signet, the *proof of death*. Two liars can be caught out by their disagreements. Two men telling the same truth are caught out by their agreements, and these two had never met before I put them in the same wagon, and their stories matched like a lock and its key.

I thought about the sketches. I had them made myself — the princess's face copied from the palace portrait, distributed to every garrison and toll-house from Thornfell to the coast. *Missing. Possibly confused or injured. Reward for safe return.* I had written the words *safe return* with my own hand. I had been the honest face of a lie, and the lie had my handwriting on it.

And I thought about the girl herself. Eighteen years old. Mother dead of the wasting fever, father dead eight months, and the uncle who stepped into the regency like a man stepping into warm water — and the first thing that warm water did was put a price on her head and dress the price up as a rescue.

There was a version of this where I did nothing. I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the only thing I had left by morning. There was a version where I burned the confessions, rode back out, found the girl, and delivered her to Glasshall alive, and told myself the clerk had been corrupt, the contract a forgery, the signet stolen. There is always a version where the kingdom is what the oaths say it is. I had lived in that version for twenty years. It had good light and clean floors and it was built on the same foundation as every other beautiful lie: that someone above me was paying attention.

But I had seen the ward-fires burning green and wrong. I had seen the sky with no stars in it, and I had watched my own soldiers stop looking up because looking up had become a kind of grief. The kingdom was unraveling, and the regent's answer was to spend silver on a girl's death while the heavens went dark over his head.

A soldier can serve a crown. A soldier can even serve a bad king, if the badness is ordinary — greed, vanity, a taste for bad counsel. Those are weather. You wait them out.

But this wasn't weather. This was a man selling the roof off the house while the rain came in.

* * *

Sergeant Brakka found me at dawn. She always found me at dawn; it was her gift, like other people's gifts for music or arithmetic. She put a cup of the garrison's terrible coffee in my hands and stood there, because Brakka never sat in my tent uninvited, a courtesy I had stopped noticing years ago and noticed now, sharply, the way you notice a floor when it tilts.

"You read the confessions," she said.

"I read the confessions."

"And?"

"And I'm deciding what I am this morning."

She looked at me for a long moment. Brakka's face is a landscape that has been farmed hard for a long time — nothing wasted, nothing soft, everything in use. "You know what I did before the army?" she said.

"Smuggled. You have never once let me forget it."

"Smuggled," she agreed. "And you know what I learned? A border's just a line some king drew. But a lie you carry — that's a border too. It's a line through you. And every year you carry it, the line gets heavier, until one day you set the load down or it sets you down." She nodded at the confessions. "That's a heavy one, Captain."

"I swore an oath to the crown, Brakka."

"You swore an oath to the *kingdom*. I was there. I heard it. It was a long speech and your knees cracked when you knelt." She took the empty cup out of my hands, which was either insubordination or kindness, and with Brakka it was always both. "The crown's a hat. The kingdom's the people under it. You get to choose which one you meant."

She left me with that. Sergeants should not be wiser than captains. It makes for poor discipline and excellent armies.

* * *

By mid-morning I had made three decisions, and I want to record them in order, because the order matters, because each one made the next possible, the way the first crack in ice makes all the others.

First: the prisoners would live. Not because they deserved it — Doss had sold worse than his silence in his time, and I knew it — but because killing them was the regent's solution to problems, and I was no longer in the business of doing his work for free.

Second: the sketches would come down. Every garrison, every toll-house, every market post from here to the coast. I wrote the order myself: *Description withdrawn. Subject's status under review.* Bureaucratic language is a wonderful thing. It can carry a mutiny inside it and no one notices until the mutiny is already dressed and armed.

Third — and this is the one I sat with longest, the one I wrote and rewrote and finally wrote plain: I would find her first. Before the regent's other hunters, before the bounty-men who would come sniffing once the word got out that the princess was loose in a starless country. I would find her, and then I would decide — with her in front of me, a person and not a paper — what the kingdom owed her and what she owed it.

It is not desertion. I want that on the record, even if the record is only this, even if no one ever reads it. Desertion is leaving your post. I was keeping mine. It was the post that had moved.

* * *

We rode out at noon, and I will admit the sky got to me on the road. You cannot understand what a starless night does to a country until you've ridden through one. The dark isn't empty. It's *full* — full of the things the stars used to keep honest. Every shadow had a suggestion in it. Every crossroads felt like a decision someone else had already made. My veterans, men and women who had stood shield-line at Colder Ford without a flicker, kept their hands on their sword-hilts and their eyes on the treeline and said nothing, and the nothing was louder than any fear I'd ever heard voiced.

And in the middle of all that dark, one girl, eighteen years old, with a price on her head and her own family's signet on the contract.

I kept thinking about the portrait they'd copied for the sketches. I'd stood in the archive while the artist worked, and I remembered thinking she had her mother's chin and a look in her eyes I couldn't name at the time — some quality of a person photographed at the edge of a decision. I know the name for it now. It was the look of someone who has already counted the exits.

I'd assumed it was the artist's flattery. Portraits always flatter. They give sitters courage they never had.

I was beginning to suspect the portrait had been honest and the palace hadn't.

* * *

It was near dusk when the rider caught us — one of the garrison's runners, blowing hard, holding a dispatch case with the black boar pressed into the wax. I broke the seal myself, in front of my officers, because whatever was coming, I wanted witnesses.

*Captain Veyra. The recovery operation is concluded by other means. Withdraw to Glasshall. All prior orders rescinded. Acknowledge.*

*Concluded by other means.*

I read it three times. The words didn't change. They never do; that's the horror of them. Words are the most loyal things in the world — they stay exactly what they are while everything around them rots.

"He knows," Brakka said quietly, at my shoulder. "About the confessions."

"Or he's cleaning up. Either way, he wants me in Glasshall, inside the walls, inside his reach." I looked at the dispatch, at the tidy, clerkish hand. Grey, soft hands like a man who'd never held anything heavier than a pen. Somewhere, that man was counting out more silver. "If I acknowledge, I'm a good soldier walking into a locked room. If I don't acknowledge, I'm a rogue captain with six soldiers and a warrant that will follow me the rest of my life."

"Which are you?"

I looked up at the sky, where the stars should have been, where for three weeks there had been nothing but a smooth black nothing, like a page with the writing scraped off. Somewhere under that nothing, a girl was running, and the whole country between her and Glasshall was filling up with men like me — men with orders, men with sketches, men who believed the words on the paper because believing was cheaper than the alternative.

"Brakka," I said, "burn the dispatch."

She didn't ask twice. That's why she was my sergeant.

I turned my horse south, toward the hills, toward the last place the princess had been seen, toward whatever I was becoming. Behind me, Glasshall sat under its green ward-fires like a jewel in a dead man's fist. Ahead of me, the dark was full.

I had spent twenty years believing that someone above me was paying attention.

It was my turn.

* * *

We made the valley in two days, and I knew we were close before any scout told me so, because the dead birds started. Small things at first — a crow on a fencepost with its head turned wrong. Then a whole roost, silent, every bird facing the same direction, toward the old smuggler roads through the hills. My soldiers wouldn't look at them. I looked. I had the feeling — the cold, certain feeling a soldier gets before an ambush, except stretched over a whole landscape — that the country itself was pointing at something.

The local priest in the last village would tell us nothing and knew everything; I could see the knowledge sitting behind his eyes like a fish under ice. But his altar-cloth was new, and the wax on his altar was fresh, and someone had been paying him to pray for a traveler's safety. I left silver for the poor box and a question he didn't answer, and as I turned to go, he said, very quietly, not looking at me:

"The carver, Captain. They say the carver hears the dead. They say the dead have been *loud* lately."

I rode out of that village with the priest's words in my ears and my hand on my sword, and I thought: *a carver who hears the dead.* In a sane country I'd have laughed.

But I had read a confession that said the regent wanted a princess's ring as proof of death, and I had watched the stars go out, and I had burned a royal dispatch with my own hands. Sanity had left the kingdom some weeks ago. We were all just finding our way around in the dark without it.

And somewhere ahead, in the hills where the birds pointed, a carver and a girl without a past were running from men with my face on their orders — and I was about to teach those men what happens when the orders stop being mine.
