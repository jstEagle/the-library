## 11. Midwinter Crossing

### CYRUS

There are three things a smuggler learns before he learns anything else, and I learned all of them from my mother before I was tall enough to reach the top shelf of her own workshop. One: never cross a mountain in winter if you can cross it in autumn. Two: if you must cross in winter, cross before the storms, not after. Three: when the sky has no stars in it and hasn't for two months, ignore rules one and two entirely, because the roads below are crawling with men who get paid by the sketch.

So that is how Astrid and I came to be standing at the mouth of the Halvard Pass on the last day of autumn's corpse, arguing with a mule.

The mule was winning.

"She doesn't like the smell of you," Astrid said. She had her hood down despite the wind, which was either bravery or a symptom. Her breath came out in little ghosts, and even those looked like they were trying to remember something. "Honestly, I don't blame her. You've been talking to your knives since dawn."

"I was *consulting* my knives."

"You were asking a spoon about the weather."

"The spoon is dead, Astrid. It has opinions." I gave the mule's lead another experimental tug. The mule, whose name was Turnip because I had bought her off a man who named all his animals after vegetables he disliked, planted her hooves with the immovability of a creature that had read rule one and taken it to heart. "The pass closes tomorrow. Maybe tonight. After that we're walking around Halvard through the lowlands, and the lowlands are where Veyra's hunters are burning every bridge they can't search."

"And the pass is where we freeze to death."

"Freezing is honest work," I said. "Hunters are politics."

She almost smiled. That was the thing about her I still hadn't gotten used to — how the smile arrived sideways, like she'd caught it doing something it shouldn't. Two months on the road and she had learned to laugh quietly, sleep in ditches, haggle like a fishwife, and lie with a straight face, which should have made me proud and instead made me feel like a man teaching his own executioner to hold the axe properly.

Because here is what I had not told her, in two months of ditches and haggling and dead spoons: I knew exactly who she was. Had known since the first night, when the wardstone hummed against my bones like a struck bell and the dead in my head went silent as a held breath. *The anchor*, they'd whispered. *The anchor of Thornfell.* And later, when the sketches started appearing on tollhouse doors — a girl with a strong jaw and eyes the color of a frozen lake — I had looked at her sleeping face across a dying fire and understood that I was not smuggling a refugee.

I was smuggling the reason the stars went out.

Or the cure for it. The dead couldn't agree. The dead rarely do; ask three corpses a question and you'll get four answers and a feud.

"Turnip's not moving," Astrid said, "and neither am I, until you tell me what the bones are saying right now. You've got that face."

"What face?"

"The one where you look like a man chewing on a sentence he can't swallow."

I looked up at the pass. The clouds above Halvard were the wrong color — a bruised, greenish slate, the color the sky had been the night the wards shattered, and I did not need a dead spoon to tell me what that meant. The storm coming over that ridge wasn't weather. It was weather with an opinion. Everything had opinions now. The dark had been sitting on the world for two months like a lid on a pot, and the whole country underneath was starting to boil — lords raising banners, villages burning each other's shrines, refugees on the roads muttering that the crown had broken the sky on purpose, or that the old kings were coming back, or both.

"We cross tonight," I said. "Ahead of the storm. It's twelve hours to the shrine on the far side, and if we're lucky—"

"If we're lucky," she repeated. "Cyrus. When have we ever been lucky?"

"There was that time in Ashmarket."

"We were nearly trampled by a riot."

"But we weren't," I said. "That's luck. Luck is just disaster with bad aim."

She stared at me for a long moment, and then she pulled her hood up, took the mule's lead out of my hand, and said something to Turnip in a low voice that I couldn't hear — and the mule moved. Just like that. Walked straight into the pass like she'd been waiting for permission from someone worth obeying.

I decided not to take that personally. I failed.

* * *

The pass was beautiful for the first hour, which is the pass's way of luring you in. Old pines wearing snow like borrowed finery, the walls of the gorge rising on either side, blue ice hanging from the cliffs like the chandeliers of some drowned palace. The dead liked it up there. Mountain dead are quieter than valley dead — fewer grudges per cubic foot — and for a while the voices in my bones were just a murmur, travelers' chatter, the long slow gossip of people who had died on this road centuries ago and never quite finished the conversation.

Then the light went.

Not dusk — the light simply left, all at once, like a purse snatched off a belt, and the wind came down the gorge with a sound I felt in my teeth before I heard it. Not a howl. A *shriek*. The storm had come down off the ridge hours early, and it had brought friends.

"Astrid!" I shouted, and the wind ate the word. I shouted again. Snow hit sideways, hard enough to sting, white on white until the world was nothing but motion. I found the mule by feel, found the rope, found — thank every dead soul I carry — her hand, gloved and small and gripping mine hard enough to crack knuckles.

We walked. There is no other word for it. We put one foot in front of the other in a white screaming void, and I navigated by the dead, which sounds poetic and is actually just a matter of listening for the voices who died on this road and asking them, politely, which way the shrine lies. Most of them were happy to help. One of them kept insisting the road curved left when it didn't, out of pure spite, and I told him so, and Astrid — shouting over the wind — demanded to know who I was insulting.

"A dead man named Ottar! Died in 612! He's been giving bad directions for three hundred years!"

"Tell him—" she began, and then the ground disappeared.

I felt it more than saw it: her hand ripped out of mine, the rope going slack, the particular horrible lightness in the air where a person used to be. The storm had found the drift edge, the cornice over the old quarry cut, and it had eaten the path out from under her.

I threw myself flat. My hands found rope. The rope was taut and humming and attached to a mule who, I will say this for Turnip until the day I die, dug in like she'd been born for it. Below me there was nothing but white noise and white snow, and somewhere down there, thirty feet down a cliff of ice, was a girl without a name, holding onto the other end of herself.

"Cyrus!" Her voice came up thin, shredded. "The rope — I can't — my hands—"

"I've got you." I wrapped the rope twice around my forearm and leaned back, boots braced against a rock that may or may not have been there. "Turnip's got you. Nobody lets go. Astrid, listen to me — can you find a foothold?"

"No. It's ice. It's all ice, I can't feel my—"

Her voice did something I'd never heard it do. It stopped being afraid and became calm, which was worse. "If I fall," she called up, "don't come after me. You'll need the rope for yourself."

"Don't be stupid."

"I'm being *practical*—"

"You are being the single most annoying person I have ever carried in my skull alongside forty generations of the deceased," I shouted, hauling hand over hand, "and if you let go of that rope I will carve your stubbornness into bone so that every carver after me knows exactly what I died of. Now *climb*, Your Highness."

The words were out before I could catch them. They went into the wind like sparks into straw.

The rope went still.

For three heartbeats, in the middle of the worst storm of the year, on the edge of a cliff, everything stopped — the wind, the climbing, my heart. Then her voice came up the rope, quiet and clear and cold as the ice under her boots:

"*What did you just call me?*"

* * *

### ASTRID

He said it like it slipped out. People say things like that — slip them out — when they think you won't notice, or when the moment is loud enough to hide in. But I had spent two months learning to hear what Cyrus Iversen didn't say, because he said so much of it. He lied beautifully, my carver. He lied about knowing the way, about having food, about the dead liking me. But he always, always told the truth with his hands, and his hands had gone rigid on the rope the instant the word left his mouth.

*Your Highness.*

I climbed. Of course I climbed. Fear makes an excellent ladder when the alternative is a ravine. Hand over hand, boot against ice, the rope burning through my gloves, while above me the shape of him leaned back against the storm like a man arguing with the mountain itself. And the whole time I climbed, I thought: *he knew. He has known. Since when?*

The answer was already there, waiting, the way answers are. Since the first night. Since the wardstone hummed. Since the dead went quiet around me in that particular way they only went quiet around me, which I had noticed and filed away and never once asked him about, because asking felt like opening a door in a house with no floors.

I got my knee over the lip. His hand closed on my collar and hauled, and then I was lying on the snow beside a mule, gasping, alive, furious.

We found the lee side of the quarry wall — a shallow overhang where the wind couldn't quite reach us — and got a fire going out of wet wood and stubbornness. Cyrus built fires the way other men apologized: carefully, thoroughly, and with visible relief when they caught. He wouldn't look at me. He fed sticks to the flames like each one was a confession he was postponing.

"Say it," I said.

"Astrid—"

"Not that name. The other one. Say the word you said on the rope." My voice came out steadier than I felt. Inside, I was shaking, and not from the cold. "Say it, or I swear I'll walk back out into that storm and take my chances with the ravine."

He looked at me across the fire. Snowmelt dripped from his hood. His face, which usually did such good work looking mild and slightly apologetic, had given up entirely.

"Astrid Veyane," he said. "Daughter of Queen Maren. Princess of the Thornfell line. Heir to a crown that wants you dead." He exhaled. "I've known since the first night. The wardstone sang against my bones, and the dead — they went silent, and silence, from the dead, is a kind of kneeling. They know royalty when they hear it. Even carved-out royalty. Even *you*."

The fire popped. Somewhere above us the storm screamed past the overhang like a thing cheated.

"Two months," I said.

"Yes."

"Every mile. Every ditch. Every time I asked you who I might be — every time I asked you *please, Cyrus, tell me what the bones say about me* —"

"They don't say much," he said quickly. "That's the truth. They call you the anchor and then they go quiet, like men changing the subject. I thought — if I told you, if I put a name on you, a *title* —"

"You thought what?" I was standing now. I didn't remember standing. "You thought I'd break? You thought I'd run? Cyrus, I have *nothing*. Someone took everything I am and left me a girl who can fight and haggle and sleep in snow, and the only person who might know why — the only person who could hear the dead who *took* me — spent two months lying to my face while I trusted him with my life."

"It wasn't like that."

"Then tell me how it was."

He opened his mouth. Closed it. And I watched the terrible honesty arrive in him, the thing I had loved about him without naming it, the thing that made him a bad liar and a good man: he would not reach for a comfortable falsehood even now, even drowning.

"I was afraid," he said. "Of what you'd become if you remembered. The dead whisper about you, Astrid. They whisper about what broke the wards, and they don't all agree, but none of them agree on anything *kind*. And I thought — if she never remembers, she never has to be whatever that was. She gets to be the girl who saved my life in the flooded mines. She gets to choose." His jaw worked. "It was arrogance. I decided your ignorance was mercy. Carvers decide things like that. We handle other people's dead; we forget they're not ours to bury."

The anger in me wanted to stay. Anger is warm, and we were on a mountain, and warmth was scarce. But underneath the fury was something colder and worse: the memory of every kindness on this road, re-sorted. Every time he'd steered us away from a town, away from a sketch, away from a question. He hadn't been protecting a refugee.

He'd been managing a princess. Managing *me*, like a relic in his pack, like one of his carved bones — something precious, something that mustn't be dropped, something that was never, at any point, asked what it wanted.

"You should have told me," I said. "Whatever I was. Whatever I'd done. You should have let me choose with open eyes. Instead you chose for me, and you chose wrong, because I'm standing here anyway, Cyrus. On a mountain, in a storm, with your rope burns on my hands. Whatever I was — I'm still here. So the secret protected nothing. It only cost you me."

He flinched like I'd struck him. Good, some small cruel part of me said. Then the rest of me hated that part.

"I'll go ahead," I said, pulling my hood up. "To the shrine. Follow when you're ready."

"Astrid, the storm—"

"*Follow when you're ready.*"

I stepped out into the white. Behind me I heard Turnip shift, and Cyrus swear softly, and the fire hiss as the wind found it. I didn't look back. Looking back is how you die on mountains, and also how you forgive people, and I was not ready to do either.

But here is what I will admit, here in the privacy of my own skull, where even the dead have the decency to pretend they aren't listening: I walked into that blizzard knowing the way. Some part of me — some buried, carved-away part — knew the shrine on the far side of Halvard Pass the way I knew how to throw a knife and speak to mules. Which meant someone had walked this road before. Someone with my feet and my instincts and, presumably, my name.

And if the road remembered me, then the shrine might too. And shrines, Cyrus had told me once, are where the ghost-king does his bargaining.

I had refused him twice. But that was before I learned that the man beside me had been keeping my own past locked in his polite, guilty, wry mouth for two months.

Perhaps it was time to buy it back — whatever the price.

Behind me, faint under the wind, I heard him calling my name. Both of them.
