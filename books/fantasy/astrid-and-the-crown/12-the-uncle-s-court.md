## 12. The Uncle's Court

They took my knives, my boots, my coat, and the small bone star Cyrus had carved for me somewhere on the road — I never asked him when; I only knew I'd found it in my pocket one morning and hadn't had the heart to mention it. The guards catalogued these items on a slate with the brisk efficiency of people who had done many terrible things before breakfast and expected to do more after lunch.

Then they walked me through the gates of Glasshall.

I want to tell you I was brave. What I actually was, was amnesiac. That's the strange mercy of my condition: you cannot dread what you cannot remember. Glasshall's spires of grey glass meant nothing to me. The banners — a stag split by a silver line, Aldric's personal arms — meant nothing. The way my stomach turned over anyway, slow and sour, as we crossed the inner courtyard — that meant something, but meaning isn't the same as knowing.

*Play the hollow girl*, Cyrus had told me, weeks ago it seemed now, in another life where the stars still existed. *Nobody suspects an empty jug of holding water.* He'd been wrong about a lot of things, and late about more, but he wasn't wrong about that.

So I let my mouth hang slightly open. I looked at the glass spires like a farmwife looking at the sea.

"Name?" asked the steward at the door, a narrow man with ink-stained fingers and eyes that catalogued people like debts.

"I don't—" I made my voice small, lost. "They said I was found on the road."

"Found on the road," he repeated, writing it down. "Clothes?"

"Clothes?"

"What you were wearing. When you were found."

I looked down at myself, genuinely baffled, which required no acting whatsoever. "I don't remember before the road," I said. "It's all just... gone. Like a page torn out."

Something flickered across his face. Satisfaction? Disappointment? Hard to say. Ink-stained men are hard to read by design.

"This way," he said.

* * *

The throne room of Glasshall was built to make you feel small, and I resented how well it worked. The ceiling arched up into grey glass that caught what little light the starless sky offered and bent it into long pale ribs across the floor. At the far end, on a dais that was frankly excessive given he wasn't king yet, sat Prince Aldric.

He was not what I expected, though I couldn't have told you what I'd expected. Younger than his portraits, presumably — the ones burned in the square at Ashmarket showed a stiff man in ceremonial plate, and the man before me wore wool the color of wet slate and no ornament at all except a signet ring. Brown hair going grey at the temples. A scholar's hands. Kind eyes, if you were the kind of fool who trusted eyes.

"Astrid," he said.

My heart did something acrobatic and landed badly. But my face — my face stayed empty, because I had trained it for weeks, and because terror and blankness look remarkably alike.

The word hung there. He watched me the way a man watches a hook in dark water.

"I'm sorry," I said slowly, "do I know you?"

"You're Astrid," he said again, gently, like he was offering me a gift. "Daughter of Queen Maren. My niece." He rose and came down the steps — no rush, no ceremony, a man greeting family. "You've been missing for two years. There are people who will weep to see you."

"There's nothing missing from me that I know of," I said, and heard Cyrus wince somewhere in my memory. Too clever. Hollow girls don't make jokes about absence. "I mean — I don't remember any of that. They told me I hit my head, or something. I woke up on a smuggler's road with nothing."

"Nothing," Aldric echoed.

"No name. No family. Nothing."

He stopped three steps away from me, close enough that I could smell him — cedar oil, and under it something faintly chemical, like the air in a tanner's yard. He studied my face with an attention that was almost tender, and underneath the tenderness, something doing arithmetic.

"That must be very frightening," he said.

"Yes," I said, and that part needed no performance at all.

"Well." He smiled, and it reached his eyes, which frightened me more than if it hadn't. "Then you're in the right place. Glasshall has the finest physicians in the realm. We'll take care of you, Astrid. Whatever you've forgotten, whatever was done to you—" a pause, delicately measured, "—we'll find out together."

*Together*. I filed the word away like a coin I might need later.

They gave me a room with a locked door and a window too high to see out of, which told me everything the kindness of his voice tried to unsay.

* * *

Three days of physicians. They were gentle, thorough, and utterly uninterested in curing me, which I gathered by watching their faces whenever they thought I wasn't looking. They tested my reflexes, my speech, my memory for shapes and songs. One of them, an old woman with kind hands, held a candle before my eyes longer than any head-injury examination required, and at the end she said, softly, not quite to me: "The scarring is consistent."

Consistent with what, she didn't say, and I didn't ask, because hollow girls don't ask sharp questions.

On the fourth day they moved me to better rooms — larger, warmer, with a real window overlooking a frozen garden — and posted a guard outside my door whom I recognized from the sketches in Ashmarket. Captain Veyra. She stood at her post like furniture, which is the highest compliment I can pay a soldier, and she didn't speak to me, and I didn't speak to her, and for two more days that was our entire relationship.

It was Veyra who let slip the first thread.

She brought my supper herself on the fifth evening — unusual; guards don't usually serve food — and set the tray down with military precision, and then, instead of leaving, stood looking out my window at the starless sky with an expression I knew. It was the expression of someone doing arithmetic that keeps refusing to come out right.

"They're saying in the city it's an omen," she said, apparently to the window. "The stars going out. Priests of the old rites are saying the wards failed because the crown grew weak. Others say it's punishment."

"For what?" I asked — carefully, hollowly, the question a blank girl would ask.

"For something," she said. "Everyone's certain it's for something. Nobody agrees on what."

She left. I sat with my cold supper and turned her words over. The wards failed. Cyrus had told me that much, that first night — that the border wards of Thornfell had shattered like glass, that the stars went with them. And the dead in his bones had said the wards were anchored to a living soul, and the living soul was me, and I had broken them, or so I'd finally admitted to myself somewhere on the flooded mine roads, in the dark, holding a rope while Cyrus bled.

But here was the thread worth pulling: *why would Aldric's captain be guarding a nameless amnesiac girl found on a road?* Kings' households didn't post captains on stray women. Unless they suspected. Unless they knew.

Unless the hunt had never been about finding a runaway at all.

* * *

Aldric came himself on the seventh day. No stewards, no physicians. Just the prince, a chair he carried in with his own hands — a nice touch, I'll admit, calculated humility — and a chessboard.

"Do you play?" he asked.

"No," I said truthfully.

"Then we'll teach you. Memory is a muscle, Astrid. Games rebuild it." He set up the pieces with the ease of long habit. "My sister used to say that. Maren." He glanced up, watching. "Your mother."

"My mother," I repeated, flat as slate. Testing him.

"She died four years ago. Fever. You would have been — fourteen." He moved a pawn. "You have her look around the eyes."

"Everyone tells me I look like someone," I said. "A woman in Ashmarket said I looked like her dead daughter. It's convenient for people, I think. Faces that could be anyone's."

That earned me a real pause. Good, I thought. Bleed a little uncertainty into the water. A fish that strikes every lure is as suspicious as a fish that strikes none.

"It must be lonely," Aldric said, "not knowing who you are."

"It's quiet," I said. "Lonely implies missing something."

He laughed — genuinely, I think — and I hated how easy his laughter was, how warm the room became around it. This was a man built to be believed. I understood suddenly, in my gut rather than my head, why Cyrus's voice went flat and careful whenever the subject of Aldric came up. Some people's charm is a lure. His was a net.

We played six moves. He let me win a pawn, which I chose to interpret as flattery rather than pity.

"Tell me about the road," he said, on the seventh move, casual as weather. "Before you were found. You said you woke on a smuggler's road. Alone?"

"Alone," I said. Lie number one, delivered smoothly. "There was a village nearby. A carver took me in."

"A carver." His hand hovered over the board. "What was his name?"

"Cyrus," I said, and cursed myself even as the word left my mouth — too fast, too available — but there was no taking it back, so I shrugged the way hollow girls shrug. "He made little bone stars. Sold them at a stall."

"Bone-carving," Aldric said softly. "An old craft. Old families." He moved his knight. "And this carver — he traveled with you? After?"

"He went to Halvard," I said. "For supplies, he said." Lie number two, and this one cost me, because it put Cyrus inside Aldric's reach if Aldric chose to reach.

"Mm." Aldric sat back, and for a moment the warmth banked itself, and I saw beneath it the other thing, the cold engine turning. "You know, there's a strange story going around the border provinces. That on the night the stars went out, a wardstone broke — the anchor-stone, the oldest one, the one the old kings bound to flesh. And that whoever broke it walked free of a bargain struck years ago." He picked up his cup, drank, set it down. "Superstition, mostly. But my hunters follow superstition the way hounds follow scent. It's all they have."

"What did they find?" I asked. Hollow voice. Empty eyes. Inside, my heartbeat was a drum in a locked room.

"A girl," he said pleasantly. "About your age. Seen near Thornfell the night it happened, traveling with a bone-carver of the Iversen line." He smiled at me across the chessboard, kind as sunrise. "Small world, isn't it? Carvers keep appearing in your story."

"Maybe carvers find lost people," I said. "We're easy to find. We don't know where we're going."

Another laugh. He rose, reset the board, and paused at the door.

"The physicians say your memory may return," he said, not turning around. "Pieces at a time, perhaps. Fragments. If anything comes back to you, Astrid — anything at all — you'll tell me. Won't you? Family should have no secrets."

"Of course," I said.

He left. I sat very still for a long time, doing my own arithmetic now, and the sum was ugly. He knew about the anchor-stone. He knew about the sacrifice — he'd said *bargain struck years ago*, which meant midwinter, which meant the ritual was planned, scheduled, approved, not discovered. And he'd sent hunters with sketches of my face months before the wards fell, or the sketches wouldn't have existed to post in Ashmarket.

Which meant the hunt predates the escape.

Which meant Aldric hadn't been trying to rescue a lost niece from whatever took her memory. He'd been trying to recover an escaped sacrifice before anyone learned what she was escaping *from*.

I lay on my bed that night and stared at the ceiling and let myself feel it, briefly, completely: my uncle had built an altar out of me, and when I climbed off it, he'd called it betrayal.

Then I got up, because feeling things completely is a luxury for people with locked doors they can open, and started counting floorboards between my window and the garden wall.

* * *

It was the ninth night when Veyra spoke first.

She'd taken the midnight watch, and I'd made a show of sleeping, and around the coldest hour I heard her shift against the doorframe — once, twice — and then, quietly, in the voice of a woman addressing a wall:

"You don't sleep like someone who doesn't remember things."

I kept my breathing even. Slow. Innocent.

"Amnesiacs sleep like the drowned," she went on, conversational, terrible. "I guarded one once, a soldier who'd taken a mace to the head. He slept like nothing could touch him, because nothing in his past could." A pause. "You sleep like someone keeping one eye on the door. I do that. I know what it looks like."

I let the silence stretch until it seemed natural, then stirred, blinked, mumbled: "Captain?"

"Go back to sleep, my lady." A beat. "If you *are* a lady. If you're not. Either way."

Silence again. Then, softer, almost to herself:

"I've served this house eleven years. I pulled three of the hunters off a smuggler last month — boy, maybe nineteen — and my orders were to bring the girl back alive and everyone else dead. Everyone. Carvers, farmers, children on roads. Dead." Her gauntlet creaked against the doorframe. "I asked why the escalation. I was told the prince's writ superseded explanation. Eleven years, and that answer used to be enough."

I rolled over, carefully, so she'd see I wasn't studying her face. "Why are you telling me this?"

Long silence. Wind in the frozen garden. Somewhere below, a watchman calling the hour.

"Because you asked me nothing," Veyra said at last. "You lie awake and hear me doubt, and you ask nothing, and you sleep like a woman running calculations. An empty girl would have asked. Would have been frightened." Another creak — her fist closing, maybe. "Or you're exactly what the physicians say and I'm a tired old soldier talking to shadows. One of those is true. I've stopped being sure which."

"Captain," I said, hollow-voiced, small. "I don't understand any of this. I just want to go home. Whatever home is."

"I believe you want that," she said. And then, so quietly I nearly missed it: "*Whether you need to want it, I don't know.*"

She didn't speak again that night. But in the morning, when she brought the breakfast tray, she set it down and stood a moment, and her eyes went — deliberately, unmistakably — to the loose iron bracket beside the window, the one I'd been testing for two nights, the one that would hold a rope.

Then she straightened, saluted nothing, and resumed her post facing away from it.

I ate my bread and did not smile, because the walls of Glasshall were grey glass, and I had begun to suspect glass remembered. But somewhere under my ribs, in the place where hope had been living hand-to-mouth since the night the stars went out, a small fire caught and held.

Two nights later, the bracket came free in my hands with less noise than a breath — and the same hour, far off beyond the garden wall, beyond the frozen fields, a sound began that I did not recognize at first because I had never heard it from outside: the voices of the dead, rising like a tide, and under them, faint and furious and unmistakably alive, someone whistling a smuggler's tune at the gates of Glasshall.
