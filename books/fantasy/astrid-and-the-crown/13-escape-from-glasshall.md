## 13. Escape from Glasshall

The relic was the size of a sparrow's egg and older than any kingdom that had ever claimed to be eternal. My mother had carved it before I could walk — a hollow sphere of whalebone, pierced through with a lattice so fine it looked like frost given a body, and inside it she'd sealed the first voice I ever carried: a drowned fisherman from the coast who sang to me on nights when the silence got too loud.

I had kept it thirty years. Through two wars, one plague, and every winter my bones told me would be my last and weren't.

Now I held it out over a smuggler's table in the cellar of a tavern called the Drowned Bell, three streets from the walls of Glasshall, and watched a woman named Maren Oduya look at it the way a starving man looks at bread.

"You understand what you're asking," she said.

"I'm asking for a way into the prince's prison."

"I mean the relic." Her dark eyes didn't leave it. "You know what that is. You know what it holds."

"Every voice I've ever carried has been a debt," I said. "This one I'm choosing to pay."

That was a lie, or mostly one. The truth was that my hands shook, and not from cold. But Maren Oduya hadn't survived forty years of fencing stolen things by trusting shaking hands, so I let her believe what she needed to.

She turned the sphere once in her palm, careful as a priest with a relic, which in a way it was. "There's a coal chute on the river side. Disused since the foundry burned. It comes up inside the guard barracks' old washhouse, which they use now for storing nothing, because Prince Aldric's men are thorough about everything except rooms they think no one wants." She set it down between us without touching it again, which cost her something; I saw it in her jaw. "The grate at the top is locked. I can get you the key. What you do after the key is your own funeral."

"And if I need a way *out*?"

Maren smiled, and there was no warmth in it at all. "Then you should have asked before you gave away your mother's bone."

* * *

I spent the hours until midnight doing what I always did when terror came calling: working. There was a bench in the corner of my rented room, and scrap bone in my kit, and I carved a star. Small, crude, the kind of thing I sold at markets for a copper. I carved it because Astrid had once — in some life neither of us could see anymore — looked up at the real ones, and because the sky over Glasshall was a black lid, starless as everywhere else, and because a man needs to put his fear somewhere or it puts itself in him.

The dead were quiet tonight. That was the worst part. Usually they murmured like wind through wheat, dozens of voices, hundreds, the accumulated chorus of everyone whose last words had ever brushed against my gift. Tonight they were silent as the sky, and I knew what their silence meant. Something big was listening. Something patient.

"He's close," I said to the empty room. "Isn't he."

No answer. Which was an answer.

I finished the star, wrapped it in cloth with my knives, and went down to the river.

* * *

The coal chute smelled like a century of burnt things. I climbed it in the dark with my palms bleeding where the old iron had rusted to knives, and when I came up through the grate into the washhouse — all stone basins and dead hearths, exactly as useless as promised — the key worked on the inner door, and then I was inside Glasshall proper, moving through corridors lit by oil lamps that guttered whenever I passed, as if the building itself flinched.

Finding her cell was the easy part. The dead may have gone quiet, but the living gossip like magpies. Two guardsmen outside the deep cells argued in whispers about wages and whether the prince had gone mad, and from their argument I learned three things: the prisoner was fed twice daily, the prisoner had asked no questions, and Captain Veyra had taken to standing watch outside her door herself, alone, which no captain does unless she's guarding something from her own side.

I found Veyra first.

She stood at the end of the cell corridor with her sword hand resting easy on her hip and her eyes fixed on nothing, and when I stepped out of the shadow she had a knife at my throat before I finished breathing in. Fast. Faster than she'd been in Ashmarket. Fear sharpens some people.

"Bone-carver," she said. No surprise in it, which told me she'd expected this, or something like it. "You have a talent for appearing in places that should kill you."

"It's the dying-slowly thing. Makes a man careless."

Her eyes flicked over my face, cataloguing. "You look worse than Ashmarket."

"You look like a woman who's been reading letters she wasn't meant to see."

Something moved behind her expression — a door opening onto a room she didn't want me in. "You don't know what you're talking about."

"I know the prince ordered her death, not rescue. I know you've figured that out too, because you've been standing outside her cell for three days instead of sending a subordinate, and captains only do that when they don't trust their own garrison." I kept my voice low and level, the way I talked to frightened animals and frightened dead. "I'm here to take her out. You can raise the alarm, or you can look at that torch bracket for the next hundred heartbeats and see nothing at all."

Veyra didn't move. Down the corridor, behind the iron door, I felt rather than heard a faint hum against my bones — the wardstone fragment Astrid still carried, singing to whatever was left of its anchor.

"Why do you care?" Veyra asked finally. Not hostile. Genuinely asking, the way soldiers ask when the map has stopped matching the ground. "She's a stranger to you. A job gone sideways."

"She's the reason the stars went out and the reason they'll come back. She's also a girl who doesn't remember her own name, being held by the man who arranged to cut her throat at midwinter." I shrugged. "And she laughs at my jokes, which almost no one does."

For a long moment Veyra said nothing. Then she stepped aside from the corridor, took the ring of keys off her belt, and held them out — but didn't let go when I took hold.

"If this goes wrong," she said quietly, "I die for it. Understand that. Whatever they promised you, whatever you think you're saving — I am spending my life on your certainty, carver. Make certain it's worth something."

"It isn't certainty," I said. "It's just the only direction left."

She let go.

* * *

Astrid sat on the cell's cot with her back straight and her hands folded, and when the door opened she looked up at me with an expression I'll carry to whatever grave takes me: not relief. Recognition. As if she'd known, in the wordless place where her memories used to live, that I'd come.

"Took you long enough," she said.

"There was a queue."

Behind me, Veyra cleared her throat. Astrid's gaze went past my shoulder and sharpened. "Captain."

"Your Highness." Veyra said it flatly, testing the word like ice on a pond. "Or whatever you are. I've read the dispatches. I've read the ones Aldric thought were burned. I'm not helping you because I believe in you. I'm helping you because I no longer believe in him, and a soldier has to believe in *something*."

"That's the saddest recruitment speech I've ever heard," Astrid said, "and I apparently grew up in a palace full of them."

Despite everything — despite the black sky and the blood-soaked throne room she'd seen in her first fragment and the secret I could feel her carrying like a swallowed stone — she grinned. And for one heartbeat, in a cell under a starless sky, we were three people laughing at the edge of an abyss, which is either madness or grace, and I've stopped trying to tell the difference.

Then the lamps died.

All of them, down the whole corridor, at once. And the silence of the dead broke like a dam.

*Cyrus.* The ghost-king's voice poured through my bones, cold as well-water, familiar now the way a scar is familiar. *You brought her to the door. Good. I wondered if you'd dare.*

"We're leaving," I said aloud, because Astrid and Veyra couldn't hear him and I refused to conduct a negotiation alone in my own skull like a madman. "Whatever bargain you think exists—"

*Oh, the bargain exists. Ask her what she accepted in the shrine.*

Astrid had gone white. She was looking at me — through me — at something standing in the air beside my shoulder, and her hand had gone to the wardstone fragment at her throat.

"He's here," she whispered. "He's *here*, Cyrus, he's—"

*Tell her,* the king said, almost gently, which was worse than any threat. *Tell her the price. Or shall I?*

"The price," I said, and my mouth tasted like ash. "In the shrine. When you took the fragment. He said you'd owe him. He never said what."

Astrid's eyes came into focus on mine, and I watched her decide not to lie, which after everything felt like a small miracle. "He said he'd collect later. He said—" Her breath caught. "He said the memories aren't gifts. They're breadcrumbs. He wants me to follow them."

*To the old capital,* the ghost-king said, and his voice filled the cell, filled the corridor, filled the dark like water fills a hull. *Every fragment she reclaims will call her home to Halvard-under-the-Hill, where her story began and where it must end. That is the price, princess: you will finish collecting yourself. All of it. Every memory, every guilt, every name. And you will do it kneeling in the throne room where your line was crowned.*

"No," Veyra said flatly. "That's a trap. That's obviously a trap."

"Obviously," I agreed.

But Astrid was looking at her own hands, turning them over as if checking they were still hers, and I recognized the look because I'd seen it in mirrors my whole life: the look of someone calculating what they can afford to lose.

"How many fragments are left?" she asked quietly.

"Astrid—"

"*How many?*"

"Two," I said. "Maybe three. The sacrifice. Your mother. Whatever happened in the throne room the night the wards broke." I hesitated. "And the name. Whatever he took last."

She nodded slowly. "Then he's not offering me a trap. He's offering me a road, and the trap is that I was always going to walk it." She looked up, at the empty air, at the cold presence coiled around our bones. "Fine. Old capital. But I walk it free, and these two walk with me, and if you touch either of them I'll shatter what's left of your wardstones myself and haunt *you*."

Silence. Long enough that I started counting my own heartbeats.

Then, softly, with something horribly like satisfaction: *Agreed.*

The lamps relit. The dead went quiet. And Astrid sagged against the cot as if a hand had released her, breathing hard, alive, committed.

"Well," Veyra said into the ringing quiet. "I hope you both understand we're now fugitives escorting a haunted princess to a cursed city on the express orders of a dead king."

"Yes," Astrid and I said together.

"Good. Just so we're clear." She pulled the keys from the door, weighed them in her palm, and dropped them down the privy shaft with a small metallic splash. "Let it look like bribery and escape, not treason. Treason gets hunted. Bribery just gets fined." She glanced at me, and for the first time there was something almost like respect in it, or at least its rough country cousin. "Move fast, carver. The garrison changes shift at the bell."

* * *

We went out through the coal chute, down the frozen riverbank, through streets where refugees huddled under awnings and stared at a sky with nothing in it, and nobody stopped us, because in a city where the stars have died, three more ghosts on the road draw no notice at all.

At the treeline beyond the walls, Astrid stopped and looked back at Glasshall — at the prince's banners hanging limp in the windless dark — and her face did something complicated.

"He'll come after us," she said.

"He'll come after everything," I said. "That's what princes do. We just have to be faster."

She touched the wardstone at her throat, and far away — days east, under the hill where a capital had been buried by its own history — I felt something answer. A pull, gentle and enormous, like tide.

Like breadcrumbs.

"One fragment at a time," she said, mostly to herself. Then she looked at me, and there was terror in her eyes, and under the terror, stubborn as bedrock, something that had decided to keep walking anyway. "Cyrus. If what I find at the end of this road is someone you can't—"

"It won't be."

"You don't know that."

"No," I admitted. "But I carve bone for a living. I know better than anyone that what's underneath is always worth keeping."

She didn't smile. But she turned east, toward the old capital and the ghost-king's waiting price, and Veyra fell in on her left and I on her right, and the three of us walked into the starless dark like people walking into a story none of us would survive unchanged.

Behind us, very faint, the bells of Glasshall began to ring the alarm.

Too late. Always too late. That, at least, was going our way.
