## 14. What Cyrus Carried

He didn't apologize.

That was the first thing I noticed about the morning after the blizzard, after I had screamed at him until my voice cracked and he had stood there in the snow taking it like a fence post takes weather. He didn't say *I'm sorry*. He didn't say *I lied to you*. He made breakfast instead — porridge with pine nuts, because apparently even a man who has just detonated the only relationship he has still believes in breakfast — and set the bowl in front of me and sat down across the fire with his own, and waited.

"You could at least grovel," I said.

"I could," Cyrus agreed. "It wouldn't help."

"It would help *me*."

"Then consider me on my knees." And there it was, that dry flat delivery that I had spent weeks mistaking for honesty when it was actually armor. "Astrid. You want an apology or you want the truth? Because I can give you one of them properly, and I've been giving you the other one badly since the night we met."

The fire popped. Somewhere above us, where stars should have been, there was nothing but the same black felt sky we'd been living under for two months, a ceiling with all the lamps removed. I wrapped my hands around the bowl because they needed something to do besides throw it at him.

"The truth," I said, because I am, whatever else is true of me, a glutton for punishment.

* * *

Here is what I knew about bone-carving before that morning: it was his trade. Knives, awls, little figures of hares and wolves sold at market stalls, the kind of craft that smells of dust and animal fat and patience. Here is what I learned over the next hour, while the porridge went cold in my hands: bone-carving is to what Cyrus actually does as a fishing rod is to drowning.

"I don't hear ghosts," he said. "I wish people would stop saying it like that. It makes it sound like voices in a room. It's not a room. It's more like—" He turned his bowl in his hands, hunting for words, this man who talked so little and then, when the dam finally broke, talked like a man paying off a debt. "You know how a song gets stuck in your head? Now imagine every dead person you've ever touched the bones of is a song stuck in your head, forever, and none of them know the words, and half of them are screaming."

I put down the bowl.

"When did it start?"

"My mother." His jaw worked. "She was a carver too. Better than me. She carved wardstones before the guild took the trade over — real ones, the old kind, anchored proper. When she died I held her hand and her bones were right there under my fingers and she—" A breath. "She didn't leave. That's the part nobody tells you. Dying isn't leaving, not entirely. Something stays in the calcium. Some echo. And I heard it, and I thought, well, that's grief, everyone hears their mother once. Except it didn't stop."

"How old were you?"

"Nine."

"And you never told anyone."

"I told a priest. He told my father I was possessed. My father told me to never say it again, and I didn't, and then I spent thirty years learning which parts of myself to carve away so the rest would fit through doors." He said it without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "Every time I touch worked bone, I pick up whoever's in it. The dead cling to their bones like burrs. And they don't let go of *me*, either. They ride along."

"What do you mean, ride along?"

"I mean," he said, and here his voice changed, went quieter, went somewhere I recognized — the place in my own chest where memories should have been and weren't — "that I have carried forty-one voices out of the places they died, because no one else could hear them asking. And carrying them costs. Every voice takes a room in your head, and the rooms are finite, and when you run out of rooms—"

He stopped.

"Cyrus." My mouth had gone dry. "What do you lose?"

And he told me. Not dramatically. That was the terrible thing; he itemized it like a ledger, like losses at market, because that was the only way he could make himself say it aloud.

He lost songs first. He used to know dozens — work songs, drinking songs, a lullaby his mother sang in a dialect nobody spoke anymore. The lullaby went when he was nineteen, crowded out by a drowned miner who hummed it endlessly in a register no living throat could reach, until the miner's version wore the melody smooth as river stone and his mother's slid out of him like water through fingers. He lost the name of his childhood dog. He lost the taste of honey — not the word, he remembered the word, but the memory of the taste itself, gone, replaced by a woman who had died craving it. He lost whole years of his twenties, thinned down to summaries. He lost faces. He lost—

"My mother's face," he said. "Three years ago. I carried a girl out of a fire in Glasshall, eleven years old, and she wouldn't stop showing me her mother's face, over and over, begging me to remember it because she couldn't bear being forgotten, and I promised, and I keep promises. So now I remember a stranger's mother perfectly and mine is—" His hands opened, empty. "I know she had gray eyes. Everyone says so. I'm taking their word for it."

The fire crackled. Above us, the starless dark pressed down like a lid.

"That's why you never asked me questions," I said slowly. "Back at the start. Why you never pushed when I couldn't answer things about myself. You'd just go quiet and change the subject, and I thought you were being polite, or careful, or—"

"I was being afraid." He looked at the fire, not at me. "Memory is the only thing I've got left that's actually mine, Astrid. I hoard it. And then I found a girl with nothing, and the dead were screaming at me that she was the anchor of the wards, and I knew — I *knew* — that if I helped her get them back, I'd be spending what I have left. Rooms in the house. And I did it anyway, and I told myself I hadn't decided yet whether to tell you who you were, and that was a lie I told so long I started believing it. But it wasn't greed that kept me quiet. It was terror. Terror that if I gave you back everything, there'd be nothing left of me to walk beside you."

I should have been angrier. Part of me was — some stubborn shard of the princess I'd apparently been, the one who broke wards and ran from altars, who did not forgive easily and had certainly never forgiven anyone mid-porridge. But most of me was looking at this hollowed-out man across the fire, this walking archive of other people's deaths, and doing arithmetic.

Forty-one voices. Thirty years. Songs, tastes, years, a face.

"You're dying," I said. "That's what the bone-singer meant. In Halvard. She read your bones and told you, and you didn't tell me that either."

"She told me I was spending faster than I earn. Same thing, eventually." He shrugged. "I've known since I was nine that the bill comes due. It's not new information. What's new is having someone around who might notice when it arrives."

"Well, I notice," I said, and my voice came out rougher than I intended. "For the record. I notice everything about you, you impossible man. I noticed you flinch at graveyards. I noticed you talk to your knife handles. I noticed you haven't hummed once in two months of travel, and I thought it was because you were dour, and it turns out it's because you *can't remember how the songs go*."

Something moved through his face — not quite a smile, but the ground where a smile might take root if conditions improved.

"That's the most words you've ever strung together at me," he said.

"Don't get used to it. I'm still furious."

"I know. You're furious and you're making me tea anyway." He nodded at my hands, and I looked down and discovered that at some point during his confession I had gotten up, found the kettle, and started heating water without any decision to do so. Traitor hands. Tender ones.

* * *

We stayed in the cave camp two more days, because the pass was choked with snow and because neither of us knew how to be near each other yet without a wall of weather between us and the world.

On the second day I asked him to teach me something. Not carving — I'd tried, and produced what he charitably called "a badger" and what was visibly a lump. I asked him to tell me about the forty-one. All of them. Names if he had them, stories if he didn't.

"Why?"

"Because you carry them alone and it's killing you, and I can't fix that, but I can at least witness it. Someone should know who they were. Even secondhand. Even wrong."

So he told me. We sat shoulder to shoulder against the cold and he went through them one by one, and I began to understand that this was the truest thing he had ever given anyone: the drowned miner who hummed; the fire-girl with her mother's face; a smuggler named Osk who died laughing at his own joke and whose joke Cyrus still tells sometimes without knowing where he got it (it was a good joke; I laughed); a border guard who had stood his post for six hours after the wound that killed him, because no one had relieved him and no one had told him he could go. Forty-one rooms. He walked me through the house of himself and showed me every tenant, and by the end my eyes were burning and his voice had gone to gravel and neither of us mentioned it.

"There's space left?" I asked. "In the house?"

"A room or two." He flexed his scarred fingers. "Enough to finish this. Whatever this turns out to be."

"Then hear me clearly, Cyrus Iversen. You do not get to spend yourself buying my freedom. I've seen what sacrifice buys in this country — I grew up in its throne room, apparently, before someone scraped me clean. It buys crowns for other people and graves for the spenders. If it comes to a choice between my memories and your mind, I choose us both or I choose nothing, and I will fight you on it, and I cheat at fighting."

"That's true," he said gravely. "You bit Veyra."

"She deserved it."

"She did, a little."

And then, because the moment was soft and I am constitutionally incapable of letting soft moments survive unattended, I said the harder thing. "Why me? Out of all of them. You've carried forty-one strangers out of death. Why does the forty-second get to stay alive and angry and eating your food?"

He was quiet long enough that I thought I'd broken something. Then: "Because the dead ask to be carried and you ask to walk. There's a difference. Carrying ends when I put them down. Walking—" He glanced at me, then away, ears going faintly red in the cold, a man in his forties undone by his own honesty. "Walking assumes company."

Oh, I thought. Oh, you ridiculous, ruined, brave man.

I leaned my shoulder into his. He didn't pull away. Outside the cave the snow kept falling on a country with no stars, and inside it, for one hour, there was warmth, and it wasn't much, but it was ours, and I held onto it the way he held onto names.

* * *

We broke camp on the third morning, because the snow eased and because staying safe in caves is how fugitives become skeletons, and I mean that literally in this country.

The road down from the pass was wrong in a way I felt in my teeth before I understood it with my eyes. Smoke, first — too many smokes for farmsteads, rising from the valley in columns that leaned together like conspirators. Then bells, tolling arrhythmically from a village we couldn't see, alarm-bells, war-bells. Then the refugees: a trickle on the road ahead of us, then a stream, families with carts and children and livestock, all moving the same direction, away from something.

An old man driving a donkey saw our faces and reined in, wild-eyed. "You coming from the high road? Did you see them? Did you see the banners?"

"No," Cyrus said carefully. "Whose banners?"

"Everyone's!" The man laughed, and there was nothing funny anywhere near it. "Lord Hessler says the Rennish burned the wards. Lord Renn says Hessler hoarded the grain while the sky went black. Half the valley's marching on the other half and the king's men are nowhere, the king's men are *everywhere*, they're pulling boys off the roads for garrison duty—" He spat. "Stars go out and suddenly every lord remembers an old grudge. Old hatreds don't die, girl. They just wait for the dark."

He clicked his tongue and the donkey plodded on, and the stream of refugees flowed past us, and Cyrus and I stood in the middle of the road looking at each other.

"The ghost-king wanted this," I said quietly. "The blackout. It was never just about hiding me or crowning himself. A country in the dark tears itself apart, and a torn country can't defend a crown. He's clearing the table."

"Then we'd better get to the capital before he finishes setting it." Cyrus's face had gone grim and settled, the look of a man picking up a weight he already knows the size of. "There's a smuggler's cut through the hills past the burning villages. Three days, if the roads hold."

"And if they don't?"

"Then we'll meet whatever's holding them." He shouldered his pack, and beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth against his spine, rode the relic he'd traded half his savings to reclaim — the piece the ghost-king demanded we bring to the old capital, the next key to my own skull. "Stay close. Stay loud if we're separated. And Astrid—"

"Yes?"

He hesitated, and for a moment I saw him weigh some sentence and decide against it, the old habit, the fear. But then he exhaled and said it anyway, and the fact that saying it cost him visible effort made it worth more than anything polished could have been:

"I'm glad you're angry at me instead of gone. Anger means you're still here. I've spent thirty years listening to people who aren't."

Before I could find an answer worthy of that, the bells below changed their rhythm — faster, frantic, closer — and down the valley, where the refugee stream bent around a wooded hill, we both saw it at the same moment: orange light leaping against the gray sky, and beneath it, small with distance, the shapes of riders driving burning torches along the edges of a village that had done nothing except stand in somebody's ancient grudge.

Cyrus swore. I was already running.
