## 15. The Road of Old Hatreds

I had served the crown for nineteen years, and in all that time I had learned one lesson above all others: when a kingdom goes dark, its lords do not look up. They look sideways. They look for someone to blame who isn't holding a mirror.

We were three days out of Halvard when I understood that the war hadn't started with a battle. It had started with a rumor.

* * *

The rumor reached us before we reached it — the way rumors do now, leaping village to village like fire through dry grass, since no one could read the stars anymore to tell them anything truer. A tinker on the road outside Drenmark told us that Lord Cassel of the Marches had sealed his borders and was hoarding grain. A pilgrim walking the other direction said Cassel had done no such thing; it was the Duke of Vael who'd closed the river crossings, and Cassel's men had burned a tollhouse in retaliation. A third traveler, a woman with frostbitten fingers and a soldier's walk she tried to hide under a peddler's cloak, said both stories were wrong and that the real trouble was further south, where the temples had started preaching that the vanished stars were punishment for a broken oath — though which oath, and broken by whom, depended entirely on which temple you asked.

"Everyone's certain," Cyrus said, "and nobody agrees."

"That's how it always starts," I said.

Astrid walked between us with her hood up and her hands buried in her sleeves, and she didn't say anything at all. She'd been quiet since Halvard, quieter than her usual quiet, which was already the kind of quiet that made you want to check whether she was still breathing. The bone-singer's words about Cyrus had shaken him too, though he pretended otherwise, whittling little bone stars by the fire each night as if his hands needed something to do besides shake.

I knew what they were thinking. I was thinking it too: *the crown did this.* And if the crown did this, then every lord blaming every other lord was barking up the wrong tree, and the whole country was sliding toward a knife fight over nothing while the actual murderer sat warm in Glasshall.

Except I wasn't sure anymore that the crown had done this. That was the problem with learning things you weren't supposed to learn. Captain Veyra, loyal hound of Prince Aldric, had gone sniffing where hounds shouldn't sniff, and now she carried knowledge like a stone in her boot — small, hard, impossible to ignore with every step.

The king's brother had ordered the princess dead. Not rescued. *Dead.* And the hunt he'd dressed up as a rescue.

I kept waiting for the stone to work itself out of my boot. It didn't. It worked itself deeper.

* * *

We came to Thornhollow on the fourth day, or what was left of it.

You have to understand what a border village looks like when the wards are down. These were places built on the edge of the old protections, places whose whole existence was a wager that the magic would hold — that the things beyond the hills would stay beyond the hills, that the old hatreds sleeping under the treaty-stones would keep sleeping. For two hundred years the wager paid out. Then the stars went out, and every debt came due at once.

Thornhollow had been a weaving village. Smoke still rose from half its chimneys, which was the first wrong thing, because the people should have fled or should have stayed, not this horrible in-between. The second wrong thing was the banners. Someone had nailed a strip of black cloth to the shrine door — the sigil of Lord Cassel, or close enough for a village that probably couldn't afford a proper banner — and someone else had torn it down and burned it in the street, and the scorch mark was shaped like a screaming mouth.

The third wrong thing was the crows.

Cyrus stopped at the edge of the square and went very still, the way he does when the voices get loud. Astrid's hand found his sleeve without either of them seeming to notice. I drew my sword because I am a simple creature and steel is my answer to most questions.

"Not soldiers," Cyrus said quietly. "It wasn't soldiers."

He was right, God help me, he was right. What was left in the square wasn't a battlefield. Battlefields have a shape to them, an order, even a terrible one. This was just — wreckage. Doors broken in from the outside. A well with things floating in it that I will not describe, because I ate that night and would prefer to keep it down. Bodies in the street wearing homespun, not uniforms, and among them bodies wearing the colors of three different lords' levies, all mixed together like a hand of cards thrown down in anger.

"They killed each other," Astrid said. Her voice was flat, which frightened me more than tears would have. "Neighbors."

"The sky went dark," Cyrus said. "Two hundred years of believing the wards would hold, and then—" He stopped. He looked at the burned banner, the scorch-mouth, the mixed bodies. "Someone said the right word to the right frightened men, and the word was *them.* It's always *them.*"

I had seen massacres before. I want to be honest about that, because I think honesty is the only thing I have left to offer anyone. I had put down riots in Ashmarket. I had burned a granary in the Vael succession dispute because a lord ordered it and I obeyed, and forty people starved that winter who might not have. I had told myself stories about all of it — necessity, order, the lesser evil — and the stories had held, mostly, the way a dam holds, mostly.

Standing in Thornhollow square, I felt the dam crack.

Because this was different. This wasn't a lord spending peasants like coin. This was peasants spending each other, freely, gladly, over a sky none of them had touched. The crown's lie — whatever Aldric had done, whatever game he was playing with a dead-or-alive niece — had fallen into the water like a spark, and the whole country was dry tinder, and nobody at court would ever see this square. Nobody at court would smell it.

"Captain." Cyrus was watching me with those pale eyes that saw too much. "You've gone white."

"I'm fine."

"You're not. You look like a woman reading her own death warrant."

"I said I'm fine." My voice came out harder than I intended, and Astrid flinched, and I hated myself for it. I sheathed the sword. "We shouldn't linger. If Cassel's levies did this—"

"Cassel's levies didn't do this," Cyrus said. "*People* did this. Which means people can do it again, anywhere, including to a hooded girl walking with a smuggler and a disgraced captain." He paused. "And before you correct me — yes, I know you haven't told them yet. About the orders. I can hear it sitting on you like a crow on a fence post."

Astrid turned. "Told us what?"

The road stretched empty in both directions. The crows waited. There was no good moment, and there never is, so I took the one I had.

* * *

I told them in the shrine of some minor harvest saint, because it had a roof and a door I could bar, and because there is something about holy ground, even neglected holy ground, that makes lying harder.

I told them about the interrogation at the tollhouse — the smuggler named Fenwick who'd talked, eventually, the way men talk when they realize their captor has stopped believing in her own side. I told them the words Aldric's courier had used, the ones meant for the hunters' ears only: *the princess is not to be returned alive. Recovery of the asset is secondary to termination. The succession must be clean.*

I said it plainly, because dressing it up would have been worse.

Astrid sat very still on the shrine steps. She had gotten better at stillness since Halvard — a stillness with weight behind it, like deep water. When she finally spoke, her voice was almost steady.

"My uncle wants me dead. I'd guessed. Guessing and hearing it are different animals."

"He signed the hunt himself," I said. "Publicly. 'Find my brother's daughter and bring her home.' Every hunter in the company believed it. I believed it. I led thirty sworn swords across half the country chasing a rescue that was always a murder."

"Why?" Cyrus asked. Not cruelly. He has a way of asking why that sounds like he genuinely wants the answer, which is rarer than you'd think in this world. "What does a dead princess buy him?"

"A clean succession. No rival claimant with a better bloodline." I heard myself reciting it and realized how much it sounded like a merchant explaining the price of cloth. "She was the true heir, Captain Veyra. Everyone at court knows it and no one says it. Aldric holds the regency and calls himself prince, but paper burns, and hers was the older claim."

Silence. Outside, wind moved through the dead village, rattling a shutter somewhere, patient as a creditor.

"So the war in the villages," Astrid said slowly, "the lords tearing at each other — he doesn't care. It's noise. Cover."

"It's worse than cover. It's *useful.* A country eating itself can't ask who darkened the sky. Can't ask why the heir had to die." I looked at my hands. Soldier's hands, scarred, obedient. "I've spent nineteen years being the instrument of men like that. I told myself the instrument bears no guilt. Standing in that square today, I couldn't find the story that makes that true."

"You could have kept it to yourself," Cyrus said. "The orders. Sold us to Veyra's own company, collected your pardon, gone back to a warm bed."

"I could have," I agreed. "I considered it. Warm beds are underrated."

"But?"

"But I watched a man in Thornhollow square die on his knees with a hay fork in his hand, defending a house that wasn't even his, against neighbors he'd drunk with at midwinter. And I thought: this is what obedience builds. This is the harvest." I met his eyes. "I'm done being the sickle."

Astrid stood. She crossed the shrine floor and did something I did not expect, which was take my hand — my scarred, obedient hand — and hold it the way you'd hold a thing you were deciding whether to keep.

"Then you're not our hunter anymore," she said. "You're ours. Whatever that's worth."

"It's worth a sword," I said, "and a fair amount of trouble."

"Trouble we have," Cyrus murmured. "Sword we need."

* * *

We buried what we could bury, which was not nearly enough, and we burned the rest, which is the mercy the living owe the dead when there's no time for anything gentler. It took until dusk. Astrid worked beside me without a word, hauling and digging with that same flat competence she'd shown in the flooded tunnels — reflexes she couldn't explain, a body that remembered a childhood of training her mind had lost. Watching her, I thought: *they trained her to be sacrificed and made her strong enough to survive everything except the altar.* There was a cruelty in that so refined it deserved its own wing at court.

Cyrus carved. Of course he carved. While we dug, he sat among the bones the crows had spared and did his quiet work, and I noticed — because I notice things, it's the whole job — that he was carving names. Little tags of bone, one letter at a time, names he pulled out of the air with his eyes half-closed, listening to whatever the dead of Thornhollow still had to say.

"You can hear them here?" I asked. "After — that?"

"They're loudest right after," he said. "Before they forget they were people." His knife didn't stop. "This one was a weaver. Marta. She's worried about her loom. They hold onto the strangest things." He tied the tag to a stake over the grave. "Somebody should remember the loom."

"Somebody should remember all of it," Astrid said, "and write it down, and make the lords read it aloud."

"Lords don't read," I said. "They have people for that."

"Then make the people read it to them."

There was something in her voice then — not the hollow girl from the smuggler's road, not the trembling amnesiac. Something with edges. I looked at her and thought, with a chill that had nothing to do with the wind: *there she is. There's the heir.*

* * *

We left Thornhollow before dawn, because staying in a graveyard felt like an invitation, and because Cyrus said the dead were starting to press on him — too many new voices in a place already crowded with old ones. We took the southern road toward the old capital, because that was where the ghost-king's price pointed, and because Astrid had stopped pretending she had anywhere else to go.

An hour out, we met the column.

Not an army. Worse. A river of people — refugees from the borderlands, whole families with carts and goats and grandmothers, flowing south away from the darkened marches. And along the column's edge, riding picket, flying the black-and-silver of Lord Cassel: soldiers. Levy soldiers, farm boys in borrowed mail, but soldiers nonetheless, collecting a toll at the bridge. Grain. Blankets. And, the officer announced in a bored voice, "any persons of interest to the Crown."

My blood went cold in a way I hadn't let it go cold in weeks. Because I knew what persons of interest meant. I had written the phrase myself, in dispatches, in a former life.

"We go around," Cyrus said.

"There's no around," I said. "That bridge is the only crossing for twenty miles, and behind us—" I glanced back at the horizon, where smoke plums stood up from the borderlands like accusatory fingers. "Behind us is what they're fleeing."

Astrid pulled her hood lower. "Then we cross."

We joined the river of people, and here is the thing I will carry to my grave, the thing that finished the job Thornhollow started: nobody questioned us. We were just three more refugees in a nation of refugees. The levy soldiers waved through the old, the young, the lame, the weeping — waved through everyone, because everyone was already broken, and broken people make no trouble. The toll was misery itself, and misery was cheap that season.

Halfway across the bridge, a soldier grabbed a boy of maybe twelve by the collar — caught him slipping past the toll line with a sack of flour. The boy's mother screamed. The soldier raised his hand, and the crowd around them went silent in that particular way crowds go silent, the way I have heard a hundred times, the silence that is the sound of a people deciding, all at once, how much more they will take.

And then an old woman — just an old woman, gray-braided, cart-drawn — stepped forward and said, in a voice that carried the length of the bridge:

"How many of you have looked up lately? How many of you have seen even *one* star?"

The silence changed key.

"And your lords collect flour," she said. "Flour. As if flour will light the sky."

I felt it move through the crowd like a current under ice — that shift from fear to fury that every soldier learns to feel in his teeth before it breaks. The soldier holding the boy looked at his officer. The officer looked at the old woman. And for one long moment, on that bridge, authority hung in the air like a rope deciding which way to swing.

The officer cut the boy loose. "Go," he said roughly. "All of you go. Toll's ended. Get off my bridge."

It wasn't mercy. I want to be clear about that. It was a man realizing, publicly, that he no longer believed in what he was guarding. I recognized the expression. I'd been wearing it for days.

We crossed. On the far bank, Astrid fell into step beside me, and under the hood her eyes were bright and terrible.

"You broke with them back there," she said. "On the bridge. I saw it."

"I broke with them in a burned village," I said. "The bridge was just where I stopped hiding it."

"Say it anyway," Cyrus said quietly. He'd stopped walking. He was looking at me with those listening eyes. "Out loud. To us. Words matter to the dead, and after today—" he gestured back at the bridge, the column, the smoking horizon, "—after today, I suspect they matter to the living too."

So I said it. I, Sera Veyra, captain of the crown's hunters, sworn sword of nineteen years, stood on a refugee road under a starless sky and said:

"I renounce my oath to Prince Aldric and his regency. I name him for what he is — a kinslayer in waiting, a liar, a man who murders children and calls it statecraft. I'll serve the true heir or no one." I swallowed. "Gods forgive me. It took me long enough."

Nobody applauded. There was no trumpets, no absolution. An old woman with a cart nodded once, the way you nod at a fact. Somewhere behind us, the borderlands burned.

But Astrid took my arm, and Cyrus clapped my shoulder with a hand that trembled slightly — from the voices, I told myself, just the voices — and we walked on, three traitors in a nation of them, heading for a capital full of ghosts.

It was another day before we learned why the roads ahead were emptying instead of filling. A peddler told us at dusk, breathless, half-mad with the news: Aldric had declared martial law in the old capital. And he had issued a proclamation, posted at every crossroads in the realm, offering a fortune in gold and a barony besides —

for the capture of Princess Astrid, *alive*, and the smugglers traveling with her,

whose faces, thanks be to whatever gods still bothered, were described in detail.

Mine included.
