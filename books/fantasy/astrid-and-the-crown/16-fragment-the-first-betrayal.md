## 16. Fragment: The First Betrayal

The capital had a smell.

I don't know what I expected — incense, maybe, or old stone, the way holy places smell in stories. What I got instead was woodsmoke and wet wool and ten thousand people pressed behind walls that were never meant to hold them, because when the stars went out, everyone in Thornfell with legs walked toward the one city that still had gates. Refugees camped along the road for a mile in every direction, tents of patched canvas and stolen sails, cookfires smoking into a sky that gave nothing back.

"Charming," I said.

"You've said that about every place we've slept since Halvard," Cyrus said. He was squinting at the gates through the crowd, doing that thing he did where his whole face went careful, like a man counting coins he already knew were short. "You hated the barn. You hated the ferry. You called the bone-singer's hut 'aggressively cozy.'"

"I stand by all three." I pulled my hood lower. Veyra walked ahead of us in her captain's coat with the royal crest torn off at the shoulder — she'd done it herself, on the road, without ceremony, and none of us had said anything about it, which was its own kind of ceremony. "How are we getting in? Please say tunnels. I've developed a real fondness for tunnels."

"No tunnels. The old smuggler roads end at the river." His jaw worked. "We walk in the front gate like pilgrims."

"Pilgrims going to see what?"

He didn't answer, and I knew why. We'd been circling it for days — the ghost-king's price, come due at last, whispering up out of the shard of memory he'd left lodged in me like a splinter. *Finish collecting. Come to the old capital. All of it waits for you here.* Every fragment I'd taken had cost something. This one, the last one, was owed whether I wanted it or not.

And I will be honest with you, whoever you are, reading this carved bone someday in some century I won't see: I wanted it. That was the shameful part. Not the taking — the wanting. I was so tired of being a woman built out of other people's descriptions. *She moves like a soldier. She hums songs she doesn't know. She flinches from crowns.* I wanted my own account back, even if the ledger came back red.

So we joined the line at the Westgate, between a family of dyers whose vats had frozen starless-blue and a one-eyed drover who kept telling anyone who'd listen that this was punishment for the tax on wool. The guards barely looked at faces anymore; they looked at hands, checking for the ash-mark of Aldric's levies. Veyra's forged papers got us through with a bored wave, and then we were inside, under walls so high the starless dark above them felt like a lid.

That's when it started.

I should describe it properly, because Cyrus asked me once, later, what it was like, and I told him *like a headache*, which was a lie, and he deserved better. It wasn't pain. It was arrival. The final fragment hadn't been hidden in a shrine or a relic or a bone-singer's song. It had been waiting in me the whole time, sealed under the others, and now that we stood inside the city where it happened — inside the radius of whatever old power the ghost-king had threaded through these stones — the seal simply opened, the way a door opens when you stop leaning against it.

* * *

I was in a room I remembered not remembering.

White marble. A brazier burning something sweet and heavy. My mother sat by the window in a gray dress, her hands folded, and across from her stood a man in ceremonial robes with a voice like dry paper unrolling.

"The wards are failing," the robed man said. "They have been failing for thirty years. The anchor-soul thins with each generation, and the next renewal must be—"

"I know what it must be." My mother's voice. I knew it the way you know a song from before language — I didn't remember ever hearing it, and yet every part of me leaned toward it. "I have read the rites."

"Then you understand that the vessel must be willing. Unwilling blood poisons the working. Half the old catastrophes—"

"She will be willing." My mother turned to the window. Below, in a courtyard, a girl was practicing sword forms alone in the snow — a girl with my build, my stubborn shoulders, though I couldn't see her face. Me. Younger me, before. "That is why it must be done gently. Why she must never be told until the night itself. A child raised knowing she is to die becomes either a martyr or a runaway, and we cannot afford either."

"And if she learns anyway?"

My mother was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had gone soft in a way that made my chest hurt worse than shouting would have.

"Then she will learn that I chose the kindest version of an unkind thing. Someone must renew the wards. If not her, the rites name no other vessel, and the borders fall, and the old things come down from the mountains, and thousands die instead of one. I have done the arithmetic a hundred times. There is no version where I am innocent. So I have chosen the version where I am *merciful*, and I will live with that, and I will make sure she never has cause to hate me for it."

The robed man murmured something about mercy being a luxury of queens.

And my mother — my mother smiled. Small, tired, certain. "No," she said. "Mercy is a luxury of people who can't bear the truth. I can't bear it. That's rather the point."

* * *

I came back to myself standing in a doorway off the processional avenue, and Cyrus was holding my elbow, and I had apparently been standing there long enough that he'd steered us out of the foot traffic.

"Astrid." His voice was very level, the way it got when he was frightened. "Your nose is bleeding."

I touched my upper lip. Blood, warm and ordinary. I laughed. It came out wrong, too loud, and a beggar by the wall gave me a wide berth.

"That's the last one," I said. "That's all of them. I'm complete. Isn't that wonderful? I'm a whole person again, and do you know what the whole person knows?" I was still laughing. I couldn't stop. "My mother designed it. Not Aldric — well, yes, Aldric, he sharpened the knife, we'll get to him — but the *design*, the gentle version, the let's-not-tell-her-until-the-night-itself version — that was her. She sat by a window and watched me practice sword forms in the snow and decided I would die beautifully at midwinter and never know why, because knowing would upset me, and my feelings mattered more than my—" My voice cracked clean through. "Cyrus. She loved me. That's the worst part. She loved me, and love was the *mechanism*. They needed a willing vessel, so they made sure I was raised grateful. Raised devoted. Raised to walk onto that altar thinking it was an honor my mother had protected for me."

Cyrus said nothing. He was good at that — at silence as a held door. But his hand stayed on my arm, steady, and the dead in his bones, I could feel them almost, a hush like snowfall.

"They were going to kill me," I said, quieter now. "And she thought that was kindness. And the truly funny thing—" the laugh again, awful, "—the truly funny thing is she was right that I'd have said yes. Don't you see? That's what I can't get out from under. I found out, and I ran, and I broke the wards, and half the country is starving in the dark because of it — and some cold little corner of me still whispers that Mother's arithmetic holds. One life against thousands. I did the same sum. I just refused to accept the answer."

"You broke the wards to save yourself," Cyrus said carefully. "That's not a crime."

"It's not a *crime*," I agreed. "It might be a catastrophe. Those aren't the same thing, and I've stopped being able to tell them apart." I wiped the blood from my lip and looked at my red fingertips. "Every fragment, I kept thinking the bottom was somewhere below, and I'd reach it and grieve and climb back up. But there isn't a bottom, Cyrus. There's just more well."

* * *

Here is what despair does that grief doesn't: grief wants company. Despair wants directions.

By late afternoon I had stopped hearing the city. We moved through it — Veyra leading now, cutting us down side streets away from the palace approaches, muttering about checkpoints — and I nodded in the right places and understood nothing, because inside my skull the memory kept replaying with additions, corrections, my mind cruelly furnishing the scene. The brazier smoke. The exact pitch of her voice. And underneath it, older memories unlocking like rooms in a house I'd inherited: midwinter eves, my mother braiding my hair, saying *you are the best of us, Astrid* — and meaning, all along, *you are the most suitable*. Every tenderness retroactively poisoned. I kept trying to find one memory of her that survived contact with the truth, and they died one after another, like stars.

Like stars. Everything came back to that, didn't it. I had put them out. I had shattered the wards with my own terrified hands on the night I fled, and I had told myself since — when I let myself think of it at all — that it was self-defense, that no one should be a sacrifice, that the arithmetic was monstrous. But standing in the capital, with my mother's voice fresh in my ears, another voice arrived, quieter and much older, wearing my own tone: *You didn't break the wards because the sacrifice was wrong. You broke them because you were afraid. If you'd been braver, kinder, more like her hoped you'd be, the stars would still be shining and the dyers' children wouldn't be freezing in a tent.*

That voice was a liar. I knew it was a liar. Knowing has never once stopped a voice.

"Astrid." Veyra had stopped at a corner, hand out flat — halt. Ahead, the plaza opened toward the palace quarter, and there were soldiers there, and banners, and a cordon around the great gates with officers in silver-and-black. Aldric's colors. "Change of route. There's a postern through the tanners' quarter, but it adds an hour and—"

"Or," I heard myself say, "we could just walk up and knock."

Both of them stared at me.

"He's looking for me," I said. Reasonably. Pleasantly. I could hear how pleasant I sounded and it frightened me more than the soldiers did. "Aldric. He's been hunting me across half the country, spending his hunters and his gold, and here I am, delivered. No more chase. No more war over my body. He gets what he wants, I get answers from the source instead of from dead women's echoes, and everyone goes home." I took a step toward the plaza. "It's tidy. I like tidy."

Veyra moved faster than a woman her size had any right to. Her fist closed in my collar and she put me back against the wall, forearm across my chest, her face inches from mine, and her eyes were furious and — this is what undid me — afraid. For me.

"Listen to me," she said, low and hard. "I served that man for eleven years. I tore the crest off my own shoulder two weeks ago and I still wake up reaching for it. Whatever you think you're walking toward, it isn't answers. It's a throne room and a knife, and he will use your guilt like a leash, because that is what men like Aldric *do* — they find the wound and they hold the reins." Her grip shook, just slightly. "I spent years following orders I suspected were rotten because it was easier than finding out. Do not make my mistake at the gates of his own house."

"I killed the sky," I whispered.

"No," Veyra said. "You didn't. And even if you had — the difference between you and him is that it would keep you up at night." She released me slowly, watching my face like a woman watching ice on a river. "Grief later. Movement now."

Cyrus hadn't spoken through any of it. Now he stepped in close, and he didn't touch me, and somehow that was louder than Veyra's grip. He just looked at me with those eyes that had carried too many dead voices for too many years, and he said, quietly:

"When you're ready to hate her honestly — not the polished grief, the honest kind, the kind that's allowed to be angry — I'll sit with you while you do it. However long it takes. But you don't get to hand yourself to Aldric tonight just because your mother's ghost told you that you deserve it. Nobody carves you but you. That's—" his mouth twitched, tired, "—that's practically my trade motto."

Something in my chest cracked — not broke, cracked, the way ice cracks before it becomes water. I laughed again, but it came out human this time, wet and ugly, and I put my forehead against his shoulder for exactly four seconds, which was all I could afford.

"Tanners' quarter," I said. "Lead on, Captain."

Veyra grunted and turned down the alley — and stopped so abruptly I walked into her back.

At the far end of the lane, unhurried, flanked by six of the silver-and-black, stood a man I knew only from a throne-room echo and a blood-soaked memory fragment: broad, silvering, handsome in the way well-maintained cruelty is handsome. Prince Aldric smiled at us down the length of the alley like a man greeting guests he'd expected all along.

"Sister," he called pleasantly. "You're late for your coronation."
