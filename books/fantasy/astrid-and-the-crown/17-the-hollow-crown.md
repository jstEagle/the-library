## 17. The Hollow Crown

The old capital had a name once. Everyone had told me what it was — Veyra, the smugglers, the dead themselves — and every one of them had said it the way you'd say the name of a man who owed you money. I'd already forgotten it by the time we crossed the gates, which was fitting, because the city had clearly forgotten itself first.

Half its towers stood like pulled teeth. The streets were silted with ash that hadn't come from any fire I could name, and the few lamps still burning burned the wrong color, a bruised violet that made every shadow look like it was thinking about something. Above it all, the sky stayed black where the stars should have been. A year of that. A hole in the heavens shaped exactly like everything we'd lost.

Astrid walked beside me with her hood down, and I kept glancing at her the way you keep touching a cracked tooth. Since the gates, since the last fragment had come home to her like a bird to a roost, she'd been quiet in a way that frightened me more than screaming would have. Screaming I knew what to do with. This silence had architecture. It had rooms.

"Say something," I said, for the fourth time since the gates.

"I'm deciding," she said, "whether I'm walking into a trap or walking home."

"Can it be both?"

"Everything in my life has been both." She looked up at the starless dark. "That's rather the problem, isn't it?"

Veyra brought up the rear with her sword drawn and her conscience, I suspected, drawn tighter still. A week ago she'd been hunting us. Two days ago she'd cut down two of her own soldiers to keep them from dragging Astrid to Prince Aldric. She hadn't apologized for the first part. I hadn't asked her to. There are kinds of arithmetic you don't perform out loud.

"The throne room's ahead," she said. "The old one. The new court sits in Glasshall, but the wards—" she stopped, corrected herself. "The wards were always anchored here. Under the mountain. Everyone knows that. Nobody knows why."

"The dead know why," I said. And they did. They'd been shouting it at me for three days now, the voices in my bones rising to a chorus I could barely sort into words. *The crown. The crown. He wants the crown. He was never crowned.* I hadn't told Astrid that part yet. I was running out of things I hadn't told her, and every one of them had a price tag.

The doors of the old palace stood open. Of course they did. Traps always come with the doors already open; it saves everyone time.

* * *

Inside, the throne room was exactly wrong.

I'd expected ruin. What I got was preservation. The banners hung clean, the stone floor gleamed, and the great throne at the far end — a chair carved from a single block of black granite, older than any kingdom I could name — sat empty and waiting the way a mouth waits.

The violet light came from nowhere and everywhere. And in the middle of that light stood a man.

He was tall the way winter is tall. His armor was the kind you see on tomb effigies, out of fashion by four hundred years, and his face was a face you recognized without ever having seen it, because it was carved into every milestone and courthouse lintel in the borderlands: the First King. The Ghost-King. The one the bone-singer of Halvard had spat when she said his name, which, coming from a woman with no eyes and infinite patience, had told me plenty.

"Astrid," he said, and his voice was the sound a wardstone makes just before it breaks. "And the bone-carver. And the captain who changed her mind. How economical of fate. It brought me all three at once."

"You're the one who darkened the sky," Veyra said. Her sword point didn't waver, which I respected, since mine had gone distinctly soft in my hand.

"I darkened nothing. I merely stopped holding the sky up." He turned to Astrid, and the whole room turned with him; the violet light bent toward her like a congregation. "The wards of this country were never anchored to stone, child. Stone is a crutch. Stone forgets. The wards were anchored to a soul — to the royal line itself, to one living daughter of the blood, renewed at each midwinter by a small and merciful death. Your mother designed the rite. Your uncle scheduled it. And you—" he smiled, and it was the worst thing I'd seen all year, and I had seen a village massacre that autumn — "you broke it. You tore yourself out of the anchor and the whole lattice came down, and the stars went out, and every old hatred in the world woke up in the dark. All of that, from one frightened girl."

Astrid didn't flinch. I watched her not flinch, and it was the bravest thing I saw that night, and I saw several.

"I know what I did," she said. "I've remembered it. All of it."

"Then you know the wards can be remade only one way. A soul of the blood, whole and willing, crowned beneath the empty sky." He spread his hands, and I swear the throne behind him leaned forward. "I have waited four hundred years beneath this floor for a crown. I built the sacrifice. I whispered to your uncle. I let the girl run, because a girl who runs and returns is *willing*, and willingness is the only coin this old magic still accepts. You were always going to bring yourself to me, Astrid. I simply arranged the road."

The silence after that had weight. I felt it settle on my shoulders like snow.

"You used her," I said. "Her whole life. Her memories. The fear. The hunters. All of it was your—"

"Your *craft*," he said, turning those tomb-effigy eyes on me, and I felt every voice I carried go silent at once, the way birds go silent before a storm. "Yes, carver. I have craft too. You of all people should appreciate the long patience of it."

Astrid stepped forward. I grabbed for her arm and got sleeve.

"You want me crowned," she said. "And then what?"

"Then the wards drink deep, the stars return, and the country is saved. And I rule it." He said it plainly, without shame, which is the most frightening register a monster has. "A kingdom needs a king. Yours has had nothing but frightened men with knives for four hundred years. I am not frightened. I am not a man. I am the only thing in this country that remembers what it was *for*."

"And me?" she asked. "After the crown drinks?"

The pause was very small. But I've spent my life listening to the spaces between words, and I heard it.

"You would be its queen," he said.

"She'd be its *anchor*," I said. "Dead or hollow, one of the two. That's what the rite was. That's what it always was. You're not offering her a throne, you're offering her a *socket*."

"Cyrus," Astrid said quietly.

"No. I've been quiet my whole life. I kept my head down, I kept my gift secret, I kept every promise except the ones that mattered, and it bought me a workshop full of dead people's voices and a slow leak in my own soul." My hands were shaking. I let them. "I'm done being quiet."

The Ghost-King regarded me the way a mason regards a pebble in his mortar.

"And what would the carver offer instead?" he said. "You have no blood of the kings. You have no soul the wards would drink. You have a gift and a grave's worth of borrowed voices and—" he tilted his head, and I felt him *look*, the way the bone-singer had looked, down through me to the marrow — "ah. Ah, yes. You have less than you did. The dead have been eating you for years, haven't they? Names. Songs. Your mother's face. You are a house with the furniture going out the windows, and still you keep the door open. Why?"

"Because someone has to," I said.

"Sentiment," he said, with the contempt of four centuries. "It rots."

"It's the only thing that doesn't," Astrid said.

And then I did the thing I'd been building toward since Halvard, since the bone-singer put her hand on my wrist and told me I was dying by inches, since before that, maybe, since the first night a dead man's voice moved through my bones and I chose to answer instead of running.

I knelt. Not to him. To the room, to the wards, to the old dark magic sleeping under the floor that had been fed on royal blood for a thousand years. I put my palm flat on the stone, and I spoke to it the way I spoke to bone — because under everything, a ward is just a very large carving, and I have never met a carving I couldn't talk to.

"I can't give you royal blood," I said to it, to him, to all of it. "But I can give you the rest of me. Every year of hearing I've got left. Every voice I carry. Take the gift whole — take the *man* the gift was keeping alive — and let her go. She walks out of here free, name intact, and the wards can drink from me until I'm empty."

"Cyrus, *no*—" Astrid's voice cracked like the wards had cracked.

"It's a fair trade," I said, and heard how wry I sounded, and was almost proud of that. "I was going to spend it all anyway, one voice at a time, in a workshop in the mountains. This way it buys something."

The Ghost-King was silent for a long moment. The violet light flickered — and I realized, with a lurch, that it wasn't light at all. It was *hunger*. It had been hunger the whole time, four hundred years of it, and it was looking at me the way I'd once looked at a rich man's commission when the winter was lean.

"You would give freely," he said. "Not under compulsion. Not in terror. *Freely.*" His voice changed, and for one instant the tomb-king sounded like something older and more tired. "Do you know how long it has been since anyone offered this magic anything freely? The kings paid in daughters. The priests paid in lies. You kneel in the ash of their throne and offer the only coin that was ever truly asked for."

"Then take it," I said. "Take it and let her go."

"Cyrus, look at me." Astrid had my shoulder, was hauling me up, and her eyes were furious and wet and more alive than anything else in that room. "You don't get to do this. You don't get to buy my freedom with *yourself*, that's not — that's not how this works, we're supposed to—"

"Supposed to what?" I said gently. "Survive each other? We were never going to manage it. One of us was always carrying too much."

"Then I carry you," she said. "That's the whole point. That's what I *learned*."

And the Ghost-King laughed.

It was a terrible sound, a sound like a mountain settling, and the whole throne room shivered with it, and I understood in that moment — with the clarity you get maybe twice in a life, and both times too late — that we had made a mistake. That the offer had not been a bargaining chip. That a freely given sacrifice was not a price this magic would accept.

It was a *key*.

"Yes," the Ghost-King breathed. "Yes. That is the rite entire. That is what the old kings never understood — the blood was only ever a substitute, a poor coin minted because no one would pay the true one. A soul given *freely*, by one who hears the dead, for the sake of another — do you know what that would do to the wards? What it would do to *me*? Four hundred years I waited for a willing royal. I should have been waiting for a willing *carver*."

The violet light came down like a curtain.

I felt it take hold of the gift in my bones — not cruelly, worse than cruelly, *gratefully* — and Astrid was screaming my name, and Veyra was shouting something with steel in it, and the voices I carried rose up all at once, hundreds of them, the dead I'd been keeping for thirty years, and they weren't screaming.

They were singing.

And under their song, cold and patient and older than the throne, I felt the crown begin to move toward us on its own.

* * *

The last thing I heard clearly — before the gift in my bones went white-hot and the room dissolved into light the color of a struck anvil — was Astrid's voice, right against my ear, fierce as a forge:

"You don't get to leave. Do you hear me, Cyrus Iversen? You don't get to *leave*."

And then, through the roaring, through the hunger and the song and the four-hundred-year dark, I felt her reach past me — past my sacrifice, past the Ghost-King's great closing hand — and lay her own palm on the stone beside mine.

She was going to take the crown herself.

The stars, somewhere far above us, felt it too.

One of them came back.
