## 19. The Price Named

*Astrid*

The stars came back one at a time, like a city relighting its lamps after a long curfew, and I stood in the wreckage of the throne room and thought: *that was too easy.*

I should have known better. Nothing in my life — the parts of it I could remember, anyway — had ever come cheap. The wards drank deep when I poured myself into them. My whole remembered self, every carved-away year, every shame and small kindness, all of it went down their throats in a single swallow. And magic, as any bone-carver will tell you, never drinks for free.

The first thing to go was my name.

I felt it leave the way you feel a tooth come loose — not pain exactly, but a strange new hollowness where something used to sit flush against the rest of me. I opened my mouth to say it, because some stubborn part of me wanted to hold on with both hands, and what came out was air shaped like nothing at all.

"Astrid," Cyrus said beside me. He said it quietly, the way you'd set down something fragile on a high shelf. "Your name is Astrid."

And there it was again, whole and warm, handed back to me in his voice. I clung to it. I want that understood: I did not let go gracefully. I gripped that name like a drowning woman grips driftwood, and if the price of keeping it was hearing it only from one mouth for the rest of my life, then so be it.

Around us, the court was waking up out of the ghost-king's long enchantment, and this is where the accounting began.

A chamberlain — a grey, precise man whose name I had learned during my weeks as Aldric's "guest" — looked straight through me. Not past me. *Through* me, the way you look through a window at weather. His eyes found Cyrus standing next to an empty space and politely addressed the empty space's companion instead.

"Sir. The princess— " He stopped. Frowned. Somewhere behind his face, a door swung open onto nothing. "Forgive me. There was someone here. Wasn't there?"

"There was," Cyrus said. "There is."

The chamberlain blinked at me for three full seconds. Then his gaze slid off me like rain off oiled leather, and he bowed to Cyrus, and he went to see about the fires.

That was the shape of it, everywhere, all that first day. Servants stepped around me without seeing me. Guards gave Cyrus odd looks for talking to himself. A kitchen girl handed me a bowl of soup — *handed it to me*, looked right at my hands taking it — and then turned to Cyrus and asked who'd taken the bowl off her tray, because she could have sworn she'd counted wrong.

Only Cyrus saw me whole. Only Cyrus said my name.

You would think that would be the worst of it. It wasn't. The worst came later, alone, when I tried to remember my own face and found the mirror in my mind fogging over. I knew I had my mother's jaw. I knew people had once told me I looked like her, and that the telling had hurt, though I couldn't say why anymore. I knew I had freckles. I was almost sure about the freckles.

"Cyrus," I said that night, in the ruined shrine where we'd sheltered after Glasshall, "tell me what I look like."

He didn't laugh at me. That's the thing about Cyrus Iversen; he has spent his whole life listening to the dead, so the living have to work very hard to surprise him.

"You've got a scar through your left eyebrow," he said. "From a riding fall, before your memories were cut. You hate being told you look stern. Your hair does what it wants and you've made peace with it, mostly. And you're—" he paused, choosing, "—you're the kind of person who breaks the world to keep from being broken in it, and then spends the rest of her life trying to mend what she broke."

"That last part isn't a face."

"It's the part I'd miss first," he said, "if it went."

I made him repeat it all twice, and I carved it into memory the way he carves into bone — deep, deliberate, meant to last. Because here is the truth I haven't said aloud yet, not even to him: my name lives safe in his mouth, but everything else is thinning. I can feel it. Like ink held under running water. Some mornings I reach for the throne room, for Aldric's face going white as the ghost-king unwove him, and find only the fact of it — *it happened* — without the picture. I am becoming a summary of myself.

But the stars are back. Every night I go outside and count them like coins, and every night they're all there, and I think: *worth it.* And then some mornings I think: *ask me again tomorrow,* and I don't know which answer is the honest one.

*Veyra*

I have served the crown since I was sixteen years old, which means I have spent eleven years learning to do things I didn't believe in and call it duty. What happened in the throne room broke something in that arrangement, and what happened after — the great fading, the country waking to find its princess gone from every mind but one — finished the job.

Here is what the morning after the coronation looked like from where I stood, which was everywhere at once:

Prince Aldric dead in the snow of the outer courtyard, killed by his own hunters when he tried to order them to seize the throne for himself. I gave the order to let it happen. I'll carry that. It sits lighter than some things, heavier than others.

The old king's council scattered like startled pigeons, each lord suddenly remembering urgent business on his own lands, because a crown with no claimant is just a hat, and hats don't command armies.

And four hundred thousand people in the capital who knew, in the marrow-deep way you know weather coming, that *something* had saved them — that the stars had gone out and come back, that the wards were singing again along every border — and who could not have told you what or who had done it. The story had a hole in it shaped exactly like a person.

Into that hole, unfortunately, walked me.

"The captain held the city together," they'll say later, and I want it recorded now, while I still control the record, that holding a city together mostly consists of standing in rooms full of frightened important men and saying *no* until they run out of terrible ideas.

Lord Merric wanted martial law. No. Lord Cassel wanted to blame Thornfell and march on the mountain provinces before "they" could strike again — strike with what? Stars? No. The guildmasters wanted the wards' restoration declared a miracle of the capital's patron saint, which at least had the virtue of hurting no one, so I allowed it, because governing is mostly deciding which lies do the least damage.

What I could not allow — what I refused, three days running, in front of increasingly red faces — was the hunt.

Because there *was* a hunt still. Half-formed, unofficial, the kind that grows in the dark corners of a nervous court. Someone had been the anchor of the wards. Someone had been the sacrifice that failed. Someone had broken the sky and mended it, and that someone was a loose end, and courts *hate* loose ends. Men I had drunk with, fought beside, trusted with my back — I watched them start asking quiet questions in quiet rooms. Where did the stranger go, the one nobody remembers? Who was she? Find her.

Find her. As if she hadn't already paid more than any of them would pay in ten lifetimes.

On the fourth day I stood up in council and said, in my best parade-ground voice, the voice that makes recruits stand straighter without knowing why: "The crisis is ended. The borders are sealed by working wards for the first time in a generation. There is no enemy. There is no conspiracy. There is no *someone*." I let that land. "And any man who goes looking for one answers to me."

They hated it. They obeyed it. That's the whole art of command, if anyone asks.

Then I went down to the shrine district, alone, out of uniform, and knocked on the door of a bone-carver's workshop, because there was a debt between us I didn't know how to carry.

She opened the door herself. I recognized her instantly — which surprised me, given what everyone said about the fading — and then I understood: I had met her *before*, in Aldric's cells at Glasshall, when I'd stood guard over a prisoner I was ordered to kill and instead started asking questions. Some memories root deeper than magic can pull. Or maybe the magic left the ones who'd earned theirs honestly.

"You're alive," I said. Brilliant opening. Eleven years of command, and that's what I brought to the door.

"I'm mostly alive," she said. "It's complicated. Do you want tea?"

I wanted about six things, and tea was none of them, but I sat and drank it anyway while she and the carver told me what the price had been. Her name, kept alive in one head. Her face, thinning like frost on glass. And the rest of the world's memory of her, gone — including, I realized with a cold turn in my stomach, mine, except for those weeks in Glasshall, which meant that someday soon even those might wear away, and I would forget I had ever sat in this room drinking bad tea with the woman who gave the sky back.

"So the crown has no heir," I said slowly, working it through. "No claimant. No bloodline anyone remembers having a reason to protect. And the lords are already circling."

"They'll fight over it," Cyrus said. He has a way of stating the obvious like it's a diagnosis. "Or they'll tear it apart."

"Or," Astrid said — and there was something in her voice I'd heard once before, in a cell, the night she talked me halfway to treason — "or someone takes it apart *on purpose*. Carefully. Before it eats anyone else."

I looked at her. She looked back, steady, half-erased, entirely unafraid.

"You want me to be regent," I said.

"I want someone to hold the door open," she said, "while we take the crown apart from the inside. And you're the only person in this city both honest enough and stubborn enough to do it."

I should have said no. Every instinct bred into me across eleven years of service said *no, crowns don't come apart, they only change heads.*

Instead I heard myself say: "I'll need a guard I can trust. And a good lawyer. And someone to tell the lords no louder than I do."

"You have a parade-ground voice," Astrid said. "Use it."

God help the country. It's in my hands now.

* * *

*Astrid*

Three weeks after the stars returned, I woke in the shrine's back room and reached for my mother's face and found nothing there at all — not the memory, not even the ache where the memory used to be. Just the knowledge that there had been an ache. Grief for a grief. It's a strange arithmetic.

I got up and went and stood over Cyrus where he slept among his carvings, and I listened to him breathe, and I checked, the way you check a wound, that the name was still there.

"Astrid," I whispered to myself, testing it.

It held. Thin as wire, but it held.

He opened one eye. Bone-carvers sleep lightly; too many voices in their bones, he says, to ever fully sink. "Bad night?"

"I'm losing the shape of things," I told him. "Not the facts. The facts stay. But the *me* of it keeps getting thinner. Soon I'll be a report about a person instead of a person."

He sat up in the dark, and I felt him take my hand and press it flat against his forearm, against the old carved lines there, the way he does when he wants me to hear what the dead remember. And the bones sang — faintly, the way they always do around him — and what they sang was me. A girl on a smuggler's road clutching a broken wardstone. A girl pressing her palm to a relic and hearing her own lost voice. A girl in a flooded mine tunnel catching a falling man without knowing how she knew to catch him. A girl in a blizzard, furious, betrayed, forgiving. A girl on a throne, pouring herself into the dark.

The dead remember me perfectly. Isn't that a joke worth carving somewhere? The living forgot me in a fortnight, but the dead hold every line of me, and they sing me to the one man who needed the song.

"See?" Cyrus said softly. "Still there. All of it."

"For how long?"

"As long as I'm carrying voices," he said, "which is as long as I'm breathing. And after that—" he touched the shelf above his bed, where a length of pale bone waited, blank and prepared, "—after that, it's written down."

"You'd carve me into bone."

"I'd carve you into anything that lasts," he said. "Stone, if bone wears poorly. I've been pricing chisels."

I laughed, and it cracked something loose in my chest, and for a moment the thinning didn't matter, because a name in one living mouth and a story in dead men's bones and a blind singer's prophecy waiting on a shelf — that's more permanence than most queens get, and I was never going to be a queen anyway. That was rather the point.

Outside, the stars burned ordinary and complete. In the city, Veyra was presumably standing in some marble room saying *no* to powerful men, holding a door open for a crown's dismantling. Somewhere south, lords were sharpening arguments they thought were knives. There would be work ahead — hard, political, tedious work, the kind I'd once fled across a country to avoid.

But that was tomorrow's ledger. Tonight I sat in a shrine full of patient ghosts, next to a man who alone in all the world could say my name, and I took account.

Paid in full: one kingdom's sky. One dynasty's claim. One girl's reflection in the world's memory.

Owed, outstanding: whatever comes next.

"Cyrus," I said, "tell me again what I look like. Start with the scar."

And he did — patiently, completely, the way you read a sacred text — while I memorized myself one more time, against the morning when I'd need it again.

*End of Chapter 19*
