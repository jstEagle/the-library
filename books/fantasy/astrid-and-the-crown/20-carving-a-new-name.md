## 20. Carving a New Name

The trouble with having your name eaten by magic is that nobody warns you about the paperwork.

There was no paperwork, of course. There never is, in the aftermath of miracles. There's just the morning after the stars come back, when you wake in a borrowed room above a carver's shop in a town called Millbrook — population, according to the sign at the crossroads, *smaller than it used to be* — and you reach for yourself and find that the reaching comes up empty.

I'd known it would happen. I had agreed to it. There is a difference between knowing a thing and living inside it, though, the way there's a difference between reading about drowning and being underwater.

My name was gone.

Not from my tongue — I could still say it, or something shaped like it, a word that slid off me like rain off oiled leather. But when I said it, nothing answered. The word had no hooks in it anymore. It didn't catch on anything inside me. And out in the world it was worse: the innkeeper who'd served us supper called me "miss" without hesitation, not because he was being polite but because the name simply wouldn't stay in his head. Veyra, who had hunted me across half a kingdom, who had once held my face in her memory like a warrant held in her fist, looked at me across the breakfast table with the pleasant blankness of a woman meeting a stranger, and asked if I was Cyrus's sister.

"Something like that," Cyrus had said, before I could answer, and his voice had been steady, and his hands around his cup had not been.

That was three days ago. Now I sat in his workshop — our workshop, if I was honest about the arrangement we were circling — surrounded by curls of bone shavings and the smell of linseed oil, watching him work, and trying to decide what to call myself.

"You're frowning at the antler," he said without looking up. He was roughing out the shape of a comb, his knife moving in those long patient strokes that made even the dead go quiet to listen. Or so he claimed. He'd been quieter lately. I'd noticed. I'd decided not to notice.

"I'm frowning at the problem of names."

"Mm." Shavings curled away from the blade. "Any candidates?"

"Everything sounds wrong. I tried *Maren* this morning. The baker's daughter is Maren. I tried *Sela*. There's a Sela who sells eels by the bridge, and she looked at me like I'd stolen the name off her stall."

"You did steal it off her stall, morally speaking."

"That's what I'm saying. Every name I pick belongs to someone, because every name belongs to someone. That's the whole point of them." I turned a scrap of bone over in my fingers, a failed attempt at one of the little stars he sold at market. My carving looked like something a goat had chewed. "And the ones that don't belong to anyone sound made up."

"They all sound made up, if you say them enough times." He set the knife down and blew across the comb's surface. "My mother named me after a river because she couldn't think of anything else. Half the men in her village were named after rivers. Nobody found it strange until they left home."

"And then they found it strange?"

"And then they found everything strange. That's what leaving home is for." He glanced up at me then, and I caught the thing in his face he was trying not to have there — the careful look, the one that weighed whether today was a day I wanted to be seen clearly. "You don't have to choose today."

"I know."

"You don't have to choose ever, technically. You could just be the woman who lives above the shop. Towns are good at letting people be *the woman who lives above the shop*. It's one of their better features."

"It's not the name I'm actually worried about."

"No," he said. "I didn't think it was."

* *

Here is what I had lost, in the accounting I did each night while the ordinary stars — small, stubborn, restored — moved over Millbrook:

I had lost my name, which was the price, and which I had agreed to pay with my eyes open, standing in a throne room under a sky going slowly back together.

I had lost my face, mostly. People saw a young woman of no particular distinction. Veyra saw a stranger. The guards at Glasshall, when we'd passed through on our way south, had looked straight through me, and one of them had flirted, which had been its own special horror.

I had kept my memories — all of them, the sacrifice and the breaking and my mother's face in the lamplight as she explained why it had to be me, why mercy wore such strange clothing. Those stayed. The magic had taken the name and let the guilt remain, which struck me as either cruelty or honesty, depending on the hour.

And I had lost — this was the part I circled and circled, like a bird over water — the certainty that anyone besides two people in the world had ever been me. Somewhere there had been nurses and tutors and a girl who shared my lessons, a groom who taught me to ride badly. They remembered a princess. They did not remember *me*, the specific weight of me, and they never would again. I had become a rumor of myself. A story with the protagonist sanded out.

"You could write it down," Cyrus had said once, early, before we understood the shape of the loss. "Paper remembers."

"Paper burns."

"So does everything else. Write it anyway."

But paper felt flimsy for it. Paper felt like the kind of promise you make to someone you expect to disappoint.

Which is how I came to be sitting in a bone-carver's workshop holding a failed star and working up to asking the largest favor of my second life.

* * *

"Cyrus."

"Hm."

"I want you to carve my story."

His knife stopped. Not dramatically — he wasn't a dramatic man; drama was wasted on someone who spent his life listening to the dead, who had heard every kind of speech there was — but it stopped, and the silence where the knife-sound had been was very loud.

"In bone," I went on, because now that I'd started I couldn't stop, and my voice had gone too quick, the way it did when I was afraid. "Not all of it. Not — I'm not asking for a saga. But the true shape of it. The princess whose name was taken. The wards, the stars, the choice I made. Carved so it lasts. So that someday, someone — a hundred years, five hundred — finds it and knows there was a person here. Not a legend. Not a warning. A person, who did a bad thing for a reason, and paid for it, and—" My throat closed. I looked at the shavings on the floor. "And was loved. Maybe. If that part isn't too much to ask."

He set the knife down all the way, and turned on his stool to face me, and for a long moment he said nothing, which from Cyrus was practically an aria.

"You understand what you're asking," he said finally.

"A story in bone. You do it for other people. You told me — the merchant's wife, the drowned boy from Halvard—"

"For other people, yes." His jaw worked. "Astrid."

The name landed on me the way it always did now — sliding off, catching nowhere, and yet from his mouth it still made something in my chest turn over, because he was the only mouth in the world it worked from. He said it like a man holding onto a rope.

"When the magic took your name," he said, "it didn't take it from everyone equally. It took it from every living memory except mine. Do you know why? Because I was carrying it. All those years of voices in my bones — the dead I hold, the names I keep — your name got tangled into that. Into what I carry. It's anchored in me now, the way the wards used to be anchored in you." He rubbed his thumb along the edge of the workbench, a nervous habit he thought I hadn't catalogued. "If I carve your story into bone and give it to the world, I'm not just telling a tale. I'm putting your name somewhere outside me. Outside the only place it lives."

"And?"

"And I don't know what happens." He said it flatly, honestly, hating every word. "Maybe nothing. Maybe the anchor holds regardless. Maybe the moment your name exists in stone — in bone — outside me, the magic finishes its work and takes it from me too. And then you're not just forgotten by the world. You're forgotten by the one person who—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I won't risk it. That's the answer. I'm sorry. Ask me for anything else."

I had expected resistance. I had prepared arguments. What I had not prepared for was the rawness in his voice, and it undid my arguments like wet string.

"You'd keep it anyway," I said. "Even if it costs you."

"What does that mean?"

"The bone-singer told you. Carrying too many voices is killing you, slowly, a season at a time. And now you've got my name woven in with the rest of it, and you won't put it down, and you won't share the weight, because sharing it might lose it." I stood up. The failed star fell from my hand and rolled under the bench. "That's not protection, Cyrus. That's hoarding. You're doing to yourself what the crown did to me — deciding what you can bear alone instead of asking."

"That's not fair."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't. Most true things aren't."

He looked at me for a long time. Outside, someone was leading a cow past the shop, complaining to it about the price of feed, and the ordinariness of it was almost unbearable — the world going on, milk and feed prices and church bells, as if the sky hadn't been dark for a season, as if a girl hadn't burned her own name down to save herself.

"If I carve it," he said at last, slowly, "it goes in the ground with me. Or it stays in this shop, and whoever inherits the shop keeps it. One copy. Small. Not a monument — a keepsake. Something a stranger might find and wonder at, and never trace back far enough to matter." His eyes came up to mine. "And you tell me, truly, that you can live with being unknown. Because I've watched people live without their names. The dead do it. It doesn't make them less. But it makes them lonely in a way I wouldn't wish on anyone breathing."

"I've been unknown for three days," I said. "It turns out I'm quite good at it. Better than I was at being a princess."

"That's a low bar. You were terrible at being a princess."

"I broke the wards and ran away. Historians will call it reckless."

"Historians will call you nothing. That's rather the point." But he smiled when he said it, tired and crooked, and I knew I'd won before he picked the knife back up.

"One condition," he said.

"Of course there's a condition."

"We choose the name together. Tonight. Over supper. And it doesn't have to be grand, and it doesn't have to fit you like a key — it just has to be one you can grow into. Names are boots, not crowns. You break them in."

"All right," I said. "Together."

He turned back to the workbench, and I watched him select a piece of bone from the drawer where he kept the good stock — dense, pale, from something old and long-dead that had waited years for a worthy job — and I understood that he had already known which piece he'd use. He'd probably known since the throne room. Since before.

* * *

We chose the name over stew and dark bread, arguing happily, absurdly, the way other couples argued about curtains.

He suggested river names, in honor of his mother's village, and I vetoed all of them on principle. I suggested plain names and he accused me of overcorrecting. We landed, eventually, on *Wren* — small, brown, common, a bird that survives winters by being unremarkable and stubborn, which the bone-singer would have told me described my prospects exactly.

"Wren," I tried aloud. It caught. Just slightly — a little hook, a little snag in me, the first thing in days that snagged anywhere at all. "Wren. Yes. That one."

"Wren," he repeated, testing it against whatever sense he used for the dead, listening for echoes. "It's clean. No one's haunting it."

"High praise."

"The highest I give."

Later, when the dishes were done and the lamp was low, I stood in the doorway of the workshop and watched him begin. He worked differently for this piece than I'd ever seen — slower, no roughing strokes, each line considered the way you'd consider words in a letter to someone you owed your life to. The first figure was taking shape: a girl, small, arms raised toward a sky full of carved stars.

"She should look frightened," I said from the doorway. "In that scene. She was terrified."

"Frightened and brave aren't different faces," he said. "I've carved both. Same face, every time."

I left him to it. In the little room above the shop, I lay in bed under the ordinary stars' faint light through the shutter-cracks and practiced my new name silently until it stopped feeling like a costume.

Somewhere below me, the knife whispered on. And beneath the whisper — so faint I might have imagined it, so faint I told myself I had imagined it — I heard Cyrus murmur, low and rhythmic, the way he spoke when he was listening to the dead.

Except tonight, I realized, sitting bolt upright in the dark, he wasn't murmuring to them.

He was murmuring to himself. Repeating something, over and over, soft as a prayer: a name, my name, *Astrid, Astrid, Astrid* — the way a man rehearses a thing he is afraid of losing. The way a man counts coins he expects to be robbed of.

And threaded through it, underneath it, I heard — unmistakably now, horribly — the other voices answering. Dozens of them. The ones he carried. And they were not quiet tonight.

They were pleading.
