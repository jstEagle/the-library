## 21. The Last Voice

The workshop smelled of pine shavings and old smoke, and for the first time in thirty years, it was quiet.

Not silent — I want to be precise about this, because precision is the last thing I have left. Silent would mean nothing at all. Quiet meant the voices had gone down to a murmur, the way a river goes quiet under ice. They were still there, all of them, the dead I'd carried across half of Thornfell and back again: the drowned miller's daughter from Halvard, the two smugglers Veyra's soldiers put in the ground outside Ashmarket, the nameless child whose finger-bone I'd found in a fox den on the midwinter crossing, the hundreds more who had pressed themselves against my gift over the years like moths against a lit window.

They were quieter now because Astrid's stars were back in the sky, and the dead, it turns out, mostly just wanted the sky back. Grief is easier to carry when you can see where you're going.

I sat at my bench with a knife in one hand and a piece of elk antler in the other, not carving anything in particular, just letting the blade find its own way, and I thought about what the bone-singer of Halvard had told me in her smoky little hut while Astrid waited outside in the snow.

*You are dying slowly,* she'd said, reading my radius like a ledger. *Every voice you take in takes something out. You've been spending yourself for thirty years, carver, and the account is nearly empty.*

She hadn't said it cruelly. That was almost worse.

* * *

Astrid found me there at dusk, the way she always did — no knock, just the door swinging and her filling it, sleeves rolled, sawdust in her hair because she refused to stop helping with the rough work no matter how many times I told her princesses didn't haul timber. She wasn't a princess anymore. None of us were sure exactly what she was. The magic had taken her name and her face from every memory but mine, and even hers had gone thin as spring ice, so that some mornings she'd stand at the washbasin and stare at her reflection like it owed her money.

"You're brooding," she said. "You do it with your shoulders. It's very distinctive."

"I'm working."

"You're holding a knife over a piece of antler and thinking about dying." She came in and leaned against the bench, arms crossed. "Veyra says you've been doing that for a week."

"Veyra should be busy. She's running a country."

"She says that too. Loudly. Usually at breakfast." Astrid picked up a curl of shaving and turned it in her fingers. "Cyrus. The singer told you how to let them go, didn't she? That's what you've been chewing on all week."

I put the knife down. There was no point lying to her; she'd learned to read my face the hard way, by having everyone else's lies stripped out from under her.

"She told me it's possible," I admitted. "Releasing them. One at a time. A voice goes out of your bones and into... somewhere else. The sky, maybe. The wards, maybe. She wasn't specific. Bone-singers are worse than priests for giving straight answers."

"And the cost?"

"That's the part she was vague about."

"Cyrus."

"It costs hearing," I said. "Each voice I release, I lose a little of the gift. And when the last one goes—" I shrugged, aiming for casual and probably missing by a mile. "When the last one goes, I'll hear nothing at all. Silence. Like everyone else. Like a normal man."

Astrid was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the first stars were coming up over Thornfell ridge, ordinary stars, honest ones, and I watched her watch them through the window with an expression I couldn't parse, which was unusual, because by then I could usually read her better than my own bones.

"You've carried them since you were nine years old," she said finally. "Your mother's first ghost. The miller's daughter. All of them. Thirty years, Cyrus. You gave them passage when nobody else could even see them."

"They paid for the passage. In stories. In company. In—" I stopped.

"In what?"

"In keeping me from being alone," I said, "which is a terrible thing to admit, because it means I kept them the way a miser keeps coins. Not because they needed me. Because I needed them."

"No," Astrid said, and her voice had an edge to it, the old edge, the one that had once faced down a prince in his own court while playing dumb with a face full of nothing. "You kept them because they asked, and you're the kind of fool who can't refuse a request. There's a difference. I know. I married into the difference."

"We're not married."

"I'm aware. I'm making a point." She pushed off the bench. "Do it tomorrow. At dawn. And I'm going to be there."

"You don't have to—"

"Yes, I do." She was already at the door. "Someone should witness it. Someone who remembers you before, so someone can tell you about you after. That's how this works now, isn't it? Memory kept in the smallest things?" She tapped her own chest, where her name used to live. "I'll keep yours if you keep mine."

And she left before I could answer, which was also how this worked now.

* * *

Dawn came up gray and clean over the valley, frost on the grass, woodsmoke standing straight up from the village chimneys below my workshop hill. Astrid arrived with bread and honey and a flask of something Veyra had sent up with instructions to administer liberally, and we ate sitting on the bench outside the door like it was any other morning, except that my hands were shaking, and she pretended not to notice, which was its own kindness.

"How does it work?" she asked.

"The singer said I hold the bone, speak their names — or whatever fragments of names I have left — and let them go. Willingly. Apparently the willingness matters. Most carriers grip too hard. We get possessive." I wiped my hands on my trousers. "Thirty years of habit. This might be like asking a man to unclench a fist he's held shut since childhood."

"Start small," Astrid said. "Like carving. Rough out the shape first."

So I went inside and took down the box.

It was a plain box, larchwood, iron hinges, unremarkable unless you knew what it held: a collection of bones, each one wrapped in cloth, each one a voice. The miller's daughter's knucklebone. The child's finger. A rib from one of the Ashmarket smugglers, given to me by his brother with tears and thanks. Dozens more, gathered over three decades, each one a soul that had knocked on the walls of my skull and been let in because there was nowhere else.

I carried the box out and set it on the bench between us, and Astrid looked at it the way people look at open graves.

"Which one first?"

"The miller's daughter," I said. "Maren. She's been waiting longest."

I unwrapped the knucklebone and held it in my palm, and Maren's voice rose in me the way it always did, young and wet and patient, *the water was cold, carver, the water was so cold*, and I closed my eyes and did what the singer had taught me. I unclenched. I opened the door I'd kept shut for thirty years and said her name — or the shape of it, *Maren, daughter of Halvard mill, drowned at seventeen* — and I let go.

It was like pulling a splinter that had grown into the bone. It hurt. It was also, immediately, unmistakably, a relief, and I made a sound I hadn't planned on, something between a laugh and a sob, and felt her go — up, out, away, a warmth leaving my hand and rising into the gray morning air like breath in winter.

And then a piece of me went with her.

I can't describe it better than that. A piece. Small, but real. Something that had been part of my hearing since I was a boy — the sound of rain on a roof, I realized later, actual rain, I would never again hear rain on a roof the way other people did — and it went with her, spent, paid out, gone.

"Oh," Astrid said softly. "Oh, Cyrus."

"There's more," I managed. "That was the easy one."

* * *

It took until sundown.

We worked through the box voice by voice, and I will not pretend it was bearable, because it wasn't; it was the hardest thing I have ever done, harder than Glasshall, harder than the blizzard, harder than standing in a throne room watching a girl flood herself into a crown. Each release cost me something specific and irreplaceable. When the smuggler's brother's rib went free, I lost the memory of my mother's humming — not her face, thank the dark, not yet, but the tune, gone like a coin dropped down a well. When the child's finger went, I lost the taste of my ninth winter, the first snow, the whole season, simply erased. When the old shepherd from the pass went home, I lost the sound of my own name spoken aloud, which I hadn't known I still carried until it left.

Astrid stayed the whole day. She didn't speak much. She held the box, handed me each bone when I was ready, took each one back when it was empty, and twice — only twice — she reached over and gripped my wrist hard enough to hurt, anchoring me the way I had once anchored her, and neither time did she say anything about it afterward, and both times it was the only reason I stayed in my body.

By the time the light went amber and then red and then blue, the box held one item.

One bone, one scrap of cloth, and beside the bench, alive and breathing and unnamed, Astrid.

Wait. No. Let me be exact, because I promised myself exactness. The box held one bone. But the last voice — the last voice wasn't in the box at all.

"You could stop here," Astrid said quietly. The stars were fully out now, all of them, thick as spilled salt. "Keep one. Nobody would blame you. After today, after everything you paid — keep one voice, Cyrus. Keep the quiet company."

I looked at the last bone in my palm. It was my mother's. Of course it was. I'd saved her for last for thirty years, telling myself it was honor, knowing it was fear.

"If I let her go," I said, "I won't hear anything ever again. The gift goes with the last voice. That's the price. Total silence."

"Yes."

"And then I'll be—" I gestured vaguely at myself. "Just a man. A carver. Who makes trinkets and can't hear the dead and doesn't know why his hands shake."

"Just a man," Astrid agreed. "Who chose it. Who freed thirty years of ghosts because they asked, and paid whatever it cost, and didn't flinch." She paused. "Well. Flinched a great deal, actually. I witnessed. But didn't stop."

"Why are you here?" I asked her, suddenly, stupidly, the question rising out of me like blood from a cut. "Really. Your name's gone. Your face is gone. The crown dissolved, the wards hold, Veyra holds the city, none of it needs you anymore. You could go anywhere. Be anyone. Start over clean, the way you always wanted. Why a mountain workshop and a dying carver and a box of bones?"

Astrid looked at me for a long moment. And then she smiled — not the court smile, not the amnesiac's blank pleasantness, but the real one, the crooked one, the one I'd seen maybe six times total and hoarded every one.

"Because everyone else forgot," she said. "My name, my face, my mother, my crimes, my crown. The whole world got to forget, and I got to be new. And you—" her voice caught, just slightly, "—you're the only person alive who remembers any of it. Who remembers *me*. If I leave, I'm not starting over. I'm just lost again. But if I stay—" she shrugged, helpless, furious at her own helplessness, "—if I stay, then somebody knows. Somebody keeps it. That's worth more than anywhere else I could go."

She looked down at the bone in my hand.

"So let her go," Astrid said. "Let them all go. And I'll stay, and I'll remember, and tomorrow morning when you wake up in silence for the first time since you were nine years old, I'll be in the kitchen burning something, and you'll come complain about it, and neither of us will be alone. That's the bargain. That's the whole bargain. Take it."

I held my mother's bone a moment longer. Her voice stirred in me, faint, familiar, the first voice, the one that had taught me the gift was a door and not a wound.

*Go on, little wren,* she said, in the tone she'd used for scraped knees and broken promises. *I've been dead thirty years. You don't need to carry me to prove you loved me.*

"I love you," I said out loud, to a piece of bone, in front of the only person alive who wouldn't think it strange.

*I know. Now open the door.*

I opened it. I let go. And my mother went up into the starlight like a bird released from a cage it had outgrown, taking with her the last of my hearing — and, I felt rather than heard, the last weight of thirty years, so that when it was done I sat on my own bench in my own yard under the returning stars and heard—

Nothing.

Wind. My own breath. Astrid's breathing beside me. A nightbird somewhere down the slope. The creak of the workshop door.

Ordinary sounds. Human sounds. The sounds of a world that had never contained a single ghost, and would never contain one for me again.

I started laughing. I couldn't help it. It came up out of me like water from a cracked dam, thirty years of dread and duty and grief coming out sideways as laughter, and Astrid stared at me, and then she started laughing too, and we sat there on the bench like a pair of lunatics, laughing at the silence, at the stars, at the sheer unbearable lightness of a skull with nothing in it but a brain.

"What's it like?" she asked finally, wiping her eyes.

"Quiet," I said. "Gods, it's quiet. Is this what everyone hears? Just — the world? Nothing underneath?"

"That's what everyone hears."

"It's obscene. How does anyone get anything done?"

"You get used to it," she said. "Apparently it's called peace."

* * *

We went inside. She burned the bread, as promised, and I complained, as promised, and it was the most ordinary evening of my adult life and I treasured every second of it like a miser with his last coin — except that this time, for once, the coin was real, and it was mine, and nobody's ghost had given it to me.

Later, much later, with the lamp low and the stars high, Astrid sat across the table from me turning a small object over in her fingers. I'd noticed her carrying it for days without asking. Now she set it on the wood between us and slid it across.

It was a star. Carved bone, small enough to hide in a fist, crude at the edges because I'd carved it in a hurry during the crossing, back when her hands were too frozen to hold a knife and she'd asked me to make her something true.

"You kept this," I said.

"You gave it to me."

"I give customers trinkets all the time."

"You gave *me* a star," she said, "on the worst night of my life, when I had no name and no past and no reason to believe I'd see morning. You said — I remember, I'm the only one who remembers — you said, *stars go out, but they come back. It's what they do.*" She closed her hand around it. "I've been thinking about what comes next. For us. For this place. Travelers will start using the passes again now the borders are open. They'll need things. Small things. True things."

"A roadside stall," I said slowly, the shape of it arriving in my head before I finished speaking. "Carved goods. Stars. Charms. Ordinary wares for ordinary travelers."

"And a woman selling them," Astrid said, "who has no name anyone remembers, and a man listening to nothing at all." She looked up at me across the lamplight, and her eyes were bright and steady and entirely hers, whoever that was. "What do you say, carver? Shall we be nobody together?"

I thought about the silence in my head, vast and clean as fresh snowfall. I thought about the box on the shelf, empty now, good for holding knives. I thought about a stall by the road, and travelers, and mornings, and her terrible cooking, and the long slow work of learning a person who was still learning herself.

"I say," I told her, "that it's the best offer anyone's made me in thirty years."

She grinned, crooked and real, and blew out the lamp, and outside the window the stars burned on over Thornfell — ordinary, faithful, coming back, the way they do —

and for the first time in my life, I fell asleep to the sound of nothing but the world,

and it was enough.
