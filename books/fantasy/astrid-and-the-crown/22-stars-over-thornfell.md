## 22. Stars Over Thornfell

### Cyrus

The trouble with silence, I've discovered, is that it takes practice.

For thirty years my skull was a market square at festival time — the dead elbowing through my thoughts, haggling for attention, complaining about the weather in graves they no longer occupied. Then, one by one over the past year, I let them go. Each release cost me something I can't name and gave back something I can't describe either. What remains is a quiet so complete that some mornings I startle at it, like a man who's lived beside a waterfall his whole life and wakes to find the river has moved.

"You're doing the face again," Astrid said this morning, not looking up from her carving.

"What face?"

"The one where you're listening to nothing and finding it suspicious."

"I am suspicious of it. Nothing has never behaved itself around me before."

She snorted — a small inelegant sound she'd have been beaten for at court, which is probably why she treasures it — and went back to work. We sit across from each other at the stall most days now, a plank table under an awning at the crossroads where the Halvard road meets the river ferry. Travelers pass. Some stop. The ones who stop usually buy something small: a bone star on a leather cord, a carved bird, a bead shaped like a mountain flower. They rarely ask why a carver would make stars when there are real ones overhead again, thick as spilled salt on a black cloth. If they did ask, I'd say what Astrid says: *the sky can't be carried in a pocket.*

I carve the stars. She sells them. It's a division of labor we fell into without discussing, the way you fall into most of the things that end up mattering.

A woman stopped yesterday with two children, a boy of maybe seven and a girl younger, both wearing the wide-eyed look of kids who'd spent a year of their childhood under a starless sky and hadn't yet decided whether the stars' return was permanent or a trick. The girl picked up a bone star, turned it over, held it up against the actual sky as if comparing goods.

"Is it real?" she asked.

"It's as real as anything," Astrid said. "More than most."

The girl looked at her with the flat skepticism children reserve for adults who are clearly lying about something important. "But it's not a *real* star."

"No," Astrid agreed. "It's better. A real star doesn't care if you lose it. This one will still be here tomorrow."

The girl considered this with the gravity of a magistrate, then produced a coin that had been clutched long enough to be warm. She wore the star out of the stall like a knight wearing a favor into battle. Her brother demanded one too, obviously, because he was seven.

I watched them go down the road toward the ferry and felt something in my chest do a thing I didn't have a word for. I've had a lot of those lately. Words failing me. It turns out that when you spend decades translating the dead, you get very good at language and very bad at using it for yourself.

Here's what I don't tell anyone, because telling it would make it smaller: every night before sleep, I check. Not the sky — the quiet. I press inward the way you press a bruise, testing whether the silence holds, whether some voice hasn't crept back in at the edges. And every night I find only my own thoughts, shabby and familiar as old boots, and Astrid breathing in the next room, alive, unnamed, staying.

That last part is the miracle I don't trust. The other two I've made peace with.

* * *

The border posts came down in spring. You'd think after everything — the wards, the dark, Aldric's fall, Veyra's regency — people would have learned their lesson about walls between neighbors. But learning lessons is not what countries do; countries mostly do what they were already doing, just with new excuses. So when the checkpoints opened and the tolls vanished, half the population assumed it was a trap.

It wasn't. It was just Veyra being stubborn about one thing, the way she's stubborn about everything. "I watched men die for a line drawn on a map," she told me once, over ale she complained about and drank anyway. "I'm done pretending the map matters." She runs the country now, or rather she refuses to run it while doing all the work of running it, which she calls "keeping the chair warm for someone who actually wants it." Nobody wants it. That's the joke of the age: the crown sits in Glasshall gathering dust, and the country muddles along better without it, and everyone is too superstitious to say so out loud.

Veyra visits sometimes. She arrives unannounced, in plain clothes that fool nobody, buys a bone star she pretends is for a niece, and stands at the edge of the awning looking at the road like a soldier reviewing troops that aren't there.

"Heard from the north?" she asked last month.

"There's always something from the north."

"Lords squabbling. Two of them raised levies over a mill dispute." She turned the little star in her fingers. "I could put it down. Probably."

"But?"

"But I'm tired, Iversen. And the mill dispute might resolve itself, the way things do, when nobody noble gets involved." She pocketed the star, paid triple, refused change, and left. At the corner of the road she paused — I saw her shoulders square, the old habit — and then she walked on like a woman going home instead of a captain going to war, and I decided to believe the difference mattered.

* * *

Today a traveler stopped who didn't buy anything.

He was young, dusty, with the particular hunger of someone who's been walking a long time toward something he can't quite name. He looked at the stars on the table, at Astrid, at me, and frowned the frown of a man assembling a memory that won't fit together.

"Do I know you?" he asked Astrid.

"I don't think so," she said pleasantly.

"You look — there was a story. Last winter. About the princess who broke the sky." He shook his head. "No. That's not right either. There was a girl. Nobody remembers her name. Isn't that strange? A whole story and no name in it."

"Stories are like that," Astrid said. "They keep what they need."

He bought a star after all — for his mother, he said, who'd been afraid of the dark all that year and still wouldn't sleep without a candle — and went on his way, and neither of us spoke until the sound of his boots faded.

"That happens more," Astrid said finally. "People almost remembering."

"Mm."

"Cyrus." She turned the carving knife over in her hands, not looking at me. "Do you ever worry? That someday even yours will go thin. That you'll look at me and know there was something and not know what."

I thought about lying to her. I'm good at it; I had practice enough. But we'd spent a year learning not to.

"Every day," I said. "And then I do this." I took her hand — the one with the old scar across the palm, the wardstone scar — and pressed my thumb into the bone of her wrist, where the pulse lives. "Feel that? That's not a name. Names are cheap. That's the truest thing I know how to read."

Her pulse jumped under my thumb, quick and alive and slightly annoyed, which is Astrid in miniature.

"That's terrible," she said. "You're a terrible romantic."

"I'm a bone-carver. We work with what we're given."

* * *

Tonight the sky is clear, and I sat outside after she went in, because I still do that — sit under the stars like a man paying off a debt in installments. A year ago they went out. Eleven months ago they came back, one by one, over three nights, and I remember standing in the ruins of the old capital watching the first one kindle and thinking: *so that's what it costs. Everything she was, except what one stubborn man refuses to misplace.*

The dead are gone from my bones. All but the living dead, the one voice I chose to keep, and she's inside sharpening knives and humming a song she doesn't know the origin of — a lullaby, though she thinks it's just a tune. I know its origin. I carved it into a rattle once, years ago, from a fragment of wardstone, and the dead sang it to me until I learned it. Somewhere in her hollowed-out self, it survived the flood. I've never told her. Some things you keep the way you keep embers: cupped, close, fed quietly.

Tomorrow travelers will pass again. Someone will buy a star for someone they love. And I'll listen to nothing at all, and find, for once, that nothing is enough.

### Astrid

I remember the sacrifice.

I want to be clear about that, because it seems like the sort of thing a person should be clear about, even — especially — when she's the only one who carries it. I remember the altar at midwinter, the knife that wasn't meant to hurt, the priests' gentle voices explaining that the wards needed renewal and renewal needed blood and my blood had been chosen before I could walk. I remember my mother holding my face in her hands and saying *it will be like falling asleep*, and meaning it, meaning it as mercy, and I remember choosing, in the last hour, to burn the whole beautiful machine down instead.

I remember all of it now. That's the strange arithmetic of what I paid: I lost my name, my face, my place in every living memory but one — and I kept the guilt. The magic took the cheap things and left me the expensive one. Sometimes I think that was the ghost-king's cruelest trick, and sometimes I think it was the magic being honest for once: you don't get to save a country and skip the bill.

Most days, though, I sell stars.

It sounds like nothing. It isn't nothing. For twenty years I was a vessel, an anchor, a bloodline, a bride-to-be for an altar. Now I am a woman at a roadside stall who talks children into believing a carved bone can hold back the dark, and nine times out of ten, they believe me, because children know a true thing when it's put in front of them plainly. Adults are harder. Adults want the story — the princess, the broken sky, the crown that dissolved like sugar in rain. I give them a star instead. It holds more.

Cyrus worries I'll fade. I see him checking, the way he checks everything, pressing two fingers to my wrist like he's taking a reading off the world. And he's right to worry — some mornings my own reflection takes a moment to arrive, some nights I dream in a voice I don't recognize and wake unsure whose grief I was borrowing. My name is gone. When I try to reach for it, I find a shape where it used to be, the way your tongue finds the gap of a lost tooth.

But here is what I've learned about gaps: they're also doors.

* * *

Last week a merchant woman stopped at the stall, a sharp-eyed trader with dust from three roads on her boots. She bought nothing for a long time, just turned a bone star over and over, and then said, without preamble:

"You were at Glasshall. During the falling. Weren't you."

It wasn't a question, exactly, but it wasn't an accusation either. I kept my hands moving, sanding a bird's wing smooth.

"Lots of people were at Glasshall," I said.

"Not lots of people stood in the throne room when the sky came back." Her eyes were steady. "I was a guard there. Second door on the left. I saw a girl stand up under a crown that was coming apart like ash, and I saw the stars come through the windows, and I have spent a year trying to remember her name, and I can't, and it's driving me mad."

I set down the bird. Looked at her. She was maybe forty, gray coming in at her temples, a scar on her jaw from some old campaign. One of Veyra's, once. One of the people who'd guarded the machine I broke.

"She didn't have a name anyone could keep," I said. "That was the price. She knew it going in."

"And you know that how?"

"Because I was there too."

We looked at each other for a long moment. Then the woman nodded slowly, the way soldiers nod — accepting intel, filing it away — and bought three stars, one for each of the guards who'd died in the throne room that night, and left without another word. I watched her go and found my eyes were wet, which annoyed me, and then didn't.

They remember the event. They can't remember me. It's the strangest inheritance anyone's ever had: a whole country keeps my deed and loses my face, and every few months someone stops at the stall with that same hungry frown, that same *do I know you*, and every time I get to choose again what to say. Most days I say nothing. Today I said *she knew it going in*, and it felt like setting down a stone I'd been carrying in my mouth.

Maybe that's what remembrance is, in the end. Not names cut into monuments. Just this: strangers buying small white stars for their dead, and one woman at a crossroads who knows why.

* * *

Veyra came through again on the equinox. She does that — times her visits to the turning of the year, as if she needs the calendar to justify wanting company. She and Cyrus argue about northern lords and bridge tolls and whether the army should shrink, and I make tea and let them, because watching two people who nearly killed each other for opposite sides bicker like siblings is one of the private pleasures of my new life.

This time, though, she stayed after Cyrus went in, and stood at the rail looking at the darkening sky, and said:

"The council wants to put a statue up. In Glasshall. To the Unnamed Princess." She said it carefully, watching me. "Officially she'd be anonymous. 'In honor of her who gave what cannot be named.' They think it's elegant."

"It's obscene," I said.

"Yes." She turned. "I told them no. I said the princess I served under would never have wanted it, and they asked how I could possibly know that, since no record of her survives, and I said—" she smiled, thin and fierce, the old Veyra breaking through the regent's polish— "I said because I've met the kind of person who breaks a sky to save a country, and they don't want statues. They want the country to work."

"What did they say to that?"

"They said I was insubordinate. I said yes, famously." She shrugged. "So. No statue. Instead I'm having the midwinter altars pulled out of every shrine in the capital and replaced with benches. Let people sit where they used to kneel. That seemed more her."

My throat did something complicated. "Thank you," I managed.

"Don't thank me. I'm still furious at you, you know. Both of you. You let me march halfway across the country hunting a girl I'd sworn to protect, and neither of you trusted me with the truth until it nearly got us all killed."

"We trust you now."

"Now," she agreed. "Now." She picked up a bone star from the display, weighed it, and set it back down without buying — the only person who ever does, and somehow it feels like the highest compliment available. "Keep the roads open, carver. Keep the stars coming. That's the monument."

* * *

Tonight I couldn't sleep, so I went outside, and found Cyrus already there, sitting on the bench he built last summer, looking up. I sat beside him. Neither of us spoke for a while. That's the other thing I've learned this year: silence shared is a different substance entirely from silence endured. His used to be full of the dead. Mine used to be full of nothing. Now ours is just ours, empty in the way a bowl is empty — ready to be filled with whatever the evening brings.

"The boy came back today," I said eventually. "The one who asked if the stars were real. He brought his sister. She showed the star to her friends like a relic."

"They are relics," Cyrus said. "Everything's a relic of something. That's just history with better marketing."

"I've been thinking about the name."

He went still. He's good at hiding it, but I know his body now the way you know a house you live in — which floorboards speak, which silences mean.

"You could try again," he said. "The bone-singer said—"

"The bone-singer said the shape of it is gone, and chasing it might take the rest of me with it. I remember." I leaned my head on his shoulder, which still surprises him every time, which I intend to keep surprising him with for as long as he'll allow. "I don't want it back, Cyrus. I've been trying to explain this to myself for months and tonight I finally have the words. Everyone who loved that name loved a girl who was going to die on an altar. Her mother designed the knife. Her uncle sold her. The name belongs to that story. I checked the price, and I paid it, and what I got in exchange is this — a stall, a crossroads, a man who reads bones, a sky full of ordinary stars. I'd make the trade again. Every time. That's the part nobody would put on a statue."

He was quiet a long moment. Then, wryly: "You realize that's the most princess-like thing you've ever said."

"Take it back."

"Never. It's going in my ledger. 'Day 341: the unnamed woman displays alarming aptitude for ruling.'"

"I will throw your ledger in the river."

"You'd miss it. It's the only place I write down the good days."

I laughed — really laughed, the kind that startles birds — and he pretended not to be pleased, and above us the stars burned on, indifferent and generous, the way they've burned over every traveler and tyrant and carver's stall since the beginning of the world. Somewhere north, lords squabbled over mills. Somewhere south, a bench stood where an altar used to. Somewhere in my chest, a name-shaped gap had become a door, and through it came a life I had chosen, piece by piece, the way you choose anything worth keeping.

A year ago the sky went out. Tonight a woman sells small white stars at a crossroads, and a man listens to nothing at all, and finds it full.

Tomorrow, travelers. Tomorrow, the road. But tonight — tonight is ours, and it is enough, and I intend to keep it.
