## 2. Astrid of the Cold Shrine

The god had been silent for three years, and the village had decided it was Astrid's fault.

She knelt on the cold flagstones of the shrine at dawn, as she did every dawn, and swept ash from the altar with a bundle of crow feathers tied to a stick. The brazier before her held no fire. It hadn't since she was thirteen. She kept it clean anyway. She polished the god's statue anyway—a squat stone figure with a hearth-bowl in its lap, its face worn smooth by centuries of hands that had believed harder than hers did.

"Good morning," she said to it, because that was what keepers said. "Nothing happened yesterday. Nobody came."

That wasn't quite true. Old Marta had come, once, to ask for rain for her beans, then stood in the doorway looking embarrassed, like she'd caught herself talking to an empty chair. And the week before that, a boy from the low farms had dared his friends to let him spit on the altar, and Astrid had hit him with the feather-bundle until he ran off crying. That was the most worship the shrine had seen in a year.

She set the feathers down and pressed her palm flat against the statue's knee. Cold stone. Always cold stone. When she was small, the elders said, the stone had been warm as bread. Pilgrims had walked three days through the pass just to lay their palms where hers was now and feel the god breathing under the mountain's skin.

Then the god went quiet, and the pilgrims stopped, and the elders started looking at Astrid the way you look at a well that's gone dry—wondering if it was ever really water down there, or just your own reflection.

"You could say something," she told the statue. "Anything. A cough. A sneeze. I'd take a sneeze."

The statue said nothing. The shrine said nothing. Outside, the village of Hollowmere woke up around her: the smith's hammer, the goats, someone's mother calling someone home. A whole town full of voices, none of them for her.

Astrid stood, brushed off her knees, and went to fetch the morning water, because gods were silent but chores were not.

* * *

It happened at dusk, three days later, at the well.

She'd heard things before. She wanted to be honest about that, even in her own head. Little things. A hum in the stones when she walked the ridge path. Once, a sound like a door closing very far underground, when old Tam Fell's mine collapsed and everyone swore they'd felt nothing. She'd never told anyone. Telling people you heard the mountain talk was how you ended up like Widow Skerry, who heard the river speak and got shut up in the prayer-house until she learned to stop hearing things.

But this time it said her name.

She had the bucket half-raised when the ground under her boots breathed. Not shook—breathed, a slow swell and settle, like something enormous turning over in sleep. And through the soles of her feet, up through her ankles and shins and spine, into the bones behind her ears, came a voice. Not words exactly. More like meaning wearing words the way you'd wear a borrowed coat, too big in the shoulders:

**ASTRID.**

The bucket dropped. Water spread across the packed dirt, dark as spilled ink. Around her, the village went on—someone laughing at the tavern, a dog barking—but nobody else had stumbled. Nobody else had gone white-knuckled on the well rope. Nobody else had heard their own name spoken by a thing with no mouth, from underneath the world.

"Astrid," she whispered back, stupidly, testing it. Her voice sounded tiny. "Yes. Here."

The mountain didn't answer. But it didn't go away either. All night she lay in her cot above the shrine and felt it there, patient as a banked coal, a warmth in the floorboards that had nothing to do with any fire she'd lit. And beneath the patience, faint but unmistakable, something that felt horribly like urgency. Like a hand tugging her sleeve.

By morning she'd made up her mind twice and unmade it three times. By noon, the elders made it for her.

They came to the shrine while she was sweeping—four of them, Elder Voss in front with his good coat on, which meant this was official, which meant it was bad. Behind them stood the whole murmuring weight of Hollowmere's opinion, crammed into the doorway because nobody respectable would actually enter a dead god's house.

"Astrid." Voss's voice had the gentle tone he used on sick animals. "Marta says you've been... speaking to the mountain."

"I speak to the shrine every day. You all know that."

"And has it spoken back?"

She should have lied. She knew, even as she opened her mouth, that she should have lied. But three years of talking to cold stone had worn her honesty thin as paper, and besides—the mountain had said her name. You didn't lie about a thing like that. It felt like lying about your own heartbeat.

"It called me," she said. "Last night. At the well. By name."

The silence that followed had a texture to it, like the air before hail. Voss exchanged a look with Elder Brand over her head, and she saw it land between them: not wonder. Fear. The particular fear of practical people confronted with something impractical.

"The mountain is waking," Brand said quietly. "There are stories. What wakes in Thornfell isn't our god, girl. Our god is gone. Whatever's stirring down there is older, and it isn't kind."

"We think," Voss said, and here he took off his hat, which somehow made it worse, "that it would be best if you went to stay with your aunt in Lowmarsh. For a season. For your safety."

Lowmarsh was nine days' walk in the wrong direction. Her aunt was a woman she'd met twice. They weren't sending her to safety; they were sending the problem away, the way you burn a field that might be blighted.

"My safety," Astrid repeated.

"For your safety," Voss agreed, with the serene confidence of a man who believed his own lies.

And that—that polite, careful, communal lie—did what three years of silence hadn't managed. It lit something in her chest, small and furious, like a spark catching in dry tinder.

"No," Astrid said.

Voss blinked. "I beg your pardon?"

"No. I'm the keeper of this shrine. The god went quiet, fine. Maybe he's dead, maybe he's sleeping, maybe he left and didn't leave a forwarding address. But I'm still the keeper, and if something under the mountain is calling my name, then somebody in this cowardly little village ought to have the spine to answer it. So I will. Just—not from Lowmarsh."

She picked up her satchel from behind the altar. She'd packed it the night before, when she was still pretending she wouldn't: two shirts, the good knife, a heel of bread, her mother's copper pendant, and the shrine's one relic worth anything, a fire-glass bead that had glowed once upon a time and now sat dull as a pebble. She'd packed it hoping she'd talk herself out of needing it.

She hadn't.

"Astrid—" Voss reached for her arm.

She stepped back, out of reach, and the strangest thing happened: the floor of the shrine warmed under her bare feet. Just slightly. Just enough to notice. Like approval.

The elders saw her flinch and look down, and whatever they read in her face finished the argument for her. They parted. Nobody grabbed her after that. Superstition is a wonderful bodyguard.

She walked out of Hollowmere at sundown, past the well where the water had spilled, past Marta's bean rows, past the last house with its lamplit window, and onto the north road with the mountain rising black against the last light. Her legs shook the whole first mile. Terror, she discovered, and stubbornness use exactly the same muscles.

Behind her, home. Ahead of her, a voice that knew her name. It wasn't much of a choice. It was the only choice she'd ever been offered, and she took it at a walk that slowly became a stride.

* * *

The smuggler caravan came through two days later, and Astrid nearly missed it, because smugglers in stories were supposed to be dramatic, and these were just tired people with too many mules.

She'd spent those two days learning the shape of her new life: how to sleep rough without freezing, how to stretch bread, how to follow the ridge paths the shepherds used rather than the valley road where questions lived. She'd also spent them listening. The mountain spoke more now—or maybe she was simply closer, and braver about admitting it. Mostly it was weather-talk, road-talk, grumbles about the deep places. But sometimes, when she pressed her palm to the rock, she caught a thread of something else. Direction. Wanting. *This way,* the stone seemed to murmur. *Come this way, keeper.*

So when the caravan wound up the switchback below her—eight mules, six riders, and a big grey-bearded man at the front whose horse moved like it trusted him absolutely—she was already waiting at the narrow place where the trail pinched between rockfall and drop.

She'd planned to ask politely for passage. What actually happened was that the lead rider reined in, looked at her—one look, taking in the satchel, the bare feet blistered in borrowed sandals, the shrine-keeper's cord still knotted at her wrist—and said, "No."

"I haven't asked anything yet."

"You're from Hollowmere. You've got the look. You want to go up-mountain, and the answer's no. We don't carry pilgrims. We don't carry trouble. Off the trail, girl."

"I'm not a pilgrim. My god's dead."

Something flickered across the big man's face at that—something that wasn't amusement. He studied her a moment longer, and she stared back with all the ferocity of sixteen years and nothing left to lose, and the staring contest might have gone either way except that the mountain chose that moment to shrug.

It was a small tremor, as tremors went. Dust slid off the rockfall. A mule screamed. Two of the riders swore in languages she didn't know, and the horses stamped and rolled their eyes, and every single person on that trail looked at the mountainside with naked fear—except Astrid, who felt the tremor roll through her like a greeting, and who heard, threaded inside it, clear as a bell in an empty room:

**THE KEEPER COMES. LET HER PASS.**

Her breath went out of her. She gripped the rock beside her and laughed—one wild, cracked bark of laughter that she couldn't have stopped with both hands.

The big man was watching her again. Watching her *not* be afraid.

"What was that?" one of the riders demanded. "Quakes don't come this far east. Everyone knows—"

"I know," Astrid said. Her voice only shook a little. "Everyone knows. And everyone knows the mountain's been silent for thirty years, and it isn't anymore, and I'm the only person on this road who can tell you why. Or—" she lifted her chin, "—you can leave me here and find out on your own."

Silence, except for the mules. The big man rubbed his jaw. Up close she could see the storm-grey of his eyes and the way they catalogued everything—her cord, her satchel, her blistered feet—with the weary precision of a man who'd learned the hard way what trouble cost.

"There's a mule cart at the back," he said finally. "Grain sacks. If you can ride in a grain sack without being seen, seen by anyone, you ride as far as Greymoss. Then you're someone else's problem."

"That's fine," said Astrid, who had absolutely no intention of being anyone's problem but her own.

"And your name?"

She hesitated. Names, she thought, were going to matter on this road; she could feel it in the stone under her feet, a caution like a hand raised. "Astrid," she said anyway. Lying to the mountain's chosen guide seemed unwise.

He nodded once, unsurprised, as if names were coins he expected to be short-changed on, and clicked his tongue, and the caravan moved.

The grain sacks smelled of dust and honest labor, and they were, without competition, the finest bed Astrid had ever slept in. She lay wedged between them in the swaying cart, watching the stars wheel over the shoulder of Thornfell, and felt the mountain's attention wrapped around her like a second blanket. Terrifying. Enormous. Awake.

*You called me,* she thought at it, fiercely, so the thought must have shown in her blood the way it showed in her grin. *So here I come. Don't you dare go quiet again.*

Far ahead, at the head of the column, the grey-bearded man glanced back at the cart—just once, just briefly—as though he'd heard something too.

As though he'd heard her.
