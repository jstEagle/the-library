## 3. Prayers Answered

Greymoss smelled of wet wool and woodsmoke, the way border towns always did in the last week of autumn. Wendell came down off the pass with a caravan of six wagons and a driver who wouldn't stop talking about his mother's rheumatism, and he knew something was wrong before he saw a single shrine.

The birds were wrong.

Thornfell's foothills kept a particular kind of silence—wrens and rock thrushes, nothing bold enough to sing near the smuggler roads. But Greymoss was loud with crows, hundreds of them, packed into the bare trees along the town's eastern wall like black fruit. They weren't cawing. They were watching the road, all of them, all at once, heads turning in unison as the wagons rolled beneath them.

"Storm-caller." The lead driver, a woman named Hesk with forearms like ship rope, spat to the side. "You seeing that?"

"I'm seeing it."

"You going to do something about it?"

"No."

She grunted, satisfied. That was the arrangement Wendell had maintained for fifteen years: he did one impossible thing—found roads through Thornfell that didn't exist on any map—and refused to do every other impossible thing anyone asked of him. It had kept him alive, anonymous, and alone, in roughly that order of importance.

The trouble started at the gate.

There should have been a toll. There was always a toll at Greymoss; the town lived on tariffs and smuggling both, and its gate-wardens were famously immune to charm. Instead the warden waved them through, eyes red-rimmed, and said only, "Room at the Drowned Bell if you want it. Don't take rooms facing the temple row."

"Why not?"

The warden looked at him like he was simple. "Because they're lit," she said, and turned back to her post.

* * *

Wendell left the caravan settled at the Drowned Bell—rooms facing the courtyard, away from the temple row, because Hesk had heard the warden too—and walked into the town alone.

He told himself it was reconnaissance. A guide needed to know if a town was about to boil over; pilgrim crowds meant closed roads, closed roads meant lost contracts. That was the story he used, the same way he'd used stories for fifteen years, and he was still telling it to himself when he stopped in front of the first shrine and felt his stomach drop through the cobblestones.

It was a wayshrine to Vellum-of-the-Roads, a minor god, a god of carters and mule-drivers, a god so small most priests couldn't recite his litany without laughing. His statue was chipped limestone, his offering bowl crusted with years of bird filth. Wendell had passed this shrine forty times over fifteen years and never once seen a candle in it.

Now it burned.

Not the bowl—the whole shrine. Flame sat inside the limestone like light inside a lantern, filling the stone from within, and where the fire touched the chipped places, the chips were smoothing over. The statue's missing hand was regrowing, pale and new, like flesh under scar tissue.

Around it, the crowd knelt. Carters, mule-drivers, a family of tanners still in their aprons. An old man pressed his forehead to the warm stone and wept without sound.

"Three days ago it went dark," a woman beside Wendell said, not to him, to everyone. "All the shrines went dark three days ago. My grandmother said that happened once before, when she was a girl. The Quiet Year. And then—" she spread her hands at the burning stone, at the crowd, at the whole trembling town, "—then they woke up. All of them. Every god at once. Isn't it wonderful?"

Wonderful. Wendell looked at the shrine's new-grown hand and felt the hair rise on his arms, because he had grown up in temples. He knew what consecrated fire looked like. It flickered. It breathed. It cast shadows that moved when you moved.

This fire didn't move. It sat in the stone like something held down, like a coal banked under ash, waiting for someone to breathe on it.

And the shadows the kneeling pilgrims cast were all leaning toward the shrine. Not toward the flame—toward the base of it, toward the ground beneath, as if pulled by something far below.

He'd seen this before. Fifteen years ago, in the week before the exile, in the week before Solveig—

He made himself stop there. He always made himself stop there.

* * *

By evening, Greymoss had gone mad in the specific way towns go mad when prayers start getting answered.

Wendell watched it from the Drowned Bell's courtyard, a mug of small beer untouched in front of him. Pilgrims flooded the gates in numbers the town had no beds for; they slept in doorways and prayed in the streets. A man who'd been blind for twenty years stood outside the tavern describing the color of the sky, weeping, while people touched his sleeves like he was a relic. A farmer showed everyone his daughter, fever-free since noon, since her mother had prayed at the lit shrine of some hearth-saint.

Real healings. Real miracles. Wendell could feel it even from across the courtyard—that warmth, that pressure, like standing near an open oven. Something was answering, and answering generously, and the whole town was drunk on it.

But.

A child came through the market square leading a goat, and the goat stopped at the corner of the temple row and would not move. Not stubbornness—the animal shook, foam at its mouth, eyes rolled white, while the child hauled the rope and cried. Its shadow, Wendell noticed, leaned downhill. Toward the mountain. Every shadow in Greymoss leaned downhill now, if you looked long enough, the way iron filings lean toward a lodestone.

And then there was the prayer that came back wrong.

He saw the woman at dusk, at the shrine of Maren-of-the-Waters, a healing saint. She was young, hollow-cheeked, holding a bundle wrapped in a shawl, and she was praying the way people pray when they've already been refused everywhere else. Wendell caught fragments. *Fever. Four days. Please. I'll give anything.*

The shrine flared.

The woman gasped, staggered, clutched the bundle—and laughed, high and cracked with relief. "Oh. Oh, thank you. Thank you, lady, thank you—" She unwrapped the shawl right there in the street to show the gathering crowd.

The baby's fever was gone. Its skin was cool and perfect.

Its eyes were wrong. Both irises had gone the deep orange of banked coals, and they didn't blink—not often, not quite on the rhythm of a living thing—and when the crowd leaned in to coo, the baby looked past them. Down. Through the cobblestones. At something underneath.

"It's a miracle," the mother said. Her voice had a strange flatness at the center of it, like a coin dropped on stone. "Isn't it? It's a miracle."

The crowd agreed. The crowd wanted to agree. Wendell watched them want it, watched the warmth of the answered prayer wash over their doubt and dissolve it, and understood with cold clarity that this was how it worked. This was how it had always worked. You didn't trick people into feeding a starving god. You gave them exactly what they begged for, and let gratitude do the rest.

The Ember Court was feeding again.

He'd known the name since he was nine years old, from the scary parts of scripture the priests performed at midwinter: the court beneath Thornfell, the old gods' jailers, the thing the new faith had buried and the old faith had never dared name. He'd thought it a story, like everyone did. Then fifteen years ago he'd learned it wasn't—learned it in the worst possible way, in a week of fire and judgment and his sister's body going into the ground while the priests called it holy.

And now the pattern was back. Shrines lighting overnight. Prayers answered generously, instantly, indiscriminately. Small wrongnesses in what came back—a hand regrown, a fever cooled, but everything tilted a half-degree toward the mountain, everything paying interest to something below. The Court bought devotion the way smugglers bought silence: cheap goods up front, the real price collected later, quietly, from people who'd stopped counting.

His exile's peace was over. He'd known it since the tremor in the pass, since the mountain spoke after thirty silent years. He'd hoped he was wrong. Standing in the Greymoss dark with every shadow in town leaning downhill, he admitted he wasn't.

"Storm-caller."

Hesk stood behind him, arms crossed, breath fogging. "You've got a face on you. The face you get when a road's washed out and you already know the detour."

"Do I."

"Fifteen years, Wendell. I know your faces." She nodded toward the temple row, the burning shrines, the crowds. "What is that?"

"Trouble," he said. "Old trouble. Deeper than the passes."

"The kind you walk toward or the kind you run from?"

He didn't answer, which was an answer, and Hesk sighed the sigh of a woman adding a problem to her ledger. "Fine. We load at dawn either way. But there's something else. Found a stowaway in the third wagon when we watered the mules. Girl, maybe sixteen, half-starved, wearing a keeper's gray. Wouldn't say where she ran from. Only thing she'd tell us—" Hesk paused, and her voice went carefully neutral, the way voices did around Wendell when they approached anything resembling his history, "—is that the mountain spoke to her. By name."

The mug stopped halfway to his mouth.

"She's in the kitchen warming up," Hesk said. "You want to look at her before I decide whether she rides with us or goes to the town guard?"

Wendell looked out at the temple row—at the flames that didn't flicker, at the shadows leaning home—and felt fifteen careful years of invisibility coming apart like wet paper.

"Yeah," he said. "I'll look at her."
