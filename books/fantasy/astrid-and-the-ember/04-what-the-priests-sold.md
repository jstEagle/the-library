## 4. What the Priests Sold

The priest was dying in the back room of a candle shop that smelled of tallow and old incense, and he had sent for Wendell by name.

That alone should have been warning enough. Nobody in Greymoss knew his name. He'd spent fifteen years making sure of it—fifteen years of being "Iversen, the pass-finder" or just "the guide," a man defined entirely by what he could do for other people's cargo. His name belonged to another life, one he'd buried so deep even he sometimes forgot which parts of it were real.

But the boy who came scratching at the caravan yard door at dusk said, "Brother Halvard asks for Wendell Iversen, son of Ivar, of the Skaldsfall temple," and Wendell felt the words go through him like a blade finding an old scar.

Astrid looked up from the map she'd been studying—Bramble's maps now, annotated in her careful hand—and read his face the way she read everything, with that unnerving directness of hers. "You know him."

"I knew a lot of people." He pulled his coat off its hook. "Stay here."

"You always say that."

"And you never listen. So save us both the speech." But she was already on her feet, and Bramble was already reaching for his axe out of pure habit, and Wendell realized with a tired sort of resignation that whatever this was, he wasn't walking into it alone anymore. That was the trouble with letting people in. They followed you into rooms you'd meant to die in privately.

* * *

Brother Halvard had been the youngest of the examiners, once. Wendell remembered him as a brisk young man with ink-stained fingers who had signed the judgment scroll without meeting anyone's eyes. Now he was a ruin propped against pillows in a room where every candle had burned down to a stub, as if he'd been rationing light the way he was rationing breath.

"Wendell Iversen." The priest's voice was ash and gravel. "You look like your mother."

"My mother's been dead thirty years."

"Yes. Well." A wet cough that went on too long. "Death is less final than we taught you. That's rather the point."

Wendell stood in the doorway, arms crossed, and did not sit. "You sent for me. Say what you sent for."

"I'm dying. The mountain's sickness—it gets into those of us who handled the rites. You've seen the pilgrims' prayers come back wrong. Imagine being the vessel they pour through." He smiled, and it was a terrible thing, mostly teeth and regret. "So I have perhaps two days, and fifteen years of cowardice to spend. Sit down, storm-caller. Or stand. But listen."

"I stopped listening to priests when they put me out of my own life."

"They didn't put you out." Halvard's eyes found his, and there was something in them Wendell hadn't expected: shame, live and festering. "We put you out because you refused to call storms for the war fleet. Because you wouldn't make your gift a weapon. And the Court—the Court beneath Thornfell—had been buying from us for years, and you were the last unspent treasure on our ledger."

The room seemed to tilt. Wendell gripped the doorframe. "What are you talking about?"

"The Ember Court. You thought it was a story we told children to keep them from the caves. It isn't. It's real, it's dying, and it has been dying for a century. A god going out like a lamp in wind. And a god that starves doesn't simply fade, boy—it *feeds*. It reached up through the mountain and made bargains with the temples above. Souls for miracles. Souls for the continued favor of heaven. We told ourselves we were selling the souls of the wicked, the condemned, the willing. We told ourselves a great many things."

Wendell's mouth had gone dry. "Fifteen years ago. When they exiled me. What did they sell?"

Halvard closed his eyes.

"When the sentence was pronounced, the elders took your sister aside. Solveig, who had argued until her voice broke that if you were exiled, she would go with you. She was told there was another way. That her sacrifice would buy your pardon—that you could come home within the year, whole and forgiven. All she had to do was give herself to the mountain's mercy."

"No." The word came out flat, automatic, a door slamming. "She died of fever on the road east. I got word through a trader two years later. Fever."

"That's what we wrote." The priest's voice dropped to almost nothing. "That's what we let you believe, because the truth would have brought you back down the mountain with lightning in your hands, and we couldn't afford that. She didn't die of fever, Wendell. She walked into the shrine at Skaldsfall at dusk, and the floor opened, and the Ember Court took her soul while her body failed on the stones. Her soul has been burning in their lanterns ever since. Fuel. Wick-oil for a starving god."

The sound Wendell made wasn't a word. Fifteen years of grief turned over in his chest like a plowed field, and everything he'd planted in it—every carefully tended belief, every night he'd whispered *I'm sorry, little sister* to the stars, believing she'd gone gently, believing the fever had taken her before the loneliness could—everything rotted at the roots in a single breath.

"They used her," he said. His voice sounded like someone else's. Like weather. "They used her to buy my obedience. To keep me gone and grateful and *broken*."

"They used all of them. There are hundreds of lanterns under that mountain. Hundreds of souls, harvested across three generations, each one sold by some frightened man in vestments telling himself a necessary lie." Halvard pushed himself upright, and the effort cost him visibly, sweat standing on his brow. "And now the god is nearly dead, and it needs one more ember to reignite—a soul bright enough, young enough, *loved enough*—and the Court has chosen. Your sister is the last lantern on the tree, Wendell. At the next dark moon, they will burn her to light their god's new dawn. Unless someone goes down and takes her back."

"Why tell me?" Wendell crossed the room in three strides and took a fistful of the priest's nightshirt, hauling him half off the pillows, and some distant part of him noted that Astrid had appeared in the doorway and Bramble behind her and neither of them moved to stop him. "Why now? Why not fifteen years ago? Why not let me grieve something true?"

"Because I was a coward then," Halvard rasped, not flinching, "and I am a dead man now, and dead men can finally afford honesty. Because the mountain has started speaking again, and it speaks to shrine-keepers"—his eyes flicked to Astrid, who had gone very still—"and because someone must carry what I know past my grave. Take it. It's heavy. I know."

Wendell let him go. His hands were shaking. He looked at them as though they belonged to a stranger—the hands that had guided caravans, tied knots, cooked camp meals, done fifteen years of small harmless work while his sister's soul hung in the dark like a fruit waiting to be picked.

"The dark moon," he said. "When?"

"Eleven days."

* * *

Outside, Greymoss was lighting its lamps, and half of them were shrine-lamps now, flickering with borrowed fire that bent wrong in the breeze. Pilgrims knelt in the streets murmuring prayers that had begun, lately, to be answered too quickly. Wendell walked through all of it without seeing any of it.

He stopped at the well at the center of town and stood there gripping the cold stone rim until his knuckles went white.

He had built his entire exile around a single grief, worn smooth as river stone from handling. *Solveig died of fever. Solveig died because I was proud, because I wouldn't kneel, because my refusal cost her brother, cost her home.* He had carried that guilt like a pack-stone for fifteen years, had let it shape every mile of every smuggler's trail, and it had been a lie. Not his guilt—that part might still be true—but the shape of it. She hadn't wasted away mourning him. She had been *taken*. Sold by men in vestments who signed scrolls and rationed candles and called it mercy.

"Astrid." He didn't turn around. He knew she was there; he'd known since the candle shop. "Go back to the inn."

"No."

"Astrid—"

"She's your sister." The girl's voice was quiet and absolutely certain, the way it got when the mountain spoke through her or when she simply decided the world would bend. "And in eleven days they're going to burn her. So you're going to want to hit something or scream or cry, and you shouldn't do any of it alone, and I'm not leaving, so you may as well start talking."

For a long moment Wendell said nothing. Then, low, almost a growl: "I spent fifteen years hating the gods for taking her. And the whole time it was *men*. Men in robes, with ledgers. Gods I could rage at. Men I have to—" His hand cracked against the well-rim, stone splitting under the blow, and the thunder that answered rolled out of a clear evening sky, three miles off but answering *him*, the way it always had, the way he'd sworn never to use again.

Bramble appeared at his other shoulder, silent, solid, saying nothing, which was its own kind of speech.

"Eleven days," Wendell said again. The fury in him had stopped being a wildfire and become something worse: a forge-fire, contained, shaped, pointed down the mountain's throat. "The Court wants an ember. It wants a soul that burns bright because she was loved." He turned, and Astrid took half a step back at what was in his face—not fear, he noted distantly, just recalibration. "Then it picked the wrong family. Get your pack. Both of you. We leave tonight, ahead of the pilgrim roads, through the passes only I know."

"And the caravan?" Bramble asked. "The contract?"

"Let them find another guide." Wendell was already walking toward the inn, coat snapping in a wind that had risen from nowhere. "I've spent fifteen years guiding people around that mountain. Time I went *in*."

Behind them, in a dying priest's rented room, Brother Halvard closed his eyes at last, and the last candle stub guttered out—as if somewhere far below, something vast and starving had just felt the first cold draft of its ending, and begun, quietly, to count the days.
