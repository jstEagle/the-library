## 5. Three Companions on a Smuggler's Road

Bramble caught up with them at the edge of Greymoss, breathing hard, one hand pressed to a bruised rib.

"You're going toward the mountain," he said. It wasn't a question. He'd watched them buy rope and lamp oil and dried food enough for three people, and Bramble had spent twenty years reading caravans the way priests read scripture.

Wendell didn't stop walking. "Go home, Bramble."

"Home's a debt ledger with my name in it." Bramble fell into step anyway, favoring his left side. "You pulled me out of the Kettleford raid when every other guide had already run. You carried me two miles on your back with an arrow in your shoulder. I've been waiting fifteen years for you to need something back."

"I don't need anything back."

"Nobody ever does." Bramble spat, cheerfully. "That's what makes it a debt instead of a job."

Astrid walked between them, hugging her bundle of shrine clothes to her chest, and thought: *this is how it starts.* Not with vows or oaths or gods speaking from mountaintops. With a stubborn man refusing to leave and another stubborn man refusing to admit he was glad.

She'd been with Wendell for four days now, and she had learned exactly three things about him: he never slept facing away from a door, he counted his steps under his breath when he was worried, and he flinched—actually flinched—whenever she mentioned the mountain talking to her.

The mountain talked to her more every day. That was the fourth thing, the one she hadn't told him yet.

* * *

They took the old salt road north, the one that wasn't on any map because maps got people arrested at borders. Wendell moved through Thornfell like a man walking through his own house in the dark—left at the split birch, down through the hollow where the stream ran backward under stone, up the goat track that didn't look like anything until you were on it and couldn't turn around.

"How do you know all this?" Astrid asked on the second morning, watching him pick a path through scree that looked identical to every other patch of scree for a mile in either direction.

"Fifteen years of not dying."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I'm selling." But something in his voice had gone soft at the edges, and Astrid filed that away too.

Bramble told stories at night. That was his contribution besides muscle—he cooked badly and talked constantly, filling the fire-silence with tales of caravans lost and found, guards who'd gone honest, honest men who'd gone guard. Astrid noticed Wendell never told stories. He listened with his eyes half-closed like a cat pretending not to care, and once, only once, when Bramble described a blizzard on the high pass, Wendell said quietly, "We lost the mules at the narrows," before catching himself and going silent again.

*Lost the mules.* As if he'd been there. As if he remembered.

Astrid decided, somewhere between the second campfire and the third, that she was going to make this man admit he was lonely if it took the whole journey.

* * *

It happened on the fifth day, at a place Wendell called the Shelf—a road cut into the mountainside above a drop so deep the river at the bottom was just a silver thread. The Shelf was wide enough for one person, maybe two pressed close, and it ran for half a mile along the cliff face.

Wendell went first. Then Bramble. Astrid came last, which meant she heard the mountain first.

It wasn't words, exactly. It was more like leaning your ear against a sleeping animal's ribs—the deep slow pulse of it, and then a wrongness in the rhythm, a stutter, a—

"*Stop,*" Astrid said.

Wendell turned. "What?"

"The road." Her voice came out strange, thin, like someone else's. "It's going to let go. Right there—" She pointed ahead, at a stretch of Shelf that looked exactly like every other stretch. "There's water underneath. It's been eating the rock for years and it's almost through and the mountain is trying to tell us and nobody ever listens, nobody *ever listens*—"

"Astrid." Wendell's voice was careful, the way you talk to a spooked horse. "Roads don't talk."

"This one does!" And then, because she was sixteen and terrified and so tired of being the girl who heard things, she stamped her foot on the Shelf and shouted at the stone itself: "Tell him! Tell him yourself!"

The mountain answered.

Not much. A shudder, low and grinding, felt through boot-soles rather than heard—and then a crack, a long lazy crack, spidering across the Shelf thirty feet ahead of them, and a slab the size of a barn door sheared away and dropped silently into the dark, taking the road with it.

The silence afterward was enormous.

Bramble sat down where he stood, right on the cliff path, legs gone. "Well," he said. "Well, then."

Wendell stared at the gap. At Astrid. At the gap again. Astrid watched him watch her, and saw him doing arithmetic behind his eyes—fifteen years of careful invisibility against a girl who could hear stone, standing on a road only smugglers knew existed, having just predicted death by thirty feet.

"You're going to ask me how," Astrid said. "And I'm going to say I don't know. And you're going to have to decide whether that's worse than being alone."

Something crossed Wendell's face—grief, maybe, or its cousin. "I already decided," he said, "when you stowed away in my wagon smelling like cold incense and wouldn't leave." He looked at the gap in the road, jaw working. "There's a way around. Two days longer. We'll lose the light soon; we camp here."

He started unpacking without looking at either of them, which Astrid had learned was how Wendell said things he couldn't say out loud.

* * *

That night the fire burned higher than usual, because Bramble found deadfall pine and pine burns loud and bright, and because—for the first time—nobody seemed to want the dark.

Astrid sat with her knees drawn up and made herself a promise: tonight she would ask. Tonight, while the fire was warm and Bramble was mid-story about a customs officer who could be bribed with cheese, she would ask Wendell about his sister.

She'd pieced it together from fragments—the way he'd gone still at Greymoss when the priest grabbed his sleeve, the way he said *Solveig* only once, in his sleep, like a word in a language he was trying to forget.

"Bramble," she said, when the story wound down, "tell the one about the guide who quit."

"There's no such story. Guides don't quit. They get exiled, they get killed, or they get old and cranky." Bramble grinned. "Present company excluded."

"Present company included," Wendell said, to the fire.

"See? Cranky." Bramble stretched out his boots toward the flames. "I knew a storm-caller once. Back before. Whole villages used to pray to men like him—ask him for rain for the barley, ask him to break a drought, ask him to keep the hail off the orchards. Good work. Holy work, near enough." He glanced sideways, casual as a knife going into a sheath. "Then somebody asked him to point the same gift at an enemy town instead. And he said no. And they broke him for it."

The fire popped. Astrid held very still.

"What happened to him?" she asked softly.

"He walked into the mountains," Bramble said, "and started guiding smugglers, and pretended he didn't care about anything anymore." He reached over and knocked his knuckles gently against Wendell's shoulder. "But he carried me two miles with an arrow in him, so I have my doubts about the pretending."

Wendell didn't shrug him off. That was the thing Astrid would remember later—that he didn't shrug him off. He just looked into the fire for a long time, and then he said, "Her name was Solveig."

Neither of them breathed.

"She wanted to be a healer. Did I ever say that? No, of course I didn't, why would I." His laugh came out wrong. "She used to practice splinting sticks. Little twigs, little bandages. Said she'd fix broken things for a living, and I'd—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "When they exiled me, she tried to follow. Up the pass, in winter. The priests said she fell." His hands were flat and still on his knees, pressing down, holding something in. "Fifteen years I believed she fell."

"And now?" Astrid whispered.

"Now I know better. And knowing better is worse." He looked up, and his eyes in the firelight were wet and furious and terribly young. "So we're going under the mountain, and I'm getting her back, and after that none of you owe me anything. Understood?"

"Understood," Bramble said easily, meaning nothing of the kind.

Astrid didn't say anything at all. She just scooted sideways until her shoulder rested against Wendell's arm—light as a bird landing—and stayed there. He went rigid. Then, by degrees, less rigid.

"The mountain talks to me," she said into the quiet. "More every day. It says things are waking up down there. Old things. Hungry things." She felt him tense. "It also says the deep passes are closed by snow in six days. So whatever we're doing, we do it fast."

For a moment Wendell said nothing. Then, so quietly she almost missed it:

"Six days." A breath. "All right. Six days." And then, grudging as a man paying out coin he'd sworn he didn't have: "Sleep, both of you. Long climb tomorrow."

Astrid closed her eyes, warm on one side from the fire and warm on the other from a man who would deny it to his grave.

* * *

She woke before dawn to the mountain speaking her name—not a whisper this time but a pull, urgent, dragging her upright with her heart slamming.

Below their camp, far down the slope, lights were moving through the dark. Dozens of them. A procession, climbing toward the Shelf, carrying lanterns that did not burn orange like fire but white and cold, like the light of the soul-lanterns in the old stories.

Behind the procession, faint across the miles, the mountain groaned—a sound like a cathedral door opening after centuries shut.

"Wendell," Astrid whispered, shaking his shoulder. "Wendell, wake up. Someone's coming. Someone's *already here*."
