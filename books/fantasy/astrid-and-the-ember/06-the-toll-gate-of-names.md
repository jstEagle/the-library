## 6. The Toll Gate of Names

The mountain's threshold was not what Wendell expected.

He had smuggled through Thornfell for fifteen years. He knew its passes the way a man knows his own hands—every crack, every hidden vein, every place where the stone forgot to be cruel. But he had never come to this. The old roads bent away from it, and he had always assumed that was his own doing, some instinct steering him clear. Now, standing before it with Astrid shivering at his shoulder and Bramble checking the straps on her pack for the third time, he understood: the mountain had been steering *him*.

The gate stood in a cleft where two cliffs leaned together like conspirators. It wasn't built so much as grown—black basalt rising in a single seamless arch, veined with threads of dull red that pulsed slow as a sleeping heart. No door. No wall. Just the arch, and the air beneath it, which shimmered faintly, like heat off summer stone though the wind here carried ice.

Astrid stopped walking three paces out. Her breath fogged. "It knows we're here."

"Everything in Thornfell knows we're here," Bramble said. "That's rather the problem."

"It knows our *names*," Astrid said, and her voice had that flat certainty she got when the mountain spoke through her. She pressed a palm flat against her own sternum, as if holding something down. "It's saying them back to me. Over and over. Like it's tasting them."

Wendell felt it too, then—a pressure behind his eyes, a whisper crawling along the inside of his skull. Not words. Not yet. More like a hand turning over coins, weighing each one.

He'd heard stories, of course. Every smuggler had. The Toll Gate of Names, the old-timers called it, though most of them laughed when they said it, the way men laugh at things they've decided aren't real because they've never had to face them. You didn't cross the arch with your whole self. The mountain took its fee. A true name—the sound of you, the shape of you, the thing your mother called you home by. Or a true memory—one moment of your life, given up entire, never to be returned.

"Nobody crosses without paying," Wendell said quietly. "There's no trick to it. No way around. I've looked."

"You've *looked*?" Bramble turned on him. "You knew about this and you're only mentioning it now?"

"I was hoping it was a story." It wasn't entirely a lie. He had hoped. Hope was cheap; he spent it freely on things he didn't believe. "We can't go around. The Court is under the mountain. Under means through."

Bramble swore, long and inventive, in the dialect of the caravan yards where she'd learned every word worth knowing. Then she squared her shoulders. "Fine. Which one do we give? Name or memory?"

"That's the trap," Wendell said. "You don't choose what the memory is. And you don't know what losing your name costs until it's gone."

He did know, actually. He'd spent fifteen years watching men trade pieces of themselves at altars smaller than this one—gamblers who sold their luck, soldiers who sold their fear, lovers who sold the memory of why they'd left. He'd seen what remained afterward. People thinned out. They became summaries of themselves.

And he had hoarded everything. That was the truth of him, ugly and simple. Fifteen years in exile and he had never once paid a price anyone else could see. He'd refused the priests their war, yes—but refusing had cost him nothing but comfort. His sister had died. His name had become a curse instead of a gift. And still he kept it all, every scrap, wrapped tight, because giving anything away meant admitting it had mattered.

The arch pulsed. The whisper sharpened.

"Wendell Iversen," said the gate.

Not aloud. Inside. In the voice of someone he hadn't heard in fifteen years—his mother, calling him in from storms he'd started himself.

Astrid gasped. Bramble went white. The gate had spoken to all of them at once, or perhaps it simply spoke and they all happened to hear.

"It wants—" Astrid began.

"I know what it wants." Wendell stepped forward before she could say more, before Bramble could argue, before the mountain could start making offers. If something had to be paid here, it would be paid by him. He was the one leading them into this. He was the one whose sister hung dimming in a lantern beneath their feet. Every step of this road was his debt.

He walked under the arch.

The world changed texture. The air thickened, warm now, smelling of hot iron and banked coals. The red veins in the stone brightened until they were the only light, and in that emberlight he saw them rise out of the basalt: shapes, tall and thin, like figures carved from smoke and slag. Three of them. Faceless. Patient. The toll-keepers.

One drifted forward. Where its face should have been there was a smoothness, and in that smoothness images moved like reflections in dark water—his childhood home burning, Solveig laughing, the priests' faces lit by lightning he had called down on their temple doors. His life, being counted.

"A name," it said. Its voice was the grinding of the mountain settling. "Or a memory. One. Freely given."

Wendell opened his mouth to offer the name.

And found he couldn't.

It wasn't courage stopping him. It was terror, pure and animal. His name was all he had left of who he'd been before—the boy who'd loved thunder, the brother, the son. Exile had taken his home, his gods, his sister. The name was the last room in the house. If he gave it up, what walked out from under this arch would be a man-shaped thing that guided smugglers and hated gods and remembered grief without remembering whose grief it was.

His mouth stayed open. Nothing came out. The toll-keeper waited, and the waiting itself was a kind of pressure, a slow tightening, and Wendell understood with sick clarity that he was going to stand there forever, hoarding himself down to nothing, unless—

"I'll pay."

Astrid's voice. Small and absolutely steady.

He turned. She had followed him under the arch—of course she had, she was sixteen and stubborn and had never once in her life done the sensible thing—and she was looking up at the toll-keeper with her chin lifted the way she looked at elders she'd already decided to defy.

"No," Wendell said.

"You don't get to decide that." She wasn't even looking at him. "It's my name. My memories. Mine to spend."

"You're a child—"

"I'm a shrine-keeper." Something flared in her then, older than her years, the ember of the office she still carried even if her god had gone quiet. "I have tended offerings since I was six years old. I know what it means to give something up. Do you?"

The words landed harder than any blow he'd taken. Because the answer was no. Fifteen years, and the answer was no.

"The memory of my mother," Astrid said to the toll-keeper. "I want to give that. Not my name. I need my name—I'm going to need it for what's coming. But my mother." Her voice caught, just once, and steadied. "She died when I was small. Everyone says I should have forgotten her by now anyway. So take it. Take her voice, her face, all of it. Let it feed your dying god or whatever it does. I'd rather it burn somewhere useful than sit in me going cold."

The toll-keeper drifted toward her. Wendell lunged—not thinking, just moving—to put himself between them, and the creature passed through him like cold water, like regret, untouchable, unstoppable.

"No," he said again, uselessly. "Astrid, listen to me, you don't understand what you're—"

"I understand better than you." She closed her eyes. "I've listened to the mountain my whole life. This is the first time I've gotten to answer it."

The toll-keeper laid fingers of smoke against her forehead.

Wendell watched it happen and could do nothing. He saw the moment leave her—not visibly, there was no light, no tearing, nothing so merciful as drama. She simply changed. The set of her shoulders shifted. Something soft behind her eyes went smooth and distant, like snow filling a footprint. When she opened her eyes again, they were dry.

"She had brown hair," Astrid said slowly, testing the hole where the memory had been. "Didn't she? I know I knew that. I know it the way you know a word in a language you've forgotten." She looked at Wendell, and her chin trembled once before she mastered it. "Tell me I'm right. Tell me something about her. Please. While I still remember that I lost something."

"You're right," Wendell said, and his voice came out wrecked. "Brown hair. And she sang, I think—you hum sometimes, when you think no one's listening. Maybe that's hers."

"Maybe." Astrid nodded, once, twice, sealing it away. "Then it's not all gone. Some of her lives in me still, even if I don't know it." She smiled, and it was the bravest, worst thing Wendell had ever seen. "See? Worth it. Your turn."

"My turn," Wendell repeated.

He faced the toll-keeper. The other two had drifted closer, patient as debt. In their smooth faces his life kept flickering—Solveig, the storm, the road, the fire. All of it priced. All of it waiting.

He tried again to speak his name. Tried to say *take it, take Wendell Iversen, take the whole cursed weight of him.* And again the word jammed in his throat like a bone. His hands were shaking. Fifteen years of exile and he discovered, here at the end of the road, that he would rather die than let go of the one thing that proved he had ever existed.

*Hoarding,* he thought. *Even now. Even here.*

"Move aside."

Bramble pushed past him.

"Bramble, no—"

"Oh, shut up." She planted herself before the toll-keeper, arms crossed, jaw set in the expression she used on bandits and dishonest merchants and, apparently, ancient spirits. "You want a price? Here's mine. Not my name—I need that, I've got debts attached to it, people to find, promises to keep. And not some random memory either. I'm choosing."

The toll-keeper inclined its head slightly. Perhaps it was amused. Perhaps nothing that old can be amused.

"The night my caravan burned," Bramble said. "The one I told you about, Wendell. The one I couldn't save. The little girl's voice calling from the wagons." Her voice didn't waver, but her knuckles had gone white where her arms were crossed. "Take all of it. The fire, the screaming, the guilt. Every hour of that night. I've carried it four years. It's yours."

"Bramble." Wendell's throat was tight. "You don't owe me that. You don't owe anyone—"

"That's exactly the point, you great stupid storm-caller." She turned her head just enough to glare at him. "You've been paying everyone's debts in your head for fifteen years and paying no one's in truth. You think guilt is a currency. It isn't. It's just weight. So let me set some down, and maybe you'll learn how it's done."

She looked back at the toll-keeper. "Well? Take it before I change my mind."

The second toll-keeper reached out and touched her brow, and Wendell watched another person lose a piece of herself to the mountain—watched Bramble's fierce, haunted face go suddenly, terribly lighter. She blinked. Frowned. Touched her own cheek as if expecting to find tears and finding none.

"Huh," she said. "I feel like I should be crying about something." She looked at Wendell, puzzled, almost cheerful, and it was the strangest cruelty he had ever witnessed. "Why do I feel lighter? What did I—"

"You gave a memory," Astrid said softly. She took Bramble's hand. "A bad one. You're free of it now."

"Did I?" Bramble frowned deeper. "Feels like something's missing. Feels like a tooth gone from the row." She shrugged, and the shrug was wrong, too easy, too unburdened. "Well. Onward, then. The arch is open, isn't it?"

It was. The third toll-keeper had withdrawn, and beneath the arch the shimmering air had cleared to a plain passage, sloping down into warm darkness. The fee was paid. Two fees. Paid by everyone but him.

Wendell stood a moment longer under the arch, shaking with something that wasn't cold.

They had paid for him. That was the truth of it, however they dressed it up—Astrid's mother, Bramble's worst night, surrendered so that he wouldn't have to surrender himself. Others will pay prices you've been hoarding your whole life, he thought, and the thought was shame and awe tangled together, indistinguishable. He had spent fifteen years believing that isolation was integrity, that carrying everything alone was strength. And these two—sixteen and disgraced, a runaway and an exile from her own regiment—had just taught him otherwise in the space of ten minutes, at a cost he could never repay.

He could carry their losses for them, though. That much he could do. He would remember Astrid's mother for her, if she ever asked. He would tell Bramble, someday, what she'd given up, if she ever wanted to know. Someone had to hold what they'd paid. He would be the vault.

"Coming?" Bramble called from the passage mouth, her voice echoing strangely off stone that breathed.

Wendell took one last look at the daylight behind them—grey sky, grey peaks, the world of roads and bargains and small survivable sins—and stepped under the arch into the mountain's throat.

The passage descended in long spirals, and the walls began to change. The basalt gave way to something darker, shot through with those red veins, and the veins grew brighter the deeper they went, until they were walking through a corridor of slow fire. The warmth increased. Somewhere far below, something vast exhaled.

And then, drifting up through the stone, faint as a memory of music, Wendell heard singing. Hundreds of voices, thin and sweet and terribly sad, harmonizing in a language he almost recognized.

Astrid stopped dead. Her hand found his sleeve.

"Wendell," she whispered. "The lanterns. They're singing."

The song rose around them, and in it, threaded through the hundreds, one voice he knew better than his own withheld name.

Solveig.

* * *

They descended faster after that. None of them spoke. There was nothing to say that the song wasn't already saying.

The spiral narrowed, then opened all at once onto a ledge overlooking a vastness that made Wendell's knees soften. Below them, filling a cavern like a cathedral's heart, hung thousands of lights—soul-lanterns, each one a captive flame, each one a voice in the choir. They swayed in a wind that came from nowhere, and their light pooled below like a sea of embers, and at the center of that sea, vast beyond sense, something slept. Something half-born. Something starving.

"There," Astrid breathed, pointing with a trembling hand. "Third row from the edge. The dim one. Wendell—that's her. That's Solveig."

And it was. He knew it the way he knew his own name—the name he had almost given away, the name that was suddenly, desperately precious, because it was the last thread connecting him to the girl hanging dimming above a hungry god.

"We get her out tonight," Wendell said. "Before the harvest."

Above them, unseen in the ember-dark, something in the deep turned over in its sleep—and listened.
