## 7. The Court Beneath

The smuggler roads ended where the mountain began to breathe.

Wendell felt it first as warmth under his boots, a faint pulse rising through the stone like blood through skin. Then came the light—not torchlight, not daylight, but something older, seeping up through cracks in the rock in slow amber veins. Astrid stopped walking and pressed her palm flat against the tunnel wall.

"It's louder here," she whispered. "It's not words anymore. It's just—want."

"Then we're close." Wendell checked the lanterns on Bramble's pack harness, though he didn't need to. Down here, they'd be moths. "Stay behind me. Touch nothing that glows."

"That's most of everything," Bramble said.

"I know."

They descended through passages that no smuggler had mapped, because no smuggler had ever come this way and returned to draw the map. The walls changed character as they went—rough granite giving way to black stone veined with emberlight, then to something smoother, worked, carved long ago by hands that had understood fire the way masons understood mortar. Wendell ran his fingers along a groove in the wall and found it warm as a living thing.

He had spent fifteen years telling himself the Ember Court was a story priests told to frighten children into tithing. He had needed it to be a story, because if it was real, then everything he'd suffered—the exile, the wandering, the silence of the high passes—had been for something that could be bargained with.

And now he was walking down its throat.

* * *

The tunnel opened without warning, the way a held breath opens.

Wendell stepped out onto a ledge of black glass and forgot, for one full moment, how to breathe.

The Court was a cathedral built inside the hollowed heart of Thornfell, vast beyond sense, its ceiling lost in a haze of drifting sparks like an inverted night sky. And hanging from that darkness—thousands upon thousands of lanterns, each one a cage of pale glass holding a soft golden light, suspended from chains so fine they seemed spun from hair. They hung in rows, in clusters, in orchards, drooping low like fruit heavy on the branch.

Soul-lanterns. Hundreds of years of them. Every prayer the old gods had ever answered wrong, every soul the Court had bought or taken or traded for, glowing patiently in the dark.

Astrid made a small sound beside him. When Wendell glanced over, her face was wet.

"I can hear them," she said. "Not the mountain. Them. They're all humming. Like—" Her voice cracked. "Like people talking in their sleep."

Bramble had gone gray around the mouth. The big guard, who had once held a bridge against nine bandits with a broken arm, gripped the strap of his pack hard enough to whiten his knuckles. "This is what the priests fed," he said quietly. "All those years of answered prayers. This is what was eating."

"Come on." Wendell's voice came out flatter than he intended. "We find her lantern before anything finds us."

* * *

They moved along the ledge, down a stair of fused glass that chimed faintly under their boots, into the orchard of lights. Up close, each lantern resolved into detail: a cage of silver filigree, a flame within that pulsed with a rhythm almost—but not quite—like breathing. Some burned bright and quick. Some were guttering. One near Wendell's shoulder flickered in time with his own heartbeat, and he flinched away from it without knowing why.

Astrid drifted between them like someone walking through a garden of sleeping relatives, head tilted, listening. "This one's a farmer," she murmured. "From before the war. This one keeps asking about her dog." She pressed her fingers lightly to a lantern's glass and the flame inside leaned toward her touch like a plant toward sun. "They're not suffering. That's the worst part. They're just... waiting."

"Don't touch them again," Wendell said.

"Why? It doesn't hurt—"

"Because I said so." Too sharp. He saw her stiffen, saw Bramble shift his weight, and forced his voice back down. "Everything in this place is listening. Everything is hungry. We don't announce ourselves."

But the truth, the truth he kept sheathed under his ribs like a knife, was simpler and more cowardly: he couldn't bear to watch her comfort them. Because every lantern she touched kindly was a lantern like Solveig's, and every kind word she offered the waiting dead was a reminder that somewhere in this orchard hung fifteen years of his own guilt, dimming by the hour.

They searched for what felt like days. Time moved strangely beneath the mountain; hunger came and went without reason, and the amber light never changed. Wendell read the small brass tags wired to each cage—names, dates, prices paid—and hated himself for the efficiency of it. Someone had kept meticulous records. Someone had believed this was bookkeeping.

Then Astrid stopped, three rows deep, beneath a lantern burning lower than any they'd seen.

"Wendell," she said softly. "I think this one's been calling your name."

He crossed to her in four strides that felt like wading through deep water.

The tag read: SOLVEIG IVERSEN. PAID IN FULL.

The flame inside was the color of a coal at dawn, barely holding itself together, pulsing slower than the others. Slower than a resting heart. As he watched, it shuddered, guttered, steadied.

Fifteen years. Fifteen years of hating the gods for taking her, of hating himself for surviving, of building a whole quiet life on top of her grave—and she had never been in the grave at all. She had been here. Hanging in the dark. Waiting. Dimming.

His knees went. He caught himself on the stair rail, and Astrid's hand found his shoulder, and he let it stay there because he had nothing left to hold himself up with.

"She's still in there," Astrid whispered. "She's tired, but she's there."

"Yes," said a voice above them. "She is."

* * *

The herald descended without descending—one moment absent, the next standing on the air a few feet away, as though the space had simply decided to contain him. He looked like a priest who had been left too close to a fire: robes the color of banked coals, skin smooth and faintly luminous, eyes like two embers in a face otherwise composed of shadow. Where he stood, the nearest lanterns brightened, leaning toward him the way flowers lean toward morning.

"You are the storm-caller," he said. His voice was gentle, resonant, the voice of a man reading scripture to the dying. "Wendell Iversen. We have waited for you longer than you have walked toward us."

"We?" Bramble put himself between the herald and Astrid, hammer already loose in its loop.

"The Court. The god. The congregation of the patient dead." The herald spread his hands, and the whole cathedral seemed to lean in. "You blame us, I think. You blame the priests who sold, the gods who were silent. But consider, Wendell Iversen: your sister is not gone. Your sister is *here*. While the graves of the world fill with the truly dead, she waits, preserved, warm, only a breath from waking. Is that not mercy?"

"Mercy." The word tasted like ash.

"Come." The herald drifted closer, and the lantern-light bent toward him adoringly. "Let me show you what the priests never understood. Our god is dying—not wicked, only starving. It was born to hold souls, as a hearth is born to hold fire, and the world has forgotten to feed it. The harvest approaches; the last embers will be gathered, and the god will either wake or go out forever. If it goes out, every soul in this place goes with it. All of them. Forever." He paused, letting the arithmetic settle. "Unless it wakes. And to wake, it needs a vessel willing to serve. A living will, wrapped around the dying flame. Your sister was chosen for this honor long ago. But she dims. She may not survive the joining."

Wendell heard the trap being built, brick by gentle brick, and stood very still inside it.

"And if I serve willingly," he said. "If I give you what you actually want."

The herald's ember-eyes brightened. "Ah. You do understand. Yes. A storm-gift is a rare currency—a living power, freely given, worth ten thousand harvested souls. Serve the god willingly, Wendell Iversen. Be its hands, its will, its keeper. And we will release your sister from the harvest. She will live on—not as fuel, but as vessel. Waking. Warm. *Alive*, in every way that matters. You will have saved her. You will have undone the worst day of your life. All you must surrender is everything you are."

Silence stretched across the cathedral. Somewhere far above, sparks drifted down like snow.

Astrid's hand found his sleeve. "Wendell. Don't listen to—"

"He's not lying," Wendell said quietly. "That's what makes it a bargain and not a trick. Look at him. He means it. She would live." He turned to the herald. "And me? What's left of me?"

"A servant of the deepest fire," the herald said, with something like tenderness. "You would not suffer. You would not even remember suffering. The god dreams gently."

Wendell looked up at the lantern. At the dim gold flicker that had been his sister's laugh, her terrible singing, her habit of stealing the blanket and denying it with total conviction. Fifteen years he had wanted one thing: her back. And here it was, priced at exactly everything else he had.

*Love means keeping someone,* said the boy he had been, the boy who had failed to save her.

*Or love means letting go,* said the man the mountain had made.

"Solveig," he said aloud, to the lantern, not the herald. "I don't know if you can hear me. If you can—I'm sorry. I'm sorry it took me fifteen years and a war's worth of bad roads to get here."

The flame pulsed. Once. Twice. And in its rhythm, faint as a memory of a memory, Wendell heard something that might have been his name, spoken the way she used to say it when he'd done something foolish—which was often, and which she had never once stopped forgiving.

His chest broke open and healed in the same breath.

"No," he said to the herald.

The ember-eyes did not dim. "Consider further. The harvest is—"

"No." Louder now, and the nearest lanterns trembled at the edge of his voice, because something in him was waking up after fifteen years of careful sleep. "You don't get her. You don't get any of them. You've been keeping the dead as livestock and calling it mercy, and I have spent half my life hiding from gods instead of fighting them, and I am *done* with both."

The warmth drained from the herald's face, leaving only the shadow and the two small fires. Around the cathedral, in row after row after row, the soul-lanterns began to brighten—all of them at once, all of them turning, like a field of eyes opening.

"The harvest begins at moonrise," the herald said, and his gentle voice was gone, replaced by something vast and cold speaking through a borrowed mouth. "You are welcome to attend. As guests—or as kindling."

The space folded, and he was gone.

* * *

For a long moment none of them moved. Then Bramble exhaled, long and shaky. "Well. That went well."

"We take the lantern and run," Astrid said, already reaching up toward the chain.

"Wait." Wendell caught her wrist. Above them, the whole orchard was stirring, chains swaying, thousands of small flames leaning toward the center of the cathedral like grass bending before wind. Toward a dais of black stone at the far end, where a shape was beginning—slowly, terribly—to gather itself out of the dark. Something with the outline of a body and the interior of a furnace, assembling itself joint by joint, breath by breath.

The god was waking early.

"It knows we're here," Wendell said. "And if we cut down one lantern, they'll know exactly which one matters." He looked at Astrid, at Bramble, at the thousands of caged lights hanging in the dark, and felt the old storm rise in his chest like water finding its level. "So we don't take one."

Astrid followed his gaze across the endless orchard, and understanding bloomed on her face, equal parts horror and awe. "Wendell. You can't mean all of them."

"Get ready," he said, as the first bell of the harvest began to toll somewhere in the deep. "Both of you. Whatever happens at moonrise—get ready."
