## 8. The Harvest of Embers

Astrid had never seen so much fire that wasn't warm.

The Ember Court's great hall was a cathedral carved from the mountain's own black stone, and every surface held flame—veins of it running through the walls like blood through skin, chandeliers of it hanging from vaulted ceilings, braziers the size of wagons burning without smoke. But when she reached toward it with the sense the mountain had given her, she felt nothing. No heat. No comfort. The fire here was hungry fire, fire that took and never gave back.

And hanging above it all, in rows upon rows, were the lanterns.

They looked like fruit. That was the worst part—the way they clustered on iron branches, hundreds of them, each one a soft-glowing sphere of light in a cage of dark metal. Soul-lanterns, Wendell had called them, his voice flat as slate when he'd described what waited below. Each one a person. Each one dimmer than it should have been, because something beneath the floor was drinking them slow.

Solveig's lantern hung near the center, on a branch by itself, as if the Court knew it was precious. It glowed a pale gold, and even from across the hall Astrid could feel it—a small warmth in all that cold, like a candle in a window of a house where someone is waiting up for you.

*She's still in there,* Astrid thought. *She's been waiting fifteen years.*

Below them, crouched in an alcove behind a curtain of woven copper, the harvest ceremony was assembling. Courtiers came first—tall figures whose bodies seemed made of banked coals wrapped in ash-grey robes, their faces smooth and featureless until they turned, and then embers kindled briefly where eyes should be. Then came the herald, the one who had offered Wendell his bargain, carrying a staff tipped with a lantern of its own. And last, borne up through a fissure in the floor on a platform of living rock, came the god.

It was not finished.

That was the thing Astrid understood immediately, the way you understand a wound is deep before you understand anything else about it. The god was a shape of fire trying to be a shape of everything else—limbs that flickered and reformed, a face that kept sliding between expressions like a reflection in disturbed water. It was vast, and it was starving, and it was *incomplete*, and every lantern in the hall leaned toward it slightly, the way flowers lean toward a sun that means to eat them.

"It needs her," Wendell breathed beside her. His knuckles were white around the hilt of his knife, which was a joke and they both knew it—a knife against this. "The final ember. She tips it over into being whole."

"Then we take her out of reach." Astrid checked the rope coiled at her hip, then the little knife Bramble had given her, then the vial of crushed glowmoss that was supposed to blind a courtier for ten heartbeats. Her hands were shaking. She let them shake. Fear could come along; fear just didn't get to drive. "Bramble's ready?"

Wendell touched two fingers to his ear, where Bramble had pressed a sliver of whisperstone before they split up. A pause. Then: "He says the smuggler road opens thirty feet behind the eastern pillar. He says, quote, 'don't dawdle, don't be heroes, and if either of you dies I'll never forgive you, which will ruin my reputation.'"

Despite everything, Astrid smiled. "That sounds like Bramble."

"Astrid." Wendell turned to look at her, and in the emberlight his eyes were the grey of storm clouds over water. "When I lift the lantern, the Court will know. There won't be time to talk or plan after that. So I'm saying it now. Whatever happens—you run. You get to the road. You don't wait for me."

"No."

"It wasn't a question."

"Yes, it was. Everything's a question. That's how questions work." She held his gaze, sixteen years old and absolutely certain. "You taught me that on the road, when you said the mountain doesn't ask permission before it moves. Well, neither do I. We both go, or we both—" 

The herald raised its staff.

Every lantern in the hall flared at once.

* * *

There was no more time for arguing, because there was no more time at all.

The ceremony moved like a tide. Courtiers drifted to their stations among the lantern-branches. The half-born god sank lower in its cradle of stone, mouth opening—not a mouth, a *hunger* shaped like a mouth—and the nearest lanterns began to drift loose from their branches, floating down like ripe fruit falling upward into waiting hands.

Toward Solveig's branch, three courtiers converged.

"Now," Wendell whispered. "Glowmoss on my mark. Three. Two—"

He went over the edge of the alcove like he'd been thrown, and Astrid threw the vial a heartbeat later. It burst against the nearest courtier's face in a bloom of searing green light, and the thing recoiled with a sound like a kettle screaming, and then they were both running—up the low ridge of stone, through the shadow between branches, into the constellation of the dead.

Up close, the lanterns hummed. Not a sound exactly—a feeling, a vibration in the teeth, in the breastbone. And they *noticed* her. As she passed, they brightened faintly, turning on their hooks like faces following a stranger through a market. They knew she could hear them. She could hear them too, now that she was among them, a chorus thin and frayed but unmistakable:

*cold, so cold, how long, how long, is someone there, is someone—*

"I hear you," she gasped, running. "I hear all of you. Hold on."

Solveig's lantern hung directly ahead, and Wendell reached it first. His hands—steady now, steadier than they'd been in fifteen years, steady the way a man is steady at the exact center of his own catastrophe—closed around the iron cage.

For one moment, nothing happened.

Then the lantern flared like a sunrise, and every alarm the Court possessed went off at once.

The hall's veins of fire turned white. The music—if it had been music, the low drone Astrid hadn't realized she'd been hearing—stopped. And the god lifted its unfinished head, and looked at them, and *knew*.

"RUN!" Wendell roared, lantern in hand, and they ran.

* * *

The eastern pillar was thirty feet away and thirty miles away. Courtiers poured from the walls themselves, peeling out of the stone like flames off a log. Wendell fought the way he did everything—economically, furiously, with no wasted motion—but a knife is a knife, and the courtiers did not bleed, and Astrid saw him driven back step by step, the lantern held high above the fray like a torch.

Then the floor split.

Not near them—behind them, along the escape route, the smuggler road's entrance tearing open into a jagged seam as the Court sealed its wounds. And standing in the narrowing gap, braced against collapsing stone with his shoulder and his back and his whole stubborn body, was Bramble.

"GO!" he bellowed. His voice cracked on it. He'd wedged an iron pry-bar into the seam and was holding it open with everything he had, stone grinding against metal against bone. "Road's open! GO, YOU IDIOTS!"

"Bramble—" Astrid skidded to a stop.

"I owe him a debt, girl, not you! Which means I get to choose how I pay it, and I choose THIS, so RUN!" A chunk of ceiling came down beside him and he didn't even flinch. "Wendell! You listen to me! Fifteen years you've carried that sister of yours like a sack of stones—well, you're carrying her OUT now, so make it count! MOVE!"

Wendell moved. Astrid moved. They ran through the gap side by side, and just before the seam swallowed the sight of him, Astrid looked back and saw Bramble turn his head and grin at her—grin, the absolute maddening wonderful man—and then the stone closed and the grin was gone and the road behind them was wall.

They ran anyway. Because Bramble had held it open, and because stopping would mean he'd held it open for nothing.

The smuggler road was narrow and black and sloped steeply upward, and Astrid's lungs burned, and Wendell's hand found hers in the dark, and for eleven seconds—one, two, three, she counted them desperately, clutching the counting like a rail—she believed they were going to make it.

Then she heard the other lanterns.

All of them. Hundreds of them, still back there in the hall, still hanging on their branches, still singing their thin frayed song as the god's hunger rose around them like floodwater. She felt them the way she felt the mountain—except the mountain had always felt like a grandfather's voice, deep and patient, and this felt like children in a burning house.

*is someone there, is someone there, please, is someone—*

Astrid stopped running.

"Astrid." Wendell's grip tightened. "Don't."

"They're going to eat them. All of them. Tonight. While we climb out with one and call it a rescue." Her voice came out strange and calm, the way it did right before she did something the elders would have called sin and she called obvious. "You told me once the mountain doesn't ask permission. Neither does the Court. Somebody has to refuse first."

"You are SIXTEEN—"

"And you were exiled at twenty for refusing to burn people, so don't you DARE tell me what I'm too young to—" 

The wall behind them opened.

No explosion, no warning. The black stone simply parted like a curtain, and the herald stood in the gap, staff blazing, and behind it the hall blazed, and behind *that* the god rose to its full unfinished height and began, at last, to speak. Its voice was every answered prayer gone wrong, every desperate wish twisted into a hook:

**"BRING ME THE KEEPER. SHE HEARS. SHE WILL DO FOR A DOOR."**

Courtiers flooded the road from both directions. Wendell put himself between her and them with the lantern in one fist and his knife in the other, and Astrid watched him calculate it—watched him arrive, in real time, at the arithmetic of a man who has spent fifteen years learning exactly how much one storm-caller can do against an army, and knows the answer.

"Wendell," she said quietly. "Take her and go."

"No."

"That's an order. I'm technically a shrine-keeper, you're technically—"

"Don't you *dare* make me laugh right now—"

She kissed his cheek, quick and fierce, the way you'd bless someone. Then she stepped past him, into the open, and raised both hands, and did the only thing left that was hers to do.

She listened—all the way down, deeper than she'd ever dared, past the Court, past the god, to the old bones of Thornfell itself—and she *called*. Not words. A keeper's call, older than the priests, older than the exile: *mountain, mountain, your keeper is here, your keeper is taken, REMEMBER ME.*

And the mountain, far above, groaned in its sleep.

Then the courtiers took her arms, gentle as falling ash and irresistible as gravity, and the last thing Astrid saw was Wendell's face—fury and grief and a dawning, terrible resolve, the look of a man watching the last door close and deciding to become the storm instead—

—and the stone folded over her like a page turning, and the light went out.

* * *

They put her in a cell of glass and ember-light, high in the Court's spire, where she could see everything.

That was the cruelty of it, she realized. They wanted her to watch. Below, the hall blazed; the harvest resumed; the lanterns sang their fading song. Somewhere down there, Wendell was loose with a stolen soul in his hands and a look on his face she'd only ever heard described in stories about the old wars.

Somewhere down there, Bramble was under a mountain of stone, and she didn't know if debt-paid meant alive.

Astrid pressed her palms to the glass and closed her eyes and listened, past her own terror, past the god's rising hunger, all the way down to the deep slow heartbeat of Thornfell.

It was answering her call. It was coming.

But it was very far away, and the harvest was tonight, and the god was almost awake.

"Hold on," she whispered to the lanterns, to Bramble, to herself. "Hold on. Hold on. Hold on."

Far below, the first true note of the harvest ceremony rang out like a bell made of hunger—and in the dark of the spire, Astrid felt the mountain's answer finally, faintly, begin to move.
