## 9. Storm Over the Deep

They took Astrid at the foot of the god's cradle, and they took her gently, which was worse.

Wendell had seen the Ember Court's guards move before—soul-lantern light given arms and patience, drifting through the cathedral dark like slow lightning that had forgotten its purpose. Now six of them closed around her, and she didn't fight. She stood with Solveig's lantern clutched to her chest, the other stolen souls swinging from her belt in a net of woven copper, and she looked at Wendell across the burning floor and said, "Don't you dare trade yourself for me."

Too late. He'd already done the arithmetic on the walk down.

The herald drifted between them, its face a smooth mask of cooling glass, its voice the sound of a fire settling into ash. "You see how it must be," it said. "The god starves. The vessel weakens. Give us the storm in you, willingly, and the girl walks out carrying nothing but her own small life. The lanterns stay. The harvest completes. Everyone is fed."

"You keep saying *fed* like it's a kindness."

"It is the only kindness we have left." The herald's glass face tilted. "We were priests once, storm-caller. Before we were this. We know what it is to give everything to something dying."

Bramble was still up there somewhere—up at the shattered toll-gate passage where he'd held the Court's rearguard off with a borrowed axe and a debt he'd finally paid. Wendell let himself feel that for one breath, sharp as swallowing a nail, and then put it away. Grief later. Work now.

Astrid's eyes were wet and furious. "The mountain told me what you're thinking," she said. "It's been telling me since the gate. It says you're going to do something stupid and holy and I should stop you, except it also says—" Her voice cracked. "It also says there might not be another way."

"There's always another way."

"Name it."

He couldn't. That was the truth of it. Fifteen years of hoarding his gift like a miser hoards coin—refusing the priests who wanted storms as weapons, refusing every smaller use too, because using it at all felt like the first step down their road—and now the whole hoard came due at once, and the interest was a girl with soot on her cheeks and a dead woman's soul in a jar.

The guards began to draw Astrid back toward the cradle, toward the half-born thing coiled in the mountain's heart—a shape like a hearth the size of a cathedral, banked coals for organs, a hunger older than the priests who'd built it. Above it hung the last unharvested lanterns, hundreds of them, each one a person someone had loved enough to pray for and been betrayed for praying.

Solveig's lantern was dimming. He could see the ember inside guttering, going gray at the edges, and fifteen years of guilt turned over in him like a ship in a squall.

"Astrid," he said. "When I start—run. Don't be brave. Be fast."

"I hate you."

"I know. Go."

* * *

Calling a storm indoors should have been impossible. Calling one under a mountain should have been blasphemy against physics itself. But Wendell Iversen had spent fifteen years learning the hidden grammar of Thornfell—the smuggler roads, the blind passes, the places where water remembered being rain—and he knew something the priests of his old order had never understood: the sky doesn't end at the stone. The stone is just sky that decided to hold still.

Every storm that ever broke over these peaks had left its water in the rock. Every river running through the dark had once been cloud. The mountain was full of weather, sleeping, waiting for someone rude enough to wake it.

He planted his feet on the burning floor and reached down into that sleep.

It came up through him like a train through a tunnel. Not gentle—nothing about it was gentle—but *his*, in a way nothing had been his in fifteen years. He felt the groundwater shiver. Felt the deep aquifers turn over. Felt, far above, snowfields crack and thunderclouds lean against the peaks like drunks against a door. His hands went white-hot with static. His hair lifted. Somewhere behind him Astrid screamed his name, and the guards holding her flinched, because even things made of lantern-light know what lightning means.

"Herald," he said, and his voice came out doubled, layered with distant thunder. "New bargain."

"The god does not accept—"

"It's not accepting. It's *receiving*." He raised both hands. "You wanted a storm to feed your dying god. Here's the whole sky. Hope it can swallow."

He brought it down.

Lightning in the open world is a guest—it strikes, it leaves. Lightning called into a mountain is an occupant. Wendell poured it into the cathedral of banked fires and did not stop pouring. The floor split. Soul-lanterns swung on their chains like censers. The half-born god convulsed, its coal-heart flaring white, and for one terrible moment Wendell thought he'd miscalculated—that he'd fed it after all, that the priests' original sin would simply complete itself through his hands.

Then the god screamed.

Not with a mouth. With everything. The sound came up through the stone and out through the roots of the mountain, and it was the sound of a hunger being answered too fast, a stomach bursting, a thing built to sip drowning in an ocean. The herald's glass face crazed with cracks. "Stop," it said, and its voice was suddenly small, suddenly priestly, suddenly human. "Storm-caller, *stop*, you'll—"

"I know what I'll do."

That was the payment. He'd known since the gate. A gift this size, spent all at once, spent *through*—it wouldn't leave anything behind. The storm wasn't borrowing his body; it was consuming it, nerve by nerve, memory by memory, the way a wick consumes a candle. He could already feel edges of himself going: the name of his first smuggling captain, the taste of Greymoss bread, the color of his sister's eyes—

No. Not that. He gripped that one and held it. If he lost everything else, he'd keep her eyes.

The god burst.

There was no better word. The coal-heart split along seams no one had ever meant it to have, and light came out—not the greedy amber of the Court but something whiter, cleaner, the light of a fire finally allowed to die. And with the light came *them*. Hundreds of souls, released from their lanterns all at once, rising through the collapsing cathedral like sparks off a bonfire struck by a falling tree. They streamed past Wendell. Some brushed him as they went, and each touch was a warmth, a gratitude, a name spoken in a voice he almost recognized.

The lantern chains snapped. The ceiling groaned. And Astrid—

He found her by her stubbornness, because that was the loudest thing left in the room. She'd gotten free in the chaos—of course she had; the guards were coming apart into loose light—and she was standing under the falling debris with her arms spread over the copper net of remaining souls, shielding them with her body, screaming at the mountain itself to wait, to give her time.

The mountain, remarkably, listened. Or maybe it was listening to her all along, and everything else had just been noise.

"Wendell!" She saw him and her face did three things at once—relief, terror, and the particular fury of a girl watching a man stand in a flood zone on purpose. "You're coming *out*. You don't get to be noble here. Bramble's alive—I heard him coughing at the gate, he's alive, and you don't get to make me carry everyone home alone—"

"I'm not being noble." He took a step toward her and the floor gave way beneath it, and he rode the tilting stone like a deck in heavy seas, still leaking light from every seam of himself. "I'm being honest. This was always the price. I just spent fifteen years pretending the bill wouldn't come."

"Then I'm contesting the charge!"

Despite everything—despite the roof coming down and the god dissolving around them—he laughed. It hurt. Everything hurt. But it was the first laugh in fifteen years that didn't taste like hiding.

The last of the storm left him. He felt it go the way you feel a fever break—one long shudder, and then a strange, clean emptiness. He sank to his knees on the ruined floor. Above, the final lanterns were emptying themselves, souls rising through cracks in the mountain toward a sky he'd never see again, and among them, one light paused.

It hovered at the edge of his failing vision. Small. Gray-gold. Shaped, if he let himself believe it, like a girl of nineteen who used to steal honey cakes and blame the cat.

*Solveig.*

He couldn't speak anymore. There wasn't enough of him left to speak with. But she knelt, or seemed to, in the falling ash, and pressed something against his forehead that wasn't quite a hand, and the warmth of it said everything the words would have: *not your fault, never your fault, go home, big brother, go home.*

Then she rose with the others, and the light took her, and Wendell Iversen lay down in the ruins of the Ember Court with an empty chest and a full heart and absolutely no idea whether either of those things would kill him first.

Above him, faintly, impossibly, he heard Astrid arguing with a mountain.

"—he's *mine*," she was saying. "Mine and Bramble's. Family. So whatever you think you're owed for the shrine and the hearth and all of it, you take it, and you give me *him*."

And the mountain, having just lost its god, considered the matter.

* * *

The darkness was total and warm, like being a coal in a banked fire, and Wendell was almost content to stay there when a voice cut through it—young, furious, tear-roughened, and utterly unwilling to negotiate.

"If you die on me, I will find whatever's left of your soul and put it in a *lantern*, and I will hang you in my kitchen, and you will listen to me sing while I cook. Do you understand me? Wake *up*."

Something cold touched his lips. Water—snowmelt, tasting of stone and morning.

Wendell opened his eyes.

Far above, through a wound torn in the mountainside, a thread of real daylight fell into the dark and landed on his face. Astrid crouched over him, soot-streaked, soaked, alive, with the copper net of freed souls—empty now, every one—slung across her back like a soldier's pack. Behind her, leaning on a broken halberd and grinning through a face full of blood, stood Bramble.

"Well," Bramble said. "Either we're all dead, or you finally did something worth the exile."

Wendell tried to answer. What came out instead was thin, cracked, and laughing—and above them, through the wound in the mountain, the first thunder in fifteen years rolled across a sky that no longer belonged to anyone, rolling away southward, toward home.
