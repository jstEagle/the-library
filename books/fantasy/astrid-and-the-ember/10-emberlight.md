## 10. Emberlight

The silence after the storm was the loudest thing Astrid had ever heard.

She stood in the ruins of the Ember Court with her hands pressed over her ears anyway, as if she could keep the quiet out. For so long the mountain had been a murmur under everything—a hunger, a whisper, a name being called in the dark—and now there was nothing. Just stone settling. Just ash drifting down through the broken cathedral roof like gray snow.

The god was dead. Truly dead, this time, not dying and grasping. Wendell's lightning had gone down its throat like a sword of daylight, and it had come apart from the inside, and every soul it had hoarded had burst free at once.

Astrid had seen them go. She would see them for the rest of her life, she thought: thousands of lights rising through the shattered vaults, streaming up through fissures in the rock like sparks off a bonfire, pouring out into the night sky over Thornfell until the shepherds in the high valleys must have thought the stars were falling upward. Some of them had passed close enough to warm her face. One had brushed her cheek—light as a moth's wing—and she'd heard, just for a heartbeat, a voice that might have been saying *thank you* or might have been saying *go home*, and then it was gone, and they were all gone, and the Court was only a grave with the bodies removed.

"Astrid."

Wendell's voice. She turned.

He was on his knees in the ash, and for one terrible moment she thought he was dying too—that the storm had taken its price all the way to the bone. His gift was gone; she could feel the absence of it the way you feel a missing tooth, an empty socket in the world where his lightning used to live. But he was breathing. He was looking at his own hands like he didn't recognize them, turning them over, flexing fingers that would never again call thunder.

"I can't feel it," he said. Not to her, exactly. To himself. "Fifteen years I carried it like a disease, and now—" He laughed, or something near a laugh, cracked down the middle. "Now it's quiet."

*You're quiet,* the mountain murmured—not dead after all, Astrid realized, just emptied, just resting. *You're both quiet. Good.*

She crouched beside Wendell and did the thing she'd been wanting to do since Greymoss, since the campfires, since the toll gate: she put her arms around him. He went stiff as a fence post. He always did. But he didn't pull away, and after a while one of those strange new hands came up and rested, very lightly, on top of her head like she was something worth sheltering.

"He's alive," Wendell said. "Bramble. I can hear him digging. Down the west passage—he's using a shield as a shovel, the idiot."

"Then let's go dig too," Astrid said.

* *

They dug Bramble out of six feet of collapsed corridor by lantern-light, because the soul-lanterns still hung in some of the deeper galleries—empty now, their glass dark, but the frames held fire-shy light the way old walls hold cold. Bramble emerged coughing, filthy, one arm cradled wrong against his chest, and looked at the two of them silhouetted against the ruin and said, in a voice like gravel poured slow:

"Told you I'd hold the door."

"You told us you'd *try* to hold the door," Wendell said.

Bramble grinned through the blood on his teeth. "Door's held, isn't it?"

His arm was broken in two places and three of his ribs were worse, but he refused to be carried, so they made a chair of their linked hands and half-dragged, half-walked him up through the smuggler roads while the mountain groaned itself back into shape around them. The paths that had been collapsing for weeks were knitting closed. The treacherous scree fields steadied. Twice Astrid felt the stone shift deliberately beneath Bramble's bad footfall, catching him, and knew the mountain was doing it on purpose.

*He owes me a debt now,* the mountain said, sounding smug about it.

"You owe *us*," Astrid corrected it aloud, and both men looked at her, and she just shook her head. "Later. It talks. You'll get used to it."

Wendell did not look like he would ever get used to it. But he kept walking.

* *

They came out of the mountain at dawn, through the low gate above the Cold Shrine valley, and the light that met them was the ordinary gold of any morning, and Astrid cried at how ordinary it was.

The shrine itself stood unchanged: small, square-shouldered, its godless altar swept clean by fifteen years of her solitary tending. She had fled this place certain she'd never want to see it again. Now she stood in its doorway with ash in her hair and Bramble snoring against the wall and Wendell building a fire in the hearth with the clumsy competence of a man who no longer had magic to cheat with, and she listened.

Not with her ears. With whatever it was the mountain had given her instead.

*I am empty,* the mountain said. Its voice was different now—smaller, or maybe just honest. *The court is dust. The god is gone. What remains is a hollow with good bones. A hearth with no fire in it.* A pause, long enough that she almost spoke. *I could be a shrine again. Not mine. Yours. A place where people bring what they carry and set it down. You hear me, keeper. You have always heard me. Will you keep me?*

Astrid thought about her mother's face, which she no longer remembered—the toll gate had taken that memory whole, and no storm had gotten it back. She thought about the elders in her village who had wanted to silence her, and the pilgrims in Greymoss whose prayers had come back twisted, and the thousands of sparks rising into the sky. She thought about how inheritance had always seemed to her like a trap: a shrine handed down, a duty handed down, a life you didn't choose.

But this wasn't that. This was the mountain asking. Asking, not commanding. That was the difference, and it was the whole difference, and she felt it settle into her chest like a coal laid gently in cupped hands.

"Yes," she said. "But I'm changing the rules. No more buying. No more souls. No more gods who eat." She looked around the little room—at Wendell frozen mid-fire-building, at Bramble blinking awake and pretending he hadn't been listening. "And no more keeping it alone. Shrines are supposed to have families."

*Agreed,* said the mountain, and beneath her feet she felt it exhale, fifteen years of hunger finally, finally let go.

* *

Solveig came at dusk.

Astrid saw her first—the way you see weather coming. The air above the shrine's doorstep thickened, went gold at the edges, and gathered itself into a woman in her thirties with storm-grey eyes and Wendell's exact stubborn jaw. She was translucent at the hem, unraveling slowly into motes of light, and she was smiling.

Wendell came out of the shrine so fast he knocked over the water bucket. He stopped three steps away, close enough to touch, far enough that touching would break whatever spell held her here, and his mouth worked twice before anything came out.

"Solveig."

"Hello, Wendell." Her voice was wind through a pass. "You look terrible."

He made a sound that was half laugh, half sob. "You've been under there fifteen years. In a *lantern*. And you're worried about—"

"Fifteen years is nothing when you're light," she said. "It's the waiting that's long, and I'm done waiting." She looked past him, at Astrid, and inclined her head the way you greet a colleague. "Keeper. Thank you for refusing to leave without the others. I heard that. We all heard that."

Astrid's throat was too tight to answer. She nodded.

Solveig turned back to her brother, and her smile gentled into something older and sadder and warmer. "I know what you've been carrying. Fifteen years of it. That my death was your fault because you wouldn't make yourself into a weapon. That if you'd said yes to the priests, you'd have been there." She raised a hand, not quite touching his cheek, the light of her fingers hovering a hair's breadth from his skin. "It was never yours to carry. They took me because I was bright, not because you were absent. And you spent yourself breaking open their god to free strangers you owed nothing. Little brother. There was never anything to forgive. But if it helps you to hear it: you're forgiven. You were forgiven before you finished walking down the mountain."

Wendell broke. Astrid watched it happen—the fifteen years going out of him all at once, his knees folding, Solveig's light-cupped hand finally landing on his hair like a benediction, holding there while he wept into the doorstep of a stranger's shrine. Bramble turned his face away and studied the horizon with tremendous interest, and his eyes were wet anyway.

When Wendell looked up again, Solveig was already thinning, her edges dissolving into the first stars.

"Live," she told him. "That's the whole prayer. Live somewhere. With someone. Be difficult about it—I know you will." Her smile was the last thing to go. "Goodbye, Wendell. Burn bright. Don't hoard it."

And she was gone—not downward, not up, just *outward*, everywhere, a warmth on the evening air that faded the way a hearth fades when everyone has finally gone to bed full and safe.

* *

They buried nothing, because there was nothing to bury. Instead they lit the shrine's great lamp—the one Astrid's grandmother had kept, the one that had gone cold when the god went quiet—and set it in the window facing the mountain road.

"It'll need tending," Astrid said. "The shrine. The lamp. Pilgrims will start coming when word spreads—good ones, hopefully, and some bad ones, and we'll need someone intimidating at the door." She looked pointedly at Bramble's splinted arm. "In a few months."

"I don't recall being asked," Bramble rumbled.

"You're being asked now."

He glared at her. He glared at Wendell. Wendell, feeding the hearth fire, said mildly, "You owe the mountain a debt. It mentioned it."

"The mountain *talks?*"

"Constantly," Astrid said. "You'll get used to it."

Bramble muttered something about smugglers and shrines and madwomen, but he was already eyeing the doorway like a man measuring it for a guard post, and nobody was fooled, least of all him.

Later—much later, when Bramble slept by the fire and the lamp burned steady in the window—Astrid found Wendell outside, standing in the cold with his empty hands in his pockets, looking up at the sky where the sparks had gone.

"They're still up there," he said quietly. "Some of them. I can't feel storms anymore, but I swear some of those lights are still moving."

"Maybe they're checking on you," Astrid said.

He huffed. But he didn't argue, and when she leaned against his side, he let her, and after a moment his arm came around her shoulders—awkwardly, like a man handling something he was afraid of breaking, and determined to learn.

"Family," he said, testing the word like a bridge he didn't trust. "You said family. Back there."

"I name things," Astrid said. "It's the job."

"Wendell Ishikawa," he said slowly, "shrine-hand. Gods help us all."

*Gods help us all,* the mountain agreed warmly, and below them, in the window of the Cold Shrine—which was cold no longer—the emberlight burned on.
