## 1. The Salt Road Prayer

The mule knew before I did.

She stopped dead on the switchback above the Kettle, ears flat, and no amount of my swearing would move her. Ten years of hauling contraband through Thornfell's high passes had taught me to trust that kind of stubbornness. Animals hear things. Gods, too, maybe.

"Jude." Tam's voice came low from behind me, the way he spoke when he wanted to be smoke. "Company below."

I eased forward along the trail until I could see past the shoulder of rock we called the Old Woman. Below us, maybe two hundred feet down where the path bent toward the salt terraces, a line of men climbed out of the mine works—six of them in the grey-and-crimson of the Regency charter companies, and behind them, stumbling, a seventh man in rags with his wrists chained to an overseer's saddle.

A miner. Runaway, or failed to make quota, or just slow that morning. Down there it hardly mattered which.

"You know the protocol," Tam murmured. He was younger than me by fifteen years, all elbows and nerves, and he still believed the warrens would keep us safe if we followed their rules hard enough. "We're not here. We're fog."

"We're fog," I agreed, and settled into the shadow of the Old Woman with my hand on the mule's bridle, and I watched a man die.

That was the part I'd never say aloud afterward, not to anyone: that I watched. That I could have done something—loosed one of the bolts from our own cargo, started a rockslide, shouted—and instead I did what I'd trained myself to do for ten years, which was nothing. Invisibility wasn't cowardice, I told myself. It was survival. It was the whole architecture of my life. Jude Nakamura, swordspeaker, didn't exist. There was only a smuggler woman with a scarred lip and a good memory for passes, who kept her mouth shut around metal and never, ever listened when steel tried to talk to her.

Below, the overseers made their judgment quick. The foreman—a fat man named Corvess whose voice carried up the mountain even in wind—read something off a slate. The miner knelt in the scree. His lips were moving.

And then he prayed.

I heard it because the mountain heard it, and the mountain has never learned to whisper. It came up through the soles of my boots first, a vibration like a struck bell buried deep in stone, and then the air itself took the words and carried them, and every hair on my arms stood up under my sleeves.

*O Veyra-of-the-Deep-Roots, Mother Under Stone, they have hollowed your body and sold your blood. I am small and I am finished, but you are not small, and you are not finished. Remember me.*

My breath went out of me like I'd been gutted.

Because I knew that name. I knew it the way you know a word from a language you swore never to speak again—the shape of it in your mouth before you can stop yourself. Veyra. One of the old gods, one of the nine the dead queen's order had bound and bargained with, one of the voices I had spent a decade refusing to hear through every blade I passed in every market stall. The kingdom fell, the queen died, the temples went dark, and everyone agreed the gods had gone with them. That was the story. That was the story I had built my life inside of, like a wall built around a grave.

The miner knelt in the scree, and the mountain answered him.

It didn't rumble. Rumbling is what mountains do when they're indifferent. This was different—this was *rising*. I felt it through the rock at my back, a long slow gathering, like a body drawing breath after ten years of holding it. The scree below us began to crawl uphill. Uphill. Stones the size of fists dragging themselves against gravity, streaming toward the overseers like iron filings toward a lodestone.

Corvess stopped reading his slate.

"Veyra's tits," one of the guards said, and then the slope beneath their feet simply stood up.

I have seen avalanches. I have seen the Regency's fire-cannons level a toll station. I have seen things in ten years of smuggling that I don't tell people over drinks. But I had never seen a mountain move like it was deciding something. The hillside peeled itself open and folded over the six of them the way a hand closes over flies—not fast, exactly, but inevitable, with a sound like the world's largest door swinging shut. The chained miner the stones flowed *around*, parting for him as if he were a child in a crowd, and left him kneeling untouched in a perfect ring of raw earth while the dust rose white and thick and hung there, unmoving, like the mountain was holding its breath.

Then everything settled. The dust drifted. Somewhere far below, a bird started up again, absurd and ordinary.

The miner stayed on his knees a long time. When he finally stood, he looked up—at the sky, at the ridgeline, at nothing—and I swear on my mother's ashes that for half a second his eyes found ours behind the Old Woman's shoulder. Then he turned and walked down the pass toward the lowlands, free, alive, and I understood with total clarity that my life as I had built it was over.

* * *

Tam talked the whole way back to the waystation. That was his coping mechanism; mine was silence, and between us we must have sounded like a landslide and its shadow.

"—could've been a fluke, right? Deep tremor, gas pocket, some kind of—" He caught my expression and faltered. "Jude. You've gone grey. What is it?"

"Nothing."

"That was not nothing. That was a god, or I'm a tax collector."

I checked the mule's hooves. I checked the cargo straps. I did everything except answer him, because the answer was a door I'd nailed shut ten years ago and I could feel it rattling on its hinges.

Here is what nobody living knows about me, except the dead: I can hear swords. Not metaphorically. Steel speaks—it always has, a thin cold voice underneath the ringing—and swordspeakers are the ones born able to listen. The old order called it a sacrament. The Regency calls it heresy and burns you for it. And the dead queen, may she rot gently wherever she rots, called it something else entirely.

She called it a leash.

I was nineteen when she put her hand on my head and made me swear. I thought I was being chosen. I thought the oath was armor, a shield against the inquisitors, permission to live hidden. It took me years to understand what I'd actually agreed to: that every blade I refused to hear was a prayer unanswered. The swordspeakers weren't priests. We were locks. The queen had bound the old gods' hunger behind our oaths, and when she died and the kingdom collapsed, the rest of us scattered and kept swearing silence to ourselves, and the gods went quiet—or seemed to.

And now a dying man had prayed to Veyra-of-the-Deep-Roots, and Veyra had answered, and the answering had felt—this is the part I couldn't stop turning over, walking that trail, feeling it in my teeth like a coming storm—the answering had felt like something straining against a chain that had finally, finally snapped.

Not mercy. I knew mercy. Mercy was what the gods had shown through us, once, channeled and leashed. What I'd felt on that mountainside was hunger wearing mercy's face.

"Jude." Tam had stopped walking. "You heard it too. Didn't you. Whatever that was—you *heard* it."

I looked at him. He was twenty-three and trusted me and had no idea what I was, and the honest thing—the thing I'd sworn to be, in the life I'd built out of not-being anything—was to lie to him.

"The mountain shifted," I said. "Old passes do that."

He didn't believe me. I could see it. But he shouldered the cargo pack and kept walking, and I followed him down into the warrens of Thornfell with the taste of stone dust in my mouth and a single thought repeating like a hammer on an anvil:

If the gods are answering prayers again, then the leash is broken. And if the leash is broken, then someone will come looking for the hands that used to hold it.

I was three days wrong about how much time I had.

Word travels faster than mules. By the time we reached the waystation—a smuggler's cache I'd spent six years making look like a collapsed shed—there was already a boy waiting outside it. Maybe twelve, hollow-cheeked, sitting on my doorstep with his knees drawn up like he'd been told this was where miracles lived.

He saw me and stood, and his eyes went wide, and he said the sentence that ended my invisibility forever:

"They said a swordspeaker walks the Salt Road. They said the mountain answered her."
