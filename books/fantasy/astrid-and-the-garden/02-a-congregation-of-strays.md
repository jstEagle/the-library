## 2. A Congregation of Strays

The boy was waiting at the waystation when Jude came down off the pass, and that was the first wrong thing. Nobody found the waystation. She'd built it that way—tucked into a fold of the mountain behind a curtain of deadfall, no smoke, no road, a door she'd paid a hedge-witch three years' wages to unnotice.

He sat on her step like he owned it, maybe twelve years old, hollow-cheeked, with a knife too big for him and the particular stillness of someone who'd learned young that being seen was dangerous. He'd been crying recently. He wasn't now.

"You're her," he said. "The one the mountain listened to."

"I'm a mule driver."

"The miner's daughter told everyone. Old Marta's granddaughter drew your face in the dirt at Kessel Ford and it wouldn't wash away." He shrugged, as if this were ordinary. "I can draw too. I didn't."

Jude looked at him for a long moment. Ten years of invisibility, and a child had unraveled it in a week with dirt and grief.

"What's your name?"

"Pip." Then, because honesty cost him something: "That's not my name."

"Get inside before someone sees you wanting something," she said, and hated herself for how gently it came out.

* * *

They came the way water finds cracks.

First the girl—Wren, sixteen, fine-boned and furious, wearing travel clothes too good for the road and a signet ring she kept turning like a worry-stone. Jude knew the crest before Wren offered it: the Vane owl, silver on black. An inquisitor's daughter traveling alone with her father's ring and no father.

"He burned a chapel," Wren said, when Jude asked nothing and she answered anyway. "A real one. Old-god carvings under the plaster, older than the Regency. He gave the congregation to the pyre and I—" Her jaw worked. "I said the names of the dead out loud. At dinner. In front of his colleagues."

"And?"

"And I'm here." She set the ring on Jude's table like a dare. "You're collecting us. The mountain-man's daughter said so. The swordspeaker gathers the faithful before the Door opens."

"I gather contraband."

"Then you're doing it badly," Wren said, "because all you've gathered lately is people."

Old Sefa came last, walking up the goat track in the gray of dawn with a pilgrim's staff and a pilgrim's smile, and Jude knew her on sight—the way you know a story you were told as a child. Sefa had been an acolyte of the Garden cult back when the cult still had temples, before the Regency scraped them off the earth. She should have been dead twenty years. She looked delighted about not being.

"You swore an oath," Sefa said, settling onto Jude's bench like her bones remembered it. "To the queen. I watched you swear it. You were so small."

Jude's hand went to her hip where the sword hung, old reflex. "You're mistaken."

"Child, I am ninety-one years old and I have buried every person who could contradict me. I am never mistaken." Sefa's eyes were milky but they fixed on Jude with terrible precision. "And the gods are awake again, which means the oath is due. That's how covenants work. They come due."

"Get out of my house."

"It's a waystation," Sefa said placidly. "It's in the name."

* * *

Interlude — from the papers of Sefa, acolyte of the Garden, written by firelight while the others slept:

The girl Jude thinks she is hiding us. She is hiding herself, and we are the excuse. I have watched prophets for sixty years and they are all the same: they would rather be struck by the god than needed by the people. Being needed is a door that opens both ways.

The thief-boy steals bread and leaves half of it outside for something he won't name. The Vane girl has stopped turning her father's ring; she wears it now, which is worse, or better—I haven't decided. And Jude sharpens her sword every night and tells it things. I hear her through the wall. She speaks to it the way you speak to a grave.

She thinks the gods answering prayers is new. It isn't. It is old. It is the oldest thing there is. What is new is that nobody is left alive who knows how to say *no* to them.

Except, perhaps, her.

* * *

Winter came down off the peaks early that year, and with it the riders.

Jude heard them before she saw them—hooves in the frozen mud of the valley, too many, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who answer to no one. From the ridge above the waystation she counted twelve. Regency black, the owl on their banners, torches already lit though the sun was barely down.

Below them lay Harrow Fen, the last village before the high passes. Forty households. A mill, a shrine, a woman who traded Jude salt for silence twice a year.

The riders stopped in the square. A man in a gray cloak—not soldier's black, inquisitor's gray—dismounted and held something up. Even at this distance Jude knew the shape of it: a drawing. A face in the dirt that wouldn't wash away.

"They'll ask who sheltered strangers," Pip said beside her. He'd come up silent as smoke; she hadn't heard him. "Then they'll ask harder."

"We leave tonight," Jude said. "Pack light. We go north over the scree, no tracks—"

"Go where?" Wren had appeared at her other shoulder, arms crossed, breath steaming. "You have a map to a door out of the country and you've told no one where it is. Fine. But we can't outride twelve men with a ninety-year-old woman and no horses."

Below, the inquisitor dragged the miller into the square. Jude couldn't hear the questions. She didn't need to. She'd spent ten years learning what inquisitors did to villages that lied for strangers, and the first lesson was always fire.

"Harrow Fen hid me," she heard herself say. "Two winters ago. Salt fever. They fed me and asked nothing."

"So we fight?" Wren's voice cracked on it—sixteen years old, a traitor's daughter, hands that had never held anything heavier than a hymnal. "Twelve against four?"

"Nobody's fighting." Jude was already moving down the ridge, and the terrible thing, the thing she'd examine later and fail to understand, was how little the decision felt like a decision. More like a joint straightening after years bent. "Pip. You know how to open a sluice?"

His grin was quick and feral. "I'm a thief. I know how to open everything."

"Then get down there and open their horse-line loose. Wren—you can read a rider's seal?"

"Of course."

"Forged orders buy an hour. An hour is enough." She drew the sword off her back, and the blade caught the last red light like it had been waiting. "Sefa stays here. If we don't come back—"

"If you don't come back," Sefa said mildly, "none of this matters, so spare me the instructions."

* * *

Later—much later, with the sluice broken and the horses scattered and the forged orders burning in a brazier and the inquisitor's gray cloak hanging soaked and furious from a saddle—Jude stood in the ruins of the mill and understood what she'd done.

Not the raid. The raid had gone clean. She meant the moment in the square, when the inquisitor had grabbed the miller's daughter and Jude had stepped out of the crowd, sword bare, and said, in a voice that carried to every soul in Harrow Fen:

*"Take me. I'm the swordspeaker."*

Twelve riders had heard. Forty villagers had heard. The gods, wherever they were listening—and they were listening, the ground under her boots had hummed like a struck bell—had heard.

Wren found her there among the ruined grain sacks. "We ride tonight," she said. "All of us. Harrow Fen will swear they never saw you, but the Regency doesn't believe villages, it burns them until they confess."

"I know."

"So where do we go? Tell me you have somewhere."

Jude looked west, toward the passes, toward the place the old maps called only *the threshold*, and felt the oath stir in her chest like a second heartbeat. Due, Sefa had said. Covenants come due.

"There's a door," she said. "And I think it's been waiting for me longer than I've been running from it."

Behind them, far down the valley, the first watch-fires of the pursuit kindled—one, then three, then a constellation of them, closing like a hand.

Sefa's voice, from the dark of the doorway, dry as old paper:

"Well then, child. Best start walking. The Garden keeps no hours, but it does keep accounts."
