## 3. What the Gods Want Back

The village of Lowmarsh smelled of rosemary and rot.

Jude felt it before she saw it—the wrongness in the air, sweet under the stink, like flowers laid on a grave too soon. She crested the ridge with Sefa wheezing behind her and the strays strung out along the goat path, and there below lay Lowmarsh: whole. Every roof mended. Every field green in a season when the blight should have taken everything.

"They were dying," Pip said. The orphan thief stood at Jude's shoulder, hollow-eyed, his stolen coat three sizes too big. "The fever. Half of them were dying when we came through last month."

"I know."

"And now they're not."

"I know, Pip."

A woman in the village square was dancing. Alone, barefoot, turning and turning with her arms flung wide, laughing at nothing. Nobody stopped her. Nobody looked at her at all.

Jude's hand went to the sword at her hip without her permission. Ten years she'd kept it wrapped and silent, ten years pretending the thing inside it was dead, and now every god-blessed mile of Thornfell hummed with voices that answered prayers like a merchant answering coin—*what will you give me, what will you give me, what will you give.*

"We shouldn't stop," she said.

"You said that about the last village," Sefa said. "And the one before. Girl, I was an acolyte for forty years. I know hunger when I see it fed." She nodded down at the square, where the dancing woman had begun to bleed from her palms, still smiling, still turning. "That's not mercy down there. That's a bill being paid in installments."

They didn't stop. They watered their mules at the well outside town while the villagers watched them with flat, grateful eyes, and one old man pressed bread into Pip's hands and whispered, *it asked for my son but I gave it my brother instead, and look how it blessed us,* and Jude walked faster after that.

* * *

Inquisitor Halvard Vane read the dispatch by lamplight and found, to his own surprise, that his hands were shaking.

*Lowmarsh: plague reversed overnight. Casualties zero. Note: subject's brother reported missing same night. Subject untroubled. Village refuses inquiry. Village sings at night.*

He had burned nine villages in two years hunting the swordspeaker. He had told himself each time that it was necessary, that faith left untended rots into something worse, that the Regency's silence on the gods was the only wall between order and the old dark. He had been very good at telling himself things.

But this was not rot. This was something *feeding*, and growing fat on it, and the people loved it.

"Sir?" His junior, a nervous boy called Emmerich, hovered at the tent flap. "The scouts found their trail. The waystation woman, the acolyte, the others. They're heading for the Grayfell passes. There's only one thing up there."

"The Garden," Vane said.

"You believe it's real?"

Vane looked at the dispatch again. *Subject untroubled.* He thought about the queen who had died ten years ago, the kingdom that had officially never existed afterward, the orders that came down unsigned telling him what could be spoken of and what must burn. He thought about how the Regency had never once, in all that time, explained why the gods' names were forbidden rather than merely discouraged.

"No," he said. "I believe it's a door. And I believe someone has wanted it shut for a very long time." He stood, buckled his sword. "Saddle the horses. We ride tonight."

* * *

It was Sefa who told her, on the third night in the high passes, huddled under an overhang while sleet came sideways.

"You should hear it from me before you hear it from a ghost." The old woman's voice was steady the way riverbeds are steady—with everything rushing over it. "I served in the capital temples, before. I was there when Queen Maren made her covenant."

Jude went cold. "Maren is a story."

"Maren is the reason you can sleep at night, or could." Sefa fed a twist of dry grass to their tiny fire. "The gods weren't worshipped, child. They were *fed*. That was the old arrangement—prayer in, mercy out, and the price hidden from the faithful because no kingdom survives its people knowing what mercy costs. And then Maren found the swordspeakers. People like you. People who could hear them."

Jude's mouth was dry. "What did she do?"

"She bound them. Through you—all of you, every throat that could speak sword-tongue. Your oath wasn't protection. It was a leash. Every swordspeaker who swore to her crown added a hand to the cage, and the gods starved quietly behind it for two hundred years, and everyone forgot, and everyone called it peace." Sefa looked at her across the fire, ancient and pitying. "Then the kingdom fell, the oaths broke with it, and the cage door swung open. You didn't fail your queen, Jude. Your queen failed you first. She built a lock out of living women and never told them they were locks."

The sleet ticked against stone. Down the slope, Wren—the inquisitor's daughter, who had not spoken in two days—said quietly, "So the healing in Lowmarsh. The mountain that buried the overseers."

"Bills coming due," Sefa said. "With interest."

Jude drew the sword then, first time in ten years, and unwrapped the blade, and the voice inside it was awake and patient and knew her name. It did not threaten. That was the worst part. It said, in the tone of an old creditor finding a debtor at last:

*You swore once. The oath is broken but the debt remains. Come to the Garden, little lock. Come and see what your silence purchased.*

* * *

They reached the threshold at dusk on the fifth day—a cleft in the Grayfell cliffs where the rock fell open like a book, and within it stood a door that was not wood or iron but something older, veined with light, humming the way a struck bell hums long after the sound should have ended.

The strays gathered around it and wept. Even Wren. Even Pip, who stole to survive and trusted nothing, pressed his palm flat against the stone and shook.

"It's real," he whispered. "It's actually real. We're going somewhere the Regency can't follow."

Jude stood apart, sword bare in her hand, and felt the trap close around her heart with a click so gentle she almost missed it.

Because the door was humming louder near her. Because the voice in the blade had led them here, every mile, through every pass, and she had thought she was guiding them. Because Sefa had said *come to the Garden* in the god's own cadence without knowing it, and the god had known exactly what bait would work: not gold, not power. A ragged family of strays, hungry for a door out.

*It wants us here,* she thought. *It doesn't want us gone. Doors open both ways.*

Hoofbeats broke the dusk.

They came down the pass in a wedge, torches blazing, maybe twenty riders, and at their head a grey-cloaked man with a tired face and a drawn blade—Halvard Vane, and Jude knew him by reputation alone, the inquisitor who burned villages looking for her.

"Halvard Vane!" Sefa shouted, stepping forward with astonishing fury for a woman of seventy. "You're late! The gods got here first!"

Vane reined in at the threshold's edge, and Jude watched his eyes go from the door, to the sword in her hand, to her face—and watched him understand, all at once, whatever his masters had spent ten years keeping from him. His blade wavered.

"Swordspeaker," he said hoarsely. "Tell me what's behind that door."

Behind her, the humming swelled to a chord. The door began, slowly, to open—and the light spilling through it was not the light of anywhere free.

Jude raised the sword, and every god in Thornfell leaned in to listen.
