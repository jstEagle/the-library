## 4. The Door That Opens Both Ways

The Garden was not a garden.

Jude had known that in her bones since the mountain first spoke, but knowing and seeing were different things, and what she saw now stole the breath from her lungs. The pass opened onto a hollow ringed with white stone, and in the center stood a door—free-standing, frameless, tall as a chapel spire—carved from a single slab of pale wood that had no business growing anywhere in the world. No wall behind it. No wall before it. Just the door, standing in the snow like a sentence waiting for its verb.

The strays went quiet behind her. Even the wind went quiet.

Wren pressed close to her side, the orphan's thin fingers finding the hem of Jude's coat the way they always did when the world got too large. "Is that it? Is that out?"

"It's a door," Jude said. "What it leads to, we're about to find out."

Sefa leaned on her walking stick and studied the carvings that crawled up the frame—kings and queens, swords and chains, a procession of figures all kneeling toward a throne that had been deliberately chiseled away. The old acolyte's lips moved, reading words in a liturgical script that had been outlawed before Wren was born.

"It's an oath-gate," Sefa said softly. "I've only read about them. The old rites. You don't open a door like this, swordspeaker. You *answer* it."

Jude's hand went to the hilt at her hip out of habit. The sword had been silent for three days—not the absence of voice, but a held breath, the way a room holds its breath before a confession.

She stepped forward.

The door woke.

Light moved beneath its grain like sap rising, and a voice came—not from the door, not from the mountain, not from any one god, but from all of them at once, braided into a single chord that vibrated in her teeth and her scars and the oath she had sworn on a dead woman's breast.

*The swordspeaker comes. The swordspeaker kneels. The swordspeaker renews what was sworn.*

The words landed on her like a yoke.

She knew this voice. She had known it since she was nine years old, kneeling in a burned chapel, swearing to Queen Maren's cooling body that she would carry the silence, keep the leash, hold the hunger down with her gift until her own death released it. She had thought the oath protected the world from the gods.

She had spent a decade learning it protected the gods from the world's anger. Or worse—that it protected the world from ever learning what it had worshipped.

*Renew the oath,* the chord said. *Kneel, and the way opens. Kneel, and you and yours may pass beyond. Kneel, and the hunger sleeps another hundred years.*

Behind her, the strays had begun to kneel. Wren was dropping first, eager, faithful. Sefa lowered herself with the practiced grace of a lifetime in temples. Even Tam the thief, who believed in nothing but his own clever hands, was bending his knees.

"Wait." Jude's voice cracked across the hollow. "Wait. Don't kneel."

They froze, looking up at her, and she saw it in their faces—Wren's hurt, Sefa's dawning dread, Tam's wariness. Behind them all, at the mouth of the pass, Halvard Vane sat his grey horse with his blade drawn and a dozen inquisitors fanned out behind him, close enough to hear every word.

Good, Jude thought. Let him hear it. Let the Regency hear it. Let the whole rotten kingdom hear it, if the wind would carry it that far.

She turned back to the door.

"You want an oath renewed," she said. "Here's what I swore instead."

She drew the sword—not to fight, but to hold it up, flat across her palms, the way she had once held it over a dead queen.

"Ten years ago I swore to keep your silence. I was a child, and a queen was dying, and she told me the gods had gone quiet because the swordspeakers held them down. She told me it was mercy. She told me the country needed its gods asleep." Jude's voice steadied as it went, the way a blade steadies as it leaves the sheath. "She lied. Or she believed the lie her own ancestors built. Either way, I carried it. I hid in the warrens of Thornfell for ten years because of it. I let people call me mute. I let a miner die in the dark because I was afraid to speak to the mountain for him—and then the mountain answered him anyway, and buried his overseers, and I learned the truth."

The chord beneath the light shuddered.

"The gods were never silent," Jude said. "They were *caged*. Not worshipped—imprisoned. The old kingdom didn't fall because it forgot the gods. It fell because it was built on a prison and called the prison a temple. Every prayer the priests ever answered with silence, every plague, every famine, every cruelty the crown called fate—that was the hunger, pressing against the bars, and the swordspeakers were the bars. I was the bars." She looked back at the strays, at Wren's wide eyes, at Sefa's tears. "And when the kingdom collapsed, they didn't tell you the truth. They told you the country no longer existed. Because a country that admits what it worshipped has to admit what it owes."

The door's light flared, furious, and the chord split into a hundred voices, all of them screaming at once, and snow shook from the ring of white stone.

*YOU SWORE.*

"I swore," Jude agreed. "And I'm breaking it. Not because I stopped believing in the gods. Because I finally believe in them—believe what they *are*. You want judgment? Fine. But judgment needs witnesses. And I'm done being a lock. I'm going to be a door."

She turned the sword in her hands and drove it point-first into the snow at the threshold.

The sound was not loud. It was worse than loud. It was *final*—a note like a struck bell that passed through the ground and the stone and the bones of everyone standing in the hollow. The door's light guttered, steadied, and then, slowly, the pale wood began to swing inward.

Vane spurred his horse forward. "Nakamura! Step away from it!"

She didn't. The door opened, and everyone looked to see the sea beyond it, or the free lands, or some green country where the Regency's writ ran thin and strays could be safe.

The door opened onto the capital.

Ruined. Blackened. The spire of the old cathedral snapped off like a broken finger. Streets choked with ash and the bones of the evacuation no one spoke of. The palace where Queen Maren had died was a shell, its windows like empty eye sockets, and above it all hung a sky the color of old bruises.

The faithful had walked a hundred miles to escape. The door had opened to show them what they were fleeing from—and what they had fled *to*, all this time, because the country had never stopped existing. It had just stopped being honest.

"No," Wren whispered. "No, that's not— that's not where we were going."

"That's where we've always been," Jude said quietly. "Every road in this kingdom leads through that. We just kept our eyes on the mountains."

Vane had dismounted. He came forward slowly, blade still drawn, and stopped an arm's length from her, close enough that she could see the war in his face—the inquisitor's training against the man's own memory of the burned villages, the plague town that had been healed and then asked for blood.

"You knew," he said. "All this time. The Regency, the crown, the church—we've been guarding a cage and calling it a country."

"Now you know too," Jude said. "So the question is what you do with a sword in your hand and the truth in your mouth."

For a long moment the only sound was the wind moving through the ruined capital beyond the door, carrying ash like grey snow.

Then Halvard Vane lowered his blade. He drove it into the earth beside hers, two swords standing sentinel at a threshold that led nowhere and everywhere.

"I was sent to burn you," he said. "I find I'd rather build something."

Sefa rose to her feet, weeping, and began reciting—not the liturgy of the cage, but something older, something from before the prison was built. A prayer of *tending*. Wren took Jude's hand. Tam, who believed in nothing, picked up a fallen beam from the white stone ring and set it upright, the first act of masonry in a kingdom that officially did not exist.

Jude looked at her found family—her strays, her believers, her inquisitor—and felt the oath's weight finally leave her shoulders, not broken but *answered*, transformed into something she had chosen freely.

"The gods will want a reckoning," Sefa said. "Judgment doesn't end, swordspeaker. It begins."

"Then we'd better be worth judging," Jude said.

She stepped through the door first, into the ash and the ruin and the beginning of everything, and the strays followed her home.

* * *

The first thing they rebuilt was not a wall or a chapel or a road.

It was the truth—carved in stone, in the square before the broken palace, in letters deep enough to outlast the next kingdom and the one after that:

*HERE THE GODS WERE CAGED. HERE THE CAGE WAS CALLED HOLY. HERE WE CHOSE OTHERWISE.*

Jude stood before it with her hand on Wren's shoulder as the first honest dawn in a hundred years broke over the capital—and far away, in the mountains, in the deep places, in the roots of the world, the gods began to speak.

Not in judgment.

Not yet.

In *question*.
