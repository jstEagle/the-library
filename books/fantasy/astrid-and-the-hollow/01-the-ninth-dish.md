## 1. The Ninth Dish

The ninth dish arrived on a plate of pale green glass, and Kestrel Eastwick tasted it the way she tasted everything: slowly, completely, and with a face that gave away nothing at all.

It was a sorbet of frozen quince and honeyed ice, meant to cleanse the palate between the fish course and the meat. It should have been sharp enough to make her eyes water—quince in Vell was grown in the acid soil of the eastern terraces, and the palace cook prided himself on a sourness that could strip paint from a door. Ten years ago, that sourness would have bloomed across her tongue like a struck bell. Tonight she might as well have been eating snow.

She held the spoonful in her mouth for the full count. One. Two. Three. Four. Five. She had learned early that a taster who swallowed too quickly looked frightened, and a frightened taster made a frightened court, and a frightened court made for short careers and shorter lives. So she counted, and she waited, and she felt nothing but the cold of the ice melting against her teeth—a sensation that lived, she realized with a small, quiet horror, entirely in her jaw. Not her tongue. Her tongue had gone somewhere else tonight. Or perhaps it had been going somewhere else for months, and this was simply the first evening she had allowed herself to notice.

Kestrel swallowed. Set down the spoon. Nodded once to the steward, a thin man named Pell whose hands always trembled slightly no matter how many decades he'd served the crown.

"The ninth dish is sound," she said, and her voice came out level, warm even, pitched exactly as Master Corvane had trained her to pitch it: the voice of a woman utterly without fear, so that everyone else at the table could afford to be afraid of smaller things.

Pell bowed. The servers moved. Down the length of the Amber Hall, forty guests picked up their spoons, and the dinner went on breathing.

* * *

The Amber Hall had been built by a king who believed that light was a form of wealth, and so the walls were paneled in honey-colored stone that caught the two hundred candles and threw them back doubled, tripled, until the room seemed less like a room than like a lamp seen from inside. The ceiling was painted with constellations that did not exist—Vell's astronomers had complained about it for three generations and been ignored for three generations—and beneath those invented stars sat the court of Queen Maren the Second, nineteen years old, eleven months crowned, laughing at something the Ambassador from Serevan had just said.

Kestrel stood at her station along the eastern wall, hands folded, spine straight, wearing the grey-and-white livery of the Tasters' Guild. It was a uniform designed to be unseen. Grey like a wall. White like a napkin. Corvane said once, in one of his dry classroom voices, *A taster should be furniture that can die for you.* He had meant it as instruction. Kestrel had understood it, even at fourteen, as prophecy.

There were four tasters stationed in the hall tonight. Two for the queen's table, one for the foreign delegation, and Kestrel, senior of them all at twenty-six, who tasted first and tasted alone. That was the honor they called it. In practice it meant that when there was poison—and there was poison, somewhere, most seasons; Vell's politics were conducted in kitchens as often as in council chambers—it reached her body before it reached anyone else's. Her body was the city's thinnest wall, and everyone knew it, and everyone slept better for it, and no one ever thanked her, because thanking a wall would be strange.

She watched the ninth dish travel down the table. Quince sorbet, spoonful by spoonful, into the mouths of duchesses and generals and the Serevani envoy with his rings. None of them died. None of them even frowned. And none of them, Kestrel thought, tasting the thought like a second course, none of them would ever know what it cost to guarantee that.

Her tongue ached. That was new—or not new, but newly honest. A deep ache, structural, like the ache of a tooth long before it announces itself properly. She pressed her tongue against the roof of her mouth and got a faint pressure, a ghost of sensation, the way you can feel a limb through layers of wool. Somewhere beneath the ache, she suspected, the quince was still screaming its sourness. She simply couldn't hear it anymore.

*Ten years,* she thought. *Ten years of controlled poisoning.*

That was the phrase Corvane used, and it was a good phrase, an honest phrase, in the way that a scalpel is honest. Every taster began at eight or nine years old with microdoses—bitterleaf, ground glass, the slow metals, a dozen compounds whose names were taught only inside the Guildhall. Doses so small the body learned to metabolize them the way a river learns to carry silt. Then larger doses. Then combinations. Then the live exercises, the ones Kestrel did not let herself remember often: the flagged cups, the deliberate mistakes, the year she spent her twelfth winter half-blind because some compound had decided to take her sight as tuition. Her body had paid for its immunity the way all bodies pay for anything. Continually. In installments.

And now, apparently, the installments were coming due in a currency she hadn't budgeted for.

*It's fatigue,* she told herself, the way she'd been telling herself for three weeks. *You've been tasting double shifts since the coronation anniversary preparations began. Numbness is exhaustion. Exhaustion passes.*

But exhaustion didn't pass. That was the thing. Three weeks of passing, and each morning the world arrived at her tongue a little more muffled than the last—the bread blander, the water flatter, the salt nearly silent. This morning she had bitten into a plum over her washbasin, alone, unobserved, and tasted nothing at all except wet. She had stood there with the juice running down her wrist and felt something open under her ribs that she refused, in daylight, to name.

Because here was the arithmetic no one in the Guildhall ever spoke aloud: a taster who could not taste was not a taster. She was a ritual. A superstition with a salary. If Corvane learned his finest instrument had gone deaf, he would not weep for her—he would reassign her, or retire her, and *retired* tasters, in Kestrel's private taxonomy of fears, occupied a category somewhere between *disgraced* and *missing*. And worse than losing the work—worse, far worse—was what the work protected her from having to be. Without the Guild, she was only Kestrel Eastwick, orphan of nowhere, charm-seller of the night market, a woman built out of small illegalities and held together by routine. The numbness wasn't just taking her tongue. It was taking the shape of her life.

So she stood at her station, grey and white and invisible, and she smiled at the right moments, and she tasted nothing, and no one knew.

Almost no one.

* * *

He was at the third table, among the minor officials and visiting scholars, and he had been looking at her for the better part of an hour.

Kestrel noticed people noticing her the way sailors notice weather—not as an event but as a condition to be read and prepared for. Most looks slid off her. That was a skill too, though no one had ever put it in a training manual: the ability to be forgettable, to arrange your face into something a memory declines to keep. She had survived ten years in Vell partly on immunity and partly on being no one twice.

This man's look did not slide off. It stayed. It had the quality of attention rather than glance, and attention, in the Amber Hall, was either dangerous or expensive.

He was perhaps thirty, dark-haired, with the ink-stained fingers of someone who worked with pen and paper professionally and did not care who saw it. His court clothes were good but worn at the cuffs, the clothes of a man who owned one set and wore it to everything. While the rest of the table bent over their sorbet and their gossip, he had a leather-bound book open beside his plate, and he was drawing.

Drawing, Kestrel realized with a slow, cool drop of dread settling through her chest, *her*.

Not obviously. He was too skilled for obvious. His pencil moved in small economical strokes while his gaze wandered the hall—up to the false constellations, across to the queen, down to his plate, and then, inevitably, back to the eastern wall, to the grey-and-white figure standing between a candelabrum and a pillar, hands folded, face arranged into pleasant furniture. Each time his eyes returned, the pencil moved again. Each time, Kestrel felt it land somewhere between her shoulder blades like a fingertip.

She catalogued him the way she'd been trained to catalogue everything: not a Guild man—Guild men wore their rings and never drew. Not a Warden—Wardens didn't sit at dinners, they stood at doors and radiated disapproval. Not a courtier—courtiers drew themselves, flattering mirrors of oil paint. A scholar, then, or something adjacent. Someone whose business was looking.

The servers brought the tenth dish. Roasted capon in a glaze of dark cherries, and the smell of it rolled down the hall rich enough to lean on. Kestrel performed her count over the sample portion—five seconds, ten, fifteen—and tasted, distinctly and unmistakably, nothing. Not blandness. Nothing. As if the food were a rumor about food, reported secondhand. She nodded to Pell. The hall ate.

And when she lifted her head from the nod, the man at the third table was watching her with an expression she recognized and could not immediately place, because it belonged to no context she had ever been given. It took her several heartbeats to name it, and when she did, the naming frightened her more than any poison ever had.

He was looking at her the way you look at something you have lost.

Not curiosity. Not appetite. Not the flat assessment of a superior examining an instrument. Recognition—grief-tinged, startled, the look of a man meeting again a face he had mourned. Except that he could not have known her. She would have remembered knowing him; she remembered everyone, it was part of the trade. And yet the look persisted, course after course, patient and helpless, until Kestrel found herself doing something she had not done in years.

She looked back.

Held it, deliberately, one full breath—an act of professional suicide, a taster does not engage, a taster is furniture—and watched his pencil stop mid-stroke. Watched color come up his throat. Watched him understand that he had been caught, and not look away anyway.

Then the queen laughed—bright, ringing, a little too loud, the laugh of a girl performing joy at her own celebration—and the whole hall turned toward the high table, and the moment broke like a dropped glass.

* * *

Later, much later, when the plates had been cleared and the guests were dissolving into clusters of wine-warmed conspiracy, Kestrel passed the third table on her way to the service corridor. It was not necessary. There was no dish left to taste. But her feet carried her there anyway, past the candlelight, close enough to see the sketchbook lying open where he had abandoned it.

She had intended only a glance. A confirmation. *He draws architecture,* she would tell herself. *Columns, ceiling, the play of light. I happened to be standing in his picture.*

But the page under his hand was not architecture.

It was a face. Her face—but rendered with a fidelity that made her stomach turn over, because it was not her face as the hall saw it. It was her face as she saw it, alone, at night, above her washbasin: the particular wariness around the eyes, the mouth held a half-degree off center, the small asymmetry at the brow that she had never once seen in a mirror and had certainly never shown anyone. He had drawn the version of her that existed only when no one was watching. He had drawn it from memory. And below it, faintly, half-erased, there was another version of the same face—older work, the paper worn as if the page had been drawn on, smudged, drawn again. And below that, another. The same woman sketched over and over down the margin of the page like a prayer repeated until the words wear out.

At the bottom of the sheet, in a small cramped hand, was written a single line:

*She tastes the ninth dish and does not know why she is afraid.*

Kestrel's skin went cold from the crown of her head downward, in a slow wave, like water poured over her. Because she *was* afraid, and she had told no one, and she had barely admitted it to herself, and here it was in a stranger's handwriting, dated—she checked, and yes, dated—today.

"Master Voss."

One of the chamber stewards had come up behind her, cheerful and oblivious, clapping the man on the shoulder. "Still haunting my hall? The queen's retired, the ambassador's retired, go home and sleep."

"In a moment." The man rose, gathering his book, and up close he was taller than he'd seemed and tired around the eyes in a way that looked chronic rather than incidental. He tucked the sketchbook under his arm—closed, thank every careful god—and inclined his head to her with the precise courtesy due to palace staff and no more. "Good evening," he said.

"You're the cartographer," Kestrel heard herself say. "Ilan Voss. You're redrawing the harbor charts."

Something flickered across his face—surprise, and under the surprise, that same stricken recognition, closer now, unguarded. "You know my work?"

"I know everyone's work," Kestrel said. "It's my job to know what goes into the queen's mouth. Everything else is habit."

"I see." He hesitated. The candles guttered in a draft from the corridor, and for one instant his face was all shadow and one lit eye, and Kestrel thought, absurdly, *he looks like a man standing at a door he doesn't remember knocking on.* "May I ask your name?"

"No," Kestrel said pleasantly.

His mouth twitched—almost a smile, almost pain. "No," he agreed softly. "I didn't think so. But I'll—" He stopped himself, visibly, the way a man stops himself from stepping onto ice. Whatever he'd been about to say, he folded it away and bowed instead, shallow and correct, and walked off down the corridor with his sketchbook under his arm, his footsteps fading into the great sleeping hush of the palace.

Kestrel stood alone in the amber light with two hundred candles burning and her tongue silent as a stone.

*Nine courses,* she thought. *I tasted nine dishes tonight, and the only thing in this whole hall with any flavor to it was fear.*

She pressed her tongue hard against her teeth, testing, willing even one thread of sensation to rise—and felt, beneath the numbness, deep and wrong and faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, something answer. Not taste. Not pain. Something older, colder, rising from underneath her own body like water rising in a well, and for half a second she could have sworn she heard it speak.

It sounded almost like her mother's voice, saying her name.

Then it was gone, and the hall was only a hall, and Kestrel Eastwick straightened her grey shoulders, fixed her white collar, and went to find out everything there was to know about the cartographer Ilan Voss.
