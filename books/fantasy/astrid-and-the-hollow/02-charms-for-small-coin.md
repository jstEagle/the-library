## 2. Charms for Small Coin

The night market in Vell began the way all forbidden things begin: quietly, and with someone pretending to be ordinary.

Kestrel arrived at her stall an hour after dusk, when the lantern-light along Tanner's Row had gone soft and amber and the day's honest commerce had given way to the other kind. She carried her wares in a flour sack, because a woman carrying flour attracted no attention, and a woman carrying luck knots did. The sack weighed almost nothing. That was the trick of it—magic, real magic, was light. It was intention folded into thread, breath sealed into wax, a wish pressed flat and small enough to hide in a pocket. The Wardens were always looking for something heavy. Something that smoked or sparked or sang. They never thought to weigh a sack of flour.

She set out her goods beneath the awning of dyed sailcloth: braided cords of red and grey wool, knotted seven times each; tallow candles poured around iron filings; little paper stars that smelled faintly of rosemary; fever wards stitched onto scraps of linen, the embroidery so crude it looked like a child's work. That was deliberate too. Nothing about her stall could look like craft. It had to look like superstition—the kind of harmless nonsense poor people bought because hope was cheaper than medicine.

"Evening, Taster," said Old Sefa from the pickle barrel next door, not looking up from her knife.

"Not a taster tonight."

"You're always a taster. It's in how you hold your hands." Sefa sliced a cucumber into translucent moons. "Like you're waiting for something to disagree with you."

Kestrel laughed and arranged her knots in rows of three. Ten years in the palace, tasting for poison nine courses at a time, and she'd thought she'd left the work behind when she descended the palace steps each evening. But the body kept its habits. Her tongue still woke before the rest of her, reading the air the way other people read print. She could taste the market before she reached it: lamp oil and brine, wet wool, the copper tang of the canal, and underneath it all—faint as a rumor—the dry mineral ghost of working charms. Every charm-seller on the Row leaked a little. Intention was a flavor. She'd never told anyone that.

It was one of the many things about herself she kept in the locked room behind her teeth.

* * *

Business was good, which meant business was dangerous.

A fishwife bought two luck knots for her husband's boat, haggling Kestrel down from four coppers to three and then pressing five into her palm anyway, because the fishwife's mother had once been saved by a knot and superstition ran deeper than arithmetic. A plasterer's apprentice took a fever ward for his sister, his hands shaking—not from cold, Kestrel noted, but from fear. The Wardens had swept Weavers' Lane two nights ago. Someone had been taken. No one said who. Everyone knew.

"For a headache," the boy said, too loudly, performing innocence for anyone listening.

"For a headache," Kestrel agreed, just as loudly, and wrapped the ward in brown paper as though it were a sweetmeat.

That was the choreography of the whole market: everyone speaking in double meanings, everyone innocent, everyone guilty. The baker selling bread that never molded. The lamplighter whose wicks burned a shade too blue. The midwife whose teas worked better than teas should. Magic had not left Vell when the Edict came down a century ago. It had simply learned to whisper. Kestrel sometimes thought the city itself was complicit—that stone and water and old timber remembered what it was to be worked upon, and leaned toward anyone who offered.

She sold a candle to a widow. She sold a knot to a soldier who wouldn't say what he needed luck for, and she didn't ask, because asking was how charm-sellers ended up in Warden ledgers. She sold nothing at all to a young man in a grey coat who stood at the edge of her stall for a long moment, looked at her face with an expression she couldn't read, and walked away without a word. Strange, but the market was full of strange. She forgot him within the minute, which was its own small skill: being forgettable was the first charm she'd ever learned, and no one had taught it to her. She'd simply understood, at eight years old, arriving at the palace gates with a dead mother and a name nobody wanted, that survival belonged to people who could not be described accurately afterward.

Small. Brown-haired. Brown-eyed. A food-taster. No one remembers twice.

She was tying off a fresh knot—wool dyed madder-red, the seventh loop pulled tight with a held breath—when the customer came who would ruin the shape of her life.

He didn't look like trouble. Trouble in Vell rarely did; it wore respectable boots and a tired face. He was perhaps fifty, broad through the shoulders in the way of men who'd done labor and then stopped, dressed in a dockworker's coat gone soft with salt. His eyes moved over her wares without hunger, which marked him immediately. Everyone else who stopped at her stall wanted something desperately. This man browsed the way a collector browses.

"The fever wards," he said. His voice was low, unhurried, with the rounded vowels of the river districts. "How much?"

"Two coppers. Three if you want it spoken over."

"What does 'spoken over' do?"

"It works." She smiled the smile she used on customers—warm, slightly foolish, a woman who believed her own nonsense. "The stitching is only the shape of it. The words are the substance."

"And the Wardens?" He glanced down the Row, casual, but she caught the precision of it. Counting exits. "They don't trouble you?"

"They think I sell flour." She held up the sack, still half-full, and let it swing. "Superstition, sir. Luck knots. Old wives' business. If they ever looked twice, I'd be selling actual flour by morning."

Something flickered across his face—amusement, maybe, or its colder cousin. He bought two fever wards, unspoken-over, and paid with coins from a drawstring purse that clinked heavier than a dockworker's purse had any right to.

One of the coins was wrong.

She felt it before she saw it—a wrongness on her tongue, though she hadn't touched it to her lips. Some coins carried flavor: the mint-fresh bite of new silver, the sour sweat of coins passed through a thousand anxious hands. This one tasted old. Not merely worn but *old*, old the way the palace cellars were old, old the way the foundation stones were old—and under the age, a flavor she had no name for except *deep*. Like licking the air above a well.

She turned it in her palm by lantern-light.

It was larger than a common penny, darker, bronze gone nearly black with handling, and stamped with a sigil she knew the way you know a scar on your own body: a single unbroken line spiraling inward, coil upon coil, tightening toward a center that was not quite a point. She had seen it exactly once before in her life, ten years ago, on her first day in the palace, when an old steward had shown her the cellars where the tasters' quarters lay and pointed out, with the bored pride of a man reciting inherited facts, the foundation stone at the threshold of the oldest wing. *Laid before the Edict,* he'd said. *Before the city walls, some say.* And cut into its face, worn shallow by centuries: the spiral.

She had asked what it meant. The steward had looked at her strangely—not annoyed, not dismissive, but *wary*, in a way that had seemed disproportionate then and seemed very proportionate now—and had said, "Nothing. Masons' marks. Old builders liked to leave their names."

Masons' marks. On a coin. In a dockworker's purse, in a night market, paid to a charm-seller for fever wards.

She looked up. The man was already turning away, folding his purchase into his coat.

"Sir." Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, later. "This coin—it's not current. I can't take it. The money-changers will think I stole it."

He paused. Did not turn all the way. Half his face in lantern-light, half in dark, and she thought again of collectors, of men who acquired things, and wondered briefly, absurdly, whether she was the thing being acquired.

"Keep it," he said. "It's worth more than it looks. Everything old in this city is." And then, before she could frame another question, in a tone so mild it barely qualified as speech: "Mind the third bell, mistress. The Wardens walk Tanner's Row early tonight. There's a sweep planned. You'll want to be flour again."

Then he was gone, swallowed by the market crowd, and Kestrel stood with a black coin in her hand and her heart going hard against her ribs like a fist on a door.

* * *

She closed early.

Not immediately—immediately would have been suspicious, and suspicion was a scent the market shared faster than any gossip—but soon enough. She sold two more knots to a girl buying luck for a courtship, wrapped them, smiled, sent her away. Then she packed her wares into the flour sack, doused her lantern, and let Old Sefa see her stretch and yawn like a woman whose feet hurt and whose bed was calling.

"Early night," Sefa observed.

"My tongue's tired. Too much talking."

Sefa snorted. Kestrel kissed her own fingers at the old woman—affection, genuine, one of the few uncomplicated things she owned—and melted into the crowd.

The coin rode in her left palm, closed fist, like a coal.

She took the long way home, which was to say the careful way: through the rag-and-bone alley where the stalls sold broken things, across the footbridge where the canal stink covered every other smell including, if you were lucky, the iron-and-ash signature of Warden cloaks. The third bell hadn't rung yet. She had time. She told herself she had time, and heard in her own voice the particular timbre of a lie.

Because here was the truth she was walking around, the way your tongue worries a cracked tooth: the man had known. Not suspected—known. Known she was a charm-seller, known the Wardens' schedule, known precisely how to warn her without warning her. And he had paid her with a coin cut from the same dark century as the palace's bones, stamped with a spiral that no mason had any earthly reason to carry in his purse.

Vell had outlawed magic a hundred years ago. The Edict was carved over the gates in letters deep enough to hold rainwater: *That which is not granted is theft; that which is hidden is treason.* Every child learned it before they learned their letters. The Wardens burned charm-sellers in the winter months, when crowds gathered easily and the smoke rose straight. And yet the palace—the great grey body of it, the seat of the ban itself—was founded on a stone bearing a sigil Kestrel had now seen twice in one lifetime, both times in places where magic was supposed to have died first.

Unless it hadn't died there at all. Unless it had gone somewhere. Down, maybe. Deep. Coiled.

She stopped on the footbridge, mid-span, and made herself breathe.

Don't spin stories, she told herself. Stories were how charm-sellers died—chasing meaning into corners, following threads that turned out to be Warden bait. Take the coin to a money-changer tomorrow, learn what it was, and forget the rest. She was small. She was forgettable. She had built a life inside the narrow space between what the palace saw and what the market saw, and that life depended on her never, ever pulling threads.

But her tongue, that traitor, that instrument, kept tasting the coin through the skin of her fist. Deep. Old. Spiraling inward toward a center that was not quite a point.

And beneath the taste, fainter than everything, there was something else now—something she might have imagined, except that her tongue did not imagine. A warmth. A patience. A sense of vast attention turned toward her from somewhere far below the canal, below the streets, below the roots of the city, like a sleeper who has felt a floorboard creak overhead and has begun, slowly, without hurry, to open one eye.

The third bell rang.

Kestrel went home the rest of the way quickly, and did not sleep, and in the grey hour before dawn, sitting at her table with the black coin before her and a single candle burning, she made the mistake that undid her careful, narrow, ten-year life:

She picked it up, and whispered the only word of power her mother had ever taught her—the small word, the listening word, the one that asked an object to say what it was—

—and the coin answered.

Not in words. In direction. The spiral sigil warmed under her thumb, and every thread of her trained perception pulled the same way at once, south and down, south and down, toward the river, toward the rock beneath the river, toward the palace—toward the oldest wing, the tasters' quarters, the foundation stone at the threshold where a bored steward had once told her that masons liked to leave their names.

The candle guttered. Somewhere far beneath her chair, beneath the floor, beneath the city's whole layered history of cellars and crypts and older things, something exhaled.

Kestrel set the coin down very gently, the way you set down a sleeping animal you no longer trust, and sat in the dark with her heart hammering, understanding that she had just asked a question she could not unask—and that tomorrow, whatever happened, she would have to walk back into the palace, past that foundation stone, and taste the air of the only place in Vell where the spiral lived.

She did not yet know that someone was already drawing her face from memory, over and over, in a room full of maps.
