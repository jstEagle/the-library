## 4. The Book Under the Floor

The taster's quarters were cleaned on the first day of every month, and had been for a hundred years before Kestrel Eastwick was born. She knew this because the duty roster nailed beside the door was written in the same careful hand as the one that had hung there when she arrived a decade ago—Master Corvane's hand, or the hand of whoever copied Corvane's lists, unchanged in its phrasing down to the small pedantry of *sweep beneath the cot; do not neglect the corners*. The corners. As if corners were where danger lived.

She had never once neglected them. But she had also never once found anything under the floorboards, until the night after the state dinner with its nine courses, when her tongue still felt like a stranger's and she could not sleep.

* * *

It began with a loose nail.

She noticed it because everything about her body had become a thing to be noticed lately—the numbness spreading across her tongue like frost across a windowpane, the way flavors arrived late and left early, the faint metallic ghost that followed every meal now no matter what she ate. Ten years of controlled poisoning, Corvane called it. *Conditioning*, he called it. She had stopped calling it anything at all, the way you stop naming a limp.

So when her bare heel caught the edge of a floorboard near the hearth—a board that had sat flush for ten years—and the nail head rose a hair's width with a sound like a held breath being released, she knelt down and paid attention.

The board was not warped. It was not swollen with damp. It had been pried up and set back down, carefully, many times, by someone who wanted it to look untouched. The wood around the nail was polished smooth in a crescent, worn by a thumb or a knife handle, over years, over decades.

Kestrel sat back on her heels in the cold light of the banked fire and considered the wisdom of leaving it alone.

She was, by trade and temperament, a woman who left things alone. She had survived Vell by being small, by being forgettable, by tasting what kings and queens were too important to risk and saying nothing about any of it. Curiosity was a luxury for people whose faces weren't already in the Wardens' books. For ten years she had walked past locked doors the way water walks past stone—not out of reverence, but out of a survival instinct so deep it had become indistinguishable from personality.

But the numbness on her tongue had been spreading for three months. And last night, across the banquet hall, a cartographer with ink-stained fingers had been sketching her face into a leather book, and when their eyes met he had looked at her the way people look at a place they've been in a dream—recognition arriving before memory, grief arriving before either. And she had felt, absurdly, horribly, that she recognized him too.

Something was changing in her body. Something was changing in the city. The loose board was either an answer or a trap, and Kestrel had learned long ago that in Vell those were frequently the same thing.

She fetched the thin knife she used for trimming lamp wicks, worked it under the board's edge, and lifted.

* * *

The cavity was shallow—no deeper than her forearm—and lined with oiled cloth gone stiff with age. Inside lay three things.

A bundle of letters tied with black ribbon, so brittle she did not dare untie them. A small tin box that rattled when she moved it, holding seven dried flowers she did not recognize, each pressed flat as a coin, each stained dark at the center as if they had been burned from within. And a book.

Not an old book, exactly. Old the way the palace was old—preserved rather than aged, kept deliberately, fed on care. Its cover was grey leather gone soft as skin, unmarked by title or sigil, and when she opened it the spine made a sound like a sigh. The pages were heavy rag paper, yellowed at the edges but clean, and the handwriting inside was fine and slanted and utterly confident, the hand of someone who wrote to be read by strangers a long way off.

The first page bore only a date.

Kestrel knew dates. Part of a taster's discipline was knowing them—the calendar of feasts, fast days, coronations, funerals, the nights when poison was most likely to move through a palace like weather through a valley. She read the date twice, then a third time, her lips moving without sound.

It was tonight's date.

Not the month. Not the season. The exact day, written down a century ago by a dead hand, waiting under her floor for her heel to find it.

She told herself it was coincidence. There were only so many days in a year; a book full of dates would inevitably strike one. She turned the page to prove herself right.

*The Hollow Man walks the upper gallery,* the dead hand had written, in that same fine slant. *He carries charcoal and paper. He draws what he has dreamed and does not know why. His face is long, his eyes are dark, his hands are always cold at the fingertips though the rest of him is warm.*

Kestrel's mouth went dry.

She had watched Ilan Voss all through the nine courses last night—or rather, she had watched him watching her, in the sideways manner of two people pretending not to look. He carried charcoal and paper. He had been sketching her from across the hall with quick, sure strokes, and when a serving girl had brushed his hand he'd flinched as if scalded, and tucked both hands briefly into his armpits, warming them.

His hands were always cold at the fingertips. She knew this because she had held one of them, two nights ago, walking back from the night market along the river wall, and had teased him about it, and he had said, *Always been that way. Only my fingers. I don't know why.* And she had thought: a draughtsman's complaint, ink and winter, nothing.

*He does not know why.* The sentence repeated itself in her skull like a bell struck once and refusing to fade.

She turned more pages. The book was not a diary, or not only a diary. It was a ledger of patterns—the same spiral sigil she'd seen stamped on the old coin at the market, drawn again and again in margins and diagrams, traced across floor plans of the palace she knew intimately and versions of it older than any she'd seen. Between the drawings ran passages of dense, deliberate prose, part prophecy and part instruction, as though the writer could not decide whether she was recording the future or arranging it.

And threaded through all of it, recurring like a refrain, was a phrase that stopped Kestrel's heart each time it surfaced:

*the Taster of Deaths.*

*The Taster of Deaths will sit at the ninth dish and feel nothing.* She read it and her own tongue throbbed, numb and foreign behind her teeth. *The Taster of Deaths was made, not born; chosen before choosing; sweetened with stolen deaths until death knows her flavor and passes her by.* Another page: *When the door grows warm, the Taster of Deaths will know her purpose, and grieve it, and do it anyway.*

Kestrel closed the book. Her hands were shaking. She opened it again, because closing it changed nothing, and because the alternative was to lie awake until dawn listening to her own heartbeat and wondering which parts of her life had ever belonged to her.

She read until the fire burned down to coals and the coals went grey.

* * *

There was a page near the end that described the palace kitchens on a specific morning—the fish delivery delayed by ice, the under-cook burning the first porridge, a girl of six standing in the doorway with a number chalked on a wooden tag around her neck. *The child will not remember her mother's face,* the entry said. *This is mercy. Memory would have made her useless.*

Kestrel sat very still.

She did not remember her mother's face. She remembered a kitchen, or a dream of a kitchen—steam, salt, a voice singing low—and she remembered the tag, the chalk number, the long corridor, the gate. She had always assumed the memory was real and the rest had fallen away the way childhood falls away, naturally, the way snow melts. She had never let herself wonder whether it had fallen away or been taken.

*Chosen before choosing.* She looked at her own hands in the dying light—small hands, steady hands, hands trained since girlhood to lift a spoon without tremor—and felt the vertigo open beneath her like a trapdoor. Every skill she owned, every instinct she trusted, every quiet pride she had built her small survivable life upon: the book described them all in advance, itemized them, claimed them. Her composure at the ninth dish. Her numbness. Even, she suspected with a horror that arrived slowly and then all at once, her taste for living unnoticed—the very smallness she had believed was her own invention, her own cleverness, her own self.

Scripted. All of it. Before she was born.

She laughed—one short, ugly sound in the empty room—and pressed her fist against her mouth. Outside, Vell lay silent under a thin fall of sleet, ten thousand chimneys breathing smoke, the Wardens' lanterns making their slow rounds along the walls. Somewhere across the city Ilan Voss was asleep, or not sleeping, dreaming whatever dreams put her face in his sketchbook. Neither of them had chosen any of it. They were characters in a book written while their grandmothers' grandmothers were children, and the book had been hidden under the floor of the room where the script required her to live.

*Who hid it?* The question surfaced through the panic and steadied her, the way practical questions always did. Not Corvane—she was almost certain. The roster's pedantic hand was nothing like this fine slant, and Corvane hoarded knowledge the way misers hoarded coin; he would sooner burn a prophecy than leave it where a taster might kneel on it. Someone else had kept this cache. Someone who had pried up this same board, over years, over decades—wearing that little crescent into the wood—and put the book back, and waited.

Waited for whom? For the girl with the chalk number? Or simply for whoever came next, generation after generation, keeping the secret alive like a coal passed hand to hand?

The letters in their black ribbon might say. The flowers in the tin might mean something. But her eyes kept returning to the book, to the final leaves, because a reader's instinct is older than fear.

The last written page ended mid-sentence.

*On the night the door opens, the Taster must choose between the crown that raised her and the truth that—*

And then nothing. The next leaf was gone. Not torn roughly—cut, cleanly, close to the binding, by someone with a sharp blade and a steady hand. The stub of it remained, a pale seam like a scar. Whoever removed the ending had done it carefully, deliberately, and had left the rest of the book behind to be found.

Kestrel turned the stubbed page over. Turned it back. Read the broken sentence four times.

*...between the crown that raised her and the truth that—*

That what? That her immunity was built from stolen deaths? That the ban on magic was a fence around a feeding pen? That the queen she served, the young lonely queen who trusted her completely, who that very week had asked her to taste an unmarked gift from a foreign envoy—was already being drained toward something warm and hungry beneath the foundations? She had pieces. She had suspicions that had been gathering for months like storm-light on the horizon. But the book's author had known the ending, had written the ending, and someone had cut it out and walked away with it.

Someone in this palace, or someone who had once been in this palace, was holding the last page of Kestrel's life in their hands.

She sat on the cold floor until her knees ached, the book open in her lap, the sleet ticking softly against the shutters. Then she did what she had trained herself to do for ten years: she put everything away exactly as she had found it. The letters, ribboned. The tin, rattling. The book, wrapped in its oiled cloth. The board pressed down until the nail seated with its small released breath. She swept the dust back into the corners, because the roster said to, because ritual is what remains when certainty is taken from you.

Then she stood at her window in the dark, looking down at the sleeping city, and made herself a promise in the old grammar of her trade: *I will find the page. I will find who took it. And I will make them tell me how my story ends—*

A knock at the door, soft and precise, stopped her heart.

Through the frosted pane, far below in the courtyard, she saw a lantern moving toward the palace's east wing—toward the archives, toward Master Corvane's rooms—and beside it, tall and hunched against the sleet, a figure carrying a leather case that might have held maps, or might have held something else entirely.

The knock came again. Three times. Paused. Twice more.

It was the rhythm she and Ilan had agreed on, that first night at the market, for knocking on each other's doors: *here is a man drawn twice, here is a woman who cannot sleep.* But it was nearly three in the morning, and no lover knocks at three unless something has frightened him—and as she crossed the room to answer, Kestrel understood with terrible clarity that whatever Ilan Voss had dreamed tonight, it had already begun coming true.
