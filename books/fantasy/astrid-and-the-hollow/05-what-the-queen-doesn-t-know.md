## 5. What the Queen Doesn't Know

The queen of Vell learned her first spell at six years old, kneeling on a cold hearthstone while her mother cupped a candle flame between their two pairs of hands.

*Feel it wanting,* her mother had said. *Fire is the greediest thing in the world. It wants to eat everything it touches. Magic is only telling it: not yet.*

Maren had felt it—the flame's small furious appetite pressing against her palms like a bird beating its wings inside a cage—and she had told it *not yet*, and the flame had gone still, and her mother had laughed and kissed the crown of her head and said, *There. You are a witch now, my love, and you must never tell anyone.*

That was eleven years ago. Her mother had been dead for four.

Now Maren knelt alone on the same hearthstone, in the same private chamber at the top of the Ivory Stair, with a candle burning on the stone before her and no one in the world who knew what she was about to do.

She checked the door twice. She always checked the door twice. The bolt was drawn, the outer guard dismissed for the evening hour, the servants sent away with the excuse of a headache that was not entirely a lie—her head did ache, lately, in a low persistent way, like a second heartbeat lodged behind her eyes. She had decided it was grief. Grief was an acceptable thing for a nineteen-year-old queen to suffer. It explained the tiredness, the wandering attention in council, the strange dreams of falling upward through dark water. No one questioned grief. Everyone offered it to her gently, like a cushion, and went away satisfied.

No one would have accepted *magic* as an explanation. Magic had been outlawed in Vell for a hundred years. The Wardens burned charm-sellers in the Ash Market every season, and the Tasters' Guild tested every dish that passed her lips, and the whole architecture of the city—the gray stone of it, the silence of it, the way people lowered their voices when they spoke of the old times—was built on the promise that magic was finished, buried, gone.

And yet her mother had taught her anyway, in this room, with the door bolted, whispering cantrips like lullabies.

*Not because the law is wrong,* her mother had said once, when Maren asked why they must hide. *Because the law is afraid. And frightened things with power do terrible things, my love. So we stay small. We stay secret. A candle here. A flower coaxed open there. Nothing the world would miss.*

Maren looked at the candle now. It was a fat tallow thing, unlovely, bought from a chandler near the kitchens so that no one would wonder where the queen's candles came from. She had learned to be careful even in small things. Especially in small things.

She spread her hands over the flame.

*Feel it wanting.*

The fire's hunger rose up to meet her, familiar as her own pulse. But lately—and this was the thing she could not explain, the thing that made her head ache and her dreams run backward—it felt less like meeting a stranger's hunger and more like being *recognized*. As if something beneath the flame knew her hands. As if the wanting flowed both ways.

She told it *not yet*, and the flame stilled, and held, and then—because she was alone, because she was lonely, because nineteen years old is young to be the most powerful person in a city that does not trust you—she pushed a little further than her mother ever allowed.

She let the flame grow.

It swelled to the height of her finger, her hand, her forearm—a pillar of soft gold light standing on the hearthstone without fuel, without smoke, bending toward her like a sunflower toward noon. Warmth poured over her face. In the light she could see the whole chamber: the tapestries her grandmother had woven, the harp no one played, the narrow bed where she slept badly, the looking glass with its hairline crack running through her reflection like a river through a map.

It was beautiful. It was hers. For three heartbeats, kneeling on cold stone in a locked room, Maren was not a queen, not an orphan, not a symbol stamped on coins—she was a girl doing the one true thing she knew how to do.

Then the ache behind her eyes flared, sharp as a needle, and the flame guttered out all at once—not dying but *leaving*, pouring downward off the edge of the hearthstone like water finding a crack, sinking into the floor, into the stone, into somewhere far below that Maren did not know existed.

She sat back on her heels, breathing hard, rubbing her temples.

Strange. Every time, it was like this now. The magic came easily—easier every month, as though the city itself were loosening around her, as though Vell were a fist slowly unclenching—but it never stayed. It slipped through her fingers and *down*, always down, and left her lighter and emptier, the way she felt after weeping. She had assumed this was what magic cost. Her mother had warned her that power took tolls. *Pay them gladly,* her mother had said, *and pay them quietly.*

So Maren paid, and told no one, and lit no more candles that night.

* * *

In the morning there was honey cake for breakfast, and Kestrel to taste it.

Kestrel Eastwick had been tasting the queen's food since before Maren wore the crown—since she was a child herself, a thin quiet girl who stood at the edge of the royal table with her hands folded and ate small precise bites of everything meant for the princess. Maren had been eight or nine when she first spoke to her. *Doesn't it frighten you?* she'd asked. *Eating poison for someone else?*

Kestrel had considered the question with grave brown eyes. *I'm good at it,* she'd said, which was not an answer, and somehow was.

Ten years on, Kestrel was the only person whose presence Maren found restful. The court was a weather system of flattery and fear; her councilors were storms; her suitors were fog. But Kestrel was still air. She stood at the queen's shoulder at every meal, tasted each dish with a small unhurried grace, and asked nothing, wanted nothing, watched everything. When Maren dreamed badly, it was Kestrel's calm face she imagined saying *not yet* to the fire.

This morning Kestrel tasted the honey cake, the preserved plums, the bread, the butter, the tea. She swallowed each morsel and waited out the customary interval with her hands folded, and Maren watched her throat move and thought, not for the first time, *What must it be like, to carry death in your mouth every day and call it work?*

"You've gone quiet," Maren said. "Quieter."

"I'm always quiet, Majesty." A ghost of a smile. "It's part of the service."

"Is your tongue well? You wince sometimes. When you taste."

Something crossed Kestrel's face—quick, quickly smoothed, like a fish under ice. "Old habit. Some flavors sit strangely."

"What flavors?"

"Bitter ones. Metallic ones." She paused. "Lately, most of them."

Maren set down her fork. "That should be reported to the Guild."

"It will sound like complaint. Ten years without a single poisoning—I'm the Guild's best argument for itself. They won't thank me for suggesting I'm going numb." Kestrel said it lightly, but her eyes stayed on the tablecloth a moment too long. Then she lifted them, and they were steady again, and Maren wondered—as she often wondered—if the taster ever felt watched by her own queen, and whether she minded.

"I trust you," Maren said, simply, because it was morning and no ministers were present and she could say true things. "You know that. Of everyone in this palace, it's you I—" She stopped. Careful. Even here, even now. "You're the only one who's never wanted anything from me."

Kestrel's expression did something complicated and brief. "Everyone wants something, Majesty. Most of us are just polite about the size of it."

"Well," said Maren, "then yours is very small indeed, because in ten years I haven't seen it once."

* * *

The gift arrived at midday, and it was wrong from the first moment.

Envoys' gifts came with ceremony: heralds, manifests, wax seals, clerks of the Gift Office recording every item in triplicate. This one came in a plain cart with a single courier, a man with dust on his boots and a letter bearing the seal of the Ambassador of Serevan—who was, Maren knew perfectly well, currently aboard a ship somewhere in the Gray Straits, weeks from home. The letter apologized for the informality. The ambassador had departed in haste, he wrote; a token of esteem had been overlooked in his baggage; he begged Her Majesty's forgiveness for the humble presentation and hoped the vintage would speak for itself.

One bottle. Dark glass, unmarked, sealed with black wax. No vintage, no vintner's stamp, no provenance.

"Ambassador Veyl sends gifts like this?" Maren asked the courier.

"The ambassador says some wines are better unknown, Majesty."

It was the kind of answer designed to sound sophisticated. It sounded, to Maren, like a man reciting words he'd been given. She dismissed him with a nod and sat looking at the bottle for a long time after the doors closed.

Her council would say destroy it. Her Warden-captain would say destroy it and burn the cart and question the courier. Protocol said every foreign gift passed through three offices before it reached the royal hand, and this one had skipped all three, arriving instead directly at her door like a secret whispered through a keyhole.

Which meant, she thought, turning the bottle in the light, that either someone believed she was foolish enough to drink an unlabeled wine from a stranger—

—or someone knew she was lonely enough.

She rang for Kestrel.

The taster came promptly, as she always did, and Maren watched her take in the bottle, the broken seal of protocol, the absence of clerks, and arrive at the same arithmetic without a word passing between them.

"Serevan honey wine, if the letter's honest," Maren said. "It usually isn't. Will you?"

Kestrel took the bottle. Turned it. Held it up to the window light, and Maren saw her go very still.

"Majesty. There's a mark under the wax."

"I see no mark."

"No." Kestrel's thumbnail worried at the black seal, flaking a corner of it free. "Underneath. Pressed into the glass itself, where the label should be." She tilted the bottle, and the light caught it: a spiral, fine as a hair, coiled at the base of the neck like a fingerprint. "There."

Maren leaned close. The spiral was delicate, deliberate, cut into the glass as it was made. She had never seen its like. And yet some deep-buried part of her mind stirred at the shape, the way one stirs at a half-remembered dream—a sense of having traced that curve before, somewhere, in another life, with another hand.

"It means nothing to me," she said slowly. "But it feels like it should."

"To me as well," Kestrel said quietly, and Maren heard the lie in it—or not a lie, exactly, but a door held shut—and chose, for reasons she could not have defended to her council, not to push against it. There were so few people left whom she trusted. She could not afford to interrogate the trust itself.

"Will you taste it?"

For a moment Kestrel didn't answer. She stood with the bottle cradled in both hands like something rescued from a fire, and her thumb rested over the spiral as though covering a wound. Then she nodded once, fetched the tasting cups, and poured.

The wine ran amber-gold, thick, catching the light like the pillar of flame had caught it last night on the hearthstone. It smelled of summer, of orchards, of her mother's garden in a year that no longer existed anywhere but in Maren's memory.

Kestrel drank. A mouthful, held on the tongue, then swallowed, as the ritual demanded. Then she stood with her hands folded and waited out the minutes, and Maren counted them with her, watching the taster's face for the flicker, the pallor, the tremor that ten years of protocol had taught them both to read.

Nothing came.

"Nothing," Kestrel said at last. Her voice was odd—flat, careful, like someone reading words off a page in a language they don't understand. "It's clean."

"Truly? All of it?"

"All of it." Kestrel turned the cup in her fingers. "Majesty, may I say something improper?"

"You may always say something improper. That's why I keep you."

"There is no poison in this wine." Kestrel looked up, and her eyes were not calm at all; they were the eyes of a woman standing at the edge of a high place. "But there should be. I know this vintage, or one like it—Serevan honey wine is traditionally dosed with a preservative that is harmless in small glasses and lethal in a full pour. Every bottle of it I've ever tasted carried the bitterness of it under the sweet. This one doesn't. Someone has filtered it. Purified it. Which costs more than the wine does." She set the cup down with a small precise click. "Someone spent a fortune making certain this gift could not hurt you. And then hid the bottle, broke every protocol, and sent it in secret. Majesty—gifts that cannot kill you are rarer and stranger than gifts that can. I don't know what to make of it. But I don't like it."

Maren looked at the bottle. At the spiral pressed into the glass, coiled tight as a sleeping thing. Behind her eyes, the old ache pulsed once, twice, like something turning over in its sleep far below the floor.

"Neither do I," she said. "And yet."

She poured a measure into her own cup. Kestrel's breath caught audibly.

"Majesty—"

"She purified it," Maren said. "Whoever sent this spent a fortune so that it couldn't hurt me. You've tasted it. You're alive. And I am so tired, Kestrel, of living in a palace where everything is tested and sealed and feared, where no one gives me anything without three offices and a manifest." She raised the cup. "Someone, somewhere, has sent me a gift that is only a gift. I'm going to accept it."

"That isn't wisdom, Majesty. That's loneliness wearing wisdom's coat."

"Yes," Maren agreed, and drank.

The wine was extraordinary—honey and smoke and something older underneath, a flavor like the memory of sunlight. Warmth spread through her chest, down her arms, pooling in her palms, and for a moment she felt the way she felt on the hearthstone at night: recognized. Welcomed. As if the wine knew her name.

When she lowered the cup, Kestrel was watching her with an expression Maren had never seen on that composed face before—something between fear and grief, aimed not at the queen but at herself, as though she had failed some test only she knew she was taking.

"More?" Maren offered.

"No," Kestrel said. "Thank you, Majesty. I think—" She stopped. Started again. "I think I should go. There are preparations. The feast."

"The anniversary feast. Yes." Three days hence. The whole court, the foreign delegations, the Wardens in their gray, the Guild in their white. Maren smiled, and found the smile came easily, easier than smiles had come in months. "We'll need everything perfect, Kestrel. Everything."

Kestrel bowed and withdrew, and at the door she paused—one heartbeat, no more—with her hand on the frame, as if she meant to turn and say something. Say *don't drink any more of it*. Say *there is something I have to tell you*. Say anything at all.

Then she was gone, and Maren was alone with the bottle and the amber light and the warm hum in her blood.

That night, on the hearthstone, the candle flame leapt to meet her hands before she even called it—eager, joyful, enormous—and when she told it *not yet*, it obeyed so instantly, so gratefully, that she laughed aloud in her empty room. The magic had never come this easily. It was as though something had opened. As though a door, somewhere far beneath her feet, had quietly swung wide.

She did not hear the faint answering hum in the stones. She did not feel her warmth draining downward, drop by drop, into the dark.

She only knew that for the first time in four years—since her mother died, since the crown settled on her head like a hand—she did not feel alone.

Far below, something felt the same way about her.
