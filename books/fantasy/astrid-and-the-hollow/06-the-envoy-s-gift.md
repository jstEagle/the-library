## 6. The Envoy's Gift

The bottle arrived at midmorning, wrapped in oilcloth and sealed with green wax, and it sat on the taster's table like a sleeping animal.

Kestrel had learned to read gifts the way other people read faces. A gift's weight told you its intent before its label did; the angle of a seal told you whether the sender wanted it opened quickly or slowly; the quality of the wrapping told you how much the sender feared being refused. This one was heavy for its size—glass, thick glass—and the wax bore no crest, which meant either humility or confidence, and in her experience the two were rarely distinguishable until someone bled.

She turned it in the gray light from the single window of the taster's quarters. Honey wine, said the tag, in a careful diplomatic hand. From the delegation of Serevan, with the compliments of Envoy Aldric Vane. For Her Majesty's table.

Serevan. She knew the vintage by reputation only; the hill country east of the Marrow had been at cold terms with Vell since before Kestrel was born, sending envoys who smiled like drawn knives and gifts that were always, always tested first. That was her job. That was, in fact, the entire architecture of her life: she stood between the queen and every bottle, every plate, every sugared plum that crossed the palace threshold, and she swallowed the world's malice so that Maren would not have to.

Ten years. Nine courses at last night's state dinner, and her tongue still numb at the edges, still tasting the ghost of nothing where nothing should have been.

Kestrel set the bottle down and looked at it for a long moment.

Then she went to find Corvane's ledger clerk, because protocol demanded documentation, and documentation was one of the few weapons a small woman could carry openly.

* * *

The tasting chamber smelled of beeswax and old stone, as it always did—a room without windows, built into the thickest part of the palace wall, furnished with a table, two chairs, a basin, and a bell. Everything a taster needed to die comfortably and report it promptly.

The clerk, a narrow man named Pell whose fingers were permanently ink-stained, recorded the provenance in his spidery hand while Kestrel unwrapped the oilcloth. "Envoy Vane presented it personally," he murmured. "At the closing audience. The queen accepted on your behalf, naturally."

"Naturally." The queen could not taste her own gifts. It was one of those absurdities of office that everyone had stopped noticing: Maren ruled a kingdom and could not safely drink a cup of wine unless another woman drank it first. "And Vane said nothing about the contents?"

"He said it was pressed the year of the old king's death. He said—" Pell hesitated, quill hovering. "He said it was a peace offering. That Vell and Serevan had shared this vintage once before, long ago, and perhaps might again."

Something cold walked up Kestrel's spine. *Long ago.* Diplomats loved history; it gave their lies respectable clothing. But there was something else under the words, some grain of wrongness, the way a coin can be the right weight and still be false.

"Note that verbatim," she said. "Every word."

Pell noted it verbatim.

The bottle itself was dark green glass, hand-blown, its shoulders slick with age or made to look aged. There was no paper label—only an impression pressed into the wax of the cork's outer seal, faint, almost apologetic, the kind of mark a maker leaves when he does not want to be found. Kestrel tilted it toward the lamp.

A spiral. Tight-coiled, turning inward on itself, drawn in a single unbroken line—the kind of spiral a child draws when asked to draw a snail shell, except that this one had been cut with a die, deliberately, precisely, by someone who wanted it there.

Her hands went very still.

She had seen that mark exactly once before. Three nights ago, in the night market, in the palm of a nervous young woman buying a fever ward, paid for with an old coin stamped with the same coiling line. Kestrel had told herself then that it was coincidence—that old coins circulated, that sigils repeated, that the world was full of spirals because spirals were easy to draw. She had sold the woman her charm and gone home and not slept, and in the morning she had told herself that too.

Now the same mark sat on a diplomatic gift bound for the queen's table, and self-deception was no longer on offer.

"Pell," she said, keeping her voice level, bored even. "Fetch me the tasting kit. And then leave me."

He left. The door closed. Kestrel stood alone in the windowless room with the bottle and the lamp and the sound of her own blood moving through her ears like a distant tide.

*Someone knows what I am.*

The thought arrived whole, uninvited, wearing no disguise, and she made herself look at it directly because looking away had never once saved anyone.

* * *

The ritual came back to her hands before it came back to her mind, the way all her training lived in her body now rather than her head. Silver spoon, silver knife, white cloth. Silver, because poison sulked in base metals and showed itself in tarnish; white cloth, because discoloration read truest against white. She poured a measure into the crystal testing glass—crystal, because glass held no opinion of its own—and held it to the lamp.

Honey wine should have been amber. This was amber, yes, but with a depth to it, a darkness gathering at the heart of the glass like sediment that refused to settle. She swirled it. The darkness swirled with it and did not disperse.

She ran the standard assays. The silver blade went in clean and came out clean—no tarnish, no bloom, no rainbowing at the edge. The cloth, dampened and pressed to the pour, took no stain. She burned a drop on the slate dish and watched the flame burn blue-gold and sweet-smelling, honeyed smoke curling toward the vent, entirely innocent.

Every test passed. Every test, in ten years, had taught her that passing tests meant nothing. The poisons that killed tasters were never the poisons tests caught; they were the ones designed by people who understood that a taster's craft was a liturgy, and liturgies could be studied.

There was one assay left. The oldest one. The one that required her mouth.

Kestrel sat down in the plain chair, laid the white cloth over her knee as she'd done ten thousand times, and lifted the glass.

Here is what no one outside the guild understood about tasting: it was not courage. Courage was for moments, singular and survivable. Tasting was arithmetic. You calculated the dose against your body, the body against the years, the years against the odds, and you put the mathematics in your mouth and waited for the answer. Kestrel Eastwick had survived a decade of that arithmetic by being excellent at it—not brave, never brave, merely precise. Precision was how the small stayed alive.

She tasted the wine.

Honey, first—real honey, dark and floral, mountain thyme maybe, the taste of a slope somewhere east of the Marrow where bees worked flowers no Vellish bee had ever met. Then the ferment underneath, warm and round, a good year, a patient year. Then—

Nothing.

Not the absence of flavor. Not blandness. Nothing. A hole in the world shaped like a swallow, a place where her tongue simply stopped reporting, as if the wine had passed through her mouth and out the far side of her senses without touching anything on the way. She had felt numbness creeping across her tongue for months now, the tax of ten years of controlled dosing, but this was different. This was localized. Surgical. As if the wine had reached into her mouth and taken something specific.

She spat into the basin, rinsed, spat again. Sat very still.

Her heart was doing something strange—not racing, but knocking, hard slow blows like a fist on a door. Because she knew what nothing meant. Nothing was not safety. Nothing was the signature of a compound that did not bother to interact with ordinary flesh, a poison engineered past detection, past assay, past every liturgy the guild had ever written—

Or engineered *for* flesh like hers.

The realization landed in her stomach like a dropped stone.

She counted. Poison that killed in minutes, if it was what she thought it was—if it was anything like the compounds Corvane drilled them on in the early years, the ones they called *courier toxins*, fast and total—then she had perhaps three hundred heartbeats to learn whether she was dying. She sat in the plain chair and counted her heartbeats and watched the lamp flame and did not pray, because prayer was a currency she'd never been able to afford.

One hundred. Two hundred. The knocking in her chest began to change—slower, deeper, each beat arriving with a strange pressure behind it, as though her heart were pushing against something dense. Three hundred. Four. The pressure spread outward, down her arms, up through her throat, a heaviness like standing in deep water. Her fingertips went cold. Her vision sharpened instead of dimming, every thread of the lamp flame distinct, every grain of the table's wood suddenly legible, and she thought, with detached professional interest: *this is what it feels like when the body decides everything matters.*

Five hundred beats. Six. The heaviness crested, held—

And drained away. All of it, in the space of ten breaths, pouring out of her limbs and down through the floor, down through the stone, down into whatever lay beneath the palace foundations, leaving her light and hollow and shaking, sitting in a windowless room with an empty testing glass and a heartbeat that had returned to its ordinary work.

Alive. Entirely, impossibly alive.

Kestrel looked at her hands. They were steady. Of course they were steady; her hands had been trained past trembling the way a soldier's are trained past flinching. But inside, beneath the steadiness, something had rearranged itself permanently, the way a room looks different after a burglary even when nothing seems missing.

Because here was the truth she could no longer refuse: the poison had known her. It had come into her mouth carrying death for any human being alive, and it had met her body and recognized it—recognized the ten years of controlled dosing, the cultivated immunity, the slow deliberate engineering of a girl into a vessel—and it had *deferred*. It had poured through her like water through an open gate and gone down, down, down into the dark under the palace, and she had felt it go. Felt it leave her the way heat leaves a stone at nightfall.

Which meant the wine was not an assassination attempt.

It was a message.

* * *

She did not report the tasting immediately. Protocol said she should ring the bell, summon Pell, record the result: *sample negative, taster unaffected.* Instead she sat with the bottle between her hands and made herself think, because thinking was the one discipline Corvane had never managed to train out of her—he'd tried, God knew, had spent years trying to make her a perfect instrument, all reflex and liturgy and no interior—but the interior kept growing back, like grass through flagstones.

Three facts, then. She laid them out on the table of her mind the way she'd lay out spoons.

First: the wine was poisoned with something exquisite, something beyond the guild's own curriculum, which meant its maker was not a rival court's alchemist playing at diplomacy. Serevan did not have this. No foreign court had this. This was *homegrown*—made by someone who had studied the Tasters' Guild from the inside, who understood courier toxins and cultivation protocols and the precise architecture of an engineered immunity.

Second: the spiral. On the bottle, on the coin, and—she was suddenly certain of it, certain the way you are certain of your own name—on the foundation stone beneath the palace, the worn coiling mark that generations of servants had called a mason's joke and no one had ever questioned. Someone was drawing a line between these things and wanting her to see it. Wanting *her* specifically. An envoy's gift was tested by the taster and no one else; whatever message rode in that bottle could only be received by the woman whose job was to drink it.

Third: she had felt the poison leave her. Go *down.* Which meant the thing beneath the palace—the thing she had spent ten years refusing to name, the hunger she'd sensed in the stones during her worst nights, the reason the guild's deepest vaults were all built downward—had just been fed. By her. Through her. Again.

Her whole life, she had believed her immunity protected the queen. Clean hands, clean mouth, a wall between Maren and the world's malice. But walls run both ways, don't they? A channel runs both ways. Ten thousand tastings, ten thousand doses absorbed and carried and *deposited* somewhere, and she had never once asked where the somewhere was, because asking questions was not what instruments did.

The bottle sat in the lamplight, and the spiral on its seal seemed almost gentle, almost patient, the way a trap is patient.

"You know what I am," she said aloud to it, quietly, in the empty room. Her voice sounded strange in the stone. "Fine. Then you know I'm going to find out who taught you."

* * *

She recorded the tasting at last—*sample negative, taster unaffected, note: unusual body response, recommend archival*—and signed it with the small neat signature that matched ten thousand others in the guild's ledgers, one more anonymous entry in an anonymous career. Then she carried the bottle herself to the guild's archive, because protocol required the physical sample be retained, and because she wanted to see Corvane's face when she handed it over.

Master Corvane received samples in his office above the archive, a room of ledgers and brass instruments and windows that looked out, pointedly, on nothing. He was older than the palace itself, it sometimes seemed, dry and precise as a scalpel, with eyes that had never once in ten years shown Kestrel anything she could use. He took the bottle, turned it, and paused.

For half a second—half a second, no more—the scalpel of his face slipped, and she saw underneath it. Not surprise. Recognition. Worse than surprise: recognition, the look of a man meeting an expected guest he had hoped would delay his visit.

"The seal," Kestrel said. "You've seen the mark."

"I've seen many marks." He set the bottle down among his instruments with exaggerated care, as if it were fragile, as if it were dangerous, and both at once. "Your report says unusual body response."

"It says recommend archival."

"It says more than that, if one reads closely." His eyes came up and fixed on hers, and for the first time in ten years, Master Corvane looked at Kestrel Eastwick as if she were a person rather than a procedure. It was the most frightening thing that had happened all day, and the day had included her own death and resurrection. "Eat nothing tonight that isn't from the palace kitchens. Drink nothing at all. Go home by the main streets, taster, and—" he paused, and something almost human moved through the dryness of him, "—and be careful whom you let sketch you."

The words landed like ice water. *Ilan.* How could he—she hadn't told anyone, hadn't logged it, the cartographer with his charcoal and his watching eyes across the state dinner hall—

But Corvane was already turning away, already reaching for a ledger, the audience concluded, the door of his attention closing as smoothly as it always did.

"One more thing," he said, without turning around. "The Serevan delegation departs tomorrow at dawn. Envoy Vane requested, particularly, to thank the taster who approved his gift. He asked for you by description."

Kestrel stood very still.

"He has never seen you," Corvane continued, in the tone of a man reading a shipping manifest. "No envoy has. The tasters' faces are not public. And yet he described you, Eastwick, down to the color of your coat." He did turn, then, and his face had reassembled itself into pure institutional blankness, but his voice, when it came, was quiet enough to be almost kind. "Go home. Lock your door. And ask yourself who, in this city, knows exactly what you are—and has been waiting, for a very long time, for you to taste it too."

She walked back through the palace corridors in the failing afternoon light, past guards who saluted her without seeing her, past courtiers who stepped aside for the queen's taster with the particular courtesy owed to someone useful and expendable, and with every step the terror in her chest cooled and hardened and changed shape, the way fear does when it finally finds a floor to stand on.

They knew her. Whoever had sent the wine, whoever had described her coat, whoever had stamped the spiral on the seal—they had built her, or bred her, or bought her, and they had decided tonight to let her know it. That was terror. But terror, Kestrel had learned in ten years of swallowing other people's malice, was only information arriving too fast.

She climbed the stairs to her quarters, locked her door as instructed, and then did the thing Corvane had not instructed: she pulled loose the third floorboard by the hearth, the one hiding the small tin box of charms she'd never sold, the magic she'd never declared, and took out the luck knot she'd woven the night of the state dinner, the one braided with a hair from her own head.

If they had made her into an instrument, fine. Instruments could be retuned.

Tomorrow at dawn, Envoy Aldric Vane would thank her for the gift. And Kestrel Eastwick—who had spent a decade being no one, who had survived by being small—intended to be standing there, unhurried and unafraid, to receive his thanks.

And then she intended to ask him, smiling, over the heads of the guards:

*Who taught you my name?*
