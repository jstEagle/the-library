## 7. Order of the Sealed Table

The chamber beneath the Guildhall had no windows, and Master Corvane had made certain of it in his first year as head of the Tasters' Guild. Windows admitted light, and light admitted memory, and memory was the one poison his order had never learned to neutralize. The room was lit instead by nine candles set in a ring of black iron—nine, always nine, one for each dish of the state table—and their flames burned with a steadiness that no ordinary wax could account for. The tallow was rendered from the fat of consecrated lambs, mixed with ash from the palace foundations, and if a candle ever guttered, everyone in the room would know what it meant.

Tonight, all nine burned true.

Corvane sat at the head of a table shaped like a horseshoe, open at the throat, so that no member ever sat with his back fully to another. Twelve chairs were occupied. Twelve was the number that remained; there had been fifteen when he took office, and the three empty places had been filled with salt and covered in white cloth, because the guild did not speak the names of the dead aloud. Not since the incident. Not since Warden-Adjunct Pellis had asked, at a meeting very much like this one, the question that Corvane had spent eleven years ensuring no one would ask again.

*What exactly is under the palace?*

Pellis's chair was salted now. His name was unsaid. His body had been committed to the earth with full honors and an empty casket, because the guild did not return what the Hollow had been given, and the Hollow had been given Pellis—or rather, Pellis had been given to the question, and the question had eaten him down to a stain on the flagstones of the undercroft, and what remained after had not been Pellis at all.

Corvane folded his hands on the table. They were old hands, liver-spotted, precise, the hands of a man who had spent forty years lifting a spoon to his own lips before any king or queen was permitted to lift theirs. He had tasted for two monarchs and one regency. He had survived things that would have killed a lesser constitution in a heartbeat—which was, he reminded himself, rather the point. His body was a ledger. Every entry written in it had been deliberate.

"Brothers. Sisters." His voice was dry as old vellum. "You will forgive me if I dispense with the usual courtesies. We have business tonight that does not benefit from warmth."

The twelve inclined their heads in unison, a small choreography of deference they had practiced so long it had become reflex. Corvane watched them do it and felt the familiar cold satisfaction settle over him like a well-fitted coat. Fear, properly administered, looked exactly like discipline.

"Three matters," he said. "I will take them in ascending order of unpleasantness."

He lifted the first finger.

"The honey wine." He let the words sit. Around the table, he saw the flinch travel like a current through water—Sub-Master Ondrey first, then the others, each remembering the envoy's gift, each remembering the report. "Our taster drank a vintage laced with heartsbane. Concentrated. Professional work—not the crude distillation of an assassin in a hurry, but the slow expression of a master poisoner who wanted the death to look natural, hours later, far from the gift itself. She should be dead. She is not dead. She reported no symptoms whatsoever."

"She has immunity," said Sub-Master Ondrey, whose specialty was toxicology and whose skepticism was a matter of professional pride. "Ten years of graduated exposure. We built her tolerance ourselves. Heartsbane is within the schedule."

"Heartsbane at that concentration is outside *any* schedule," Corvane said. "Ondrey. You wrote the schedules. Would you care to revise your statement?"

A pause. Ondrey's jaw worked. "No, Master."

"No," Corvane agreed. "Because what absorbed that poison was not tolerance. Tolerance is a wall. What she has is a door that opens inward. The poison went somewhere. Poison does not simply cease; it is the most honest substance in the world—it goes where it is sent, and it does what it is made to do, or it is changed into something else by whatever changes it." He leaned forward slightly, and the candlelight caught the ridges of his face and deepened them. "Something in Kestrel Eastwick changed it. Something in her *took it*. And I want every one of you to sit quietly with the implications of that while I proceed to the second matter."

He lifted the second finger.

"The coin." From inside his coat he drew a small cloth pouch and upended it onto the table. A single coin rolled out and spun, wobbling, and lay flat with a sound too loud for its size. It was old silver gone black at the edges, worn nearly smooth, and stamped in its center with a spiral—the sigil that appeared on the foundation stone beneath the palace's oldest corner, the stone laid before Vell had a name, the stone that the official histories attributed to the city's pious founders and their gratitude to the gods.

"We all know what the histories say," Corvane said. "We wrote them. Or our predecessors did, which amounts to the same thing. The spiral is not a symbol of founding gratitude. It is a seal-diagram. It appears on the foundation stone because the foundation stone is not a foundation stone. It is a *capstone*. There is a difference, brothers and sisters, between a stone that holds a building up and a stone that holds something else down, and the men who laid it understood the difference perfectly well, which is why they carved the spiral where only the patient and the curious would ever find it."

"Where did the coin come from?" asked Sister Vael, the youngest of the twelve, sharp-eyed, ambitious, dangerous in the way that the young are dangerous—by not yet knowing enough to be afraid.

"From the night market," Corvane said. "Paid to a charm-seller. Paid, specifically, to *our* charm-seller, whether the payer knew it or not." He watched Vael's face carefully as he said it. He wanted to see whether the name landed on her like a weight. It did. Good. "Someone is circulating the old sigil again. Someone wants it seen. Coins are how you put a symbol into a thousand pockets without anyone asking why. I have purchased eleven more this month through intermediaries. Eleven coins, all old, all worn, all bearing the spiral. Someone has opened a vault that has been closed for a century, or someone has begun minting copies, and either possibility displeases me more than I can conveniently express."

"And the third matter." His voice dropped, and though the room was sealed and silent, he found himself lowering it further still, until the twelve had to lean in to hear him, which was precisely the effect he intended. One did not announce the third matter. One *confided* it.

"You have all served the guild long enough to know that we taste for reasons beyond the safety of the crown. You have read the restricted ledgers. You have seen the tithe-schedules—the graduated doses, the compounds, the years of careful cultivation that produced the woman currently poisoning herself for us three meals a day. You know that everything she swallows passes through her and goes *somewhere*. You have signed the oaths. You have taken the stipends. But I find, in my last years of service, that I am tired of the architecture of euphemism, and so tonight I am going to say the thing plainly, once, in this room, and then we will never say it again."

The candles burned. Somewhere above them, muffled by six floors of Guildhall, a cart rattled past on the evening streets of Vell, and the sound reached the chamber as a vibration in the floor, faint as a pulse.

"There is a thing beneath the palace," Corvane said. "It is older than the dynasty. It is older than the ban on magic, which, I will remind you, this guild authored—we drafted the edicts, we trained the first Wardens, we built the apparatus of prohibition brick by brick for one purpose: so that no practitioner outside these walls would ever grow strong enough to wake it, or strong enough to contest us for the privilege of managing it. The ban is not piety. The ban is a fence around a pasture on which we graze alone. Every spell cast illegally in this city drains downward, along channels we mapped a hundred years ago, into the foundations, into the dark, into *it*. It feeds on worked magic the way a leech feeds on blood—slowly, invisibly, and always, always wanting more."

Sister Vael had gone pale. Ondrey's hands had gone still on the table. The eldest among them, Brother Hask, who had sat in this room under two previous Masters, merely closed his eyes, as a man does when a wound he has carried for decades is finally acknowledged aloud.

"It has a name in the oldest records," Corvane continued. "We do not use the name. Names are handles, and handles are for pulling, and we are not in the business of pulling. In the ledgers it is designated only as the Sealed Tenant. In the field reports of the first Wardens, it is called the Hollow, and that word has stuck to it like burrs, and so the Hollow it is. It was a person once. The records agree on that much, across sources that never touched one another—a queen, in the time before the dynasty, consumed from the inside by her own working, hollowed out the way a tree is hollowed by rot while the bark still stands and looks, from the lane, entirely alive. What sits beneath the throne room is what was left when the person was used up. An appetite wearing the memory of a shape. It dreams, the old Wardens wrote. It reaches. And once a generation—once a *reign*, if the reign is careless—it finds a handhold in the world above and begins, very gently, to climb."

"And we feed it," said Brother Hask, eyes still closed. Not a question. A confession offered on behalf of them all.

"We feed it," Corvane said. "Through the tithe. Through the tasters. Through ten years of scheduled doses poured down the throat of a woman bred for the purpose, whose blood carries the compounds downward along the channels, whose body is not a wall against poison but a *funnel* for it. That is what the immunity is. That is what the immunity was always for. We did not protect Kestrel Eastwick. We *built* her, the way you build a mill beside a river—not to stop the water, but to make it turn a wheel."

He paused. The pause was long enough that the silence acquired texture, a pressure, a hum.

"Now the unpleasantness."

He rose from his chair. He did not often stand during addresses; standing was for verdicts. He walked the length of the horseshoe slowly, and the twelve turned their heads to follow him, twelve necks moving in reluctant unison, and he found the sight of it—this room full of the most disciplined poison-handlers in the empire, turning toward him like flowers toward a sun they feared—both gratifying and, in some cellar of himself he did not visit often, faintly obscene.

"The wine failed to kill her," he said. "That is the first anomaly. The coins are circulating. That is the second. And the third is this: four days ago, a cartographer attached to the Office of Works requested access to the restricted survey archives. Ilan Voss. Young. Talented. Unaffiliated with any order we watch, which is itself unusual—in Vell, the unaffiliated are either harmless or hiding. He requested the foundation surveys. The *deep* surveys. The ones that show the channels. The request was denied by the archivist on standard grounds, and the denial was logged, and the log was flagged to me by routine, and I ordered the flag buried, and then I ordered the man followed."

He stopped at the curve of the horseshoe, directly opposite his own empty chair, so that he stood at the mouth of the U, facing all twelve at once.

"Our watchers report that Voss spends his evenings drawing. Not maps. *Patterns*. Spirals. He has traced the sigil across seventeen structures in the old quarter, all pre-dynastic, all sited along lines that converge—he does not yet know this, but our geometrician confirmed it yesterday—at a single point beneath the throne room floor. He draws the same face, over and over, in the margins of his working sheets. Our watcher obtained a discarded sketch. The face is Kestrel Eastwick's."

The silence that followed had weight, like deep water.

"A man none of us recruited," Corvane said softly, "who has never met our taster, is dreaming her face and mapping her prison. Brothers and sisters, I have read the old reports. I have read what happened in the reign of the Regent Aldous, when the Hollow climbed halfway up through a kitchen girl before the guild burned the undercroft and salted the ashes and hanged three innocent cooks to make the story go away. The reports use a phrase I have never forgotten. They say the Hollow *prepares its instruments*. It cannot act. It can only arrange. It arranges people the way a chess player arranges pieces—slowly, patiently, across years, across generations—until the board is set and some small human hand makes the final move believing it was the hand's own idea."

He returned to his chair and sat, and the twelve exhaled almost audibly.

"I do not believe in coincidences," he said. "Coincidence is what ignorance calls pattern. The wine, the coins, the cartographer, the convergence—I do not yet know what the pattern *is*, but I know it is one, and I know it is accelerating, and acceleration at this depth means only one thing. It is waking. Slowly. The way ice wakes in spring—not all at once, but in cracks, in sounds in the night, in the groan of things shifting that have held still for a hundred years. The tithe must increase. The feeding must deepen. If it starves, it does not die—nothing down there dies—but it stops being drowsy, and a hungry waking is a fast waking, and a fast waking is a city with no morning."

"Increase it how?" Ondrey asked. "The schedule is already at the ceiling of what her constitution—"

"The schedule," Corvane said, "was designed for a woman with thirty years of service ahead of her. We will compress it. We will double the compound load within the season and trust the breeding." He heard the flatness in his own voice and did not soften it. Softness here was a leak, and leaks drowned rooms. "And we will watch her. Constantly. I want eyes on Kestrel Eastwick at every hour—her stall, her quarters, her route to the palace, the company she keeps, the coins she takes, the men she lets close. Especially the men. Especially *that* man. If Voss approaches her, I want to know within the hour. If she approaches *him*, I want to know within the minute. Assign Sister Vael to the market detail—she is young, she is forgettable, and she buys charms credibly."

Vael stiffened. "Master, if the girl is compromised—if she is already an instrument—am I not walking into—"

"Yes," Corvane said. "You are. That is why it is you. The guild asks its members to taste poison; it can scarcely balk at asking them to walk past it." He saw her swallow her objection, saw the fear go down her throat like a dose, and felt the old cold satisfaction again, and this time he did not examine it.

There was one more thing. He had saved it, the way he saved the bitter draught for the bottom of the cup, because the bottom of the cup was where the tongue expected bitterness and flinched least.

"You will notice," he said, "that I have described the Hollow's design, the prophecy-shaped convergence of events, the prepared instruments—and I have not told you what the instruments are *for*. Because I do not know. The old records end where the old records always end: at the torn page. Every copy of the Sealed Book in our archive lacks its final leaf, and the absence is not accident, and the absence is not damage, and I have spent nineteen years telling myself it was one or the other." He folded his hands again. "The final page named the ending. Whoever tore it out knew the ending, and chose to blind us to it, and I have never decided whether that man was a coward or the only honest servant this guild ever had."

He dismissed them with the old formula—*the table is cleared, the tongues are sealed*—and one by one the twelve rose, bowed, and filed out through the iron door, and the sound of the bolts sliding home behind the last of them was the sound Corvane loved best in the world: the sound of a secret, relocked.

He stayed. He always stayed. Nine candles, burning true.

Alone, he allowed himself the thing he never permitted in company: he took out the small tin from his coat pocket, thumbed it open, and looked at what lay inside on a bed of black wool. A curl of hair, brown, fine, cut from a child's head twenty-three years ago in a birthing room in the Lowmarsh district, by a guild midwife whose name was salted now like all the others. The child had been selected that night. Blooded, measured, the family tree pruned back four generations to a woman who had died tasting for a regent, and a man who had died in the undercroft asking questions. The child had been bought from a mother too poor and too frightened to refuse, installed in a guild nursery, raised on schedules, and delivered at fourteen to the palace as an orphan of the crown's charity.

Kestrel Eastwick. He had chosen her name himself. He had given her a name the way a gardener gives a trellis—something for the growing thing to climb.

And now the thing he had grown was drinking poisons that should have killed her and passing them somewhere, and a stranger was drawing her face in spirals, and the coins were moving through the market like seeds on wind, and somewhere below six floors of stone something old had begun, very gently, to climb.

Corvane closed the tin. He resented her—not the girl, not anymore; the instrument, the funnel, the bred thing with her steady hands and her forgettable face and her ten flawless years of service. He resented that the guild's whole architecture, his whole life's work, hung upon the constitution of a woman he had purchased for less than the price of a horse. He resented that the old records suggested—had always suggested—that the funnel might be more than a funnel. That the breeding program had aimed at something the founders had never written down, something that lived only in the torn page, something that made the taster not the lock on the door but the *keyhole*.

He snuffed eight of the candles with a brass snuffer, leaving the ninth—the ninth dish, the last course, the course that was always poison—burning alone.

Then he sat in the dark with his ledgers and began, in a hand as neat as a mortician's, to write the orders that would double her dose by season's end.

Below his feet, faint as a pulse, the floor was warm.

* * *

Two days later, the report came up from the market detail, and Sister Vael delivered it in person, white-faced, having run the last three streets.

"Master." She could barely get breath behind the words. "She wasn't at the stall."

Corvane set down his pen. "Where was she?"

"Gone. Since dawn. The awning was up, the stock was out, the luck knots were hanging—but she wasn't there. And then—" Vael's voice cracked, and she mastered it the way the guild had taught her, swallowing the fear like a dose. "Then the Wardens came. Six of them. Not for a raid—for *her*. They had papers. Sealed orders, countersigned inside the palace. They seized her stock, they broke open her strongbox, and when they demanded to know where she'd gone, the neighboring vendors said she'd left with a man. A tall man. They said she'd left with the cartographer."

Outside the shuttered window of Corvane's study, the afternoon light of Vell lay gold and indifferent on the rooftops, and beneath the rooftops, beneath the Guildhall, beneath the palace, beneath the capstone with its patient spiral, something that had waited a hundred years felt the board shift, and stirred, and began—to borrow the phrase of the first Wardens, the phrase Corvane had never forgotten—to climb.
