## 10. Wardens at the Door

I did not sleep after the bell beneath the floorboards. I sat in my narrow room above the tannery with a candle burned down to a coin of wax and my mother's charm kit open on my knees, and I told myself the things I always told myself: that Vell was full of strange sounds, that old buildings groaned like old animals, that a woman who had survived ten years by being forgettable could not afford to be frightened by a noise.

But my hands would not stop shaking, so I did what my hands knew how to do. I worked.

I made charms until the sky went grey at its edges—luck knots from red thread, fever wards folded into paper boats, a small brass bell charmed to ring once when someone lied to you. Small magics. Honest ones, or as honest as anything is in this city, where magic itself was outlawed by the same order that had fed it to me with a spoon when I was too young to know the taste. Each knot I tied, I thought of Ilan's map lying in its drawer, thirty-one points converging under the throne room like threads pulled toward a single throat. Each ward I folded, I thought of the honey wine, and how it had tasted of nothing, and how nothing was the loudest flavor I had ever known.

By dusk I had filled the tray. By dark I was in the night market.

* * *

The Saltglass Market lived in the bones of an old fish auction house, all iron ribs and hanging lanterns, and it only truly woke after midnight, when the Wardens' patrols thinned and the honest city went to bed and the other city—the real one—came out to trade. I had kept a stall there for six years. A folding table, a striped awning gone pink with age, a sign that read CHARMS & SMALL BLESSINGS in letters deliberately clumsy, as if written by someone who barely knew her letters. Nothing draws a Warden faster than fine calligraphy.

I sold luck knots to dockhands. Fever wards to a mother whose son coughed through every winter. A charm against bad dreams to a courier girl who paid in buttons and never met my eyes, which I respected. I haggled. I smiled the smile that made people look past me. And all the while I watched the mouths of the market the way I watched a dish before tasting it—not for what it showed me, but for what it failed to show.

The market was too quiet.

Not empty—Saltglass was never empty—but quiet in the wrong places. The dice game under the east arch, usually roaring, was murmuring. Old Sefa, who sold pickled eels and gossip in equal measure, had closed early. The lanterns nearest the canal gate were unlit, and no one had relit them, and everyone was very carefully not looking at them.

I was weighing out dried lavender for a sleep charm when the girl came to warn me.

She couldn't have been more than twelve, one of the gutter-runners who earned coppers carrying messages between stalls, and she came up to my table with the studied boredom children wear when they are doing something dangerous. "You're wanted," she said. "Back lane. Man says he has coin for fever wards."

"Tell him to come to the stall."

"He can't." Her eyes flicked left, right, left again. "He says—" and then her voice dropped out of its bored register entirely, went thin and young, "—he says the greycoats came in through the fish gate. Six of them. They asked Sefa which stall sells the strongest charms, and she wouldn't say, and they—" She swallowed. "They took her sign."

They had taken down Sefa's painted sign, which meant they had taken Sefa, or meant to. The Wardens did not remove signs for shoplifters. They removed signs for names.

I looked at my tray of charms—at the little paper boats, the knotted thread, the brass bell—and understood, with the particular clarity that comes a half-second too late to be useful, that none of it mattered anymore. For six years I had believed my safety lay in being small: a charm-seller of no consequence, a taster of no renown, a woman whose face slid off memory like rain off glass. But Ilan had drawn my face without knowing why. A book a century old had named me without knowing me. Someone in the palace had sent poison in a bottle stamped with the spiral, and now someone in the palace had sent greycoats into the Saltglass Market, and neither of those someones was looking for a criminal.

They were looking for the Taster of Deaths.

"Run home another way," I told the girl, pressing three coppers into her hand. "Don't run past the fish gate."

She ran. I began, calmly, to pack my tray.

* * *

They gave me less time than I needed, because they were not trying to catch me packing. They were trying to catch me looking.

That was the tell I should have seen sooner. Greycoat raids were loud—boots, shouts, lanterns thrust into faces, the whole ugly theater of it, because fear was half their function. These came silent. No horns. No herald. Just six shapes in slate-grey oilskins moving through the market's edges the way water moves through cloth, cutting off the canal gate first, then the stair to the tanner's row, then the arch where the dice players had been. Not searching for charms. Sealing exits.

Which meant whoever had ordered this had told them exactly what I was worth, and exactly that I must not be allowed to run.

I kept my head down over my lavender and let the market close around me. Stalls folding. Lanterns dying one by one, vendors melting into the warren of passages behind the auction hall—they knew the drill; everyone in Saltglass knew the drill—and leaving me alone under my pink-striped awning like the last lit window in a drowning street.

"Kestrel Eastwick."

The voice came from behind me, level and official, the voice of a man reading from a list. I turned. The lead Warden stood at the mouth of the central aisle, lantern raised, and the light caught the badge on his chest: the crossed keys of the Office of Sealed Things. Not the regular watch. The deep watch. The ones who answered not to the queen's magistrate but to the guilds.

"You're wanted for questioning regarding unlicensed thaumaturgy," he said.

"Everyone here is," I said. "It's a charm market."

"Not everyone here is named in a guild warrant." He came forward, unhurried, boots ringing on the old stone. His men fanned out with the smooth economy of people who had done this many times. "Come quietly and this stays simple."

"And if I don't?"

"Then it doesn't." He said it without heat, almost kindly, and that kindness frightened me more than cruelty would have. Cruelty wants something from you. Kindness like his had already decided you were a thing to be moved.

My mind did what it always did under pressure—it tasted. Fear has a flavor, I've learned; it tastes of copper and cold water. And underneath it, fainter, stranger: a taste I could not name, like the air before snow, like the space inside a sealed jar. The Hollow, reaching. Or my own dread, wearing its perfume. I honestly could not tell anymore, and that frightened me worst of all.

"I'll come quietly," I said, and lifted my hands, and dropped the sleep-charm packet.

It burst on the stones between us in a soft grey bloom—lavender and valerian and powdered moonpetal, harmless, legal even, the kind of thing any herbalist might spill. Three of the Wardens flinched back anyway, because in Saltglass nothing spilled is ever innocent, and in that half-second of flinching I was already moving, over the table, through the gap between the eel-stall and the wall, into the dark.

Behind me the lead Warden didn't shout. That was the worst part. He simply said, in the same level voice, "East side. She'll go east," as though he had read it off the warrant, and boots began to ring behind me in perfect, terrible order.

They knew me. Not my face—my habits. My routes. Someone had written me down the way Ilan drew me: studied, mapped, predicted. I cut west instead, against my own instinct, and heard the pursuit split without hesitation, two men peeling off to cover the western passage I had chosen precisely because no one should have expected it.

There was nowhere in the market they hadn't already thought of. So I stopped running like a person with routes.

* * *

Behind the fish auction house, where the building's iron ribs met the older stone of whatever had stood there before Vell was Vell, there was a crawlspace I'd found in my first year—a crack between two foundation blocks, wide enough for a thin woman, opening into the root cellar of the tannery. I had shown it to no one. I went to it now, fingers finding the cold seam in the dark, and stopped.

Because I could hear breathing on the other side.

Slow. Patient. The breathing of someone waiting, not chasing. Whoever held the warrant had known about the crawlspace too—or had simply posted a man in every dark place a small woman might fit, and let the market do the hunting for him.

I put my forehead against the cold stone and made myself think, really think, for the first time since the girl had come to my stall. Running was spent. Hiding was spent. Every trick of smallness I had built my life on had been catalogued by someone with a seal and a warrant, and the only things left were the things I had never used, because using them would mean admitting what I was.

In my left sleeve, sewn into the seam where no searcher had ever thought to feel, was a charm I had made four years ago on a night I couldn't sleep, and had never sold, and had never spoken of, because it was not a small magic. It was not a luck knot. It was the kind of working that, if the Wardens had known of it, would have earned me not a warrant but a pyre.

A charm for erasing.

Not memories of me—I wasn't vain enough, or foolish enough, to think I could pull myself out of six years of market gossip. It worked smaller and crueler than that. It reached into the last few minutes of a mind and took the shape out of them: not the events, but the meaning. You would remember lanterns, shouting, a woman running. You would not remember that she mattered. You would remember a chase the way you remember a dream of falling—with your heart pounding and no face attached to it. I had tested it once, on a Warden who caught me selling a fever ward, and I had vomited afterward behind Sefa's stall and not slept for two days, because the man had walked away rubbing his eyes like a person waking, and I had understood exactly what I had become capable of.

It cost. Magic in Vell always cost; the guild had just taught us to pay in other people's coin. This one paid in mine—each use took a sliver of my own remembering, and I had never learned what sliver it would choose.

Six Wardens. One charm. The arithmetic was obscene.

But the charm didn't need to touch all six. It needed to touch the warrant—the certainty in the lead Warden's level voice, the knowledge of my name and my routes and my worth. If the man holding the hunt forgot what he was hunting, the hunt would collapse into six confused greycoats standing in an empty market wondering why they'd drawn their lanterns.

I backed out of the crawlspace's shadow, into the open aisle, and let them find me.

They came from three directions, lanterns high, and the lead Warden stepped forward with his list and his kindness and said, "Kestrel Eastwick, you're wanted for—"

"For questioning regarding unlicensed thaumaturgy," I finished. "Yes. You said." I raised my hands. "Before we go—there's something you should see. Something I was told to give whoever came. They said you'd know the sigil."

His eyes narrowed, but greed and duty make the same face. He came closer. Close enough. Close enough to see me draw the charm from my sleeve, and to mistake it—for one heartbeat, the heartbeat I needed—for a token, a proof, a thing handed over rather than a thing unleashed.

I broke it against my palm.

It went out of me like breath in winter. There was no light, no sound—that was the horror of it, that so vast a theft should be so quiet. I felt it leave through my fingertips and enter the world, and I felt the price come due somewhere behind my eyes, a small door opening in a room of my memory and something dear being carried out of it before I could see what. I felt all of that. What the Wardens felt was nothing at all, which is the nature of the working.

The lead Warden stopped mid-stride.

He looked at me. He looked at his lantern. He looked around the emptying market with the mild bafflement of a man who has walked into a room and forgotten why, and I watched the hunt drain out of his face like water out of a cracked bowl. Around him, his men slowed, lowered their lanterns, exchanged glances that held no coordination in them at all anymore—just five strangers in grey oilskins standing in a fish market at two in the morning.

"Why are we here?" one of them said.

"Patrol," said another, uncertainly. "Night patrol. Isn't it?"

The lead Warden rubbed his eyes like a man waking. His gaze passed over me—over my face, my sleeves, my trembling hands—and slid off, the way gazes had slid off me for ten years, and for the first time in my life I was grateful for it.

"Market's closing," he said at last, to no one and everyone. "Back to the gate."

They went. All six, boots ringing in no order at all, ordinary men walking home through the ordinary dark, carrying a hole apiece where the last quarter hour had been.

I stood alone under my pink-striped awning until their footsteps faded, and then I sat down on the ground among my scattered lavender and my unsold luck knots, and I shook.

* * *

Later—much later, in the grey hour when even Saltglass sleeps—I sat in the tannery cellar with a single candle and took inventory of myself, the way I'd take inventory of a spice cabinet after a break-in.

The charm had taken its price. I searched for it the way you search for a missing tooth with your tongue. My mother's face: still there, still warm, still gone. Ilan's face: there, painfully there, drawn in light behind my eyelids. The taste of the honey wine: there. The sound of the underwater bell: there, and I almost wished it weren't.

Then I found it. The gap. Small, clean-edged, unmistakable.

It was the memory of my mother's voice saying my name.

Everything else about her remained—her hands grinding herbs, the smell of her cloak, the shape of her silence. But the specific music of her voice speaking *Kestrel*, the one sound in the world that had ever been mine before I was anyone's—gone. Lifted cleanly out of the room, carried off as payment, and I sat in the candlelight with my hand pressed over my mouth and understood that I had paid for my survival with the last living piece of her.

And beneath the grief, colder and quieter, a thought formed that I could not put down:

Someone had sent six deep-watch Wardens with a guild warrant and a map of my habits. That order had come from inside the palace—from Corvane, surely, or from whoever stood behind Corvane, the hand that had fed me poisons for ten years and stamped the spiral on a bottle of honey wine. They had decided I was no longer an asset to be cultivated. I was a vessel to be contained—or emptied.

And the Hollow had rung its bell the moment Ilan and I touched hands over the map. It knew we had met. Which meant it knew what we might do next, and something below the foundations—or above them—was moving to stop us before we could do it.

I blew out the candle. In the dark I packed one bag: the spiral map, the prophecy book, three days of food, and every charm I owned, including the sleeve-sewn sisters of the one I had just broken, each one a theft I hoped I'd never have to spend.

Then I climbed the stairs to the tannery yard, where a cartographer was waiting in the shadow of the drying sheds with his satchel of impossible drawings, because I had sent word, and because he had said he refused to draw any other ending.

"We're out of time," I told him. "They know what I am. Tonight they came for me, and next time they won't send six men—they'll send sixty, and they won't forget why." I looked north, toward the palace, where the towers stood black against a sky that had no business glowing the way it did, a faint sick shimmer along the roofline, like light leaking upward from somewhere far beneath the throne room. Like something down there turning over in its sleep and smiling.

"And Kestrel," I said, though he hadn't asked, "I think it's waking up hungry."
