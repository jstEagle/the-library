## 11. The Queen's Hunger

The chandelier weighed as much as a horse. Maren knew this because the master of works had told her so, twice, in the tone men used when they wanted her to understand that things above her head were heavy and permanent and not to be questioned. *Four hundred pounds of Vellish crystal and bronze, Your Majesty, anchored with chains that have held for a hundred years.*

She had lifted it anyway.

It happened in the long gallery on a gray morning, with only two chambermaids and a footman present, none of whom she had intended to impress or even notice. She had been practicing—she still called it practicing, though the word had begun to feel like a child's coat she'd outgrown—the small warm cantrips her mother had taught her at night, breath against her ear: *a light in the palm, a whisper carried across a room, a flower coaxed open out of season.* Her mother's voice had always sounded half-afraid even while teaching, like a woman passing along something precious and forbidden in equal measure. *Small things, Maren. Only small things. Magic is a door, and doors remember who opens them.*

But the small things had stopped being small.

The warmth gathered in her chest the way it always did now—eagerly, before she'd finished deciding—and when she raised one hand toward the chandelier, meaning nothing by it, meaning perhaps only to feel the shape of the power the way a musician touches a string without playing it, the great fixture rose. All four hundred pounds of it. It climbed off its chains with a sound like a held breath released, crystals shivering, throwing fractured light across the gallery walls, and it hung there turning slowly, obediently, beautifully, while Maren stood beneath it with her hand outstretched and her heart hammering not with fear but with something far more dangerous.

Joy.

Pure, unrationed, unsanctioned joy. Ten years of hiding her mother's lessons behind locked doors and muffled pillows, ten years of being the most watched girl in Vell, and here was proof that all of it—all the pretending, all the smallness—had been unnecessary. She could hold four hundred pounds aloft as easily as a thought. She could hold *more*. She could feel the more waiting, patient as deep water.

The chambermaids had dropped into curtsies so low they trembled. The footman had pressed himself against the wall. And Maren, nineteen years old and crowned too young, had looked at their faces—at the awe there, and beneath the awe, the first pale edge of terror—and understood that she should set the chandelier down gently and laugh and say something disarming, because a queen who frightened her own household was a queen alone.

Instead she let it turn a moment longer. Just a moment. Just to feel it.

Then she lowered it, and the chains caught, and everyone applauded—nervously, uncertainly, but they applauded, because what else does one do when a monarch performs a miracle before breakfast?—and Maren smiled and said, "You saw nothing," in a voice that made it a joke, and they laughed, and the moment sealed itself over like water closing after a dropped stone.

That was three days ago. She had not remembered doing it until Kestrel mentioned it.

* * *

"You levitated the chandelier."

Kestrel said it quietly, in the queen's private sitting room, while pouring the morning tea. She did it the way she reported everything—tastes, temperatures, the mood of the kitchens—with a flatness that made the extraordinary sound administrative. She was the only person in Vell who spoke to Maren without ornament, and Maren loved her for it, though lately the flatness had begun to feel like a wall she couldn't find the door in.

"Did I?" Maren kept her eyes on her cup. "I don't recall."

"You don't recall." Kestrel set down the pot. "Your Majesty, you lifted four hundred pounds of crystal into the air in front of your household and you don't recall."

"I've been tired." It was true. She was exhausted in a way sleep didn't touch, a weariness that lived somewhere below her ribs, as if something inside her were doing work all night that she paid for in the morning. "There are state matters. The envoy season. You know how it is—you taste everything and I sign everything, and neither of us sleeps."

Kestrel didn't smile. That was unusual. Kestrel almost always smiled, a small professional curve of the mouth, armor polished daily. Today her face was bare and it made Maren uneasy, the way an empty chair makes a room uneasy.

"May I ask you something strange?" Kestrel said.

"You may ask me anything. You know that."

"Have you been practicing magic?"

The word landed in the room like a dropped knife. Maren felt her whole body go still, the trained stillness of royalty, the stillness that had kept her alive through three regencies and one attempted coup. *Magic.* In this city, in this palace, where the Wardens walked the halls with their gray hoods and their iron senses, where her own mother had died whispering cantrips into a pillow—

"I don't know what you mean," Maren said.

Kestrel looked at her for a long moment. There was something in her face Maren had never seen before—not judgment, exactly. Grief, maybe. Or recognition. As if Kestrel were looking at a younger version of someone she had once known and failed.

"The tea is safe," Kestrel said finally. "I tasted it myself from the pot. But Your Majesty—" she hesitated, and Maren watched her decide not to finish whatever she'd meant to say. "Never mind. The honey wine arrived yesterday, from the southern envoy. I'll taste it before noon."

She left, and Maren sat alone with her cooling tea and the terrible, thrilling knowledge that she had lied to the only person she trusted, and that lying had become easy, and that *that* was the thing that frightened her—not the chandelier, not the power, but how little the lie had cost.

* * *

At night, the practice grew.

She told herself she would stop. Every morning she told herself, with the clean resolve of the newly repentant, *today I will not*, and every evening found her in the sealed solar at the top of the east tower with the shutters barred and the candles lit, doing—what? Not practicing. Practicing implied effort, and there was no effort anymore. The cantrips her mother had taught her, the ones that once left her trembling and nose-bleeding after a single candle-flame, now came like breathing. She lit every candle in the tower with a glance. She poured wine across the room into a cup. She unpicked a lock, mended a shattered plate, turned a page of a book she wasn't reading.

And then, last night, she had done the other thing. The new thing. The thing she hadn't decided to do, the way she hadn't decided to lift the chandelier.

She had floated.

Not high. A hand's width above the floor, in the dark, her hair drifting around her face as if underwater, her nightgown weightless, and the sensation had been so exquisite—like being forgiven for everything—that she had wept without knowing why, and then drifted back down, and gone to bed, and slept deeper than she had in months.

This morning she woke with dirt under her fingernails.

She stared at her hands in the gray light. Earth, dark and fine and smelling of deep places, packed under every nail, ground into the creases of her knuckles. She had bathed before bed. She always bathed before bed; it was a ritual her mother had taught her, *clean hands, clear mind*. There was no explanation. There was no memory.

She scrubbed her hands until they stung and told no one, and all through the day's audiences she felt the grit ghosting under her nails like a message written in a language she almost read.

* * *

The voice came on the fourth night.

She was in the solar again—she no longer pretended to herself that she might not go—and she was holding a candleflame in her open palm, letting it burn without burning, watching the light move through her skin like sun through honey, when she heard it.

Not with her ears. Deeper than ears. It came up through the floor, through the stone, through the bones of her feet and legs and spine, a voice like water speaking at the bottom of a well, vast and slow and patient, and it said:

*Open hand.*

Maren went cold. The flame guttered. "Who's there?"

*Open hand,* the voice said again, and it was not threatening—that was the worst of it. It was warm. It was the warmest thing she had ever heard, warmer than her mother's voice in memory, warmer than praise, warmer than the power itself. It said *open hand* the way one says *beloved*, and Maren felt her whole body lean toward the floor, toward the sound, like a plant toward light.

"Who are you?" she whispered.

*You opened,* the voice said—or she thought it said; the words came less as language than as weather, pressure and warmth shaping meaning in her mind. *You opened and opened. Small doors. So many small doors. Each one a sip. Come down, open hand. Come down and be filled.*

"I don't—" Her voice cracked. "I don't know any doors."

*Every spell,* the voice said, gentle as a mother settling a child, *is a door. And every door in this house opens downward.*

The candle went out. Not blown—extinguished, plucked from existence, and in the sudden dark Maren felt the presence withdraw, unhurried, satisfied, the way a lender leaves after extending credit. She stood shaking in the black solar with her heart slamming and her skin crawling and—she was ashamed of it even then, even in the terror—a terrible, humming want.

Because it had offered to fill her. And some part of her, some part she didn't recognize and couldn't disown, had wanted to say yes.

* * *

In the morning she sent for Kestrel and then, when Kestrel came, sent her away again without saying why. She couldn't explain it. What would she say? *A voice from under the floor calls me beloved*? The Wardens would come within the hour. Gray hoods, iron senses, and her mother's fate waiting at the end of them—whatever her mother's fate had actually been; Maren had never been told, only that the queen mother had died of a fever, quickly, and that Maren must never ask questions about the east tower.

The east tower. Where she practiced. Where her mother had taught her. Where the voice rose from below as if it had known her mother too—as if it had been *listening* to her mother, all those years of whispered lessons, drinking each small spell like a sip of wine.

*Each one a sip.*

Maren sat at her writing desk with her hands flat on the blotter, pressing down as if the desk might anchor her, and made herself think it plainly for the first time: *Something under the palace is feeding on my magic. Something under the palace knows my name. And I cannot stop casting, because I love it more than I have ever loved anything, and that is not an accident.*

Was it an accident? Had her mother taught her those cantrips out of love—or instruction? *Small things, Maren. Only small things.* A drip feed. A cultivation. Someone, sometime, had planted magic in the royal line the way a farmer plants a field, and Maren was the harvest coming ripe.

She thought of Kestrel's face yesterday, that naked grief, that recognition. *Have you been practicing magic?* Kestrel had asked it like a physician confirming symptoms. Like someone who already knew the disease.

Maren looked down at her hands. Clean now, scrubbed pink, resting on the blotter. But beneath the clean nails, in her imagination, the dark earth remained.

*Open hand,* the voice had called her. And she understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the name was not a compliment. An open hand gives. An open hand pours out. An open hand is how you pour anything from one vessel into another.

She was not the source of the power. She was the spout.

That afternoon, the southern envoy's gift arrived: a bottle of honey wine, unmarked, wrapped in plain cloth, delivered with a bow and a smile. Maren had it brought to her private rooms instead of the cellars—an impulse she didn't examine—and when Kestrel came at the appointed hour, Maren watched her old friend lift the bottle, uncork it, and taste, and Maren watched her face do something it had never done in ten years of service.

Nothing.

Kestrel swallowed, and stood perfectly still, and said, "It's sweet," in a voice like a woman reading her own eulogy, and Maren felt the floor tilt beneath everything she believed about her safe, ordered world.

"Kestrel?" Maren said. "What is it? What did you taste?"

And Kestrel, setting down the bottle with hands that were not quite steady, looking past Maren toward the window, toward the foundations, toward whatever waited below, said:

"Nothing, Your Majesty. I tasted nothing at all."
