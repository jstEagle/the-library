## 12. Below the Foundations

*Kestrel*

The spiral map did not want to be followed at night.

Ilan had warned me of this as we stood in the doorway of his workroom, the parchment rolled between us like something sleeping. "It's not that the way is harder after dark," he'd said, and his hands had done that thing they always did when he was nervous—turned over, palms up, as if presenting evidence to a court. "It's that the way is *different*. The tunnels shift. I've charted them three times this year and gotten three different maps."

"Then your map is wrong."

"My map is honest," he said. "Which is worse."

Now, an hour past midnight, I understood what he meant. We had entered through the old laundry chute behind the palace kitchens—a stone throat slick with condensation, wide enough for one body, too narrow for two to walk abreast—and already the corridor had betrayed us twice. Once it forked where Ilan's drawing showed solid wall. Once it simply stopped, a dead end faced in brick so old the mortar had gone the color of dried blood, and we had to double back through a passage that had not been there on our first pass. Ilan marked each turn in charcoal on a scrap of vellum, his movements quick and precise even here, especially here, because precision was how he prayed.

"You're afraid," I said. It wasn't a question. His fear had a smell I was learning—copper and cold air, like a coin held too long in the mouth.

"I'm a cartographer," he said. "I'm afraid of everything. That's why I'm good at my work." He glanced back at me, and in the guttering light of the tallow candle he carried, his face looked younger and older at once. "But this is a different kind of afraid. This is the feeling of being *expected*."

I knew that feeling intimately. I had been expected since before I could walk. Every meal set before me, every poison measured into my cup by careful guild hands—I had been expected, anticipated, prepared for, like a dish itself. Ten years of being someone's answer to a question I'd never been allowed to hear.

And now the tunnels were expecting me too.

We descended. The stairs were worn in their centers, hollowed by centuries of feet—the servant ways, the bones of the palace, the routes built for people whose names never appeared in any ledger worth keeping. My kind of architecture. I ran my palm along the wall as we went down, and the stone changed under my fingers: first the pale dressed granite of the upper levels, then rougher blocks, then something darker, denser, almost warm.

"Ilan." I stopped. "Touch this."

He pressed his hand flat against the wall beside mine. His breath came out slow.

"That's not residual heat," he said. "That's not a furnace flue or a hot spring. Stone doesn't hold warmth like this unless—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. Unless something was making it warm, continuously, patiently, the way a body makes itself warm.

We walked on. The candle flame leaned toward the wall as if drawn there, and the shadows it threw made the tunnel look ribbed, organic, like the inside of some enormous throat. I thought of all the dishes I had tasted. Nine courses at a state dinner, and beneath them, always, the tenth course no one named—the faint mineral nothing that sat under every flavor in the palace, the taste I had assumed was my own numbness. What if it had never been me? What if it was the water, the wine, the very salt of Vell, all of it seeping down from above and draining through these warm stones like rain through soil?

What if I had been tasting the Hollow my whole life, course by course, without knowing I was setting its table?

"Kestrel." Ilan's voice had gone quiet in the way it did when he was trying not to frighten me, which only frightened me more. "Look at the floor."

The flagstones had begun to bear marks. Not wear—*marks*. Thin lines cut into the stone, deliberate, curving, and when I crouched to trace one with my fingertip I felt the groove continue past the edge of the slab, onto the next, and the next. A line running the length of the passage. And then another line beside it. And another.

"It's one drawing," Ilan breathed. He was kneeling now, candle held low, his face lit gold from beneath like a man at prayer before an altar of darkness. "One continuous spiral. It starts somewhere behind us and it—" He turned, following the grooves with his eyes, then with his finger, then standing and walking backward down the tunnel with his hand trailing along the floor like a blind man reading. "It goes *down*. Kestrel, the whole palace is sitting on top of it. Every building I mapped, every foundation stone with the sigil—they're not copies of a pattern. They're *stitches*. Someone sewed the city shut over this."

"And now?" My voice sounded far away to me. "If it's sewn shut?"

Ilan looked up at me from the floor, and I saw him arrive at the same place I had already arrived, the way lovers sometimes reach the same terrible conclusion in silence, holding hands across it.

"Then someone has been unpicking the stitches," he said. "For a long time."

* * *

*Ilan*

There is a particular silence that lives underground, and I had spent my whole career learning silences—the hush of archives, the held breath of map rooms, the quiet of a city seen from above at dawn when it looks like a drawing not yet inked. But this silence was none of those. This silence was *occupied*. It pressed against my ears like water, and beneath it, so faint I could not swear to hearing it, was a rhythm. Slow. Vast. Patient as geology.

A heartbeat, if hearts beat once a minute.

Kestrel walked ahead of me now. She had been ahead of me since the marked floor, since the warmth, since her name—though she hadn't told me about the name yet. I saw it in her shoulders instead, in the way she carried herself like a woman walking toward a verdict. I loved her. I had loved her since the night market, since the moment I understood that the face I had drawn a hundred times in my sleep belonged to a real woman selling luck knots for small coin, and loving her had felt like the one true thing in a life increasingly made of false coordinates. Now even that love felt drafted, plotted, placed on the page by another hand. The prophecy had drawn us both. The question that gnawed at me with every step downward was whether it had merely described us—or *made* us.

The tunnel opened.

Not gradually. One moment we were stooping through a low crawl beneath a fallen lintel, and the next the ceiling rose away into darkness and our candlelight fell short of any wall. We stood in a space that should not have existed beneath the throne room—vast, circular, its floor a smooth sweep of that dark dense stone, unmarked here, polished as if by ten thousand years of attention. The air was warm and smelled of honey and ash together, a smell that reached into my chest and turned something over.

At the center of the chamber stood the door.

I say *door*, but I am a man who draws doors for a living and I have never drawn one like this. It was not set into a wall; the wall had been built *around* it, the dark stones fitted to its frame like flesh around a wound. It was taller than three men and shaped like a keyhole viewed head-on, and its surface was not wood, not metal, not stone—it moved slightly, like the skin of something breathing, and across it spiraled the sigil, carved deep, each groove packed with a metal gone black with age except where our light touched it and it gleamed like a vein of living silver.

And it was warm. I knew this before I touched it. I knew it the way you know a stove is hot from across a kitchen. The heat came off it in slow waves, carrying that honey-and-ash smell, and the rhythm I had heard in the tunnels was louder here—not sound exactly, but pressure, a pulse in the air that I felt in my teeth, in the soft tissue behind my eyes, in the scar on my knee that ached before rain.

"Older than the city," I said. My voice came out flattened, stripped of everything but fact, because fact was the rope I climbed hand over hand. "The oldest foundations in Vell are four hundred years. The chronicles say the first queen raised the palace on virgin rock. This isn't virgin rock. This was built *before*—and the city was built on top of it to hide it, or to hold it down, or—" I stopped. My hands were shaking. I watched them shake with a cartographer's detached interest, the way I'd watch a compass needle swing near iron. "Or both."

Kestrel hadn't spoken. She was standing closer to the door than I had seen her go, close enough that its heat must have been touching her face, and she was utterly still in the way that only she could be still—that taster's stillness, the stillness of a woman who has learned that the safest response to any unknown substance is to become, briefly, a stone.

"Kestrel," I said softly.

Her lips parted. When she spoke, her voice was strange—thin, wondering, scraped hollow.

"Ilan. It knows my name."

* * *

*Kestrel*

My mother died when I was six years old, in the winter of the gray fever, and I remember almost nothing of her voice. This is the cruelest theft of a poor childhood—not the hunger, not the cold, but the erosion of the small archive: the exact pitch of a lullaby, the way she said my name when I was in trouble versus when I was beloved. I have spent twenty years unable to reconstruct it. The guild gave me a new name anyway—Eastwick, a good guild name, a name like a locked drawer—and the girl called Kestrel before that became a rumor in my own memory.

So when the voice came through the door, I knew it instantly, completely, the way you know your own reflection.

"*Kestrel.*"

Warm. Low. Amused, almost, the way she used to be amused when I hid from bedtime and she found me in minutes because she always found me. It did not come from beyond the door the way sound comes through wood. It came from inside my skull and outside it simultaneously, from the warm air, from the pulse in the stones, from the dark behind my own eyes.

"Ilan." My voice sounded far away. "It knows my name."

He came to stand beside me, and I felt his hand find mine, cold fingers lacing through mine, anchoring me to the world of the living and the recent. The door breathed before us. The silver veins in the sigil brightened as I watched, dimming and brightening in time with that vast slow pulse, and I understood—with the certainty that has lived in my body longer than any thought, the certainty of the tongue, which knows poison before the mind consents to it—that the door was not speaking.

Something behind the door was speaking. The door was only its mouth.

"*Little taster,*" said my mother's voice, gentle as steam off milk. "*You came. You always come when called. You were made to come when called.*"

Every warning I owned rang at once. Corvane's voice in my memory: *Your immunity is not protection, child. It is cultivation.* The prophecy book's cramped century-old script: *the Taster of Deaths.* The honey wine that tasted of nothing. All of it converging here, in the warm dark, on this door, on me.

"What do you want?" I asked. It was the wrong question and I knew it even as I asked it, because things like this do not want; they *offer*. I had read enough of the torn prophecy's edges, heard enough of Corvane's cold liturgies, to know the shape of what waited.

The voice laughed, and it was my mother laughing, and it was also nothing like my mother at all—it was layered, many-voiced, a choir compressed into one throat, and underneath the tenderness ran a note of such vast tired hunger that my knees nearly folded.

"*Come and see what I keep,*" it said. "*Come and taste. You are the only one left who can taste Me truly. They fed you to Me spoon by spoon, little bird, and you never once flinched. Do you know how lonely that made Me? A hundred years of meals and no one to share them with.*"

Beside me, Ilan whispered, "Don't answer it."

But I was already answering. Not with words. With the thing the guild had spent ten years building in me without my consent: I opened my awareness the way I opened it over every dish, letting the flavor of the air come in—the honey, the ash, the old old grief of it, the patience, the *appetite*—and what I tasted made me weep without deciding to, tears cutting warm tracks down my cold face.

Because beneath the hunger, beneath the choir of stolen voices, I tasted a person. Remnants of one. A woman who had swallowed her own power until it swallowed her, who had been sealed here by the ones she trusted, who had spent a century becoming a hunger because hunger was the only shape they left her.

The first queen. The monster with a history. And she spoke in my mother's voice because she had reached into me and taken the dearest thing I'd lost and put it on like a dress.

"You're not her," I said aloud. My voice cracked and steadied, the way it does over a poisoned cup. "You don't get to wear her."

The pulse stuttered. For one heartbeat—one vast geological heartbeat—the warmth faltered, and the door's surface rippled like the skin of disturbed water, and the voice that came next was not amused at all.

"*No,*" it agreed, soft and terrible. "*But you will come to Me anyway. The stitches are out, little bird. The cartographer has already begun to open what he was drawn to open. Ask him what he dreams of now.*"

I turned to Ilan. His face had gone the color of candle wax, and his free hand—the one not gripping mine—was pressed flat against his own chest, over his heart, as though holding something in.

"Ilan," I said. "What does the door mean, *begun*?"

He didn't answer. Behind him, in the smooth dark floor of the ancient chamber, thin lines of silver light were rising through the stone—spiraling outward from the base of the door, slow and deliberate, tracing a pattern I had seen once before in a book under the floorboards of the taster's quarters.

The final page. The torn one. The ending, drawing itself in light on the floor at our feet.

And at its center, exactly where the pattern would place it, stood Ilan—his shadow cast long and keyhole-shaped against the breathing door.
