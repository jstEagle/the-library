## 13. The Torn Page

The Tasters' Guild kept its secrets the way a miser keeps coins: counted nightly, touched often, and never spent where anyone could see.

Kestrel learned this over six nights of watching. She had told Ilan she needed time, and that was true—but time was also a weapon, and she had always known how to sharpen it. Ten years in Vell taught her that no lock was ever really locked; it was only a door someone had grown bored of guarding. So she watched Master Corvane's habits the way she once watched for poison in wine: patiently, hungrily, noting every ritual until the ritual became a map.

He left the guildhall at half past nine each night, always by the eastern stair, always with two of his inner circle flanking him like crows escorting something they didn't trust not to fly away. He returned before dawn. The safe in his study—a squat iron thing set into the wall behind a tapestry of the Sealing of Vell, because men like Corvane loved to hide their sins behind their own mythology—was opened twice daily: once at dusk, once at dawn. Between those hours it belonged to whoever was bold enough and quiet enough to take what it held.

She went on the seventh night, when rain came down the way it did only three or four times a year in Vell—hard, drumming, drowning the sound of footsteps on cobblestone. She wore grey. She carried nothing but a strip of waxed leather, a sliver of iron filed thin as a fingernail, and the small honest magic she had never sold to anyone: a charm to make her footsteps taste like silence.

The guildhall's night porter knew her. That was the beauty of being small; doors opened for women who were no one. He nodded her through with the vague courtesy owed to a colleague, already half-asleep, and she climbed the western stair where the tallow candles burned low and the walls sweated cold.

Corvane's study smelled of ink and old paper and, beneath it, faintly, of the deep-earth smell she had come to know from the tunnels beneath the throne room—the smell of the sealed door, warm and mineral, like a stone that had been held too long in a living hand. Her stomach turned. Even here, even behind iron, the Hollow's breath had soaked into everything the guild touched.

The tapestry came away from the wall easier than she expected. Dust ghosted down. The safe's dial was worn smooth at three numbers—the ones Corvane touched every day without thinking—and Kestrel had watched his hands long enough to know them. She turned the dial the way he did: quick to the first number, slow through the second, a little hitch of hesitation before the third, because Corvane was an old man whose fingers had begun to doubt themselves.

The door swung open with a sound like a sigh.

Inside: ledgers. A lacquered box that hummed faintly against her fingertips when she brushed it—warded, and warded well; she left it alone. A bundle of letters tied with black cord, stamped with the spiral sigil. And at the back, folded into quarters and wrapped in oilcloth gone brittle with age, a single page.

Her hands were steady. Ten years of tasting had trained her hands above all things; they did not shake over cups, over vials, over the bodies of the poisoned. But her heart was hammering against her ribs like something trying to get out, and she made herself breathe—in through the nose, out slow—before she unwrapped the oilcloth and carried the page to the candlelight.

The paper was older than the book. She knew this the way a taster knows a vintage: by the weight of it, the color, the way it drank the light. Whoever had torn it from the prophecy book had done so a century ago, and had kept it hidden ever since, because it was the one page that could not be allowed to circulate.

The handwriting was the same as the rest of the book—that looping, urgent script—but the ink here was different. Darker. Stained, in places, with something brownish that might have been age and might have been blood.

*And at the last,* she read, *when the door is opened by the Hollow Man who does not know his own hand, the Taster of Deaths shall stand before the dark that wears my face.*

Kestrel went very still.

*Wears my face.*

*Let the guilds record it truly, though they will burn these words rather than carry them: I was Vell's first queen, and I am the thing beneath its stones. My magic grew as all magic grows, by feeding, and I fed it on myself when there was nothing else, and it ate me hollow and walked wearing my shape, and what they buried under the palace is not a monster. It is me. It is what remains when a person is consumed entirely by her own hunger for power.*

*The Taster swallows the dark, and the dark learns her name.*

*This is the mercy I could not give myself. Tell her—tell the girl who is not yet born, who will be made for this as I was made for crowning—that the swallowing is not death. It is a keeping. Someone must hold what cannot be destroyed, and hold it forever, and be neither saved nor damned but simply full.*

*I am sorry. I chose you the way my hunger chose me.*

The candle guttered. Rain lashed the window. Kestrel stood in Corvane's study holding a dead woman's apology and felt the floor tilt beneath her—not the floor, she realized distantly, but the whole architecture of her hatred.

She had hated the Hollow. She had hated it cleanly, the way you hate a rat in the grain or rot in a wall: as a thing, a threat, an *it*. She had imagined it beneath the palace coiled and hungry, some ancient appetite wearing the shapes of the dead to lure the living closer. When it spoke to her through the sealed door in her mother's voice, she had understood it as mimicry—cruel, clever, empty.

But it wasn't empty. It wasn't mimicry.

It was a woman. It had been a woman, a queen, a girl raised for a crown the way Kestrel had been raised for a table—bred for a purpose, fed to a function, consumed by the very power everyone told her was hers. *My magic grew as all magic grows, by feeding.* Maren's cantrips draining into the foundations. The guild's ban on magic, not to destroy it but to monopolize it, to starve everything else so the thing below would have only one mouth to feed through. And the first queen, standing in her own palace, feeling herself being eaten from the inside, writing warnings backward across a century to a girl who did not yet exist—

*I chose you the way my hunger chose me.*

"No," Kestrel said aloud, softly, to the empty room. "You didn't choose me. They did. There's a difference."

But even as she said it, she heard how thin the difference was. Corvane had chosen her bloodline. The guild had chosen her childhood, her poisons, her numbness spreading year by year across her tongue like frost claiming a windowpane. But the prophecy—the prophecy had been written by the Hollow itself, or by the woman the Hollow had been, and either way Kestrel had been named in it before her grandmother was born. Chosen by a corpse. Addressed by a hunger. Loved, maybe, in the terrible way that a drowning woman loves the stranger she grabs for.

Grief arrived without warning, the way grief always did—as a physical fact, a pressure behind the eyes, a tightness in the throat like a swallowed stone. She grieved for the first queen, who had sat on Maren's throne and felt her own self being devoured bite by bite and had no one to tell. She grieved for the girl she had been, the orphan brought to the guildhall at six years old, told she was special, told she was protected, fed carefully measured doses of death so that death would learn to live inside her. She grieved, absurdly, for Corvane—for whatever young man he had been before the institution calcified around him, before mercy became a ledger entry and duty became a wall he hid his sins behind.

And underneath the grief, colder and more durable than any of it, purpose settled into place like a bone knitting true.

The monster had a history. Which meant the monster had a humanity still buried somewhere inside it, the way a seed sleeps in ash. Which meant that when Kestrel swallowed the dark—if she swallowed the dark—it would not be like swallowing poison. It would be like swallowing a person. It would be a keeping, the page said. *Neither saved nor damned but simply full.*

She thought of Ilan's face when he'd shown her the spiral map, his hands trembling, saying *I think I'm meant to open something, and I'm more afraid of what happens if I refuse.* She thought of Maren at nineteen, levitating chandeliers to applause she couldn't remember, hearing a voice from below call her *the open hand.* She thought of the condemned prisoners the guild kept in cells beneath the guildhall, the ones Corvane intended to feed to the Hollow in her place if she refused.

One way or another, someone was going to be full.

She folded the page along its century-old creases, wrapped it back in the oilcloth, and slid it inside her shirt, against her skin, where it lay warm as a second heartbeat.

That was when the safe's lacquered box began to hum louder.

* * *

She had missed it. Of course she had missed it—she had been looking at the page, reading a dead queen's confession, and the box had been humming quietly all along, patient as everything else in this city, and now it was not quiet anymore.

Kestrel assessed the room in the space of a breath, the way she'd been trained: exits, light, weight of the door, distance to the window. Two floors up. Rain-slick roof tiles beyond glass that hadn't been cleaned in a decade. The stair she'd come by, and the eastern stair Corvane used, and somewhere below, the night porter who knew her face and would remember it now whether he wanted to or not.

The box stopped humming.

In the silence that followed, she heard footsteps on the eastern stair. Unhurried. Familiar. A man who had left at half past nine every night for six nights had, on the seventh, come back early—and she understood, with the cold clarity of a taster identifying a flavor she should have caught sooner, that he had wanted her to come. That the safe's worn dial and the dozing porter and the tapestry hanging a finger's width too far from the wall had all been arranged the way a table is laid for a guest.

Corvane opened the door without knocking, because it was his door.

He looked exactly as he always looked: silver-bearded, immaculate, his guild chain resting on his chest like a sleeping serpent. But his eyes went immediately—to the open safe, to the empty space at the back of it where the oilcloth bundle had been—and something moved through his face that was not anger. It took her a moment to name it, because she had never seen it there before.

Relief.

"You read it," he said. Not a question. His voice was quieter than she had ever heard it, stripped of the lecture-hall authority he wore like armor. "I wondered if you would. I wondered if you'd dare."

"Give me one reason not to scream for the Wardens right now," Kestrel said. "You're an old man. I'm faster than you."

"The Wardens are mine, child. They have always been mine; the ban was our founding act." He crossed the room slowly, joints cracking, and lowered himself into the chair behind his desk as if the conversation were a meal he intended to eat properly, course by course. "Sit down, Kestrel."

"I'll stand."

"Of course you will." He studied her the way he had studied her since she was six years old—not with affection, exactly, but with the proprietary attention of a man examining a vintage he had blended himself. "You have questions. You've had them since you found the book under your floor. Ask them."

She should have asked about the breeding program. About her mother. About ten years of dishes and what each one had fed. Those questions were lined up inside her like arrows in a quiver.

Instead she heard herself ask the one that mattered most, the one the page had put there:

"She wrote it. The first queen. She wrote the whole prophecy—the Hollow Man, the Taster of Deaths, all of it. Why would the thing beneath the palace write instructions for its own imprisonment?"

Corvane's hands folded together on the desk. For a long moment the only sound was the rain, drumming its thousand small fingers against the glass.

"Because it isn't a thing," he said finally. "Because it remembers being a queen. Because every year it gets hungrier and less human, and some part of it—the part that scratched warnings into its own prison walls—is terrified of what it's becoming." His eyes lifted to hers, and she saw the fear beneath the institutional calm at last, the fear that had been running the guild for a hundred years like water running beneath ice. "We have kept it fed, Kestrel. Carefully. Measured doses, measured centuries. And now the queen above us is tearing holes in the work of a hundred years, and the Wardens are seizing charm-sellers they were ordered to leave alone, and someone in this palace wants you contained before you can do what that page says you can do." He leaned forward. "So yes. Sit down. Because you are going to want to hear the offer I came back to make you—and you are going to want to be sitting when you refuse it."

Outside, the rain fell harder, and beneath the rain, beneath the guildhall, beneath the whole hungry city, Kestrel felt—or imagined she felt—the faint warmth of a sealed door, and a voice that was not her mother's after all, but a dead queen's, whispering the only word it had left:

*Soon.*
