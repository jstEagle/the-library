## 14. Loyalty's Price

Master Corvane had always believed that mercy was a matter of arithmetic.

He had learned it young, in the guild's counting rooms, where the ledgers of the Tasters' Guild were kept in ink that never faded and hands that never trembled. Every death prevented was a column. Every death permitted was a column. The art of governance—true governance, the kind no crown ever admitted to—was ensuring the first column always outweighed the second. He had spent forty years making certain of it. Forty years of small, necessary cruelties, each one entered neatly into the book, each one justified by the sum at the bottom of the page.

Tonight the sum had grown very large indeed.

He stood in his study above the Tasting Hall, where the guild's oldest records slept behind a wall of false brick, and watched the city through leaded glass. Vell at night was a thing of grey roofs and guttered lamps, smoke rising from ten thousand chimneys like the breath of some vast sleeping animal. Somewhere down there, Kestrel Eastwick was running. The Wardens' report had come an hour past: she had slipped the net at the night market, vanished into the warren of streets below the Salt Quarter, and taken with her a charm that erased memory from the minds of three grown men. Three Wardens, veterans all, now standing in a doorway with soft smiles and empty eyes, asking passersby politely if they had seen themselves.

Corvane should have been furious. Instead he felt something colder and more familiar: the particular exhaustion of a man watching a machine he had built begin to turn against its maker.

*She was never meant to run,* he thought. *She was meant to be found.*

The fire in the grate had burned down to a red heart. On his desk lay the things he would need: the guild's seal in black wax, a vial of taster's cordial—the old recipe, the true one, sweetened with poppy and laced with enough sedative to quiet a horse—and beneath both, wrapped in oilcloth, the torn page.

He did not look at it yet. Looking at it too often was a weakness he permitted himself only on the worst nights, and this was not yet the worst. The worst would come when the Hollow woke fully, when the foundations of the palace began to sing, when the spiral burned itself into the sky above Vell and every soul in the city understood, all at once, what had been sleeping beneath their feet for a hundred years.

A knock at the door. Two soft raps, then one—the guild's rhythm, older than any password.

"Enter."

Sister Halloway came in with the silence of long practice. She was sixty, grey as ash, with hands that had held more dying men than any priest in Vell. She had been the one who carried Kestrel Eastwick out of the birthing house twenty-three years ago, who had swaddled her in guild wool and whispered the first lie over her small head: *you are an orphan; you are lucky; you are chosen.* Corvane had written that lie himself. It had seemed gentle, at the time.

"They've lost her," Halloway said. "Truly lost her, this time. And there's worse." She set a folded paper on the desk. "The queen."

Corvane read it standing. The Warden inside the palace—a man named Doss, reliable until recently—reported that Queen Maren had levitated the coronation chandelier before the full court, forty pounds of gilded bronze hanging in the air like a caught bird, while the girl smiled and did not remember doing it afterward. That she had begun to speak to herself in a voice not entirely her own. That the palace dogs refused to enter the east wing, where the foundations ran deepest.

"The Hollow has found its open hand," Corvane said quietly.

"It was always going to. You said so yourself, Master. The prophecy gives us two figures and a door. We have the door—" Halloway's eyes flicked, unwillingly, toward the oilcloth bundle. "And we have the Taster. What we do not have is time."

"No," Corvane agreed. "We do not have time."

He crossed to the false brick and pressed the third stone from the floor, left of center, exactly as his predecessor had taught him and his predecessor's predecessor before that. The wall exhaled dust and swung inward. Cold air moved against his face, air that had not touched a living lung in years, and beyond it the guild's oldest records waited in their shelves: the founding charter, the breeding registries, the ledgers of fed deaths going back a century in unbroken columns.

He took out the registry marked with the double spiral and laid it open on his desk beside the torn page. Two documents, side by side. The promise and the price.

"You disagree with what I'm about to do," he said. It was not a question. Halloway had been disagreeing with him for thirty years, silently, the way stone disagrees with water.

"I think," she said, "that you are about to offer that girl a bargain in which every coin is hers to pay and every benefit ours to keep. And I think you will call it mercy while you do it. And I think she will see through it in half a heartbeat, because she has survived ten years in this city by seeing through people like us."

"And if she refuses?"

Halloway was quiet a moment. In the fire's last light her face looked carved from the same grey stone as the city itself.

"Then we take her," she said. "And you will have to live with having offered first. That is the difference between us, Master. You need the ritual to be clean. I only need it done."

Corvane almost smiled. Almost. "Bring me the prisoner list."

* * *

They brought Kestrel Eastwick to him at dawn, or rather she allowed herself to be brought, which was not the same thing and which Corvane noted with the weary respect of one craftsman observing another. The Wardens had found her at the Salt Gate an hour before sunrise—or she had let them find her, standing in the pale light with her hood back and her hands visible, as though surrender were simply another dish she had decided to taste.

They sat across from each other in the guild's judgment room, a chamber designed centuries ago to make visitors feel small: a table of black oak worn smooth by generations of nervous palms, walls hung with the portraits of dead Masters, windows of thick green glass that turned daylight the color of pond water. Corvane had conducted a hundred interrogations here. This was not an interrogation. He made sure the room said so—he had the guards dismissed, the door left unlocked, wine poured into two cups instead of one.

Kestrel noticed everything. Of course she did. She sat with her spine straight and her hands loose in her lap, a small woman with a forgettable face and unforgettable eyes, and she looked at the unlocked door, and the two cups, and the ledger lying closed at his elbow, and Corvane watched her assemble the truth of the room piece by piece, the way she must once have assembled the flavors of a poisoned soup.

"You wanted me found," she said. No greeting. Her voice was low and even, a voice built for saying nothing loudly.

"I wanted you alive," Corvane said. "Found was a means to that end."

"Were the Wardens with the empty eyes also a means?"

"A regrettable cost." He pushed one cup toward her. "Willowine. From my own cellar. If I meant to poison you, Kestrel, I promise you would never know until it was already done. That is rather the whole of our craft."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, but the ghost of where a smile might have lived, once, in a different life. She did not touch the cup. He hadn't expected her to.

"Then say what you brought me here to say," she said. "I've read your prophecy, Master Corvane. I've read your registry—I found the cache under the taster's quarters, and I found what you hid in the safe, because you taught me to look, all those years of tasting lessons. *Look for what isn't there,* you used to say. *The poison is always in what's missing.* So don't dress it up. Tell me what you want."

Corvane had rehearsed this conversation eleven times in the dark hours, walking the length of his study, testing words the way a taster tests a spoon. He had prepared arguments, appeals, threats. Now, facing her, he found he wanted none of them. There was a strange relief in speaking plainly to someone who could taste a lie through three courses and a change of subject.

"What I want," he said, "is for the Hollow to stay asleep. Everything else—all of it, the ban, the guild, the breeding program, your whole life arranged like a garden bed—everything was in service of that single purpose. You may hate the purpose. You may hate me. But understand that the alternative to our arithmetic is not freedom. It is the first queen. It is a city of ash with a hole in the middle of it where a hundred thousand souls used to be."

"I know what the Hollow was," Kestrel said. "I read the torn page. She was a person. She loved her city, and her magic ate her, and your founders sealed what was left under the throne room and called it order." Her voice did not rise. That was the terrible thing about her; even her rage came served cold, portioned, controlled. "So tell me the deal, Corvane. You didn't spend a vial of sedative and three bribed Wardens to give me a history lesson."

He opened the ledger.

Inside, in his own precise hand, were names. Forty-one names, each with a date of condemnation, a crime, a sentence. Criminals of the deepest cells: murderers, poisoners, men who had sold children to slavers, women who had burned orphanages for insurance money. He had chosen them carefully over the past two days, and he hated how carefully, and he hated more that the hating changed nothing.

"The guild has a ritual," he said. "Older than the ban. Older than the sealing, in its bones—it was how the first binding was anchored, a century ago. A vessel must be prepared. Blood opened, the way yours was opened, over years. The Hollow does not transfer to an unwilling body easily—but to a *prepared* body, a body trained to hold death without breaking, it can be decanted. Poured from one vessel into another, like wine between cups."

Kestrel had gone very still. Stillness, in her, was louder than shouting. "You want to pour it out of me."

"There is nothing in you to pour out. Not yet. The Hollow reaches for you through the door Ilan Voss will open—that is written, that is fixed, the dreams have already begun to pull him. When the door opens, it will offer itself to everyone present, and it will take the shape of whatever they most desire, and most of them will say yes, and those who say yes will be eaten from the inside out. Including the queen. Including, if you stand in that room, *you*—because you are the vessel it was bred for, Kestrel. Your blood calls to it the way a bell calls to a hammer. Unless."

"Unless."

"Unless we fill the vessel first." He turned the ledger so she could read the names. "We choose one of these. A condemned prisoner. We prepare them—weeks, perhaps less, using methods the guild perfected on you, though compressed. And when the door opens, the ritual draws the Hollow into *them* instead of into you. Into a murderer. Into a monster who has already forfeited his place among the living. The door is sealed with the Hollow bound in a cage that costs the world nothing, and you walk away. You walk away, Kestrel. Alive. Free. With the man you love, if he survives the sealing, and I give you my word I will spend everything I have to make certain he does."

The green light lay across the table between them like stagnant water. Kestrel looked down at the ledger. He watched her read the names—one, then another, then a third—and he watched her finger stop on the fourth line, and he knew which name she had found, because he had known she would find it.

"Tomas Rill," she said quietly.

"A poisoner. He killed six people in the Lantern District. Three of them children."

"He killed six people because the guild paid him to test their poisons, and then condemned him for it when the testing was done." Her eyes came up. They were not angry. They were worse than angry—they were *knowing*, and being known by her felt like being tasted. "You've done this before, haven't you? Not the ritual. The arithmetic. You find a man who is already damned, or you make him damned, and then you spend him, and you sleep at night because the ledger balances."

Corvane did not flinch. Flinching was for men with something left to lose. "Every life in this city rests on that ledger. Yours included. The bread you ate this morning, the roof over your stall, the ten years you survived in Vell—all of it exists because someone was willing to make entries like these and bear the weight of them. That is what loyalty is, Kestrel. Not love. Not flags and anthems. Loyalty is doing the sum honestly and paying what it costs."

"And what does it cost Tomas Rill?"

"Everything. As it would have cost him anyway, within the year, at the end of a rope." Corvane leaned forward, and for the first time he let her see the thing beneath the Master's calm—not warmth, nothing so dishonest as warmth, but the genuine conviction that had carried him through forty years of necessary darkness. "You think I am damning an innocent. Look at the list. Read the crimes. These are not innocents. These are men and women who chose cruelty, freely, repeatedly, and were sentenced for it by courts that never knew what they were truly being saved *for*. If the Hollow must wear someone, let it wear the guilty. If a cage must be built of flesh, let it be flesh that the world had already thrown away. That is not evil, Kestrel. That is the only mercy large enough to cover a city."

For a long moment she said nothing. The fire ticked in the grate. Somewhere above them, the Tasting Hall was waking—apprentices lighting lamps, novices carrying trays, the great machinery of the guild beginning another day of pretending it was merely a service to the crown.

"You believe that," she said at last, and there was something almost gentle in it, which frightened him more than hatred would have. "You actually believe it. That's the horror of you, Master Corvane. You're not a monster. You're a good steward of a monstrous thing, and you've spent so long balancing the books that you've forgotten the books are written in blood." She turned the ledger back toward him and closed it with one finger. "Here is my counter-offer. No prisoner. No ritual. You give me the torn page, the registry, and every name in your breeding records—every child, every mother, every grave—and I will decide what my body is for. Mine. Not the guild's. Not the prophecy's. Mine."

"And if the Hollow takes you?" Corvane asked. "If it pours into you and wears you as the first queen was worn? Who decides then? Who tastes *your* death?"

"That," said Kestrel Eastwick, rising, "is my problem. You taught me that too. *The taster dies alone, ahead of everyone, so no one else has to.* I finally understand the lesson, Corvane. I just refuse to let you choose the menu."

She walked to the door. Unlocked, as promised. She paused with her hand on the iron ring, and did not look back.

"For what it's worth," she said, "I believe you think this is mercy. That's why I'm not going to kill you. But hear me clearly: I will not be poured into a ledger. And neither will Tomas Rill. Whatever happens under the palace—you'll watch me choose, and it won't be the choice you wrote for me."

The door closed behind her. The Wardens in the corridor, per his instructions, let her pass. Let her go to him—to the cartographer, to the dreams, to the door. Because Corvane had counted the hours remaining, and they were fewer than the days, and a man who has spent his life doing sums knows when the numbers have stopped favoring him.

He sat alone in the green light with the closed ledger and the torn page and the two untouched cups of wine, and he told himself, as he had told himself ten thousand times, that the sum still balanced.

Then he rang the bell for Sister Halloway, and gave the order he had sworn he would never give.

"If she will not choose the vessel," Master Corvane said, "then prepare Tomas Rill anyway. And ready the guild's men for the feast. If the door opens, we open it on *our* terms—or we bury the terms, the door, and everyone standing near them."

Above him, faint through the floors, the palace bells began to ring the hour—and beneath the ringing, so low that only a man who had listened his whole life for it could hear, the foundations of Vell began, very softly, to sing.
