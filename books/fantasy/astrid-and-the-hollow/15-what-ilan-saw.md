## 15. What Ilan Saw

The defector had chosen the drowned chapel.

Ilan understood why the moment he descended into it. The old district beneath the Salt Quarter had been abandoned a century ago when the river changed its mind and swallowed three streets whole, and the chapel of some god whose name no one now remembered stood half-submerged in black water, its bell tower leaning like a drunk against the dark. The air smelled of mineral rot and old incense, and every sound he made came back to him softened, as if the water were listening too. No Warden patrol would come here. No one came here at all, except things that preferred not to be seen.

He carried a lantern with the shutter drawn down to a coin's width of light, and in his satchel, wrapped in oilcloth, he carried the spiral map—the one that had cost him four sleepless nights and, he suspected, whatever remained of his ordinary life. The lines converged beneath the throne room. He had known that for weeks. He had told no one but Kestrel, and telling Kestrel had felt less like a choice than like a door opening in him that had always been there, waiting for her hand.

*You are meant to open something,* she had said, and he had not contradicted her, because the dreams had already told him the same thing in a voice that was not quite a voice.

A ripple moved at the edge of the lantern's thin blade of light.

"You're early," said a woman's voice, low and roughened, coming from the direction of the ruined font. "Cartographers are never early. You people live by your measurements."

"I measured wrong," Ilan said. "I left an hour ago."

"Then you measured right." She stepped into view.

She was older than he'd expected—fifty, perhaps, with grey shot through her cropped hair and a Warden's coat worn inside-out so the sigil faced her own heart. Her left hand ended at the wrist in a smooth cap of scarred leather. She moved without favoring it, which told him the loss was old and long since priced into her body. Her eyes did the work her hand could not: they went over him once, thoroughly, the way a customs officer goes over cargo.

"Ilana Vess," she said. "Formerly Senior Archivist, Third Circle Wardens. Formerly a great many things." She almost smiled. "You can call me Vess. Everyone does, eventually, usually while apologizing."

"Ilan Voss."

"I know." She turned and waded toward the far wall, boots sending slow rings across the flooded floor. "Your name is in our files, cartographer. Has been since you took the commission to survey the palace undercrofts. Do you know how many men have surveyed those undercrofts in the last hundred years?"

"I'd guess—"

"Four. And two of them retired to farms very far away, and one of them drowned in a cistern that was later found to be dry." She glanced back at him. "You are the fourth. I would like you to remain alive, which is why we are meeting in a room full of water where no one can listen through the walls. Sound doesn't carry well over standing water. Neither does magic, if it comes to that. The old builders knew what they were doing."

"What is it you want to show me?"

Vess reached the wall, where a stone shelf jutted above the waterline—once a place for votive candles, perhaps—and lifted a flat case from it, wrapped in waxed canvas gone stiff with age and damp. She set it on the shelf between them like a card player laying down a hand.

"The Wardens keep records," she said. "That surprises people. They think of us as swords and torches, door-kickers and burners. But a ban is only as strong as its paperwork. Every charm confiscated, every practitioner named, every execution ordered—it all goes into the archive beneath the Wardenhouse. Eleven floors underground. I spent nineteen years down there." She worked at the canvas wrappings with her single hand, unhurried. "Nineteen years reading about small crimes. A grandmother's fever ward. A boy who could make milk keep. And then, eight months ago, I was assigned to a restricted inventory—a recataloguing of materials seized from the Tasters' Guild during the succession crisis, back before Queen Maren's mother died. Things the guild had held for a century. Things no one had looked at since."

She peeled the last of the canvas away.

Inside the case, packed in wool gone grey, lay a book.

Ilan's breath went out of him slowly. He knew that book. He had held a copy of it—or what he had believed was a copy—in Kestrel's taster's quarters, had watched her turn pages that described this very season, this very year, a man with his face, a woman called *the Taster of Deaths*. He had watched her close it with hands that shook.

"That's—" he began.

"A fragment," Vess said. "Not the book itself. The book you saw is real; I've read the inventory entries. But this—" she opened the cover with a care that seemed almost devotional, "—this is the draft. Or part of one. Sealed inside a lead-lined strongbox with a guild seal I've only ever seen on documents signed by the first queen herself. And cartographer, I need you to look at the hand."

She angled the book toward his shuttered lantern. The light fell across vellum gone the color of weak tea, covered in a fine, slanted script—and beneath the script, sketches. Spirals. Dozens of them, drawn in the margins with the obsessive repetition of someone working out a problem they could not put down. And among the spirals, faces.

Ilan felt the floor tilt under him, or imagined it did.

One of the faces was his own.

Not approximately. Not the way prophecy speaks in riddles and symbols, a bearded king standing in for any king. It was *his* face—the particular set of his brow, the small asymmetry of his mouth, the way his hair grew back from the temple. Drawn with the confidence of a hand that had looked at that face many times. Dated, in the margin, in numerals a century old.

"I don't understand," he said, and heard how young his voice sounded in the drowned chapel.

"No," Vess agreed. "Neither do I. And I have read everything the archive has on the Hollow, the founding of the ban, and the first queen, which is more than anyone alive can say. So let me tell you what I *do* understand, and then you can tell me whether you want to hear the rest."

She turned pages with her fingertips, gentle as touching a sleeping animal.

"The first queen's name was Sarelle. That much survives in the public record, though barely—a statue in the Hall of Founders with the face weathered blank, a mention in two charters. What doesn't survive, because the Wardens buried it along with everything else, is how she died. The official histories say fever. The guild's private histories say childbirth. The truth, according to documents I was not authorized to read and read anyway, is that she went down beneath the palace with a company of masons and sealed something into the foundations, and did not come back up. The masons came back. She didn't. And within a generation, the new dynasty outlawed magic, founded the Wardens, and built a guild of poison-tasters whose true function—" her mouth twisted, "—was never written down anywhere. Except, apparently, in prophecy."

"And the prophecy was written by her."

"By her hand. Before she went down. That's what the dating says, that's what the seal says, that's what the ink analysis my last friend in the archive performed before he stopped answering my letters says." Vess closed the book softly. "Here is what keeps me awake, cartographer. A warning sent backward makes sense. She knew what was below. She knew it would reach up through the centuries, through the foundations, through bloodlines and bread and honey wine. So she wrote down what was coming, and hid it where the right person might find it. A message in a bottle, thrown upstream through time." She paused. "But a trap laid forward also makes sense. Because think about what the prophecy actually does. It names a man—you—who will open a door. It names a woman—her—who will swallow a darkness. It gives instructions. And instructions, in my experience, are how you get people to do exactly what you want them to do."

"You think she wanted the door opened."

"I think," Vess said, "that a century ago, a dying queen wrote a book that has now caused a taster to find a hidden cache, a cartographer to draw a map, a guild master to begin moving pieces, and a Hollow to stir in its sleep after a hundred years of quiet. Everything the book describes is happening. Either she foresaw all of it—which is a kind of magic the ban exists to prevent—or she arranged all of it, which is a kind of magic the ban exists to prevent. Either way, cartographer, we are living inside her design. The only question is whether the design ends with the door shut or the door open. And the torn page—" she tapped the book's ragged final leaf, "—the torn page is where that answer lived."

Ilan stared at the water. In the lantern's sliver of light it lay perfectly still, holding their reflections upside down, two figures standing in a sky.

"Kestrel thinks Corvane has the missing page," he said. "His safe. She's going to take it."

"Then she's braver than I am, and I lost a hand to these people." Vess's voice flattened. "Listen to me. I didn't come here tonight to give you a history lesson. I came because in eight months of reading, I found one more thing, and I couldn't decide whether to burn it or bring it to you, and I've decided that deciding is a luxury I no longer have."

She reached into her coat and drew out a slim folder of loose papers, copied in her own cramped hand—originals, he guessed, stayed eleven floors beneath the Wardenhouse.

"The guild's feeding schedule," she said. "A hundred years of it. Doses, intervals, bloodline notations. Every dish Kestrel Eastwick ever tasted is recorded in these pages—not what was in the dishes, but what the dishes *did*. What drained out of her and down. The guild calls it tithe. They've been farming her since she was six years old, cartographer, with the patience of orchard-keepers. And the last entry—the entry made nine days ago—is annotated in Corvane's own hand. One word."

She laid the folder open on the stone shelf beside the book.

*Ripening.*

The word sat there in the lantern light, patient as a hook.

"She's ready," Ilan said quietly. "They think she's ready. Whatever they've been growing her for, they believe the fruit is ripe."

"They believe the vessel is ready to be filled. Yes." Vess looked at him, and for the first time he saw fear in her face—not the fear of a woman in danger, but the fear of a woman who has read too much and understood too well. "And here is the arithmetic that broke me, cartographer. If the guild opens the door and fills her by force, using you as the key whether you consent or not—then the first queen's trap closes on an innocent woman, and the Hollow gets a cage built by the same hands that fed it. But if the prophecy runs as *she* wrote it—if Kestrel chooses, if you choose—then maybe, maybe, the door shuts the other way. Maybe the warning was a warning. I cannot prove it. Nineteen years in an archive, and I cannot prove which kind of document I'm holding. All I can tell you is that a woman who seals a monster into the earth and then writes a book describing its escape does not strike me as a woman who wanted it free."

"Unless she wasn't sealing it," Ilan said. The thought arrived whole, the way his best maps arrived, all at once and already true. "Unless she was *becoming* it. You said she went down and didn't come back. What if the Hollow isn't a thing she imprisoned? What if it's a thing she turned into? What if the prophecy isn't a warning or a trap at all—it's a *request*. From someone who has been alone down there for a hundred years, asking to be finished."

The water made no sound. The chapel made no sound. Even the dripping had stopped, as though the building were listening.

Vess exhaled through her teeth. "That is the worst thing anyone has said to me in nineteen years of archives," she said, "and I believe it may be correct."

* * *

They talked until the lantern burned low.

She told him about the archive's eleventh floor, where the oldest records were kept behind a door that was warm to the touch, and how she had told herself for years that it was the boiler flue running behind the wall. She told him about the defector network—six people, scattered through the Wardens and the guild, communicating through laundry marks and hymn numbers, waiting for years for a reason to act. She told him that the reason had arrived wearing his face in a century-old book, and that she had chosen him not because he was special but because he was *documented*, and in her experience documented men either became instruments or became dead, and she preferred to work with instruments who were still breathing.

"And if I refuse?" Ilan asked. "If I walk away tonight, go north, change my name, take up fishing?"

"Then the guild finds another key. The dreams will move. They always move—I found references to three previous cartographers who dreamed of spirals, going back a hundred years. Two fled. One drowned in a dry cistern." She shrugged with terrible mildness. "The prophecy does not require your cooperation, cartographer. It requires your face. You can spend the rest of your life being hunted by your own reflection, or you can stand where you're standing and choose what the opening means. Those are the options. I'm sorry. I didn't write them."

Ilan looked down at the flooded nave. Somewhere beneath the black water lay the chapel floor, and beneath the floor the bones of the old district, and beneath the bones the deep stone of the city, and beneath the city—he was certain of it now, as certain as he had ever been of any line on any map—a door, warm as skin, with his name on the far side of it.

He thought of Kestrel's hands shaking around the prophecy book. He thought of her saying *you are meant to open something*, and the strange tenderness in it, and the terror. He thought of the dream that had come to him again last night, unchanged after all these weeks: a corridor of green stone, a door, and behind the door not a roar but a silence so complete it had weight, pressing against the wood like a palm pressed against the inside of a coffin lid. And beneath the silence, faintly, patiently, a voice that was almost a voice, saying words he could never quite bring up into waking, though he had tried, though he had filled half a sketchbook trying.

Last night, for the first time, he had caught one word of it.

*Please.*

"I'm not doing this because of the prophecy," he said at last. "I want that understood. If I do it, I do it because a woman I love was bred in a cellar for someone else's purpose, and because a girl of nineteen is hearing voices through her floor, and because whatever is down there has been asking—for a hundred years, in whatever way it can ask—and no one has answered. Prophecy or trap or request, it doesn't matter. Someone has to go down and find out what wants the door open. And apparently it's me."

Vess studied him for a long moment. Then she did something he did not expect: she bowed. Shallow, ironic, but unmistakably a bow, the bow of one professional to another across a shared trade.

"Cartographer," she said, "you have just done the only honest thing anyone has done in this city in a hundred years. You've admitted you're complicit." She began wrapping the book again, folding the waxed canvas with quick, practiced motions. "Keep that. Hold onto it. On the night it matters—and it's coming soon, Corvane's schedule accelerates after the anniversary feast—it will matter more than courage. Courage is common down there. Honest men are rare."

She handed him the wrapped book. Their hands met over it—her scarred leather cap, his ink-stained fingers.

"The feast," Ilan said. "Five days."

"Five days. And the guild moves at moonset." Vess stepped back, and the water took her weight without a sound. "There's a laundry mark you should learn, and a hymn number, and a name you can trust in the Second Circle—come to the fish market tomorrow at dawn and I'll give you all three. But tonight, go home, cartographer. Sleep if you can. And if you dream of the door—"

She paused at the edge of the lantern light, half-dissolved into the dark.

"—listen carefully. For a hundred years, no one has answered it. It's time somebody asked what it wants."

Then she was gone, and the water closed over the place where she had been, and Ilan stood alone in the drowned chapel with a dead queen's book in his arms and one word ringing in him like a struck bell.

*Please.*

He climbed the broken stairs toward the living city, and behind him, far below the water, far below the stone, he thought he felt it—the faintest warmth rising through the soles of his boots, like a hand laid gently against the underside of the world.

Five days. And somewhere above him, in a palace built over a grave, the feast of ashes was already being prepared.
