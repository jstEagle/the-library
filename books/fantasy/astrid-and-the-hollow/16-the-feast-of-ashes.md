## 16. The Feast of Ashes

### Kestrel

The hall had been dressed for a miracle.

Kestrel felt it before she saw it—the way the air in the Grand Refectory hung too still, too sweet, like the held breath before a sneeze. A thousand candles burned in tiers along the walls, their flames standing unnaturally straight, none of them guttering. Garlands of white jasmine and ash-grey ribbon draped from the vaulted ceiling. The coronation-anniversary feast was always lavish, but this year the court had outdone itself, as though everyone, without agreeing to, understood that something needed celebrating *now*, quickly, before the chance went away.

She moved through the service corridor behind the high table with her tray balanced on one hand, her other hand hidden in the folds of her skirt. Beneath her tongue, tucked into the hollow where she kept her emergency antidotes, sat nothing at all. She had stopped carrying them three days ago. Antidotes were for women who could be poisoned. Whatever Kestrel Eastwick was now, poison was no longer the thing that would kill her.

The queen's place was set at the center of the high table, beneath the great chandelier of Vellish crystal—a thousand cut drops suspended on silver chains, made to look like frozen rain. Maren had loved it since childhood, Corvane said. She used to stand beneath it and pretend the light was falling stars.

Kestrel set down the ninth dish—quail in pomegranate glaze, a recipe from the old kingdom—and tasted it with the small silver spoon every taster carried chained to her wrist. The meat was fine. The glaze was fine. And beneath both, faint as a fingerprint on glass, she tasted the Hollow: cold iron and deep water and something older than either, something like the smell of a room that has been shut for a hundred years.

It was in the food now. It was in the *wine*. It was in the candle smoke and the jasmine and the sweat of the courtiers pressing close to see their queen. For weeks it had been seeping upward through the palace foundations, and Kestrel had told herself the wards in her chamber were holding, that Ilan's chalked spirals were holding, that there was time.

Then the doors opened, and Maren came in, and Kestrel knew there had never been any time at all.

* * *

### Queen Maren

Maren had never felt so well in her life.

That was the first wrongness, though she did not yet know to name it. She had slept nine hours—she never slept more than five—and woken clear-headed, hungry, *bright*, as if someone had polished the inside of her skull. Her mother's little cantrips, the ones she'd practiced guiltily for years in the dark of her rooms—the candle-flame cupped in bare hands, the rose coaxed open out of season—had become effortless. This morning she had lifted the entire washbasin with a thought and let it drift around her chamber like a slow grey moon, laughing, alone, delighted.

Tonight she walked into the feast and the hall *rose* to meet her. She felt it: four hundred hearts, four hundred small warm flames of attention, all bending toward her like grass toward sun. It should have been frightening. It was not. It was like coming home to a house she'd forgotten she owned.

"Your Majesty." Lord Chancellor Deverell bowed. "The court is honored."

"The court is *hungry*," Maren heard herself say, and smiled, and did not know where the words came from, only that they were true and pleasing and hers.

She took her seat. The taster came forward—Kestrel, quiet Kestrel, who had fed her safe food for six years and never once asked for anything—and touched the spoon to each dish. Maren watched her lips move through the old silent litany of the guild, watched her gray eyes flick up once, briefly, and something in them made Maren's stomach clench, some animal warning she brushed aside the way you brush aside a fly during a prayer.

*Kestrel is afraid,* thought some small buried part of her. *Kestrel is afraid of me.*

But then the first course arrived and the hall began to murmur its pleasure, and the small buried part of her sank back down, and the voice from below—the patient, loving voice that had been speaking to her for eleven nights, the one that called her *the open hand*—said: *Now. Show them. They have waited so long to be shown.*

Maren raised her wine glass to make the anniversary toast.

And the chandelier above the high table began to rise.

* * *

### Ilan

Ilan Voss stood among the cartographers' benches at the far end of the hall, and he was the only person watching the ceiling when it happened.

He had spent the evening doing what he always did at feasts: counting. The spiral recurred eleven times in the refectory's stonework—he'd mapped them all months ago, inked them onto the great chart hidden in his quarters, the one whose lines converged beneath the throne room like rivers running to a drowned sea. Tonight he counted them again because counting kept his hands steady. Because Kestrel had told him, three nights ago in the dark of the archive, her voice flat with a terror she refused to perform: *If it happens, it will happen at a feast. Power wants an audience.*

So he counted spirals, and he watched the queen, and he watched Kestrel, and when the chandelier shuddered on its chains he was already moving.

It rose slowly, horribly gently, a thousand crystal drops lifting through the candlelight, silver chains paying out below it like the tendrils of some luminous jellyfish. Gasps rippled outward from the high table in a perfect ring. Someone laughed—one of the young lords, drunk, delighted, assuming a trick, a surprise staged by the Master of Revels.

Then the chandelier turned. All of it, all at once, the whole vast glittering weight of it rotating in midair, and every facet of every drop caught the candleflame and threw it back, and suddenly the entire hall was filled with wheeling points of light, hundreds of them, orbiting, and Ilan's blood went cold because he knew that pattern. He had drawn it ten thousand times in dreams. He had traced it on the foundation stones of buildings older than the city's oldest records.

A spiral. The chandelier was turning in a spiral, and the light it scattered was spinning across the walls in slow golden arms, and the court had gone silent—not afraid yet, not quite, but *held*, the way a bird is held by a snake.

At the center of it stood Queen Maren with her glass still raised, her face lit from above, and her eyes—

Her eyes were black. Not dark. Black, and in the black, turning slowly, a ring of pale gold: a spiral seen end-on, an eye looking back from the bottom of a very deep well.

"*Long live the queen*," Maren said, and her voice came out layered, two voices braided into one, hers and something vast underneath it that spoke the way caves speak, with echo built into the throat. "*Long live the queen, who opens what was shut.*"

The toast died in four hundred throats.

* * *

### Kestrel

Kestrel was already moving when the screaming started.

Not away—toward. Ten years of training did not permit anything else; the taster's first duty was the sovereign's body, and whatever occupied that body now, some stubborn animal core of her still answered to the old law. She reached the high table as the first lords began to shove backward from their benches, as a countess fainted into the arms of her neighbor, as the great doors at the far end banged open and the Wardens poured in—two dozen of them, grey-cloaked, batons drawn, moving with the terrible coordination of soldiers who have been waiting all evening for exactly this.

*Corvane,* Kestrel thought. *You absolute bastard. You knew.*

"Warden-Captain!" she shouted, planting herself between Maren's chair and the advancing line. "The queen is ill! Clear the hall—gently—"

"The queen," said a voice behind her, "is *occupied*."

Master Corvane came through the servants' door with six guild-tasters in dove-grey livery, and Kestrel had never seen him move fast in ten years of knowing him, but he crossed the floor now like a man walking downhill, quick and inevitable. In his hands he carried a thing she had only read about in the guild's forbidden ledger: a collar of white iron worked with tiny stamped spirals, thin as a necklace, meant for the throat.

"You will step aside, Kestrel," he said quietly. "This is guild business. This is what you were *made* for."

"She is nineteen years old."

"She is a *door*," Corvane snapped, and for one unguarded instant Kestrel saw through the institutional calm to the thing beneath it, and the thing beneath it was fear, pure and animal and ancient. "Look at her eyes. Look at what is wearing her. Every hour we debate, it drinks deeper. The ritual can pull it out of her and bind it where it can be starved. I have spent my whole life keeping that thing fed and asleep so that tonight would never come, and I will not watch it wake because you've developed a *conscience*—"

Above them, the chandelier sang.

It was not breaking—it was *ringing*, a thousand crystal drops vibrating in sympathy with some note too low for human ears, and the sound went through Kestrel's bones like cold water. The candles guttered, all together, all at once, and relit themselves blue.

Maren rose from her chair. She floated—there was no other word—an inch above the floor, her gown settling around her like water finding its level, and she spread her arms wide, the open hand, and looked out over her terrified court with those spiral-black eyes, and *smiled*.

"Do not be afraid," she said, in the doubled voice, and the terrible thing, the thing Kestrel would remember for the rest of her life, was that the kindness in it was real. "*I have gifts for everyone.*"

* * *

### Queen Maren

Inside herself, Maren was screaming, and outside herself, she was healing.

She could see both at once, which was the worst of it—like watching her own body from a doorway. The thing that wore her was gentle. That was the horror no one had warned her about: it did not seize, it *offered*. It reached into the crowd and found the Lord Chancellor's dying mother, three streets away, abed with the wasting sickness, and Maren felt the chancellor's sudden stunned hope bloom in the hall like heat from an oven as word of it arrived—no, not arrived, simply *became known*, the way everyone in the room suddenly knew things—and the old woman was sitting up asking for broth.

It found the Warden-Captain's dead son and showed him the boy's laugh, brief and unbearable, in the space behind his own eyes. It found the scullery girl in the gallery who prayed every night for her father's debts to be forgiven, and somewhere across the city a moneylender woke from a dream weeping and tore up four contracts.

Gifts. Gifts everywhere, raining down on Vell, and every gift cost something Maren could feel being taken—something from the foundations, from the deep sealed dark, from the *hunger*—no. Not taken. *Spent.* The thing beneath the palace was spending itself through her, lavishly, gloriously, and she understood with perfect clarity that it was buying something.

*What do you want?* she screamed inwardly. *What are you buying?*

And the voice answered, patient, loving, from everywhere:

*Attention, little hand. Only attention. Every soul in this room is looking at me now. Every soul in this city will look by morning. I have been shut in the dark for a hundred years, and I am so very tired of the dark, and doors are only doors until someone looks at them.*

Below, in the chaos, a small grey-eyed woman was fighting her way toward the dais against a tide of fleeing courtiers, and the thing wearing Maren's body turned its borrowed face toward Kestrel Eastwick and said, in Maren's own voice, soft enough that only Kestrel could hear:

"*There you are, Taster. I saved the best gift for last. Shall I give you your mother?*"

* * *

### Kestrel

Kestrel stopped moving.

The hall was madness—Wardens swinging batons at guild-tasters, Corvane shouting orders no one obeyed, the chandelier shedding crystal drops that burst on the flagstones like ice, courtiers trampling each other toward the doors—but around Kestrel there opened a pocket of terrible quiet, because the Hollow had spoken to her in her mother's voice, and her mother had been dead for twenty-two years, and she had never once, not even to Ilan, said aloud how much she missed her.

*I could give her back,* the voice said, gentle as fever. *Flesh and breath and memory. I have her shape down here in the dark. I have worn it a long time, waiting for you. Come down, Taster. Open your mouth. You were made to receive me—you know it, you have always known it, every dish you ever swallowed was practice for this. Why fight the shape of your own life?*

And Kestrel, standing in the burning blue candlelight amid the wreckage of the court that raised her, did the only thing she had ever truly been trained to do.

She tasted.

She opened her senses the way the guild had drilled into her since childhood—*taste everything, trust nothing, name what kills you*—and she tasted the air of the Grand Refectory, and what she found nearly dropped her to her knees.

Death. Everywhere. Not the Hollow's death—that was something else, cold and vast and patient—but ordinary human death, thick in the room like smoke: the fear-sweat of four hundred people, the copper tang of a Warden's split scalp, the sour note of the countess's failing heart as she lay crumpled by the dais steps, the thin bright thread of the scullery girl crushed against the doorframe. Ten years of controlled poisons had built in Kestrel a palate finer than any physician's, and now it opened fully, horribly, and she could taste *how everyone in this room was going to die*, and most of them were going to die tonight, here, in this beautiful wrecked hall, unless—

Unless someone swallowed the thing that had promised them miracles.

"Maren," Kestrel said aloud, and climbed the dais steps into the floating dark of the queen's shadow. "Listen to me. Your mother taught you the small magics. Did she ever teach you what they *cost*?"

The spiral eyes turned down toward her. For a moment—just a moment—the black thinned, and beneath it Maren's own brown eyes surfaced like a drowning woman's face breaking water, and the queen whispered with her own single voice:

"Kestrel. It's in the *foundations*, it's under everything, it says it only wants—"

"I know what it wants." Kestrel reached up and took the queen's hovering hand. It was cold as well-water. "It wants a door. And there's only one door it can't walk through on its own."

Behind her, steel rang on stone: Corvane's people forcing the collar toward the dais, Wardens reforming their line, Ilan pushing through the smoke with his map-case clutched to his chest and his mouth shaping her name. Above them all, the chandelier wheeled its slow golden spiral, scattering light like a benediction over the Feast of Ashes.

Kestrel tightened her grip on the queen's cold hand, met the spiral gaze, and bared her teeth in something that was almost a smile.

"But first," she said, "you're going to tell me exactly what my mother's voice sounds like. Because if you're going to wear her, monster, you'll do it *correctly*—and then you and I are going downstairs."

The Hollow laughed through the queen's mouth, and every candle in Vell's palace leaned toward the sound like flowers toward a sun that had never existed.

* * *

### Ilan

From the floor of the ruined hall, Ilan Voss watched Kestrel ascend the dais into the dark, and understood, with the awful clarity of a man reading a map he has drawn himself, that the prophecy had brought them all to this table and was now serving the final course.

He pulled the chart from its case. The spiral converged beneath the throne room. The door was below. And the torn page—the page Corvane kept locked away—had named the ending in words Ilan had only heard secondhand, words that rang in him now like a struck bell:

*The Taster swallows the dark, and the dark learns her name.*

He started toward the dais, toward her, through the smoke and the screaming and the falling crystal light.

The door would have to be opened. And he already knew, in the dreaming part of him that had drawn her face ten thousand times without knowing why, who would have to open it.
