## 21. The Keeper's Room

The room they built for me is round.

I asked for that, in the beginning—asked the masons to curve every wall so there would be no corners for it to gather in. Corners are where silence pools, and pooled silence is a kind of invitation. A circle has no edges to hide behind. If the Hollow wanted to speak to me, it would have to do it in the open, face to face, the way honest things speak.

It speaks anyway. But we come to an understanding slowly, the way two people who share a bed learn each other's breathing.

The chamber lies three levels beneath the rebuilt palace, below the wine cellars and the old taster's quarters I once kept so meticulously clean, below even the sealed door Ilan closed behind me. The walls are grey granite veined with white quartz that catches lamplight like frost on a window. Someone—Maren, I suspect, though she has never admitted it—had the ceiling painted before I was brought down. A sky. Clouds the color of unbleached linen, a sun rendered in gold leaf no larger than a coin. It should be absurd. Some nights it is the only thing that keeps me from forgetting what morning looks like.

They ward the door with iron and salt and seven seals of the old script, renewed each month by a Warden who will not meet my eyes. They bring my meals on a tray through a slot no wider than a hand. And they leave me alone with the thing I swallowed, because there is nothing else anyone can do.

I have been here four years. Or five. Time moves strangely underground, where the days arrive only as footsteps overhead and the changing of the tray.

* * *

This morning—or what I have decided is morning—the tray holds barley porridge with honey, brown bread still warm enough to fog the slot's iron edge, and a pear cut into thin slices fanned out like a hand of cards.

I taste none of it.

That is the arithmetic of my survival, the ledger no one taught me until it was too late: ten years of controlled poisoning built a body that cannot be poisoned, and a body that cannot be poisoned cannot taste. Numbness was the first symptom, back at that state dinner when I noticed the ninth dish going down like warm water. Now it is simply the country I live in. Porridge, honey, bread, pear—all of it texture without voice, food as geometry. I eat anyway. Eating is a discipline, and discipline is the last craft I own.

But I have learned tricks. I hold the pear slice against the roof of my mouth and wait for the ghost of sweetness—not flavor exactly, but the memory of flavor, the way a man who has lost his sight still flinches at brightness remembered. Sometimes, if I am very still, the ghost comes. Today it does not. I eat the pear anyway, all eight slices, and I thank the kitchen in my head, because whoever cut them fanned them like cards and that small vanity deserves gratitude.

"You could let me give it back to you," says the Hollow, from the place behind my sternum where it lives. "Taste. One tongue's worth of sweetness. You would not even feel me reach."

I say nothing.

I have said nothing for four years, and it has not stopped asking, but it has stopped expecting. There is a difference between a prisoner who screams at her cell wall and one who has made her peace with the stone. I am the second kind. Mostly.

"It is only a small thing," it says. Its voice is my mother's. That was its first cruelty and its most durable one; it found her voice in me, folded flat and preserved like a flower in a book, and it wears that voice the way a thief wears a stolen coat. "A sweetness. A warmth. You keep yourself in poverty when I hold riches."

I chew the bread. I look at the painted sky.

Silence, I have learned, is not the absence of speech. Silence is a wall you build one brick at a time, every hour, every day, and the Hollow tests it constantly the way water tests a dam—not out of malice, precisely, but because that is its nature. It was a queen once. It knows how power erodes resistance: not by force but by patience, by the slow drip of *why not*, by making the smallest concession seem reasonable until the flood follows.

So I lay bricks. Tray by tray. Breath by breath.

By midday it has gone quiet, sulking in whatever passes for sulking inside a vast absence wearing a dead woman's shape. I take out the things that are mine—a comb with a broken tooth, a needle and thread, the little knife I use to carve charms I am no longer permitted to sell—and I mend the hem of my sleeve. Small work. Honest work. My hands remember being useful even when the rest of me is only a cage.

* * *

Maren comes on Thursdays.

I know it is Thursday because she told me once that Thursdays were the only day the council did not summon her before noon, and she has kept the appointment with a punctuality that would shame a clock. Four years of Thursdays. She is twenty-three now. She was nineteen when the chandelier rose over her head and her eyes went spiral-black before the whole court, and sometimes I look at her and try to find that girl in the woman who descends my stairs, and mostly I fail, and I think that is what growing up is.

Today she brings news and a book.

"Corvane's trial concluded," she says, settling onto the chair they installed for her visits—it is bolted to the floor, a precaution I insisted on, though neither of us pretends it is for my protection. "Guilty on all counts. He asked to see you, before sentencing."

"And?"

"I refused." She sets the book on the small table between us. Her hands are steady now; they were not, the first year. "He spent thirty years feeding people to a hole in the ground because he was afraid of it. I don't owe him your face on top of everything else."

"What did he ask for, at the end? Leniency?"

"He asked whether you had spoken yet." She almost smiles. It doesn't reach her eyes. "Whether the Hollow had gotten a word out of you. I think he wanted to know if his life's work had been worth something after all. I told him you hadn't said a syllable in four years, and I watched him understand that silence was the one result he never planned for."

Good, I do not say. I do not say anything. But Maren reads my face the way she reads dispatches—quickly, thoroughly, missing little—and she nods as if I had spoken.

She slides the book across the table. It is a history, newly printed, *The Vell Reckoning: An Account of the Sealed Years*. My name appears in it. So does Ilan's. She watches me turn the pages with the particular attention of someone waiting for a wound to reopen.

"They got most of it right," she says. "The guild, the breeding program, the first queen. There's a chapter about—" She stops. Starts again. "About the door. About what you did."

I keep turning pages. The prose is florid. Historians always make sacrifice sound cleaner than it was. There was nothing clean about that night: the taste of the Hollow going down like swallowing a winter, the way it filled me and filled me and did not stop filling me, the sound Ilan made when he drew the spiral around us both and closed the door. There was blood. There was a moment—I have never told anyone this—when I understood that I could refuse, that I could step back and let it take the court, the city, the world, and no one would ever know I had chosen it. That moment lasted the length of one heartbeat. Then I opened my throat and drank.

"The archivists found something else," Maren says quietly. "In the foundation rubble. Under the old throne room."

I look up.

"Pages. Loose pages, water-damaged, older than anything in the collection. The script matches the prophecy book—the one you found under the floor." She hesitates, and I realize she has been carrying this carefully, the way one carries a full cup across a crowded room. "Kestrel, some of them aren't prophecies. They're letters. Written backward, or forward, or—I don't know. The archivists can't agree. But one of them is addressed to *the Keeper*. By name."

The room feels smaller. Behind my ribs, something shifts its weight, attentive as a cat hearing its name.

"Your name," Maren says. "*Kestrel Eastwick.* Written a hundred years ago."

* * *

After she leaves, I sit with the afternoon light—lamplight, really, but I have decided it is afternoon—and I do not move for a long time.

The Hollow waits until the footsteps fade above. It always knows when we are alone.

"A hundred years," it says, in my mother's voice, soft as ash settling. "And still she writes to you. Do you want to know what the letter says? I was there when she wrote it. I was the hand that held the pen."

I build another brick. Another. But my hands are not quite steady, and it notices. It always notices.

"You think silence protects you," it murmurs. "But silence is just a door you haven't opened yet. And doors, little taster—doors are the one thing in this world I understand completely."

I pick up the needle. I go back to mending my sleeve, stitch by stitch, in the round room with no corners, under the painted sky, while the thing inside me begins, very softly, to laugh.

* * *

That night the tray comes early, which is wrong, and the porridge smells of cinnamon, which is impossible, because I cannot smell.

I stand over it for a long moment. Steam rises. The scent curls up warm and familiar—my mother's kitchen, a lifetime ago, a kitchen that burned before I was old enough to remember it clearly—and I understand, with a cold clarity that has nothing to do with temperature, that the Hollow is offering me more than memory now. It is offering me proof. Proof that it can reach past my numbness, past my walls, into the oldest rooms of me. Proof that silence has a price, and the price is rising.

Or proof that somewhere above me, someone is learning to send messages down through stone.

I carry the tray to the door slot and set it back through, untouched. Then I return to my chair, fold my hands, and wait—because tomorrow is Thursday, and Maren will come, and I have decided that when she does, I will break four years of silence with six words:

*Bring me the girl from the archives.*

I do not know why those words rise in me. I do not know whose thought they are—mine, or the first queen's, or the Hollow's, planted like a seed in the dark.

But as I sit listening to the granite breathe, the voice behind my sternum goes very still, and then says, almost gently, in my mother's voice:

"*Ah.* There you are."
