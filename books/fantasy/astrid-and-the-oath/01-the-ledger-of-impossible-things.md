## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The Vesper Guild kept its accounts by candlelight, because daylight was for honest businesses, and the guild had never been one.

Ulyana Delacroix sat at the long counting table in the cellar of the guildhall, sleeves rolled to the elbow, ink to the second knuckle, and worked through the autumn inventory while the city above them slept off its market-day wine. Three candles burned at their elbow. A fourth stood unlit in reserve, because Odile said a ledger-keeper who ran out of light deserved whatever errors followed, and Ulyana had taken the lesson to heart with the particular fervor of someone who had once been given a second chance and intended never to need a third.

The inventory book lay open before them, and the inventory itself lay all around them on shelves of salt-bleached pine: the impossible things.

Item forty-one. *One bottle, green glass, sealed with wax and wire, containing thunder.* Ulyana lifted it carefully — you did not shake a bottled storm, the way you did not jostle a sleeping dog — and held it up to the candle. The glass was warm. It was always warm. Inside, something turned over slowly, like a whale surfacing in deep water, and a sound came through the wax seal that was not quite a sound, more a pressure against the ear, the memory of a noise rather than the noise itself. The fisherman who'd sold it to them had wept when he handed it over. He'd caught it off the Broken Shoals three summers past, he said, in a net meant for herring, and it had screamed the whole way to shore in a voice that made his dogs go mad and his wife leave him, though he only admitted the second part after the third cup. The guild had paid him fairly. The guild always paid fairly; that was the whole trick of them, the thing nobody outside the Vesper quarter ever quite believed. Fair prices, quiet doors, no questions asked — and none answered either.

Ulyana weighed the bottle on the brass scale, recorded the weight to the dram, noted that the storm inside had grown heavier since spring. Storms fed on time, Bram said. They got older and denser, like grief. Then they wrapped the bottle in wool, set it back between its felt cradles, and wrote in the margin, in their small precise hand: *Handle as living. It is.*

* * *

"You're doing the widow's year tonight," Odile said from the stair, and Ulyana's pen jumped, laying a small black comet across the page.

Odile Vess descended into the cellar the way she did everything: without hurry, without apology, a broad woman in a grey dress with her iron-grey hair pinned up with what appeared to be two pencils and an actual nail. She carried a tray — bread, hard cheese, a pot of honey, tea gone lukewarm out of consideration for nothing — and she set it down on the corner of the counting table with the air of a general provisioning a siege.

"I'm doing the whole shelf tonight," Ulyana said. "Items thirty-eight through fifty."

"You're doing the widow's year last," Odile said, "because you do it first every time and then you mope through the rest of the inventory thinking about her." She poured the tea anyway, which was how Odile argued: she stated a thing as fact, and then she was kind about it, and you found you'd agreed before you noticed the door closing behind you. "Eat something. You've been down here since the bells. Your handwriting's gone drunk."

"My handwriting is immaculate."

"Your handwriting has been drinking," Odile confirmed, and went back up the stairs, pausing on the fourth step to add, without turning around: "And when you get to item forty-four, count the scale twice. I don't trust it. It's been shedding."

Which was true, and they both knew it, and this was the other half of the guild's trick — not just fair prices and quiet doors, but the smaller mercies. That Odile knew Ulyana's habits well enough to predict which entry would sit heavy on their chest, and provisioned against it with bread and cheese, and never once said anything so naked as *I know you*, because in the Vesper Guild you did not say such things. You simply acted as though you already knew, and had known for years, and would keep knowing without being asked.

Ulyana ate the bread. It helped. Most things, in Ulyana's experience, were survivable if there was bread.

* * *

Item forty-two. *One jar, borrowed years, quantity: one (1), remainder of a widow's allotment.*

This was the entry that sat heavy.

The widow was named Halvora, though everyone called her Mother Sennet because she'd fed half the quarter through the famine winter and refused, to this day, to accept payment in anything but gossip. Two years ago her husband had died, and Mother Sennet had come to the guild not to buy magic but to sell it — or rather, to sell what the guild could make of it. She had sixty years left in her, she said, sitting very straight in Odile's office with her hands folded like a woman at prayer, and she wanted to lend twenty of them to her granddaughter, whose lungs were failing, so the girl might live long enough to marry the cooper's son and see her own children grown. Not give. Lend. She'd have it back, she said, when she was done with it, and her eyes had dared anyone in the room to explain to her exactly how she planned to collect.

Bram had done the binding himself, down here in the cellar, with the fire. Twenty years, drawn out of a stubborn old woman like thread from a spindle, coiled and cooled and stoppered into a jar of smoked glass. The granddaughter had taken the years the way you take bitter medicine, all at once, and bloomed. She'd married the cooper's son in the spring. There had been a wedding, and Ulyana had gone, and Mother Sennet had danced until midnight on her remaining forty years and told Ulyana, breathless, gripping their arm: "Best bargain I ever struck, and I once traded a pig for a horse."

Now the jar sat on the shelf, faintly gold even in candlelight, humming almost inaudibly — the sound of twenty years waiting to be spent. And beside it, in the ledger, Ulyana had written and rewritten the same note each season: *Interest accruing. Holder may reclaim upon request. See covenant terms, ref. vault.*

See covenant terms. Ulyana had written those words a hundred times in four years and had never once seen the covenant terms. Nobody below Odile and Bram had. The oldest ledger — the black one, chained shut, kept in the iron box under the floor — was referenced constantly in the margins of everything, and opened by no one, and Ulyana had learned early that in the guild you did not ask about the black ledger the way you did not ask about the scar under Odile's jaw or why Bram slept in the dragon-house instead of his own bed. Some questions were doors you didn't knock on, because the house was kind enough to let you live in it.

Belonging, purchased with secrecy. Ulyana knew the price to the copper. They paid it nightly.

Because here was the thing the ledger did not record, the thing no entry could hold: Ulyana Delacroix had a gift. Small. Stubborn. Useless for trade and fatal for keeping. When they touched a wound — their own, mostly; they weren't stupid — warmth gathered in their palm, a low amber flicker under the skin, and the hurt eased. Not healing, not really. Maren had called it "a hearth-gift, a candle where others carry bonfires," and had said it with love, and then the village had said it with torches, and Ulyana had run, and now the gift lived folded up small behind their breastbone like a letter never sent, and once a month, alone, in the dark, Ulyana let it out for the length of one breath. Just to be sure it still lived. Just to feel the warmth climb their wrist. Then they put it away, and locked the ledger of themselves, and balanced the accounts of everyone else's magic instead.

They were doing it now, actually. They realized it halfway through item forty-three — the ember-scale — because their right hand had drifted to the candle flame and was hovering there, close enough to feel the heat lean toward their skin like a cat toward a familiar lap. The flame bent, very slightly, toward their fingers. Not blown. *Drawn.* Listening.

Ulyana snatched the hand back and looked at the doorway, heart slamming. Empty. Stairs empty. Cellar empty but for the shelves and the candles and the impossible things, all of them safely asleep in their glass.

No one saw. No one ever saw. That was the arrangement Ulyana had made with the world: the guild gets my hands, my ledgers, my loyalty; the gift stays mine, folded small, unspent, unspoken. Four years of clean entries. Four years of belonging bought at the price of one secret, and Ulyana had thought the price fixed forever.

* * *

Item forty-four. *One scale, shed, of living ember. Provenance: Ember-of-Endings, last of the wyrms. Status: still alive. Still warm. Do not store near flammables, which is a joke, because everything near it becomes flammable.*

The scale was the size of Ulyana's palm, dark red-black like a coal that had decided to become jewelry, and it sat in its sand-filled dish radiating a heat you could feel from three steps away. It was the only item in the cellar that required no candle. When Ulyana leaned over it, the scale brightened — a slow pulse, deep in its grain, like a heartbeat heard through a wall — and the light it threw was the color of the warmth Ulyana kept folded behind their breastbone, and Ulyana hated how much that felt like recognition.

Bram came down while they were counting it the second time, as instructed.

He came down the way he always did, too — no announcement, just suddenly present, a big weathered man with a beard going to ash-grey and hands that looked like they'd been carved for holding something much larger than anything he currently held. He smelled of smoke and lamp-oil and the particular dry mineral smell of the dragon-house. He stood at the bottom of the stairs a moment, looking at the shelves, at the candles, at Ulyana, in that way he had of taking inventory of people the way Ulyana took inventory of goods.

"Twice?" he said.

"Three times," Ulyana said. "It shed again while I watched. A grain of it. Look." They tipped the dish. In the sand at the edge, a speck no bigger than a peppercorn glowed and dimmed, glowed and dimmed. "It's getting smaller, Bram. The scale. It's been losing weight all year — I have the numbers, every season, less and less. Like it's —"

"Burning down," Bram said.

"Burning down," Ulyana agreed. "But it's a *scale*. Shed. Dead thing. Dead things don't burn down, they rot or they crumble, they don't —" They stopped. Bram was looking at the scale with an expression Ulyana had never seen him wear, and it took them a moment to name it, because it was so out of place on his blunt, calm face.

It was fear.

"Is Ember-of-Endings all right?" Ulyana asked. "In the dragon-house. Is she —"

"She's old," Bram said. Which was not an answer, and was the only answer there was. He crossed the cellar, and — this was the strange part, the part Ulyana would turn over later, much later, in worse times — he laid one carved hand flat on the counting table beside the open ledger, and looked at the entries, the neat columns, the marginal notes in Ulyana's precise hand. *Handle as living. It is.* His eyes moved down the page slow, like a man reading a letter from home.

"You balance these well," he said. "Four years, and you've never once asked what the fire is *for*."

"The fire?"

"The binding-fire. Down in the deep vault, under this floor. Every jar, every bottle, every borrowed year in this room — you think we make them with chalk and good intentions?" He tapped the table once, gently, as if knocking on the floor beneath it. "There's a fire down there older than the crown, girl. Older than the guild. Everything we sell, everything we shelter, everything this family is — it all runs through that flame. And you've kept our books for four years and never asked where the money comes from."

Ulyana's mouth was dry. "I assumed —"

"You assumed it wasn't your business. That was the price of the room, and you paid it glad, and I don't blame you." Bram straightened. He looked tired, suddenly, in a way that had nothing to do with the hour. "Keep your secrets, keeper. Keep them folded small. But mark me —" and here his voice dropped, and the candles seemed to lean in along with Ulyana, and somewhere above them the guildhall creaked like a ship — "there are debts in these books that aren't owed in coin. And one day soon, the collector's coming. For all of it."

He went back up the stairs. The fear stayed behind, pooled in the cellar like cold air.

Ulyana sat a long moment with the pen uncapped. Then, because the ledger demanded it, because the ledger always demanded it, they dipped the nib and wrote the night's final line beneath item forty-four:

*Scale of living ember: weight declining. Cause unknown. Recommend inquiry.*

And then, after a moment, in smaller letters, honestly, the way they wrote everything:

*Recommend inquiry soon.*

Above them, faint through the floorboards, the guildhall settled into its nighttime sounds — Odile banking the office stove, Pip the apprentice-boy laughing at something in the kitchen, the front door's triple lock turning over, one, two, three. Home sounds. Bought-and-paid-for sounds.

Ulyana blew out two of the three candles, kept the third, and reached for item forty-five.

They did not yet know that within the week, criers would stand at the market cross reading the crown's proclamation in voices trained to carry, and that the word *register* would land in the Vesper quarter like a stone in a well. They did not yet know that the collector Bram spoke of wore white gloves, and had a face Ulyana had loved since childhood.

They knew only that the scale was burning down, that the fire beneath the floor was older than the crown, and that for the first time in four years of clean entries, the ledger in their hands and the ledger behind their ribs had begun, quietly, to tell the same story.

They wrote on.
