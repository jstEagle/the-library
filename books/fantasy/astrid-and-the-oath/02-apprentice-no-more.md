## 2. Apprentice No More

The ink was still wet on the widow's receipt when the memory came for Ulyana, the way it always did on nights when the guildhouse went quiet enough to hear its own bones settle.

They set down the pen. Pressed their palms flat against the ledger desk until the grain of the oak bit into skin. Counted, as they'd taught themselves to count: one bottle of thunder on the third shelf, two jars of river-dark, a scale of living ember wrapped in wool in the strongroom below. Things that were real. Things that could be touched and tallied and trusted not to change.

The cottage at Aldenmoor had smelled of rosemary and burnt sugar. It had smelled of that the morning Ulyana left it forever, and they could not smell either scent now without their hands going cold.

* * *

Seven years ago. Autumn, and the light coming through Maren's kitchen window the color of weak cider.

"Again," Maren said.

Ulyana knelt beside the pallet where the miller's boy lay fevered, his small body shivering under three blankets though sweat stood on his brow like dew. The gift sat in Ulyana's chest the way a held breath sits—aching, insistent, wanting out. They had been doing this work for two years by then: learning to find the sickness in a body's warmth, to cup it gently, to draw it up and out like a splinter of cold iron.

"It hurts him when I pull."

"Of course it hurts. Sickness doesn't leave politely." Maren crouched across the pallet from them, gray-haired, sharp-eyed, her hands folded as if in prayer but really just resting, because she had learned long ago that her own gift was better spent on knowing than on doing. "You're not hurting him, love. You're hurting the fever. There's a difference, and someday you'll believe me."

Ulyana closed their eyes. Found the boy's warmth—the bright thread of him under the sick, hot tangle—and took hold.

And the fever took hold back.

Later, trying to explain it to themselves across seven years of sleepless nights, Ulyana would think of it as grabbing a rope expecting a bucket and finding a snake. The fever wasn't just heat. It was something older that had gotten into the well-water, into the boy's blood, something that *liked* being touched, that flowed up Ulyana's arms and into their chest and *laughed* there, a sound no one else could hear, a sound made of rattling breath and burning straw.

They screamed. That much they remembered clearly. They screamed and pulled away and the boy's fever broke all at once—not gently, not the way Maren had taught, but like a dam bursting, the sickness flooding outward into the room, into the floorboards, into—

Into the dog sleeping by the hearth.

The dog did not die. That was the mercy Ulyana clung to in the worst years, the rope thrown to a drowning self: *the dog did not die.* But it woke screaming too, in whatever way dogs scream, and it never stopped shaking again, and it lived another four years as a trembling, haunted thing that would not let anyone touch it, and everyone in Aldenmoor knew whose fault that was.

Because the boy recovered. He was up and asking for bread within the hour. And Ulyana sat on Maren's kitchen floor with frost forming on their own lips—they had pulled so hard they'd pulled some of the boy's warmth along with the sickness, and couldn't give it back—and heard the miller's wife in the yard saying words that traveled faster than any fever:

*The witch's apprentice. The apprentice did this.*

* * *

"You're bleeding on my ledger," Odile said, from the doorway of the counting room.

Ulyana blinked down. Their palm had opened somewhere between then and now; a thin line of red crossed the page beneath it, bisecting the widow's borrowed year like a horizon. "Sorry. I'll copy it fair."

"Don't apologize to me, apologize to the arithmetic." Odile crossed the room, took Ulyana's hand without ceremony, and wrapped it in a square of linen with the brisk tenderness she used on everything—people, debts, dangerous cargo. She was sixty if she was a day, small and iron-colored, with the forearms of a woman who had once hauled storm-barrels herself. Guildmaster of the Vesper Guild, keeper of impossible things, and the only person alive who knew what Ulyana's palms looked like when they were working.

She didn't ask what Ulyana had been remembering. She never asked. That was the covenant of the guild before Ulyana ever knew there was an older, formal covenant written in older ink: *you come in with nothing declared, and we ask nothing declared, and what you were before this door is not our business unless you make it so.*

"Third shelf inventory done?" Odile asked.

"Thunder, both jars of river-dark, the winter that belonged to the ferryman. Ember-scale's in the strongroom, untouched."

"And you've been sitting here for an hour staring at a page."

"I've been *balancing* a page."

"Mm." Odile released the bandaged hand and looked at Ulyana for a moment longer than bookkeeping required. Her eyes were the pale gray of old pewter, and they missed very little. "There's stew on the stove. Bram's up on the roof arguing with the weathercock. Eat something before you finish, or you'll faint into the accounts and I'll have to explain to the widow why her year arrived smudged."

She left. Ulyana sat a while longer, listening to the guildhouse breathe: the tick of the bottled thunder cooling on its shelf, the far-off mutter of Bram's voice on the roof, the creak of the sign outside—*VESPER GUILD, EST. BY NECESSITY*—swinging in the wind off the harbor.

They had walked into this building seven years ago with burned feet and a stolen loaf and frostbite in their chest that no fire would fix, because the warmth they'd taken from the miller's boy had gone somewhere even they couldn't reach. They had knocked on the door because it was the last door on the street with light behind it.

Odile had opened it, looked at the half-dead runaway on her step, and said, "Can you hold a pen?"

That was all. *Can you hold a pen.* Not *what are you*, not *what did you do*, not *I can smell the gift on you like smoke on cloth*. Just the question, and then, when Ulyana nodded with cracked lips: "Then come in out of the rain. We're behind on our entries and the last apprentice ran off to marry a lighthouse keeper."

It had taken Ulyana three months to stop flinching at raised voices. A year to stop checking the window every time boots passed in the street. Two years to say the word *home* aloud, and even then only to the cat.

But the guilt had never unpacked its bags. It lived in Ulyana the way the gift lived in them—quiet, coiled, patient. Because here was the truth they carried under every neat column of figures: the guild had taken them in without questions, and questions were exactly what Ulyana deserved. Somewhere in Aldenmoor there was a dog that shook itself apart whenever anyone reached toward it, and a boy who grew up hearing the story of the night the witch's apprentice nearly killed him, and a village that had stood in Maren's yard with torches unlit but *carried*, the way you carry a threat you're saving for later.

Maren had told them to run. Had pressed a bundle of bread and her good cloak into their arms and said, "Go south, past the river, find the city, find people who deal in strange things—they'll know what to do with you"—and then she had turned around and faced the crowd alone, and Ulyana had run, and had never once, in seven years, dared to ask what happened after.

* * *

Sleep came late and went early. Ulyana woke before dawn in the narrow room above the counting room, in the bed they'd slept in since the first week, and lay listening to the guild wake up around them: Pip the runner thumping down the stairs two steps at a time, already late for a delivery of bottled fog; the clatter of the kitchen where Colette swore at the stove in three languages; Bram's slow tread on the roof boards overhead, going up to sit with the weathercock and whatever he actually sat with up there, which Ulyana suspected was memories.

They dressed. Went down. Put water on for tea and found Colette had already done it, and stood shoulder to shoulder with her in the warm kitchen while she talked about a client who wanted to borrow someone else's courage for a wedding and hadn't understood why the price was so high.

"Courage is heavy," Colette said, chopping onions with the violence of a woman settling scores. "People think because it's invisible it weighs nothing. It weighs everything. You want to carry another person's courage to your own wedding? That's double weight. I charged him triple."

"He paid?"

"He cried, and then he paid." She pointed the knife at Ulyana. "You'd have liked the ledger entry. Very satisfying. *One borrowed courage, returned slightly used.*"

Ulyana laughed—a real laugh, surprised out of them—and felt, as they always felt in this kitchen, the terrible unfairness of it. That they got to laugh here. That the guild had folded them in like they belonged, fed them, taught them, defended them, and all of it built on a foundation they hadn't examined because examining it might reveal that the foundation was them—that the scrappy, generous, impossible family of the Vesper Guild had taken in a child whose hands broke things, and never once asked whether the child might break *them*.

The fever had jumped to the dog. What if it jumped to Pip? To Colette? To the whole house?

They kept the gift leashed so tightly that most days they could pretend it wasn't there. A flicker, that was all—warmth-sense, the ability to feel the heat of living things through walls, to nudge a chill out of a joint or a fever-edge off a headache when no one was looking. Small things. Deniable things. Seven years of small deniable mercies performed in secret, each one a coin paid into an account of debt that never seemed to shrink.

The kettle sang. Ulyana poured tea for themselves and, on impulse, a second cup, and carried it up the outside stair to the roof.

Bram sat on his crate by the chimney, as expected, a blanket over his knees despite the season, watching the harbor fog burn off. He was the oldest thing in the guildhouse except the stock—older than Odile's tenure, older than the sign, maybe older than the ledgers. His hands were scarred in patterns Ulyana had never asked about, long parallel lines like the marks of very large claws, healed silver.

He took the tea without looking at them. "You dreamt about the cottage again."

Ulyana nearly scalded themselves. "How—"

"You make the same face you make when you're copying the difficult entries. Like the numbers are personally insulting you." He sipped. "Sit down, ledger-keeper. The fog's doing something worth seeing."

Ulyana sat. Below them the city was emerging from white in patches—rooftops first, then spires, then the long gray ribbon of the river—as if the world were being sketched in by a careful hand. It was beautiful. It was the kind of beauty that hurt, because it reminded Ulyana that they had chosen this view, this roof, these people, and that choosing could be taken away.

"Bram," they said, before courage could fail, "why did the guild take me in?"

"Odile needed a pen-holder."

"You know what I mean."

He did look at them then, and his eyes were brown and deep and unhurried as wells. "No," he said. "I don't think I do. So tell me what you mean, and I'll tell you if I know."

"Why us?" Ulyana's voice came out smaller than intended. "Why does a guild that trades in storms and years take in runaways with—*flaws*—and never ask a single question? Everyone here is running from something. Pip. Colette. Me. Odile collects us like—" they gestured helplessly at the fog, the city, the waking world, "—like strays. And I've never understood it, and lately I can't stop understanding that I *need* to understand it, because if there's a reason, if there's a rule, then maybe there's a limit, and I need to know how close I am to it."

Bram was quiet for a long time. Long enough that the fog finished burning off and the whole harbor lay bare and glittering below them, gulls wheeling, ships crawling in from the deep water.

"There's a fire," he said finally, "that you've never seen."

Ulyana waited.

"Downstairs. Deeper than the strongroom. Older than the guild, older than the crown, older than most of the lies this kingdom tells about itself." He turned the teacup in his scarred hands. "Everything in this house that shouldn't work—every bottled storm, every borrowed year, every impossible thing on every shelf—it works because of that fire. And the fire has rules, and the rules are old, and one of them is that this door stays open to people like you."

"People like me." The words tasted like ash and honey at once. "Gifted."

Bram's gaze went past them, east, to where the hills rose beyond the river—hills Ulyana had never thought much about, brown and green and ordinary.

"In my life," he said, "I have known one creature whose heart burned hotter than that fire downstairs. And she is dying, little ledger-keeper, the way everything old dies: slowly, and then suddenly. So no, I won't tell you today why the guild took you in. But soon—sooner than any of us wants—I'm going to have to show you. And when I do, you'll understand that the question isn't whether there's a limit to how long we'll keep you."

He drank the last of his tea and set the cup down with a click like a lock turning.

"The question is what happens to every gift in this city when the fire goes out."

Below them, in the street, a town crier unfurled a paper and began to shout, and the sound came up the side of the building thin and distorted—but not so distorted that Ulyana couldn't catch the first words, repeated twice, rolling out over the waking city like a stone dropped down a well:

*"By order of the Crown—by order of the Crown—"*

Bram closed his eyes.

"It's starting again," he said.
