## 3. Proclamation at the Market Cross

The morning the criers came, I was counting thunder.

That sounds like a figure of speech. At the Vesper Guild it is not. Jar forty-one, third shelf of the storm-cellar, held a bottled squall off the Graywater coast—fourteen ounces of lightning by weight, though the weight shifted when it sulked. It had been sulking all week. Every time I lifted the ledger to mark its condition, the jar rattled against its lead cradle and the ink shivered on the page, as if the storm knew it was being written about and resented the indignity.

*Condition: stable,* I wrote. *Temperament: poor.*

That was the whole of my morning's work, or should have been. Inventory day came once a season, when Odile locked the front doors, hung the *closed for devotion* sign—which was a lie so old it had worn smooth—and set me loose among the shelves with a pen, an inkpot, and the master ledger that weighed more than the cat. The cat, incidentally, was named Receipts, and she supervised from atop the strongest shelf with the air of one auditing my performance.

I loved these days. I want to be honest about that, because honesty became expensive later. I loved the hush of the guildhall with everyone else upstairs eating breakfast, the smell of cold candle-wax and older things, the way the light came through the storm-glass windows greenish and underwater. I loved knowing where everything was. A widow's borrowed year in its hourglass case, down to its last eleven weeks. A scale of living ember, still faintly warm after two hundred years, wrapped in wool and kept away from anything flammable, which in our establishment meant kept away from nearly everything. A vial of a river's memory, cloudy as regret. None of it was mine. All of it was ours.

And threaded through all of it, quiet as a stitch through cloth, was the small stubborn thing I carried under my ribs and never named. My gift. A flicker, truly—a hedge-spark, nothing like what Maren could do, nothing worth a pyre. When I balanced a column of numbers, I could nudge them true. When a jar of thunder threatened to crack, I could breathe along its seams and settle it. Small magics. Bookkeeping magics. The kind of magic that would never feed a village or stop a fever, only keep ledgers honest and storms asleep.

I told myself, most mornings, that it hardly counted.

I was telling myself exactly that when the bells began.

Not the market bells. Not the hour bells. The *crown* bells—the three deep strokes from the citadel tower that meant the king's voice was about to be spoken in every square of the city at once. I had heard them twice in my life. Once for the old queen's death. Once for a war ending.

Receipts stood up, arched, and left the room. Animals always know first.

* * *

By the time I reached the Market Cross, the crowd had already gone strange—not loud, not yet, but tight, the way a rope goes tight before something breaks. The crier stood on the cross's steps in crown-blue, flanked by two soldiers whose spears were not ceremonial. He unrolled his proclamation with the crisp ceremony of a man who had rehearsed his pauses.

"Hear the word of King Aldric the Third," he called, and his voice carried too well, pitched by practice to reach the back rows and the windows above. "By decree of the Crown and the Court of Inquiry: let it be known throughout the realm that the practice of sorcery is an offense against God and kingdom."

The words landed one at a time, like stones dropped into still water.

"Let all persons gifted with unlawful power present themselves to their parish priest within thirty days, there to make declaration and register their names upon the rolls of the crown."

A woman near me crossed herself. Someone's baby started crying and was hastily hushed, as if even crying might draw attention.

"Those who register shall submit to examination and binding, and shall thereafter live under the crown's watch, in peace."

In peace. The pause he put after it was almost gentle. Almost merciful. That was the part designed to work—to give the frightened ones a door to walk through, and let them believe it was a door out rather than a door shut.

"And those who conceal their gift—" the crier's voice hardened here, professionally, the way a blade hardens, "—or harbor those who conceal it, let them know that the mercy of the crown has limits. Let them be sought. Let them be found. And let them burn."

No one moved. That was the worst of it. Three hundred people standing in the morning sun, and no one moved, because moving meant admitting you had a reason to.

Then, slowly, the crowd began to breathe again, and to dissolve, and everyone walked home very carefully, very normally, looking at nothing and no one, each of us suddenly fluent in the same language of not-seeing.

Except that I saw. Standing there in the thinning crowd, I saw a fishmonger's boy glance at his own hands as if they'd betrayed him. I saw an old woman pull her shawl over her hair and walk faster than her hips wanted to. I saw the way people's eyes flicked sideways—at neighbors, at friends, at the person beside them—as if the proclamation had handed everyone a lantern and told them to hold it up to everyone they'd ever known.

Thirty days. Register or burn.

I thought of the jar of thunder sulking in our cellar. Of the widow's borrowed year. Of the ember-scale, warm as a sleeping bird. Of every impossible thing on our shelves, and the people whose names were written beside them in my careful hand.

Then I did what any sensible bookkeeper does when the accounts turn dangerous.

I went home to hide the books.

* * *

Odile had heard before I got back. Of course she had; she was Guildmaster Odile Vesper, and information reached her the way water reaches low ground. When I came through the front door she was already in the great room, and the guild was already changing shape around her the way a body changes around a wound.

Bram had pulled the storm-shutters. Not the ordinary ones—the inner shutters, iron-banded, that we used for hail-season and had not needed in six years. Perrin was up a ladder pulling down the good ward-chains, the ones braided with cold-iron thread that made your teeth ache if you touched them bare-handed. Tansy had taken the sign down from the door and replaced it with a plain board reading *VESPER & CO., FACTORS IN RARE GOODS*, which was technically true and had been our disguise for longer than I'd been alive.

Nobody was shouting. That was the thing about my family—we didn't shout. We *reorganized*.

"The ledgers," Odile said when she saw me. Not a greeting. An order shaped like a greeting.

"I hid the client books last night," I said. "After the rumor from Harrowgate. They're behind the false wall in the cellar, behind the storm-jars." I hesitated. "The master inventory is still on my desk."

"Bring it to me. Then bring me every scrap of paper in this building with a name on it that shouldn't be read aloud."

I brought her the master ledger, and then I brought her armfuls. Trade records I could defend—those were just commerce, factors buying and selling, dull enough to bore an inquisitor into kindness. But woven through them, in my neat columns, were the truths: *one year, borrowed, repaid in kind; one grief, eased, payment deferred; one small fire, taught to sleep.* The guild's real business. The covenant's business, though I didn't know that word yet, didn't know what slept beneath our shelves. To me it was simply the work. We traded in impossible things because impossible things were what people needed, and someone had to keep the accounts.

Odile took the pages without sentiment. She fed them to the kitchen fire herself, one at a time, watching each catch, and I stood there watching history curl and go orange, and felt something in my chest tear along a seam I hadn't known I had.

"We can rewrite the trade records," I said. My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "But some of those—some of those were the only proof certain things ever happened. The widow's year. If we burn her entry, and she dies, and no one—"

"Then she will have had her year," Odile said, "and no one will have burned her for it. Ulyana." She looked at me over the fire, and for once she didn't look like a guildmaster. She looked tired, and old in the way that people get old all at once, overnight, between one bell and the next. "Paper is a confession waiting to be found. From today, this family keeps its accounts where no inquisitor can seize them." She tapped her own temple. "Here. And here." She tapped mine.

I understood her. I even agreed with her. But I stood there while the fire ate the widow's borrowed year, and I grieved for it like a person, because that is the kind of fool I am.

* * *

We doubled the wards by afternoon. Perrin walked the perimeter muttering the old bindings, re-tying the frayed ones, and I followed with the chalk and salt because my hands needed work or they'd start shaking. The wards weren't much—hedge-magic, really, the kind that makes a hostile eye slide off a doorway and a stranger forget why he stopped—but they were ours, laid down by generations of frightened clever people, and we patched them like sailors patching a hull in weather.

It was while I was chalking the threshold of the cellar stair that I found Bram.

He was sitting on the bottom step in the dark, which was itself alarming, because Bram did not sit. Bram was seventy if he was a day, built like the anchor of a sunk ship, and in forty years of living above him I had never once seen him still. He tended the furnace that heated our water and, less officially, he tended something else—something below, behind the deep door at the end of the cellar corridor that I had been told since childhood was *the cold storage, don't go in, you'll spoil the wine.*

He was holding his hands open in his lap, palms up, the way you sit with a dying animal.

"Bram?" I said. "Are you hurt?"

"No, girl." He didn't look at me. His eyes were fixed on the deep door, and on the thin line of light beneath it—or not light, exactly. Warmth-colored. Flickering. "Come here. Sit. Look."

I sat. I looked. Beneath the door, the glow pulsed, slow and uneven, like breath with a rattle in it.

"It's weaker," Bram said. "You wouldn't have noticed. Most wouldn't. But I've kept her company forty years, and a flame talks to the one who feeds it." His big hands closed slowly, as if closing around something that was already slipping. "She's guttering, Ulyana. Been guttering for years. But this week—" he shook his head. "This week she's hungry."

"What is she?" I asked. And then, because I was raised honest in a house of secrets: "What's down there, Bram?"

He was quiet long enough that the glow pulsed four more times, each one fainter-seeming than the last. Somewhere above us, muffled by iron shutters, the city went on being afraid.

"That," Bram said finally, "is a longer story than tonight allows. And tonight—" he pushed himself up off the step with a grunt, joints cracking like green wood, and looked down at me with eyes that had seen whatever it was I hadn't. "Tonight, the story doesn't matter. What matters is what I've seen before. I was a young man the last time the crown came for the gifted. I watched them take my mother's whole street. Registration first. Then examination. Then the pyres." He said it flatly, the way you recite a list you've hated for fifty years. "They always start polite. They always end in smoke."

He turned toward the deep door, and rested one broad palm against the wood, gently, the way you'd touch a horse's neck.

"So when you ask me what's coming," he said, "I'll tell you what I told the others at supper, and it's all I'm going to say, because saying more would need a fire and a bottle and a night we may not have."

He looked back at me over his shoulder, lit from below by that failing, hungry warmth.

"It's starting again."

* * *

That night the guild ate early and ate together, which we always did in bad weather, literal or otherwise. Odile sat at the head and said grace, and for the first time since I'd come to her door as a half-starved runaway, her grace included a line about *delivering us from the hands of our enemies*, which is not standard in the Vesper household.

Tansy joked. That was Tansy's way—she'd joke at funerals, she'd joke at the end of the world, and it wasn't cowardice, it was ballast. Perrin didn't joke, which was worse. Perrin counted things when he was frightened, under his breath, and I caught him doing it: *thirty days, twenty-nine after tonight, twenty-eight—*

And Bram ate slowly, and watched the hearth.

Our hearth-fire was ordinary oak and coal, but he watched it the way other men watch a sick child, and once—I was the only one who saw—he leaned toward it and murmured something too low to catch, and the flames leaned toward him. Just slightly. Just for a moment. Like a plant toward sun.

I looked at my own hands under the table, at the ink stains and the small callus where my pen rested, hands that could nudge a column of numbers true and settle a jar of sulking thunder. Small magics. Bookkeeping magics. Hardly worth naming.

Hardly worth burning for.

Except that the proclamation hadn't asked whether your gift was large or small. It had asked one question, in the crier's bright carrying voice, in every square of the city at once: *what are you hiding?*

And I had spent seven years building a life out of the answer *nothing.*

After supper I climbed to my room under the eaves and took out the one object I owned that had no business existing: a stub of tallow candle Maren had given me at fourteen, charmed so it would only light for me. I hadn't lit it in years. Lighting it felt like opening a letter you already knew contained bad news.

I lit it anyway. The little flame stood up straight and steady, warm gold, utterly indifferent to kings and proclamations, and I sat there in the dark with my small stubborn flicker of a self burning openly in my cupped hands, and I let myself want, just for one minute, what I could not have: to carry it downstairs and say *look, this is me, all of me, and I'm staying anyway.*

Then I pinched it out, because thirty days is not a long time, and somewhere across the city a priest was sharpening a quill over a registration roll with my name not on it yet.

Below me, through the floorboards, I heard Bram's boots on the cellar stairs, going down, going deep, keeping his nightly vigil with the thing behind the door that glowed and guttered and, this week, was hungry.

It's starting again, he'd said.

I lay awake in the dark and did not yet know how right he was.
