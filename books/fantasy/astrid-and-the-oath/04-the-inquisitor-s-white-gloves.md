## 4. The Inquisitor's White Gloves

The river fog came off the Vesper water like something poured, and Sera Vance watched it from the deck of the *Charity of Ash* as the city assembled itself out of the grey—first the bridge towers, then the bell spires, then the long dark spine of the Guildhall quarter where, if the reports were true, a ledger-keeper had been trading in magic for longer than the crown had kept records of it.

She put on her gloves before the gangplank dropped.

They were white kidskin, supple as a lie, and she had owned them for eleven years. She had owned them since the morning she had knelt beside Tomas in the ashes of the alder grove and found that his hands—his clever, restless, wonderful hands—had gone cold while the rest of the world was still burning. She had owned them since she understood, in the wordless way grief understands things, that she would never again touch anything that mattered with her bare skin. Not suspects. Not evidence. Not the small warm weight of another person's trust.

The gloves were not a penance. She had stopped believing in penance around the same time she'd stopped believing the crown's version of the war. The gloves were a *discipline*. They were the line she drew each morning between the woman who had loved a gifted man and lost him, and the officer who could not afford to love anything she might be ordered to burn.

"Inquisitor." The harbormaster's man met her at the plank, sweating despite the cold, holding a lantern he didn't seem to know what to do with. "The magistrate sends his regards. There's a—there's a carriage, if you'd prefer—"

"I'd prefer the streets," Sera said.

The city, when she walked into it, smelled of coal smoke and wet stone and frying onions, and beneath all of it, faint as a memory, something sweeter. Ozone, almost. The smell before lightning. She had smelled it once before in her life, in a cottage in the Aldervale, on a night she had spent the last eleven years not thinking about.

She thought about it now.

* * *

The magistrate's office in Vesper City was a room designed to make petitioners feel small, which meant it was the wrong room entirely for receiving Sera Vance. She stood in the middle of it with her travel coat still on and let the magistrate's clerk read the warrant aloud in a voice that trembled at the wrong places, and when he finished she took the document from him without asking.

"Read it back to me," she said. "The operative clause."

"The—the crown commands all persons possessed of unlawful gifts to present themselves for registration within—"

"The other clause."

He swallowed. "All persons found to be *aiding, harboring, concealing, or profiting from* unlawful gifts shall be held to the same standard as the gifted themselves."

"That's the one," Sera said. "That's why I'm here."

She had read the file on the crossing. It was thin, which bothered her more than a thick file would have. Thick files meant the crown was watching. Thin files meant the crown had been *not watching*—deliberately, for years, the way one does not look too long at a door one suspects is load-bearing. The file named no guild. It named no names. It named only a function: a *ledger-keeper*. Someone in Vesper City who kept accounts of impossible transactions. Bottled storms. Borrowed years. The file's informant—anonymous, of course; informants always were, and always lied about the things that mattered—claimed the ledger-keeper could make a candle flame stand up and dance. Claimed it like a joke, the way people describe miracles when they're frightened of them.

"Registration opens in nine days," the clerk said. "The criers go out tomorrow."

"I know the schedule."

"The pyre court has been—restored. The masons finished the platform last week. There was some question about whether—given that we haven't held a sentencing in—"

"Given that we haven't held a *murder* in a hundred years," Sera said, "yes. There would be questions."

The clerk went very still. She watched him decide, in real time, whether she had blasphemed or merely spoken plainly, and watched him settle on the safer interpretation. That was the thing about the white gloves. People assumed they meant she was fastidious. Let them. Fastidiousness was a fine disguise for a conscience.

"Where do I begin?" she asked.

"Begin?"

"When I hunt a thing," Sera said, "I begin where the thing is *wanted*. Every market in this city has someone who sells what people are ashamed to need. Show me the Vesper quarter at night. Show me the pawnshops that never ask questions. Show me where a frightened person with a gift goes when they can't go to a temple."

* * *

She spent three days in the Vesper quarter before she found the first thread, and the thread was a boy of perhaps nine who was selling luck.

Not charms, not talismans—those were everywhere, stamped tin and rabbit bone, honest fraud. This boy sat on an overturned crate outside a chandlery with a bowl of river water in his lap, and when a fisherman dropped a copper in the bowl, the boy would swirl the water with one finger and the water would *agree with him*. Sera watched from the shadow of an awning for an hour. The swirls were small. A current that bent toward the winning boat. A knot of foam shaped like the fisherman's dead wife's face, just for a moment, just long enough to make the old man weep and pay again. Small magic. Hedge magic. The kind that got people like Tomas killed.

The kind that got people like Tomas to kill *themselves*, trying to do one more small kindness with it.

She crossed the street. The boy saw the white gloves first—that was always how it went, the gloves announced her before her face did—and his bowl was in his hands and his feet were under him before she'd reached the crate.

"I'm not going to touch you," Sera said. "Or the bowl."

"You're the burn-lady." His chin came up, defiant, and his eyes were wet. "Mam said the burn-lady came on the gray ship."

"Your mother is smarter than the crown's criers. That's not a high bar." She crouched, keeping her distance, keeping her hands visible and still. "I'm looking for someone who keeps books. Accounts. Someone who *buys* things like what you do."

The boy's face did something complicated—fear and longing fighting for the same ground—and lost to neither. "I don't know anyone like that."

"That's the best lie I've heard in Vesper City, and I've been here three days." She set a silver coin on the crate, not in the bowl. "I'm not going to ask you again. But I'll leave this here, and I'll leave my question here, and you can decide which one to bring home to your mother."

She walked away before he could answer, because the answer didn't matter. What mattered was the *look*. She had seen it before, in the Aldervale, on Tomas's face when she'd asked him—only once, only stupidly, only in love—what it was like. The look of someone standing in a doorway between two rooms, hearing voices in both, unable to live in either.

She had loved that look. She had hated it. She had been too young to know the difference, and by the time she'd learned, the grove was ash and the gloves were on her hands and the crown had a new instrument with a spotless reputation.

* * *

The fourth night, she found the pawnshop.

It was called the Kindly Scale, which was the kind of name that meant it was neither, and the man behind the counter had the flat, patient face of someone who had watched a great many things come through his door and judged none of them. Sera laid her warrant on the glass. He read it upside down, which told her something, and slid it back without touching it, which told her more.

"Inquisitor," he said. "We're honest traders here."

"Then this will be quick." She set a folded paper beside the warrant. On it, in her own careful hand, she had copied the informant's phrase: *ledger-keeper, traffics in the impossible.* "I'm told there's a house in this quarter that deals in bottled storms. Borrowed years. That sort of inventory."

The pawnbroker's face did not change, which was itself a change; something behind it had gone very quiet, the way a household goes quiet when the wrong knock comes at the door. "Bottled storms," he repeated. "In Vesper City."

"So I'm told."

"Then you were told a story, Inquisitor. People tell stories about this quarter. It's an old quarter. Old quarters collect rumors the way ships collect barnacles." He smiled, and it reached his eyes, and it reached nothing behind his eyes. "I could sell you a bottled storm, if you like. I have a very good one. Caught it off the Teeth in a bad year. Costs more than your warrant's worth in wax."

"I don't want the storm."

"No," he said. "They never do."

She left the coin question with him too, and the paper, and her card, and walked back out into the fog with the ozone smell stronger now, threading the coal smoke like a vein of silver in lead. It was coming from somewhere in the quarter, faint and steady, like a hearth that never went out. Like a fire that had been burning, patiently, under the whole city's notice, for a very long time.

*Back at the Kindly Scale*, after she'd gone, the pawnbroker turned the silver coin over in his fingers and did not put it in his till.

* * *

That night, in the room the crown had taken for her above the magistrate's offices, Sera did what she did every night in every city: she took off the gloves, one finger at a time, and set them on the sill, and looked at her hands.

They were ordinary hands. A burn scar on the left palm, old, from the grove. She had gotten it reaching for Tomas when the light took him—reaching, and closing on nothing but heat, and learning in one white instant what the crown's mercy actually was. Eleven years, and the scar had faded to a pale seam. Eleven years, and she still slept with the palm turned down.

The file on her desk said *ledger-keeper*, and beneath it, in her own hand, she had written a question she had not shown anyone: *What happens to the gifts the crown never catches?* Because that was the question under the question. The hunt was not for the hedge-witches and the luck-selling boys; they were kindling, and the crown knew it. The hunt was for whatever kept them. Whatever gathered them up and *accounted* for them, the way you account for anything you intend to keep.

Someone in this city was keeping them.

She thought of Tomas, who had healed a fevered child in the Aldervale and been seen doing it, and who had run to her first—not to a temple, not to a road out, but to *her*, a magistrate's daughter with no power but a fast horse—and she had gotten him to the treeline and no farther. She thought of how he had turned around at the edge of the burning grove and looked back at her, and how his face had not been afraid. It had been *apologetic*. As though he were sorry to leave her holding all that love with nowhere to put it.

She had put it in the gloves. She had worn it every day since.

"Ledger-keeper," she said to the dark room, and the word sat in the air like the smell of ozone. Somewhere out in the Vesper quarter, under the fog, under the coal smoke, under a hundred years of the crown's determined not-looking, a fire was burning that kept accounts of its own.

Tomorrow the criers would read the proclamation at the market cross. Tomorrow the whole city would learn that the age of not-looking was over. And somewhere in the Vesper quarter, a boy was deciding whether to bring a silver coin or a question home to his mother, and a pawnbroker was holding a coin he hadn't banked, and a fire was burning low.

Sera put the gloves back on. They went on easier than they came off; they always had. She flexed her fingers once, feeling the line of them, the discipline of them, and blew out the candle.

In the dark, she allowed herself the thing she allowed herself every night, no more: one minute of Tomas's voice, saying her name, in the grove, before the light.

Then she was the Inquisitor again, and the Inquisitor slept badly, as she always did, with her palm turned down and her gloves within reach, waiting for morning to give her a reason to wear them.
