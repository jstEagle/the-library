## 7. Sera Finds a Thread

The rain had not stopped since the storm that wasn't.

Sera Vance stood at the window of her rented room above the chandler's shop on Candlewick Row and watched water sheet down the glass, and she thought about weather. That was the trouble with this city. It made you think about weather. In the capital, weather was simply weather—gray, indifferent, the sky's own business. Here in Vesperhold, three days after a bottled tempest had torn loose in the Saltmarket and been put away again by no one anyone could name, weather had become testimony. Every gust was a rumor. Every cloud had a witness.

She had read the reports four times. A storm in a jar, they said—a storm *sold*, if the whispers were to be believed—had cracked open in a debtor's cellar and come out angry. Hail in high summer. Wind that bent cart axles. And then, at the height of it, when the thing should have flattened half the street: it stopped. Not spent. *Stopped.* Gathered itself like a scolded dog and went back into its bottle, or so the story ran, because a young person in a gray coat had stood in the middle of the chaos with their hands out and their eyes shut, and the storm had obeyed them.

The witnesses could not agree on much. Height, hair, whether the gray coat was wool or linen, whether the figure was man, woman, or neither. But they agreed on the hands. They agreed on the stillness. They agreed that when it was over, the figure had looked *afraid*—not of the storm, but of the crowd—and had walked away fast, and no one had dared follow.

Someone had put the wind back in its bottle with their bare will. And then they had run.

Sera turned from the window. Her white gloves lay on the table where she had left them, folded one inside the other, immaculate. She did not put them on until she worked. That was the ritual, and rituals were the spine of what she did: gloves first, then questions, then the ledger of names, then the fire if the ledger demanded it. Clean hands for dirty work. The gloves were not vanity. They were a promise—that nothing she touched would be touched carelessly, that every life she handled would be handled as if it mattered, because once, terribly, it had.

*Ilse,* she thought, the way she thought it every morning, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth. *Ilse, who warmed her own tea without a kettle.*

She took up her coat instead of the gloves. There were interviews to conduct, and interviews were better done ungloved. People trusted bare hands. It was one of the small hypocrisies of the trade that she had long ago stopped apologizing for, even to herself.

* * *

The Vesper quarter began where the cobblestones gave up pretending.

The streets narrowed, leaned together overhead until the sky was a ribbon of wet gray, and the buildings wore their age openly—timber blackened by old fires, shutters patched with boat planking, laundry strung between chimneys like bunting for a holiday nobody celebrated. Sera walked it slowly, hatless despite the drizzle, letting herself be seen. An inquisitor who skulked learned only what people wanted hidden. An inquisitor who walked in the open, patient and polite, learned what people were afraid of. Fear was more honest than secrecy. Fear always left tracks.

The Saltmarket still bore the storm's signature. She read it the way other women read embroidery: the shutter hanging by one hinge, wrenched outward rather than battered in; the hail-scarred plaster, pocked in a spiral rather than a scatter; the smell under the wet stone, faint but unmistakable to her trained nose—ozone and something older beneath it, a mineral tang like the air before lightning, or like the inside of a mountain. Magic left residue the way snails left silver trails. Most people walked through it blind. Sera had been taught to see, and seeing was a curse she carried with reasonable grace.

At the corner of Brine Street, an old woman was sweeping hailwater out of her doorway with the grim rhythm of someone who had done it all her life and expected to do it again tomorrow.

"Madam." Sera inclined her head. "Inquisitor Vance, crown service. Might I ask you about the night of the storm?"

The broom paused. The old woman's eyes did the arithmetic Sera had seen a thousand times—the white gloves at the belt, the crown badge, the polite voice—and arrived at the only answer that mattered. "Don't know anything."

"You swept this step the morning after. You found hail here, I'd wager, though we've had no thunderclouds worth the name in six weeks."

"Cellar flooded. Bad drains." The broom resumed, harder than before.

"Bad drains," Sera agreed mildly. "That would also explain the salt crust on your lintel. Storm-water leaves salt, madam, when the storm comes off the sea. Yours came from somewhere else entirely." She crouched, unhurried, and touched the stone of the doorstep with two bare fingers. Cold lingered there. Cold that had no business lingering in high summer. "I'm not here for you. Whoever sold you that storm broke crown law, yes—but so did whoever *made* it, and I want the maker far more than the buyer. Help me find them, and your name never enters my ledger."

The broom stopped again. This time it stayed stopped.

The old woman looked down the street both ways, an instinct so old it predated the current hunt by generations, and lowered her voice to a thread. "It weren't bought from no hedge-witch. It came from the guild."

"What guild?"

"The Vesper Guild. On Lantern Close. They—" The word caught, and the woman's face did something complicated: fear and loyalty wrestling behind wrinkles, loyalty losing by a margin. "They keep accounts. Odd accounts. My late husband bought his good years there, if you take my meaning. Bought back his strength when the wasting took it. Three years of it. Died gentle at seventy instead of slow at sixty-seven." Her jaw set. "You'll not have him burned for it. He's dead. He's past your fire."

"I don't burn the dead, madam. I record them." Sera rose. "The guild keeps ledgers, you say. Of borrowed years. Of storms."

"Of things that can't be had any other way." The old woman's chin lifted, defiant and terrified at once, and Sera recognized the expression with a lurch she kept off her face. Ilse's mother had worn it exactly so, the day the king's men came to the cottage door and Ilse's mother had stood in it with her arms spread and said, *she's just a girl, she's just my girl, whatever she is she's mine.*

"The guild," the old woman said, "has been in Vesperhold longer than the crown has bothered to count. Whatever you think you'll find there, inquisitor—you'll find neighbors. Think on that before you bring your fire to a close full of them."

* * *

Lantern Close was a courtyard disguised as a street, entered under an archway hung with actual lanterns—dozens of them, glass and horn and paper, unlit in the daylight but waiting, a congregation of small faithful fires. Sera stood beneath them for a long moment and felt the hair rise on her forearms.

Wards. Old ones. Not the crude chalk circles of hedge-craft, which flared and fizzled and announced themselves like drunkards. These were woven into the archway itself, into the lantern-hangers, into the very grain of the wood, and they were quiet the way a sleeping hound is quiet. She could walk through. She was almost certain she could walk through. But the wards would know she had, and something on the other side would be told.

So she didn't go through. Not yet. Instead she spent the afternoon where inquisitors do their truest work: in the taverns and stairwells and washhouses at the edge of the close, buying cider she didn't drink and listening.

The Vesper Guild, the neighborhood said—or didn't say, precisely; the neighborhood said everything *around* it, the way a river says the shape of a stone. The guild paid the lamplighters' winter bonus. The guild had bought the widow Tarn's debts and burned the notes at her husband's funeral. The guild's scrivener could be seen of a morning carrying bread to the fisher children on the quay, and if the bread was sometimes warm when it shouldn't have been, well, ovens held heat, didn't they? The guildmaster was stern and fair and had buried two husbands and out-negotiated a customs magistrate into tears. Nobody knew where the goods came from. Everybody knew better than to ask.

And everyone, when they thought Sera wasn't watching, glanced toward the close with the same expression: love wearing fear's clothing.

"They're good people," whispered a washerwoman, wringing sheets in water gone gray. "Whatever else, they're *good* people."

"So are most of the people on my lists," Sera said quietly. "Goodness isn't the question. The law is."

"And what's the law worth," the washerwoman muttered, not quite under her breath, "when it burns girls for warming tea?"

Sera's hands went still in her lap.

The washerwoman froze, realizing what she'd said and to whom. The washhouse seemed to hold its breath. Somewhere a kettle whistled and was hastily silenced.

"Warming tea," Sera repeated. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, later. "Who told you about warming tea?"

"N-no one. Forget it. I say foolish things."

"Yes," Sera said. "People often do, around me." She left coin on the bench, more than the cider cost, and walked out into the drizzle with her heart beating wrong.

Because there it was. The thread. *Girls who warm tea.* Ilse had warmed tea. Ilse had warmed her hands over nothing on cold mornings, cupped around invisible heat, laughing, saying *it's nothing, Sera, it's less than nothing, it's just how I am*—and Sera had loved her for it, had loved the smallness of it, the harmlessness, the sheer domestic innocence of a gift that could do no more than make a teacup pleasant. And then the fever year, and Ilse trying to pull the sickness out of her little brother with those same harmless hands, and the gift turning out not to be small at all, only *unpracticed*—and Ilse burning out like a lamp with its wick cut, three days of light and then dark, seventeen years old, and Sera holding her hand while it cooled.

*Less than nothing. Just how I am.*

The crown called it contagion. The pyre courts called it abomination. Sera had signed the oath and taken the gloves and told herself she hunted the dangerous ones, the storm-sellers and curse-mongers, never the tea-warmers—and every list she filed grew longer, and every list had tea-warmers on it, because the law did not distinguish, and she filed them anyway, and the filing had a sound like a small door closing.

Now a whole quarter of Vesperhold spoke of tea-warming like a memory, like a kindness, like something worth protecting. And a storm had obeyed a stranger's hands in the Saltmarket, and run home afterward like a dog that knows its yard.

Home. The word snagged. She turned it over as she walked. The storm hadn't dissipated—it had *returned*. Bottled things didn't return to bottles on their own. Someone had called them back, and calls answered fastest along lines already drawn. Which meant the gray-coated stranger in the Saltmarket had not merely been gifted. They had been *connected*—to the storm, to the guild, to whatever web of quiet power the Vesper quarter had been spinning for longer than the crown had bothered to count.

A ledger-keeper, her informants in the capital had said. A scrivener who trafficked in impossible things. Find the pen, they'd told her, and you find the hand.

* * *

Dusk came down on Lantern Close like a lid. The lanterns lit themselves—one, then ten, then all of them at once, a soft gold blooming under the arch with no lamplighter anywhere in sight, and Sera, watching from the shadow of the chandler's awning across the way, felt her breath go shallow.

Not magic performed. Magic *inhabited*. The light wasn't cast; it was kept, the way a hearth is kept, and it welcomed the evening home.

Through the arch, in the courtyard beyond, she could see a slice of ordinary life: a tall old man with a keeper's stoop crossing toward some inner door, moving with the careful gait of someone who tended something fragile; a girl of perhaps twelve chasing a cat; a figure at an upper window, gray-coated, setting a lamp in the glass, whose face turned briefly toward the street—

Too far. Too dark. But the posture snagged something in Sera's memory, some silhouette from a crowd report, hands held slightly away from the body, watchful, *afraid*—and then the curtain moved and the slice of courtyard was only lamplight and shadow.

She wrote nothing down. That was the discipline within the discipline: never write a name before you can speak it to a face. But in the ledger of her mind, in the column reserved for entries she could not yet justify, she inked three words in a hand only she would ever read:

*The gray coat.*

Tomorrow she would request the guild's trade registry through proper channels, and the channels would stall, and the stalling would tell her more than the registry would. Tomorrow she would interview the lamplighters and the harbor clerk and the widow Tarn. Tomorrow she would stand under the arch with her white gloves on and ask, politely, to see the ledgers of impossible things.

Tonight she stood a moment longer in the wet dark, looking at the small gold congregation of lanterns, and let herself feel what she felt, which was not hatred and was not duty and was not justice. It was recognition. Somewhere behind that arch was a household of people keeping each other's secrets warm, exactly the way Ilse had once warmed her hands over nothing, saying *it's just how I am*—and Sera had believed her, had loved her for it, and had lost her anyway, and the crown had sent Sera here to make sure it never happened again, by which the crown meant: make sure it ends.

Her breath fogged the chill air. Across the close, the gray-coated figure's lamp moved away from the window.

"One of you started a storm in my city," Sera said softly, to no one, to the lanterns, to a dead girl seventeen years gone. "Show me it wasn't what I think it is. Show me a reason."

The lanterns burned on, warm and silent, keeping their secrets.

She pulled up her collar and went to write her report—everything except the three words. Those she kept.
