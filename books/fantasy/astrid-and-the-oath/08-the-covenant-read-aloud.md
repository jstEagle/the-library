## 8. The Covenant Read Aloud

Bram came for me at the third bell of the night, when the guildhall had gone quiet enough that I could hear the bottled thunder ticking in its crate like a heart counting down to something.

He didn't knock. He never knocked. He simply filled the doorway of my ledger-room, a mountain of a man made soft by years, his beard singed at the edges as always from the dragon's breath he stood too close to on purpose, and he said, "Bring your spectacles, ledger-keeper. You're going to want to see every word."

I looked up from the widow's borrowed year — I'd been re-counting it again, though I knew to the hour how much remained — and saw something in his face I had never seen there before. Bram was the steadiest thing in my life. He was bedrock with a pitchfork. But tonight his hands were not still.

"Bram?"

"Odile's asleep. Good. This isn't hers to carry yet." He turned without waiting for me, which was also like him, and after a moment I blew out my candle and followed him into the dark.

* * *

The undercroft beneath the guildhall was older than the guildhall. Everyone said so; no one could prove it. The stairs went down farther than the building stood tall, and the walls down there were not brick but something darker, veined with a faint warmth, as if the stone itself remembered fire. I had been down twice in six years — once to fetch storm-glass for Odile, once when a rat died somewhere and needed finding — and both times I had felt the same thing: a pressure against my chest, gentle but insistent, like a hand asking permission.

Tonight the pressure was stronger. Tonight it felt less like asking.

Bram carried a lamp whose flame burned low and blue, which should have been impossible, and at the bottom of the stairs he stopped before a wall I had always taken for the end of the world and pressed his palm flat against it.

"Ledger-keeper," he said quietly, "how much do you know about why this guild exists?"

"We trade in impossible things," I recited, because it was true and because I needed a moment. "We keep accounts honest in a dishonest city. We shelter people who need—"

"Who need what?" He turned his head. In the blue light his eyes were deep wells. "Say it plainly, girl. Who does this guild shelter?"

My mouth went dry. Six years of careful silence sat on my tongue like a stone. "People," I managed. "People who are... different."

"Gifted." He said the word the way other men said *heretic* or *traitor*, except that Bram's voice held no fear of it at all — only a kind of tired tenderness, the way you'd say the name of someone you'd buried long ago and still missed. "Gifted folk. Say it. No one's listening but the stones, and the stones already know."

"Gifted folk," I whispered. Saying it aloud, even here, even now, made my skin prickle from wrist to shoulder, where my own small stubborn flicker lived — the little warmth I had spent six years pretending wasn't mine. "Why are you telling me this? Why now?"

"Because the criers came today." His jaw worked. "Because the hunt is starting again, and because you balance our books better than anyone since old Corvane himself, and because—" He stopped. Started again. "Because you deserve to know what you've been keeping the accounts *for*. And because if the worst comes, you'll be the one holding the ledgers when I'm not here to explain them. So come. See what you've been guarding."

The wall opened.

Not dramatically — nothing about Bram was dramatic. It simply swung inward on hinges older than any hinge had a right to be, groaning like a waking animal, and beyond it was a room I had never imagined: small, round, domed, lit by a single ember resting in a dish of black iron. Not a fire. An *ember* — orange-red, pulsing slow as a sleeping heartbeat, and around it the air bent slightly, the way air bends over a road in high summer.

And in the center of the room, on a stand of dragon-bone, lay a book.

I have handled impossible things for six years. I have inventoried bottled storms and borrowed years and a scale of living ember that hummed when I touched it. Nothing — nothing — prepared me for the way my body reacted to that book. My gift woke all along my arm like a cat stretching, pressing toward it, and I actually staggered, caught myself on the doorframe, and heard Bram make a sound low in his throat that might have been grief.

"So it knows you," he said softly. "Good. That would have been harder to explain."

"What *is* it?"

"The oldest ledger we have. Older than Odile. Older than her master, and hers, and hers, back and back until the names stop being names people use." He lifted it with both hands, reverent, and set it on a reading table worn smooth by centuries of exactly this. "The first ledger. The covenant itself. Sit down, Ulyana. This will take a while, and you'll want to be sitting for the end."

* * *

The book was bound in something I didn't recognize — dark, faintly scaled, warm to the touch — and its pages were not paper. They were vellum, maybe, or maybe something thinner and more alive, and they were covered in two hands of writing. One was human ink, brown and faded, looping script that changed style every few pages as generations of keepers took up the pen. The other—

The other was burned in. Letters of scorch and gold, as if written with a fingertip of living flame, and they did not fade. They shimmered when I moved, like coals breathing.

"The gold is the dragon's hand," Bram said. "Ember-of-Endings wrote the first terms herself, before the crown had a crown. The brown is ours. Every guildmaster adds their oath. Every keeper signs the witness page." He turned to the last brown-written page, and there, in a list going back further than kingdoms, were hundreds of signatures — and near the bottom, in Odile's sharp upright hand, and below that, in a younger version of Bram's own blunt scrawl.

"There's space left," I said stupidly.

"There's always space left. That's rather the point." He drew the lamp closer. "Read the first page. Aloud. The covenant wants to be spoken; it settles better in the mind that way. And I—" his voice roughened, "—I have read it alone too many times. Words like these shouldn't live in one person's chest."

So I read.

> **Herein is struck the bargain between the Last of the Wyrms and the Hidden Folk of the City of Bells.**
>
> **Let it be known that the gifted are hunted in every age, and that hiding is a slow death, and that burning is a fast one. Let it be known that the Wyrm Ember-of-Endings, who has seen the first war and mourns it, offers a third way.**
>
> **Let each gifted soul who chooses it bring their gift to the Wyrm's flame, and let the flame hold it — not extinguished, but banked, as a coal sleeps in ash. The gift shall not die. The gift shall sleep, and the sleeper shall dream in safety, and the hunter shall find only ordinary folk doing ordinary work.**
>
> **In exchange, the flame shall be fed and tended and never left alone, and the Hidden Folk shall keep the Wyrm's last years warm, and no gift shall be taken unwillingly, for a stolen gift poisons the fire that holds it.**
>
> **This is the covenant. Let those who swear it swear truly, for the fire remembers oaths longer than flesh remembers faces.**

I stopped. My voice had gone thin somewhere in the middle of it, and the room seemed larger than it had been, and smaller, both at once.

"The goods," I said. My merchant's mind reached for numbers the way a drowning man reaches for rope. "The bottled storms. The borrowed years. The ember-scale. Those aren't stock. They're—"

"Gifts," Bram said. "Bound gifts, drawn out of the fire in small amounts, shaped into sellable form by craft and wards. A storm some weather-witch gave up forty years ago, portioned into glass. Years a dying woman surrendered so her grandchildren wouldn't starve. Every impossible thing in our inventory is somebody's surrendered power, kept safe and put to work." He smiled, and it was the saddest smile I have ever seen on him. "You thought we were merchants. We're *custodians*, girl. The shop is the smallest part of it."

"And the people?" I thought of Tamsin in the kitchen, who sang to bread and made it rise twice as high as it should. Of Odile, who could read a lie in a man's pulse. Of myself, hiding my flicker behind careful bookkeeping. "The guild members. Are we all—"

"Not all. Some are just folk who needed family. But most." He tapped the witness page. "Most brought their gift here young and scared, the way you nearly did, if I'm any judge of the way you flinch when lightning sells well."

I should have denied it. Six years of practice said deny it, deflect it, laugh it off. Instead I pulled my sleeve down over my wrist and said nothing, and Bram nodded as if I'd confessed everything, which I suppose I had.

"How many?" I asked. "How many gifts are in the fire?"

His silence answered before he did. "Four hundred and eleven," he said finally. "That's the count as of the last tending. Four hundred and eleven sleeping gifts, some of them older than the city walls. Weather-work and healing and shape-shifting and things with no common name. Four hundred and eleven people walking around this city as ordinary as bread, because their power sleeps in an ember downstairs."

"That's beautiful," I said, and meant it, and then — because I am a ledger-keeper, because balancing books teaches you that every column has a matching column — I asked the question that had begun to crawl up my spine the moment I read the word *banked*. "Bram. What happens to a banked coal when the fire goes out?"

* * *

He didn't answer right away. He rose, went to the iron dish, and knelt beside the ember the way you'd kneel beside a sick parent. Up close I could see what I hadn't seen from the door: the ember was smaller than it should have been. There was grey in it. Ash forming *inside* the flame, which I am told flames do not do.

"She's dying," Bram said simply. "Dragons live a very long time, but not forever, and Ember-of-Endings was old when the first war ended. She gave almost everything she had to end it. What's left is what holds four hundred and eleven gifts — and her." He stroked the air above the ember without touching it. "She's been guttering for thirty years. Slowly. Like a candle in a drafty church. I've fed her everything a keeper can feed a fire — oak and rowan and grief and time — and she burns on, but lower every winter. Lower every year."

"Bram." My voice came out strange. "What happens when she goes out?"

He looked at me over his shoulder, and I watched him decide to tell me the truth, and I understood in that moment that this decision — this exact decision, made over and over across centuries by keepers kneeling where he knelt — was the real covenant. The words in the book were just its shadow.

"When the fire dies," he said, "every gift wakes at once."

The words landed in the silent room like stones into still water.

"All of them. All four hundred and eleven. Unbound, untended, unmastered — most of them asleep so long their owners have forgotten how to hold them, some of them owned by people long dead, drifting loose with nowhere to anchor." He turned fully to face me. "A weather-gift wakes in a baker. A fire-gift wakes in a child. Gifts that belonged to the dead wake in *nothing*, and go looking for anything warm to pour into. The first war started over less than that, Ulyana. The first war started because power woke up leaderless and frightened, and frightened power breaks things. The sky broke. Do you understand me? The *sky broke*, and it took three dragons and a generation of dead to mend it."

I sat very still. Somewhere above us, muffled through ancient stone, the guildhall settled and creaked — Tamsin banking the kitchen oven, Odile turning in her sleep, the whole dear fragile structure of my life going about its night without knowing it stood on a countdown.

"How long?" I asked. It was the ledger-keeper's question, the only question I had. "How long does she have?"

"A year. Perhaps two. Perhaps less, if the hunt drives fear through the city — fear feeds on her somehow, or starves her, I've never understood which. She says the city's feelings get into the flame." He laughed, short and bitter. "An ancient wyrm, and she's sensitive."

"Why show me this tonight?" I whispered. "Why not Odile? Why me?"

"Because Odile knows. She's known for twenty years, and she carries it the way she carries everything — alone, and behind a locked face, and it's eating her alive, and she thinks she must solve it alone because that's what guildmasters do." His voice gentled. "But you, girl. You keep the books. You see the whole shape of things — every debt, every credit, every entry that doesn't balance. And you're young enough to be angry about this instead of just tired." He knelt there, huge and humble, in the ember-light. "I showed you because someone has to start asking the question nobody in six hundred years has dared ask out loud: *what do we do when the fire dies?* And I'm too old to be the one who asks it. I'm the one who tends. Tenders don't ask. Keepers of accounts ask."

I looked at the ember. At its grey-threaded heart. At the book open on its bone stand, six hundred years of oaths stacked like sediment, and that empty space at the bottom of the witness page waiting patiently for names that hadn't been born yet.

Every impossible thing in our inventory was a person's surrendered self. Every member of my found family was a secret kept safe by a dying flame. The belonging I had purchased with six years of secrecy — the warm kitchen, the honest work, the place where no one asked questions because questions were dangerous — all of it rested on a single coal that was going out, and when it went out, everything hidden would break loose at once, in a city full of criers shouting *register or burn*.

We weren't a family sitting safe behind good walls.

We were a family standing on a fuse.

"The hunt," I said slowly, and my mind was moving now the way it moved over a ledger, line by line, finding the entries that didn't match. "If the crown takes gifted folk north and burns them — what happens to their gifts? The ones already bound. If the *owners* die, does the binding—"

"I don't know," Bram said, and the words seemed to cost him physically. "No keeper has ever had to know. The fire has never died while the covenant held. We may be about to learn whether a bound gift survives its owner's burning, and girl, I pray to every god including the one sleeping in that dish that we don't learn it the easy way."

The ember pulsed. Once. Twice. And I would swear on every ledger I've ever balanced that it dimmed, just slightly, as if it had heard us — as if the old dragon dreaming inside it had turned over in her sleep and murmured something in a language made of heat.

Above us, faint through the stone, a bell began to ring. Not the guild's bell. A city bell — the alarm pattern, the one that meant soldiers in the streets at night.

Bram closed the covenant with hands that shook, and looked at me, and said the words that ended my old life as surely as any raid ever could:

"Fetch your coat, ledger-keeper. I think the countdown just got shorter."
