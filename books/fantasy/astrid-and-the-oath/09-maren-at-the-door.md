## 9. Maren at the Door

The knock came at the hour when the Vesper Guild pretended to sleep.

Ulyana heard it from the ledger room, where they had fallen asleep over the covenant pages for the third night running—cheek on the oldest book in the guild's keeping, ink ghosts pressed into their skin like a second set of scars. The knock was soft. Three taps, a pause, two more. A rhythm. Ulyana knew that rhythm the way they knew their own heartbeat, and for a long moment they simply sat there in the dark with the candle guttering, unable to make their body obey.

No one knocked like that anymore. No one alive.

They rose. Their knees cracked. In the corridor outside, the guildhouse breathed its nighttime breaths: Odile's slow snore through her door, Bram's boots hanging by the stair as always, the faint tick-tick of Ember-of-Endings' ember-scale cooling in its lead-lined case down in the vault. Ulyana passed all of it without waking anyone, because some part of them already knew this knock was theirs alone to answer.

They lifted the bar. They opened the door.

Rain stood in the street in silver sheets, and out of it came a woman wrapped in a fishmonger's oilcloth, hood back, gray hair plastered to her cheeks, eyes the color of river ice in thaw. Older than Ulyana remembered. Thinner. But the same face—the face that had bent over every scraped knee and every botched poultice of Ulyana's childhood, the face they had last seen across a burning field with a village screaming behind it.

"Maren," Ulyana said. It came out as almost nothing.

"Hello, little ledger-keeper." Her voice was rougher than memory. She smiled, and it cost her something; Ulyana could see the cost sitting in the corners of her mouth. "Are you going to leave your old teacher standing in the rain, or has the guild taught you worse manners than I ever did?"

Ulyana stepped aside. Maren crossed the threshold like water finding its level, and the moment she was inside, she sagged against the wall, and Ulyana saw—for the first time, in the lamplight—that there was blood on the hem of her dress, dried brown, days old, and that her left hand was cradled against her chest like something broken.

* * *

They worked in silence first, because that was how Maren had taught them. Silence while you cleaned a wound. Silence while you measured the willow bark and the comfrey and the moss that grew only on the north side of stones. Ulyana's hands remembered everything even as their mind insisted it had forgotten; they splinted two fingers, stitched a gash along Maren's forearm where something—a blade? a wire strung across an alley?—had opened her to the bone, and all the while Maren watched them with that patient, terrible attention she had never once turned off.

"You kept the gift," Maren said at last, quietly, as Ulyana tied off the final stitch. "I wondered."

"I don't keep it." Ulyana cut the thread. "I hide it. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"Yes." The word came out harder than intended. Ulyana busied themselves with the bandage roll. "There is when hiding it keeps people alive."

Maren said nothing to that. Outside, the rain went on working at the city like a patient scribe, wearing letters into stone. Somewhere below them, deep in the guild's foundations, Ulyana felt—or imagined they felt—the great slow pulse of the dragon-fire, the heartbeat under the floorboards that Bram had shown them barely a fortnight past. *Every impossible thing we sell is a gift someone gave up,* he had said. *And the fire that holds them is dying.* Since that night Ulyana had not slept properly once. Now here was Maren, hunted, bleeding, knocking at the one door in the city that should have been the most dangerous for her to find.

"How did you know to come here?" Ulyana asked.

Maren flexed her splinted fingers, testing. "Hedge-witches talk, even now. Especially now. There's a rhyme children skip rope to in the river towns—*the Vesper keeps what cannot be kept.* I've known what this house was since before you were born, child." Her eyes flicked up, sharp as a lancet. "I've known since before you ran."

The word landed like a slap. *Ran.* Not fled. Not escaped. Ran—as if Ulyana were a debt unpaid, a page torn from a book.

"I didn't run," Ulyana said. "You told me to go."

"I told you to go *that night*. I never told you to stay gone nine years." Maren's voice didn't rise. That was the worst of it; it never rose, it only got quieter and closer to the bone. "I wrote to you. Care of every post between here and the coast. Fourteen letters, Ulyana. I counted."

"And I burned them unread," Ulyana said, "because the last thing you ever asked me to do was disappear, and I was very good at disappearing. You taught me that too."

The silence that followed had weight, texture, temperature. Maren looked down at her bandaged arm as though reading something written there. When she spoke again, her voice had gentled into something almost too careful. "Tomas died three winters ago. The fever, not the fear. I thought you'd want to know."

Ulyana's hands went still. Tomas—Maren's other apprentice, older, steadier, the one whose healing had gone wrong that autumn day, the one whose fever Ulyana's desperate gift had driven higher instead of breaking, the mistake that had put the whole village at the cottage door with torches. Dead three winters, and no letter had reached Ulyana, or perhaps it had and lay unburned somewhere in a drawer Ulyana did not open.

"I'm sorry," Ulyana said, and found they meant it, and found that meaning it made everything worse.

"So am I." Maren reached out with her good hand and took Ulyana's wrist—not gripping, just resting, the way she used to when checking a pulse. "Not for telling you. For the years we lost to a mistake a frightened girl made trying to save a friend. I have been angry at you for nine years, little one, and I am tired of it, and I would rather spend whatever time is left being angry at things that deserve it." Her thumb moved once over Ulyana's pulse point. "Forgive me for my part. And forgive yourself for yours. Those are the only two medicines left in my kit that matter."

Ulyana wanted to say yes. They felt the yes rising in them like water in a well. And beneath it, older and colder, the memory of torchlight on wet grass, of Maren's voice cracking as she shoved them toward the treeline—*go, go now, don't look back*—and the nine years of looking back anyway, every single night.

"Not yet," Ulyana heard themselves say. "I—I can't yet. Ask me again when I'm not bleeding you and you're not bleeding."

Maren huffed something that was half laugh, half grief. "Stubborn as ever. Good. Stubbornness is the only gift that never gets taken from you." She let go of Ulyana's wrist. "But hear me, because I didn't walk eighty miles in the rain to trade apologies. There are wagons going north out of Millbrook, Corren's Ford, all the river towns. Crown wagons, iron-caged, guarded by men who wear white gloves and never take them off. They're collecting us, Ulyana. Hedge-witches, cunning-folk, the woman in Harrowgate who charms warts, the boy who talks to bees. Taken north, and none come back south." Her ice-colored eyes held steady. "I passed three burned cottages on the road. One of them was still warm."

* * *

Ulyana took her down to the kitchen, because grief and terror both go better with tea, and because the kitchen was where the guild lived when it wasn't performing itself. Odile was already there—Odile was always already there, as though she germinated from the flagstones at dusk—and she took one look at Maren and put a third cup on the tray without asking a single question, which was the guild's oldest courtesy.

"Hedge-witch," Odile said, by way of greeting.

"Guildmaster." Maren inclined her head. "Your reputation travels. So does your smell of secrets."

"Only the good ones." Odile poured. Steam curled up between them like a small gray ghost. "You'll have sanctuary. That word still means something under this roof, whatever it means outside it. But you'll tell me everything, and then you'll sleep, and tomorrow we decide what everything costs."

So Maren told it, cup cradled in her good hand, and Ulyana listened with the covenant pages heavy in memory against their ribs. The hunt had moved faster than the criers announced. Registration edicts were only the polite face of it; in the countryside there were no edicts, just wagons and white gloves and the sudden empty chairs at market. The inquisitors had a list. Nobody knew who kept the list. Whole families had registered faithfully and been taken anyway, which meant registration was not protection but inventory.

"They're not hunting witches," Maren said flatly. "Witches they could burn locally, cheaply, the old way. This is collection. Someone wants the gifted gathered in one place, north, behind the walls of the Pyre Court." She looked around the kitchen—at Odile's carved calm, at Ulyana's carefully blank face. "And I'd wager my remaining teeth that someone in this room knows why."

Ulyana's cup paused halfway to their lips. Odile's expression did not change at all, which was itself an answer, and Maren saw it, because Maren had spent forty years reading faces over sickbeds where the truth was the difference between life and death.

"The fire," Maren said softly. "There's a fire involved. Isn't there. Something old." She set down her cup. "Child, I bound my own gift into a river-stone twenty years ago so the crown would pass me by. I know what binding costs. If your guild is sitting on something that holds hundreds of bindings—"

"You should sleep," Odile said, pleasant as a drawn knife.

Maren held her gaze for a long moment. Then, remarkably, she smiled, tired and crooked, and rose. "Aye. Sleep. But tomorrow, Guildmaster, you'll show me your fire, or I'll start telling your apprentices stories about you, and I know *hundreds*."

She let Ulyana walk her up to the spare room above the kitchen—the one with the good window and the quilt Odile pretended not to love. At the door Maren stopped, hand on the frame, and looked back, and for a moment she was not the gaunt fugitive from the rain but the woman from the cottage, flour on her apron, teaching a child to stir a tincture sunwise.

"You've grown into your hands," she said. "They're healer's hands now. Whatever else you do with them."

Then the door closed, and Ulyana stood alone in the corridor with a lamp and nine years and the words *not yet* still sour on their tongue.

* * *

Sleep refused them, so Ulyana went down to the ledger room and lit the candle and opened the oldest book to the covenant pages, as if the answer might have changed overnight. It hadn't. *The guild shelters the gifted. The fire holds their power. When the fire dies, every bound gift breaks loose at once.* And now, underneath those lines in their own recent handwriting, the arithmetic they could not stop doing: Maren's river-stone binding. Every hedge-witch in the river towns. Every cunning-man and bee-charmer taken north in iron cages—if the Pyre Court was full of the gifted, and the fire under the Vesper Guild failed, what happened to gifts bound elsewhere? Bram hadn't known. Or hadn't said. The covenant was silent on the question, and silence, Ulyana was learning, was the guild's native language.

The floorboard behind them creaked.

They didn't turn. Only one person in the house walked that heavily on purpose. "If you're here to tell me I shouldn't have brought her in," Ulyana said, "she was bleeding on your doorstep. Your rules, not mine."

Bram lowered himself onto the stool across the table, joints popping like green kindling. He looked older tonight, or maybe Ulyana was finally seeing him truly. "I'm here because the dragon woke me," he said. "Ember-of-Endings doesn't wake for weather. Doesn't wake for wolves." His eyes, pale as ash, went to the covenant pages, then to the ceiling—to the room above the kitchen where Maren slept. "That woman's binding is in a river-stone, you said?"

"A stone. Twenty years bound."

Bram nodded slowly, and his face did something Ulyana had never seen it do: it went afraid. "Then she needs to see the fire tomorrow. Because tonight, for the first time in three hundred years, Ember-of-Endings dreamed—and when I put my hand to the scale, the ember was calling a name." He leaned forward into the candlelight. "It was calling hers."
