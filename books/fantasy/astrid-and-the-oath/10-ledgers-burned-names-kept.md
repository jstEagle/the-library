## 10. Ledgers Burned, Names Kept

Odile Vesper had buried three guildmasters before her, and she had learned from each of them the same lesson, though none of them had spoken it aloud: a guild of impossible things survives on paper the way a candle survives underwater.

She stood in the counting room at midnight with the ledgers spread before her like a battlefield map, and she made her decision the way she made all her decisions—quickly, and then forever.

"Bring me the lamp oil," she said to Perrin, who was mending a cracked storm-bottle by the window and pretending not to listen. "The good oil. Not the stuff we water down for the street lamps."

Perrin's needle stopped mid-stitch. "Guildmaster?"

"You heard me."

"The ledgers?" He set down the bottle with exaggerated care, as if it might detonate. "All of them?"

"All of them."

He looked at her the way a man looks at a woman who has just suggested burning the family silver, the portraits, and the ancestors' bones all in one fire. "Odile. Those records go back four hundred years. There are entries in there from *before* the first dragon war. The widow's year—that was your grandmother's hand that wrote that entry. The covenant pages—"

"Are not in these books." She kept her voice level. "Those are elsewhere, where they should be. These—" she laid her palm flat on the nearest ledger, its spine cracked, its corners soft with a century of handling, "—these are names. Client names. Debtor names. The woman in Copperlane who bought three hours of borrowed courage when her husband was dying. The ferryman who keeps a bottled calm under his seat for the bad crossings. The midwife. The schoolteacher. Forty years of small mercies sold to frightened people, written down in my own hand or my predecessors', dated, signed, *catalogued*."

"And that's a treasure," Perrin said.

"That is a pyre list." She let the words sit in the room until they stopped smelling like blasphemy and started smelling like smoke. "The criers read the proclamation again today. Register or burn. And Sera Vance is in the city—I've had two separate reports, one from the fish market and one from a boy we pay to count soldiers' boots. She's asking after a ledger-keeper. Someone told her the Vesper Guild traffics in magic, and someone else told her we keep accounts." Odile smiled without any humor in it at all. "We do. That has always been our pride and it will now be our noose. Every name in these books is a person who can be arrested, questioned, burned—or worse, turned. Do you understand? They won't just punish the clients. They'll offer them a trade. Give us the guild, and your name comes off the list. And every one of them, every frightened soul in Copperlane and the ferryman and the midwife, will have to decide whether we're worth their children's lives. I won't make them decide. I'll take the choice away."

Perrin was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the city murmured its midnight murmur—the watch calling the hour, a dog somewhere, the river doing what rivers do. Inside, the counting room smelled of ink and tallow and forty years of Odile's own life.

"You'll need help carrying them to the yard," he said finally.

"I know."

* * *

They did it in the courtyard behind the guildhall, where the laundry was strung and the rain barrels stood and Bram kept his coals for the forge under an iron hood. Odile insisted on doing the first book herself—the oldest one, the one with her grandmother's handwriting going soft and brown at the edges—and she insisted on reading each page once before it went into the brazier.

Not because she needed to. Because they deserved to be read.

"Bettine Harrow, Copperlane, one borrowed hour of patience, paid in mending." She read it aloud, quietly, and fed the page to the flames. "Tomas Ferro, ferryman, renewal of the calm-bottle, third year running, paid in fish and never once late." Page into the fire. "Sister Ameline, hospice of Saint Vered, six vials of eased passing, payment deferred indefinitely because she said God would settle up." Into the fire.

The names went up in little blossoms of orange light, the ink flaring green where the old iron-gall mix caught, and Odile watched each one go with the particular stillness she had learned from watching her grandmother die: present, unflinching, taking note of everything so that nothing would be lost merely because it was gone.

Ulyana found them an hour in.

She heard the footsteps on the back stairs and knew them before she saw the face—quick, light, always half-apologetic, the walk of a child raised to believe they took up too much room. Ulyana came out into the courtyard with a candle stub in one hand and their ledger-clerk's shawl crooked over one shoulder, and they stopped dead when they saw the brazier, the stacked books, the smell of burning ink on the night air.

"What are you doing."

It wasn't a question. It was the voice of someone watching a house burn.

Odile didn't stop feeding pages. "Housekeeping."

"*Odile.*" Ulyana crossed the yard in six strides and put their hand flat on the next ledger in the stack—a gesture so much like her own from an hour ago that something in her chest twisted. "That's the autumn book. That's this year. There are entries in there I balanced last week. You can't—you can't just—"

"I can," Odile said, "and I am, and you're going to help me, because your memory is better than mine and I intend to use it."

"My—" Ulyana stared at her. Then at the fire. Then at her again, and Odile saw the exact moment understanding arrived, because the anger drained out of their face and left behind something rawer. "The proclamation."

"The proclamation."

"They'd take the ledgers and work backward. Name by name."

"Name by name," Odile confirmed. "And then they'd come here, and they'd find the books conveniently intact, full of confessions, and they wouldn't even need to torture anyone. We'd have done their work for them. Four hundred years of careful record-keeping, delivered to the pyre court like a gift basket."

Ulyana looked down at the ledger under their palm. Their thumb moved along the page edge, back and forth, the way it did when they were balancing a column that wouldn't come right. "History matters," they said, and their voice cracked in the middle of it. "Odile, *history matters*. If we burn all of it, then in fifty years nobody will know we existed. Nobody will know what we did, or why, or that any of this—" they gestured at the guildhall rising dark above the courtyard walls, at the whole secret architecture of shelter and trade and stubborn mercy, "—was ever built. The people who come after us will think magic just... appeared. Or worse, they'll believe the crown's version. That we were monsters hiding in cellars. The ledgers are the proof that we weren't. That we were *people*, helping people, one borrowed hour at a time. You can't burn proof."

Odile set down the ledger she was holding. She wiped her hands on her apron—ink and ash, ash and ink, the two substances her life had always been made of—and she turned to face her clerk fully, because this deserved her full face.

"Ulyana. How many entries are in the autumn book?"

"I—what?"

"How many. Roughly."

Ulyana blinked. "Two hundred and eleven. Two hundred and twelve if you count the storm-bottle default, which I do, because a debt is a debt even when it's being repossessed."

"And how many of those two hundred and twelve people know about each other?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere across the yard, Perrin fed another decade to the flames.

"They don't," Ulyana said slowly. "Most of them don't know anyone else. That's the point. That's how we've kept them safe—compartmentalized. Bettine doesn't know Tomas. The midwife doesn't know Sister Ameline."

"So tell me what happens to Bettine Harrow when the inquisitor reads her name in a captured ledger." Odile kept her voice gentle, which cost her more than shouting would have. "Bettine, who bought one borrowed hour of patience because her son's grief was wearing her into the ground. They knock on her door. They show her the entry in her own hand, signed, dated. And they say: tell us where you bought this, or your name goes on the register, and your son watches you burn. What does Bettine do?"

"She wouldn't talk. She's loyal—"

"To whom?" Odile asked. "To a guild whose face she's never seen? To a name on a receipt? Ulyana, loyalty to an abstraction is a luxury of people who aren't afraid. I have been afraid, and I am telling you: she talks. Most of them talk. And the ones who don't talk burn, and either way, the ledger kills them. The ledger we kept because history matters." She picked the book back up. "I have loved these books since before you were born. I learned my letters copying my grandmother's hand. I have defended them against flood, fire, thieves, and one truly memorable tax assessor. Don't stand in my courtyard and tell me they matter. Stand there and tell me who they matter *to*."

Ulyana's jaw worked. The candle stub guttered in their hand, forgotten, wax running hot over their fingers until Perrin reached over and took it from them without comment.

"The truth matters," Ulyana said at last, quieter.

"Yes."

"Somebody has to carry it."

"Yes," Odile said. "Somebody does. And paper," she tapped the burning ledger, "is a terrible place to keep a truth you want to survive. Paper burns. Paper rots. Paper gets seized, copied, forged, misread. Paper tells its secrets to whoever holds it, which means paper holds its secrets only as long as nobody wants them. But a person—" she reached out and pressed two fingers, briefly, against Ulyana's sternum, over the heart, "—a person can carry a truth through fire, through raids, through forty years of silence, and choose, every single day, whether to give it up. Paper has no loyalty. People do. That's the whole difference between an archive and a family, and we, my dear impossible clerk, are not an archive."

Ulyana stood very still. In the firelight their eyes were wet, but their voice, when it came, was steady. "Then teach me the names."

Odile felt something in her chest loosen—a knot she hadn't known she'd tied, tied sometime between the first crier and the second report of white gloves in the fish market.

"Perrin," she called. "Fetch the rest. All of them. And bring wine; this will take until dawn."

* * *

They worked until the sky went grey at the edges.

Page by page, ledger by ledger, Odile read and Ulyana repeated, and Perrin fed the fire, and the three of them built a new kind of archive out of breath and memory. Names, trades, debts, kindnesses. *Marta Quill, weaver, one winter of warm dreams, paid in cloth.* *The boys at the Saltgate watch, one shared bottle of borrowed bravery before the flood year, payment: they never spoke of it, and never did.* *A nameless client, entry marked only with a small drawn bird, who traded a secret for a second chance and whose secret Odile had taken to her own grave and would now take to Ulyana's keeping.*

Ulyana's memory was, as advertised, monstrous. By the third ledger they had stopped needing the repetition. By the fifth they were correcting Odile—*"no, that was the spring of the comet, she paid in star-charts"*—and Odile found herself laughing, actually laughing, at two in the morning over a brazier full of her own life's work turning to smoke.

It hurt. She wanted that recorded too, somewhere no inquisitor could reach: it hurt like cutting off a hand, and she would do it again, and again, as many times as the world demanded.

When the last book was ash and the brazier held nothing but heat and ghost-light, Odile sat back on the cold bench and looked at her two remaining clerks—her frightened, faithful, impossible people—and gave them the only order left.

"Now forget nothing," she said. "Every name. Every debt. Every kindness. You are the ledgers now, both of you, and the crown cannot seize what lives behind your ribs. Guard it the way you'd guard a child. Because that's what it is. It's everyone's child."

Ulyana nodded, solemn as an ordination. And then, because they were Ulyana, and because grief and duty had never yet managed to fully colonize that restless mind, they frowned suddenly and said:

"The storm-bottle. The defaulted one. It's still out there—with Halvard's people, in the warehouse by the tannery. It's not in any ledger anymore. If we don't retrieve it, there's no record it ever belonged to a client at all. No trail. No protection. Just a bottled storm sitting in a rented storeroom, waiting for whoever opens it."

Odile closed her eyes. Of course. Of course the night she burned four centuries of caution, the world would immediately present the bill.

"Tomorrow," she began—

But Ulyana was already pulling their shawl straight, already reaching for the coat hook by the door, already moving with that quick light step toward the stairs and the dark and the tannery district beyond it.

"No," they said. "Tonight. A debt is a debt, Guildmaster. Even now. *Especially* now."

And they were gone down the back stairs before Odile could weigh the wisdom of stopping them—which was fortunate, she reflected later, because she would have weighed it, and she might have chosen wrong.
