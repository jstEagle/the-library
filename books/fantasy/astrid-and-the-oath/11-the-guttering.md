## 11. The Guttering

Bram had kept the fire for fifty-one years, and in all that time he had learned one lesson above the rest: a flame tells you how it means to die.

A flame that meant to die angry spat and cracked and threw sparks at the ceiling. A flame that meant to die sullen went blue at the edges and burned low and mean. But Ember-of-Endings was dying the way an old woman dies on a warm afternoon—slowly, quietly, with long pauses between breaths that made you lean closer to see if there would be another.

Bram leaned closer now. He had been leaning closer for three days.

The dragon's roost beneath the Vesper Guildhall was not a cave so much as a cathedral that had forgotten it was one. Vaulted stone overhead, black with two centuries of smoke. Walls ribbed with old iron brackets where lanterns once hung, back when anyone but Bram had been allowed down here. The floor was sand, raked each morning into slow spirals, because Ember-of-Endings liked patterns under her feet even when she could no longer lift her head to look at them.

She lay in the center of the spiral like a ship run aground. Her scales, once the color of forge-coals seen through a keyhole—that deep, breathing red-orange that seemed lit from within—had gone the gray of banked ash. Only along her throat, where the last of her fire pooled, did any color remain: a dull pulse, like a coal wrapped in cloth, dimming by degrees no ledger could measure.

"Morning, old mother," Bram said, though it was past noon. He said morning every time he came down, because she had told him once, a hundred years before his time, that dragons counted days from waking, not from clocks. She hadn't woken in four days.

He knelt in the sand beside her jaw, which was longer than he was tall, and pressed his palm flat against the warm scale there. The warmth came through thin. Like touching bread that had gone cold hours ago and only pretended otherwise.

"I know," he murmured. "I know. I'm not hurrying you."

But he was hurrying. That was the thing he could not say aloud, not to her, not to Odile, not to the ledgers themselves. He was hurrying, because for three nights he had sat in this cathedral of smoke and done arithmetic he did not want to do.

The guild's goods were not goods. Every bottled storm on their shelves held a gift—a person's gift, bound into the fire and poured out again as something sellable, something impossible. The widow's borrowed year. The thunder in glass. The scale of living ember that Ulyana had inventoried with such careful, unknowing hands. All of it drawn from the same well. And the well was the dragon, and the dragon was going out.

When the fire died, the covenant died with it. And when the covenant died—

Bram closed his eyes and saw it the way he had seen it in dreams since he was a young keeper: every bound gift snapping loose at once, all across the city, all at the same terrible instant. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Gifts that had been folded small for decades, some for generations, released without warning into hands that had forgotten they ever held anything at all.

He had brought the arithmetic to Odile twice. Both times she had listened with her hands flat on the table and her face like a locked door, and both times she had said, "Then we keep the fire alive," as if keeping a fire alive were a matter of wood and bellows and will.

It was not a matter of wood. It had never been a matter of wood.

* * *

That evening, Bram climbed the narrow stair from the roost with the smell of ash in his clothes and found Ulyana waiting at the bottom of the guildhall steps, a lamp in hand and a ledger under one arm.

"You've been down there long," Ulyana said. Not a question. Ulyana had a way of stating observations as if daring them to be argued with.

"She sleeps more than she wakes."

"How much more?"

Bram looked at the young ledger-keeper—at the sharp face, the ink-stained fingers, the flicker of something carefully hidden behind the eyes that Bram had noticed the first week and never mentioned—and felt the weight of fifty-one years of silence settle onto his shoulders like a yoke.

"Come down tomorrow," he said instead. "There are things you should see while there's still seeing to be done."

Ulyana's chin lifted. "Is she dying?"

"Everything is dying, child. Some things just have further to fall."

Ulyana didn't laugh at that. Ulyana never laughed at the right things, which Bram privately considered the mark of a serious mind. They stood a moment in the lamplight, and then Ulyana said, quietly, "The accounts don't balance anymore."

"What do you mean?"

"The stock. The storms, the years, the embers. I've counted the shelves three times. We're selling faster than—" Ulyana stopped. Bram watched the thought arrive, watched it land, watched the color go out of the young face by inches. "We're not making new stock. Are we. There's nothing left to make it *from*."

"No," Bram said. "There isn't."

Ulyana stood very still. Somewhere above them, in the rooms of the guildhall, someone laughed—the low rumble of Odile, the bright bark of the twins who worked the receiving desk—and the sound fell down the stairwell into the silence between the two of them like a coin dropped into a well.

"Does Odile know how bad it is?"

"Odile knows what Odile can bear to know." Bram took up his own lamp. "Tomorrow. After the morning raking. Come alone."

* * *

He did not sleep that night. He rarely slept well anymore; the roost had a sound to it now, a rhythm, and when the rhythm faltered he woke as if someone had called his name. Near the smallest hour, it faltered.

Bram went down the stairs two at a time, which at seventy-three years of age was an act of either devotion or stupidity, and found Ember-of-Endings with her throat-flare guttering—not dimming now but actually guttering, the light stuttering like a candle in a draft, and for three full seconds the great cathedral of the roost went dark as a grave.

Then the light returned. Weaker.

"Ah," Bram said, and his voice cracked in the middle of it. "Ah, no. Not yet. Not yet, old mother. Not while they're hunting us. Not while the pyres are being built. You hold on. Do you hear me? You *hold on*."

Her eye slid open. Just one, the near one, vast as a cartwheel and dark as deep water, and it found him in the half-dark with the terrible patience of a creature who had watched kings be born and be buried and be forgotten.

*I am holding,* she said. Dragons did not speak with sound anymore, not her; the words arrived inside the skull like memories that weren't yours. *Holding is not the same as living, keeper.*

"I know it."

*You do not. You are a man who mends roofs in a flood.* The eye began to close. *Count your days differently, Bram. Count them from my waking.*

And she slept again, and the flare at her throat steadied—not recovered, only steadied, the way a dying man steadies when he decides to give it one more hour—and Bram sat down in the sand beside her jaw and put his forehead against the warm-warm-not-warm scale and stayed there until the spiral shadows turned with the lamp and dawn came gray through the high vent-slits.

* * *

It happened at midday, on the day he was to show Ulyana the truth.

Later, everyone would tell the story differently. The criers would say the child was a demon. The neighbors would say the child had always been strange. The crown's men would say the child proved the proclamation right, that the gifted were everywhere, hidden, waiting. But Bram knew how it truly began, because he felt it begin—in the roost, in the fire, in the space of a single missed heartbeat.

He was showing Ulyana the throat-flare. He had brought them down after the morning raking as promised, and Ulyana stood at the edge of the sand-spiral with their lamp held too high and too steady, the way people hold lamps when they are frightened and determined not to show it.

"That's her heart?" Ulyana asked. "The light?"

"That's what's left of what her heart used to feed." Bram crouched, laid his palm against the sand. "Every impossible thing upstairs comes out of this. The storms. The years. The ember-scale. Gifts, Ulyana. Bound into the fire and drawn off slow, like—" He searched for the word. "Like tapping a tree. The tree gives sap. The fire gives miracles. And the folk who gave their gifts—" His voice caught on it, fifty-one years of saying it to no one. "The folk who gave their gifts were sheltered here first. That's the covenant. That's what the guild *is*. We don't trade in magic. We're an ark that learned to sell ballast."

Ulyana had gone white. "Bound gifts. In people. Living people, walking around the city with their power tied off in this fire, and if the fire—"

"Aye."

"—then the gifts come *loose*."

"Aye."

"When?"

Bram opened his mouth to say *not yet*, to say *we have time*, to say any of the lies a keeper says—and the world answered for him.

The flare guttered. Not the long stutter of the night before but a single hard gulp, a swallowed beat, and Bram felt it pass through the sand, through the stone, through the soles of his boots, out into the foundations of the city like a crack running through ice. Above them—muffled by floors and distance but unmistakable—came a sound like a struck bell, if bells screamed.

Then the screaming started.

* * *

They came up the stairs together and out into the Vesper quarter, and the street was already a wound.

The cart stood in the middle of the square outside the chandler's shop. It stood *in the air*, six feet up, wheels turning slowly, a load of tallow crates swaying—and beneath it, arms raised over her head, face contorted with effort and terror in equal measure, stood a girl of perhaps nine years whom Bram recognized distantly as the cooper's daughter, the quiet one who swept her father's doorstep every morning.

Around her, the square had become a ring. Neighbors pressed back against walls and doorways. A man was shouting for the watch. A woman had fallen to her knees and was praying loudly enough to be heard over everything. Someone had thrown a boot—it lay in the dust halfway to the girl—and someone else was running, already running, toward the market cross where the criers stood, toward the men in crown-gray who had set up their registration tables under the elm.

"It's happening," Ulyana breathed beside him. "It's *happening*, Bram, that's—one of ours? Whose gift—"

"Not one of ours." Bram's mouth had gone dry. "Not bound recently. This is an old binding. Grandmother's, maybe. Great-grandmother's. Folded down through a family that never knew they carried it." He watched the cart sway. "The fire doesn't just hold the guild's stock, child. It holds *everything we ever bound*. Four hundred years of it. Every family. Every hiding place. When the fire stumbles, the oldest knots shake loose first."

The girl's knees buckled. The cart lurched higher—eight feet now, ten—and a crate tipped and spilled tallow blocks into the street, and the crowd flinched back as one animal, and the shouting doubled.

"We have to get her out of there," Ulyana said, and stepped forward, and Bram caught their arm.

"If you touch that with your gift, in front of all these eyes—"

"If nobody does, they'll take her anyway! Look at them, Bram. Look at their faces. They're not frightened of the cart. They're frightened of *her*, and frightened people fetch the graycoats, and the graycoats fetch the pyre." Ulyana pulled free. "I'll be careful. I'll be small. Watch the crowd—tell me if anyone looks at me too long."

And before Bram could find the argument strong enough to stop a Yarrow-tempered apprentice—for he had known what Ulyana was from the first week, known the flicker behind the eyes, and had said nothing, because the guild's whole art was saying nothing—Ulyana walked into the open square.

What happened next happened fast, and Bram would spend many nights afterward turning it over, and it never got less frightening.

Ulyana crossed to the girl in maybe ten strides, knelt, spoke low—no one heard the words—and then lifted one hand, casually, the way a person waves off flies. The cart sank. Slowly, gently, like a boat settling at its mooring, until the wheels kissed the cobblestones and the crates stopped swaying and the whole impossible weight of it simply became a cart again.

To the crowd, it might have been nothing. A trick of panic. A rope that snapped. People wanted it to be nothing; Bram could see them wanting it, could see the hunger in their faces to unsee what they'd seen.

But the girl looked at Ulyana. And Ulyana looked at the girl. And between those two looks passed something that no crowd could unsee even if no crowd understood it—recognition, the nod of one secret to another—and a woman near the chandler's door saw it, and her face changed, and she turned and ran toward the market cross.

"Toward the elm!" Bram shouted. "Ulyana—*go*!"

Ulyana scooped the girl up—they weighed nothing, the little cooper's daughter, all elbows and fear—and ran, and Bram turned to meet the crowd's confusion with fifty-one years of practiced blandness, arms spread, voice pitched to grumble: "Rope snapped, is all! Rotten rope! Somebody get the cooper, his cart's spilled!"

It half-worked. It half-worked the way a bucket half-bails a flooding boat. Enough heads turned away. Enough shoulders relaxed. But the woman had reached the criers' tables, and Bram saw her talking, saw her pointing—first at the cart, then back toward the chandler's, then, horribly, sweeping in a wide arc that took in the whole quarter, the guildhall's corner, the lane where Ulyana had vanished with the child.

Two men in crown-gray stood up from the registration tables. One of them unrolled a fresh sheet of paper. The other began asking questions, and the woman answered eagerly, gratefully, the way people answer when they have been afraid for weeks and finally have somewhere to put it.

* * *

By nightfall there were graycoats on three corners of the Vesper quarter, and the criers had a new proclamation, and Bram heard it read aloud from the guildhall steps with the door open behind him and the whole household gathered in the hall, silent as a congregation:

*"By order of the Crown: the manifestation of sorcery in the Vesper quarter this day confirms the presence of unregistered gifted persons among you. The reward for information leading to the seizure of any gifted person or any person harboring the same is hereby doubled. Registration closes in seven days. After seven days, no mercy."*

Seven days. Bram stood in the doorway with his hands behind his back and did the only arithmetic that mattered anymore.

Ember-of-Endings had been guttering for three days. Each stumble shook older knots loose. Today it was a child and a cart, witnessed by a hundred eyes, reported within the hour. Tomorrow it might be two gifts. Next week, a dozen. And every loose gift brought the graycoats deeper into the quarter, and every graycoat brought the pyres closer, and the pyres—Bram's stomach turned at the thought he had been refusing for a month—the pyres were not empty theater. The crown remembered the first war. The crown remembered what dragon-fire had done to their grandfathers' cities. If they caught the dragon, they would not merely kill her.

They would make certain of it.

Behind him, Odile's voice, low and iron: "Bram. My office. Now. Bring everything you know about how long we have."

He turned from the door. Down the street, past the graycoats at their corners, the last light was going off the city, and somewhere beneath his feet, in the dark cathedral of sand and smoke, an old mother slept with a coal for a heart, counting her days from waking.

"Warden," Bram said, "you'd best sit down."

And far across the rooftops, unseen in the gathering dark, a rider in white gloves was already asking at the city gate for directions to the Vesper quarter.
