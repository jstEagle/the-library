## 12. Old Friends, New Warrants

The report came to Sera Vance at the ninth hour of the morning, folded twice and sealed with the plain grey wax of the Office of Inquiry. She read it standing in the doorway of her rented room on Candlemaker's Row, because she had learned long ago that bad news was easier to bear on her feet.

*A disturbance in the Vesper quarter. A bottled storm, unlicensed. Witnesses describe a clerk—slight build, ink-stained fingers—who calmed the tempest with a word and a gesture. The storm obeyed.*

*The storm obeyed,* Sera thought, and something cold slid down between her shoulder blades. Storms did not obey. Storms were bargained with, bribed, bound. For one to simply *obey* meant the hand that held it had power of its own—not borrowed, not bottled. The kind of power the crown had spent three hundred years teaching itself to fear.

She sat down on the edge of the bed and read the description again. *Slight build. Ink-stained fingers. Dark hair cut short for convenience rather than fashion.* There were ten thousand such clerks in Vharen. She told herself that. She said it aloud, to the empty room, the way one says a prayer whose answer one dreads.

Then she opened her case and took out the sketch.

She had commissioned it two days after arriving in the city, working from the rumors that had led her here: a ledger-keeper who trafficked in impossible things, who kept accounts of borrowed years and bottled thunder. The artist had given her a face that was no face at all—generic, forgettable, the kind of portrait that could hang in an office for a decade without anyone looking twice. That had been deliberate. Sera had wanted a ghost to hunt, not a person. Ghosts were easier.

But ghosts had a way of putting on flesh.

---

She went first to the cooper's shop near the canal where the storm incident had happened, because witnesses were most honest in the places where their fear still lived. The cooper himself was a broad man with sawdust in his beard and the particular evasiveness of someone who had seen too much and wished he hadn't.

"You were there," Sera said. It wasn't a question. "When the storm broke loose."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Master Cooper." She set her warrant-case on his counter, open, so he could see the crown's seal. "I am not here to punish you. I am not even here to punish them. I want only a name."

He wiped his hands on his apron, once, twice, three times. "There was a clerk," he said finally. "From the guild on Lantern Street—the Vesper place. Came to collect what the dead man owed. And when the bottle cracked—" He shook his head. "I've seen storms, my lady. Real ones, off the sea. They don't *listen*. This one listened. Like a dog hearing its master's whistle."

"What did the clerk look like?"

"Small. Quick. Kept their head down." He hesitated, and Sera watched the war move across his face—the war between fear of her and something older, some loyalty he couldn't name. "They had ink on their fingers," he said at last. "Up past the second knuckle. Like they'd been writing since before they could walk."

Ink past the second knuckle. Sera closed the warrant-case slowly.

She knew exactly one person who wrote like that.

---

It was not proof. She made herself say it, walking back through the Vesper quarter with her collar up against the drizzle. It was not proof; it was a coincidence wearing proof's clothing. Ten thousand clerks. Ink to the knuckle was common among scribes. The gift for storms—if it was a gift, if it was anything—could belong to anyone.

But memory is not reasonable, and hers had already begun its work.

She was eleven years old again, sitting on the sea-wall at Greyharbor with her legs dangling over the drop, beside a girl who smelled of salt and lamp-oil and always, always had ink on her fingers. Ulyana Yarrow, the lighthouse-keeper's daughter, who could make gulls land on her outstretched hand as though they'd been summoned, who once held a lantern-flame steady through a whole night of gale-wind so the fishing fleet could find the harbor mouth—and who laughed afterward and said, *the wind just liked me today, Sera, that's all.*

Sera had believed her. Sera had been twelve and in love in the way children are in love, entirely and without vocabulary, and she had believed every word.

Then the fever year came, and Ulyana's father died, and the girl and her mother vanished from Greyharbor between one tide and the next, and Sera waited on the sea-wall every evening for a month until her own father forbade it. *That family was strange,* he'd said. *Best forgotten.* And Sera had forgotten—or had buried forgetting so deep it looked like forgetting from above.

Until now.

She stopped in the middle of the market square, rain beading on her white gloves, and let the truth arrive all at once, the way a wave arrives.

The ledger-keeper of the Vesper Guild. The one the reports called the heart of the operation, the one who kept the accounts of impossible things. *Ulyana.*

"No," she said quietly, to the rain, to the city, to herself.

Because it couldn't be. Because the child on the sea-wall had been gentle and funny and afraid of nothing but deep water. Because the woman described by witnesses kept her head down and her hands hidden and moved through the world like someone carrying something breakable. Those were not the same person.

Except that they were. Except that Sera knew, with the certainty that lived below reasoning, in the cellar of the self where the true things were stored—they were the same person, and the breakable thing Ulyana carried was the same thing Sera had spent her career hunting.

She thought of Tomas then. She tried not to; she hadn't let herself think of Tomas in years. But the memory came anyway, the way it always did, uninvited: Tomas with his cupped hands full of light, showing her what he could do in the orchard behind the seminary, laughing, saying *you're the only one I trust, Sera, you have to promise—* And she had promised. And then the register had come to the village, and the priest had asked his questions, and Sera—fifteen years old, terrified, wanting desperately to be good, to be *right*—had said nothing. Had said nothing, which was its own kind of telling. And they had taken him north in a cart in winter, and she had never learned which was worse: that she betrayed him, or that she told herself for twenty years that silence was loyalty.

Her hands, inside the white gloves, had gone cold.

*Not this time,* she thought. And then, immediately, with horror: *that is not your decision to make. You are the Inquisitor's hand. You do not get to choose whom the fire takes.*

---

The Office of Inquiry kept rooms in the customs house by the river, and it was there, that afternoon, that Sera sat before a blank warrant form and did not write.

The form was simple enough. Name or description of the accused. Nature of the offense. Evidence. Her signature. One page of paper, and a life would begin to end—slowly at first, through summons and questioning, and then quickly, through the pyre court, whose scaffolds were being rebuilt in King's Square even now, she'd heard the hammers from her window at night.

She dipped the pen. Held it over the line marked *NAME OF ACCUSED.*

*Ulyana Yarrow.*

She did not write it. She wrote instead: *Clerk, Vesper Guild, Lantern Street. Identity under investigation.*

A delay. Nothing more. A technicality—a real one, defensible, even diligent. An inquisitor who filed warrants on rumor alone was a careless inquisitor, and careless inquisitors burned innocent people, and the Office frowned on that, at least officially. She would investigate properly. She would gather testimony. She would take a week, perhaps two, verifying what any fool could see in an afternoon.

She was buying days. She knew it, and she did it anyway, and the shame of it sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

*You are protecting a witch,* said the voice of her training, dry as old vellum. *You are doing what you failed to do for Tomas, and calling it justice.*

*Or,* said another voice, younger, salt-rimed, from a sea-wall in Greyharbor, *you are finally keeping a promise.*

She put down the pen.

---

For three days she investigated, and the investigation was a farce she performed with perfect seriousness. She interviewed the neighbors of Lantern Street, who knew nothing and admitted knowing less. She examined the guild's public ledger, displayed in its front room for any customer to browse—prices for weather-work, terms for memory-mending, all of it licensed, all of it legal, all of it a mask so well-made that Sera almost admired it. She walked past the guildhall at dusk and saw lamplight in the windows and heard, faintly, laughter—several voices, easy and warm, the sound of people who trusted their walls.

And on the third evening, she saw Ulyana.

It was an accident, or Providence, or whatever force governs the small cruelties of the world. Ulyana came out of the guild's side door with a basket on their arm, hood up against the drizzle, and turned toward the chandler's shop, and passed within six feet of where Sera stood in a doorway pretending to read a notice-board.

Sera did not move. Did not breathe. The rain ticked softly on her hood.

Ulyana was thinner than the child she remembered. Careful in their movements, eyes down, counting the ground as they walked. But when they paused at the chandler's door to shake the rain from their hood, they pushed it back, and the lamplight caught their face, and Sera felt twenty years fall away like a coat dropped from her shoulders.

*The wind just liked me today, Sera. That's all.*

Their eyes met. One heartbeat. Two.

Sera saw the recognition arrive—saw it hit Ulyana like a blow, saw the color leave their face, saw the basket shift in their grip as their body made the ancient calculation: run, fight, freeze. And Sera understood, in that stretched moment, that Ulyana knew her too. Knew the white gloves. Knew what they meant. Knew that the girl from the sea-wall had become the thing from the proclamations.

Sera did the only thing she could think to do. She looked away.

She turned back to the notice-board, studied a proclamation about grain tariffs as though it were scripture, and counted to sixty. When she looked again, the street was empty, and the chandler's door swung shut, and somewhere down the lane footsteps were running.

She should have followed. Every rule she had sworn herself to said *follow*. Instead she stood in the rain and pressed one gloved hand flat against her sternum, as though she could hold shut a door that had already burst open.

*They ran from me,* she thought. *Of course they ran from me. I am the fire.*

And beneath that thought, quieter, more dangerous, rising like water into a hold: *I was the one they used to run toward.*

---

That night she took out the warrant form again.

Three days of grace. Three days in which she had confirmed everything—identity, location, nature of the offense—and written none of it down where it counted. Tomorrow the weekly dispatch went south to the Office. If she filed nothing, the silence itself would become suspicious. Inquisitors who went quiet in the provinces got replaced, and replacements asked fewer questions and burned faster.

She wrote: *Investigation ongoing. Subject identified but not yet located. Request additional time.*

It was a lie built of truths, and her pen shook making it.

Then she sat for a long while by the window, watching the lamps of the Vesper quarter burn across the river, and allowed herself, for the first time in twenty years, to remember without flinching.

Tomas's hands full of light. Ulyana's laugh over the harbor wind. The promise she had made and broken and made again in her heart a thousand times since, to no one, uselessly: *never again.*

She had told herself, coming to this city, that she hunted the ledger-keeper to protect the gifted from worse hunters—that a careful inquisitor was a mercy, that her presence here saved lives. Some of that had even been true. But standing at the window now, she admitted the rest: she had come because she could not stop looking. Because somewhere in the labyrinth of her guilt lived the belief that if she found the ones who survived—if she found *Ulyana*—she might find her way back to the girl she had been before the register, before the cart, before the silence.

And now she had found them. And they had looked at her with terror.

"Well," she said aloud, to the empty room, and her voice cracked on it. "What did you expect?"

The dispatch lay sealed on the table. Days, bought. Perhaps a week, if she was clever. Not enough—she understood that already. Whatever the Vesper Guild was, whatever it hid behind its legal front, it was not something that could be saved by a week of forged patience. Sooner or later the Office would send someone who didn't remember sea-walls and gull-tamed girls. Sooner or later the fire would come for Lantern Street whether Sera lit it or not.

Which meant the question was no longer *duty or love*. She had asked herself that question for three days, and it had no good answer, and she was done asking it.

The real question, the one that made her stomach turn and her hands go strangely steady, was this:

*If the fire is coming anyway—whose side will you be standing on when it arrives?*

She blew out the lamp. Across the river, the lights of the Vesper quarter glimmered on the black water, small and stubborn, like a hearth seen from a long way off in winter.

And in the dark, Sera Vance began—for the first time—to plan not how to catch them, but how to warn them.
