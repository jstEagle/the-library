## 13. The War Nobody Wants Again

The sealed archive beneath the Saltspire Library smelled of iron and old grief.

Ulyana felt it before they understood it—the way one feels a storm building behind hills. The air down here was different. Denser. As if the books themselves had been breathing out something heavy for two hundred years and no one had opened a window since.

"This is as far as I'm permitted," whispered the under-librarian, a stooped woman named Pell whose keys outnumbered her teeth. She stood at the foot of the stair, holding her lantern high so its light wouldn't touch the door ahead. "Archivist's writ only. If anyone asks, you're cataloguing water damage."

"Water damage," Ulyana repeated. "In an archive that's never seen rain."

"Best lies are boring ones." Pell pressed a cold iron key into their palm and retreated up the stairs with the haste of someone who wanted to be able to say, honestly, that she'd never gone down there at all.

The lock turned like a bone breaking.

Inside, the dark was total until Ulyana coaxed a thread of light from the small lamp they'd brought—oil, wick, nothing magical, though their fingers itched to do it the other way, the easy way, the way they hadn't done in public since the storm. The flame caught and threw shadows across shelves that rose higher than any ladder could reach, crammed with volumes bound in leather gone green with age, in vellum, in bark, in what looked disturbingly like dragon-hide, though Ulyana chose not to examine that too closely.

They had come for one thing: everything written about the first dragon war. And they had come because of a sentence Bram had let slip three nights ago, standing over Ember-of-Endings' failing fire, his voice so quiet Ulyana might have imagined it:

*"When the fire dies unbound, it won't just break loose. It'll break loose the way it did last time. And last time, the sky came down."*

Ulyana had spent two days telling themself he was an old man being dramatic. On the third day they'd forged an archivist's writ, bribed Pell with a bottle of plum brandy from the guild stores, and come down into the dark to find out whether the old man was right.

* * *

The first hour yielded nothing but inventory lists. Grain shipments. Tithes. A census of a city called Vaelmoor that had, according to everything else Ulyana knew, been swallowed by the sea. They were beginning to think the war had simply not been written down—erased, perhaps, the way Odile had erased the client ledgers, truth carried out of paper and into ash—when they pulled a volume at random from a shelf near the back and felt the spine resist.

Not stuck. *Resisting.* The way a cat resists being picked up.

Ulyana set the lamp on the floor and used both hands. The book came free with a sound like a held breath released, and dust poured off it in a curtain that made them cough until their eyes streamed. When they could see again, they were looking at a cover stamped with a sigil they knew intimately: the crowned flame of the royal pyre court, crossed below by a sword.

A war chronicle. Official. Commissioned by the crown itself.

They carried it to the reading table in the center of the room—a slab of black stone worn smooth by generations of elbows—and opened it.

The first pages were triumph. Of course they were. *The Cleansing of the Roosts,* the heading read, in gold leaf that still glinted after two centuries. Ulyana read about the campaign in flat, administrative prose: eleven roosts, nine burned, two surrendered. Dragon-mothers driven from the cliffs of the Skerrow Coast. Eggs—"recovered assets"—hauled south in wagons. The language was the language of ledgers, which was why it cut so deep; Ulyana had spent years writing entries like this, reducing impossible things to columns and figures, and they knew exactly how much cruelty could hide inside a tidy number.

*Assets recovered: 214.*

Two hundred and fourteen eggs. Ulyana sat very still and let the number be a number for a while, because if they let it be anything else they would have to leave the archive and they couldn't afford to.

Then the tone of the chronicle changed.

The scribe's hand shifted—same ink, same page, but suddenly hurried, the letters leaning forward as if running. The date jumped by months. And the content stopped being a victory record and became something else entirely.

*The allies of the dragons have broken the sky over Vaelmoor. We do not mean this as poets mean it. The firmament itself has failed. Rain falls upward in the harbor districts. The sun has risen in the west for six days running. The gifted who fought beside the dragons—those the crown had registered, taxed, and trusted—have turned their arts against us all at once, and without the dragons to command them, there is no one to command them either. There is no negotiating with a storm that has lost its shepherd.*

Ulyana's mouth went dry.

*Without the dragons to command them.*

Bram's words, two hundred years early. Ulyana turned pages faster now, past accounts of cities where gravity had simply stopped applying to certain streets, past a description of a plague of uncontrolled healings that grew flesh over whole villages—"the Green Mercy," the scribe called it, and even through the formal script Ulyana could hear the horror—past lists of names, thousands of names, gifted folk who had been bound to the dragons' service and who, when the dragons burned, came apart like knots cut loose.

That was the part no one told children. The war stories everyone knew were simple: crown against dragon, fire against fire, and peace at the end when the last dragon flew east. What the chronicle described instead was a world where the crown had killed the shepherds and then discovered, far too late, that the flock was made of wolves.

Or—or lambs, Ulyana thought, remembering the child in the market square, the cart floating, the mother screaming. Power didn't make you a wolf. Power without a shape made you weather.

* * *

Near midnight, Ulyana found the covenant's shadow.

It wasn't named as such. The word *covenant* appeared nowhere in the chronicle—of course it didn't; the guild's founding secret would hardly be recorded in a crown commission. But between one chapter and the next, tucked into the account of the war's ending, was a passage so strange that Ulyana read it four times before accepting it.

*In the final year, a faction among the surviving dragons proposed a different ending. Rather than continue the breaking of the sky, they would gather the unbound gifts of their fallen allies—all of them, every scattered power—and hold them within their own fire, as a shepherd holds a flock within a fold. In exchange, the crown would cease its burning and permit one roost to stand. The negotiations were conducted by intermediaries whose names the crown has ordered struck from all records. The resulting accord is known in some quarters as the Quiet Peace. The crown does not acknowledge that any such accord exists.*

Ulyana read it again. And again.

One roost permitted to stand. Intermediaries whose names were struck away. An accord the crown pretended had never happened.

The Vesper Guild. The guild *was* the intermediaries. Had been, all along, since before it had a name—since the ashes were still warm. Every impossible thing in their ledgers, every bottled storm and borrowed year, every scrap of power they'd ever traded or sheltered, flowed from gifts held safe inside a dragon's fire, held there by a promise made in the last year of a war that had nearly ended the sky.

And the crown had agreed to it, signed it, sealed it—and then spent two hundred years pretending otherwise, so that when the hunt came again, no one would remember there had ever been a treaty to break.

Ulyana put their head down on the cold stone table and laughed, once, a sound with no humor in it at all. Odile had burned the ledgers to protect the guild. But the oldest ledger—the one Bram kept wrapped in oilcloth beneath the dragon's bier—was older than the crown's amnesia. Older than the lie. It was the treaty itself, hidden in plain sight inside the family that had been built on top of it.

*When the fire dies,* the chronicle had said, in its careful bureaucratic way, *the powers return to their bearers. Or to whoever stands nearest.*

There it was. Not metaphor. Not Bram being dramatic. Arithmetic.

Ember-of-Endings was dying. When the last flame went out, every gift bound across two centuries would come home at once—to the descendants of the bound, to strangers, to the city itself. Hundreds of powers, unshaped, unshepherded, arriving simultaneously in a kingdom whose answer to magic was currently a pyre court and a registration list.

The sky had broken once with only a generation's worth of unbound gifts loose in the world. How much worse would it be with two hundred years?

* * *

Ulyana didn't remember deciding to keep reading. But the lamp had burned low, and the chronicle was finished, and their hands had found another volume without consulting them—one of the struck-from-record documents, apparently not struck thoroughly enough, a bundle of loose correspondence tied with wire that had rusted to lace.

Letters. Negotiation letters. And on the third one, a signature that stopped Ulyana's heart.

*Vesper.*

Not Delacroix. Not Odile. Just *Vesper*—the name the guild wore now like a borrowed coat, and here it was at the bottom of a letter proposing terms to a crown envoy: the fold of fire, the one standing roost, the guild's eternal office as keeper of the bound. The handwriting was old-fashioned but legible, and beside the signature, in smaller script, a line that Ulyana copied onto the back of their hand with a stub of pencil because they could not stop looking at it:

*Let it be a hearth, not a hiding place. Let them come to the fire freely, or the fire will fail, for a fold built on fear holds only until the wolf arrives—and we have made the wolf ourselves.*

Ulyana stared at the words until they blurred.

*A hearth, not a hiding place.* Whoever Vesper had been—founder, dreamer, traitor to both sides—they had seen the flaw in their own bargain from the very start. A covenant built on concealment could only last as long as the secret held. The moment the crown came hunting again, the whole structure would be tested, and the fire—fed for centuries by gifts given in fear, in desperation, in the dark—would have nothing left to hold it together.

The fire was failing not because dragons' flames ran out. It was failing because fear was a poor fuel, and the guild had been feeding it nothing else for two hundred years.

Ulyana sat back in the dark and felt the shape of the whole terrible problem settle around them like the archive's cold. It wasn't just that Ember-of-Endings was dying. It was that everything the guild had done to survive—every hiding place, every ward, every ledger kept locked and every gift kept bound—had been slowly starving the thing that kept them all safe. The family's foundation wasn't just a countdown. It was a countdown they had wound themselves.

And somewhere north, in a pyre court, Maren was in a cell, and Sera Vance was somewhere with a warrant and a memory of loving someone gifted, and a child in the Vesper quarter was carrying a power that had snapped loose early, a single thread already unraveling from the fold.

One thread. Ulyana tried to imagine hundreds. Thousands. All at once, all leaderless, in a city full of people taught for two hundred years to burn what they feared.

They closed the correspondence bundle carefully, the way you close a door on a sickroom.

* * *

It was nearly dawn when Ulyana climbed the stairs, and Pell was waiting at the top with her lantern and her worried face, and behind her, impossibly, stood Odile.

The guildmaster looked as though she hadn't slept—which, Ulyana reflected, none of them had, these days. Her silver-streaked hair was pinned up hastily, and she held a folded paper in one hand that Ulyana recognized by its seal: crown issue. A proclamation. Fresh ones had been going up on every wall since the raid.

"You're supposed to be cataloguing water damage," Odile said.

"I found some," Ulyana said. "Extensive."

Something flickered at the corner of Odile's mouth—not quite a smile, more the ghost of one, visiting from a time when smiling had been affordable. Then she held out the paper. "You should see this before the criers finish their rounds."

Ulyana took it. The print swam for a moment; they made themselves focus.

It was a schedule. That was the worst part—it wasn't a threat, it was an *itinerary*. A list of dates and districts for the coming month. Public sentencings at the pyre court, weekly now instead of monthly. And at the bottom, underlined twice in the clerk's officious hand:

*In the event of widespread magical disturbance, martial law will be declared and the pyre court empowered to act summarily throughout the capital.*

"Widespread magical disturbance," Ulyana read aloud. Their voice sounded far away. "They've written it down. They've *scheduled* the apocalypse's paperwork."

"They don't know what's coming," Odile said quietly. "But they can feel it. Everyone can. The wards have been itching for a fortnight. Animals won't sleep. The river ran backward Tuesday morning for eleven minutes and the harbormaster swore his clerks to silence." She looked at Ulyana—really looked, the way she did when she was weighing whether to trust someone with the family's bones. "What did you find down there?"

Ulyana hesitated. Everything in them wanted to protect her—to carry this alone the way Bram carried the fire, the way the guild carried everything, in the dark, in the folds of a coat. But the letter on the table below had said *a hearth, not a hiding place*, and Ulyana was suddenly, desperately tired of hiding places.

"The first dragon war didn't end the way they teach it," they said. "It ended with a bargain. Our bargain. The guild brokered it—we've been the crown's secret treaty-keepers for two hundred years, and the crown erased our names so it could hunt us with a clean conscience." They held up the schedule. "And when the fire goes out, every gift it holds comes loose at once. All of them. The same powers that broke the sky over Vaelmoor—but two centuries' worth, and no dragons left to herd them."

Odile was silent for a long moment. Down the corridor, somewhere above, the library's morning bells began, muffled and ordinary, marking an ordinary day in a city that didn't know it was standing on a fuse.

"Then we have less time than I thought," she said at last, "and a harder choice than I hoped." She folded the proclamation away. "Because there's news from the north, Ulyana. The pyre court has posted its list for the week's sentencing."

Ulyana's chest went tight. "Maren?"

Odile met their eyes. "Third on the list. Saturday at sundown." A pause, and then, gently, in the voice she used for delivering wounds she wished she didn't have to: "And Ulyana—the second name on it is Bram."
