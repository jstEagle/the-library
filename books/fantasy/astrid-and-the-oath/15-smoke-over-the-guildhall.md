## 15. Smoke Over the Guildhall

The wards broke like a held breath finally let go.

Ulyana felt it before they heard it—a thinning in the air of the guildhall's cellar, where they slept curled among the ledgers that Odile had refused to burn and could not bring herself to keep. For three days the books had sat between them like an argument neither would finish. Ulyana had taken to sleeping beside them, one hand on the oldest ledger, as if proximity could settle the question of what a person owed to the past.

Then the wards went, all at once, the way a candle goes when pinched—not a flicker, not a struggle, just absence. The hum Bram had woven into the stones for longer than Ulyana had been alive simply stopped, and in its place came silence so complete it rang.

They were on their feet before they knew they'd moved.

Dawn was still an hour off. The guildhall above them should have been asleep: Odile in her rooms with the door open because she claimed she liked to hear the house breathe; Maren in the apprentice cot she'd reclaimed after nine years away; the child—Pell, eight years old, whose gift had snapped loose from the fire eleven days ago and who now floated spoons and door-latches whenever he dreamed—tucked into the alcove behind the kitchen.

Ulyana took the stairs two at a time and came out into the front room, where the smell hit them first.

Smoke. Not hearth-smoke. Torch-smoke, pitch and sulfur, carried on air that moved wrong, moving *in* through walls that should have kept the world out.

"Maren," they said, and their voice cracked on the name, and then the windows did.

Glass burst inward along the whole eastern face of the guildhall, not shattered but *opened*, each pane folding aside like a page turned by an enormous careful hand, and through them came men in crown-iron—grey cloaks, white gloves, the sunburst sigil of the Pyre Court stamped on every breastplate. They poured through the windows and the doors at once, and Ulyana understood, with a cold clarity that felt almost like calm, that this had never been a search. This was a harvest.

"*Down*," Maren's voice, from the top of the stairs, and then she was past Ulyana, moving with a speed that belied her grey-streaked hair, hands already sketching shapes in the air that left faint gold afterimages—the old hedge-craft, the craft the crown called witchcraft and burned for, done quick and dirty and brilliant.

The first soldier reached the foot of the stairs and stopped as if he'd walked into a wall of water. The second tried to shove past him and folded, gasping, to his knees. Maren stood above them both with her hand outstretched and her face terrible and calm.

"Ulyana." She didn't look back. "The cellar. Take the boy and the pages and go down through the cistern, the way I showed you yesterday. *Now.*"

"I'm not leaving you."

"You are." Now she did look, and what was in her eyes was not the barbed tenderness of their reunion, not the old wound of the healing gone wrong and the village and the running. It was something simpler and worse. It was a teacher looking at a student who had finally learned the lesson. "I spent nine years telling myself I'd find you and make it right. Let me make it right. Go."

Boots on the roof. More of them, coming over the tiles like beetles. Ulyana hesitated one heartbeat too long, and Maren made the choice for them—she turned, flung both arms wide, and the whole staircase bloomed with light, a lattice of gold threads snapping taut across every step, every rail, every gap, a web with no spider in it except her.

"Go," she said again, gentler now, and smiled, and it was the smile from the cottage, from before everything, the smile that had made a frightened child believe that being strange might be survivable. "Ledgers keep, little bird. So do I."

Ulyana went.

* * *

Pell was awake in the alcove, eyes huge, a spoon hovering over his fist without his noticing—he always levitated something when he was scared, a spoon or a button or once, horribly, himself—and Ulyana scooped him up with one arm and the covenant pages with the other, the brittle vellum wrapped in oilcloth against their chest, and took the cellar stairs in the dark by memory.

Bram was already there.

He stood before the low arch that led to Ember-of-Endings' den beneath the guildhall, the last dragon in the world, guttering like a lamp in wind, and he was dressed—not in his nightclothes but in his old keeper's coat, brass buckles and dragon-scale patches, the coat he'd told Ulyana once he'd wear only for a funeral or a war.

"Bram—" Ulyana started.

"I know." His voice was gravel and ash. "I felt them come over the ward-line an hour ago. Wards don't fail, girl. They're *let go*. Someone inside." He didn't say the name. He didn't have to; they both thought of Toma, terrified for his registered daughter, and said nothing, because some betrayals were too heavy to lift twice. "Listen. The cistern passage runs under the Vesper quarter and comes up in the tannery row. You take the boy. You take the pages. You do not stop, you do not come back, and you do not let anyone read those pages until it's time."

"The fire—" Ulyana's throat closed. "If they reach Ember-of-Endings—"

"They won't reach her while I'm standing in the doorway." Bram turned and looked at Ulyana then, and his eyes were wet, which Ulyana had never seen, not once in four years, not even when he spoke of the other dragons, the roosts, the sky breaking. "Forty years I've fed her splinters of myself, Ulyana. My memories, my years, my anger—everything the covenant asked and more, because someone had to keep her lit. I am nearly empty. But an empty man standing in a doorway is still a wall."

Above them, muffled through floors of stone, the guildhall shook. Something heavy fell. A man shouted, and the shout cut off.

"Odile," Ulyana whispered.

"Is the hardest woman in this city and has escaped better soldiers than these." Bram pushed the oilcloth bundle harder against Ulyana's chest. "But she can't escape if you stand here weeping about it. *Move.*"

Ulyana moved. At the mouth of the cistern passage they looked back once—only once—and saw Bram settle himself before the dragon's arch like a man settling into a chair by his own hearth, and saw, beyond him, deep in the dark, a single ember-colored eye open and regard him with something that was not animal and had never been animal, and then the dark of the passage took them both.

* * *

The cistern was a cathedral nobody prayed in.

Water ran shin-deep along channels cut before the crown existed, and the sound of it filled the vaulted dark the way an organ fills a nave, and Pell rode on Ulyana's hip with his face buried in their neck and his small fists knotted in their shirt, and the spoon—of course the spoon—drifted along behind them like a loyal, ridiculous duckling.

They had gone perhaps two hundred paces when the first light came down.

It fell through a grate overhead, torchlight, and with it voices—crown-iron voices, crisp with command. "Seal the quarter. Every door. The inquisitor wants the ledger-keeper alive." A pause. "The old keeper too, if he surrenders her."

*Her.* Ulyana pressed against the wet stone below the grate, Pell clutched tight, and understood that Sera Vance had finally assembled the truth they'd watched her carry around like a splinter for weeks: that the ledger-keeper of the Vesper Guild, the storm-defaulting apprentice who had caught a bottled tempest out of the air in front of forty witnesses, was someone she used to know.

Someone she used to love, if the way she'd hesitated at the market cross meant anything, if the way she'd looked at Ulyana across a crowd and then deliberately looked away meant anything.

"Penny for the eel-seller," Pell whispered against Ulyana's ear, repeating the countersign Odile had drilled into them, and Ulyana hushed him and moved on, wading, counting side-passages by the chalk marks Maren had shown them yesterday—yesterday, when there had still been a yesterday.

Behind them, muffled by stone and water, the guildhall began to burn.

There was no mistaking it. The sound changed—the shouts took on a different timbre, the commands turned from *search* to *out, out, it's catching*—and then came a light in the passage itself, orange and wrong, dancing on the water ahead of them, and smoke followed it, rolling low along the ceiling like a living thing seeking a way out and finding none.

Ulyana ran. Pell bounced against their hip, crying now, silently, the way children cry when they've learned crying brings soldiers, and the spoon spun wildly behind them, and the smoke thickened, and somewhere above—impossibly, terribly, through six feet of stone and burning timber—came a sound Ulyana felt in their teeth before they heard it with their ears.

A roar. Old and vast and ragged, like a bell cracking, like the sky remembering how to break.

Ember-of-Endings, awake, and furious, and dying.

And beneath the roar, thinner, human, a voice Ulyana knew, shouting words the stone swallowed—but Ulyana caught the shape of them, the cadence, and it sounded like a man reciting something. A covenant, maybe. Or a goodbye.

Then the passage forked, and the chalk mark pointed left toward the tannery row, and there was no more time for listening, only for running, and Ulyana ran.

* * *

They came up through the trapdoor behind the tannery vats into air that tasted of lye and dawn, and the Vesper quarter was already lost.

Soldiers at every corner. Criers already at work, their voices hoarse with repetition: *"By order of the Crown, the Vesper Guild is dissolved, its members declared felons, its premises forfeit. Any who shelter the fugitives will share their sentence."* Smoke stood over the guildhall in a black column, and against it, wheeling, circling, a shape that made Ulyana's heart stop and start again—a dragon-shape, vast, but flying wrong, laboring, trailing embers like a wound trails blood, rising from the burning hall on wings that beat too slowly and climbing anyway, because apparently even a dying dragon could not be made to stay in a cage.

People lined the rooftops to watch it pass. Nobody cheered. Nobody shouted for the guards either. They just watched, hundreds of faces turned upward, and Ulyana, crouched behind a rain barrel with a trembling child, saw in those upturned faces something that looked less like fear than like hunger—as though the whole city had forgotten it was allowed to look at magic, and now that it was looking, could not stop.

A woman near the tannery gate crossed herself and then, glancing around, uncrossed it.

"Where's your granny?" Pell whispered, meaning Odile, whom he adored, who taught him cards.

"She's coming to meet us," Ulyana lied, and hated how easily it came, and pulled him deeper into the shadow of the barrels.

Because Odile had not come out. Neither had Toma, nor the twins from the counting room, nor half a dozen others whose names lived only in memory now, since the ledgers were ash or seized. And Maren—

Maren had stood on a staircase and built a web of light with her bare hands and smiled at Ulyana with the old cottage smile, and Ulyana had run, because she told them to, because that was the lesson, because teachers give the hardest lessons last.

Grief arrived then, all of it at once, in the space between one breath and the next: grief for the guildhall burning, for Bram alone in the dark with a dying god, for Odile unaccounted for, for Maren most of all, and underneath it, older and colder, the guilt that had driven them to the guild in the first place—that whatever Ulyana touched, however carefully, ended broken or burning, and everyone who loved them paid the price.

"No," they said aloud, to themselves, to the guilt, to the nine-year-old who had run from Maren's cottage believing exactly this. Pell looked up at them, startled. Ulyana knelt, took his small floating spoon out of the air, and closed his fingers around it. "Listen to me. We are going to walk out of this quarter like we belong to it, because we do. You're going to keep that spoon down and your eyes down and your gift *quiet*, and when I tell you to run, you'll run, and you won't look back no matter what you hear. Can you do that?"

Pell nodded. Then, with the terrible directness of children: "Are we going to save everybody?"

Ulyana thought of the covenant pages against their chest. Of the clause Bram had made them read three times until they understood: *when the fire dies, every bound gift breaks loose at once.* Of the archive maps, the first war, the sky breaking. Of Sera Vance, hunting them through streets she used to know another life in.

"Not everybody," Ulyana said. "But everybody we can. Starting with us."

They went out through the tannery gate into the smoking dawn, two small figures in a crowd of watchers, and behind them the column of smoke bent eastward on the morning wind, pointing, like a finger, like an accusation, toward the pyre court on the far hill—where, if the criers were to be believed, the condemned would be brought at week's end for the crown's great public sentencing.

Where, among the names read aloud to the crowd, Ulyana would have bet everything they had left, there would be a hedge-witch called Maren.

The undercity yawned ahead of them, dark and damp and full of old secrets, and Ulyana stepped down into it carrying the covenant, the child, and the beginning of a plan that would need, before it was finished, an inquisitor's help.

First, though, they had to survive long enough to ask her.
