## 16. Undercity Parley

The undercity smelled of wet stone and old smoke, the way Sera remembered it from childhood—before the white gloves, before the title, before she learned that the dark beneath a city keeps every secret the city above wants forgotten.

She had followed Ulyana Delacroix down through three locked gates and one collapsed stair, and now she stood at the mouth of an aqueduct older than the crown itself, watching a figure in a rain-dark coat pick their way along a ledge slick with moss. The lantern in Ulyana's hand threw a small, stubborn circle of light. It was the only warm thing down here.

Sera had her warrant in her breast pocket. She had carried it for two days without filing it, and every hour she hadn't filed it felt like a stone added to a scale that was already tipping.

*Ledger-keeper of the Vesper Guild,* the reports said. *Trafficker in magic. Wanted for questioning.*

They had not said: *the boy who once pulled a splinter out of your palm with a touch and swore you to secrecy with a crooked smile.*

They had not said: *Ulyana.*

"You walk loudly," Sera said into the dark, "for someone running."

Ulyana froze. The lantern swung. For a long moment there was only the drip of water somewhere far off, counting seconds neither of them wanted counted. Then Ulyana turned, slowly, and the light found Sera's face, and Sera watched recognition move across it like weather—the flinch first, then something softer and more dangerous than fear.

"Sera." Ulyana's voice came out rough. "You're supposed to be wearing gloves."

"I am." Sera raised her hands. White kid leather, immaculate, the uniform of the Pyre Court's inquisitorial office. "I'm always wearing them. That's rather the point of me."

"Then arrest me." Ulyana lifted their chin, but Sera saw the tremor underneath, the exhaustion of someone who had been running since dawn, since the raid, since smoke rose over the guildhall and took Maren Yarrow with it. "You've got your warrant. You've got me cornered in a hole with no wards and no witnesses. Do it."

Sera didn't move. Her hands stayed where they were, half-raised, absurdly formal, like a dancer caught mid-figure with no music.

"No," she said.

Ulyana blinked. "No?"

"No." She let her arms fall. "Not until you tell me what I'd be arresting you *for*. And don't give me the official version. I wrote half the official versions myself; I know how they lie."

*

They walked. There was nothing else to do—standing still in the undercity was how people became part of its architecture—and so they moved together along the aqueduct ledge, Sera's boots sure on stone she hadn't touched in fifteen years, Ulyana's lantern lighting the way like a held breath.

"You know this place," Ulyana said finally.

"My mother ran messages down here when I was small. Before the last purge made it fashionable to forget the undercity existed." Sera kept her eyes forward. "There are tunnels under the Vesper quarter that predate the guild by four hundred years. Did you know that? The guild likes to think it's old. It's a child playing in ruins."

"The guild doesn't think it's old. The guild knows exactly what it's sitting on." A pause. "That's rather the problem."

Sera stopped walking. "Say that again."

Ulyana turned. In the lantern light their face looked carved out of tiredness—hollow cheeks, a cut above the eyebrow still crusted dark, eyes that had the particular shine of someone who has cried recently and decided not to do it again in front of witnesses.

"You want the truth?" Ulyana said. "All of it? Because if I give you all of it, Inquisitor Vance, you can't un-know it. And then you'll have to choose what to do with it, and I've watched enough people make that choice badly this week to last me a lifetime."

"I've spent my whole career choosing," Sera said quietly. "Mostly badly. Give me something new."

Ulyana laughed—a short, cracked sound with no humor in it. Then they sat down on the ledge, right there, in the muck and the dark, and set the lantern between them like a candle on a table between two disputants.

"All right," Ulyana said. "Sit down, Sera. This is going to take longer than an arrest."

*

Sera sat. The stone was cold through her coat. Above them, faint as memory, the city went about its evening—cart wheels, a drunk singing, the distant bell of the Pyre Court tolling the hour, and she flinched at that sound before she could stop herself.

"You heard it too," Ulyana said. Not a question.

"The sentencing bells. Three burnings tomorrow. Two hedge-folk and—" Sera's throat closed. "And Maren Yarrow."

"Maren taught me everything I was afraid of about myself," Ulyana said. "And everything I loved about it, too. So." They drew a breath. "The truth. From the beginning, because you'll want the beginning."

And they told her.

They told her about the ledgers—not client accounts, not inventory, but something else woven through them, page after page in cipher and old script: names. Hundreds of years of names. Gifted folk who had come to the Vesper Guild frightened and hunted, the way Ulyana had come, and been taken in without questions, and been given a choice.

"They weren't cured," Ulyana said. "They weren't hidden, exactly. They were *bound*. Into the fire."

"What fire?"

"There's a dragon under the guildhall, Sera."

Sera stared at them. Somewhere above, the drunk finished his song.

"A dragon," she repeated.

"Ember-of-Endings. Last of the roosts the crown burned in the first war. Ancient. Dying. Has been dying for longer than anyone alive can remember, and the guild has been keeping the flame fed." Ulyana's hands moved as they spoke, shaping the story in the air the way they used to shape little lights when they were children and thought no one watched. "Here is the covenant, the one Bram showed me in the oldest ledger: the gifted come to the guild, and they give their power—not freely, not all at once, but bound, thread by thread, into the dragon-fire. The fire holds it. The fire *is* it, partly. Every impossible thing the guild ever sold—a bottled storm, a borrowed year, a scale of living ember—all of it powered by gifts that came in frightened and left their power behind so they could live in peace."

Sera's gloved hands curled against her knees. "That's not shelter. That's—"

"It's both," Ulyana said sharply. "Don't you dare sit there and judge it. Those people would have burned otherwise. My mother would have burned. *I* would have burned. The covenant bought centuries of quiet lives for people the crown called abominations. Whatever it cost, it was paid by people who chose it, and it worked."

"And now?"

Ulyana was quiet for a moment. The lantern flame leaned, steadied.

"Now the fire is guttering out. And here's the part you really can't un-know, Sera." Their voice dropped, and the undercity seemed to lean in around it. "When the fire dies, every gift bound into it breaks loose. At once. All of them. Centuries of power, unbound, unheld, in the middle of a city, in the middle of a witch-hunt. No guild to steady it. No dragon to hold it. Just—everything, everywhere, all at once."

Sera thought of the child. Everyone in the city had thought of the child by now—the girl who had lifted a cart over her head in the fish market while grown men screamed and ran, who had set it down gently, weeping, saying she didn't know how it happened, she'd never done anything like it before. Sera had filed the incident report herself. She had written *unregistered manifestation, source unknown*, and her pen had shaken.

"That child," Sera said slowly. "Her gift broke loose from the fire."

"Bram thinks so. He calls it the Guttering. The flame falters, and things start slipping free." Ulyana looked at her directly. "It's going to get worse. Much worse. And the crown's answer is more pyres, which means more panic, which means more gifted folk running to the guild for shelter—which means more gifts bound into a fire that can't hold what it already has. We are filling a cup that is already cracking, and the crown keeps pouring."

Sera sat with it. She was trained to sit with terrible information—that was the job, the whole rotten job: receive the horror, order it, file it, act. But this horror wouldn't order. It kept spilling over the edges of every category she owned.

"How long?" she asked.

"Bram says weeks. Maybe days, now, after the raid. The wards failing stressed the flame. And they've moved the dragon—did you know that? Your Pyre Court has Ember-of-Endings in a cage under the sentencing yard, feeding off its heat to light the pyres." Bitterness scraped the words raw. "They're burning my family with the last breaths of the creature that kept us safe for five hundred years."

Sera closed her eyes. She saw the sentencing yard as she'd seen it that morning from her office window: the great iron pyre structure, the crowd stands, the scaffolds being raised. She had assumed the heat that shimmered above it came from furnaces below. Furnaces. Of course she'd assumed furnaces.

She had signed requisitions for that fuel.

"Oh," she said. It was a very small word for a very large falling.

"Sera." Ulyana's voice, careful. "Look at me."

She looked. Lantern light, wet stone, the face of a person she had loved when love was simple—two children in a market town, one of whom could make sparks dance on their fingertips and the other of whom promised, solemnly, to keep the secret forever. She had kept it forever. She had kept it so well she had built an entire career on the keeping, become the keeper of everyone's secrets whether they wanted her or not, hunted the gifted with a thoroughness that was just another way of never stopping looking for—

For him. For the friend she'd lost. For the boy in front of her, who wasn't lost at all, who had simply been hiding somewhere she'd never thought to look because looking would have meant finding.

"Why didn't you register?" she asked. It came out harsher than she intended. "When the proclamation went up. You knew the terms. Register and live. Why run?"

"Register and be bound to the crown's leash, or register and be bound to a dying fire, or don't register and burn." Ulyana shrugged. "Those were the options. None of them were living. And besides—" a ghost of the old crooked smile, gone almost before it arrived— "you of all people should understand. Some things aren't yours to hand over. Not to a court. Not to a guild. Not even to a fire."

"And yet you gave yours anyway. To the guild. To the covenant."

"I was seventeen and terrified and they offered me a family." Ulyana's jaw tightened. "Yes. I gave it. And I've regretted it and been grateful for it in the same breath for eleven years, and if you'd lived one week inside that contradiction you'd stop asking questions with easy answers."

Silence. Water dripped its patient count somewhere in the dark.

"I lost someone," Sera said, before she could decide not to say it. "Years ago. Gifted. He hid it from everyone but me, and then he couldn't hide it anymore, and the village—healing that went wrong, a fever he tried to break, and the fever broke and so did something in him, and the village turned on him inside a day." Her voice stayed flat, professional, the tone she used for casualty reports, because the alternative was not a tone she could survive using. "He ran. I never found out what happened to him. I told myself I joined the inquisitors to change the system from inside. Make registration humane. Make sanctuary real. And instead I—" She stopped. Started again. "Instead I became very good at finding people like him."

Ulyana said nothing. But their hand moved, slowly, across the space between them, and stopped short of hers—an offering, not a demand. The same gesture, Sera realized, from a market stall twenty years ago: *take it or don't, but I'm putting it here.*

She didn't take it. She looked at it instead, the lantern light pooling in Ulyana's palm, and asked the question that mattered.

"If I help you—if I walk away from the warrant, from the Court, from everything I built—what is it you actually want? Not the grand version. The next step."

Ulyana pulled the hand back. Reached into their coat instead, and drew out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth: pages, old ones, edges browned, covered in dense script and diagrams Sera half-recognized from forbidden archives.

"The covenant," Ulyana said. "The original. Bram and I have been reading it since the raid. And there's a clause near the end—we only found it yesterday. The binding isn't permanent. It was never meant to be permanent. It can be *renewed*. The fire can be refed, rekindled, the whole covenant restated—but only by a willing gift, freely given. Permanently surrendered into the flame. Not hidden. Not caged. Given."

Sera went very still. "Given by whom?"

"Anyone gifted. One is enough to renew it, if the giving is true." Ulyana met her eyes, and Sera saw it there, plain as a signature: the plan already written, the decision already made, the price already accepted. "I've been drafting the wording for two days. Rewriting the covenant. Making it a hearth instead of a hiding place. Something the whole city could warm at, openly, honestly. But it needs a dragon, and the crown has the dragon, and the crown has Maren, and the crown has a sentencing tomorrow with three names on the list."

"So you're going to storm the Pyre Court," Sera said. "With a handful of fugitives and a dying dragon."

"With whatever I have," Ulyana said. "Which, currently, is a handful of fugitives, a dying dragon, and—" they gestured at her, at the white gloves, at the warrant she hadn't filed— "apparently, an inquisitor who follows people into holes in the ground and then doesn't arrest them."

Sera laughed. It surprised her, the laugh—short and cracked, a twin to Ulyana's own, and it broke something loose in her chest that had been locked since the bells.

She reached into her breast pocket and drew out the warrant. Unfolded it. Read her own name at the bottom, her own seal, the sentence she had drafted and redrafted and never delivered: *Ulyana Delacroix, ledger-keeper of the Vesper Guild, wanted for questioning regarding trafficking in magic.*

Then she held it to the lantern's flame.

The paper caught reluctantly, the way old paper does, curling and browning at the edges before the fire took hold. Sera held it until the heat bit through her glove, until the last corner crumbled, until ash drifted down between them onto the wet stone like dirty snow.

"That's treason," Ulyana said softly.

"That's a choice." She brushed ash from her gloves. "You said I couldn't un-know it. Fine. Then I'm knowing it all the way. Tomorrow morning, the sentencing yard will have four hundred crown soldiers, a public crowd, three prisoners, and one dragon in an iron cage. If you're going to storm it, you'll need someone who knows the yard's rotations, the gate codes, and which of the officers on duty owes me favors." She stood, joints aching, and offered Ulyana her gloved hand—properly, this time, palm up. "I'm not doing this for you, Ulyana. I'm doing it for a boy who pulled a splinter out of my hand and never asked me for anything except a secret kept. Consider it paid in full."

Ulyana took her hand. Their grip was cold and strong and shaking.

"One condition," Ulyana said. "When this is done—whatever happens—you hear the whole covenant read aloud. New version. I want a witness from the other side of the war. I want it witnessed by someone who used to hunt us."

"Then you'd better make it worth witnessing," Sera said.

Above them, muffled by forty feet of stone, the Pyre Court bell tolled the midnight hour. Three names. One dawn. And somewhere beneath the sentencing yard, in an iron cage, an ancient fire waited—guttering, patient, and listening—for what would come next.
