## 17. The Price of the Flame

The undercity smelled of wet stone and old smoke, and by the third hour I had learned to stop flinching at every drip that sounded like boots.

We had made a nest of sorts in the flooded crypt beneath the old tannery—a place the guild had used twice in living memory for exactly this kind of hiding, which told you something about how often we needed it. The child, Pip, slept against Maren's spare cloak with her head on Odile's satchel, her small hands still twitching through dreams where carts floated and grown men screamed. The covenant pages lay spread across an upturned barrel, weighted at the corners with four candle stubs because Bram had taught me never to let old paper lie flat without weight; paper remembers being rolled, and it wants to go back.

I had read the covenant eleven times since the raid. I could recite it now the way Maren had once made me recite the humors—*blood, bile, phlegm, melancholy*—until the words stopped meaning anything and became only rhythm.

*A gift freely given shall not be taken. A gift surrendered whole shall hold the flame. What is given cannot be reclaimed, nor returned, nor called back by love or grief or crown.*

The last line was the one I kept returning to. *Nor called back by love.* Whoever wrote it had known someone would try.

Sera sat across the crypt from me, close enough that the candlelight reached us both but far enough that neither of us had to pretend this was comfortable. She had taken off the white gloves. That was the thing I kept noticing—the gloves folded neatly beside her knee, her bare hands open in her lap like she was learning what they were for. Inquisitors wore the gloves so their touch never touched. She'd worn them the day she came to arrest my family. She was not wearing them now.

"You've read it more times than I have," she said quietly, nodding at the pages. "And?"

"And it's a door," I said. "It opens one way."

Odile stirred from where she stood watch at the stair, arms crossed, her silver guild-ring turned inward on her finger out of habit even though there was no one left to hide it from. "Ulyana."

"It's true," I said. "You know it's true. You've known longer than any of us. The fire doesn't just *hold* gifts—it's fed by them. Every impossible thing we ever sold, every bottled storm, every borrowed year—the power came from someone who gave it up. Willingly. That's why the covenant works and why it's dying. Nobody's given anything in three hundred years. We've been spending down an inheritance and calling it trade."

Bram sat apart from all of us, nearest the water, his ruined hands resting on his knees. He had not spoken since we came down here except to count us—twice now, aloud, like a man checking his pockets. When he spoke again his voice was gravel dragged over gravel.

"There were seven givers," he said. "In the founding years. Not all at once. Over a lifetime. The first gave her gift when the dragons were already falling, and the fire caught it like a breath catches a spark. The others followed because they saw what she bought with it—not safety. *Time*. Time for the gifted to live unburned." He looked at me then, and his eyes in the candlelight were the color of the ash at the bottom of a hearth. "Seven. And each one knew what they were doing. Each one said goodbye first."

"I'm not saying goodbye," I said. "I'm saying *here*. Here is the answer that's been sitting in your ledgers for three centuries, and everyone in this room knows it, and nobody will say it because saying it means admitting the price."

The crypt went very quiet. Even the drips seemed to wait.

Maren broke it. Of course she did. My old teacher had a talent for breaking silences the way other people broke bread—generously, messily, with no thought for who got splashed. "No," she said simply, from her place beside sleeping Pip. "Absolutely not. Sit down, Ulyana."

"I am sitting down."

"Then sit down harder." She pushed herself up, stiff from the flight through the undercity, and came to stand over the covenant pages as if she could shield them with her body. "I've watched you do arithmetic your whole life. You balance everything. You balanced the ledgers at fourteen better than Odile's own clerks. So let me balance this one for you." She held up one hand. "One apprentice, stubborn, half-trained, whose gift is small—" 

"It isn't small."

"—whose gift is *modest*," she continued, relentless, "against—" the second hand came up, fingers spreading, "—every bound gift in this city breaking loose at once. Every ward failing. Every hidden witch dragged into the street mid-working. Every dragon-covenant in every kingdom snapping like dry thread. You are proposing to pay a debt of a mountain with a pebble, and the pebble is *you*, and I did not carry you out of a burning village so you could walk into a fire on purpose."

"The village wasn't burning," I said. It came out sharper than I meant. "The village was *afraid*. There's a difference. I've spent ten years learning the difference."

Something flickered across her face—hurt, old and familiar—and I hated myself for it, and kept going anyway, because if I stopped going I would stop entirely.

"Maren. Look at the numbers." I pulled the oldest ledger toward me, the one with the hollow spine where the covenant lived, and opened it to the page I'd marked. "Ember-of-Endings held two hundred and six bound gifts at the height of the covenant. Gifts given by two hundred and six people over four hundred years—no, wait." I corrected myself before she could. "That's wrong, and the ledger proves it's wrong. Most of those weren't given. They were *bound*—pressed into the fire during the wars, when the gifted fled to the guild and the alternative was the pyre. Binding isn't giving. Binding is a cage. Giving is—" I looked down at my own hands, the ink-stained fingers that had hidden a flicker of light for ten years behind careful bookkeeping and careful silence. "Giving is what the first seven did. And the ledger says the flame burned brightest after each giving. Brighter, and *gentler*. The bound gifts make it strong. The given gifts make it kind. That's why it's guttering now—it's been running on cages, and cages don't feed a fire, they smother it."

"You don't know that giving yours will be enough," Sera said.

Her voice was level. Too level. I had known Sera Vance since we were children stealing plums off the temple cart, and I knew every register of her voice, and the too-level one meant she was arguing with herself and using me as the battlefield.

"No," I admitted. "I don't. But I know what happens if nobody gives. The Guttering accelerates—I've seen the curve in the binding records, Bram showed me. Weeks, maybe. Then every caged gift breaks at once, leaderless, terrified, in a city full of soldiers who've spent a month learning to burn witches. The war comes back. Not the war between crown and dragons—the worse one. The war between neighbors." I gestured upward, toward the streets above where dawn was probably graying the drains. "Pip levitated a cart in daylight and half the market saw it. There are hundreds like her. Hundreds of small frightened fires, and when the cage breaks they'll all flare together, and the crown will call it an uprising whether it is one or not."

"So we run," Odile said. "There are roads north. There's always been a road north."

"For how long?" I asked. "For how many? The guild sheltered maybe sixty people, Odile. Sixty, with wards and ledgers and three hundred years of practice at hiding. And the raid still found us in one night. How many can you fit on a northern road?"

She didn't answer. Her jaw worked, and I saw her do the same arithmetic I was doing, and arrive at the same ugly sum.

"There's another option," Sera said, and now her voice wasn't level at all. "You're all talking about feeding a person to a fire like it's bookkeeping. Like it's a line item. I've spent my career watching what happens when institutions decide a sacrifice is *necessary*." She stood, and in the low crypt she suddenly looked taller than she was, the way she always had, even at twelve. "I burned my warrant for you. I burned my *career*. Don't ask me to stand here and help you burn yourself."

"I'm not asking you to help. I'm telling you what I'm going to do."

"That's worse."

"Sera—"

"No." She crossed the crypt in four strides and took my face in her bare hands, and her palms were warm and slightly rough, and I realized I couldn't remember the last time anyone had touched my face. "Listen to me. I lost someone to the gift. You know that. Everyone in this room knows that story by now, the version I tell interrogations. But you don't know the real version." Her thumb moved once along my cheekbone, an old gesture from an older life. "His name was Tomas. He was my brother. And when his gift came in—small, like yours, a little warmth in his hands, nothing dangerous—he hid it. For years. Because our mother told him to, because the law told him to, because hiding was *safe*. And the hiding ate him alive, Ulyana. Slowly. He got smaller every year. And the last thing he ever said to me, the night before he walked into the sea, was that he wished there had been somewhere he could have set it down. Somewhere he could have put it *down*." Her voice cracked and she let it. "So don't you dare tell me that surrendering yourself into a fire is the same as setting something down. It isn't. It's the sea with extra steps."

The crypt was silent. Pip stirred in her sleep and murmured, and Odile moved instinctively to lay a hand on the child's shoulder, and settled her again.

"It's not the sea," I said softly. "Because the sea takes you nowhere. The fire takes you into everyone else. That's the whole point—that's what the covenant says, if you read past the legal language. *What is given shall warm all who gather at it.* I wouldn't be erased, Sera. I'd be—spread out. Into the hearth. Into every ward and every warming-stone and every small frightened gift in this city. They'd feel steadier because I'm in there. Isn't that—" my voice betrayed me, wobbled, and I steadied it the way I steadied a ledger, line by line, "—isn't that better than Tomas got? Better than hiding until the hiding kills you?"

"No," she said, "because Tomas didn't get a choice about the sea either, in the end. The world made the sea the only door. And you're doing the same thing—you're standing in front of the only door you can see and calling it destiny." She stepped back, and her hands closed into fists at her sides, and I watched her force the inquisitor's calm back over herself like pulling on the gloves. "There has to be another way. Find it. You're the best bookkeeper in this city—find it in the numbers."

"The numbers say what they say."

"Then read them again!"

"*Sera.*"

"*No!*" It tore out of her, loud enough that Bram's head came up sharp, loud enough that Pip woke with a small frightened sound. Sera stopped. Looked at the child. Looked at her own raised voice hanging in the dark. And when she spoke again it was barely a whisper. "I didn't come all this way. I didn't lose everything. To trade one pyre for another with better lighting."

Maren put her arm around Sera's shoulders, and to my astonishment Sera let her.

Bram rose, slow as a ship coming about, and came to stand beside me at the barrel. He looked down at the covenant pages for a long time. His hands, those terrible scarred hands, hovered over the old ink without touching it.

"When the seventh giver went into the fire," he said, "she was younger than you. Nineteen. And the guild tried to stop her, exactly like this. Exactly these words. And do you know what she said?" He didn't wait. "She said: *you are all arguing about the cost of the match while the house burns down.*" He turned to look at me, and there was something in his face I had never seen there in all my years at the guild—something that might have been hope, or might have been grief wearing hope's coat. "But she also said one thing more, and it's written in the margin of the founding ledger, and I've never shown it to anyone because I hoped I'd never need to."

He drew a slim volume from inside his coat—one I hadn't seen since the raid, one I'd thought burned with the rest. The founding ledger. Its cover was scorched at one corner.

"She said: *a gift given in despair feeds a fire that despairs. A gift given in joy feeds a fire that joys. If the time comes, do not come to the flame grieving. Come to it laughing, or stay home.*"

He laid the ledger open on the barrel beside the covenant, and there in the margin, in faded brown ink, in a woman's looping hand:

*Come laughing, or stay home.*

I stared at it for a long moment. And then I laughed—short, cracked, startled out of me like a sneeze—and every head in the crypt turned.

"What?" Odile demanded.

"Nothing. It's just—" I wiped my eyes. "I've been trying to be brave about this. Grim and noble and ledger-balanced. And the founder is telling me that's worthless. That the fire won't take grim." I looked around at them—at Maren with her arm still around Sera, at Odile with her hand on a sleeping child, at Bram with his ash-colored eyes, at Sera with her bare hands and her burned career. "You all think I'm sacrificing something. I'm not. Do you understand? Ten years I've been holding this gift clenched in my fist in the dark, afraid to open my hand even a crack. Ten years of balancing accounts so carefully because it was the only place my light was allowed to exist—in ink, in columns, in other people's impossible things. And you want me to mourn letting go of *that*?"

My gift rose in me as I spoke—I let it, for the first time in front of all of them, deliberately, the warmth spilling up through my chest and into my fingertips until they glowed faint gold in the crypt's darkness, and Pip gasped awake and sat up and stared with her mouth open, and none of them stepped back.

"This isn't a sacrifice," I said. "This is the first honest entry I've ever gotten to write."

"Then write it tomorrow," Sera said hoarsely. "Give me one day. One day to find another way. If I can't—if the numbers truly say what you say—then—" She couldn't finish it. She turned away, shoulders rigid, and looked at the black water of the flooded nave as though the answer might surface there.

"One day," I agreed, because she deserved that much, and because some traitor part of me wanted her to find the other way, wanted it desperately, wanted to be talked out of this by someone who loved me.

Above us, muffled through stone and earth, a bell began to ring. Not the alarm bells of the raid—this was different, slower, a great bronze heartbeat rolling down through the undercity. Bram cocked his head, listening, and his face went gray.

"That's the Pyre Court," he said. "They're ringing for a sentencing." He counted us again, lips moving—still five, plus the child—and then his eyes fixed on the stair, and his voice dropped to almost nothing.

"And I'd wager my last tooth I know whose name is on the list."
