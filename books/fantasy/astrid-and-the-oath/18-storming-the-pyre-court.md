## 18. Storming the Pyre Court

The pyre court had been a market square once, before the first dragon war. Ulyana knew this because they had read it in the sealed archives, in a hand so old the ink had gone brown as dried blood: *Here stood the Corn Exchange, where the city fed itself. Here it feeds again.*

They stood now in the shadow of the grain-merchants' arcade, hood drawn low, and watched the crown feed.

Scaffolding ringed the square on three sides, built overnight by conscripted carpenters, tiered seating for a spectacle no one wanted to attend and everyone would come to see. At the square's center rose the pyres — three of them, stacked oak and pitch, each one crowned with an iron stake. Between the pyres and the crowd stood a cordon of soldiers in crown-iron mail, and behind them, on a raised dais hung with white banners, sat the sentencing bench: three magistrates in gray, and beside them, a chair that was more throne than chair, empty, waiting for the High Inquisitor.

Maren would be third on the list. Ulyana had heard the criers read the names at dawn, standing close enough to touch the paper without being seen to want it. *Hedge-witch Maren of the Fenmarch, convicted of unregistered craft.* Third. Which meant there was time, if everything went right, which nothing ever did.

*You'll be in the crowd,* Odile had said the night before, spreading the pyre court's plans across the guildhall's scarred table — plans Sera had copied from her own order's files, her white gloves folded in her lap while she drew the guard rotations from memory. *Not in the front. Not in the back. The middle, where faces blur.*

*I should be closer,* Ulyana had said.

*You should be alive,* Odile had answered, in the voice she used for ledgers and for lies she refused to sign. *The covenant pages are in your coat. If we fail, you carry them out. That is your post, and I will hear no argument from someone who once tried to argue me down about the depreciation of bottled thunder.*

Now, in the middle distance of the crowd, Ulyana found her: Guildmaster Odile Vesper, dressed in mourning gray, hands folded like any widow come to watch justice done. Somewhere behind her moved Bram, broad as a door, his dragon-keeper's crook disguised as a walking staff. And somewhere above them all —

*There.* A flicker at the edge of the eastern scaffolding. A child sitting very still among strangers, swinging legs that did not quite reach the bench, eyes fixed on the pyres with a focus no child should have. The child whose gift had snapped loose when Ember-of-Endings faltered, who could lift a cart with a thought and had been hidden in the undercity ever since, wrapped in wards and Odile's fiercest promises.

*You're supposed to be below,* Ulyana breathed, though the child could not possibly hear.

But the child was not looking at the pyres. The child was looking at the iron cage behind the dais — the holding pen where the condemned waited — and the cart-sized stack of oak beneath it shivered, just slightly, as though something underneath the world had leaned against it.

*Oh,* Ulyana thought. *Oh, you brilliant, terrible girl. You're going to do it yourself.*

* * *

Sera Vance had dressed in her full regalia for the first time since arriving in the city: white gloves, white cloak, the silver sunburst of the Inquisition at her throat. It felt like putting on armor made of her own old mistakes.

She stood at the foot of the dais with her warrant-book in hand and read the names again, though she knew them by heart. Nine condemned. Eight were strangers to her — names gathered by lesser inquisitors, hedge-crafters and charm-sellers, most of them guilty of nothing worse than easing a neighbor's fever or finding a lost goat. The ninth name sat on the page like a splinter working toward her heart.

*Maren of the Fenmarch.*

She had interviewed her two days past, through the bars of the holding cage, and Maren had looked at her with those fen-green eyes and said, *You have her chin, you know. Ulyana. Stubborn as a mule and twice as likely to kick.* And then, before Sera could speak: *Whatever you're carrying, girl, set it down before it sets you down.*

Sera had not slept since.

Below the dais, the crowd murmured like a sea deciding whether to be calm. She scanned it out of habit — and habit caught something. A figure in the arcade shadow, hood up, posture wrong for a beggar and too still for a merchant. Watching the cage, not the pyres.

*Ulyana.*

Her heart did a thing she refused to name. They had agreed on this — she had insisted on it, standing in the undercity's dripping dark with the covenant pages spread between them like a map to the end of the world. *I go in uniform. I stand where I can see the magistrates. When the moment comes, I am the one who opens the cage, because no one questions my keys.* And Ulyana had looked at her across four years of silence and one week of terrible honesty and said, *If you betray us, I will never forgive you. If you die for us, I will never forgive myself. Those are my terms.*

Sera had accepted both. She was beginning to understand that loving Ulyana Delacroix had always meant accepting terms that hurt.

The trumpets sounded. The crowd hushed. And the High Inquisitor came out onto the dais.

He was not what the rumors promised. No black robes, no burning eyes — just a dry, neat man in dove-gray, with the mild face of a tax collector, and that was somehow worse. He took the throne-chair, arranged his papers, and began to read the sentences in a voice like flour sifting.

Sera's hand closed around the cage key in her pocket until the teeth bit her palm.

*Soon,* she thought. *Wait for the fire. Everything turns on the fire.*

* * *

Bram had kept dragons for fifty-one years, and he had learned this: you do not rush a flame. You bank it. You give it air when it asks and starve it when it must, and above all you never, ever let it see your fear, because fire reads fear the way scholars read books.

So he walked toward the pyre court's western gate at the pace of an old man with bad knees, leaning on his crook, and inside his chest his heart was hammering out a rhythm he had last heard the night the roosts burned.

Ember-of-Endings was here. He had known it since dawn, when the wind off the square had carried a scent no human nose could parse — ozone and hot stone and the particular sweetness of a dying dragon's breath. They had moved her in the night, after the raid, chained in a wagon under oilcloth, and brought her to the pyre court not to burn but to *be* the burning: the crown's engineers had built the execution pyres around her, feeding their kindling from the heat of her body, a monstrous economy. Kill the gifted with the last warmth of the last dragon. Bram had wept when Sera told him, quietly, in the undercity, and then he had stopped weeping and started planning, because grief was a luxury and Ember-of-Endings had never once afforded him his.

The plan was simple, which meant it would fail beautifully. Sera opened the cage. Odile's people raised the cry — a staged panic, a false alarm of fire in the eastern tiers — and in the confusion, Bram went to the dragon. Fifty-one years, and every path he had ever walked ended at a dragon.

The gate guards waved him through. Old men were invisible; that was the one mercy of age.

Inside the service yard, between the wood-stacks and the great wagon, he smelled her clearly now, and under the smell, fainter than memory, he heard her. Not words. Dragons did not use words unless they wished to frighten you. It was more like standing near a bell that had already been struck — a resonance in the ribs, in the teeth.

*Little keeper,* the resonance said. *You came.*

*I always come,* Bram said, and put his hand against the wagon's rail, and felt through six inches of crown-oak the heat of her answering.

*They mean to burn the witch-folk on my back, keeper. They have staked my fire to their stakes. If I rise, I may burn this whole square, and the innocent with the guilty.*

*Then we choose together,* Bram said. *Like always.*

Above him, the High Inquisitor's voice sifted through the air, reading the second name. The crowd was quiet in the way crowds are quiet before they are anything else.

Bram slid the wagon's pin free with hands that did not shake, and waited for the cry.

* * *

It came from the eastern tiers, right on time — a scream of *fire*, thin and theatrical, followed by Odile's people doing the finest work of their lives: a dozen voices taking it up, a smoke-pot cracking against the scaffold boards, the crowd surging like water finding a breach. Soldiers turned east. Magistrates stood. The High Inquisitor paused, mid-sentence, with the mild irritation of a man interrupted at his accounts.

And in the holding cage, Maren of the Fenmarch lifted her head, because she had spent forty years listening for exactly this sound: trouble, arriving punctually.

The cage lock was old. Sera had noted it in her file — *mechanism pre-dates current standard, recommend replacement* — and recommendation had become salvation. Her key turned with a grinding reluctance, and the door swung, and nine people looked at her out of the dark.

"Inquisitor," said the nearest, a goat-finder with a broken nose. "Is this a trick?"

"It's an escape," Sera said. "Move."

They poured out through the gap she made, and she steered them west, toward the service yard, away from the soldiers who were even now turning back from the false fire with the slow confusion of men whose morning had stopped making sense. Someone shouted her title. Someone shouted *the Vance woman, the cage —* and she kept walking, gloves white, face blank, the picture of an inquisitor escorting prisoners, until the picture was no longer needed and the yard swallowed them all.

Maren was last out. She stopped in front of Sera, close enough that Sera could see the burns on her wrists from the irons, and studied her the way she had once studied a failing poultice.

"You're the one," Maren said. "The friend."

"I was."

"And now?"

"Now I'm burning my warrant," Sera said, "as soon as I can find a fire worth the paper."

Maren smiled — a quick, fierce, fen-country smile — and then her eyes went past Sera's shoulder and went wide.

"Bram," she said. "Behind you."

* * *

Ulyana saw it all from the arcade, and afterward could never make the pieces sit in order; memory broke the moment like a dropped plate.

The smoke-pot. The scream. The crowd breaking like a wave against the cordon. The child on the eastern scaffolding standing up, arms out, and the entire iron cage — the *empty* cage, thank every merciful power, empty — tearing loose from its moorings and lifting, groaning, twenty feet into the air before it crashed down across the square like a fallen cathedral, sealing the eastern exit in a wall of twisted iron.

The High Inquisitor rising, papers scattering, his mild voice cracking into its first real note: *"Seal the square!"*

Odile moving through the panicked crowd with terrifying calm, gathering her people like a shepherd in a storm. Sera emerging from the smoke with nine stumbling figures and her silver sunburst torn from her throat. Bram at the great wagon, hauling the pin, the oilcloth sliding away —

—and Ember-of-Endings raising her head.

She was smaller than the archives had promised. Starved, chain-scarred, her scales gone dull as old coins, her wings pinned to the wagon bed with iron bolts the size of fence posts. But when she lifted her head and opened her eyes, the whole square saw them: furnace-gold, guttering, *dying*, and more terrible for it. A candle-flame in the shape of a god.

The crowd did not run. That was the thing Ulyana would remember longest. The crowd went silent, thousands of people forgetting at once how to breathe, because no living soul in that square had ever seen a dragon, and some inheritance older than the crown woke in their spines.

Ember-of-Endings looked at the pyres. At the stakes. At the scorch-marks on the stones that predated every building here. Then she looked at Bram, and her voice, when it came, needed no magic to carry.

*"LITTLE KEEPER,"* she said, in a voice like a landslide learning tenderness, *"THEY HAVE BUILT THEIR FIRE ON MINE."*

"I know, old mother," Bram said, and put both hands on the first bolt. "Hold still."

Soldiers were rushing the yard now, pikes leveled, officers screaming orders over the din. Ulyana was moving before any decision was made — through the crowd, over the barrier, into the open, hood thrown back, because there was no more hiding, not today, not with Maren standing in the open and the dragon chained and the child on the scaffolding swaying with the effort of holding a hundred tons of iron aloft.

A soldier barred Ulyana's path. Ulyana did not stop, did not think, simply *pushed* — the small stubborn gift flaring for the first time in years, not a weapon but a shove, a hand of warm air that sent the man sprawling — and kept running.

"Ulyana!" Maren's voice, ragged with fury and fear and something underneath both that might have been pride. "Get to the wagon! The wing-bolts — there's a release —"

"I know! I read the engineer's plans!"

"Of course you did," Maren snarled, and turned to meet two soldiers with nothing but her chains and forty years of hedge-craft, buying seconds.

Ulyana reached the wagon. Bram was on the second bolt, blood on his knuckles, the metal shrieking. The first bolt came free in his hands and half the square flinched as one of the dragon's wings snapped open, torn and magnificent, blotting out the sun.

"One more," Bram grunted. "One more and she flies, or limps, or falls — but she *rises*—"

Across the square, on the dais, the High Inquisitor had recovered his voice and his guards, and was pointing — not at the dragon, not at the fleeing prisoners, but at the pyres.

"Light them," he said. "Light them *now*. Burn the witch. Burn them all — let the city see what comes of sheltering—"

The torches went down to the pitch.

And Ulyana, wrenching at the final bolt with bleeding hands, understood with perfect clarity that they were out of time — that the fire would take Maren before the bolt came free, that everything — the covenant, the guild, the child, the whole long secret history of the hidden and the hunted — had come down to this single screaming instant between iron and flame.

The bolt gave way.

Ember-of-Endings rose.

* * *

The last thing Sera Vance saw before the world became wings and firelight was Ulyana turning from the freed dragon and running — not away from the pyres, but toward them, toward the third pyre, the one already smoking, where Maren stood chained to the stake with her chin up and her eyes open.

Toward the fire.

"No," Sera said, and ran too.
