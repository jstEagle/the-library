## 19. Dragon-Fire and Crown-Iron

The pyre court smelled of pitch and lilies.

That was the cruelty of it, Ulyana thought as they crouched in the shadow of the scaffold's undercroft, pressed between Sera and the shivering child, Pip, whose gift had broken loose three days ago and whose hand they had not let go since. The crown had scattered white flowers across the execution ground—*for purity*, the heralds said—and beneath them lay a floor of fresh-cut timber, stacked like cordwood, soaked in resin so that the whole courtyard gleamed faintly gold in the morning light. It looked almost festive. It was meant to. The crown wanted spectacle, wanted the city to come out with their children on their shoulders and watch magic die in the clean bright way that made fear feel like virtue.

Above them, the sentencing platform stood draped in royal blue. And beside it, chained to an iron cradle built for something far larger than a woman, lay Ember-of-Endings.

Ulyana's breath caught every time they looked at her. They had known the dragon only as Bram knew her—a vast stillness in the dark beneath the guildhall, a warmth like a banked hearth, eyes half-lidded with centuries. Here, dragged into daylight by forty oxen and a hundred chains, she looked *small*. Her scales, which should have burned copper and rose-gold, were dull as old pennies. Her wings lay folded tight against her flanks like a bird's broken umbrella. The iron cradle groaned whenever she breathed, and each breath came slower than the last.

They had starved her of everything but air for six days. Not food—dragons could go without—but flame. Every spark she coughed up, soldiers doused with sand. A fire that cannot burn begins to eat itself. Bram had explained it once, in the quiet under the guildhall: *the flame is not what she makes, girl. It is what she is. Take it from her and you take the shape, and leave only the weight.*

Bram was somewhere above them now. That was the plan's terrible hinge—the one part Odile had hated, the one part Ulyana had argued against until their voice gave out. Someone had to reach the dragon before the pyres were lit, someone the guards would not think to stop, because he was supposed to be there. Bram had been the guild's dragon-keeper for fifty years; his name was on the court's own supply ledgers, tending the captive's feed and water. He would walk in through the front gate with a cart of charcoal and a keeper's yoke, and no one would look twice at an old man doing his lawful work.

And when the moment came, he would cut the first chain.

"Say it again," Pip whispered. The child's fingers were ice in Ulyana's grip. "Say the part where we live."

"We get Maren," Ulyana murmured, lips barely moving. "We get everyone on that list. Sera opens the pens. Odile's people take them down the drain-tunnel to the river barges. We do not fight unless we must."

"And the dragon?"

Ulyana looked up at the dull copper bulk of her, at the slow rise and fall of her ruined sides. "The dragon does what dragons do."

On the platform, a herald in white raised a scroll. The crowd hushed—not from reverence, Ulyana thought, but from the particular silence of people who have come to enjoy something and are ashamed of it.

* * *

Sera stood at the base of the platform in her formal whites, gloves immaculate, face carved from duty. She had not slept. Ulyana could see it even from thirty feet away, in the gray beneath her eyes, in the way her left hand kept finding the scroll tube at her belt—the warrant, the list of names, the paper with Maren's name third from the top.

Their eyes met once across the flower-strewn ground. Just once. Sera's gaze flicked to the drain-grate behind the woodpile, then away, and she became again the Inquisitor Vance the crowd expected: spine straight, chin high, the crown's clean knife.

Ulyana's heart was a fist in their chest. Six days ago, in the dark beneath the city, Sera Vance had put down her sword, sat on a wet stone floor in her fine white coat, and listened while Ulyana read her the covenant aloud—the guild's four-hundred-year bargain, the fire full of bound gifts, the countdown ticking toward an unbinding that would crack the city open like an egg. She had listened, and she had wept once, briefly, furiously, wiping her face with those white gloves as if grief were dirt, and then she had said: *Then we are out of time, ledger-keeper.*

Now the herald unrolled his scroll and began the names.

"Hedgewitch Maren Solane, of Thornbury. Found harboring the unregistered gifted. Sentenced to cleansing by fire."

The crowd made its sound—that low animal murmur—and Ulyana's whole body lurched toward the platform before discipline caught them. There. At the edge of the pen beside the scaffold, wrists bound, gray hair loose: Maren. Older than Ulyana remembered, thinner, a bruise yellowing along her jaw. But her back was straight, and when the guards prodded her forward she did not stumble, and she lifted her chin at the crowd with the same stubborn grace she had once used to scold Ulyana over a mispronounced cantrip twenty years ago.

*I left her cottage in the night,* Ulyana thought. *I ran, and I never wrote, and I let her believe I was dead or worse.* And now here she was, about to burn for the same gift Ulyana had hidden like a stolen coin.

"Not yet," Sera breathed. She was beside them now, having drifted along the scaffold's shadow as if inspecting preparations. Her voice was barely air. "Wait for Bram. If we move before the chains are off, we all die on this gravel."

"And if the chains don't come off?"

Sera didn't answer. Below the platform, at the far gate, a cart rattled through—charcoal sacks, a bent old man in a keeper's smock, moving with the unhurried shuffle of a man who had done this errand a hundred times. No one looked at him. Ulyana's chest ached with how completely no one looked at him.

*It's starting again,* Bram had said, the day of the proclamation, and Ulyana understood now that he had meant more than the hunt. He had meant this. He had lived the last war's ending, and he had always known how it would try to begin again.

The herald read on. A lamplighter who had lit his lamps with a word. Two sisters who traded small luck at the fish market. A boy of eleven whose hands mended crockery. Name after name after name, and each one made the timber smell sweeter and the lilies whiter, and Ulyana held Pip's hand so tightly the child whimpered and did not complain.

* * *

Bram reached the dragon at the reading of the fourth name.

He set his cart beside the iron cradle, unhitched the yoke, and began, visibly, lawfully, to shovel charcoal into the feeding trough bolted to the cage's flank. The guards at the cradle glanced at him and looked away. Fifty years of being invisible had been his armor, and he wore it well.

But Ulyana, watching from the shadows, saw what the guards did not: that Bram was not shoveling charcoal *into* the trough. He was packing it *under* the cradle's iron feet, around the anchor bolts, in a wide black ring, and mixed through the charcoal—Ulyana's stomach dropped as they recognized it—were flakes of dried scale-shedding, the ones he had collected from Ember-of-Endings for decades. Dragon-scale kindling. Fire that remembers how to be fire.

"Oh," Ulyana whispered. "Oh, you clever, terrible old man."

"What?" Sera hissed.

"He's not cutting the chains." Ulyana's mouth had gone dry. "He's going to give her back her flame. All at once. Right under the cage."

"That will kill the guards. It will kill *him*—"

"It might kill half the courtyard." Ulyana's mind raced through the arithmetic of it, the ledger-keeper's cold sums: forty guards, hundreds of civilians, the scaffold, the pens, the drain-tunnel exit barely sixty feet from the cradle. "We have to move *now*. Before he lights it. Get to the pens. Get Maren. Go."

Sera went. For one heartbeat she hesitated—Ulyana saw the war cross her face, duty and love tearing the seam between them—and then she pulled off her white gloves, stuffed them in her belt, and walked toward the holding pens with the stride of a woman going to inspect prisoners, and no one stopped the Inquisitor either.

Ulyana took Pip's hand and ran for the woodpile.

* * *

Afterward, Ulyana would remember the next three minutes in fragments, the way a storm is remembered: not as a sequence but as a series of blows.

The herald reading the ninth name. Maren being marched to the first pyre, ropes looped to the stake. Sera at the pen gate, showing her warrant to a guard, then her knife instead. Ulyana hauling at the drain-grate with Pip pulling the rusted pin free, the child's small gift flickering uselessly—levitation, only levitation, useless here—and Ulyana's own gift rising in their chest like water climbing a well, begging to be spent, *no, not yet, not until—*

And then Bram struck his flint.

He did not strike it at the charcoal ring. Ulyana realized that too late; they all did. He climbed onto the cart, stood upright in the open beside the dragon's great dull head, and struck the flint against the iron of her muzzle-cage—one spark, two, three—and spoke to her in a language Ulyana had heard only once, in the deeps beneath the guildhall, a language like stones rolling downhill in a river of embers.

*Old one. Ending-of-endings. Keeper-of-my-keeping. Take it back.*

Ember-of-Endings opened her eyes.

They had been closed—or nearly—for six days, and before that dimming for years, and Ulyana had believed, had been *told*, that nothing of the ancient fire remained. But when her eyes opened they were furnaces. Not the banked coals Ulyana knew. Furnaces, gold-shot and furious, and the sound she made was not a roar but a word, one word in that rolling stone-language, and the charcoal ring beneath the cradle caught all at once, everywhere, blue-white, and the world turned inside out.

The blast took the cradle's legs off like a carpenter's saw. Iron shrieked. Guards flew. The shockwave flattened the nearest pyre and threw Ulyana flat against the woodpile with Pip crushed safely beneath their body, ears ringing, vision white. Through the ringing, through the smoke, Ulyana heard the crowd screaming, heard the herald's scroll fluttering away like a wounded bird, heard—

Heard Bram laughing.

He was still on the cart. The fire had taken his smock, his sleeves, his hair, and he stood in the heart of it with his arms spread wide, an old man embracing an old friend, and he was *laughing*, deep and delighted, the laugh he'd had in the guild kitchen over bad wine, and Ember-of-Endings rose—rose—her ruined wings snapping open like banners, chains ripping free of the collapsing cradle in a rain of molten links, and she curved her long neck down and touched her forehead to his.

Ulyana understood, in the way one understands things in the space between lightning and thunder, that this was goodbye. That Bram had packed the charcoal knowing exactly how much fire a dying dragon needed and exactly how much a man could stand beside. That he had chosen this the way he had chosen the guild, quietly, completely, without asking anyone's permission.

His lips moved. Smoke poured from them. Even across the burning courtyard, Ulyana heard it, or dreamed they heard it:

*Told you, girl. Some debts you pay in storms.*

Then the dragon's wing swept down and covered him, and when it rose again there was no cart, no fire-ring, no old man—only a drift of ash turning slowly in the heat, catching the light like snow falling upward.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, because it had to.

Ember-of-Endings rose to the height of the scaffold and hung there, wings beating in slow, laborious strokes—she was weak, terribly weak, each downbeat a struggle, and Ulyana's heart broke watching the mightiest creature of the age fight gravity like a fledgling—but weak was not harmless. Her shadow passed over the guards and they broke. Of course they broke. Men who had trained their whole lives to fight other men do not stand before a dragon; it is not cowardice, it is sanity. They scattered, dropping pikes, trampling the white lilies into the mud.

Sera came out of the pen smoke with Maren on her arm and six freed prisoners stumbling behind her, driving them toward the drain-grate where Ulyana knelt waving them in. Maren's eyes found Ulyana's over Sera's shoulder, and whatever passed between them in that glance—twenty years of silence, of guilt, of the healing gone wrong and the village and the running—it passed in a single heartbeat, and Maren gripped Ulyana's face in both smoke-stained hands and said, "Later. Grieve later. *Move.*"

A horn sounded from the citadel road. Reinforcements. Crossbows. Ulyana shoved the last prisoner down into the tunnel and turned to shout for Sera and saw her standing alone in the open courtyard, facing the dragon, holding up a scroll.

"No," Ulyana breathed. "*No, Sera, run—*"

But Ember-of-Endings had already seen her. The dragon's head swung down, furnace-eyes narrowing, nostrils flaring—and Ulyana saw it happen, saw the exact moment the dragon recognized what Sera carried. The warrant. Crown-iron in paper form, the list of names, the machine of the hunt distilled to a single document. Smoke curled from the dragon's jaws. Her throat lit from within, gold rising up her neck like dawn coming up a valley.

Sera did not run.

She unrolled the warrant, held it high so the dragon—and the fleeing crowd, and the arriving soldiers, and everyone—could see it. And then Inquisitor Sera Vance, the crown's clean knife, the woman who had hunted Ulyana across half a city, drew her knife and slashed her own palm and pressed her bleeding hand to the parchment and said, in a voice that carried to the citadel gates:

"I, Sera Vance, Inquisitor of the Pyre Court, refuse this charge. Let the record show it. Let the *crown* show it." And she struck flint to the warrant's corner, and it caught, and she held it as it burned, held it until the flames licked her fingers, held it while the dragon's gathered fire died unspent in her throat, and through the smoke and the pain Sera looked up into the furnace-eyes and said, quieter, only for her: "I lost one already. I'm done losing more."

Ember-of-Endings made a sound then that Ulyana had no name for—half roar, half something older and sadder—and dipped her head, and with a gentleness impossible in something so vast, she nudged Sera backward, toward the grate, toward the tunnel, toward the living.

"GO!" Ulyana screamed, and Sera ran, and Ulyana hauled her down into the dark as the first crossbow bolts from the citadel road came hissing into the courtyard and skittered off scales like rain off a roof.

* * *

The tunnel swallowed them—Maren, Sera, six freed strangers, Pip—and behind them the pyre court burned, and above it, laboring, magnificent, *awake*, Ember-of-Endings clawed her way into the sky.

She did not fly well. She flew the way a starving man walks: stubbornly, swaying, each wingbeat bought with pain. But she flew. She circled the smoking court once, twice, a slow defiant spiral, and her cry rolled over the city—over the market quarter and the Vesper quarter and the crown's white towers—and every soul in Vesperhal heard it and knew, in their bones, in the oldest part of them, what it meant.

*A dragon flies over the city. The war has not ended. It has only been waiting.*

In the tunnel's dripping dark, lit by the glow of the burning court behind them, Maren took Ulyana's hand and squeezed it, and Sera wrung out her burned fingers and would not meet anyone's eyes, and Pip asked in a small voice, "Where's the old man? The one with the cart?"

Ulyana opened their mouth and found nothing in it. No words. No ledger entry neat enough for this. Only ash, drifting upward, catching the light.

Ahead, deeper in the undercity, the tunnel forked—one way to the river and the barges and Odile's waiting arms, one way down, down toward the sealed places where the covenant pages said the fire's root still lived. Ulyana felt it before they understood it: a pull in the chest, in the gift itself, their small stubborn flicker straining toward the deep like a moth toward a window. Toward the last flame. Toward the place where the unbinding waited, patient as arithmetic, counting down.

The dragon was free, but the fire was still dying. And somewhere in the dark below, Ulyana felt the truth settle over them like ash: freedom had cost Bram everything, and it had bought them only time.

Not enough time. Never enough.

"The barges," Sera said hoarsely, pointing the wrong way, toward the river.

Ulyana looked down the other passage, into the breathing dark, and their gift pulsed once, twice, like a second heartbeat answering a call.

"No," Ulyana said. "Not yet. There's one more debt to pay."
