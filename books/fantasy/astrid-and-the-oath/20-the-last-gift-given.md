## 20. The Last Gift Given

The dragon's flame stood no taller than a candle.

That was the first thing Ulyana understood, kneeling in the wreckage of the pyre court with Ember-of-Endings breathing in shallow, rattling pulls beside them: all of this—the centuries of sheltering, the bound gifts humming in every impossible thing the guild had ever sold, the covenant written in a hand older than the crown—rested on a flame that would not outlast the hour.

Around them, the city was coming apart.

It began gently, the way disasters sometimes do. A shutter unlatched itself three streets away. A weathervane turned against the wind. Then a fishmonger's cart rose six feet into the air while its owner screamed and clung to the wheel, and somewhere a bell tower rang without a hand upon it, and then it was not gentle at all. Every gift bound into the dying fire was straining at its tether now, snapping loose one by one or thrashing against bonds that thinned like old rope. Ulyana could feel them through the covenant pages clutched against their chest—not names anymore, not people, just pressures, hungers, griefs let out of cages too small for what had been put in them.

"They're waking," Bram said. He was on his knees in the ash of the pyre court, one hand pressed to Ember-of-Endings' scaled flank, and there was blood on his face from where the crown-iron chains had torn him when they fell. He did not seem to notice. "All of them. It's like a dam going, Ulyana. You can hear the cracks."

Ulyana could. Underneath the screams of the square, underneath the groan of timber lifting and iron bending, there was a sound like a harp with every string failing at once—and woven through it, faint as breath, the voices of the gifted whose power the fire had held for lifetimes. Some were dead. Their gifts remained, orphaned things still bound by an oath their makers had never fully understood.

"I have to do it now," Ulyana said.

* * *

There had been no time to argue, though Sera had tried.

When the dragon rose—if rising could be called what Ember-of-Endings had done, hauling herself up on legs that trembled like an old woman's, wings unfurling in tatters—the crowd in the pyre court had fallen back in a great ragged wave. Soldiers dropped pikes. The pyres meant for the condemned stood unlit and forgotten. Maren was out of her cage, soot-streaked and alive, gripping Ulyana's arm hard enough to bruise, saying their name over and over as if to prove to herself they still had one.

And Sera Vance stood in the center of it all with her white gloves in her hand and her warrant burning to black flakes between her fingers, looking at Ulyana across twenty years and one broken sky.

"You said the covenant could be rewritten," she had said. Her voice carried strangely in the hush. "You said it needed a willing gift."

"Yes."

"Whose?"

And Ulyana had already known, and Sera had already known they knew, and Maren's grip had gone from bruising to desperate.

They argued anyway, because that was what family did. Odile, arriving breathless from the undercity with the freed guild members behind her, planted herself in Ulyana's path like a door barred against a storm. "No," she said, flat and final. "I've buried enough of my people. I will not stand here and let the guild eat its youngest."

"It's not eating me." Ulyana heard their own voice come out steady and was vaguely surprised. "Odile. Listen to what I'm saying. The fire doesn't want another prisoner. That's why it's dying. That's why the binding is breaking—it was never meant to be a cage, it was meant to be a *hearth*, and we made it a prison, and prisons exhaust even flames. If I give it my gift freely—not surrendered, *given*—it changes the terms. It has to. That's how covenants work. They remember what they're fed."

"And what happens to you?" Maren's voice cracked down the middle of the question. Her hands framed Ulyana's face, and Ulyana saw in them the cottage in the fen country, the smell of herbs, the healing gone wrong, everything they had run from and everything they had run *to*. "What's left of you after?"

"I don't know." The honest answer, given aloud at last. "But I know what happens if I don't. Every bound gift breaks loose in a city of forty thousand people. Some of those gifts are storms. Some are fires. And the dragons—" They looked up at Ember-of-Endings, whose ancient eye watched them with something that was not hope yet but might become it. "If the fire dies unbound, the old powers wake with no covenant over them and no one leading them. We read what happened last time, Maren. The sky broke. I won't watch it break twice."

Sera spoke quietly, to Ulyana alone. "Is there any other way?"

"No."

"Then I'll be standing next to you when you do it." She pulled the last scrap of her warrant from her belt and let the wind take it. "Consider it witnessed."

* * *

Now the arguing was done, and there was only the walking.

Ulyana crossed the pyre court toward the dragon one step at a time, and the world came with them whether it wanted to or not. Above, the clouds had begun to turn wrong—a greenish bruise-color spreading from the north, where the old roosts had burned two hundred years ago, where powers with no masters were stirring in their long graves. Below, in the press of the crowd, Ulyana saw faces they knew: neighbors from the Vesper quarter, clients who had bought borrowed years and bottled thunder, the fishmonger whose cart still hovered, forgotten, over the rooftops. All of them watching. All of them about to learn what had been kept from them for three centuries.

Ember-of-Endings lowered her head as Ulyana approached. This close, the dragon smelled of woodsmoke and old libraries and the very end of winter. Her flame guttered in a hollow beneath her breastbone, visible through scales worn thin as parchment—a coin of gold light shrinking as Ulyana watched.

"Little ledger-keeper," the dragon said. Her voice was dry leaves and banked coals. "Bram has told you what I am. Do you understand what you offer?"

"Not entirely." Ulyana knelt in front of her. "But I understand ledgers. Debts and credits. What's owed and what's held. For three hundred years this fire has held debts that weren't its own—gifts taken in fear, bound in desperation, kept like stolen goods. No wonder it's dying. Fire wasn't made to hoard. It was made to *share*." They spread their hands. "So take mine. Not as a deposit in your vault. As fuel in your hearth. Let it warm instead of bind. Let everyone the flame holds feel their own gift steadied instead of caged. Rewrite the terms, and I write them with you—willingly, freely, permanently. That's the offer."

The dragon was silent a long moment. Around the court, the unbinding worsened—a section of rooftop lifted, screaming hinges; a woman in the crowd began to glow faintly at the fingertips, some inherited gift shaking loose down her family line. Time was running like sand through fingers.

"Why?" Ember-of-Endings asked. "You could run. You could live small and hidden, as your guild has lived for centuries. Why give away the thing you hid so well?"

Because I spent ten years pretending I was nothing, Ulyana thought. Because belonging bought with secrecy isn't belonging; it's just hiding in company. Because I balanced the ledgers of impossible things for six years and every entry was someone's life filed under *concealed*.

"Because a gift hidden is a debt unpaid," they said aloud. "I learned that from your covenant. I'm just the first one willing to settle up."

Something moved in the dragon's ancient eye—amusement, or approval, or simply the reflection of the guttering flame. "Then come, child of the Vesper Guild. Bring your small stubborn light. Let us see what it becomes when it stops being afraid."

* * *

Ulyana had expected the giving to hurt.

It did not. That frightened them more than pain would have.

They pressed both palms to the dragon's breastbone, to the scales thin as memory, and reached inward to the place where their gift had lived since childhood—that flicker, that stubborn candle, the warmth that had mended a dying sparrow when they were seven and terrified a village when they were seventeen. They had spent half a life smothering it. Now they opened their hands inside themselves and said, in the wordless grammar of the gifted, *take all of it, freely given*, and felt it rise.

It left like a breath leaves the body. Not torn—released. For one heartbeat Ulyana felt it whole, unhidden, *seen*, larger than they had ever dared let it be, a hearth-flame in miniature that could have warmed a room, a street, a city if it had ever been allowed. Six years of balancing ledgers in the dark. Ten years before that of flinching from their own hands. All of it folded into one act of surrender—

—and then the fire took it, and did not erase it.

That was the miracle Ulyana would spend the rest of their life trying to describe. The gift did not vanish. It *spread*. It went into the dying coin of flame beneath the dragon's breastbone the way dawn goes into a frozen morning—not adding heat so much as changing what the cold meant. The flame caught, steadied, rose—gold, then deeper gold, then a color Ulyana had no name for, warm as bread and old wood and the lamp Odile left burning in the guildhall window. And through the fire, out into the trembling city, it moved like a tide.

Ulyana felt every bond it touched.

The levitating cart settled gently to the cobbles, its driver weeping with relief. The glowing woman in the crowd gasped as whatever shook loose in her blood suddenly *settled*—not suppressed, but held, the way a friend holds a trembling shoulder. In attics and cellars across the city, in the guild's own hidden vaults, every impossible thing the Vesper Guild had ever made hummed once and grew calm. Bottled storms turned softly in their glass like sleeping birds. Borrowed years stopped straining against their hourglasses. And the orphaned gifts of the dead, the ones with no living keeper, wrapped themselves around the new hearth-flame like children around a fire, quieted, and stayed.

The green bruise on the northern clouds thinned. Broke. Let through one slant of ordinary evening sun.

"It works," Bram whispered, and then he was laughing and crying at once, an old man undone in the ash. "Three hundred years. Three hundred years it wanted this. It just needed someone to *ask*."

* * *

"The covenant is broken," Ulyana said.

Their voice came out strange to them—lighter, emptier, the voice of someone setting down a weight carried so long the shoulders had grown around it. They stood, and found their legs held, and turned to face the crowd. Sera moved to stand at their right side. Maren took their left. Behind them, Ember-of-Endings raised her head, and the new flame shone through her breastbone like a lantern through horn, and the whole pyre court lit gold.

"The covenant made between the first guildmasters and the last dragons is broken," Ulyana said again, louder, because the crowd had gone silent and silence was a thing that carried words far. "Broken by consent of the fire, which held it. And a new one is made in its place, spoken aloud so that no one—crown, guild, dragon, or child—can say they did not know."

They had thought, in the sleepless hours before the raid, about what words to use. Now they came without searching, plain as an inventory, honest as a ledger.

"Hear the terms. The fire of Ember-of-Endings shall hold no gift against its keeper's will. No gift shall be bound in secrecy, taken in fear, or stored like stolen goods. Any gifted person may bring their gift to the hearth-flame, and the flame will steady it—not cage it, not consume it, but hold it as a harbor holds a boat: freely boarded, freely left. The Vesper Guild keeps this flame open to all who come in peace. The crown—" Ulyana glanced at Sera, and Sera nodded, jaw set, and Ulyana went on, "—the crown shall send no hunters after those the hearth shelters, and the guild shall hide no one from justice rightly earned, but shelter every soul from fire wrongly lit. Witnessed by dragon, by crown, by crowd. Written nowhere, so it cannot burn. Carried in every person who hears it now. Say it back if you agree, so the fire knows it was heard."

For a moment, nothing.

Then a woman near the front—the glowing one, the one whose inherited gift had nearly shaken itself apart—said in a clear shaking voice, "Witnessed," and put her hand over her heart. And a soldier lowered his pike and said it. And a baker, and a dockhand, and the fishmonger climbing down off his rescued cart, and Odile in her ruined guildmaster's coat, and Bram on his knees in the ash with his arms around the dragon's foreleg, and hundreds of voices taking it up until the words rolled off the pyre court walls and out into the streets, into the Vesper quarter, into the undercity where the frightened and the hunted were listening:

"Witnessed. Witnessed. Witnessed."

On the far side of the square, a delegation of the crown's officials stood pale and still—magistrates, a herald, a captain of the guard whose warrant book hung open and forgotten in his hand. They had come to oversee burnings. Instead they stood in dragon-light, hearing a city make law out loud.

Ember-of-Endings dipped her great head until her eye was level with Ulyana, and the fire behind her scales pulsed once, slow and warm, like a heartbeat finally resting.

"Well traded, little ledger-keeper," she said. "Your gift was never small. It was only waiting for a big enough room."

Ulyana reached inside themselves, out of habit, to find the flicker that had always been there.

It was gone. The space where it had lived was quiet—an emptiness, but not a wound. More like a hearth swept clean after the fire is laid elsewhere, ready for whatever comes next. They felt lighter than they had ever felt and poorer than they had ever been, both at once, and found to their astonishment that they were smiling.

Maren's arms came around them from behind, and Ulyana let them.

* * *

It was Sera who broke the moment, because Sera was the kind of person who understood that moments, like fires, must be tended or they die.

"The magistrates," she said low, at Ulyana's shoulder. Her white gloves were back on her hands, spotless, absurd, defiant. "They'll go back to the palace tonight and report that a dragon rose in the pyre court and the city swore an oath the crown never authorized. Some of them will want to call it treason. Some will want to call it war."

"Which will they choose?"

Sera watched the crown's delegation retreating up the processional road, the herald already running ahead with news that would reach the palace by midnight. She watched the crowd, still murmuring the covenant to itself like a psalm. She watched Ember-of-Endings settle her tattered wings around Bram like a grandmother drawing a shawl.

"Neither," she said. "Not yet. They'll choose to negotiate—because the alternative just got wings, and forty thousand witnesses, and a fire that can't be threatened. There will be terms to argue. Sanctuaries to charter. Old laws to unpick thread by thread." She exhaled. "And someone will have to sit across that table from the palace and speak for the guild. Someone the crown has already met. Someone who knows both sides of the ledger."

Ulyana looked at her. "Sera—"

"Don't thank me." But her voice was rough, and her hand, briefly, found Ulyana's sleeve and held it—the way you hold someone at a crossroads, the way you held them once at a village well twenty years ago, before the roads divided. "Get some sleep, ledger-keeper. Tomorrow we go and teach a kingdom how to keep its promises."

Above the pyre court, above the rooftops and the hovering dust and the first stars coming out shy through the clearing smoke, the sky held.
