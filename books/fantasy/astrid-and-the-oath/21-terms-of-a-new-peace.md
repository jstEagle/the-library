## 21. Terms of a New Peace

The pyre court smelled of rain for the first time in its existence.

Sera Vance stood at the edge of the scaffold square where she had presided over eleven sentencings, and watched the water pool in the scorch marks where the stakes had been. Workmen were already prying the iron rings from the stones—the crown's order had come down within hours of the dragon rising, carried by clerks whose hands shook so badly the wax seals smudged. *Dismantle the Pyre Court. Hold all prisoners. Await terms.*

Await terms. As if the crown had any left to bargain with.

Above the rooftops, past the spires of the chancellery and the smoke still drifting from the guildhall's ruined quarter, Ember-of-Endings lay coiled on the broken hillside above the city like a mountain that had decided, for now, to breathe. Weak. Ancient. Awake. Every so often the dragon's flank rose and fell with a slowness that made Sera's chest ache to watch, and every time it did, half the city paused in whatever it was doing—hauling rubble, hauling water, hauling the dead—to look.

They had never seen a dragon before. None living had. And yet they looked at it the way children look at a fire in winter: with the old, buried knowledge that warmth had once been shared, not hoarded.

"You're not going in," said Captain Ilse Varrow behind her. Not a question. Ilse had served beside her for six years, had held her cloak in the snow outside heretic trials, had never once asked what Sera kept in the locked drawer of her desk. "Chancellor Redgrave will want your head for the warrant."

"I burned the warrant."

"You burned a crown document in front of two hundred witnesses and a dragon." Ilse came to stand at her shoulder, arms folded against the drizzle. "In the histories they'll call it either treason or the bravest thing anyone did that day. Depends who writes them."

"Then I'd better write them myself." Sera turned up her collar. "Is he receiving me?"

"He's receiving everyone. He has no choice." Ilse's voice dropped. "There are three hundred registered names in the northern camps, Vance. Families. If this accord goes wrong—"

"It won't go wrong." Sera watched the dragon breathe. "It can't afford to."

* * *

The chancellery's long hall had hosted coronation feasts and declaration ceremonies, but Sera could not remember it ever hosting anything like this: a table set for enemies, because there was no longer any honest word for what sat on either side of it.

On the crown's side: Chancellor Aldous Redgrave, gray as wet ash, flanked by the Lord Advocate and two treasury men who kept glancing out the tall windows toward the hillside as if expecting the horizon to catch fire. On the other side: Guildmaster Odile Ferrow, upright and immaculate despite a healing burn along one forearm, with Bram of the Vesper Guild beside her—and Bram, Sera noted, had insisted on bringing a single sheet of parchment so old it had gone the color of amber, covered in script that predated the kingdom itself.

Ulyana Yarrow was not at the table. They stood at the back of the hall near the doors, hands folded, watching Sera with an expression she had spent three sleepless nights trying to name. In the end she had given up naming it. Some things between old friends did not need words; some things needed too many.

"Master Ferrow." Redgrave's voice was dry paper. "The crown acknowledges the—situation."

"The situation," Odile repeated pleasantly, "is a dragon."

"A dying dragon."

"A dragon," Bram said, in his gravel-and-ash voice, "that is no longer dying. You've seen it eat, Chancellor?"

Redgrave went very still. "I beg your pardon?"

"Three days ago that creature couldn't lift its head. Yesterday it flew the length of the valley." Bram laid one scarred hand flat on the ancient parchment. "You want to know why. I'll tell you, and then we can stop pretending any of us has leverage we don't have. The fire was fed. It's being fed still. Freely, and by name." His eyes crossed the hall—not to Sera, but past her, to Ulyana—and something in his weathered face gentled and broke at once. "By my people."

Silence. The treasury men had stopped looking out the windows.

"So," Redgrave said at last, carefully, "the rumors of a covenant are true."

"The covenant is older than your crown," Odile said. "Older than this city's foundations. For four centuries the Vesper Guild has sheltered the gifted of this realm—your bakers, your midwives, your soldiers' mothers—who would otherwise have been burned or driven into the wilds. We bound their gifts into the dragon-fire to hide them. That binding is over. What comes next is a matter for negotiation." She spread her hands. "We are negotiating."

"The alternative," Bram said, "was letting three hundred gifts tear loose at once when the fire failed. You'd have had your war. Nobody would have won it. We chose this instead. Remember that, when the terms get hard."

Sera watched Redgrave absorb it. He was a cold man, but not a stupid one, and stupidity had been the crown's chief weapon against itself for a generation. She saw him do the arithmetic: a risen dragon, a united guild, a populace that had watched a girl levitate a cart in the market and a woman walk unburned out of a pyre-court cell, and three hundred families in northern camps whose return the realm would have to account for sooner or later, in blood or in ink.

Ink, Redgrave decided. She saw him decide it.

"Terms," he said. "You have the floor, Master Ferrow."

Odile slid a document across the table. Sera had seen it drafted through two nights and three arguments in the undercity, Ulyana's handwriting steady even when their eyes weren't. She knew every clause by heart.

"First," Odile read, "the register is dissolved. Every name entered in it becomes a name under the guild's protection, and the crown's obligation shifts from surveillance to sanctuary. Second, the pyre court is closed permanently, its records surrendered, its sentences voided. Third—" and here Odile's voice took on an edge, "—the northern camps open within the week, and every soul in them comes home."

"And in exchange," Redgrave said, "the guild surrenders its secrets."

"The guild surrenders nothing," Odile said. "The guild *opens*. Our ledgers become public record. Our goods, our trades, our practices—inspected, taxed, lawful. We step out of the shadow you drove us into, Chancellor, and we do it in daylight where everyone can see exactly what we are." She smiled without warmth. "You wanted magic registered. Congratulations. Now it registers itself, daily, in a book any citizen may read."

The Lord Advocate leaned in to murmur. Redgrave listened, jaw tight. Then his gaze moved down the hall and settled on Sera, and she felt the weight of it like a hand on her shoulder.

"Inquisitor Vance," he said. "Your office built the register. Your office knows better than anyone in this room how it fails. The guild proposes that oversight of the new accord pass to—an officer of the crown acceptable to both parties." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "Would you accept such an appointment?"

Every head turned. Sera thought of the locked drawer in her desk, and the ribbon inside it, faded rose-colored, that she had carried for nineteen years. She thought of Wren Ashdale, who could coax honeybees into shapes, who had registered on a Tuesday and been taken on a Thursday, whose name was scratched out of the parish rolls so thoroughly it was as if the ground had opened. She thought of a girl in a hayfield teaching her to whistle through cupped hands, before either of them knew what the gift in that girl's blood would cost.

She thought: *they gave you a way to make it mean something, at last, and you will take it or you do not deserve to have loved anyone.*

"I accept," Sera said.

* * *

They argued until the candles guttered.

Not about the great clauses—those held—but about the small ones, which Sera knew from long experience were where accords lived or died. Who adjudicated disputes between guild traders and crown magistrates. Whether guild-trained healers owed service to army hospitals. What happened, in precise legal language, if a child manifested a gift at seven years old in a village that had burned a hedge-witch twenty years ago.

That last one she wrote herself, sitting at the hall's side table while the principals rested, while Bram dozed upright in his chair like an old soldier and Odile spoke in low tones with Redgrave about reparations for the raid. She wrote it slowly, crossing out and rewriting, because the wording mattered more than anything else she would draft in her life:

*A child found gifted shall be brought neither to fear nor to flame, but to the hearth, where their gift shall be known, named, and taught; and no officer of the crown shall take them thence.*

When she finished, she found Ulyana standing beside her, reading over her shoulder. Up close they looked hollowed—there was a stillness where their gift used to live, Sera realized, a quietness in them like a room after the fire's gone out, and yet somehow they stood straighter than she had ever seen them.

"It's good," Ulyana said quietly. "Better than good. 'Known, named, and taught.'" They glanced at her. "Wren would have liked that."

Sera's pen stopped.

"How—" she began, and stopped again. Nineteen years, and she had never spoken the name aloud in this city. "How did you know that name?"

"Because Maren knew them." Ulyana pulled out the chair opposite and sat, elbows on the table, and looked at her with the same steady dark eyes she remembered from a market fair when they were both nine and the world was only horses and fried dough. "Maren sheltered Wren's mother, after. Wren had a cousin. Hedge-folk remember, Sera. They remember everything, because nobody else does." A pause. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I didn't know until after the raid, and I've been waiting for the right moment, and there isn't one. There never is."

Sera looked down at the clause in her own handwriting. *Neither to fear nor to flame.* Her eyes stung; she let them, here in the emptying hall, where only old Bram saw and pretended not to.

"She had ribbons in her hair," Sera said. "Rose-colored. She gave me one the day before the criers came. I've kept it in a drawer for nineteen years like it was evidence." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "I hunted your people for the crown, Ulyana. Eleven sentencings. I told myself I was keeping the hunt *orderly*, that orderly meant fewer burnings, that a careful inquisitor was a mercy. Do you know how many nights I've needed that arithmetic to sleep?"

"I know exactly," Ulyana said. "I balanced ledgers for a guild that hid people by hiding them. Same sums. Different columns." They reached across the table—not taking her hand, just resting theirs near hers, an offering rather than a claim. "The accord isn't forgiveness, Sera. Forgiveness takes longer, and it isn't mine alone to give. But it's a start, and you're the right person to hold it. Because you know precisely what happens when the terms fail. You've spent your whole career learning it."

"Then help me make sure they don't." Sera turned the drafted page toward them. "The guild gets a seat in the oversight. Not a veto—a seat. Odile's too visible now, and Bram won't leave the dragon. I need someone who knows the ledgers, the old covenant law, the wards. Someone the gifted will trust and the crown will grudgingly respect."

Ulyana stared at her. "You're offering me a crown appointment."

"I'm offering you a desk across from mine," Sera said, "and the worst paperwork in the kingdom, and every morning the chance to be the person neither of us had." She allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "The pay is insulting."

For the first time since the raid, since the fire, since the terrible bright moment when Ulyana had walked into the dragon-flame and come out changed, Ulyana Yarrow laughed. It was short and rough and it ended quickly, but it was real, and Sera held onto the sound of it the way she had once held onto a rose-colored ribbon: proof that something survived.

* * *

Seven days later, the first wagons came down from the north.

Sera stood at the city gate to receive them, in her new gray-and-white livery—the pyre-black formally retired, burned in the square by her own hand before witnesses, which Ilse said would either settle the question of her loyalty or end her career, and Sera had replied that those were now the same thing. Beside her at the gate stood Odile; behind them, a delegation of guild members carrying bread, and hot cider, and blankets, and no weapons at all, which was its own kind of statement.

The wagons came slow through the drizzle. Three hundred souls had gone north; two hundred and seventy-one came back. Sera had the missing twenty-nine names already, copied into her own hand, each one a case file she would work until the graves were found or the people were, whichever truth waited. Imperfect justice. She was learning that all justice was, and that the answer to imperfection was not despair but persistence.

At the gate arch, a family climbed down from the second wagon—a mother with a boy of perhaps eight clutching a carved wooden bird. The boy froze at the sight of Sera's uniform, and Sera watched his mother's arm come around him, watched her body become a wall between her child and the crown.

Sera knelt. Slowly, hands open, the way you kneel to a frightened animal—or a dragon.

"What's your name?" she asked the boy.

He looked at his mother. His mother looked at Odile, and Odile nodded, once.

"Tomas," the boy whispered.

"Well met, Tomas." Sera reached into her coat and produced, of all things, the ribbon—she had moved it from the drawer to her breast pocket the day she signed the accord, and could not entirely explain why, except that evidence belonged with its case. She tied it, careful and clumsy, around the wooden bird's neck. "There's a place in the Vesper quarter with a fire that doesn't go out. Anyone can sit at it. You'll like it there. Tell them Sera sent you, and that you're expected."

The mother's eyes were wet and furious and grateful all at once, and Sera rose, knees aching, and thought: *this. Only this. Everything else was prologue.*

Behind her, up on the hillside, Ember-of-Endings lifted her head against the gray sky and breathed out—one long ribbon of gold-red flame that rose and curled and hung above the city like a banner, and every soul at the gate, crown and guild alike, turned to watch it fall as warm rain.

Sera unrolled the accord copy under her arm, checked the date against the ledger page Ulyana had drafted that morning, and found, appended beneath the final seal in her former enemy's neat clerk's hand, a single line she had not authorized and would never strike out:

*Entry the first, kept honestly: the fire remembers everything, and so shall we.*

She smiled, closed the book, and went in to work.
