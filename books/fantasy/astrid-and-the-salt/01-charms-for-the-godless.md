## 1. Charms for the Godless

The trick to selling luck in a city that has outlawed it is to never call it luck.

I learned that from my mother, who learned it from hers, back when the guild still had a name you could say out loud and a hall with a roof that didn't leak salt. *Call it a keepsake,* she told me, pressing my small fingers around a knotted thread. *Call it a souvenir. Call it nothing at all. People will pay more for nothing than they ever paid for magic.*

So when Brannick the dockworker came shuffling up to my stall at the edge of the fish market — big hands, bigger shoulders, the particular hunch of a man who'd been carrying other people's cargo since before his spine gave up arguing about it — I didn't say *charm*. I said, "Morning. Looking for anything?"

"Something for the *Marrow's Grace*," he said. "She sails on the tide after next. Captain's been having nightmares."

"Nightmares." I let the word sit there like a coin on a counter. "Bad ones?"

"The kind where you wake up tasting seawater and there's no water in your mouth." He rubbed the back of his neck. "Third man on the crew's had them too. Same dream."

That was interesting. That was *very* interesting, in the way that things are interesting right up until they get you arrested. Dreams that travel between sleepers aren't nightmares; they're messages, or leaks, or invitations, depending on who's sending them and how much you like being invited anywhere.

But Brannick wasn't asking me to interpret dreams. He was asking me for a knotted red thread with a river-stone inside it, which is what I had, and which is what I sold him, and which is what he paid four coppers for because four coppers is what a souvenir costs and souvenirs are legal.

"For the captain," I said, wrapping it in plain paper. "Tell him it's from his grandmother."

"He hasn't got one."

"All the better. Grandmothers who don't exist can't be prosecuted." I slid the packet across. "Keep it under his pillow. If the dreams stop, it worked. If they don't—" I shrugged, the universal gesture of the honest merchant. "Come back and I'll give you your coppers' worth of sympathy instead."

He almost smiled. Dockworkers don't smile much in Greyharbor; the gulls have first claim on anything that lifts off you. But he almost did, and that's four coppers well spent as far as I'm concerned.

He was three stalls away when the patrol came around the corner by the chandlery, and I did what I always do, which is become boring.

Becoming boring is an art. It requires good posture of the soul. You lower your eyes, you arrange your face into mild commercial fatigue, you start counting stock you've already counted. The patrol — two of them, grey coats, the Minister's new insignia stitched on the collars, all sharp angles like they were designed by someone who hated curves — moved down the row of stalls with the unhurried confidence of people paid to find something wrong.

One of them stopped at the fortune-teller's table two down from mine. Old Sefa. She reads palms with cold cream and theatrical sighs, no magic in her at all, but she has the misfortune of looking like a witch, which in Greyharbor is a crime with a fine attached.

"License," the taller one said.

Sefa produced it. They examined it longer than a license deserves. I counted my jars of river-stones. Fourteen. Fifteen. Sixteen.

Then the shorter one turned and looked at me, and I felt it — that prickle along the inside of my left wrist, where the guild mark sits under my skin like a swallowed ember. It does that sometimes. It *wakes*, when authority gets close, the way a dog stiffens before it barks. A century ago that waking would have been useful: a warning system, a shield, a flare. Now it's just a liability with a heartbeat.

I kept counting stones. Seventeen. Eighteen. My heart was doing arithmetic of its own.

"Charm-seller," the short patrolman said. It wasn't a question, but I answered it anyway, because guilty people argue and bored people confirm.

"That's me. Luck, love, safe travel. Entirely decorative."

He came over. He looked at my wares the way a customs officer looks at luggage — hoping, professionally, to be disappointed. Knotted threads. Dried lavender in sachets labeled *FOR CALM* in my own careful handwriting. Little clay beads fired with salt glaze, which is just pottery, officer, pottery is very legal.

"What's this one?" He picked up a thread with a sliver of driftwood in the knot.

"Souvenir of the shore. For people who miss the sea."

"You're ten feet from the sea."

"People are strange," I agreed.

He held it a moment too long. And here is the thing about hiding a guild mark beneath a leather cuff: it only works if your pulse doesn't climb, because the mark answers the pulse, warms with it, and a warm mark can be felt through leather by anyone who knows what warmth means. This patrolman might not know. His partner might. The whole system is built on maybe.

I thought about rain. I thought about the coldest morning I could remember, the winter Maren made us all stand in the fountain behind the guildhall until we stopped complaining about our lessons. I thought about absolutely nothing, which is the hardest thing to think about, and the mark cooled, and the patrolman set the driftwood thread down and said, "These are rubbish," and moved on.

Rubbish. From his mouth, a benediction.

They shook Sefa down for a fine anyway — unlicensed *ambiance*, whatever that means this week — and moved on down the row, leaving boredom in their wake like a tide going out. My hands took another minute to stop shaking. That's normal. That's the tax. Every charm I sell, I pay a little of myself in fear, and the Minister collects it whether he knows it or not.

Brannick was gone. I hoped the thread worked. I hoped the dreams stopped. I hoped, privately and illegally, that whatever was swimming through the crew of the *Marrow's Grace* found quieter waters.

Because here's what nobody says out loud in Greyharbor: the magic didn't leave when they purged it. It just went underground, and underground things grow strange. The city sits on top of its own confiscated past like a cork on a bottle, and every so often, something bubbles.

*

The night market opens when the honest market closes, which tells you everything about Greyharbor's relationship with honesty.

It sets up in Tanner's Row under strings of paper lanterns that are technically fire hazards and spiritually essential. There's fried dough. There's a man who sells songbirds and a woman who buys them and releases them, every week, and neither of them acknowledges the other, and it's the most beautiful transaction in the city. There are knives, and silks, and a fortune-teller who is definitely not Sefa but might be Sefa's cousin, and there is — at the far end, past the lantern with the tear in it, past the oyster cart —

Astrid.

She was at the map-seller's stall, the way she is most weeks, holding a chart of the northern coast up to the lantern light with that particular frown she gets, the one that says the map has lied to her personally and she intends to write a letter about it. Cartographer's assistant, she calls herself. Apprentice, really. She has ink stains on her fingers in patterns that suggest she draws maps even when she isn't being paid to, the way some people pray.

I should explain something about Astrid, and I'm going to do it badly, because there's no good way to explain a person you've been orbiting for eight months without landing.

Her name is Astrid. She came to Greyharbor two years ago from somewhere north — she doesn't say where, and I don't ask, because in this city *where are you from* is a question you only ask if you want to watch someone flinch. She has grey eyes that go silver in lantern light, and hair the color of wet sand, and she talks to strangers easily and to me carefully, and I have built entire cathedrals out of the difference.

We met over an argument about a compass. Mine. She said it was miscalibrated. I said it was charmed. She said that was the same thing. I have never recovered.

"Felix." She said it without turning around, which means she'd already seen me in the reflection of the glass case, which means she watches for me, which means I'm going to think about that for the rest of the week.

"You're frowning at Norway's coastline."

"I'm frowning at whoever drew this coastline." She tapped the chart. "Look. The inlets are wrong. All of them. North of the Tide-Line, someone just... invented geography. Lazy loops. No cartographer draws lazy loops unless they've never seen the coast, or unless—"

"Unless?"

Her mouth did the thing it does when she decides not to finish a thought. "Unless they weren't allowed to." She set the chart down and smiled at me instead, and the unfinished sentence went somewhere behind her eyes and sat down to wait. "Evening, charm-seller. Sold any luck today?"

"Only the legal kind. You?"

"I mapped three streets that don't exist anymore." She bought two oysters from the cart and handed me one without asking, which is either friendship or habit, and I've decided not to examine which. "The survey office wants them documented for the land registry. Streets that were filled in before anyone living can remember. Everyone I ask says *there was never a street there.* But the cellar doors say otherwise. Cellar doors don't lie. They open onto brick walls now, but they open *outward*, Felix. Doors are optimistic. Nobody builds a door facing a wall."

I ate my oyster and fell a little further, quietly, the way you sink in deep water — no splashing, just pressure, and then you're somewhere new and pretending you always were.

"There's a story in that," I said.

"There's a story in everything. That's the problem." She wiped her fingers on a napkin. "My grandmother used to sing a song about a door. I thought it was nonsense until I started mapping. Now I think songs are just maps people memorized before the paper burned."

"A cheery theory."

"I have others." She looked at me then, fully, and the lantern light did its silver thing to her eyes, and the market noise went soft and distant, the way it does, the way I hate that it does, because wanting someone in a city like this is like keeping a lit candle in a wind tunnel. "You look tired."

"I'm always tired. It's part of my charm."

"One of them?"

"The foundational one. Everything else is load-bearing."

She laughed — short, surprised, real — and I banked it like a coal against the winter. This is what we do, Astrid and I. We meet at markets and exchange wit like currency and touch nothing, and I go home to a leaking guildhall and nine other people who'd tell me, gently, in Elder Maren's voice, that attachment is a liability, that everyone we love is a lever someone else can pull, that the last hundred years have proven it.

And I agree with them. I agree completely. And then Astrid laughs, and agreement goes up like smoke.

A bell rang, far off — the harbor bell, one stroke, meaning nothing urgent, just the hour. Around us the market thinned by degrees. The bird-woman released her weekly prisoner; it went up past the torn lantern and out over the rooftops, free and illegal and gone.

"Walk with me?" Astrid said. "I'm mapping the lamplighters' route tomorrow. I like to walk it first. Get the feel of a street before I draw it."

"It's dangerous, walking at night."

"So am I."

She said it lightly. She meant it lightly. But something in me — the same something that makes the mark wake under my cuff — heard it twice.

We walked. Tanner's Row to the canal, canal to the steps, the steps up along the sea wall where the wind comes in off the water smelling of kelp and distance. She talked about contour lines. I talked about nothing important. Our hands swung at our sides, six inches apart, a border neither of us crossed, a border I have drawn maps of in my head more detailed than anything in her survey office.

At the top of the steps she stopped. Below us Greyharbor lay in its bowl of light and fog, lamps like drowned stars, the Minister's tower rising out of the middle of it, black and certain.

"Do you ever feel watched?" she asked.

"Constantly. Occupational hazard."

"No, I mean—" She hugged her elbows. The wind pulled at her hair. "Like the city itself is watching. Like there's a page somewhere with your name on it, waiting."

I laughed, because the alternative was answering honestly, and honesty in Greyharbor is contraband worse than any charm I sell.

"Astrid. Nobody's writing pages about us. We're background. We're the *lazy loops*."

She smiled, but it didn't take all the way. "Maybe. It's just — lately I keep dreaming about a burning door. And salt water. And I woke up this morning with the taste of it in my mouth, actual salt, and I haven't been near the sea all week except—" She stopped herself. Shook her head. "Listen to me. Blame the oysters."

But I wasn't listening to her anymore, not entirely. Because dockworkers on the *Marrow's Grace* were dreaming salt-water dreams, and the mark on my wrist had been warm since sundown, and when a city starts dreaming in chorus, somebody or something is doing the conducting.

"Burning door," I said, keeping my voice light, keeping my pulse flat, keeping my mark cool. "Sounds dramatic. What's behind it?"

"I don't know. That's the worst part. In the dream I know it's the most important thing in the world, and I can't remember what it is." She turned to me, and the fog had come in close, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Felix. Do you ever feel like you're forgetting something enormous? Like there's a shape in your memory with nothing in it?"

Every instinct I have — the guild-trained, mother-honed, survival-installed instinct — said *say nothing, deflect, joke, become boring.*

Instead I said, "Sometimes. Lately, more than sometimes."

And she reached over and took my hand — the first time, eight months, the first time — and her fingers were cold from the sea wind and steady as a drawn line, and above us the fog rolled off the water in slow grey sheets, and somewhere down in the city a patrol bell rang twice, meaning *something found, nothing urgent.*

Not yet.

But the mark under my cuff had begun, very softly, to glow.
