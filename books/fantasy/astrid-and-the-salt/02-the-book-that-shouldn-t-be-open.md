## 2. The Book That Shouldn't Be Open

The summons came the way bad news always comes in Greyharbor: wrapped in something mundane and smelling faintly of fish.

A boy I didn't know pressed a folded paper into my hand outside the chandlery where I'd just sold a widow a charm against her late husband's ghost—harmless thing, mostly a placebo with a thread of warmth stitched through it—and by the time I looked up from reading it, he'd vanished into the crowd like a coin dropped down a well.

*Come tonight. Come alone. Come before you do anything else.* — M.

No signature flourish, no guild seal. Elder Maren had been born under a different set of laws, back when the guild could still afford signatures. The paper itself was old stock, the kind we hadn't bought in years, and it carried that particular dry smell of a house that was slowly forgetting how to keep itself warm.

I stood there on the dock road with gulls screaming their opinions overhead and thought: *no.*

Not *no* as in refusal. *No* as in recognition. You know that feeling—the one where your body understands a thing three full seconds before your mind consents to? My hands had gone cold around the note. My feet already knew which streets to take.

Because here's what nobody tells you about dread: it's punctual. It arrives exactly when summoned, dressed appropriately, and it waits for you without complaint. Dread is the most patient creature in Greyharbor, and I have sold charms against nearly everything else.

* * *

The guildhouse sits behind a shuttered candle-maker's shop off Brine Street, up a staircase that creaks in nine distinct notes, one for each member we have left. I used to think that was charming. A staircase that counted us. Lately I've started wondering whether it was counting down.

Maren was waiting in the scriptorium, which she almost never used, because the scriptorium was where the guild kept its memory and Maren preferred, most days, not to look at it directly. She's seventy-something and built like a mooring post—all weathered wood and iron stubbornness—with hands so scarred they look like a map of somewhere dangerous. She'd lit every lamp in the room. That alone should have told me. We ration light the way other people ration money.

"You got my note," she said.

"I got your note." I held it up. "Very cryptic. Very 'come alone.' If this is an intervention about the luck-charms I've been selling at a markup, I want it noted that Odell takes a cut too."

"Sit down, Felix."

There are perhaps four sentences in the world that make me sit down immediately, and that tone of hers is two of them. I sat. The chair by the long table was cold, and the table itself was bare except for one object, centered, with the careful placement of a thing someone had spent all day deciding whether to put out or burn.

A book.

It was small—smaller than a hymnal—and bound in leather that had gone the color of wet sand. Salt crust along the spine. Clasps of tarnished brass shaped, when I leaned close enough to see, like two hands holding each other shut.

"That's—" I started.

"Yes."

"It shouldn't be out of the vault."

"No," Maren agreed. "It shouldn't be open, either. And yet."

She said it the way you'd say *and yet the tide comes in*, and my stomach did something complicated and slow, like a knot tying itself underwater.

The Salt Codex. Every guild child grows up knowing about it the way children grow up knowing about the thing under the bed: it exists, it is not to be touched, and if you ever hear it mentioned aloud, pretend you didn't. It predated the purge. It predated, supposedly, the founding of half the coastal cities. It was the reason our guild existed at all—or so the elders muttered when they'd had enough plum wine to forget they weren't supposed to mutter.

"Why now?" I asked. "It's been locked since before I joined. Since before *you* joined."

"Because three nights ago, it unlocked itself." Maren's voice was flat, but her hands, resting on the table, were very still in the way of things trying not to tremble. "The clasps opened on their own. No spell. No key. I found it sitting in the vault with its pages turned to the middle, as if it had been waiting for someone to notice."

"And you noticed."

"I'm the last elder who can read it. That's less an accomplishment than a diagnosis." She pushed the book an inch toward me. An inch. It might as well have been a mile across open water. "Read the page it chose."

"Felix of Greyharbor," I read, because the heading was in plain script, no cipher, which frightened me more than any cipher would have. The Codex wasn't supposed to speak plainly. Plain speech was for things that wanted to be understood.

*Felix of Greyharbor, daughter of no recorded line, bearer of the ninth mark.*

My mouth went dry. The ninth mark. The guild mark, burned into the inside of my left wrist when I took my vows at nineteen—a small salt-white spiral that only shows when I'm afraid, which is a design flaw I've complained about formally, twice.

"Keep going," Maren said quietly.

Below my name, in ink that looked fresher than it had any right to look, the prophecy ran on:

*She will be given a choice between the woman of the tide-line and the flame that remains. What she keeps, she keeps for the world. What she loses, she loses forever.*

"Okay," I said, when I could speak. "That's ominous but vague. Prophecies are always vague. It's practically the job description. 'Woman of the tide-line' could be anyone. Half this city lives within sight of the water."

"Turn the page."

"Maren—"

"*Turn the page, Felix.*"

I turned the page.

And there she was.

Not a portrait—the Codex doesn't paint—but a description, written in that same unsettlingly fresh ink, in a hand that was almost mine but older, as though I'd written it years from now and sent it back:

*Astrid of the Tide-Line. She who dreams of the burning door. Keeper or killer of the last flame. By her hand: the country returns, or the fire goes out, and with it every door that was ever closed to keep something safe.*

The room got very quiet. Quiet the way the bottom of the sea is quiet.

"Astrid," I said. Just the name. It came out strange, like a word in a language I'd only heard spoken by one person.

"The cartographer's assistant," Maren said. Not unkindly. Which was worse. "The woman you've been meeting at the night market every Thirdday for four months. Yes. We know. The wards report comings and goings; you know that."

"The wards report—" I laughed, and it came out wrong, high and thin. "You've been *reading my mail*?"

"We've been reading the city's mail. You happen to be part of the city." She finally moved, folding her scarred hands together. "Felix. Listen to me. When the clasps opened, I hoped—I prayed, and I haven't prayed since the purge—that the names would be strangers'. Some fishwife in Vessing. Some inquisitor's daughter. Anyone whose face I'd never have to learn."

"But it's her."

"It's her."

I looked down at the page again. *Keeper or killer.* As if those were the only two options. As if Astrid—who haggled over the price of ink like it was a moral principle, who drew coastlines with a tenderness I'd never seen anyone bring to anything, who laughed with her whole chest like the sound cost her nothing—as if *she* were a hinge the whole world swung on.

"She sells maps," I said stupidly. "She complains about her boots. Last week she fed a stray cat half her dinner and then lied to me about it because she was embarrassed."

"Prophecy doesn't care what people eat."

"No," I said. "It really doesn't, does it. That's the whole problem with it."

* * *

Here is the thing I couldn't say to Maren, standing there in that lamplit room with the book breathing its salt smell between us:

I had been keeping Astrid out of history on purpose.

Not consciously—not as a plan laid out in ledgers—but deliberately, the way you keep a match away from a curtain. Everyone I loved who mattered to the guild ended up *in* something. In the records. In the wards. In the ledger of names the inquisitors would someday find if the guild fell, because we wrote everything down, we sentimental fools, we archivists of our own executions. My mother died in the second purge-wave with her name in three separate registries. I learned early: the safest place for a person you love is nowhere. Unrecorded. Unremarked. A woman you meet at a night market, whose surname you never ask, whose dreams you listen to without writing them down—even when she talks about the burning door, even when the hair on your arms stands up, even when some deep guild-trained instinct whispers *that's a detail, that's a detail, write that down*—

I hadn't written it down. Four months of Thirddays, and I'd kept her dreams in my head and nowhere else, and I'd told myself it was respect. Privacy. *Her* dreams, *her* business.

It was cowardice wearing respect's coat. And now the universe had gone ahead and written her down anyway, in a book older than the city, in ink that hadn't dried.

"Felix." Maren's voice pulled me back. "What does she dream? Exactly. All of it. Don't edit."

So I told her. The burning door—green wood, black iron, flames that didn't consume. The salt water rising around her ankles, warm, not cold. The feeling—she'd described it once, embarrassed, wine-loosened—that the door was *waiting for her specifically*, the way a dog waits at a gate. And the tide-line: she'd never explained the phrase, but she drew it on everything, a looping mark along every coastline in her drafts, and when I'd asked her why, she'd shrugged and said, "It's just where the country starts, isn't it? Somebody has to remember where things begin."

"Somebody has to remember where things begin." Maren repeated it softly, tasting it. "That's not a dream, Felix. That's a job description. That's what the wardens of Vellmark used to say."

"Vellmark doesn't exist."

"No," Maren said. "It doesn't. That was rather the point of the exercise."

I stared at her. "The exercise?"

Something crossed her face then—something old and guilty, surfacing like a body after a drowning—and she looked, for the first time since I'd known her, like a woman deciding how much truth a person can carry before it breaks her back.

"Not tonight," she said finally. "Tonight you need to know three things, and only three. One: the Codex has named her, and the Codex has never been wrong. Two: the Minister's people are asking questions about the guild again—real questions, with lists attached. Three—" she closed the book, and the clasped hands sealed shut with a sound like a sigh, "—you cannot tell her. None of it. Not the prophecy, not the book, not the door."

"The door?" I seized on it. "What door? The burning door is real?"

"Felix."

"She's dreaming about a real door and you're telling me not to tell her?"

"I'm telling you that the moment Astrid of the Tide-Line knows what she is, every power in this country that benefits from Vellmark staying erased will know it too. Corvane's people. The inquisitors. Things older than either." Maren leaned forward, and up close her eyes were the pale grey of sea-glass, worn smooth by things I didn't want to imagine. "Right now she's safe because she's ignorant. Ignorance is a ward, girl. Crude, but it holds. The day you tell her, the ward breaks—and everything that's been circling this city for a century will turn and look at her."

"And what about what's circling turns and looks at *me*?"

"That's already happened," Maren said gently. "You're in the book too."

* * *

I walked home the long way, along the seawall, because the short way passed the night market and it was Thirdday and I could not—could *not*—be trusted near it tonight.

The water below the wall was black and patient. Somewhere out past the harbor lights lay the drowned coast, the shapeless stretch of map that every chart labeled simply *shallows, unsafe, uncharted*, as if a whole country could be reduced to a footnote. As if it had volunteered.

*I'm just where the country starts,* she'd said, laughing, drawing her little loop. *Somebody has to remember.*

She remembered. She'd always remembered. She'd crossed half the world chasing a dream of a burning door, and I'd sat across from her at a market stall eating candied ginger and thought: *how lovely, how strange, how lucky I am that she's nobody special.* That she was outside all of it. Safe in her beautiful, ordinary ignorance.

And the whole time, a hundred-year-old book had been holding a page open for her like a reserved seat.

I stopped at the wall's end and pressed my left wrist against the cold stone until the salt spiral rose white against my skin. Afraid. The mark knew before I did. It always did.

*What she keeps, she keeps for the world. What she loses, she loses forever.*

There was a third option, obviously. There's always a third option, the one where you lie well instead of badly, where you fold the prophecy into a drawer and love her anyway in the gaps of it, Thirdday by Thirdday, until one of you dies of old age with the door still burning politely somewhere out past the shallows, unwalked through, unchosen. Cowardice wearing respect's coat. I'd worn it before. It fit.

But the Codex had opened *itself*. Books don't do that. Books wait. Which meant something had stopped waiting—and whatever it was, it hadn't waited a century just to be defeated by my talent for avoidance.

Behind me, up the hill, the night market was closing. Somewhere in it, a cartographer's assistant was walking home with ink on her fingers and salt in her hair, humming a song she didn't know the words to—a lullaby, though neither of us knew it yet, from a country that officially had never sung anything at all.

Thirdday after next, she'd ask me, casually, the way she asked everything: *"Any plans this week?"*

And for the first time in my life, I would have to answer her honestly:

*Yes. Lying to you. It's going very badly already.*
