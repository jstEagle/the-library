## 3. Salt on Her Lips

There are three things you should know about dreams, if you're going to understand me at all.

First: I don't dream in pictures. Most people get a little theatre behind their eyes—faces, rooms, their dead grandmother waving from a train platform. I get weather. Pressure. The sense of a room before the room arrives.

Second: I have had the same dream since I was six years old, and it has never once changed.

Third: I told Felix about it before I told anyone alive, and I still don't know whether that was trust or just the particular recklessness of wanting someone.

But I'm getting ahead of myself, and Astrid Vess of the Tide-Line does not get ahead of herself. That's practically my whole personality. Ask anyone at the cartography office. Ask the ink stains on my left cuff, permanent as sins.

* * *

The night market on Cordwainer's Stair opens when the fog does, which in Greyharbor means most evenings and all of the good ones. It's not a market so much as an argument between light and dark that the lanterns are winning narrowly. Paper lamps strung on fishing line. Oyster shells full of burning tallow. A man selling candied ginger who swears on his mother's grave it's imported, though his mother is alive and works the till beside him.

I was there for oysters. That's the honest version. The flattering version—the one I'd tell at a party—is that I was there because the city felt like it was holding its breath, and I wanted to be somewhere it exhaled.

The truth is probably both. Truth usually is, in Greyharbor. We're a city of boths. Both free port and garrison town. Both pious and superstitious. Both the kind of place where you can buy anything and the kind of place where you learn to want quietly.

I found Felix at the charm-seller's row, which surprised me, because Felix doesn't strike me as the type to buy luck off strangers. She strikes me as the type who makes her own and resents having to.

She was standing at a stall hung with knotted cords and glass beads, holding a small brass compass up to the lamplight like she was checking it for forgery. She wasn't buying. She was *assessing*. There's a look people get when they know more than the person talking to them—it's a specific kind of patience, patient the way a cat is patient—and Felix had it on like a coat.

"You're going to tell him his lodestones are misaligned," I said, arriving at her elbow.

"I'm going to *think* it very loudly," Felix said, "and hope the shame travels."

The stall-keeper, a round man with flour on his apron despite selling nothing resembling bread, beamed at us both with the confidence of a man who had never once been shamed by anything, including his own reflection.

Felix turned to me and something happened to her face. I want to be precise about this, because precision is my trade. When she saw me, the assessing look didn't drop away so much as fold itself up, quick and neat, like a map being closed. And underneath it there was just—her. Slightly wary. Slightly pleased. Pleased in the way of someone who had been pleased already and was annoyed to find it showing.

"Astrid," she said.

"Felix." I nodded at the compass. "Buying your way north?"

"Checking whether he's selling north or just selling." She set it down. "It points eleven degrees off true. Either he's a fraud or the city's magnetic field has opinions."

"Maybe both," I said. "Greyharbor's allowed."

That got the almost-smile. The real one lives further back; I've seen it twice and hoarded both sightings like a miser. The almost-smile comes free but costs something anyway.

We walked. That's what we do, mostly—walk. Through the market, past the eel-pie cart whose proprietor blesses each pie individually, past the pawnshop window where a taxidermied otter wears a tiny hat for reasons no one has ever successfully explained to me. Felix walks like someone keeping half an eye on exits. I walk like someone reading the street. Between us we make one reasonably complete person.

"So," she said, after a while. "You look like you've decided something."

"Do I?"

"You get a crease." She touched the space between her own brows, briefly, without thinking about how it looked, which is exactly why it looked like that. "Right there. Decision crease."

"It's a frown, Felix."

"It's a decision wearing a frown's clothing."

And that—that stupid, precise, infuriating sentence—is when I decided to tell her. Not because of the crease. Because of the walking. Because she matched my pace without being asked, and because the fog had come in soft off the harbor and turned every lantern into a small private sun, and because I was tired of carrying the dream alone. You can carry a thing alone for twenty years and call it character. Eventually you notice you're just lonely with good posture.

"There's a door," I said.

Felix's step hitched. Half a beat, no more. If I hadn't spent three months learning the rhythm of her I'd have missed it entirely.

"A door," she repeated.

"In the dream. The one I've had since I was six." I kept my eyes forward, on the lanterns, because some things are easier said to paper lamps than to faces. "It stands in salt water—not in it, *in* it, the way a pier leg stands in it—and it's burning. Not consumed. Burning the way a wick burns, steady, like it's meant to. And behind the door—" I laughed a little, embarrassed, and heard how thin the laugh came out. "Behind the door there's sunlight. Old sunlight. The kind that feels stored, like wine. And I'm always on the outside. Every time. Twenty years of the same dream and I have never once gotten through."

The market noise went on around us. Ginger and tallow and somebody arguing about the price of rope. Felix said nothing, which was unlike her, and I made the mistake of looking over.

Her face had gone very still. Not blank—still, the way water goes still before it freezes. Her eyes were doing arithmetic I couldn't see.

"That's—" she started.

"Mad," I supplied. "Yes. I'm aware. A cartographer haunted by a burning door. My mother hoped for grandchildren."

"No," Felix said. "That's not what I was going to say."

"What were you going to say?"

A long pause. A lantern guttered and recovered. Somewhere behind us the pie man blessed another pie.

"I was going to say," Felix said carefully, "that salt-water dreams are common in Greyharbor. Harbor city. Everyone dreams of water here. It's practically municipal."

"But the door isn't."

"The door might be—" She stopped. Started again. "Have you ever drawn it? Mapped it? Where would you put it, if you had to put it somewhere?"

And here is the part I have turned over maybe four hundred times since, examining it from every angle like a suspect coin: she asked the right question. Not *are you well*, not *have you seen a physician*, not any of the polite deflections. She asked *where would you put it*, which is a cartographer's question, which meant she was taking it seriously, which should have made me feel less alone and did, enormously, and also—

Also she asked it too quickly. Like a student who studied the night before.

I didn't notice then. I want to be fair to myself: I didn't notice then. I noticed everything else—her hand near mine on the rail of the Stair, not touching, occupying adjacent air; the way she exhaled when I answered; the fact that she walked me all the way to my lodging on Tanner's Cut even though it was a mile opposite her direction, which I knew because I'd mapped her route home weeks ago out of pure unsanctioned interest, and yes, I am that person, I've made peace with it.

"North," I said. "In the dream I always know it's north. Past where the maps give up. There's a coast that isn't on any chart I've ever worked from—drowned country, old country—and the door stands where the tide-line used to be."

Something crossed her face then that I had no name for. I have names for most expressions; it's a professional hazard. This one I'd have called grief, except grief needs a corpse, and nothing had died. Unless something had died a hundred years ago and she'd only just remembered.

"North," Felix repeated softly. "Past where the maps give up."

"You think I'm mad."

"I think," she said, "that the maps giving up is not the same as the land ending. Any decent cartographer knows that." And then, quieter, almost to herself: "My grandmother used to hum a song about a door."

"Your grandmother sounds interesting."

"She was interesting the way a locked box is interesting." Felix smiled, and it was the real one, third sighting, and it hurt to look at because it was aimed slightly past me, at something twenty years gone. "She'd sing it while mending nets. *Open the door where the salt remembers.* I thought it was nonsense. Everything she said sounded like nonsense until it didn't."

"*Where the salt remembers,*" I repeated. My mouth had gone dry. "Felix. That's the exact phrasing. From the dream. The door says it—or I say it—I've never been sure which."

For a moment neither of us moved. The fog pressed close. Two women on a stair in a city that runs on secrets, sharing one between us like contraband, and I remember thinking: *this is the most honest conversation I've had in years.*

I remember thinking that. I want it on the record that I thought that, because of what happens later, and because whatever I'm guilty of, self-deception shouldn't be among the charges. I was paying attention. I was paying attention with everything I had.

I just didn't yet know what I was looking at.

* * *

We ended up sitting on the harbor steps with oysters, because romance in Greyharbor is ninety percent shellfish and ten percent pretending the wind isn't freezing. Felix shucked them with a small knife she produced from somewhere in her coat—a coat with many somewheres, I'd learned—and handed them to me with lemon she'd also produced, and I stopped asking where she kept things because the answers were always worse than the mystery.

"My turn for a confession," she said, working a blade under a shell. "I came to Greyharbor chasing something too."

"Oh?"

"Not a dream. An apprenticeship. Or the ruins of one." She passed me the oyster, brine and lemon and cold iron taste. "I have a—guild. Of sorts. Small. Very small. We keep certain knowledge alive. Knowledge nobody's hiring for anymore."

"What kind of knowledge?"

"Old kind." The knife paused. "The kind that gets you looked at sideways if you mention it in public."

I should note, for the record, that Greyharbor has laws about magic. Not bans—bans are honest. Laws. Registration, licensing, confiscation. The Ministry of Contested Properties keeps a vault of seized charms somewhere under the Custom House, and everyone agrees it exists and no one agrees on what's in it. Magic in Greyharbor is legal the way speech is legal in a police state: technically, until it isn't.

So when Felix said *the kind that gets you looked at sideways*, I did what everyone in this city does. I nodded, and I filed it away, and I didn't ask a follow-up question, because asking follow-up questions is how neighbors disappear.

"Keeping knowledge alive," I said instead. "Like a library."

"Like a library," Felix agreed, "if libraries could burn down and take the concept of fire with them."

She said it lightly. She didn't mean it lightly. I could see the weight go through her like a current through wire.

"And you?" she asked, turning it, the way she always turned it. "Why Greyharbor? The dream brought you north—but why here? Why a mapmaker's desk?"

Because the dream's coastline matched no chart in my home archives. Because I wrote to six cartographic houses and only Greyharbor's replied. Because I wanted to stand in a city built on drowned ground and feel how deep the water went.

What I said was: "Work. And the light. Have you seen the light here? Fog does things to it. Everything looks like it's remembering being beautiful."

Felix looked at me across the oyster knife, and her expression did the folding thing again, the map closing, and underneath it was something raw and careful.

"Yes," she said. "Everything here looks like it's remembering being beautiful." A beat. "Some of it actually is."

* * *

Here is what I did not say on those steps, and what I have regretted in the specific way you regret unposted letters:

I did not say that the dream has been getting stronger since I met her. That for nineteen years it came once a season, tide-regular, and now it comes nightly, and the door in it has begun to look less like a symbol and more like a destination. That last week I woke with actual salt drying on my lips—real salt, mineral, stinging—and had to sit on the edge of the bed convincing myself I hadn't sleepwalked to the harbor.

I did not say that when I told her *where would you put it*, the answer arrived in my mouth fully formed, in words I have never written on any chart: *where the chapel drowned, where the guild kept faith, where the salt remembers.* Three phrases. I don't know what chapel. I don't know what guild. I have never heard either phrase spoken aloud in my life.

Except once. Except that night, when Felix flinched at the second one so slightly that the oyster knife sang against a shell.

I did not say any of this, because saying it felt like opening a door of my own, and I have spent my whole life on the outside of doors, and I wanted—God help me, I wanted—one evening where I was just a woman eating shellfish with a woman she liked, in a city that let them both pretend.

Pretending, it turns out, is a skill. We were both excellent at it. That should have warned me. Nobody gets that good at normal by accident.

* * *

She walked me home again. Of course she did. At the door of my lodging on Tanner's Cut, under the broken lamp the landlord has sworn to fix since the previous administration, Felix hesitated in a way I'd never seen her hesitate.

"Astrid." Her hands were in her coat pockets. Her voice had gone careful, the way ice goes careful before it takes your weight. "If you ever—if the dream changes. If the door ever opens, or you learn its name, or anything about it stops being just a dream. You'd tell me?"

"Why?"

"Because—" She stopped. I watched her choose, visibly, between several sentences, and reject all of them, and pick the smallest true thing left. "Because I think you might be important. To things. To—to older things than either of us. And I'd hate for you to face them without someone in your corner."

It was, I recognized even then, a strange thing to say. It was too large for the moment and too small for the feeling behind it. But her eyes were doing the arithmetic again, fast and frightened, and I understood suddenly that Felix Eastwick was afraid—for me, of something, of something she knew and I didn't—and that she had just asked me, in the only language her caution permitted, to let her protect me.

I could have pushed. I could have said *what things, what older things, Felix, what do you know.* I was raised by a mother who interrogated fishmongers for sport; pushing is my inheritance.

Instead I said: "You'll be in my corner?"

"Front row," she said. "Throwing things."

"Then yes. I'd tell you."

She exhaled. Relief, I thought. Just relief.

Later—much later, with different eyes—I would replay that exhale and hear what else lived inside it: guilt. The specific relief of a person who has just secured a promise they intend to break themselves.

But that night I only knew that she leaned in and kissed my cheek, salt-adjacent, oyster-brined, and said goodnight like the word was a place she wished she could stay, and I stood in my doorway watching her walk into the fog until the fog won, and I touched my cheek where her mouth had been and found it, absurdly, faintly salty.

Dream-salt. Real-salt. By then I honestly couldn't have told you the difference.

* * *

Three days later, a liveried clerk from the Ministry of Contested Properties arrived at the cartography office with a summons in a black envelope, requesting—requesting, the word doing such heavy lifting—my presence at the Custom House to assist in the cataloguing of certain holdings.

My supervisor went pale in the way of men who have survived ministries by never being interesting. "Vess," he said, "what did you do?"

Nothing, I almost said. I'm nobody. I map coastlines and want a charm-seller and dream about a door.

But standing there with the black envelope in my hands, I felt it again—the pull, low and tidal, the same pull the dream has, coming not from the north this time but from beneath my feet, from the vaults under the Custom House, from something down in the dark that knew my name before I ever learned it had one.

"Nothing," I said. "I haven't done anything yet."

Which, I would come to understand, was precisely the problem.
