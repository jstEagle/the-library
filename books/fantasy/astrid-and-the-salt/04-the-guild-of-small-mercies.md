## 4. The Guild of Small Mercies

Elder Maren's summons arrived the way all her summons did: folded into a paper boat and left floating in my washbasin, which meant she'd been in my rooms again without asking, which meant I needed a better lock, which meant I'd stop at the ironmonger's tomorrow and then absolutely not buy one.

*Come to the hall before dawn,* it said, in her cramped hand. *Wear something you can kneel in.*

I wore trousers. Maren had been trying to get me to kneel for eleven years and I'd been declining with great politeness the whole time.

The guildhall sat on Candlemaker's Row like an old dog that refused to die in front of company — narrow, salt-stained, leaning slightly into its neighbor as if eavesdropping. From the street you'd take it for a chandler's gone bust. That was rather the point. The Order of Small Mercies had survived two purges, three inquisitions, and one disastrous attempt at incorporation by being so aggressively unremarkable that even memory skimmed past us. We were the smudge on the window. The cough in another room.

I let myself in with the brass key that only worked if you apologized to it first — guild keys were temperamental things — and found the main hall lit by eight candles.

There should have been nine.

I counted twice, because counting is what you do when your stomach drops and you want to pretend it hasn't. Eight candles in iron sconces along the eastern wall, each one named for a living member, each one burning with the small steady flame that meant the wards still knew whose heart to listen to. The ninth sconce — Brother Aldous's, third from the end — held a candle burnt to a stub, cold, wax pooled around the wick like something melted down to its essentials.

"He was taken Tuesday," Maren said from the shadows by the hearth, because of course she was already there. She was seventy-something and moved like smoke deciding where to settle. "Inquisitors came at dusk. He'd gone out to buy thread."

"Thread?"

"For his knots. He was teaching himself knot-charms. Said his hands were getting stupid." Her voice didn't crack. Maren's voice hadn't cracked since the last purge, and possibly not even then; I suspected she'd been born mid-sentence, giving instructions. "They took him off the street between the lamplighter and the fishmonger's. Nobody saw. Nobody ever sees."

I looked at the dead candle. "The snuff-ward failed."

"The snuff-ward failed." She said it flatly, but her thumb was moving over her ring — the guild signet, worn smooth as a river stone — and I'd known Maren long enough to read that thumb like a headline. "A hundred and six years, that ward has warned us when one of ours is taken. It has never once slept through it. And now it has, and I don't know why, and I hate not knowing why more than I hate most things."

"You hate everything equally. It's one of your virtues."

"And you deflect with jokes, which is one of yours, and we'll both be dead eventually, so sit down, Felix."

I sat. There are arguments you win and arguments you survive, and arguing with Maren about sitting was firmly in the second category.

* * *

She gave me tea first, which frightened me more than anything else she could have done. Maren gave tea when she wanted something large. The cups were thin old porcelain with hairline cracks, and mine had a chip on the rim shaped like a tooth, and I drank anyway because refusing would only prolong things.

"Do you know how many of us there are?" she asked.

"Nine."

"Were nine. Eight now, until we know whether Aldous can be got back." She said *got back* the way you'd say *fished out of a well*. "Eight candles. When I joined, there were forty-one. When my mother joined, sixty-three. We are a guild of small mercies, Felix — luck-charms, finding-spells, wards against bad dreams and worse landlords. Small work. Quiet work. Work that keeps ordinary people ordinary instead of terrified. And every year there are fewer of us doing it, because every year the city decides magic is a disease and we are the carriers."

"I know. I sell charms under a false name in a market stall. I'm aware of the market conditions."

"Yes." She smiled without warmth. "And do you know why the guild exists? Not the charter answer. The real one."

This was a test. Everything Maren said was a test, including her recipes, including her goodbyes. "To help people," I said, because it was the true answer and also the wrong one, and I wanted to see which she'd take.

"To guard a door," she said.

I put my cup down. Porcelain clicks very loudly when your hands aren't steady, and I heard the chip-tooth rattle against the saucer and hated myself for it.

"A door," I repeated.

Maren rose, joints announcing themselves like a ship in weather, and crossed to the back wall of the hall — the one hung with moth-eaten tapestries nobody had ever explained to me. She took down the third one, the ugly one, the one depicting what I'd always assumed was a fishing scene, and behind it was a door.

Not a real door. A drawing of one, chalked directly onto the stone, old enough that the lines had gone grey and soft, redrawn so many times they'd thickened into something almost sculptural. A tall archway. Salt crystals sketched around the threshold. And beneath it, in a script older than Greyharbor's charter, words I couldn't read.

"The Door of Vellmark," Maren said. "That's why we exist. Not mercy. Custody. Mercy is just what we do while we wait."

"Vellmark isn't real."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "It isn't. That was the entire point."

* * *

Here is what every child in Greyharbor knows: north of the drowned coast there used to be a country called Vellmark, and then there wasn't. Not conquered — erased. A hundred years ago the Ministries of three nations agreed, in documents no one may read, that Vellmark's magic was too old, too wild, too *theirs*, and so they unwrote it. Not burned it. Unwrote it. Maps redrew themselves. Songs lost their verses. Grandmothers who'd been born there woke up unable to say where. The sea came in over fields that had never been seabed, and everyone agreed this was natural, because agreeing was easier than remembering.

You don't remember a country being erased. You remember the shape where it should be, the way you feel a missing tooth.

"Maren," I said carefully, "if this is a recruitment speech, I should tell you I'm already a member. You made me swear oaths when I was nineteen. I bled on a book."

"You bled on a *small* book." She turned back to face me, and in the candlelight her eyes had the flat shine of sea glass. "Tonight you bleed on a large one, or you walk out that door and stay a charm-seller until the inquisitors learn your name. Those are the options. I've spent eleven years hoping I'd never have to give them to you, and Tuesday night I ran out of hope, so here we are."

"Why me? Why now?"

"Because the Codex has opened."

I want to record, honestly, that I did not understand her at first. My mind offered several alternatives — a codex of fish prices, a codex of knots (poor Aldous), a codex of Maren's grievances, which would run to many volumes — before the word landed properly and went through me like cold water through cloth.

"The Salt Codex," I said. "The prophecy book. The one that's supposed to be sealed."

"It unsealed itself. Three nights ago. At moonrise, without anyone touching it, which has never happened, and which I have decided not to find interesting because finding it interesting would require feeling things I've scheduled for later." She said it briskly, but her hand went to the ring again. Thumb. Circle. Circle. "Felix. The Codex names someone. A keeper-or-killer of the last dragon-fire. It gives a name and a tide-line and a birth under a burning door."

The room got very far away. The eight candles seemed to lean in.

"Maren—"

"*Astrid of the Tide-Line.*" She said the name gently, which from Maren was worse than shouting. "Yes. I know you know her. I've watched you watch her at the night market for the better part of a year, and I'm an old woman, not a blind one. The question is not whether you know her. The question is what you intend to do now that history does."

* * *

I need to explain what the Codex is, or try, though Maren's explanation took an hour and left me with a headache shaped like a coastline.

The Salt Codex was written by the founder of our order — the Warden, she called her, though the title felt wrong in my mouth, too grand for a guild of luck-charms and dream-wards. Before the erasure, the Warden had seen what was coming: the ministries gathering, the treaties being drafted, the slow consensus forming that a whole country could be deleted if enough important people signed their names. And she had seen something else, something the Codex recorded in pages that shift when you're not looking — that Vellmark's heart was a fire. The last dragon-fire in the world. Not a metaphor. An actual flame, kept burning at the center of the country, older than the kingdoms around it, and when they unwrote Vellmark they could not unwrote the fire, so they hid it instead, behind a door, inside a country folded up like a letter no one would open.

And the Codex said — Maren recited it from memory, her voice going strange and formal, the way people sound when quoting scripture they don't entirely believe — that one day the door would need opening again. And that it would be opened by one born under a burning door, of the tide-line, who would either carry the fire back into the world or snuff it out forever. Keeper or killer. The Codex, being a century old and written in prophecy, declined to specify which.

"And Astrid," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "She told me — she told me she came to Greyharbor chasing a dream. A burning door. Salt water. She's had it since she was a child."

Maren closed her eyes briefly. "Of course she has. Of course. The Codex calls to its own. That's how it's always worked — the marked ones dream their way toward it, generation after generation, and the guild's job was to find them first and—" She stopped.

"And what?"

"And decide," she said quietly, "whether to train them or turn them aside. My predecessors turned aside four marked ones. Each time, the dreams stopped, the person lived an ordinary life, and the door stayed shut. It can stay shut, Felix. It has stayed shut for a hundred years. All we have to do is keep doing nothing, very carefully, forever."

"That doesn't sound like nothing. That sounds like standing on a trapdoor and calling it a floor."

"Welcome to custody." She almost smiled. "Now you understand the guild."

* * *

After that she walked me through the hall, and I saw it — really saw it — for the first time in eleven years of membership.

I saw the wards fraying along the ceiling beams, silver threads gone grey at the ends like old hair. I saw the charm-bench where we used to make finding-spells, half its tools sold off in the hard winter two years back. I saw Sister Olla asleep in her chair with her fingers still curled around a cooling soldering iron, working past exhaustion because there was no one else to work the east-side clients. I saw the ledger of members, and how the recent pages were additions crossed out faster than they were written — recruits who lasted a season, a year, who decided that poverty plus danger was a poor trade for purpose.

Eight candles. Nine names, one of them Aldous's, waiting in a cell somewhere to become eight names.

"This is what I'm inheriting," I said. "A dying order and a locked door and a woman I—" I stopped. Recalibrated. "And a prophecy I want nothing to do with."

"What do you want to do with it?"

"Nothing. I want to sell charms. I want to drink cheap wine with Astrid at the night market and lie to her about what I am and have her lie to me about what she is, and I want both of us to go on being safe and small and unrecorded. That's what I want. History can find somebody else."

Maren stood looking at the chalked door for a long moment. The candlelight made the salt crystals around the arch seem almost real, almost glittering, almost a threshold you could step through if you were foolish enough.

"When I was young," she said, "I asked my elder the same thing. Why us? Why carry this? And she told me that the guild was founded by cowards — good ones, useful ones, but cowards. People who watched a country be erased and survived it by hiding, and who swore afterward that they would guard the door not out of duty but out of shame. Every generation since has inherited that shame and dressed it up as mercy." She turned to me. "I won't dress it up for you, Felix. This is not an honor. It is a debt, and it is overdue, and the interest is paid in people like Aldous."

"So what do you want from me?"

"I want you to choose knowing. That's all an elder can give you anymore — the whole truth, unlovely, and then the door." She gestured, and I realized with a lurch that she meant the actual front door, the exit, the ordinary world. "Walk out, keep your little life, love your cartographer, and pray the Codex stays shut and the inquisitors stay lazy. Or stay, and learn what's behind the chalk, and spend whatever years you have left paying down a debt you never signed for."

The eight candles burned. Somewhere upstairs, Sister Olla's soldering iron clattered as she woke. The chalked door waited under its tapestry, patient as only a century-old secret can be.

I thought about Astrid's face at the night market, lit by paper lanterns, telling me about her dream like it was a confession — *a burning door, and salt water, and I always wake up homesick for a place I've never been.* I thought about how I'd held her hand and said nothing, and how the nothing had felt, that night, like a kindness.

It felt different now. It felt like the first brick of a wall I'd be building between us, brick by brick, until neither of us could see the other.

"Felix," Maren said. "Choose."

And I opened my mouth to say the safe thing — I could feel it coming up my throat, smooth and rehearsed, *thank you, Elder, I'll consider it* — when the ninth candle, cold and dead in its sconce, lit itself.

No wind. No hand. Just a small blue flame standing up out of Aldous's pooled wax like a finger raised in a classroom, and Maren went white as the chalked door, because in a hundred and six years, the snuff-ward had never done *that*.

"That," she said softly, "is new."

The flame bent — not flickering, *bending* — pointing north, toward the drowned coast, toward the door, toward everything I had just been promised I could refuse.

I looked at it for a long time. Then I picked up my chipped cup, drank the last of the cold tea, and heard myself say the other thing instead:

"Show me what's behind the chalk."
