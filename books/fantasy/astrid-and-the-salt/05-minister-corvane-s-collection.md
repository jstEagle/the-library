## 5. Minister Corvane's Collection

The summons came on cream paper with a wax seal the color of dried blood, and it did not ask.

*Minister Corvane requests the presence of Astrid of the Tide-Line, assistant cartographer, at the Ministry of Reclamation, ninth bell.*

*Requests.* I'd been in Greyharbor three years and I had learned that when a minister *requests*, you bring your good boots and your will, written out on a slip of paper and left with someone who might notice if you don't come home.

My employer, Master Ondrey of the Cartographic House, read the letter twice over my shoulder and went the color of old porridge. "You didn't do anything," he said. It wasn't a question so much as a prayer he wanted me to confirm.

"I make maps," I said. "Maps are boring. Nobody summons you to the Ministry for being boring."

"Then why—"

"Because I'm good at it." I folded the letter along its crease, carefully, like it might bite. "And because someone up there finally noticed."

He wanted to say something else. I watched him decide not to. That's the other thing about Greyharbor: everyone is fluent in the language of deciding not to say things.

* * *

The Ministry of Reclamation sat on the high side of the city where the fog couldn't afford the rent, all pale stone and tall windows, beautiful the way a heron is beautiful—standing very still in shallow water, waiting for something small to make a mistake.

They took my name at the door. They took my satchel. They took a lock of my hair, which was new, and which I did not enjoy.

"For the registry," said the clerk, sealing it into a little glass vial. "Standard procedure."

"Whose standard?"

"The Minister's." He smiled the way doors close.

A different clerk walked me down. Down, and down again, past offices with real glass in the windows, past a hall where clerks were feeding ledgers into a machine that chewed them and stamped them and sang them back out as certificates of lawful disposal. The further we went, the older the stone got. The Ministry had been built on top of something, the way cities build on top of the things they'd rather not look at, and we were descending through the layers of not-looking.

"You'll map the collection," my escort said. She was young, with ink-stained cuffs and the flat voice of someone paid to have no opinions. "Item by item. Location, dimensions, classification. You will not touch anything. You will not speak to anything. If anything speaks to you—" she paused, just long enough to let me know this was not a joke, though she delivered it like one, "—you report it immediately."

"Does that happen?" I asked. "Things speaking?"

"It happens," she said, and opened the last door.

* * *

I have drawn harbors. I have drawn mountain passes in winter, coastlines after storms, the drowned streets under the Tide-Line quarter where the old city sank and nobody talks about why. I thought I understood what it meant for a thing to be *held* — held in ink, held in memory, held against its nature.

I was wrong about that until I saw the vault.

It was enormous. A cavern of a room, or maybe several rooms knocked together, lit by lamps that burned without oil — confiscated, presumably, from whoever had made them. Shelves rose three stories high, iron and salt-bleached wood, and every shelf was full. Jars. Lanterns. Rings. A child's wooden horse with one eye missing. A mirror draped in canvas like a prisoner with a hood. A birdcage containing nothing visible, which rattled gently whenever I looked away from it and went still whenever I looked back.

Thousands of objects. Tens of thousands. Each one tagged with a lead seal stamped with the Ministry's crest: a hand closing.

"What is this place?" I asked, though I knew. Everyone knew what the Ministry of Reclamation *did*. They collected magic the way harbors collect driftwood — whatever washed loose, whatever got reported by neighbors, whatever was found in a raid. Confiscated charms. Unlicensed workings. Heretical objects. It was all very tidy and very legal and everyone agreed not to wonder where it all *went*.

Here. It all went here. Into the dark, under the beautiful building, sealed in lead, arranged by year.

"You'll start at the north wall," my escort said. "Work east. The catalog is alphabetical by confiscation date. Do not deviate."

"And if I need to measure something that's, say, humming?"

"Then you'll note that it hums," she said, "and keep your hands flat on the table, and wait for it to stop."

She left me alone with it. Of course she did. That was the point of the whole arrangement, though I wouldn't understand that until later.

* * *

I worked. I'm good at working; it's the one prayer I know how to say properly. Measure, note, sketch, move on. A lantern that burned cold and showed the room behind you instead of the room ahead. A jar of storm, actual storm, gray-green and turning, pressing against the glass like a face. A set of dice that always landed showing the number their owner most feared. Someone had loved these things once. Someone had made them, or bought them, or been given them by a grandmother, and then someone else had come with a warrant and a lead seal and put a hand over all of it.

I told myself it wasn't my business. I'm a cartographer. My business is where things are, not whether they should be there. Draw the border, don't ask who died drawing it. That's the trade. That's how you survive in Greyharbor with anything worth taking hidden in your coat pocket — and yes, I had something hidden in my coat pocket, a small luck-charm Felix had sold me two winters ago, warm as a held hand, and I had never once declared it, and I was thinking about it now with every lead seal I wrote down.

Three hours in, my hand started cramping and the lamps guttered all at once, and that's when I heard my name.

Not heard. *Felt.* The way you feel rain before it arrives, a pressure change, a shift in the air's intention. It came from the far end of the vault, past the shelves, past the hooded mirror, from a case I hadn't reached yet.

"Astrid."

My name, spoken quietly, the way you'd say it across a pillow. Not a voice exactly. More like the memory of a voice, kept somewhere dry, taken out and used.

I stood very still, hands flat on the table, exactly as instructed. "Report it immediately," I reminded myself, out loud, and my voice sounded absurdly small in all that dark.

I didn't report it. I want to be honest about that, since honesty became important later. I didn't report it, because reporting it meant leaving, and leaving meant never knowing, and I have never in my life been able to leave a door unopened. Ask anyone. Ask the sea. I walked toward it.

* * *

The case stood apart from the others, on its own plinth, under its own lamp. Lead-sealed like everything else, but the seal here was older, and had been broken and restamped so many times the crest was a blur of overlapping hands.

Inside, on a bed of gray felt, lay a compass.

Ordinary-looking. Brass, green with age, the glass cracked in a spiderweb from its center. But the needle wasn't pointing north. It was pointing at me. It had been pointing at me, I realized, since the moment I entered the room, and it swung slowly, patiently, tracking me across the whole length of the vault like a dog too well-trained to bark.

"Astrid of the Tide-Line," the not-voice said again, and this time I felt it in my teeth, in the salt-taste at the back of my throat that I'd had since childhood and never been able to explain to any doctor. "You came."

"I work here," I said. "Today. Temporarily."

The needle trembled. Something almost like laughter moved through the case, through the floor, through the soles of my boots.

"You dream of a burning door," it said. "Salt water to the sill. You have dreamed it since you were six years old, and everyone told you it was nothing, and you believed them because believing them was easier than being the girl who dreams true."

My mouth went dry. "Who are you?"

"I am what they took." And the lamp above the case dimmed, and for a moment I saw it — not the compass but *through* it, the way you see the bottom of a harbor through clear water — a country. Green hills going gold in late light. A road running down to a white door in a cliff face. Smoke from chimneys, ordinary smoke, the smoke of people making dinner. A whole country, small and complete and *alive*, and then the lamp steadied and it was only brass and cracked glass and gray felt again.

"That's not possible," I whispered. "There's no country there. There's no—"

"There was." The needle spun once, slowly, and settled on me again. "There was, and they unwrote it, and they put what remained in a box, and the box has been waiting in the dark for someone who could read it. Not a clerk. Not a thief. A *mapmaker*." The needle quivered, eager. "Someone who looks at an erased coastline and sees the shape of what was removed. You've felt it your whole life, haven't you? The missing piece. The song with the verse torn out. You draw borders and your hand hesitates at a certain latitude and you've never known why."

I hadn't told anyone that. Not Ondrey, not anyone. The hesitation at the northern coast, the way my pen always dragged, like the paper itself remembered something I didn't.

"How do you know my name?"

"Everyone here knows your name." And now the voice changed, deepened, lost its hunger and gained something colder, and I understood with a lurch that I had not been talking to the compass at all. "Astrid of the Tide-Line. Keeper or killer. The book is quite specific."

The lamps came back up. All of them. Every lamp in the vault, at once, and at the far end of the room, between the shelves, stood a man I had never met and recognized instantly, because his portrait hung in the Cartographic House lobby beside the charter of the city, and because power like his doesn't bother disguising itself.

Minister Corvane wore gray the way other men wear black. He was not large. He did not need to be. He stood among ten thousand stolen wonders the way a man stands in his own pantry, and he smiled at me with genuine, unhurried pleasure, the smile of a collector finding a piece he'd been hunting.

"You didn't report it," he said. "Good. The ones who report it are no use to anyone."

* * *

He walked me out himself. Past the humming birdcage, past the hooded mirror, past the jar of storm that pressed toward him as he passed — and I swear the storm *flinched* — and up the stairs, layer by layer, back through the strata of not-looking, while he talked pleasantly about cartography as though we were colleagues.

"Erasure is a discipline," he said. "Most people think destroying a thing is the hard part. It isn't. Destruction is easy; grief does most of the work for you. No — the discipline is making the absence *tidy*. Filling the space so smoothly that no one's hand ever drags at that latitude again." He glanced at me. "Yours still drags. Interesting."

"You read my work."

"I read everything that matters." We crossed the great hall; the ledger-chewing machine sang and stamped. "Which brings me to why you're here, Miss Tide-Line. The compass spoke to you. That means the compass wants something from you, and objects in my collection only want things when those things are possible. So either you are more interesting than your file suggests—" his file on me, I noted, was apparently a thing that existed, "—or you are about to become so. Either way, I'd like you to continue mapping the vault. Regularly. Privately. Just you and the collection."

Every instinct I owned stood up and said *no* in chorus. But the compass was still below us, pointing at me through forty feet of stone, and the taste of that green country was still in my mouth like a word I'd forgotten I knew, and I was so tired of my hand dragging at latitudes I couldn't explain.

"Why me?" I asked.

"Because," Corvane said, opening the great door onto the grey afternoon, "you're the only person in thirty years it's called. And I collect *callings*." He smiled again, warmly, terribly. "Ninth bell, Thursday. Don't be late, Miss Tide-Line. Things in the dark get lonely, and lonely things get careless, and careless things get hurt."

I walked out into the street with my satchel light on my shoulder and my luck-charm cold in my pocket for the first time since Felix made it, and I did not go home. I walked toward the night market, toward the one person in Greyharbor who might understand what it means when a locked thing says your name — and stopped halfway there, on the bridge, remembering that telling her would mean explaining where I'd been, and explaining where I'd been would mean admitting I'd said yes.

Below me the tide came in over the drowned streets, patient as a debt, and somewhere under all that water, I was suddenly, horribly certain, there was a white door in a cliff face, waiting for me to find it first.
