## 6. What Felix Didn't Say

There are three kinds of lies in Greyharbor, and I've learned to price them all.

The cheap lie is the one you tell a customs officer: *No, sir, nothing to declare.* The mid-priced lie is the one you tell yourself: *I'm not falling for her, I just like her company.* And the expensive lie — the one that costs everything — is the one someone you love tells you with their whole face while their hands shake.

I didn't know yet that Felix was spending that third kind on me. I only knew that she'd invited me to dinner, and that she'd been strange about it. Strange the way weather is strange before a storm: nothing you can point to, everything you can feel.

* * *

She chose the Lanternfish, which was a mistake, because the Lanternfish was where we'd had our first almost-date, back when we were both still pretending we were people who had first dates. It's a narrow place down by the fish-market stairs, all steam and vinegar and lamps the color of weak tea. The owner, a woman named Bree with forearms like dock rope, pretends not to know Felix orders the same thing every time. Felix pretends not to know that Bree waters the wine. Greyharbor runs on mutual pretending. It's practically our civic religion.

"You look terrible," I told her, by way of hello.

"Thank you. I worked hard on it." She sat down across from me and smiled, and the smile was right, and her eyes were wrong, and I filed that away in the drawer where I keep things I'm not ready to open.

She'd worn her good coat. That was the first clue, though I didn't know it yet — the grey wool one with the brass buttons she claimed she'd found in a gutter, which was a lie so committed it had almost become true through sheer repetition. Nobody finds a coat like that in a gutter. Somebody made that coat for somebody, and Felix wore it like an apology to a person she'd never name.

We ordered. Bree brought the watered wine and the little fried fish you eat whole, bones and all, because in Greyharbor we don't believe in wasting anything, not fish, not luck, not time.

"So," Felix said, turning her glass by the stem. "How's the map trade."

"The map trade," I said, "is thriving, in the sense that I am extremely busy drawing coastlines that don't exist."

"That's most coastlines, isn't it? From a certain distance."

"From a certain distance, yes." I watched her. She was doing the thing where she looked at me like I was the only lit window on a dark street, and it worked, it always worked, and I hated a little how much it worked. "Minister Corvane has me drawing something for him. A survey."

Something moved behind her face. Quick, like a fish under ice. "A survey of what?"

"The city. The harbor approaches. Official business." I shrugged. "Boring. Pays well. Boring is the whole point of pays well."

"Right," Felix said. "Boring. Good."

And here is the thing I want to be honest about, since apparently honesty is the theme of the evening I didn't know I was attending: I believed her. That's the part that still stings. Not that she lied — everyone lies, I lie, I'd lied twice already that day about where I'd been and what I'd seen. I believed her because I *wanted* the evening to be boring, because I'd had a week of Corvane's vault and its humming shelves and its objects that whispered my name in a voice like wet silk, and all I wanted was fried fish and Felix's terrible jokes and one hour of being a woman who was only a woman.

The universe, which has a sense of humor like a dropped anchor, had other plans.

* * *

It happened because of the coat, and because of the cold, and because I am, professionally speaking, a person who notices things.

Felix went to the counter to argue with Bree about the bill — a ritual they performed every time, Felix insisting, Bree refusing, both of them enjoying it immensely. Felix took off her coat first, the way you do when you're settling in for a performance, and draped it over my chair. Because of course she did. Because she trusted me with her coat.

The lamp above our table had a cracked shade. It threw light in one direction and shadow in the other, and when I reached over to pull my scarf from where it had fallen against the coat — a small domestic motion, nothing — my knuckles brushed the inside pocket, and the pocket was heavier than a pocket should be, and there was a fold of paper in it, and the paper had come half-open, and I am a cartographer. Reading documents that are only half-hidden is most of my job.

I want to say I didn't look. I looked.

It was a transcription — someone's careful, cramped hand, not Felix's, copied from something older. The ink had bled a little at the edges like it had been carried through rain. And I read, in the space of one held breath, words I would spend the rest of the night unable to unread:

*...and the keeper of the tide-line shall come to the door with salt on her lips, and the last fire shall know her, and by her hand it shall be kept — or by her hand it shall be spent...*

Below it, in Felix's handwriting, small and pressed hard enough to tear the page: *Astrid. It's her. It's Astrid of the Tide-Line.*

My name. My actual name, in a book I'd never heard of, next to words like *fire* and *spent*, in the coat of the woman I — well. Next to my name, in her coat.

The room did a thing rooms do in stories: went quiet, tilted, filled with the sound of my own pulse. In reality it kept being the Lanternfish. Bree was still refusing Felix's money. Someone's child was banging a spoon. The fish tasted like vinegar and, suddenly, like nothing at all.

I folded the scrap back along its creases — I'm precise even when my hands aren't steady, it's the training — and put it back in the pocket, and sat there, and thought: *salt on her lips.*

I'd had salt on my lips that morning. I always do. I live by the harbor; it gets into everything, the tea, the bread, the corners of your mouth when you lick them against the wind. I'd told Felix once, early on, laughing: *I was born with salt on my lips, my mother said it was a sign I'd never be satisfied with fresh water.* I'd told her that in her bed, in the grey light, in the hour when people tell each other true things.

And somewhere in her head, apparently, that had been a *clue*.

* * *

She came back looking pleased with herself, having lost the argument with Bree on purpose, which is how Felix pays for things she doesn't want to admit she can afford. She sat down. She picked up her wine. She said, "So. I was thinking — there's a thing at the night market on Thirdday, the glass-seller from the southern quays, and I thought—"

"Felix."

"—and you said you liked the blue ones, the ones with the—"

"Felix. What's the Salt Codex?"

I have thrown things at people before. I've shouted. I've done the whole theater of anger, and it has its uses. But I've learned that the most devastating thing you can hand someone is a quiet question in a level voice, and so that's what I handed her, and I watched it land like a dropped knife.

Her face went through four seasons in two seconds. Winter, mostly.

"That's — " she started. "Where did you hear that?"

"Where did *I* hear it." I turned my glass the way she'd been turning hers, and discovered it's not as soothing as it looks. "That's your answer? Deflection, with a side of counterattack? I taught you better, and I wasn't even trying."

"Astrid." She lowered her voice. The Lanternfish was loud enough that we were safe, mostly, but she leaned in anyway, and her knee pressed mine under the table, and I hated that my body still counted that as comfort. "What exactly did you read?"

"Ah," I said. "So there *is* something to read. Good. I was worried I'd invented it. The keeper of the tide-line. The last fire. Shall be kept, or shall be spent." I kept my voice flat as a chart. "And then, in your handwriting — and I'd know your handwriting anywhere, Felix, I've gotten letters from you that I've read so many times the creases have creases — *Astrid. It's her.*"

The child with the spoon banged it again. Somewhere a pot lid clattered. Felix looked at her wine like it might contain instructions.

"It's not what you think," she said.

"Remarkable," I said. "Because I don't think anything yet. I have a scrap of paper and a hundred questions, and you're the only person in this city who can answer them, and your opening move is *it's not what you think.* Which, for the record, is what people say when it is *exactly* what I think."

"It's an old book," she said. "Guild property. Old books say old things. It's — half of it's nonsense, Maren says the prophecies are—"

"Maren." I seized the name like a rope thrown to a drowning woman, which is what it was. "Who's Maren?"

"My — my guild elder. My boss, essentially." Felix was talking faster now, and Felix does not talk fast; Felix talks like the tide, slow and certain, and watching her speed up was like watching a lighthouse spin the wrong direction. "She showed it to me because — because the guild keeps records, and your name came up in a routine — "

"A routine what? Felix. A routine *what*?"

"A survey," she said. "Of names. Of — residents."

I sat back. I looked at her. And I felt the specific, exquisite pain of watching someone you love lie to you badly — not the pain of the lie itself, but the pain of knowing they think you're stupid enough to believe it. There's an insult buried in a bad lie. *I think you are a fool* is what *a routine survey* means, coming from her mouth, to my face.

"Felix," I said, gently, because I was going to give her exactly one door and it was going to be a wide one. "I map coastlines for a living. I spend my days detecting where people have faked a river or moved a border to dodge a tax. I know what a doctored document looks like. I'm looking at one. It's just — the first one that's ever been about *me*."

"It's not doctored, it's just — old. It's not important. It's guild business, and it's boring, and I brought it up because I thought you'd want to know there's nothing in it, and I was right, there's nothing in it." She was holding her glass so hard I worried for the glass. "Can we please just — can we have dinner? I wanted one evening. One evening where you were just — where we were just—"

"Just what?"

"Just *us*," she said, and her voice cracked on it, and that was almost worse than the lie, because the crack was real. The lie was rehearsed and the crack was not, and I sat there in the steam and the lamplight and understood that I was looking at a woman who had decided, deliberately, with care, that I was safer not knowing what was written about me — and that she'd dressed that decision up as protection, and that she might even be right, and that none of that made it less of a betrayal.

Because here's what she didn't understand, what she still doesn't fully understand: I grew up on stories about people who were protected out of their own lives. My grandmother's whole village was *protected* out of its name on the maps. Every erased country, every unwritten family, every quiet disappearance in every history book — someone, somewhere, called it protection. I would rather be endangered by the truth than kept safe inside a lie, and I would rather have been asked. *Asked.* That's all. One sentence: *Astrid, there's a book, and it has your name in it, and I'm scared.* I'd have been scared with her. That's what we were for.

"You wanted one evening where we were just us," I said. "Fine. Here's us. I found a prophecy about myself in your coat, and you lied to my face about it twice, and you're still doing it, and the evening is now about that. That's us. That's the most us we've ever been."

"Astrid—"

"Is it true?" I asked. "The tide-line. The fire. Is it about me?"

And she looked at me — and this is the part I keep turning over, the part I'll be turning over for a long time — she looked at me with such naked, drowning love that for one second I thought she'd tell me everything. Her mouth opened. The truth was right there behind her teeth; I could almost see it, the way you can see a fish turn in dark water.

"No," she said.

The word fell on the table between us like a coin that everyone knows is counterfeit.

I nodded. I paid for the fish — Bree tried to refuse, and for once I insisted, because I was not going to owe that table anything. I put on my scarf. I did not touch her coat, or her hand, or her knee, though every one of them was right there, though my whole body leaned toward them the way a compass leans, stupid and faithful, toward a north that turns out to be lying about where north is.

"Astrid." She caught my sleeve at the door. The night air came in cold off the harbor, and it tasted of salt, of course it did, it always does, and I thought: *salt on her lips, salt on her lips, she knew, she's known, she's been kissing me and reading prophecy over my shoulder.* "Please. It's not — I'm trying to keep you safe."

"I know," I said. "That's the worst part."

I walked home along the water the long way, past the fish-market stairs and the shuttered stalls and the lamps going out one by one, and the whole time I could feel the harbor breathing against the seawall, patient, tidal, the way it always is, the way it has always been, like it knows something about me that I don't.

And behind me — I didn't turn around, but I heard it — the Lanternfish door opened and closed, and footsteps followed me at a distance for three streets, and then stopped.

She let me go. That's what she chose. She stood in the dark with her prophecy and her guild and her gutter coat, and she let me walk away rather than tell me the truth.

I got home. I lit my lamp. And I sat down at my drafting table, in front of the survey maps Corvane had given me, and I unrolled them, and I looked — really looked, for the first time — at the region the Minister wanted charted so urgently, so quietly, so boringly.

The drowned coast. North of Greyharbor. Where the charts say there's nothing.

Where, if you hold the old charts up to the light the way I'd learned to do that very week, there used to be a country.

*Vellmark*, whispered the ghost-lines under my pen.

I didn't know yet that Felix's guild had nine members and a secret. I didn't know about doors, or wardens, or what a minister keeps in his vault besides humming objects that know my name. I knew only this: the woman I loved had lied to me about a book, and a man with a vault had hired me to map the one place in the world that book seemed to be about.

Two liars, pulling me in opposite directions.

I sharpened my pen, and I started to draw.
