## 7. Nine Names, Eight Candles

The candle went out at midnight, and the world did not end.

That was the trouble. I'd spent my whole life believing the guild's rituals held something up — that if we ever missed a step, ever fumbled a word, the wards would fail and Greyharbor would notice us the way you notice a splinter working toward your heart. Nine members, nine candles, snuffed in order of seniority every night at the turning of the tide. A hundred years of small flames going out on schedule while the city above us slept on, never once wondering why the cellar under the chandlery smelled of salt and old paper instead of nothing at all.

I was the one who found it still burning.

Not out — *burning*, which was worse. Eight candles stood dark and proper in their row along the stone shelf, and the ninth — Odile's candle, because Odile had been with the guild forty years and outranked everyone but Maren — sat there with its flame standing straight as a soldier refusing an order. The wick hissed. The wax pooled. And the little ward-ring drawn around each candle in salt, the one meant to close like a hand when the flame died, stayed open. Waiting.

I stood in the undercroft in my nightshirt with a taper I didn't need, feeling like a servant who'd caught the house breathing.

"Odile," I said to the empty room, because the alternative was saying what I actually thought, which was: *she's not coming back to snuff it.*

* * *

You have to understand what the ritual is, or rather what it isn't. It isn't magic, not really, not the way Corvane's ministry means it — not confiscated, catalogued, fed into the vaults beneath the Custom House until the objects inside start to *want things*. It's bookkeeping. That's all the guild has ever been, at the root: accountants of the unnoticed. Every charm I sold at the night market, every luck-knot and fog-lure and fever-salt packet, drew from a ledger so old its first pages were vellum, and every night the nine of us closed our accounts with a candle apiece, and the city forgot us for another day. Not erased. Just — filed. Shelved where no one browses.

The ritual failing wasn't like a lock breaking. It was like a ledger suddenly showing a number that shouldn't exist.

Maren arrived before I'd finished dressing, which told me she'd felt it too, in whatever place elders keep the things they don't say. She was seventy-one and moved like a ship's mast — everything above deck swaying, everything below bolted down. She looked at the burning candle for a long time.

"When?" she said.

"Just past midnight. I came down to check the salt lines."

"And?"

"The eastern line's thinned. Near the grate."

She nodded slowly, the way she nodded at storms. "Odile was taken at dusk. Two inquisitors and a clerk. They walked her up Saltmonger's Row in front of everyone."

My stomach dropped through the floorboards and kept going. "Taken how? What pretext?"

"There wasn't one." Maren's voice was flat as slate. "That's the message. For a hundred years they needed pretexts. Now they don't."

* * *

By dawn all nine of us were in the undercroft, which took some arranging, since two of us had day work and one — Tam Broadstairs, who mended nets and charms with equal indifference — had to abandon a customer mid-haggle. We stood around the shelf of candles like mourners at a funeral where the body wouldn't lie down.

Eight names. Eight dark candles. And Odile's flame still going, stubborn and obscene, because the ritual only worked if the person snuffed their own light, and Odile was somewhere in the Custom House vaults being asked questions by men whose questions had edges.

"We do it for her," said Tam. He reached for the candle.

Maren's hand closed on his wrist without her even looking. "You touch that flame, you break the sequence. The sequence is the ward. You break the ward, every charm in this city that carries our mark goes visible at once." She let him go. "Every luck-knot. Every fever-salt. Every piece of quiet we've sold to people who'd be hanged for owning it."

"So we wait," said Petra, the youngest of us, twenty-three and all elbows and fury. "We wait for what? For them to come down here next? Maren, they took Odile off the street. Off the *street*. In daylight."

"They took her because she was visible," Maren said. "Because she argued with a tithe-collector about the new registry levy, loudly, in front of the fishwives. Because she'd forgotten, in her forty years, that being remembered is the one thing we can't afford." Her eyes went around the circle, and stopped on me, and stayed. "Which brings me to the other matter."

I knew. God help me, I knew before she said it. There's a particular dread in watching an elder draw breath to say a thing they've already decided, and I'd seen Maren draw that breath twice before in my life — once to tell Odile her husband hadn't drowned, he'd been taken, and once to tell the whole guild the wards over Vellmark's coast had shifted a half-mile north.

"Felix," she said. "Come here."

"I'm standing right here."

"Come *here*."

I crossed the undercroft. The salt crunched under my feet. Up close, the ninth candle's flame leaned toward me, just slightly, like a dog hearing its name.

"Maren—"

"The succession isn't sentimental. It's structural. The heir holds the second key to the ledger-vellum, and the ledger-vellum holds—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening, aware of seven pairs of ears. Aware, maybe, of what she'd already shown me alone: the Salt Codex, and the name written in it that I couldn't stop hearing when the room went quiet. *Astrid of the Tide-Line.* "It holds what it holds. Odile held the second key. Odile is gone. You're next in the line."

"I'm fourth from the bottom," I said. "There are six people—"

"Petra's too young. Tam would sell the vellum for drink money within a year, and we both know it, and he knows it too, which is why he's not arguing." Tam, to his credit, raised his mug in rueful confirmation. "And the others are marked. The inquisitors have their faces. Yours they don't. You hide better than any of us, Felix. You always have." Something moved behind her eyes that might have been apology. "Hiding well is the whole inheritance. I'm sorry. It shouldn't be, but it is."

The candle flame leaned further. I could feel warmth on my cheek now, faint as a held breath.

"What happens to it?" I asked. "Her candle. If she never comes back to snuff it."

Maren was quiet long enough that Petra answered for her, voice cracking on the arithmetic: "Then the ward stays open. Then we're visible. All of us. Everything we've ever sold, everyone who ever bought — it all surfaces."

"A hundred years of accounts," Tam said softly, "coming due."

* * *

I went up into the grey morning and walked, because walking is what I do instead of screaming. Past the chandlery, past Saltmonger's Row where the cobbles still held the shape of yesterday's crowd, past the Custom House with its new black banners and its clerks counting confiscated magic into ledgers of their own. Somewhere under that roof Odile was learning what the ministry does with a woman who won't give them a name.

And underneath everything, under the fear and the grief and the cold weight of the second key now sitting against my sternum like a swallowed coal, was the thought I'd been carrying for days and couldn't put down:

*Astrid doesn't know.*

Astrid, who dreamed of a burning door and salt water. Astrid, whose name sat in a century-old book of prophecies like a coin waiting to be spent. Astrid, who smiled at me across the night market stalls and believed — because I let her believe, because lying to her had started to feel like protecting her — that I was nobody. A charm-girl. A small life. Someone safely outside history.

Odile had been taken for being visible. For one loud argument on one street.

What would the ministry do with a woman written in prophecy?

I stopped at the harbor rail. The tide was coming in, grey and patient, covering the mudflats where Vellmark used to be — where it still was, if half of what Maren showed me was true. Somewhere north, under that water, a door. Somewhere in a book, a name. Somewhere in my chest, a decision I'd been avoiding since the night Elder Maren opened the Codex and I read my own future spelled out in someone else's fate.

The terror didn't leave. I want to be honest about that. Terror doesn't leave; it changes jobs. It stops being the thing that keeps you hidden and becomes the thing that makes you move.

I turned back toward the guild, and my feet were already faster than they'd been an hour ago, and I understood, finally, what Maren had been telling me in her mast-straight way: hiding well was the inheritance. But hiding was over. The candle was burning whether we liked it or not, and a lit candle in a dark room is a summons.

When I got back down to the undercroft, Maren was waiting with the ledger-vellum unrolled and a look on her face that said she'd known, all along, exactly how long my walk would take.

"Good," she said. "Now learn what you're inheriting. We haven't much time — and Felix?" She laid her hand flat on the open Codex, on the page I'd hoped never to see again. "Before the inquisitors find her, *you* need to decide what Astrid gets to know. Because they will find her. Books like this are how they hunt."

The ninth candle burned on between us, and for the first time in a hundred years, the guild of small mercies had a fire it couldn't put out.
