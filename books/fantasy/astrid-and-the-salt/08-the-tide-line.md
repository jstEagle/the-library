## 8. The Tide-Line

**FELIX**

She didn't knock. Of course she didn't knock. Astrid had never once knocked in the three years I'd known her, and I'd built a whole philosophy around pretending that was charming rather than alarming.

She stood in my doorway with a canvas satchel over one shoulder and a look on her face that I recognized from the inside: the expression of someone who has decided to be furious later, after the work is done.

"I found the rest of it," she said. "In the guild archive. Don't look surprised. Maren left the reading room unlocked, which either means she's careless or she wanted me to see it, and Maren has never been careless in her life."

"Astrid—"

"You wrote 'the tide-line remembers' on a scrap of paper and hid it in your coat like a love note from a ghost." She stepped past me into the room, uninvited, dropping the satchel on my bed with the confidence of someone who had already decided she was coming north. "I've been a cartographer's assistant for six years, Felix. I know what a coastline looks like when someone has drawn it and then tried very hard to forget they did. There's a whole shelf of charts in the Minister's vault where the ink's been scraped but the grooves are still there. You can feel Vellmark under your fingertips if you close your eyes."

I should have said something. I had a whole speech prepared, actually—three drafts, each more honest than the last, each abandoned at the moment of delivery. Instead I said, "You went back to the vault?"

"I resigned." She said it the way other people announce the weather. "Corvane asked me to map the drowned coast for him. Officially, it's a survey of navigational hazards. Unofficially, he wanted to know where the door is, and he wanted me to find it for him." Her jaw did something tight. "So I'm going to find it first."

"That's a terrible plan."

"It's the only plan anyone's offered me. You've been hoarding yours."

She had me there. I sat down on the windowsill because my legs had opinions about standing. The morning market noise drifted up from the street—fishwives, gulls, a patrol drum somewhere, always a patrol drum somewhere—and it all sounded suddenly very far away, like the city was already letting go of me.

"I was trying to protect you," I said. It came out smaller than I'd rehearsed.

"I know." She looked at me then, really looked, and the anger in her face cracked down the middle to show the hurt underneath, which was worse. "That's the part I can't decide whether to forgive. You lied to me the way you'd lie to a patrol. Like I was a danger to you. Like I was something to be hidden."

"You're the most dangerous thing in Greyharbor," I said. "You're written in a book that inquisitors would burn a city to hold."

"And you decided that alone." She picked up the satchel again. "So here's how it's going to be. We go north together. You tell me everything the Codex says, in order, even the parts that frighten you. Especially those. And if you lie to me again—not even a big lie, Felix, a small one, a kind one—I'll walk into the sea and you can explain to your guild why their prophecy drowned."

"That's dramatic."

"I learned from a charm-seller who fakes a limp to avoid patrol inspections."

That was one time. It had been a very convincing limp.

I got my coat. Underneath the floorboard, wrapped in oilcloth, the Salt Codex waited with its pages of scraped geography and its one unbearable sentence about Astrid of the Tide-Line. I didn't tell her I was bringing it. Not yet. Call it a small lie, a kind one. Call it the last one I'd get to keep.

We went down the stairs together, out into the grey morning, and north.

* * *

**ASTRID**

Here is what nobody tells you about the drowned coast: it isn't drowned. That's the lie of the name. The sea took the low country, yes—the maps in the Minister's vault showed harbors and salt meadows where the charts now showed open water—but the coast itself still stood, black rock and grey shingle running north for a hundred miles, empty as a pulled tooth.

We walked it for two days. Felix talked. That was our treaty, and to her credit, she kept it.

"The Codex gives the geography in riddles," she said, on the second morning, walking backward ahead of me on the shingle so she could watch my face while she talked, which was either honesty or a tactic. "Salt where the bells once rang. The tide-line remembers. A chapel that keeps the sea's hours. Maren thinks the chapel is the marker. The door is under it, or behind it, or through it—I honestly don't know, the Codex gets very poetic right at the part where poetry is least useful."

"Poetry is how you hide a location in a book inquisitors can read," I said. "You encode it in images only the right reader can decode. It's a map for people who already know the country."

Felix stopped walking. "Say that again."

"Which part?"

"The right reader part." She was doing the thing with her eyes where they go bright and slightly unhinged, the thing that had gotten her thrown out of two markets and one wedding. "Astrid. You've read the scraped charts. You've felt the grooves. You've spent six years reading coastlines for a man who wanted to own them. If anyone alive can decode a map written as poetry, it's you."

"I'm flattered."

"I'm serious. I've been reading the Codex like a guild member—looking for the wards, the rituals, the things I was trained to see. But you'd read it like a country."

I took the transcription from her that night, in the lee of a fallen sea-wall, and read it by the light of a charm she made from a fish-oil lamp and, she claimed, a stolen prayer. And she was right, damn her. It was a map. It was the best map I'd ever read, because it was a map of a country that had been written to be remembered by people who loved it—every image a landmark, every verse a bearing.

*"Where the bells rang salt"*—a drowned belfry, and I'd seen one, in a vault chart, marked off the headland at Vessing. *"The tide-line remembers"*—the old high-water mark, which on scraped charts showed as a groove in the rock like a scar. *"A chapel that keeps the sea's hours"*—a church flooded at high tide, dry at low. Accessible twice a day, to the minute, to anyone who could read a tide table.

Which I could.

"There," I said, and put my finger on the page, and Felix looked at my finger and then at me with an expression I hadn't seen since the night market, since before the lie, and I felt something in my chest unclench that I hadn't known was clenched.

"Vessing," she said. "Two days further north."

"Two days, and we need to time the tide."

"I know a charm for tides."

"Of course you do."

"It's a good charm."

"I didn't say it wasn't." I lay back against the sea-wall and looked up at the stars, which were enormous out here without the city to crowd them. "Felix."

"Mm."

"Why did you lie? Really. Not the noble version."

A long silence. The sea did its work against the rocks. When she finally spoke, her voice had lost its performance quality entirely, which is how I knew it was true.

"Because the Codex says you'll either save the last dragon-fire or destroy it. Because the moment I read your name I understood that every dangerous person in this country was going to come looking for you, and I thought—if she doesn't know, she can't be made to know, she can be argued out of it, hidden from it. I thought ignorance was armor." A pause. "It was a coward's arithmetic. I counted what the truth would cost you and not what the lie would cost us."

I turned my head and looked at her. She was sitting with her knees up, watching the water like it might offer a better answer than hers.

"The lie cost us three days," I said.

"I know."

"We're not getting those back."

"I know."

"But we're getting the rest." I reached over and took her hand. Her palm was calloused in the places charm-work callouses you—thumb, first two fingers, the burn scar on the heel from the incident with the luck-knot she still refuses to describe. "So here's my arithmetic, Felix Eastwick. You're going to stop deciding things alone. I'm going to stop punishing you for it. And we're going to find out what the book actually wants from me, together, and then we're going to decide whether to give it."

Her fingers closed around mine. "That's a terrible plan," she said, and I could hear the smile in it.

"It's the only one anyone's offered."

* * *

**FELIX**

I want to record, for the sake of honesty, that I slept better that night than I had in weeks, and that I hated myself a little for it. Guilt is supposed to keep you awake. That's the whole point of guilt. But Astrid's hand had gone heavy and warm in mine and the sea had done its patient work and I'd slept like a paid debt.

The third day north, the coast changed. The shingle gave way to black rock scored with grooves—old high-water marks, dozens of them, a staircase of scars climbing the cliffs, each one a century of tide. And the emptiness started to feel less like emptiness and more like absence, which is a different thing. Empty land is just land. Absent land is land where something was taken.

"Look at the walls," Astrid said, near dusk.

I'd walked past them all day—low stone walls running in field-patterns down to the waterline, overgrown, sheepless, pointless. I'd filed them under *old*. Astrid filed them under evidence.

"Field walls mean farms. Farms mean families. Families mean—" She stopped. Started again. "Felix, there were villages here. Whole villages, and nobody in Greyharbor has ever said the word Vellmark to me in my life. Not as history, not as a curse, not as a warning. My grandmother sang me a lullaby once that she said was 'from the old coast,' and when I asked which coast, she looked frightened and never sang it again."

The lullaby. I knew it. Every guild child knew it—*the tide-line remembers, the tide-line keeps, sleep in the salt where the country sleeps*—and we'd been taught it was nonsense, a nonsense rhyme to teach children the guild's caution. It had never occurred to me, until Astrid stood there with her hand on a farmer's wall, that it was a memory. That it had always been a memory.

"We should make camp," I said, because my voice was doing something unreliable.

"Off the beach," she said. "High ground. If Corvane's people are following us, I'd rather see them coming."

"If Corvane's people are following us, we have a bigger problem than sightlines."

"Yes," she said placidly, "but I'd still rather see them coming."

We camped in the ruin of what might have been a chapel—irony noted, filed, ignored—on the cliff-top, and I set the guild's small wards, the fraying ones, the ones Maren had re-woven so many times they were more knot than pattern. They took. Barely. I sat back and watched Astrid watch the horizon, and I thought about the Codex in my pack, and about how the book had said *Astrid of the Tide-Line* a century before Astrid had been born, and how she had come to Greyharbor chasing a dream of a burning door, and how none of that was chance, and how much I suddenly wanted to not think about any of it.

"Felix," she said, without turning. "There's someone on the headland."

I followed her gaze. A figure, small with distance, standing at the tip of the northern headland where the land gave out. Not moving. Not approaching. Just—waiting. Watching us the way you watch a tide you've been told is coming in.

"Patrol?"

"Patrols don't stand still," Astrid said. "And patrols come in pairs. That's one person, and they've been there since the light changed. I checked."

Of course she had. I reached for my charms and found my hands steady, which surprised me. Somewhere between the sea-wall and the chapel ruin, terror had finished hardening into something else. Not courage exactly. Purpose. The guild had spent a century guarding a door out of a country that didn't exist, and here I was, sitting in the ruins of that country, watching someone watch us back.

"They're not inquisitor-grey," Astrid murmured.

"No," I said. "They're not."

The figure raised one arm. Not a threat—a gesture. Deliberate, slow, unmistakable even across the distance.

They pointed north. Then they turned and walked away, over the lip of the headland, gone.

Astrid and I looked at each other in the last of the light.

"Well," she said. "Someone wants us to keep going."

"Someone who knows we're here."

"Someone who isn't hiding it." She was already packing the satchel, quick and certain, and watching her I felt the old warmth come up through the fear like heat through floorboards. "Felix. Whoever that is—they're between us and the chapel. And they wanted us to know it."

I shouldered my pack. The Codex shifted against my spine like something breathing.

"Then I suppose," I said, "we'd better find out what they want before Corvane does."

We walked north into the dark, along the tide-line, which remembered.
