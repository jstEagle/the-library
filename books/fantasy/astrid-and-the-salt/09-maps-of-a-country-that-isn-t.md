## 9. Maps of a Country That Isn't

I have drawn maps of places that don't exist before. Every cartographer has. There's always some merchant who wants his route to look shorter than it is, some minister who wants a coastline moved six miles so the tax boundary favors him, some nobleman who wants his estate drawn larger than his neighbor's. Lying with ink is the oldest trade in Greyharbor, older than charm-selling, and I was good at it.

But I had never drawn a map of a place that had been *made* not to exist.

We were three days north along the drowned coast when I first felt it — the wrongness in the land itself, like a word stuck on the tip of the tongue. Felix walked ahead of me on the shingle road, her coat collar up against the salt wind, and I stopped without meaning to and just stood there, staring at nothing.

"Astrid?" She'd learned my footsteps by then. Three days of walking will do that, even between two people who are still learning how to be honest with each other again.

"Give me a moment."

The coast here was all low cliffs and gray water, the tide out so far that the sea seemed embarrassed by it, exposing mudflats studded with old posts — mooring posts, I realized, for boats, except there was no harbor, no village, no reason for anyone to have driven hundreds of posts into the mud. They marched out toward the horizon in rows, rotting, patient, like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down here and been forgotten.

There should have been a town. I didn't know how I knew it; I knew it the way you know a room is missing its furniture. The shape of the absence was too specific. My hand ached to hold a pen.

"Felix," I said. "What was here?"

She came back to me slowly, and I watched her decide — watched her weigh the truth against the habit of protecting me, and choose the truth, which was new enough between us that I wanted to kiss her for it and also wanted to be angry about every time she hadn't.

"Vellmark," she said. "This whole coast. The Codex says there were eleven towns from here to the tide-line. Harbors, markets, a university that taught navigation by singing to the stars." She said it flatly, the way you recite something you've been told rather than something you've seen. "You won't find any of them on any map. You won't find them in any record. That's what the purge did. It didn't burn the country. It unwrote it."

"You can't unwrite a country."

"No," she agreed. "You can't. But they tried, and the trying left scars, and the scars are the only map left." She looked at the mooring posts, and something crossed her face — grief, or guilt, the particular guilt of someone whose people built the lock. "Show me what you see."

So I took out my field book, because she asked, and because my hands needed something to do, and I stood on the cliff edge and let myself look the way I look at anything I'm about to draw. Not with my eyes. With the part of me that finds the bones of a landscape under its skin.

And the bones were *there*.

It's hard to explain to someone who doesn't draw. When I map a city, I don't start with streets. I start with pressure — where the weight of the place settles, where the river wants to go, where the ground rises to meet a bridge that hasn't been built yet. A city has a skeleton, and buildings are just the flesh hung on it. Flesh rots. Skeletons stay.

The skeleton of Vellmark stayed.

I could feel the harbor basin under the mudflats, its curve as clean as a drawn arc. I could feel the road leaving it, climbing the bluff in switchbacks because whoever laid it out had refused to cut the slope and had instead made the road negotiate with the hill, politely, the way old roads do. I could feel where the market square would have been — a widening in the road pattern, a place where five paths met and none of them quite went straight through, because markets make paths shy around them. And there, on the highest point inland, a knot of density that could only be a church or a keep, something heavy, something that pulled the surrounding lanes toward it like a stone pulls a blanket.

I drew it. Fast, messy, the way I sketch when the thing is coming through me faster than craft can polish it. Harbor, road, market, keep. Eleven towns' worth of ghost-lines, if I followed the coast far enough, and I did follow it, walking and stopping and drawing while Felix watched me with an expression I couldn't name until later, when I understood it was awe mixed with fear — the fear of watching someone do exactly what the prophecy said she would do.

When I finally looked up, the light was going gold and my pages were full of a country that officially had never existed.

"That's Brantvik," Felix said quietly, pointing at my harbor. "My grandmother used to sing about Brantvik. I thought it was a nonsense word. A made-up place in a lullaby." Her voice caught on the last part. "You just drew it."

* * *

That night we sheltered in the ruin of a shepherd's hut, one of the few structures on this stretch of coast old enough to predate the purge — or perhaps simply too insignificant to bother erasing. The purge, I was learning, had been thorough but not omniscient. It had gone after records, monuments, gravestones, deeds. It had not bothered with a hut where nobody important had ever slept.

Felix built a small fire, shielded behind a curve of wall, and I sat close to it and closer to her, our shoulders touching, and I asked her about the songs.

"My grandmother raised me," she said. "In the Tidewards, the poor quarter. She sang constantly. Work songs, mostly, and half of them were in a language she called 'the old talk,' though she'd laugh if you asked her to translate. She said she didn't know it properly, just the sounds her own mother had made." Felix poked the fire. "One song had a refrain — *over the salt, over the salt, the door remembers what the map forgets.* I sang it as a child thinking it was about fish. Or God. Children don't ask; they just carry things."

"And now?"

"Now I know it's a set of directions." She almost smiled. "The guild has fragments. Songs, mostly. When the purge burned the archives, the songs survived because songs live in mouths, not paper. You can't confiscate a melody. So the elders spent a hundred years collecting them — buying rounds of drinks in dockside taverns, trading charms for verses, writing down whatever grandmothers remembered. Half the Codex's geography comes from nursery rhymes. The fate of the last dragon-fire, hidden in counting songs and lullabies, because no inquisitor thinks to arrest a woman for singing her baby to sleep."

I thought about that. I thought about all the songs I'd absorbed as a child in my mother's house, the ones with words nobody explained — the green door, the sister under the hill, the country past the tide. I had assumed they were the same nonsense every family invents.

"Felix. Sing me one. One of your grandmother's."

She hesitated, then sang, low, barely above the fire-crackle. *Over the salt, over the salt, the door remembers what the map forgets. Nine daughters washed the harbor stones, the tenth came home with wet sleeves yet.*

"It's a tide song," she said. "A work rhythm for hauling nets. But 'nine daughters' — the guild has nine founding names. And 'wet sleeves yet' — Maren thinks it means the Door itself, standing in the water, still wet after a century."

I lay back against the cold wall and stared at the dark beyond the firelight, and the song worked in me the way songs do, settling into the same place where my dreams of the burning door lived. Because here is what I haven't told Felix yet, what I've been carrying since the night market, since before: I grew up on those songs too. Different words, maybe, different tune, but the same bones underneath. The green door. The country past the tide. My mother sang them, and my mother's family came from somewhere she never named, somewhere she went quiet about whenever I asked, the way people go quiet about a wound.

I had chased a dream to Greyharbor. I had told myself it was ambition, a cartographer's assistant wanting more than copying other men's charts. But it wasn't. It was homesickness for a home I'd never seen, and I think — I think the purge reached further than maps. I think it reached into families, into memory, into the parts of us that know things we can't say we know. My mother hummed a drowned country to sleep in me, and I've been walking toward it my whole life without knowing the address.

"Do you want to hear something terrible?" I said.

"I want to hear everything you've decided to tell me. That's the arrangement now."

"When I drew Brantvik today — the harbor, the roads — it didn't feel like discovery. It felt like *recognition*. Like I was copying down something I already had. Like the map was already in me and I was just being allowed to see it." I turned my hands over, palms up, empty of everything but calluses from the pen. "Felix, I want it back. Not the flame, not the prophecy, not any of it. The *country*. The streets. The market where five paths meet. I want to walk down a road that negotiates with a hill, in a town that has a name, and I want the name to be mine somehow. Is that terrible? Everyone says the dead should rest and erased things should stay erased, and all I can think is that I've been erased too, my whole life, everyone has, and I am so tired of pretending the emptiness is normal."

The fire popped. Felix reached over and took my hand, and her palm was warm and slightly ink-stained the way mine always is, and she didn't say *you're right* or *you're wrong* or *it's dangerous to want things*. She said:

"My grandmother's last words were in the old talk. Nobody in the room understood them. I've spent ten years wondering what she said." She squeezed my fingers. "Maybe we'll find someone up the coast who does."

* * *

The village, when we reached it the next afternoon, was called Saltmere on the current maps, and it was the kind of place that exists to be passed through — a single street, a chapel with a leaning spire, an inn with a sign showing a fish that had been painted by someone who had clearly never seen a fish. Forty houses, maybe fifty. The kind of village where strangers are noticed before they finish arriving.

But it sat inside one of my ghost-towns. I'd drawn the outline that morning from the ridge above: another harbor basin, another market-widening, another pull toward high ground. Saltmere's street ran exactly along one of my predicted lanes. Its chapel stood on my knot of density. Whoever founded Saltmere had built inside the skeleton of the erased town without knowing it, the way a bird builds a nest in the ribs of a whale.

The innkeeper was a broad woman named Doss who took our coin without curiosity and fed us barley stew without comment, and it was only when Felix, testing, hummed four notes of the tide song that the room changed.

Doss's hands stopped moving. An old man by the fire lifted his head. A girl stacking bowls went still with her arms full.

"Where'd you get that?" Doss asked. Not hostile. Hungry. That was the thing I noticed — hungry, the way you get hungry for a food you ate as a child and can't find anywhere anymore.

"My grandmother," Felix said. "From the Tidewards, in Greyharbor."

"Huh." Doss set down the pot. "My mother sang that. Said her mother sang it hauling nets, back when there were nets worth hauling." She wiped her hands on her apron, and I watched her wrestle with something — embarrassment, maybe, or the fear of seeming foolish. "We sing them here. The old ones. Won't mean much to you. Just sounds. But there's a tune we sing at midwinter that my gran swore was a *map*, if you had ears for it. She said the words got scrubbed but the tune couldn't be, because tunes live in the body." She shrugged, self-conscious. "Silly, I expect."

"It's not silly," I said, and my voice came out stranger than I intended, and Doss looked at me with sudden attention, the way you look at someone who has just confirmed something you've suspected alone for years.

The old man by the fire spoke up. His name was Perren, and he had a fisherman's knotted hands and eyes the color of the mudflats. "My grandfather said there was a town here before Saltmere. Bigger. Stone quays, he said, and a bell tower, and the bell rang the tides." He tapped his temple. "Everyone said he was addled. But when I dig — I mend walls, and I dig post-holes — I find dressed stone down deep. Cut blocks. No mason in Saltmere works stone like that. Hasn't the skill. I find them and I use them and I don't ask."

"Does anyone remember the town's name?" Felix asked.

Perren was quiet a long moment. Then: "There's a word. We say it to quiet children. When a little one cries at night, we say *hush, or Marrowgate will hear you.*" He smiled, apologetic. "Nonsense, of course. A bogey-word. Except—" and here he paused, and I saw him decide, as Felix had decided on the cliff, to set down the thing he usually kept hidden, "—except my grandfather said it wasn't a warning. He said it was a *greeting*. He said the old folk called the town Marrowgate the way you'd call a friend by name, and after the soldiers came, the name curdled, the way a name does when the person dies. You stop saying it kindly. You start saying it to frighten. That's how grief works in a mouth."

Marrowgate. I wrote it in my field book under my sketch of the harbor, and my pen pressed hard enough to nearly tear the page, because a name is a resurrection, however small, however secondhand. A town that has a name can be found again. A town that is only an absence stays lost forever.

That evening, Doss brought out a jug of cider and the whole room sang, awkwardly, self-mockingly, the songs they claimed meant nothing — and I sat with my book open and transcribed melodies the way I transcribe coastlines, noting intervals, repetitions, places where the tune bent around an absent word the way a stream bends around a stone. Three of the songs, when I laid the transcriptions side by side, shared a melodic phrase that appeared at moments of naming — when a verse mentioned the sea, the door, the daughters. A signature. A watermark. The same phrase, in villages a hundred miles apart, surviving in bodies that had forgotten why.

The purge had taken the words. It had not gotten the music. And music, I realized, sitting in that smoky room with cider in my blood and ink on my fingers, is a kind of map. Maybe the oldest kind. You can burn an archive. You cannot burn what a grandmother puts into a child's ear, note by note, generation by generation, a smuggled cargo crossing a century of silence.

They unwrote a country, and it survived anyway — in dressed stones reused in pig walls, in bogey-words that were once greetings, in tunes that bend around absent names. It survived the way water survives being dammed: patiently, finding every crack.

I wanted it back. Sitting there, I understood that I had always wanted it back, that every blank space I'd ever filled on a chart was practice for this, that my whole life had been the long approach march to a door I'd dreamed of burning. Wonder and horror in equal measure — wonder at how much had endured, horror at how much hadn't, at the dressed stones in the pig wall and the greeting turned to threat and the eleven towns reduced to forty-house villages huddled in their skeletons like survivors in a wreck's lifeboat.

Felix caught my eye across the room during the third song, and neither of us smiled, exactly. It was more solemn than smiling. Two people recognizing that they're on the same errand.

* * *

We left Saltmere at dawn, and Doss pressed a loaf of bread and a warning on us: "Roads north wash out past the point. And there's folk asking questions — a gentleman in a fine coat came through two days back, showing a portrait, asking after two women traveling together." She didn't say the word *inquisitor*. She didn't have to.

We took the cliff path instead, and near midday I stopped again — the third time on the journey, and Felix no longer needed to ask. She just waited, patient, while I stood at the edge and read the land.

This time the reading was different. This time, as I let my sight sink beneath the skin of the world, I felt something answer.

Not imagination. I know imagination; I live in it professionally. This was pressure against pressure, like pressing your palm to a pane of glass and feeling, impossibly, a palm press back. The ghost-shapes I mapped were lines on paper. This was warm. This was *aware*. Somewhere under the mud and the forgetting, something vast and slow had noticed me noticing it, and it leaned toward me the way a sleeper leans toward a voice saying their name.

The lantern, I thought. The last dragon-fire. Held, waiting, a hundred years in the dark, and here was a daughter of the scattered songs standing on its doorstep reading its address aloud.

My knees actually buckled. Felix caught my arm.

"What is it? What did you feel?"

"It felt me back," I said, and heard my own voice shake. "Felix, it's not sleeping. It's *waiting*. All this time I thought we were following the Codex north. We're not." I looked at the horizon, where the coast curved toward the tide-line and the light lay on the water like a struck match. "It's been following me."

That night, camped in the lee of the cliffs, I dreamed the dream again — the burning door, the salt water, the voice like a banked fire saying a word I couldn't hold onto. But this time the dream had a detail it had never had before, and I woke with it sharp as a splinter behind my eyes:

A bell tower, ringing the tides.

And under the ringing, faint but unmistakable, a woman's voice singing the watermark phrase — the one from Saltmere — but with *words* attached, old words, in the old talk, and though I didn't know their meaning, I knew — the way you know your own name — that they were an instruction, and that when I finally understood them, the door would open.

I woke Felix with my shaking. She sat up instantly, alert the way she always is, and listened, and when I finished she was silent a long time.

"There's a verse Maren could never place," she said finally. "A fragment the elders have carried for sixty years. Four words in the old talk, no translation. They call it the Key-Verses. They've tried every scholar, every codebreaker." She looked at me across the dying fire, and her face held something I'd never seen on it before — hope with terror standing just behind it, holding its coat. "Astrid. If the songs gave you the tune, and the land gives you the lines, and the dream gives you the words—"

"Then I'm not reading the map," I said. "I'm finishing it."

The tide was coming in. Far below our camp, the sea crept up the flats and swallowed the mooring posts one by one, and somewhere under that gray water, a drowned chapel waited with a ward keyed to guild blood, and a bell that remembered how to ring.
