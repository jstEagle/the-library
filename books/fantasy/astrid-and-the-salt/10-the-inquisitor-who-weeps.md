## 10. The Inquisitor Who Weeps

I want it on the record that I did not scream when the man stepped out of the fog. I made a sound, yes—a small, dignified noise, like a kettle deciding against boiling—but I did not scream, and in the circumstances I think history should be generous about that.

We had made camp in the lee of a collapsed sea-wall, forty miles north of Greyharbor, where the drowned coast does its best impression of nowhere. Astrid was asleep with her head on her pack and her boots still on, the way she sleeps when she's pretending she isn't exhausted, which is to say always. I was on watch, feeding a fire the size of a coin, because we'd agreed that warmth was worth the risk of being seen and I'd lost the argument by not having a better idea.

The fog came in off the flats the way it always does here—thick, patient, smelling of old iron—and then it produced an Inquisitor.

He wasn't wearing the gray-and-gold. That was the first strange thing. He stood there in a fisherman's oilskin two sizes too big for him, hood back, hands empty and held slightly away from his body in the universal gesture of *I am not about to do the thing you're afraid of*, which, coming from an Inquisitor of the Ministry of Contained Arts, is roughly as reassuring as a wolf showing you its teeth are pulled.

"Inquisitor Hale," I said. My hand found the knife at my belt without consulting me. "You're out of uniform."

"The uniform drowns," he said. "If you go in the water in gray wool, you sink. I've seen it."

"Is that a threat?"

"It's a fact." He crouched down slowly, the way you approach a dog that's been kicked, and looked at our fire instead of at me. Up close, without the coat and the rank-pins, he was younger than I'd thought. Late twenties. There was sand dried white in the creases of his knuckles, and under his eyes were shadows the color of bruised plums, the kind you get from riding hard and sleeping worse. "May I sit? I've been walking since the tide turned."

"You've been following us since Greyharbor," I said. "Since before Greyharbor. You were at the night market the night Astrid bought the compass rose charm. You had a different face then."

"Glamour," he said, with a shrug that managed to be apologetic. "It's cheap work. Any hedge-witch can do it. We requisition them from the evidence vaults." Something crossed his face when he said *evidence vaults*—a flicker, quickly smothered, like a moth against glass. "The charm worked, by the way. The one your guild sold her. It's why I found you so fast. Ministry tracking charms point at each other like magnets. Yours pointed away from everything else. Very distinctive craftsmanship."

"You're complimenting my forgery skills while threatening me."

"I'm not threatening you." He said it quietly, and the terrible thing—the thing I would turn over for weeks afterward—was that I believed him. "If I were threatening you, you'd already be in irons. There are four more of me within a day's ride. I told them the trail went cold at the salt marshes. They believe me because I'm good at my job, and because nobody wants to walk the flats in this weather."

Astrid stirred behind me. I put my body between her and him without thinking about it, and something in his expression shifted—not offense, not amusement. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man watching someone do a thing he understood from the inside.

"Felix Eastwick," he said. "Charm-seller. Ninth mark of the Guild of Small Mercies. Apprentice to Elder Maren Voss, who taught you to cut wards with your left hand because your right shakes when you're frightened." He paused. "Your right hand is shaking now."

I looked at it. Traitor.

"What do you want?" I said.

"To talk. Ten minutes. Then I'll walk north until you can't see me anymore, and you'll never see me again unless you make a very large mistake." He settled onto a fallen slab of the sea-wall, oilskin pooling around him, and held his palms toward our tiny fire like a man warming himself at the last fire in the world. "Ask me anything. You won't get better answers from anyone else in gray."

Behind me, Astrid sat up. I heard the soft sounds of her waking—breath, the rustle of the blanket, the particular silence of a person going very still while they take inventory. She didn't speak. She just moved to sit beside me, shoulder against mine, and looked at Hale with the flat, measuring stare she used on bad maps.

"You're the one who searched my master's office," she said.

"I am."

"You read my letters."

"I read everyone's letters. It's less personal than it sounds." He rubbed his jaw. "Mostly it's people writing to their mothers about money."

"Why are you here?" Astrid asked. "Really."

Hale was quiet for a long moment. The tide breathed somewhere below us in the dark, in and out, in and out, the whole coast rising and falling like something asleep.

"When I was six years old," he said, "my grandmother used to sing me to sleep. Not Ministry hymns. Not the Anthem of the Unified Coast. A song in no language I knew, about a girl who goes down to the water and calls the boats home, and the boats come up out of the sea with lanterns burning, though there's no one aboard." His voice stayed level, but his hands had gone still around his knees, gripping. "She died when I was nine. Before that, she made me promise never to sing it where anyone could hear. She said if I sang it where anyone could hear, men would come, and they wouldn't be kind, and afterward I'd wish I'd never learned the words."

"And then you became one of the men who come," I said.

"Yes." No flinch. No excuse. Just the word, set down on the ground between us like a stone. "I became one of the men who come. Because the men who come have bread, and beds, and a pension, and because a boy who sings songs in dead languages learns quickly which side of the door keeps him fed." He looked up at me, and his eyes were wet, and he did nothing whatsoever to hide it, which was somehow worse than tears. "Three days ago I stood in Minister Corvane's office while he opened a case of confiscated charms and ate one. A luck-charm. A little thing, tin and river-glass, the kind your guild sells dockworkers for two coppers. He swallowed it like a sweet and the room got warmer and he smiled, and I stood at attention, and I thought: *my grandmother sang me a song about boats coming home with lanterns burning, and I am standing at attention for a man who eats other people's luck.*"

Nobody spoke. The fire popped. Somewhere out on the flats, a bird made a sound like a hinge.

"So I'm going to tell you what happens next," Hale said, "and then I'm going to keep my word and walk north, and you're going to pretend you never saw me weep, because I'll deny it, and we'll both be happier."

"We haven't agreed to anything," I said.

"No. But you're going to listen, because the alternative is finding out the hard way, and I've watched people find things out the hard way, and it's ugly." He leaned forward. The firelight caught the wet on his cheeks and made it shine. "Corvane knows where you are. He has known since the marshes—he doesn't need trackers, he has the confiscated scrying-mirrors, and one of them remembers your guild mark, Felix, because your guild's wards were catalogued sixty years ago when the Ministry seized the Codex transcription rights. Everything you've done since Greyharbor, he's watched through glass. He let me chase you because chasing is cheaper than marching, and because he wanted to see what you'd do when cornered. He wanted data." Hale's mouth twisted around the word like it was rotten. "But the data's come back wrong. You didn't run for the border. You didn't sell the Codex page. You went *north*. Along the tide-line. And Corvane grew up in a Ministry school like I did, and he knows exactly what lies along the tide-line, and it has made him afraid, and Corvane afraid is Corvane coming personally, with the full vault, with every stolen thing he's eaten in twenty years, and he will be here in three days."

"How do you know all this?" Astrid asked.

"Because he told me to prepare the escort. Because he dictates his correspondence aloud while I take notes, because he thinks clerks are furniture." Hale laughed once, short and awful. "Because I have spent eleven years being furniture, and furniture hears everything."

I looked at Astrid. She was watching Hale with an expression I couldn't fully read—pity, maybe, or its close cousin, recognition. She'd grown up on stories too; she'd told me that much on the road, in the fragments she allowed herself. A grandmother's voice. A song about a door.

"If he's three days out," I said slowly, "why warn us? Why burn yourself? If Corvane finds out you tipped us—"

"He won't find out. That's the point of walking north and never seeing you again." Hale stood, joints cracking, suddenly looking every hour of his tired age. "And if he does find out—" He shrugged inside the oilskin. "Then I'll be a traitor to the Ministry, and I'll finally have committed to something."

"That's not a plan," I said. "That's a mood."

"I'm aware." For the first time, he almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, crooked and grief-shaped, and it made him look about nine years old. "I'm an Inquisitor, Mistress Eastwick. Moods are the only plan I've ever been issued."

* * *

He didn't leave immediately. That was the part I hadn't expected—the lingering. He crouched by our fire and asked, in a voice carefully stripped of authority, whether he could see the Codex page.

I said no.

He nodded like he'd expected that too. "Then describe it to me. Just—the geography. The part about the chapel."

"Why?"

"Because my grandmother drew a picture once. When she was dying. She drew a chapel on a shore and made me memorize it and then burned the drawing, and I have spent twenty years wondering if I invented it." His voice cracked on the last word, barely, a hairline fracture. "I need to know if I invented it."

So I told him. Not everything—I kept back the ward, the blood-key, the specifics Maren had sworn us to—but I gave him the shape of it: the drowned chapel, the bell, the shore that appears only at low spring tides, the door beneath the altar. And as I spoke, Hale closed his eyes, and his lips moved, following along, and I realized with a lurch that he was mouthing the words *before* I said them.

He knew it. All of it. Every landmark, every turn of the tide-line, in order, like a prayer.

"She taught you the route," Astrid said softly. "Your grandmother. She taught you how to get home."

"She taught me a bedtime walk," Hale said. His eyes were open now, fixed on the fire. "She called it a bedtime walk. Down past the white stones, past the chapel, ring the bell twice, mind the mud. I thought it was a game. I thought—" He stopped. Started again. "There were eight hundred children in my Ministry school year. Do you know how many of us had grandmothers who sang in dead languages? How many of us hummed tunes we weren't supposed to know, in dormitories after lights-out, passing them along like contraband candy? Half. More than half. Nobody questioned it, because the songs sounded like nonsense, like gull-cries, like nothing." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, rough, angry at himself. "They weren't nonsense. They were maps. An entire country, folded up small enough to fit inside lullabies, and hidden in the one place the Ministry never thought to search, because the Ministry doesn't believe children remember anything worth keeping."

The tide turned below us. I felt it rather than heard it—that faint slackening in the air, the sea drawing breath to come in.

"They'll remember," Astrid said. "When Vellmark comes back. The songs will un-fold. Everyone who ever hummed one will suddenly know the words mean something."

"Yes," Hale said. "And that is precisely why Corvane is coming personally, with the vault, and why he will burn every archive, every school record, every grandmother's grave he can find, if you fail." He stood. Brushed off the oilskin with brisk, soldierly motions, as if he could tidy the last ten minutes off himself. "So don't fail. I don't say that as an Inquisitor. I say it as a man who would like, someday, to sing in public."

* * *

At the edge of the firelight he stopped, half-turned back into the dark, and delivered his last words over his shoulder like a man tossing something heavy off a bridge.

"Astrid of the Tide-Line." He used her full name, the Codex name, and I felt Astrid go rigid beside me. "Corvane wants you alive. Remember that. Whatever else happens—whatever he offers you, whatever he threatens—he needs you breathing and he needs you willing, because the Door answers to the keeper, not to force. That's your leverage. Don't spend it cheaply." A pause. "And Felix Eastwick—if you get the chance to save her or save the flame, and you hesitate even one second trying to find a way to do both, Corvane will make the choice for you, and he will choose wrong, on purpose, because a broken keeper is easier to carry than a whole one."

"That's cheerful," I managed.

"I'm an Inquisitor," he said. "Cheerful is above my pay grade."

Then the fog took him. One moment the shape of him, big and tired and honest in a way that hurt to look at; the next, only the flats and the dark and the sound of boots on wet stone, going north, keeping his word.

* * *

Astrid didn't sleep again. Neither did I. We sat by the coin-sized fire while the tide climbed the flats below, and after a long time she said, without preamble:

"He cried."

"Yes."

"An Inquisitor of the Ministry of Contained Arts sat in our camp and cried about a lullaby."

"Yes."

"And he let us go." She turned her hands toward the fire, studying them as if they belonged to someone else. "Felix. Half the country's been humming Vellmark this whole time. Grandmothers, schoolchildren, *clerks*. It was never erased. It was just—hidden in plain sight, in the smallest voices, waiting." Her eyes were shining in the firelight, and her voice had gone low and fierce, the voice she used for coastlines. "Do you understand what that means? It means the country never left. It means it's been *waiting for us*."

"It also means Corvane arrives in three days with a vault full of eaten miracles," I said, "and a man who cries is the only reason we know."

"Both things are true."

"Both things are always true. That's the trouble with the world."

She laughed—short, surprised, real—and leaned her head against my shoulder, and for a while neither of us said anything, and the fire burned down to its last copper coin of light.

But I didn't sleep, because Hale's last warning had lodged itself under my ribs like a splinter: *save her or save the flame, and don't hesitate.* I lay awake doing the arithmetic I'd been refusing to do since the night Maren opened the Codex, and the arithmetic kept coming out the same ugly way, and every path through it seemed to end with either Astrid or the world minus one of them, and I was not built for that equation. I was built for selling luck to dockworkers. Two coppers, tin and river-glass, small mercies only.

Toward dawn, Astrid finally drifted off against my arm, and I sat watching the east go gray, and I noticed two things.

First: the fog was lifting, and far up the beach, at the exact spot where Hale had vanished, the sand was printed with boot-prints that walked north and simply stopped, mid-stride, as if the man had stepped out of the world entirely—which, given the glamour-work the Ministry requisitions from its evidence vaults, perhaps he had.

Second: Astrid's pack lay open beside her, and sticking out of it, catching the first light, was a scrap of paper I had never seen before. Cream-colored. Expensive. Ministry stock, with the gray-and-gold watermark ghosting through it, and a seal half-broken at the fold.

Someone had left her a letter.

And the handwriting on it was fine, precise, and terribly familiar—because I had spent eleven years taking dictation in that hand, and I knew it the way you know a knife by the scar it left.

It was addressed to her. And it was signed, in elegant black ink: *E. Corvane.*
