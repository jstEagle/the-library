## 11. Corvane's Offer

I should have known something was wrong when the tide came in without any gulls.

The coast north of Greyharbor had been loud with them for three days—wheeling, screaming, dropping shells on the rocks like small vindictive gods. Then, on the morning we reached the drowned chapel's estuary, nothing. Just water sliding in over the flats, gray and smooth as poured pewter, and a silence that felt less like absence and more like attention.

Felix went down to the waterline to test the ward-stones, her sleeves rolled up, guild mark hidden under a wrap of linen she still wouldn't explain. I stayed with the cart. That was the arrangement: Felix handled magic, I handled maps, and neither of us mentioned the scrap of Codex transcription I'd found in her coat pocket two weeks ago, or the cold night that followed it, or the careful, exhausted way we'd started rebuilding trust on the road north—one honest sentence at a time, laid down like flagstones over deep water.

So when the carriage came up the coast road, I did what any sensible person would do. I stood very still and hoped it would pass.

It didn't pass. It stopped.

Two horses, black as wet slate, and a driver who never turned his head, and from the far door stepped a man in a coat the color of dried blood, cut so well it looked less tailored than *grown*. Minister Corvane. I'd seen him twice before, both times across the width of a vault, both times at a distance calculated to remind you he was a minister and you were furniture. Up close he was smaller than his reputation and larger than his portrait, which is a trick I've never understood and suspect involves magic of a kind no one confiscates.

"Astrid Vell," he said.

My whole body went cold, and not from the sea wind.

Not Astrid of Greyharbor. Not the cartographer's assistant, no family name on record because no family name existed anymore—not since the purge, not since the maps were redrawn and the archives burned and an entire country folded away behind a door that officially had never been built. My name. The one my grandmother whispered into my hair when she thought I was asleep, the one that meant *of the tide-line*, the one I'd been told all my life was dangerous to say aloud even in a whisper.

"You knew," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved.

"I make it my business to know what people are called before they're called anything." He smiled. It was a good smile. That was the worst part—it wasn't a predator's smile or a bureaucrat's smile, it was a warm one, the kind you'd trust with your luggage. "Your grandmother fought hard to keep it off the registers. Fought hard generally. You have her jaw."

"You didn't know my grandmother."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "I unmade her. Or rather, my predecessors did, and I inherited the work, the way one inherits a house with stains in the carpet." He gestured toward the flats with a hand wearing three rings, each stone dark as a closed eye. "Walk with me, Astrid Vell. Ten minutes. I promise you'll want every one of them."

Every instinct I owned said run. But running would bring Felix back up from the waterline, and whatever Corvane wanted, he clearly wanted it without witnesses—and if it came to violence, I'd rather it found me alone than found her standing between me and it.

So I walked.

* * *

He didn't take me toward the water. He took me along the ridge above the estuary, where the gorse grew in wind-flattened hedges and you could see the whole drowned coast spread out below—the mudflats, the ghost-lines of old field boundaries showing through the eelgrass, the places where, if you knew how to look, a country was still written in negative space.

"You've been reading the land," he observed. "Good. Your grandmother read it the same way. She could walk a shoreline and tell you where a village had been by the way the grass sulked."

"Is there a point to this flattery?"

"There is always a point to flattery. The question is whether it's aimed at you or at me." He stopped walking and turned to face me, and for a moment the warmth dropped out of his face entirely, and what was underneath was older and hungrier and very, very patient. "You've seen my collection."

"The vault. Yes."

"Then you know I don't destroy magic. I *keep* it. Every confiscated charm, every bound spirit, every heirloom hex the Ministry pried out of some widow's hands—I keep them safe. Safe from decay, safe from misuse, safe from the sentimental fools who think power belongs in the hands of whoever happens to be born holding it." He touched one of the dark rings absently, the way a man touches a rosary. "Do you know what happens to magic nobody keeps? It dies. Or worse—it wanders. Magic wants a keeper, Astrid. It has always wanted a keeper. The only question history ever asks is: who is willing to do the keeping?"

"And you volunteered."

"I was *appointed*," he said, with genuine indignation, and that flash of pettiness was somehow more frightening than everything else. "There is a difference."

Below us, tiny and distant, Felix moved along the waterline among the ward-stones, her head bent, her hands moving in the small precise gestures of someone coaxing a stubborn lock. I watched her longer than I meant to. Corvane noticed me noticing. Of course he did.

"The Salt Codex," he said.

And there it was.

"Its location is unknown to the Ministry," he continued, mild as milk. "Its contents are known to me in summary. A book of prophecies concerning the last dragon-fire, the Door of Vellmark, and—" he paused, delicately, "—a woman of the tide-line who will either carry the flame through the door or let it gutter forever. Prophecy is such imprecise instruments. So much depends on interpretation. On *incentive*."

"You want me to steal it."

"I want you to *bring* it to me, and I want to be very clear about the distinction, because the distinction is the entire offer." He drew something from inside his coat—a document, heavy paper, sealed with the Ministerial crest in wax so dark it looked like a wound. "This is a restoration decree. Full pardon of bloodline. The name Vell entered back into the civil rolls, the lands at the tide-line regranted, the archive reconstructed from Ministry records—which, I assure you, were never actually burned. We burned *copies*. One does not burn the only inventory of what one has stolen."

The paper sat in his hand between us, and I hated myself, honestly and completely, because my first feeling wasn't outrage. It was hunger.

I thought of my grandmother teaching me letters from a book she kept wrapped in oilcloth under a floorboard, pausing at every page to check the window. I thought of my mother, who never once said our name aloud in her entire adult life, who introduced herself as *the widow Sennet* until the day she died, and died still performing the lie. I thought of every form I'd ever filled out with a blank where the family line should be, every clerk's raised eyebrow, every time some official had squinted at me and asked, *and your people?* and I'd learned to shrug like a woman with no past, because a woman with no past couldn't be prosecuted for having one.

A name. A country's worth of name, handed back, signed and sealed, legitimate. All I had to do was hand over a book and abandon a woman I—

I stopped there. Corvane watched me stop there, and smiled his warm smile, and gave me room to finish the thought myself, which is the cruelest thing a negotiator can do.

"She'll lie to you again," he said quietly. "She already has. You found the transcription. You know what the Codex says about you—keeper or killer, flame or flood—and you know she read it weeks ago and chose to let you walk north beside her without telling you that your entire life was somebody else's footnote. That isn't love, Astrid. That's *custody*. Her guild has spent a century deciding things on behalf of everyone they claim to protect. They locked away a country, if my sources are accurate, and called it guardianship. What do you imagine they'll call it, locking away a person?"

"They didn't lock anything away," I said. "That's your predecessors' work."

"My predecessors' work, executed by their founders' hands, maintained by their wards, guarded by their bloodline for a hundred years." He shrugged elegantly. "Guilt is a shared meal. Everyone eats."

The tide was coming in below. I could see it taking the flats inch by inch, erasing Felix's footprints behind her as fast as she made them, and there was something unbearable about that image that I refused to examine.

"If I refuse?" I asked.

"Then nothing happens today. I am not a thug; thugs are a budgetary line item and I resent the expense." He tucked the decree back inside his coat, unhurried. "But I will tell you what happens eventually, so that your refusal is an informed one. The Ministry knows the Door's general vicinity now—we have known for months. Inquisitor Hale's reports are thorough, if sentimental. When we come, we will come in force, and we will open that door whether or not anyone cuts their palm for us, and everything behind it—the fire, the country, the held hour, whatever survives of your grandmother's world—will be catalogued, confiscated, and *kept*. By me. Forever. That is the future you purchase by refusing me. I'm offering you the chance to shape it. Refuse, and you merely get to watch it happen to you."

"And if I accept? If I bring you the Codex?"

"Then the restoration stands, the flame comes under Ministry protection—" he said the word *protection* the way other men say *possession*, without any apparent awareness of the overlap, "—and the woman Eastwick goes free. I have no quarrel with her personally. Guilds are structures; structures fall on their own. She's welcome to sell her little charms in Greyharbor until the end of her days, provided she sells them nowhere near me."

No quarrel with her personally. As if Felix were a weather system. As if the last three months—the night market, the salt-bread, the way she hummed when she worked a charm and pretended she didn't—were a jurisdictional matter.

I should have told him no right then. Cleanly, immediately, with the whole spine of me.

Here is what I actually said:

"How do I know the decree is real?"

Corvane's smile widened by exactly one degree. He'd been waiting for that question. He'd probably rehearsed my hesitation in front of a mirror, and the fact that he'd guessed me correctly made me want to break something.

"Because I signed it," he said, "and I have never once broken a signed agreement. Ask anyone I've ruined—they'll confirm I honored every term to the letter. I am many terrible things, Astrid Vell, but I am not a liar. Liars require imagination. I simply keep accounts." He pressed the decree into my hand before I could decide whether to take it, folding my fingers over the wax seal with his own, and his rings were cold as harbor water. "Three days. The chapel opens at the next high spring tide—that much even your charming guild doesn't fully understand yet, though they will. Bring me the Codex before the door opens, or don't. Either way, I'll see you on the other side of it."

He walked back toward the carriage. At the door he paused, without turning around.

"For what it's worth—and it isn't worth much—your grandmother was offered this same choice. Different terms, same shape. She chose the country over the name." He opened the door. "I've often wondered, these hundred years, whether she regretted it. Do give my regards to Eastwick. Tell her the candles burn shorter than she thinks."

The carriage rolled away south along the coast road, and the gulls came back all at once, screaming, as if they'd been holding their breath.

* * *

I stood on the ridge until the sound of the wheels faded, holding the decree the way you hold something that might be a gift or might be a lit fuse.

Restoration of bloodline. Lands at the tide-line. My mother's real name, carved back into the world's records, un-erased. Everything my family had bled a century to preserve in whispers, offered to me on one sheet of paper by the man whose office had done the erasing—and all it would cost was a book, a flame, and the one person in Greyharbor who looked at me like I was a person instead of a blank field on a form.

I thought about saying nothing. About burning the decree on the gorse and going down to the waterline and helping Felix with the ward-stones and carrying the secret the way she'd carried hers—badly, but alone.

Instead, when she climbed up the path toward me, hair wind-tangled, palms nicked from the stones, face opening into that cautious almost-smile she'd been relearning how to give me—

I held out the paper.

"Felix," I said. "We need to talk. And this time *you're* not allowed to lie first."

She looked at the seal. I watched her recognize it. I watched the color leave her face in the exact order that hope leaves a room.

And far to the south, faint on the wind, I could have sworn I heard bells—inquisitors' bells, the kind they ring when a hunt stops searching and starts closing.

Three days. High spring tide. And somewhere behind us, Corvane was already coming to collect on every answer at once.
