## 12. The Drowned Chapel

The tide came in like a debt collector.

We'd been walking the drowned coast for six days by then, following the Codex's geography the way you'd follow a rumor—half faith, half stubbornness. Astrid read the land the way other people read letters from home, tracing shorelines that weren't there anymore, naming villages that had been salt for a hundred years. *Vellmark was here.* *The road bent here.* *There was a bell tower, once; my grandmother sang about it.*

I mostly carried things and made jokes. Between us we were almost one competent person.

It was Astrid who stopped first, on the morning of the sixth day, at a stretch of coast where the cliffs folded inward like two hands cupped around something they didn't want to drop.

"Here," she said.

"Here's where we die of exposure? Because I had money on further north."

"Felix." She wasn't smiling. She was holding the Codex transcription against her chest with both hands, and the wind was pulling her hair across her face, and she looked—gods help me—she looked like something out of her own maps. Certain. Drawn in ink that wouldn't smudge. "The chapel. It's here."

I looked where she was looking and saw water. Grey-green, patient, lapping at black rock. The kind of water that has drowned things before and isn't embarrassed about it.

"The Codex says the Door is marked by a chapel," she went on, in the tone of someone reciting scripture to an atheist. "Submerged at high tide. Visible at low. We came at the wrong hour, that's all. We wait for the tide to turn."

"And then?"

"And then you do whatever it is your guild does." She said it lightly. Too lightly. There was still a bruise on the words from that night in Greyharbor, when she'd found the scrap of transcription in my coat and I'd lied to her so badly that even the lie had seemed embarrassed to be there. Six days of walking had scabbed it over, but scabs aren't healing. They're just the body deciding to postpone the argument.

"We'll see," I said, which is what cowards say instead of *yes*.

* * *

We waited out the tide in the lee of the cliff, sharing bread gone hard as doctrine and watching the water perform its slow, indifferent arithmetic. The moon would pull the sea back around midnight. Astrid slept against my shoulder for an hour or two, and I sat very still, because I have learned that some moments are load-bearing, and if you move, everything above them comes down.

Here is what I hadn't told her. Here is the inventory of my cowardice, itemized:

The ward on the Door of Vellmark is keyed to guild blood. Maren told me this the night she showed me the Codex, in her voice that makes even good news sound like a diagnosis. *Guild blood, freely given,* she'd said. *Not spilled. Given. There's a difference, and the Door knows it.* Nine of us left in the guild of small mercies. Nine names, eight candles, and now a door that opens only for the blood of people who spent a century keeping a country locked away like a shameful letter in a drawer.

And Astrid's name was written in the book. And Astrid could not open the door. And somewhere between those two facts was a cut on my palm that I hadn't mentioned, because mentioning it would have meant explaining why, and explaining why would have meant telling her that her whole destiny was gated behind my family's oldest sin.

So I sat under the cliff with the woman I loved asleep on my shoulder, and I watched the tide go out, and I thought: *one more night of not saying it. One more night of being the person she thinks I am.*

The trouble with one more nights is that they stack.

* * *

The sea withdrew around midnight like a host pulling back a curtain it was ashamed of.

And there it was.

A chapel, small and black and barnacled, standing in the wet sand with its steeple snapped off clean, as though the sea had taken it as payment and left the rest as a receipt. Its doors were stone—actual stone, fused shut, carved over every inch with script I half-recognized from the guild's oldest charms. The old tongue. The one Maren prayed in when she thought no one was listening.

Astrid stood up so fast she knocked over our water flask, and didn't apologize, and didn't look at it either. She walked down onto the seabed—because that's what it was, a seabed wearing the temporary costume of a beach—and I followed, and the sand gave under our boots with a sound like slow applause.

"It's real," she whispered. "Gods, Felix. It's real."

"I did mention the guild doesn't guard nothing."

"You mentioned it the way you mention everything. Sideways, while making tea." But she was laughing, or something adjacent to laughing, wet-eyed and shaking, and she put her hand flat against the stone doors the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you were afraid of waking. "A hundred years. A hundred years and it's just *standing here*, and nobody knew, nobody—"

"Somebody knew," I said quietly. "Nine somebodies. Eight candles' worth."

She turned. And I saw it happen in her face—the laughter draining out, the cartographer taking over, the woman who reads borders for a living reading me instead, line by line, looking for the thing I'd drawn wrong.

"What aren't you telling me?"

"Astrid—"

"No. You've been carrying something since Greyharbor. You carry it in your left side, like a stitch. Every time I ask about the guild, you change the subject with a joke, and you're good at it, you're *so* good at it, but I map coastlines for a living, Felix. I know a hidden inlet when I see one." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word. "What does the door need?"

I looked at the stone. I looked at her. I thought about lying one more time, and discovered that I had finally run out of one more nights.

"Blood," I said. "Guild blood. Freely given—not spilled, given, there's a difference, the Door knows it. It's keyed to the families that locked Vellmark away. My family. My guild. It won't open for anything else. Not for prophecy. Not for—" I gestured at her, at all of her, at the name in the book and the fire in her dreams. "Not for you."

The wind moved through the empty chapel arches and made a sound almost like breathing.

"So that's why you didn't tell me," she said. Not angry. Worse than angry. Understanding. "You thought if I knew, I'd feel like a passenger in my own story."

"I thought if you knew, you'd look at me differently."

"How?"

"Like I'm the lock instead of the key."

She was quiet for a long moment. Out past the low-tide line, the sea stood waiting, patient as a creditor, patient as a guild elder, patient as history itself.

"Felix Eastwick," she said at last, "you are the worst liar I have ever loved."

"Loved," I repeated. "Past tense. That's—"

"Don't fish. It's undignified." She took my hand—the right one, the unmarked one—and held it, and her grip was steady and warm and absolutely unforgiving. "Tell me what happens when you give it your blood."

"I don't know, exactly. Maren says the ward drinks it. Says it tastes the lineage. Says—" I swallowed. "Says sometimes it takes more than you planned to give. The founding generation wrote it during a purge, Astrid. People who write wards during purges don't design them to be polite."

"And you were going to do that without telling me. Just walk up, open a vein for a country neither of us has ever seen, and let me find out after, from the shape of the wound."

"When you say it like that—"

"I'm going to keep saying it like that until it stops being true."

* * *

Here is a thing about sacrifice that nobody puts in the songs: it's mostly logistics.

You stand in front of the thing. You take out the knife. Your hands shake, and you notice your hands shaking, and you think *how embarrassing, of all the times,* and then you stop noticing anything except the next three seconds.

Astrid insisted on standing beside me. Not behind—beside. "If this goes wrong," she said, "I want to be close enough to catch you," which is the least romantic sentence ever spoken and also the most.

I drew my belt knife. The chapel doors waited, carved and silent, wearing their century of salt like a shroud worn so long it's become skin.

"For the record," I said, "if this kills me, I want it noted that my last words were charming."

"Noted. Go."

I opened my palm along the old line, deep enough to be honest, shallow enough to come back from. It hurt exactly as much as cuts hurt—no poetry, no revelation, just the body filing its complaint. I pressed my hand to the stone.

For a moment, nothing. Long enough for doubt to get its boots on.

Then the ward woke.

I felt it the way you feel a hook set in your mouth—a pull, ancient and specific, tasting along the cut, reading me like Maren reading the Codex. It went through my blood the way Astrid went through maps: line by line, border by border, checking me against a ledger a hundred years old. And I felt it find what it was looking for. I felt it *recognize* me, and gods forgive me, recognition has never hurt so much—to be known completely by a thing built to keep a secret, to be claimed by the very inheritance I'd spent my whole life pretending was just a birthmark.

The stone doors groaned. Salt fell from the carvings like the chapel shedding tears it had been holding since before my grandmother was born. And the seam between the doors glowed—a thin line of gold-white light, the color of the candle in Maren's ritual room, the color of a flame kept burning in the dark by people who never got to explain themselves.

"Is it supposed to do that?" Astrid asked, gripping my arm.

"I genuinely don't know. Nobody alive has done this."

"Wonderful. We're pioneers."

The doors swung inward—slowly, grudgingly, stone grinding on stone—and the light poured out over the seabed, over the waiting tide, over both of us standing hand in bleeding hand at the threshold of a country that wasn't supposed to exist anymore.

Warm air came through. Warm air, smelling of grass and woodsmoke and something older underneath, something like sun on stone. From inside came a sound I will hear until I die: bells. Distant, unhurried, ringing the hour in a village that had been underwater on every map for a hundred years.

Astrid made a sound beside me that was half laugh, half sob.

And then, from the light—from somewhere deep in the held heart of Vellmark—came footsteps. Unhurried. Deliberate. The footsteps of someone who has been waiting a very long time and is not surprised the door finally opened.

Astrid's fingers tightened around mine.

"Felix," she breathed. "Someone's coming."

"Yes," I said, pressing my bleeding palm against my coat, watching the doorway fill with light and shadow and the outline of a figure I somehow already dreaded. "I rather think that was the point."
