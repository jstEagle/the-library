## 13. Behind the Door

**FELIX**

The door did not open so much as admit us.

There's a difference, and I felt it in my teeth. A door opens because someone wills it open. This one *permitted*—the way a customs officer permits, the way a priest permits you into a shrine you've already dirtied with your boots. The ward on my blood sang through my cut palm, a low note like a wet finger on glass, and then the chapel wall stopped being a wall.

I had expected darkness. Everyone expects darkness behind a sealed door; it's the whole grammar of secrets. What I got instead was light—ordinary, golden, late-afternoon light, the kind that makes even laundry on a line look like an argument for the existence of gods.

A street. An actual street, cobbled, running downhill between houses with blue shutters and geraniums going fat and red in window boxes. Somewhere below, gulls. Somewhere below, a woman was beating a rug off a balcony rail, three stories up, and the sound of it carried clean as a bell.

"Is this," Astrid said slowly, "a trick?"

"If it is, it's a very committed one." My voice came out strange. Thin. I cleared my throat and tried again. "Vellmark."

She didn't answer right away. She'd gone still in the particular way she went still over maps—total attention, body forgotten, everything routed to the eyes. She was reading the street the way she read coastlines: every roofline, every drainpipe, every crooked chimney catalogued somewhere behind her face.

"It's mid-century," she said finally. "Look at the shutters. That hinge style died out forty years ago. And the well cover—" She pointed down the hill at a stone circle I'd have walked past without a glance. "That carving is pre-purge. Nobody's made those since."

"Maybe they're slow to change."

"Felix." She turned to me, and her eyes were doing something complicated. "Nobody is *this* slow to change."

We went down the hill together, our footsteps loud on cobbles that should have been choked with a century of moss and silence. They weren't. They were worn smooth, polished almost, as if ten thousand feet crossed them daily—and yet we passed no one. The rug-beating woman kept her rhythm. A cat watched us from a windowsill with the flat patience of a painted thing. Laundry turned on its lines in a wind I couldn't feel on my own skin.

"Hello?" Astrid called, and the word bounced back off the blue shutters sounding wrong, doubled, like the street was repeating it to itself for practice.

No one answered. But halfway down, a door stood open onto a kitchen where bread sat cooling on a rack, and when Astrid touched the loaf—she couldn't help herself, I saw her reach before she decided to—it was warm.

Warm bread. In a country erased from every map and memory for a hundred years.

"That's not possible," she whispered.

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

**ASTRID**

I am a cartographer's assistant, which means I am professionally trained to notice what is missing from a picture. Coastlines that end too neatly. Rivers that stop at borders. Whole districts left blank because no one bothered or no one dared.

Everything about Vellmark was missing, and none of it was blank.

We walked the length of the town—I think it was a town; it had the scale of one, maybe four thousand souls' worth of houses—and I counted the absences the way I'd count them on any survey. No graves newer than fifty years. No repairs. A roof with storm damage on the north side, tiles slipped, and beneath the damage, scaffolding—old scaffolding, weathered silver, exactly as weathered as the day the work stopped. As if the roofer had climbed down for his lunch a hundred years ago and simply never come back.

And everywhere, everywhere, the light. It was late afternoon when we came through, and it stayed late afternoon. I checked. I'm not proud of how long I stood in the market square watching my own shadow hold its angle like a dog told to stay, but I needed to know, and knowing was worse than wondering.

"The sun isn't moving," I told Felix.

"I noticed."

"It's been an hour."

"I noticed, Astrid."

Her voice had a frayed edge I recognized from the night she lied to me about the Codex—the same tightness, the same guilt-shaped silence. But there was nothing to lie about here, or rather, everything was the lie, the whole sunlit street and the warm bread and the geraniums, all of it one enormous maintained falsehood, and neither of us knew who was maintaining it.

At the heart of the square stood a fountain, dry, its basin furred with salt crystals that caught the frozen sunlight and threw it back in little knives. And beyond the fountain, rising above the rooftops like a hand raised in class, stood a tower. White stone. Slender. With a single lit window near the top.

Even from across the town I could see that the light in that window wasn't sunlight. It moved. It breathed. It was the color of the coals in Felix's charm-forge, the color of the luck-charm she'd sold me once without telling me what it cost her—a deep, banked orange-gold, alive in a way nothing else in Vellmark was alive.

"There," I said. "That's it. That's what I've been dreaming about."

Felix followed my finger and went pale in a way I found, absurdly, reassuring. At least one of us could still be frightened on schedule.

"The last dragon-fire," she said. "It has to be. Maren said it was kept at the heart of—" She stopped. Swallowed. "At the heart of Vellmark."

"You've known that phrase your whole life, haven't you? 'Heart of Vellmark.' You guild people say it like a prayer."

"Astrid—"

"Don't." I held up a hand. We'd had this fight on the road north, and again at the chapel, and I'd won it by refusing to lose it: she was done deciding what I could bear. Whatever was in that tower, whatever was keeping the sun nailed to the sky and the bread warm and the rug-beater beating, I would see it with my eyes open. "Together. Remember?"

Something in her shoulders let go, a half-inch, which from Felix Eastwick was a public confession of relief.

"Together," she said.

* * *

**FELIX**

The tower door wasn't locked. Of course it wasn't locked. Nothing in Vellmark was locked; locks are for places where time happens.

Inside, stairs spiraled up through cool white stone, and the air changed with every turn—salt first, then smoke, then something older underneath both, a smell like struck flint and hot iron that I felt in my back teeth before I identified it. Dragon-fire. Or the memory of it. Or the *custody* of it, which may be the same thing.

Astrid climbed ahead of me. I watched the lantern-light from the window above grow on the walls, breathing with that slow rhythm, and I thought about Maren's hands when she'd shown me the Codex—how they'd trembled, how she'd pressed the page flat as though the book might bolt. *Keeper-or-killer,* the prophecy said. *Of the last flame.* I had spent the whole journey north telling myself the words meant something manageable. Save it or destroy it. A choice. Choices I can handle; I make small ones daily, selling charms to people whose luck I cannot actually improve.

But standing on those stairs, listening to the fire breathe, I began to suspect the Codex hadn't offered a choice at all. It had offered a description.

The stair ended at a single room, round, its walls unbroken white stone except for one window facing west into that held golden hour. And in the center of the room, on a plain iron stand—

The lantern.

It was small. That's what undid me first, the smallness of it, a fisherman's storm-lantern, brass gone green at the seams, the kind of thing you'd buy for six coppers in Greyharbor's night market. Through its glass burned a flame the size of my two cupped hands, and it was the most beautiful thing I have ever seen, and I include in that category the sunrise over Greyharbor harbor the morning after my mother died, which until now had held the title uncontested.

The flame was gold at its heart and red at its edges and it moved like a living lung, in, out, in, out, and every time it swelled, the whole room warmed, and every time it ebbed, I felt the cold lean in like a question.

Astrid stepped toward it and the flame *leaned toward her.*

Not much. An inch of bend, a flower turning to a window. But I saw it, and she saw it, and we looked at each other across the lantern with the same expression, because we had both just understood that the prophecy in the Salt Codex had not been guessing.

"It knows me," Astrid said. Her voice was steady. Her hand, reaching toward the glass, was not quite.

"Don't touch it."

"I wasn't going to—" She pulled back. Frowned. "Felix. Look around. Really look."

I did. And looking properly, I saw what awe had papered over on the climb: the room was immaculate. Not clean—immaculate. No dust. No cobwebs. The iron stand bore no rust. The window glass was free of the salt-haze that coated every other pane in the town below. Something tended this room. Something tended *all of it*—the warm bread, the beaten rugs, the worn cobbles, the geraniums. A country does not stay fresh for a hundred years on its own.

"Who keeps it?" Astrid whispered. "Who's been keeping it?"

As if the question were a key, the flame flared—bright, sudden, gold flooding the white walls—and guttered just as fast, and in the space between flare and ebb I heard it.

Or felt it. It's hard to write honestly about this part. It wasn't a voice. It was more like standing next to a very large animal in the dark and feeling it become aware of you—the pressure of attention, vast and old and unbearably patient, turning toward us through the flame like a lighthouse beam.

And under that attention, faint but unmistakable, threaded something that felt horribly like *recognition.*

It knew Astrid. The Codex had said so, and I'd dreaded it, and here was the dread wearing flesh—or wearing fire, rather—but that wasn't the worst of it.

The worst of it was that, standing there with my palm still scabbed from the chapel ward, my guild mark itching under my sleeve, I felt the attention pass over me the way a landlord's eye passes over furniture. Noted. Filed. Irrelevant.

The fire had been waiting a hundred years, and it had not been waiting for the Guild of Small Mercies.

It had been waiting for her.

"Astrid," I said, and my voice cracked on her name, and I hated myself for the crack, because she turned to me with her chin up and her eyes bright and absolutely no fear anywhere in her, and I realized I was about to say the most dangerous sentence I own: *we should go back.*

Before I could say it, the flame leaned toward her again—farther this time, pressing against the glass like a child at a window—and down in the town below, faint through the tower's stone, the rug-beating woman stopped.

Mid-beat. Mid-swing.

The silence that followed was total, and it was worse than any sound, because in it I could hear the truth settling over Vellmark like snowfall: the country wasn't dead, wasn't sleeping, wasn't preserved.

It was *held.* Every street, every loaf, every frozen shadow—held in one hand, the way you'd hold a breath.

And somewhere below us, in the sunlit streets we'd walked through believing them empty, something that had been holding its breath for a hundred years had just noticed we were here.

The flame pulsed once, hard, gold washing the walls—and in the pulse I heard, clear as a bell over water:

*She's come.*

Then the stairs, which had been empty a moment ago, creaked under a footstep.

Someone was climbing up.
