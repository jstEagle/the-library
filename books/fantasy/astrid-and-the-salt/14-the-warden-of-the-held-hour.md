## 14. The Warden of the Held Hour

The lantern hung from a hook of black iron in the center of the square, and it was burning.

I want to be clear about what I mean by *burning*, because I have spent my life around fire and I know its moods. This was not a flame that ate anything. It did not flicker, lean, or gutter. It stood in the glass like a woman standing very still so as not to wake a child, and it gave off light the way a memory gives off warmth — evenly, patiently, without asking to be fed.

Around it, Vellmark held its breath.

We had walked through the Door and into a country that should not exist, and every street of it was mid-afternoon on a day a hundred years gone. Laundry on lines. A dog asleep in a doorway, mid-dream, one paw twitching at something it would never catch. A baker's window full of loaves that had been warm for a century. Somewhere a fountain ran — or rather, water hung in the shape of running, each drop fixed like beads on a string.

Felix's hand found mine. Her palm was still sticky with her own blood from the chapel ward, and she didn't apologize for it, which I appreciated. There is a kind of person who bleeds for you and then makes you manage their feelings about it. Felix just bled and kept walking.

"It's not dead," she said quietly. "It's *held*."

"That's worse," I said, before I could stop myself.

She looked at me. "Worse than dead?"

"Dead things get to rest." I nodded at the dog, the laundry, the loaves. "This is a country caught mid-sentence. Nobody gets to finish."

Felix didn't answer, but her thumb moved once across my knuckles, and I understood that she agreed and hated agreeing. We had come through the Door carrying the Codex and a hundred years of other people's secrets between us, and we were only beginning to learn how heavy they were.

The square itself was small and beautiful in the way of places built by people who expected to grow old in them. Cobbles worn into soft curves. A well with a roof of green copper. Benches facing each other instead of the view, because the view was each other. And at the far end, where any sensible town would put a church or a market hall, stood a low stone building with a single door, and above the door, carved deep enough to survive weather and war and being erased from every map ever drawn:

**THE HOUR IS KEPT HERE.**

"I don't like that," Felix said.

"Neither do I."

"Clocks keep hours. Hours don't need keeping."

"No," I said. "But prisoners do."

She flinched. I hadn't meant it as an accusation, or maybe I had; we were both still raw from the chapel, from the blood, from the way the ward had known her guild-blood like a mother knows a footstep on the stair. We went to the door together anyway. That was becoming our whole story: going anyway.

* * *

Inside, the building was one room, round, with no windows, lit entirely by the lantern-light that came through the open door behind us. Shelves lined the walls, and the shelves were empty — not abandoned-empty, but *waiting*-empty, dusted and ordered, holding nothing the way a table holds nothing before a meal.

And in the middle of the room sat an old woman, working.

She was mending a coat. That was the first thing. Not enthroned, not praying, not waiting in dramatic silhouette. She sat on a plain chair with a needle in her hand and a coat across her lap, and she was sewing a button with the fussy concentration of anyone who has ever lost a fight with a button. Her hair was white and long and tied back with what looked suspiciously like fishing twine. Her hands were spotted and steady.

She looked up at us without surprise.

"You took your time," she said. "I've had the kettle on since before your grandmothers were born."

Felix made a sound I'd never heard from her before — half gasp, half prayer. "You're—" She stopped. Started again. "The founder. The first Elder. Maren has your portrait in the guildhall. You're—"

"Older than the portrait," the woman said. "Portraits are flattering about everything except time." She bit the thread, set the coat aside, and folded her hands. "Sit down, children. Both of you. Astrid of the Tide-Line, and Felix Eastwick, ninth of her name in my guild. Yes, I know. The book told me you were coming, and then the book stopped telling me things, which is how I knew you were close."

"The Codex talks to you?" I asked.

"The Codex *is* me," she said, mildly, as if correcting my grammar. "Or near enough. When you write a prophecy, you leave a little of yourself in the ink. Sit."

There were two chairs, exactly two, placed where guests' chairs go. They had been waiting a hundred years too. We sat.

"Felix," she said, "your palm."

"It's fine."

"It isn't, and it doesn't need to be brave. Ward-cuts fester if you pretend they're nothing. There's a basin behind you." Then, to me, in the same breath, the same tone: "Astrid. Do you know why the fire hasn't gone out?"

I looked back at the doorway, at the light standing patient in the glass. "Because it's magic?"

"Everything here is magic. That's like asking why a fish is wet." She leaned forward. "Do you know why?"

And I realized I did, in the way you realize you've known something all along and simply declined to say it, even to yourself. I thought of the town outside, mid-sentence. Mid-*hour*. Everything held at the moment of the locking — the bread warm, the dog dreaming, the water hanging.

"Because nothing here is allowed to happen," I said. "Fire goes out because it burns. If nothing is allowed to burn—"

"—nothing is allowed to end," she finished. "Yes. The flame is kept alive by keeping everything else suspended. Every loaf, every dream, every unfinished afternoon. The whole country is a wick, child. Vellmark isn't hiding behind the fire. The fire is hiding inside Vellmark."

The room felt suddenly smaller. Or I felt suddenly larger, which is worse.

"And if the country wakes up," I said slowly, "if the hour moves forward — the laundry dries, the bread goes stale, the dog finishes its dream—"

"Then the wick is spent," the Warden said. "And the last dragon-fire gutters out with it. Forever. There is no second flame. There is no relighting. What burns now is all the burning there will ever be."

Silence. Behind us, in the doorway, the light stood perfectly still, and for the first time I heard it correctly: not patience. *Held breath.*

Felix came back from the basin, hand wrapped in a strip of linen, her face doing the thing it does when she's arithmetic-ing her way out of a feeling. "Then we don't open the Door fully. We seal it again. We go home, we tell Maren—"

"Tell Maren what?" the Warden asked, gently, terribly. "That you found the country and left it locked? That you met me and walked away?" She shook her head. "The Door has been opened, Felix. Not wide — a crack, when your blood touched the ward. But a held hour is like a held note. Once it trembles, it can't untremble. Look outside."

I looked. Through the doorway, past the still-burning lantern, I saw the baker's window — and one loaf, just one, had begun, very slowly, to go stale. A crust softening at the edge after a century of perfect warmth. A single drop of fountain-water had fallen.

"How long?" Felix whispered.

"A season. Perhaps two. The crack widens on its own now. Which means the choice you came here to make has already been made for you — the only question left is *how* it ends." The Warden rose, joints cracking like green wood, and went to the empty shelves. She touched them almost tenderly. "I built these shelves to hold a country's future. Books, ledgers, seeds, songs. Everything Vellmark would need when it woke up. And then I learned the truth, and I never filled them, because there was no future to hold."

"What truth?" I asked, though my stomach already knew.

She turned around. Up close, her eyes were the exact grey of the sea before weather, and I understood why they'd made her a Warden and not a grandmother.

"That I didn't save Vellmark," she said. "I imprisoned it. To save the fire, I stopped a nation's heart. Every person out there — the baker, the dreamer, the child who was mid-laugh when I closed the Door — none of them consented. None of them will ever finish anything, unless someone chooses to let them die properly, along with the flame." She smiled, and it was the saddest thing I had seen in a life lately full of sad things. "So. Now you know the trap, keeper-or-killer. Save the last dragon-fire, and a country sleeps in amber until the world ends. Wake the country, and the fire dies forever. There is no clean way. There was never a clean way. I spent a hundred years looking for one, and all I found was this chair, and this needle, and this coat that belongs to a man who will never come back for it."

* * *

We stayed while she made tea, because refusing seemed crueler than the tea could possibly be. It was good tea. Of course it was; it had been perfect for a hundred years.

Outside, through the doorway, the light of the lantern lay across the cobbles, and I watched a shadow — my own — move in it. My shadow moved. Nothing else did. I don't know why that undid me more than anything else, but it did: I was the only living thing in a country of the nearly-living, walking around in the one patch of light that refused to change, casting the only moving shadow Vellmark had seen in a century.

Felix stood beside me. She'd been quiet a long time, which for Felix is seismic.

"When I was small," she said finally, "Maren told me the guild's purpose was mercy. Small mercies, she called them. Charms for luck, wards against fever, little kindnesses nobody pays for." Her jaw tightened. "She never said the big mercy was locking a whole country in a jar."

"She may believe it," I said. "People can believe anything if they're the ones holding the lid."

"Do you hate her? The Warden?"

I considered it honestly, watching the old woman through the doorway, sewing again, because what else does a person do with a hundred years and a broken heart but mend things that will never be worn.

"No," I said. "I hate the choice she was given. Someone handed her a scale and said *one side or the other*, and she picked, and she's been paying for picking ever since." I turned to Felix. "But I'm not her. And neither are you. Maybe the trap is real. Maybe there really are only two doors. But traps are built by people, Felix, and people make mistakes. Prophecies are written by people, and people lie, even in ink. Especially in ink."

The Warden's voice came from behind us, dry as old paper. "Careful, Tide-Line. That's dangerously close to hope."

"Good," I said. "I've been told I carry it well."

She laughed — a real laugh, rusty, startled out of her like a bird from a hedge — and then stopped herself, pressing her hand to her mouth as if laughter were also something the hour forbade. And maybe it was. Maybe every laugh in here had been swallowed for a hundred years, and hers had just been the first crack in the dam.

That was when I saw it.

On the shelf behind her — the empty shelves, the waiting shelves — one shelf was not empty. One object sat there, small and dark, and it had not been there when we entered. I am a cartographer's assistant; I notice when a room adds furniture.

It was a lantern. Unlit. Old iron, salt-eaten, with a handle shaped like a wave.

And as I looked at it, it warmed — not glowing, not yet, but warming the way a stone warms in sun — and somewhere under my breastbone, in the place where the dreams lived, the burning door and the salt water and the tide that sang, something answered it. Something said, in no language at all:

*Not yet. But soon. And when I ask, you'll know what I want, and it won't be what anyone told you.*

"Astrid," Felix said. "Your face. What is it? What do you see?"

I couldn't look away from the unlit lantern.

"The fire," I said. "It's not waiting to be saved."

Behind me, the Warden went very still.

"It's waiting to ask."
