## 15. Maren's Confession

I was mending a ward-stitch on the chapel door when the tide brought the guild.

Not all of it. What was left of it. Seven figures coming down the drowned coast road in the grey light before dawn, moving the way people move when they've walked all night and stopped pretending they haven't: Elder Maren at the front with her staff planted like a fence post, and behind her six others I'd known my whole life, carrying candle-boxes and satchels and one wrapped bundle that made my stomach drop before I could say why.

Astrid woke at the sound of boots on wet stone. She'd been sleeping against the chapel wall with her coat balled under her head, and she came up out of sleep the way she did everything—fast, quiet, hand already finding mine.

"Friends?" she asked.

"Family," I said. "The kind you don't get to choose."

Maren stopped ten feet from us and looked at Astrid for a long moment. Then at me. Then at the chapel, which stood knee-deep in retreating water, its doors open for the first time in a century because I'd opened them two days ago with my blood and hadn't been able to close them since.

"You went inside," Maren said.

"We went inside."

"Alone."

"With each other," I said. "Which is not alone, though I understand how the paperwork might be confusing."

One of the younger members—Tobias, who'd been taken by inquisitors' fear his whole life and had never once learned to hide it—made a small sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Maren didn't smile. Maren hadn't smiled since Odile was taken, and I was beginning to suspect she'd forgotten where she'd left it.

"The Door should never have been opened," she said.

"Well," I said. "It has been. So we can either stand here discussing what shouldn't have happened until the tide comes back, or we can go in and see what we've got."

* * *

Inside Vellmark, the held hour was doing what it always did: being noon on a day that had ended a hundred years ago. Sunlight without warmth. Bread smell from ovens nobody tended. A town square full of people who weren't people—impressions of people, the memory of a market mid-morning, frozen so completely that even their shadows had stopped moving.

The surviving guild filed in behind us and stopped, every one of them, like they'd hit glass.

Some of them had never seen it. That was the thing about the guild—we guarded a Door most of us were never allowed through. Nine names, eight candles, and a rule older than any of us: *look after the lock, never look through it.* I'd broken that rule with Astrid two days ago, and now seven elders and juniors and candle-keepers stood in the doorway of a country and understood, all at once, exactly what they'd spent their lives keeping shut.

Tobias sat down on the chapel steps. Not fainted—just sat, deliberate, like his legs had filed a formal complaint.

"It's real," he said. "It's actually—" He pressed both hands flat against the stone. "My grandmother used to sing a song about a river with a bend like a elbow. I thought she made it up. It's *there.*" He pointed across the square, to where a river bent around a mill. "That's her river."

Nobody answered him. Maren was looking at the lantern.

You could see it from the chapel steps if you knew where to look—a glow at the heart of the town, past the square, in a building that might once have been a hall or a temple or just somebody's very large kitchen. The last dragon-fire, burning in its lantern, keeping the whole country pickled in amber around it.

And standing between us and it, at the end of the street, a figure that hadn't been there yesterday.

"She knows we're here," Astrid murmured.

The Warden didn't approach. She never approached. She stood at her distance like a woman at the far end of a ballroom deciding whether to dance, and then she turned and walked away, and somehow that felt worse than if she'd screamed at us.

Maren watched her go. Something crossed her face—not surprise. Recognition. And I thought, in the way you think things you immediately wish you hadn't: *she knows her.*

"Everyone inside the chapel," Maren said. "Now. Felix, Astrid—you too. There are things that need saying, and I will not say them standing in a dead woman's marketplace."

"They're not dead," Tobias said quietly. "That's rather the entire problem, isn't it?"

* * *

The chapel smelled of salt and old incense and, faintly, of my own blood from two days ago—the ward keyed to guild blood still humming in the stones like a struck bell that wouldn't finish fading. We crowded in among the pews. Eight of us plus Astrid, which meant the guild fit into three rows, which told you everything about what nine hundred years of guardianship had come down to.

Maren set her staff against the altar. Then she took off her ring.

I'd seen that ring every day of my life. Iron band, worn smooth, set with a chip of grey stone that wasn't quite any stone I could name. Every guild elder wore one. I'd assumed I'd wear one someday, if the guild survived long enough to bother promoting me. She laid it on the altar like a woman setting down something heavy she'd carried too far.

"When I joined," she said, "I swore an oath. You all know it. *To hold the Door. To keep the flame. To guard what sleeps until waking is safe.*" Her voice was level, the way it got when she was holding something down. "I taught you that oath. My elder taught me. Hers taught her. Back and back, nine hundred years, each generation handing the next a lock and calling it a sacred duty."

"Maren," I said, "whatever this is—"

"I am *talking*, Felix." Not unkindly. But final. "You opened the Door. You deserve to know what you opened."

She looked at the altar. At the ring. At none of us.

"The guild was not founded to guard Vellmark," she said. "The guild was founded to imprison it."

The words went into the room and just—stayed there. Salt dripped somewhere outside. One of the candles guttered and righted itself.

"There was no purge," Maren said. "There was no plague, no invasion, no disaster. The histories Greyharbor teaches are wrong, and the histories the guild teaches are wronger, because ours were written by the people who did it." She finally looked up. "Vellmark was the strongest magical nation on the coast. Strong enough that the kingdoms around it were afraid—and rightly. And when the fear grew teeth, it wasn't armies that came. It was a compact. Six kingdoms, one working, and a handful of Vellmark's own mages bought or frightened into helping. They couldn't destroy a power that size. So they folded it. Folded the whole country into an hour and hid the fold behind a door, and the mages who did the folding stayed behind to keep it folded."

"Why," Astrid said. It wasn't really a question. It was the question, sharpened to a point. "Why would Vellmark's own mages do that to their own country?"

"Because the alternative was Corvane's predecessors getting their hands on it," Maren said. "The first Minister of Seals—his name was Aldous Vane, and yes, the family kept the name, they're sentimental that way—wanted the dragon-fire. Not hidden. *His.* He'd already begun collecting confiscated magic; the vault your Minister keeps today is his invention, grown fat on four generations of theft. The compact mages had a choice: let Vane take the flame and become something the world had no defense against, or take the flame themselves and hide it somewhere even he couldn't reach." Her jaw tightened. "So they hid it inside its own country. Locked the land and the fire together, and locked themselves in with it as wardens, and left the rest of us outside to maintain the lock."

"And the people?" Tobias's voice cracked on it. "The people who lived here? The bakeries, the—the children—"

"Frozen," Maren said. "Held. Not suffering—I need you to hear that, because I have needed to hear it for forty years. The Warden designed the holding so that no one inside feels a moment pass. They are not in pain. They are not aware. As far as any soul in this country knows, it is still Tuesday morning, a century ago, and the bread is rising." She paused. "But they are also not free. And they did not choose it. And the mages who chose it for them founded our guild afterward and wrote the oath so that no one would ever ask the question you just asked."

I found I was gripping the pew in front of me hard enough to hurt my hands. I made myself let go.

"The Codex," I said. "The prophecy. Astrid of the Tide-Line, keeper-or-killer. Was that in the original working? Did they write her in a hundred years before she was born?"

"No." Maren shook her head slowly. "The Codex is older than the compact. It's a tide-book—it doesn't predict, it *weighs*. It shows the shape of what's coming the way water shows the shape of what's underneath. When the founders locked the Door, they consulted it, and it gave them the answer they couldn't bear: that the lock would not hold forever. That someone would come, born of the tide-line, who could either give the fire back to the land—or open the Door wide while the fire was still needed to hold the hour, and let everything inside unravel into salt." She met Astrid's eyes. "They spent nine hundred years making sure no such person would ever find the Door. The guild's true purpose, the one beneath the oath, was to keep you from existing in the same place as this chapel."

Astrid took that the way she takes everything—still, with her chin up, absorbing the blow somewhere deep where it wouldn't show for hours. But her hand found mine on the pew, and her grip said what her face wouldn't.

"So Odile," I said. My voice came out strange. "When the inquisitors took her. When they've been picking us off one by one for years, fraying the wards, starving the guild down to nine names—that's not persecution. That's maintenance. Corvane's been dismantling the lock."

"Corvane doesn't know what the lock is," Maren said. "He knows the guild stands between him and certain magics. He thinks he's hunting thieves. He's actually pulling threads off a knot, and when the last thread goes—" She gestured, small and terrible, at the sunlight beyond the chapel doors. "The hour ends. Everything held comes due. And whatever's left of Vellmark comes apart, because the only thing keeping it coherent is a fire that was never meant to burn this long without being given back."

"Given back," Astrid repeated.

"That's what the Warden told us," I said. "The trap. The fire stays lit only while the country stays hidden. Open the Door fully, give the flame to the land, and either Vellmark wakes—or it burns out trying."

"Yes," Maren said simply. "That is the choice. And it was always going to be yours, Astrid. The founders knew it. They hated knowing it. Some of them wanted to drown the Codex in the sea and be done with it, but you can't drown a tide-book; it only washes up again." She laughed, briefly, horribly. "I know. Someone tried. 1741. It came back in a fisherman's net with the pages dry."

I stared at her. Forty years I'd known this woman. She'd taught me my first ward-signs, held my hair back when I was sick from charm-sickness at fourteen, promoted me over people senior to me because she said I had "a talent for doors"—and I'd thought that was a compliment.

Every story I had about my own life rearranged itself around the hole in the middle of it.

"You knew," I said. "All of it. The whole time. You let us sing the founding songs. You let us light the candles and say the oath and believe we were *guardians*—"

"What would you have done with the truth at twelve, Felix?" Maren asked, and there it was at last, the crack in her voice, the thing she'd been holding down with that level tone. "What would any of us have done? I'll tell you what I did at thirty-five, when my own elder finally told me. I sat in this exact chapel—" she touched the pew "—this may be this exact pew—and I was sick, and then I went home, and I lit the candles, and I kept the lock. Because the alternative was opening it, and opening it might kill everyone inside, and keeping it shut was at least a sin I could survive." Her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "That is what inheritance is, child. It's receiving a sin and calling it a duty because the other word is unbearable."

Silence. Outside, the held noon went on shining, indifferent, beautiful, obscene.

Tobias was crying without noise, the way boys learn to. Old Sef, the eldest after Maren, had his rosary of ward-beads out and was moving them through his fingers like he could count his way back to a version of the guild that existed. And I stood there in the wreckage of everything I'd believed and tried to find, underneath it, something solid.

Here's what I found: nothing. The guild wasn't a legacy. It was a cover-up with good posture. Nine hundred years of small mercies, and the mercy was for us—for the descendants of the jailers, spared the knowledge of the jail.

And yet.

Yet Maren had come. She'd walked all night down a drowned coast with six survivors and a wrapped bundle I still hadn't asked about, to a Door she believed should never have been opened, because her heir had opened it and she would not let us face what came next alone. Whatever the guild was, it had also taught me every true thing I knew. It had also, in its way, led me here—to Astrid, to the truth, to the choice.

Maybe that's what inheritance means. Not the sin. Not the duty. Just the fact that someone hands you something broken and you decide, fresh, every generation, whether to keep hiding it or start fixing it.

"Maren," I said. "The bundle. What's in the bundle?"

She looked at it for a long moment. Then she nodded to Sef, who unwrapped it on the altar beside her abandoned ring.

The Salt Codex. The real one—not the transcription scrap Astrid had found in my coat, not the copies, the book itself, salt-crusted and swollen with age, its covers breathing faintly like something asleep.

"If we're doing this," Maren said, "we do it with everything on the table. All of it. The book, the truth, the fire, the choice." She turned to face me fully, and behind the exhaustion in her eyes was something I'd never seen there before, and it took me a moment to name it.

Hope. Terrified, guilty, ninety-years-old hope.

"But understand what you're asking the guild to become, Felix. Because the moment we open that book in earnest—here, inside the fold, with the Warden watching—we stop being jailers. And there is no oath, no candle, no nine-hundred-year tradition to catch us on the other side. Whatever we are after that, we build from nothing."

I looked at Astrid. She was already looking at the Codex, and her lips were moving slightly, reading the salt-swollen script upside down faster than I could have read it right-side up, and I remembered that she'd dreamed of this door her whole life before she ever knew it existed. The tide-line doesn't predict. It weighs. Maybe it had been weighing her since before either of us was born.

"Good," I said. "I was never much good at traditions anyway. Ask anyone. I sell luck-charms to godless dockworkers and lie badly to women I love. I'm exactly the wrong person to inherit a prison."

I put my hand on the Codex's cover. It was warm.

"So let's find out what I'm the right person for."

The Codex opened itself under my palm. And every candle in the chapel went out at once—all eight, and the ninth that had never been lit, the empty socket in the ritual box flaring suddenly bright with a flame that burned cold and blue and expectant, as if something on the other side of the book had been waiting nine hundred years for someone to finally ask it a question.

From the heart of the town, faint but unmistakable, the dragon-fire flickered.

And the Warden began to walk toward the chapel.
