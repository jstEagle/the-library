## 16. What the Fire Wants

I dreamed of salt again that night, but this time the water was warm.

That's how I knew it wasn't my own dream. My dreams of Vellmark—the ones I'd carried since childhood, the burning door, the tide-line, all of it—had always been cold dreams. Cold water, cold light, the particular chill of a thing remembered by someone who was never there. But this warmth was new. This warmth had a pulse.

I stood on a shore made of pages. I want to be precise about that, because precision is the only craft I have and even in dreams I reach for it: the beach beneath my bare feet was layered paper, soft as silt, each sheet printed faintly with streets and harbors and names that dissolved when I tried to read them. The sea beyond was ink. Not dark—*black*, deliberate, patient, the way ink waits in a well.

And in the middle of the shore, on a driftwood table that hadn't been there a moment before, sat a lantern.

The flame inside it didn't flicker. It *breathed*. In and out, gold to white to gold, and with every exhale I felt something press gently against the inside of my skull, the way a cat presses its forehead against a hand it has decided to trust.

"You're the one who's been dreaming me," I said. My voice came out steadier than I felt. "All those years. The door. The water."

The flame flared, which I chose to interpret as yes.

"I thought you were a memory," I said. "My grandmother sang songs about Vellmark. I thought I was just—" I gestured at the paper shore, the ink sea, myself standing barefoot in the middle of an erased country's subconscious. "Inheriting. Echoes."

The flame did something I can only describe as a shrug. Fire should not be capable of wry humor. This one managed it anyway, and I felt an absurd, hysterical urge to laugh, because of course. Of course the last dragon-fire in the world had a sense of humor. Everything else in my life had conspired toward irony; why should this be different?

"Say what you came to say," I told it. "I'm a cartographer. I don't do riddles. I do borders."

And the flame spoke—not in words, not exactly, but in the way a map speaks when you finally understand its projection. The whole shape of a thing arriving in your head at once, and then your mind spends weeks translating it into lines.

Here is what it said:

*I am not a treasure.*

I blinked. "Come again?"

*They hid me like a treasure,* the flame said, and the warmth in the dream turned almost unbearably tender, the way a hand goes gentle over a wound. *A lantern in a locked country. Fed, kept, preserved. A hundred years of guild blood spilled to keep me burning behind a door no one may open. Do you know what that is? That is not love. That is hoarding.*

The word landed in my chest like a hook finding meat. Hoarding. I thought of Corvane's vault, the shelves of confiscated magic humming under glass, the amulet that had whispered my name. I thought of Maren's face when she talked about the guild's sacred duty. I thought of Felix, lying badly to protect me, and understood—in one cold, clarifying instant—that preservation and protection are not the same act, and that people confuse them constantly, catastrophically, usually while meaning well.

"You want out," I said.

*I want to be given back.* The flame leaned toward me. *There is a difference. Out is escape. Given back is a gift. I was never meant to sit in a jar while a country slept around me. I was meant to be spent. Poured into soil and stone and hearth-smoke. Every fire wants to end, cartographer. Every fire wants to become warmth in someone else's hands instead of light in its own. That is the whole secret of fire, and your guild built a religion on refusing to hear it.*

I sat down on the paper shore. It seemed wise, given the direction of the conversation.

"The Warden said if the door opens fully, you gutter out," I said. "She said Vellmark stays hidden or you die."

*The Warden has been alive for a hundred and forty years,* the flame said, with a dryness that would have done credit to Elder Maren. *She has had a very long time to mistake her cage for a sanctuary. Ask yourself, Astrid of the Tide-Line: who is she preserving? Me? Or the version of history where her sacrifice was noble?*

"That's cruel."

*It's true. Truth is frequently cruel. You of all people should know—you've been carrying a transcription of my prophecy in your lover's coat pocket for two weeks, and every day you don't mention it, you choose a small comfortable lie over a large uncomfortable truth. How is that working for you?*

The dream went very quiet. Somewhere in the waking world, I knew, Felix was asleep beside me in the chapel's antechamber, her bandaged palm curled under her chin, breathing the slow breath of someone who has bled for a door and still isn't sure she's forgiven. And here was a lamp calling me out in front of God and geography.

"We're working on it," I said stiffly.

*Mm.* The flame pulsed, amused. *Work faster. The minister is coming, and he does not preserve. He consumes. When he arrives, you will have perhaps a day. Maybe less. And in that day you will be offered every safe lie ever minted: keep the door shut, keep the country sleeping, keep the girl you love by keeping her ignorant. They will all sound reasonable. They will all be wrong.*

"Why me?" I asked, and my voice cracked on it, finally, the question I'd been swallowing since the Codex first said my name. "Keeper or killer. Why does it have to be me? I map coastlines. I make copies of other people's charts. I'm nobody's chosen anything."

The flame was silent for a long moment. When it answered, the wryness was gone, and what remained was older and stranger and terribly kind.

*Because you were raised on songs about a country that doesn't exist, and instead of deciding the songs were nonsense, you went looking for the coastline. Because you felt my cousin whisper your name in Corvane's vault and you were frightened, but you didn't look away. Because you love a woman who lies to protect you, and you haven't decided yet whether to forgive her—and that undecided place, Astrid, that exact unresolved hinge, is where keepers live. Certainty makes tyrants. Doubt makes doors.*

"I don't want to be a door."

*Nobody does. That's how you know you're ready.*

The tide was coming in. I could feel it—the ink sea sliding up the paper shore, dissolving streets into their component sentences. The dream was ending, or being ended, and the flame shrank down to a single point of gold, a star caught in glass.

*One more thing,* it said, quick now, urgent. *When they offer you the safe lie—and someone will, soon, wearing a face you trust—remember this: I have watched a hundred years through lantern-glass. I have seen every member of the guild come and kneel and promise to keep me. Not one of them ever asked what I wanted. You are the first. That is the whole qualification. That is everything.*

"What do I tell Felix?"

The flame flared once, bright as a struck match.

*The truth. All of it. Even the parts that cost you. Especially those.*

Then the water took the shore, and the lantern, and me.

* * *

I woke with salt on my lips, which was nothing new, but also with the taste of smoke, which was.

The chapel antechamber was grey with pre-dawn. Rain ticked against the stones above us—the drowned chapel's tower broke the surface at low tide, and we'd learned to sleep in the hours when it did. Felix lay beside me, still deep under, her breathing even, her cut hand wrapped in linen I'd changed twice yesterday because she kept insisting she was fine and kept reopening it on door hinges like a woman determined to prove her blood was renewable.

For a long time I just watched her. This is an embarrassing confession for a woman who prides herself on clear sight, but there it is: I watched her the way you watch a chart you're afraid to correct. She had lied to me. She had carried the Codex's words about me in her pocket for weeks and said nothing, and I had found them, and she had lied badly, and then she had cut her hand open on a ward to bring me through a door into a country that officially did not exist, and both of these things were true, and I had been carrying them side by side like mismatched map sheets, waiting for one of them to prove false.

Neither had. That was the problem. She was genuinely sorry and genuinely protective and genuinely mine, and the lie and the devotion came from the same stubborn, terrified, loving place, and no amount of anger could make them separable.

The flame had asked me a question in the dream, without asking it: *what will you choose?*

I got up quietly, took the scrap of transcription from my own coat now—I'd confiscated it from her as evidence, which we had both pretended was a joke—and read it again by the light of a stub of candle.

*Astrid of the Tide-Line, keeper-or-killer. What she opens, lives. What she closes, burns.*

Below it, in Maren's cramped hand, the guild's interpretation: *The keeper guards the flame. The killer gives it away. The Codex is clear.*

Except the Codex wasn't clear. The Codex was a sentence written by frightened people a century ago and then guarded so jealously that no one had dared re-read it in living memory. Keeper-or-killer. Guard-or-give. The guild had assumed the choice was between safety and sin.

The flame had suggested a third reading: that the killer might be the only honest name on the page, and the guild had simply never had the courage to use it.

Felix stirred. "Astrid?" Her voice was rough with sleep, and wary in a way that hurt to hear—wary of me, of my silences, of the weather I carried. "You're up early."

"I dreamed," I said.

Her whole body tensed. In this country, in this story, that sentence had stopped meaning rest. "About what?"

I sat back down beside her. I took her unbandaged hand, the left one, and turned it over, and traced the lines of her palm the way I'd trace a coastline—because it calmed me, and because I wanted the last thing I memorized before speaking to be something true.

"The flame talked to me," I said. "Really talked. Not the whispering from the vault. Words. Well—map-words. Shape-truths. You know how a chart is a lie that agrees with itself until suddenly it isn't? It was like that."

Felix sat up slowly. "What did it say?"

"It said it doesn't want to be preserved." I made myself hold her eyes. "It says the guild hasn't been guarding it. It's been imprisoned. Politely. Devoutly. For a hundred years. And it wants to be given back—to the land, to the country, poured out until Vellmark burns again from the inside. And it says that when the door opens fully, it won't gutter out like the Warden claims. It'll *spread*. Or it dies trying. It's not sure which. Neither am I."

The silence stretched. Outside, the rain thickened; somewhere below us, the tide began its slow climb up the chapel steps, patient as a debt collector.

"Maren will say it's deception," Felix said finally. "The Warden. They'll say the flame is corrupted, or that you misread it, or that Corvane got into your head—"

"They'll say whatever keeps the door shut," I said. "That's the point, Felix. Everyone in this story has a reason to keep something closed. The guild has its guilt. The Warden has her century. Corvane has his vault. And you—" I squeezed her hand, gently, so she'd know the next part wasn't a blow but a bridge. "You had your reasons too. And I've decided something about them."

"Astrid—"

"I'm done with safe lies. Mine and yours." I said it plainly, without heat, and watched it land. "You lied to protect me and it nearly cost us everything. The Warden preserves the flame and it's killing her. The guild hides a country and calls it mercy. Every single person I've met since Greyharbor has chosen the lock, and the lock has eaten them all, one by one, politely, devoutly. I'm not doing it. Whatever happens next—Corvane, the Warden, the prophecy, all of it—I'm choosing the open version. Even if it costs. *Especially* if it costs."

Felix looked at me for a long moment. Rain, tide, candle-stub, her bandaged hand trembling slightly in mine.

"You're scaring me," she admitted.

"I know." I kissed her knuckles. "Me too. But I'd rather be scared with you than safe without you, and I'd rather burn a country awake than let it sleep another hundred years in a lantern. So. When the others wake up, I'm going to tell Maren what the flame said, and she's going to call me a killer, and I'm going to say yes. The Codex had it right all along. It just had the valence backwards."

Felix exhaled—a long, shaking breath, the sound of a woman setting down something heavy she'd forgotten she was carrying. Then, wry despite everything, because she was Felix: "You know the elders are going to excommunicate you from a guild with nine members."

"Eight," I said. "You're coming with me."

"Am I?"

"You cut your hand open for this door. You're implicated."

A ghost of her old smile. "God help me. I am."

Above us, the rain stopped all at once, the way rain does when the weather changes its mind, and through the arrow-slit window I saw the sky going strange—not dawn-grey but gold-shot, a light with a pulse in it, rising off the sea like something answering.

The flame, I thought. Feeling me decide. Or warning me.

Either way, the tide was coming in, and this time I intended to be standing on the right side of it when it did.

Down in the chapel nave, faint but unmistakable, a bell began to ring—an alarm none of us had set, in a country no one was supposed to be able to enter.

Corvane had found the door.
