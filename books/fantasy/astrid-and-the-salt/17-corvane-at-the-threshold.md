## 17. Corvane at the Threshold

The held hour inside Vellmark had a sound to it, once you learned to listen: a low, warm hum, like a hive dreaming of summer. For three days Astrid and I had walked its streets and I'd stopped flinching at the birdsong that never changed key, stopped counting the laundry lines where the same shirts dried in the same wind. It was beautiful the way a snow globe is beautiful, which is to say beautifully wrong, and I was trying very hard not to think about it when the humming stopped.

I felt it in my teeth first. Then in the guild mark on my wrist, which woke up for the first time since we'd crossed the Door and burned like a brand pressed to my pulse.

"Felix." Astrid was already looking east, toward the Door, toward the chapel we'd left standing open behind us like a wound. "The light."

The light was wrong. Vellmark's sun hung fixed in its eternal mid-afternoon, gold as honey, but now a bruise was spreading across it from the eastern hills — a darkness with edges, moving with the patient certainty of ink dropped into milk.

"The hoard," I said. "That's Corvane's hoard coming through."

* * *

We ran. There wasn't much else to do. The Codex rode against my chest in its sling of oiled leather, heavier than any book has a right to be, and every hundred paces it seemed to gain weight, as if it knew what was coming and wanted to be somewhere safe. Books are like cats that way. They know.

Behind us, the bruise spread. Where it passed, things didn't die so much as *forget themselves*. A hedgerow went grey and stood there like a sketch of a hedgerow. A well kept its stones but lost its water. And the hour itself — the held hour, the frozen afternoon the Warden had spent a century preserving — began to fray at those edges, threads of golden light pulling loose and drifting upward like smoke.

"It's eating the preservation," Astrid said, running beside me. She'd said it before, twice, but saying it again seemed to help her, the way some people say a prayer over and over not because the god needs convincing.

"Corvane isn't here to destroy anything," I said. "He's here to *collect*. He just doesn't understand that this whole country is one object, and you can't pocket an entire country without breaking it."

"Have you met him?"

"I've sold him three luck-charms under a false name. He haggled. A man who haggles with a charm-seller will absolutely try to hagle with a country."

The town square came up around us — the fountain with its stone mermaid, the bakery with bread forever half-risen in its window — and there, spilling out of the lane that led back to the chapel, came the guild.

All seven who were left. Maren at the front, leaning on her cane like it owed her money, her white braid whipping as she scanned the square. Behind her: Odile and Tam, the twins Brannagh, old Piet with his bad hip, and young Sef, nineteen and terrified and trying to hide it by gripping his candle-snuffer like a sword.

"You're supposed to be at the Door," Maren snapped, before I'd even caught my breath. "You're supposed to be *gone*, deeper in, with the Codex—"

"And leave you all to hold the line? You raised me better than that. Well. You raised me worse than that, actually, but the point stands."

Maren's mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling. "Sentiment. In my day we called it a tactical liability."

"In your day the guild still had wards that worked."

Something flickered across her face — guilt, quick as a fish under ice — and then the eastern sky split open and there was no more time for family history.

* * *

They came through the Door in formation, which told me everything about how long Corvane had planned this. Twelve inquisitors in their grey lacquered coats, walking two abreast down the chapel lane with the unhurried confidence of men who have never once been told no. And behind them, filling the lane wall to wall, came the hoard.

I don't know what I expected. Carts, maybe. Crates. What I got was a river.

Confiscated magic moves strangely when it's been caged too long. Charms crawled. Hexes slithered low to the ground like eels. There were objects I recognized from Greyharbor's seizure auctions — a music box that played your regrets, a mirror that showed you honest, a child's kite stitched from storm-cloud — and things older, stranger, that must have come from vaults deeper than the Minister's. All of it straining forward on invisible leashes, all of it *hungry*, because magic that's locked in a box for fifty years doesn't stay polite. It ferments.

And above it all, walking on the road like an ordinary man taking an ordinary stroll, came Minister Corvane.

He'd dressed down for the occasion. No ministerial sash, no chain of office. Just a plain dark coat and his mild, forgettable face, the face of a man you'd trust with your keys. That face had always frightened me more than any scowl could. Scowls tell you what they want. Corvane's face told you nothing, and took everything anyway.

"Astrid," he called, pleasantly, when he saw us. "And the charm-seller. How domestic."

"Minister." Astrid's voice was steady. Mine wasn't going to be, so I let her handle the pleasantries while I counted inquisitors and did arithmetic I didn't like.

"I confess I hoped you'd run further," he said. "It would have saved wear on the architecture. But then, sentiment runs in your family, doesn't it, Astrid. Your grandmother had it too. Right up until we needed her name for something useful."

Astrid went very still beside me. I put my hand on her arm. Not restraining. Just *there*.

"You can't take the flame," she said. "The Warden told us what happens. If you pull it out of the lantern, Vellmark unravels, and the fire goes with it. You'll have ash and a hole in the world."

"Yes," Corvane agreed, in the tone of a man agreeing about the weather. "The Warden always did lack ambition. She thought the flame was something to be *kept*. But fire, Astrid — real fire, last-of-its-kind fire — is a currency. And I am very rich, and I intend to be richer. If the country burns down around the transaction, well." He gestured at the fraying sky with something close to fondness. "Countries burn. It's practically traditional."

"He's lying," Maren said quietly, at my shoulder. "Or worse — he believes it. Either way, talking is done."

She was right. Because even as he spoke, Corvane unclipped something from his belt — a small brass whistle, innocuous, the kind dockhands use — and blew a single note that no living ear should have heard.

The hoard surged.

* * *

What followed, I will describe as best I can, though describing a magical siege is like describing a flood to someone who's only seen rain.

The inquisitors fanned out through the square and began driving the hoard forward with their whistles and their ward-rods, herding stolen magic like sheepdogs herd panic. And the hoard hit the held hour of Vellmark and began to *drink*.

Where the first wave touched, the afternoon dimmed. The fountain's water went still and grey. The stone mermaid's smile faded to blankness. A row of houses along the north side simply became *less* — not ruined, not burned, just thinner, as if someone had turned down the world's saturation.

"They're feeding the hoard on the preservation," Piet shouted, hobbling into position near the fountain. "Every street it eats makes the charms stronger—"

He was right, and it was worse than right. I could feel it through my guild mark: the confiscated magic swelling fat and gleeful on a century of stored time, growing brighter, hungrier, less controllable. Somewhere in that seething mass, something laughed. Objects shouldn't laugh. These days, apparently, they do.

"Guild of Small Mercies!" Maren's voice cracked across the square like a whip, and seven people — seven! — moved as one, because whatever else the guild was, it was drilled. We formed the circle. Old formation, older than me, older than Maren: candles out, snuffers ready, each of us keyed to one of the nine points of the guild's founding ward.

Except the ward had eight candles lit these days. Nine names, eight candles. And the circle has a hole in it where the ninth person should stand, and holes in circles are doors, and doors are what we were supposedly guarding.

The first hour of the siege, we held. I'll say that plainly, because I intend to say very few plain things after this: for one hour, seven guild members and one cartographer's assistant held a town square against the collected greed of a nation-state, and we held it.

Odile sang the binding verses — she had the voice for it, smoky and relentless — and the hoard slowed where her words fell. Tam and the Brannaghs worked the candle-snuffers along the ward-lines, pinning stray hexes to the cobblestones where they writhed and spat and dissolved. Piet anchored the fountain, hands flat on the mermaid's head, feeding her what little craft he had left. Sef — terrified, nineteen, magnificent Sef — discovered in the middle of a battle that his snuffing-craft was the strongest anyone had seen in a generation, and cried while he used it, and didn't stop working.

And Astrid stood at the center with the map of Vellmark spread on the cobbles, weighted with four smooth stones, reading the country's own geography aloud like a spell — because that's what it was, we'd learned that much. A cartographer who loves a place can speak it into strength. Every street name she said, every boundary she traced, made that street and that boundary *more real*, harder to drink.

And me. I stood between the Codex and the world, and I did what I've done my whole dishonest life: I lied to powerful things until they believed me.

Charms, mostly, thrown fast and cheap. False-scent charms to make the inquisitors' ward-rods read me as one of them. Misdirection hexes to send the hoard lunging after phantoms. One truly inspired piece of work where I convinced a captured luck-charm — a big one, municipal-grade, seized from Greyharbor's old lottery house — that Corvane was its original owner and had come to free it. It turned on its handlers with forty years of pent-up gratitude and took three inquisitors off the board before they figured out the trick.

"Eastwick!" Corvane's voice, cutting through the din, genuinely amused. "Is that *you* doing that? The little charm-peddler from Fishgutter Lane?"

"Customer retention," I called back, "is the soul of small business."

"I offered you a position once."

"You offered me a cage with better lighting."

"Well," he said, and sighed, and nodded to the tall inquisitor at his left hand. "Then we do this the expensive way."

* * *

The betrayal, when it came, came from the direction I least wanted to look.

Tam broke first. I want to be fair to him — Tam, who'd carried the guild's ledgers for thirty years, who'd taught me to snuff a candle-wraith when I was twelve, whose hands shook now as he lowered his snuffer and stepped backward out of the circle.

"They're going to win," he said, to all of us, to no one. "Look at it. Look at what's coming through that door. Seven of us against *that*. Maren, please. He said the guild would be pardoned. He said—"

"He said," Maren repeated, terribly quiet, "and you believed him. Thirty years of ledgers, Tam, and you never learned to audit a promise."

Two inquisitors crossed the square to collect him, unhurried, courteous. That was the worst part. Nobody even gloated. Tam was simply a line item being reconciled.

The circle broke where he'd stood, and the hoard poured through the gap like water finding the crack in a dam.

After that, it was arithmetic again, and the arithmetic was ugly. We gave ground. The fountain went — Piet stayed with it too long, and Odile dragged him clear with the mermaid's face already smoothing into blankness behind them. The bakery went. The lane of hanging shirts went, and I thought, absurdly, *those shirts have been drying for a hundred years and now they'll never be dry,* and then a hex the size of a dog hit me in the side and thinking became a luxury.

I remember the ground. Cobblestones, warm as skin — the held hour keeping even the pavement at perfect afternoon temperature. I remember Astrid screaming my name from the center of the square. I remember the Codex's sling strap snapping, the book sliding free across the stones, falling open to a page I'd never seen, and light pouring up out of it like a struck match.

And I remember crawling. Crawling on my elbows through a battlefield of drunk, bloated, stolen magic, with blood filling my boot and my ribs announcing their resignation, toward a book, because the book was the last true thing in a square full of lies, and I had spent my whole life among lies and I knew — the way you know your own front door in the dark — that this one was worth bleeding on.

An inquisitor reached it first. Tall, grey coat, ward-rod leveled at the open pages.

"No," I said. My voice came out wrecked. I said it anyway, and I put my body between the rod and the book, and I looked up at him and waited to stop existing.

The rod wavered.

"Move, charm-seller," he said, and his voice was strange. Thick. "Please move. Don't make me—"

"Hale," I said. Because I'd finally placed him — the inquisitor who wept, the one who'd let us go on the drowned coast, the man raised on lullabies from a country that officially didn't exist. "You're standing in Vellmark, Hale. Listen to it. Just for a second. Listen."

And he did. God help us both, he did. His eyes went past me, past the Codex, to the hills beyond the square — the ones still holding their shape, still gold, still *real* — and his rod dipped, and something in his face broke along a seam that had been waiting years for permission.

Then Corvane's voice came across the square, mild as milk: "Hale. The book."

Hale closed his eyes.

And the hoard hit us both.

* * *

I woke to Astrid's hands pressing my side and her voice saying my name in the tone people use when they've said it many times already and gotten nothing back. The pain arrived a moment later, punctual as rent.

"Don't," I managed. "Don't stop the bleeding entirely. Leave a little. It's very becoming."

"You're delirious."

"I'm *witty*. Delirious is when I start reviewing your cartography."

Her laugh came out wet and wrong, and she bent over me, and behind her the square was lost. Half of it gone to grey, the hoard ramping fat and bright through the ruins, inquisitors everywhere, Maren and the survivors backed against the chapel lane with snuffers blazing. The held hour was guttering like a candle in a doorway. I could see the edges of the sky now, actual edges, where Vellmark simply stopped.

And at the center of it all, Corvane stood before the fallen Codex, reading.

He read the way he did everything — patiently, thoroughly, with the air of a man confirming a price he'd already negotiated. Then he looked up, found my eyes across the ruin of the square, and smiled his forgettable smile.

"There are two names on the last page," he called to me. "Unwritten. Blank. Do you know what that means, Eastwick? It means the prophecy hasn't decided. It means the ending is still *for sale*." He lifted the Codex in both hands, tenderly, the way he'd lift anything he intended to own. "Everything is for sale. You taught me that, charm-seller. You and everyone like you."

The Codex vanished into his coat, and the light in the sky guttered, and somewhere far away — or very close; by then I couldn't tell — the last dragon-fire drew a breath that sounded like a sob.

Astrid pulled my arm across her shoulders and stood, lifting me, this woman with ink-stained fingers and no magic at all except the kind you can't confiscate.

"We get it back," she said. Not a question. Not a hope. An invoice.

The chapel lane was still open. The Door was still ours, barely, for now.

We went.
