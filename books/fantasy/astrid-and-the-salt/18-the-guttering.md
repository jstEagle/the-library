## 18. The Guttering

**FELIX**

I have been in exactly one siege before this one, and it was a card game at Elder Piotr's house where the stakes were pastries and the loser had to sing. I lost. I sang. Nobody dissolved into salt.

This was going less well.

Corvane came through the Door the way water comes through a cracked hull — not all at once, but with a patience that was worse than violence. First his hoard. That's what I couldn't stop looking at: not the man, but the *collection* of him. He walked into Vellmark trailing confiscated magic like a comet of stolen light, and every charm he'd swallowed over twenty years of ministerial appetite strained against its bindings, hungry for the held hour around us.

The hour didn't like being tasted.

"Stay behind me," Maren said, which would have been more convincing if she hadn't said it while standing directly in front of the Codex, arms spread, looking for all the world like a woman defending a bread stall from pigeons. Nine guild members. Eight candles' worth of ritual left unlit because we'd spent our last snuffing on the Door. Against a minister, six inquisitors, and whatever Corvane had become by eating other people's birthrights for two decades.

The arithmetic was bad. The arithmetic had always been bad. We were a guild of small mercies facing a man who collected large ones.

"Felix." Astrid's hand found mine in the dark behind the chapel wall. Her palm was slick. "The lantern."

I looked. At the heart of the square, where the Warden had kept her vigil for a hundred years, the lantern sat on its stone plinth — and the flame inside it was doing something flames should not do. It was *leaning*. Bending toward Corvane like a plant toward a window, like a child toward a stranger holding out sweets.

"It can smell him," Astrid whispered. "All that magic. It thinks—" She stopped. Swallowed. "It thinks it's found something to burn."

"That's not comforting."

"I wasn't going for comforting. I was going for accurate."

There she was. My cartographer, my tide-line girl, standing in a country that had been unwritten before either of us was born, giving me accuracy instead of comfort because she'd learned somewhere that I could survive on the first and drown on the second.

I loved her so much it was frankly inconvenient.

Then Corvane spoke, and the square stopped being a square.

* * *

**ASTRID**

He didn't shout. That was the horror of it. Ministers who shout are performing; ministers who murmur have already won.

"You've brought guests," Corvane said to the Warden, or to the country, or possibly to no one, his voice carrying the way sound carries underwater. His inquisitors fanned out along the chapel colonnade, and their boots made no prints in the dust. No prints. As if the ground beneath them had already agreed to forget they'd ever stood there. "A century of careful keeping, and you let two girls walk in through the front door."

"The door was always meant to open," the Warden said. She sounded tired in a way I recognized — the tiredness of someone who has done the same brave thing so long it has worn grooves in her soul. "That was the whole point of keeping it."

"And look how well that's gone." He gestured, lazily, at the sky.

I looked up and my stomach dropped through the cobblestones.

Vellmark's sky had been wrong since we arrived — too still, too golden, the light of an afternoon that never ended. But now there were seams in it. Hairline fractures running from horizon to horizon, and through each fracture I could see... nothing. Not darkness. Nothing. The color of a page before anyone writes on it, if pages could be a place.

"He's spending it," Felix breathed beside me. "His hoard is *eating* the held hour."

She was right, and worse than right. Every step Corvane took, the street behind him changed. A baker's awning went grey, then translucent, then simply wasn't, and where it had been there was fine white powder drifting on a wind that shouldn't exist indoors. Salt. The buildings weren't burning. They were *dissolving* — unraveling back into the sea they'd been conjured out of, or the memory they'd been preserved as, or whatever raw material you use to hold a country in amber for a hundred years.

A woman stepped out of a doorway ahead of us. One of the frozen townsfolk, mid-century, mid-laugh, a basket of apples on her hip. She took three steps and came apart at the shoulders like wet paper, and the apples rolled, and then the apples were salt too, and then the doorway, and then the woman entirely, and the laugh hung in the air a moment longer than she did.

Felix made a sound I'd never heard from her. Small. Wounded. The sound of a charm-seller watching the largest charm in the world fail.

"We have to move," I said. "Now. To the lantern."

"Astrid—"

"*Now*, Felix. Whatever the fire wants, it isn't going to get a chance to want anything if he reaches it first."

* * *

**FELIX**

We ran. Behind us, Corvane's voice followed like weather.

"Guildmaster Maren." Amiable. Dreadful. "You've brought your little remnant out of hiding. Nine of you? I counted eight candles at the Door. Someone's been skipping rituals."

"They died," Maren said flatly. "Tomas. Two weeks ago. Your inquisitors took him and he didn't come back, and we burned his candle anyway, because that's what the ritual is *for*. You wouldn't know about rituals that cost the performer something. Everything you own was taken from people who paid."

Something in the air shifted. I felt it in my teeth — the particular pressure change of a ward straining. And then, from the direction of the Door, a sound I will hear until I die: a candle going out. Not blown. *Snuffed*, the way ours did, the way the guild's protective rite had done for a hundred years — except this time nothing replaced the light. This time the dark just stayed dark, and through the chapel archway I saw the Door itself flicker like a lamp with a dying wick.

The ritual was failing. Had failed. For the first time in a century, the guild's protection was a word in a dead language.

"No," Piotr said behind me. Old Piotr, who taught me my first sleight-of-hand, whose hands shook now as he stared at the guttering Door. "No, no, the snuffing holds, the snuffing *always* holds—"

"Piotr." Maren didn't turn. "Get to the Door. Hold it with your body if you have to. Body is the only thing left that works."

And that — that was when Hale moved.

* * *

**ASTRID**

I had been watching Inquisitor Hale since Greyharbor, since the drowned coast, since the night he let us go with saltwater in his eyes and a warning in his mouth. I'd told myself he was a loose thread. Convenient. Temporary. Men like him always snapped back to their masters eventually; mercy was a loan, and loans got called.

So when he raised his hand toward Corvane, I assumed he was signaling. Pointing us out. Finishing the job.

What he did instead was put himself between Corvane and the lantern.

"Hale," Corvane said, and there was genuine surprise in it, which told me everything about how rarely this man was surprised. "Step aside."

"No."

"You were raised better than this. By the state. By *me*."

"Yes," Hale said. His voice cracked down the middle and he let it. "On lullabies I wasn't supposed to remember. In a language the maps say never existed. You fed us Vellmark in our cradles and called it folklore, Minister, and then you built a vault full of everything you stole from it, and you thought none of us would ever wonder what the songs were *about*." He drew his blade — not at Corvane, at the space between them, a line drawn in the oldest way there is. "I wondered. I've been wondering my whole life."

Two of the six inquisitors turned to face him. The other four hesitated, and hesitation, in a place where a country was coming apart at the seams, was its own kind of treason.

Corvane sighed, and opened his coat, and the hoard looked out.

I don't have better words than that. Charms and relics and bottled spells, hundreds of them, sewn into the lining like scales, and every one of them turned — I swear this — *turned* to look at us. Light bent wrong around him. The nearest seam in the sky widened.

"Last chance," he said pleasantly. "Bring me the Codex and the flame, and I'll let the guild walk out. I'm generous. Ask anyone I haven't ruined."

"Maren," Felix shouted across the square, "the Codex — do NOT give him the—"

But Maren was already moving, and not away.

* * *

**FELIX**

Elder Maren, Guildmaster of the Order of Small Mercies, seventy-one years old with a bad hip and a walking stick she'd once used to beat a tax collector, walked to the Door of Vellmark and put her back against it.

"Come take it," she said.

Corvane sent the hoard.

I will not describe all of it. Some things you carry folded up small so you can keep walking. What I can tell you is that Maren held. Not with wards — the wards were ash. Not with ritual — the ritual was spent. She held with the only currency the guild had ever truly traded in, the one we'd been spending quietly for a hundred years while pretending we dealt in charms: she held with *refusal*. Pure, stubborn, unglamorous refusal, the kind that doesn't glow or sing but simply does not move.

The stolen magic broke against her like surf.

"Go," she said through her teeth, and blood was already at the corner of her mouth, and the Door's frame was cracking above her head in patterns like frost. "Felix. *Girl.* Look at me."

I looked. God help me, I looked.

"You spent your whole life hiding," she said. "Your mark, your craft, your name. I taught you that hiding. I owe you a debt for every year of it." The hoard pressed again; her boots slid an inch on the stone; she set herself and stopped sliding. "So here's the repayment. Stop hiding. Take her to the fire. Do the thing we were too ashamed to do for a hundred years, and when they ask who you are — and they *will* ask, everyone asks eventually — tell them your name like it costs you nothing."

"Maren—"

"I'm not asking. I'm guildmastering. There's a difference and you're about to learn it."

Piotr reached her first, then Sefa, then the others, forming a chain of bodies and old hands and ninety combined years of small mercies between Corvane and the Door. It was the worst tactical decision I have ever witnessed and the finest thing I have ever seen.

Maren caught my eye one last time. She smiled. It was the smile she gave when a charm worked perfectly and nobody clapped.

"Burn bright," she said. "It's the only prayer we ever had."

Then the hoard came down like a wave, and the Door flared white, and I turned and ran for the lantern because staying would have killed me and she had spent her death making sure dying wasn't my job anymore.

* * *

**ASTRID**

The flame was almost out.

Up close, in the last clean circle of stone around the plinth, I could see what the Warden had tried to tell us and grief had made me refuse to hear: the fire wasn't strong. It had never been strong. It was a coal pretending to be a blaze, kept alive by a century of stillness, of hiding, of a whole country held its breath so one small light could go on pretending it was warm.

Hiding, I understood suddenly, kills you slowly and calls it safety. I had watched Felix do it for a year. I had done it myself my whole life, folding my dreams small, telling no one about the burning door. And here was a nation that had done it so well it had nearly succeeded in not existing.

The flame leaned toward me.

Not toward Corvane. Toward *me*. And it spoke — not in words, not quite, but in the language of my dreams, salt and heat and a door standing open:

*Give me back.*

"I know," I said aloud, and my voice broke on it. "I know. I'm coming."

Behind me: shouting, white light, the crack of the Door's frame, Felix's voice saying my name like a rope thrown to someone drowning. Ahead of me: the lantern, and the unraveling streets, and a choice that had never actually been a choice, only a road I'd been walking since the first dream.

I picked it up.

The lantern weighed nothing. That was the terrible part. A hundred years of a country's heart, and it weighed nothing at all, because things freely given never do.

* * *

**FELIX**

I reached her as the square began to go.

The cobblestones under our feet had started to drift — actual grains lifting off the ground and hanging in the air like the world had forgotten which way down was. Through the widening seams overhead, the nothing pressed in. Somewhere behind us Maren was still holding, or had stopped holding, and I could not let myself know which, because knowing would put me on my knees and Astrid needed me walking.

"Astrid." I caught her wrist. The lantern swung between us, its flame barely a thumbnail now, blue at the edges. "Tell me you have a plan."

"I have a direction," she said. "The heart of it. Where the chapel stood before they flooded it. The fire goes back into the land or nothing survives — not Vellmark, not the flame, not us."

"Nothing survives. Great. Love a plan with honest marketing."

Her mouth twitched — even now, even here, my girl — and then the ground shuddered and half the colonnade lifted off its foundations and came apart into a slow white snowfall of salt, and her hand closed hard around mine.

"Together?" she said.

It was the same word from the night market, from the coast road, from every small ordinary evening I'd lied to her through. Together. I had spent my whole life learning a hundred ways to hide and she had spent hers learning one way to be found, and the Door was breaking behind us and Maren's name was in my throat like a stone and the sky was coming apart in seams, and none of it mattered even slightly, because she asked, and the answer had been yes since the beginning.

"Together," I said.

We ran into the unraveling heart of Vellmark, carrying the last light of a country that everyone had sworn never existed — and behind us, faint through the roar of the dissolving world, I heard the Door give way, and I did not look back.

Some inheritances you carry forward. Hers, I carried in my hand.

Ahead, the salt was rising.
