## 20. Rekindling

**Astrid**

The lantern weighed nothing.

That was the first lie of it, I think—the way it sat in my hands like an empty jar, like something you could carry to market and back without noticing. The second lie was the light itself, which had gone thin as broth, guttering in its glass the way a candle gutters when someone has left a window open somewhere far away. It should have felt heavy. It was holding a country. But grief is always lighter than you expect until you try to put it down.

Behind me, Vellmark was coming apart.

I don't mean burning. Burning would have been honest. I mean *unwriting*—the cobbler's shop on Lantern Row losing its roof first, then its walls, then the memory of walls, the street beneath it going pale and grainy like paper held too long over steam. A woman's laundry line dissolved mid-swing, shirts becoming salt-smoke becoming nothing, and somewhere under my ribs I understood that those shirts had been hanging there for a hundred years, waiting for no one, and now they would never come down at all.

Corvane's hoard was eating the hour. That was the phrase the Warden had used, back when there was still a Warden and still an hour to hold: *it eats the hour*. All that confiscated magic, all those stolen charms and bottled blessings and names pried out of families like teeth—piled up in one man's vault, they didn't sit quietly. They hungered. And what they hungered for was exactly this: a place where time had been stopped and stored, a larder of preserved miracle, and Corvane had kicked the door in with forty inquisitors behind him and let them feed.

Felix caught up to me on the chapel steps—or where the chapel steps had been. The tide was in, black and glittering, and the water came right up to the threshold of the unraveling world like a customer leaning over a counter.

"Astrid." Her voice did the thing it always did when she was frightened and trying to sell me calm: went warm, went easy, went about half a note too smooth. "The Warden said if we open the door fully, the flame goes out. She said—"

"She said it stays lit while Vellmark stays hidden. Yes." I looked down at the lantern. "She also said the flame doesn't want preserving. It wants giving back. Those are two different sentences, Felix, and I've decided to believe the second one."

"That's not deciding, that's gambling."

"I'm a cartographer's assistant from Greyharbor who found out last month she's in a prophecy book. Gambling is the only trade I've got."

She laughed. It was a bad laugh, cracked down the middle, but it was hers, and I held onto it the way you hold onto a rail in rough water.

"Tell me what to do," she said. "You're the keeper. Tell me."

And there it was—that word again, *keeper*, that had been chasing me since the Codex first put it next to my name in ink older than my grandmother. Keeper or killer. Save or doom. Everyone from Elder Maren to Minister Corvane to a weeping inquisitor had told me what the flame needed from me, and every version had been a cage with nicer upholstery than the last.

But the flame itself had never once asked me to keep anything.

It had come to me in dreams for years—a burning door, salt water, a country singing—and not once had it said *guard me* or *hide me* or *spend yourself on my behalf*. What it said, the night before last, lying in Felix's arms in a house that was busy forgetting it existed, was simpler than any of it. It said:

*I was given. Give me.*

So I knelt at the edge of the drowned chapel, where the ward keyed to guild blood had opened for Felix's palm, and I pulled the stopper from the lantern, and I poured the last dragon-fire into the sea.

* * *

**Felix**

I want it noted, by whatever god keeps books on charm-sellers, that I did not stop her.

There were a hundred reasons to stop her. Maren had spent her whole life on reasons; the guild had spent a century building a religion out of them. *The fire stays lit only while the door stays shut.* Nine members, eight candles, one sacred arithmetic: one country's freedom traded against one flame's survival, forever, amen. I'd been raised on that sum the way other children are raised on prayers, and every instinct I own—which is a professional instinct, honed by years of selling luck to people who need it—screamed at me to grab Astrid's wrist and say *wait, wait, there's another way, there's always another way, that's literally my job.*

But I watched her kneel at the waterline with the lantern in both hands, this woman I had lied to and been lied to about, this woman whose name was written in a dead language in a book my guild would kill to control, and I thought: *every other way is just a slower version of this same choice.* Keep it hidden and watch it gutter anyway, because Corvane had already breached the door and his hoard was already drinking the hour. Or give it away, all at once, freely, and find out whether the Codex's cruelest sentence had a second clause nobody read aloud.

Astrid tipped the lantern.

The flame didn't fall. Fire doesn't fall, not really—not when it's the last of its kind and it has been waiting a hundred years for someone to ask. It rose. It came out of the glass like a breath held too long finally let go, and it went *down*, into the black tide, into the salt, into the drowned foundations of a chapel and the drowned streets beyond it and the drowned everything, and for one horrible heartbeat nothing happened at all.

Then the sea lit up from underneath.

I have sold light charms. I've bottled dawn for insomniacs and sunset for romantics and one truly regrettable batch of glow for a fishmonger who wanted his eels to look fresher than they were. I know light. This was not light. This was *land remembering it was land*—the tide going gold from below, then silver, then simply gone, running backward off streets that were reassembling themselves out of salt and certainty, cobbles knitting, walls rising, roofs un-dissolving, the laundry line snapping taut again with shirts on it that had never actually stopped existing, only been *unadmitted*. The air filled with the smell of bread baking somewhere, because apparently a hundred years of held hour doesn't stop the ovens, it just makes them patient.

And sound. God, the sound. A whole country inhaling at once.

Astrid stood in the middle of it with empty hands, soaked to the knees, laughing and crying in the ugly way people do when they've stopped being able to afford dignity, and I got to her as fast as the reborn cobblestones would let me, which was fast, because they were eager, because everything here was eager, everything had been waiting a century for permission.

"You gambled," I said.

"We gambled," she said. "You didn't stop me."

"I'm noting it for the record."

"There's a record?"

"Honey, there's a *Codex*."

* * *

**Astrid**

Here is what I will tell my grandchildren someday, if the world is kind enough to give me some and cruel enough to leave me alive to describe it: the moment Vellmark came back, the light did not stay politely within its borders.

Light never does. That's rather the point of it.

It went up off the rooftops of the resurrected city like heat-shimmer made visible, a column of gold standing on the horizon, and then it bent—south, toward Greyharbor, toward the Door, toward the man standing in the doorway with forty inquisitors and a vault's worth of stolen magic strapped to his soul like armor.

Minister Corvane had come personally. Of course he had. Men like him always do; they can't bear for the ending to happen without them in the front row. He stood at the threshold of the Door with his hoard around him—I could see it even from the chapel steps, or feel it, rather, the way you feel a storm in your teeth—all those confiscated charms and chained blessings and bottled names he'd spent thirty years prying out of people like me, worn close, hoarded, *kept*. Everything my guild believed about keeping, wearing a minister's seal.

The returning light touched him the way sunrise touches frost.

He screamed. I'll be honest with you, whoever ends up reading this: I expected to feel guilty about how much I didn't feel guilty. I braced for it, the way you brace for cold water. It didn't come. What came instead was something I can only call *bookkeeping*—the deep, clean satisfaction of a ledger balancing. Because that's what his power had always been: ours. Every charm in his vault had been taken from someone, every blessing confiscated from some family that sang to it at night, and magic, it turns out, remembers whose it is. The light wasn't destroying him. It was *returning deliveries*.

His hoard burned off him in streams—gold threads tearing loose and flying south and east and west, home to their owners or their owners' graves, and with each thread that left him he shrank. Not physically. He stayed a big man in a good coat. But the *size* of him, the pressure he exerted on the air, the way rooms rearranged themselves around his wanting—all of it drained away, until what stood at the Door was just a tired bureaucrat in expensive clothes, watching thirty years of accumulation turn to ash on the wind.

The inquisitors threw down their weapons one by one. Not all of them. Some ran. But enough—enough that I understood Hale hadn't been an exception after all. He'd been a majority, waiting for permission.

Felix's hand found mine. Her palm was still cut from the chapel ward, healing crooked the way guild cuts do, and her grip hurt, and I didn't say so.

"So," she said, in the voice she uses when she's about to sell something outrageous, "any chance the flame does refunds?"

"Felix."

"Because I'm just saying, we gave it the entire country, and I'd like to at least get a receipt—"

I kissed her. It seemed structurally necessary. Around us, Vellmark blazed and sang and baked its bread, and somewhere south, a corrupt minister walked back through a door that no longer had anything to guard, carrying nothing, which was, I reflected against her mouth, the first honest weight he'd held in decades.

* * *

**Felix**

Later—much later, when the gold had settled into ordinary daylight and the reborn city had remembered how to be a place instead of a miracle—Maren found us sitting on the chapel steps, sharing a heel of bread that some unseen oven had produced specifically, I'm convinced, to make a point.

The old woman looked terrible. She looked, in fact, like a woman whose life's work had just been spent in a single evening, which was accurate. Nine names, eight candles, a hundred years of vigilance, and it had ended with a cartographer's assistant pouring the family silver into the ocean and the ocean saying *thank you*.

"The Codex," she said. No greeting. There never was, with Maren; ceremony was for people with time.

"What about it?" Astrid asked.

"It's writing." Maren held the book out—it had survived the siege, somehow, the way important books do, the way cats and debts do. The final page, the blank one, the one that had named Astrid alone in letters like frost: it was moving. Ink surfacing slowly, the way things surface in tide-pools. Two lines. Two names, being written by no hand either of us could see.

Astrid leaned closer and went very still.

"Maren," she said carefully. "This says *keeper of the last flame* twice."

"I see that."

"It's wrong. The flame isn't last anymore. It's everywhere. It's in the ground. It's in the *bread*."

Maren smiled. It was a terrible smile, exhausted and relieved and about ten percent bitter, which for Maren was practically giddy. "Child, the Codex has been wrong about one thing or another since the day we locked this country away. We wrote it to warn ourselves. Turns out it was a set of instructions, and we were too ashamed to follow them." She closed the book and pressed it into Astrid's hands—not mine, though I was the guild heir, though the blood in my palm had opened the ward. Into Astrid's. "The guild is dissolving. Tonight. There's nothing left to guard."

"Maren—"

"Don't argue with your elder. It's undignified at your age and unforgivable at mine." She turned to go, then paused, looking out at the city—the lit windows, the smoke from chimneys, a child somewhere shrieking with what was unmistakably joy. "Your mother's family came from Vellmark, Felix. Did I ever tell you? Eastwick. It's a Vellmark name. It means *east of the tide-line*."

She walked away down the street that had un-dissolved, an old woman in a dying guild's colors, and I sat there holding Astrid's hand and a piece of bread and a name I'd carried all my life without knowing it was luggage from somewhere.

"Well," Astrid said eventually. "East of the tide-line. That's here, isn't it? We're standing in it."

"Apparently I've been homesick my whole life without knowing the address."

She laughed, and the light caught her face, real sunlight, unhurried, belonging to an afternoon in a country that had its name back, and far to the south I could just make out figures on the road—refugees, exiles, the unwritten, walking north through an open door toward a city that was singing to welcome them home.

One of them was carrying a white flag and walking faster than the rest.

I squinted. Then I stood up so fast the bread fell.

"Astrid," I said. "That's Hale. And he's not slowing down, and he's waving the terms of surrender, and—" I grabbed her hand. "—and somebody has to be here to negotiate with Greyharbor, and the guild just dissolved itself, which means, horrifyingly, that the responsible parties are *us*."

Astrid looked at the approaching inquisitor, then at me, then at the bread on the ground with genuine mourning.

"I liked it better when our biggest problem was hiding your guild mark from patrols," she said. But she was smiling, and her hand was tight in mine, and together we walked east of the tide-line to meet whatever came through the door next.
