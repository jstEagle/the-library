## 1. The Road That Isn't There

The wind came down off the high shoulders of the Thornfells like a debt collector.

Sable Marchetti stood at the mouth of the pass an hour before dawn, boots planted on scree that had never known a cartwheel, and read the sky the way other people read letters from home. There was no letter. There hadn't been one in six years. But the sky still wrote to them—had always written to them—and Sable had learned, after everything, to open those messages with one hand and keep the other hand pressed flat against their own chest, as if holding shut a door they'd nailed closed from the inside.

Snow above the col. Wind veering northwest by midmorning, hard enough to strip a man off a ledge. A window of maybe four hours before the weather turned ugly, and ugly, in the Thornfells in late autumn, meant dead.

They could feel all of it. That was the trouble. The pressure dropping along the ridge like a finger drawn down a wound. The static prickling at the roots of their hair, faint as a whisper in an empty house. Somewhere past the eastern peaks, two air masses were grinding against each other, and Sable's body knew it before any instrument would, knew it the way a dog knows thunder before the first crack. The old gift. The old curse. They had spent six years teaching themselves not to answer when it called.

So they stood there with their hand on their chest and translated.

"Four hours," they said aloud, to no one, because saying it aloud made it a decision instead of a feeling. "Maybe five if we push the mules."

Nobody answered. Nobody was there yet. That was the point of this road: nobody was ever here.

* * *

The smugglers arrived at first light, seven wagons' worth of them, or rather six wagons and one long, low sledge hauling tanned hides and untaxed brandy and a crate of something the manifest described, in Liss's careful forgery of a hand, as *agricultural implements*. Sable had met the caravan's owner twice before and both times had been careful to be someone slightly different—a surveyor once, a widowed trapper another time—because being the same person twice was how you got remembered, and being remembered was how you got found.

This time they were Master Aldous Vey, retired guide, references available upon request, though no one had ever requested them. It was a good name. Plain enough not to invite curiosity, odd enough to seem real. Sable wore it like a coat borrowed off a scarecrow.

"You're certain about this pass?" The caravan master was a broad, gray-bearded man named Ferro who chewed the same blackroot stick he'd chewed for twenty years and trusted nothing he couldn't spit on. "My grandfather ran these hills and he never mentioned a way through the White Teeth."

"Your grandfather wasn't looking." Sable kept their voice level, unhurried, the voice of a person with nothing to hide because there was so thoroughly nothing left to find. "There's a seam in the rock where the glacier cut back during the thaw, forty years ago. It closes a little more every winter. In three years it won't exist. Right now it does."

"And the wardens?"

"The wardens have a map of the passes that are worth taxing." Sable turned and started walking, because a guide who explained too much began to sound like a guide with something to prove. "This one isn't worth anything until someone makes it worth something. We move before it occurs to anyone."

Ferro spat, considered, and gave the signal. Wheels groaned. The caravan came up the frozen creek bed like a funeral procession that had gotten lost on its way to somewhere profitable.

Sable walked point, as always, alone, as always.

That was the arrangement. Guides who walked with the caravan made conversation, and conversation was a ledger, and every entry in a ledger was a thread someone could pull. So Sable walked fifty yards ahead, close enough to be useful, far enough to be a silhouette against the snow—a shape without a face, a voice without a history. The smugglers joked about them the way people joke about figures in stories: *the ghost of the pass*, Ferro's men called them, half-mocking, half-serious, because no one had ever seen the ghost eat, or sleep, or bleed, and the ghost always knew which side of the mountain the weather would come from.

Let them joke. Jokes didn't ask questions.

The pass itself was exactly what Sable had promised: a seam, narrow enough that the wagon beds scraped stone on both sides, walled in ice the color of old bottle glass. Above them the White Teeth caught the early light and burned orange, beautiful in the way that dangerous things are permitted to be beautiful, since beauty costs them nothing. Sable did not look up much. Looking up was for tourists. Sable looked at the snow underfoot, reading it the way Odile Vane—the only person left alive who might have called them a friend, if either of them believed in such things—read her mold-eaten manuscripts: for structure, for age, for intent.

Wind-packed, meaning recent wind, meaning the drifts ahead would be firm enough to bear a wheel. No fresh slide debris across the seam floor, meaning the slopes above had held through the night's cold snap. A single set of chamois tracks crossing diagonally, hours old, heading down and out—meaning the animals had left the high ground before dawn, meaning the weather had told them something, and Sable felt a cold thread of confirmation slide down their spine. The animals always knew first. The animals had never sworn anything off.

Four hours. Maybe less now. Sable adjusted the number silently and said nothing, because guides who revised their forecasts out loud sounded nervous, and nervous guides got questioned, and questioned guides got names attached to them.

* * *

By midday they were through the worst of the seam and into the hanging valley beyond, a bowl of white silence ringed by peaks, and this was where Sable hated it most—not the danger but the quiet. The kind of quiet that gets inside you and takes inventory.

Six years ago there had been a village. Ashford Hollow, down in the low country where the storms came up the river valley fat and slow and mostly harmless, summer rain, winter snow, the ordinary traffic of sky. Sable had grown up reading that weather like scripture, had been the child who announced hail before it fell and the teenager who could stand on the church roof in a gale and feel, with absolute certainty, where the lightning wanted to go. A gift. Everyone said so. A gift, right up until the autumn the sky went strange and green-black over the western hills and Sable, nineteen years old and certain of their own power, called the storm off the mountains to break it apart over empty moorland—

—and the moorland was not empty.

The storm had done what storms do. It had gone where it was sent and then some, spilling down the valley like a flood given claws, and it took the granary, and the mill, and the row of houses by the bridge, and eleven people, among them Sable's mother, who had spent her whole life telling everyone who'd listen that her child was going to be famous someday. Famous. She had meant it kindly. The word had come true in the cruelest possible translation: for two seasons afterward, Sable Marchetti's name was in every mouth in three counties, spoken with awe in some and bile in others, printed in warden reports, argued over in taverns, entered into ledgers. *The storm-caller of Ashford Hollow.* A name is a handle. The world had picked Sable up by theirs and shaken them until everything fell out.

So Sable had put the name down. Had walked north into the Thornfells with nothing, had unlearned, deliberately, methodically, the way you'd unlearn a language—refusing the gift every time it rose, pressing it down below the threshold of action, keeping only the passive sense, the reading, never the answering. Reading the sky was safe. Reading was just listening, and listening committed you to nothing. Calling—that was a signature on someone else's death warrant. They had signed it once. Never again.

And the world, obligingly, had let them go. Records burned in the Ashford parish fire two winters after the storm—an accident, probably, though Sable had never entirely believed in probably. The warden who'd filed the original report died of a fever. The taverns forgot. Grief has a short shelf life in the low country; scandal shorter. Within three years, *storm-caller Marchetti* had faded from a cautionary tale into a rumor, from a rumor into a story drunkards told, and from a story into nothing at all. Sable had watched themselves be erased with the peculiar gratitude of a person watching their own house burn down when what they wanted most was to not live in it anymore.

It had worked. That was the terrible thing. It had worked completely. Sable Marchetti existed now only in the memory of one archivist in a hut full of rotting paper, and in the muscle-deep reflexes of their own body, and nowhere else on earth. No debts, because debts required a debtor. No family, because family required acknowledgment. No home address, because a home was the first thing a hunter looks for. They owned a cabin so remote that reaching it required three days' walk from the last honest road, and even that they thought of not as home but as *the place where the gear doesn't freeze*, because thinking of a place as home was how you started leaving traces in it—a chair moved, a habit formed, smoke rising at the same hour each evening until some passing trapper thought: *someone lives there,* and told someone else.

Sable had gotten very good at not existing. It was, they sometimes thought bitterly, the one skill the world had ever rewarded them for.

But loneliness is the tax on invisibility, and the Crown collected it daily.

* * *

The trouble started, as trouble in the Thornfells always did, with the light.

It was the quality of it, past the third hour—too clean, too hard, the shadows going knife-edged when they should have been softening toward afternoon. Sable stopped at the head of the column and stood very still with their hand flat against their chest, and beneath the palm, beneath the ribs, beneath the six years of deliberate silence, something stirred. Not the suppressed calling. Just the reading. Just the sky, talking.

Pressure falling in the west, fast. Faster than it should. The chamois had known at dawn; Sable had felt the confirmation at noon; and now the light was confirming again, a third witness, and three witnesses made a fact. The window wasn't four hours. It wasn't two. The front was coming down the far side of the range like a thrown axe, and it would reach the hanging valley within the hour, and it was carrying more than snow. Sable could taste the difference on the static at their hairline—snow was silent, snow was neutral—but this had an edge underneath, a dry metallic bite, the flavor of a sky that had been rubbing its hands together somewhere out past the peaks, building charge.

A storm. A real one. The kind Sable had sworn never to touch, coming to touch them whether they liked it or not.

"Ferro." They turned back down the column, walking fast, keeping their voice the same flat instrument it always was. "Change of numbers. You've got perhaps half an hour to get the wagons under the overhang at the valley's north wall. After that you'll want to be anywhere else."

Ferro spat and squinted west, where the sky was still, to his eyes, innocent. "Looks clear as a bell to me."

"It will look clear until it doesn't. Then it'll look like the end of the world." Sable held his gaze, and there was a moment—the familiar moment, the one that happened with every caravan, the small test—where Ferro weighed the word of a guide he'd hired on a forged reference against the word of the sky he could see with his own eyes. Sable watched him do the arithmetic. Watched him remember, vaguely, that the ghost of the pass had never yet been wrong about weather, not once in three runs, and that men who argued with the ghost tended to lose mules.

"Overhang," Ferro said finally, and spat again, and turned his mules.

They made it with minutes to spare. Sable counted them without meaning to—it was an old habit, counting seconds between what the sky promised and what it delivered, a private audit of their own accuracy, a penance disguised as pride. Fourteen hundred seconds, give or take, between Ferro's last wagon rolling under the stone lip and the front hitting the valley wall.

It hit like a fist. The temperature dropped through the floor of the world. Snow came sideways, then came worse than sideways, came in the flat screaming sheets that the high country used to erase things, and the wind found the gap between the overhang and the valley floor and squeezed through it howling like something with a throat. The mules screamed back. The men cursed. And Sable stood at the edge of the shelter with the storm hammering at them, and felt—for the first time in six years, felt honestly, felt all the way down—the old answering pull rise in their chest.

Because they could have stopped it.

That was the knowledge that lived in their body like a splinter too deep to dig out: standing there in the white roar, hand pressed to their sternum, Sable could feel the storm's structure entire—could feel the shear lines, the charge pools, the cold core spinning up somewhere above the northern peak, could feel where the wind wanted to go and how badly it wanted to go there. And some younger, louder part of them, the part that had stood on a church roof at nineteen and believed the sky was a thing you could argue with, rose up and whispered what it always whispered: *You could turn it. You could break it apart over the empty scree. You could save these people their wagons, their cargo, their night. You could be useful. You could matter.*

And the older part, the scarred part, the part that had buried a mother and eleven neighbors under the consequences of mattering, answered the only way it knew how: *No.*

Not *I mustn't.* Not *it's forbidden.* Just: *no.* Flat. Final. The sound of a door with no handle on the inside.

Sable stood in the storm's edge for a long time, letting it scream at them, letting the static crawl up their arms and pool in their palms and dissipate harmlessly into the frozen ground, grounding themselves like a wire, like a lightning rod that refused on principle to conduct. This was the discipline. This was the whole of the last six years, distilled: not the absence of power but its refusal, renewed hourly, renewed in weather like this especially, because weather like this was when the gift got loud.

The crew huddled under the overhang and watched the ghost of the pass stand out in the blizzard like a man arguing with God, and they drew their own conclusions, and the conclusions were wrong, and Sable let them be wrong. Let them think the ghost was praying. Let them think the ghost was mad. Anything but the truth, which was that the ghost was doing the hardest work of their life, which was nothing at all.

* * *

The storm blew itself hoarse by midnight, as storms did when they were born fast, and the stars came back over the White Teeth with the brutal clarity of altitudes, and the camp slept, and Sable did not.

They sat at the edge of the fire's last coals, wrapped in a wool blanket that smelled of woodsmoke and other people's journeys, and did the accounting they did every night, the invisible ledger no customs officer would ever think to inspect. Cargo accounted for. Route held. Cover intact—Master Aldous Vey remained, as far as anyone knew, a retired guide with good boots and no past. The wardens' patrols, as far as Sable's network of paid eyes in the low towns could tell, were running their usual circuits, bored and seasonal, shaking down the river ferries and ignoring the high passes entirely.

Everything balanced. Everything was fine.

And none of it mattered, and Sable knew it didn't matter, and the knowing sat beside them at the dead fire like a second traveler no one else could see. Because this was the shape their life had become: competent, hidden, safe, and hollow as a blown egg. They guided caravans through a road that wasn't on any map, for men who didn't know their name, toward towns where no one waited for them, and then they walked back into the mountains alone, to a cabin with no photographs on its walls because there was no one to photograph, and they did it again, and again, and the days stacked up like coins in a jar belonging to a man who had forgotten what he was saving for.

Six years of erasure, and here was the thing no one warned you about, the thing Sable had discovered slowly and then all at once, the way you discover water has reached your waist: you cannot erase yourself halfway. The world's records of you and your own record of yourself are the same document, written in the same ink. Burn one and the other smolders. Every year the ghost of the pass grew thinner—not in body but in self. Fewer opinions. Fewer wants. A voice trained into perfect neutrality until neutrality was all that was left in it. Some nights Sable caught themselves speaking aloud in the cabin just to hear a human sound, and the sound they heard was a stranger's: pleasant, measured, empty as a rented room.

Somewhere down in the low country, in a parish register that had burned, in a warden file that had moldered, in the heads of a handful of drunks who'd tell it wrong, the real Sable Marchetti still technically existed—a storm-caller, a killer, a cautionary tale with a mother's grave at the bottom of it. Sable had spent six years making sure that version stayed dead. What they had never figured out, sitting alone at a thousand dead fires, was what was supposed to grow on the ground where it had been burned over.

Nothing grew. That was the answer. Nothing grew on scorched earth. You could keep the fire from spreading—you could keep the gift locked, the name buried, the trail cold—but the land stayed bare, and bare land, given enough winters, starts to forget it was ever anything but bare.

The coals ticked and settled. High overhead, a wind that had no business existing at this hour moved a thin veil of cloud across the stars, and Sable felt it in their teeth, faint, distant, already dying—a stray gust off the retreating front, a last message from a sky that had nothing more to say tonight.

They almost missed it. Almost. The way you can almost miss a knock at a door you've convinced yourself no one knows the location of.

Because underneath the dying wind, underneath the static fading out of the air like a radio station signing off, there was something else. Something that did not fit. Sable had spent their whole life learning the grammar of weather, and weather had a grammar—pressure spoke in curves, charge spoke in prickles, wind spoke in vectors—and whatever this was, it was not weather. It was smaller than weather. Closer. A thread of wrongness in the night's texture, delicate as a spider's line, coming not from the peaks or the valleys or any direction the compass recognized, but from—

From the east. From the low country. From the direction of the last honest road, three days' hard walk from a cabin whose location Sable had never given to anyone, because giving it to anyone would have meant having a someone.

Something was moving through the storm's aftermath. Something small. Something that carried, impossibly, against all sense, against all distance, a faint charge of its own—not the sky's electricity but something older, something that tasted to Sable's suppressed senses the way a dead language tastes to a scholar: familiar in structure, unknown in meaning, humming at the edge of perception like a word remembered from a dream.

Sable stood up. Stood very still. Told themselves it was nothing—residual charge, a trick of the clearing air, the nerves of a lonely woman playing tunes on a broken instrument. Told themselves the road ahead was down, out of the mountains, away, and that in the morning they would collect Ferro's pay and walk home to the cabin and bar the door and not exist, the way they had done a thousand nights before.

But they did not sit back down. And they did not stop listening.

Three days later, in the blue dark before dawn, with a blizzard standing on the roof of the world and the cabin's fire burned to embers, someone knocked at Sable Marchetti's door.

Bare feet in the snow. No tracks leading in.

A child, small and silent and utterly unafraid of the cold, speaking words no kingdom had used in a thousand years—and when Sable opened the door and the wind gusted in and the child looked up at them, the static that crawled up Sable's arm was not the sky's at all.

It was the storm's grammar, in a voice that had never learned weather.

It was magic, knocking. And magic, Sable knew better than anyone alive, always found them in the end.
