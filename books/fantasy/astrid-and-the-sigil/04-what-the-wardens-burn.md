## 4. What the Wardens Burn

The smoke came before the wardens did.

Sable smelled it at dawn, standing in Odile's doorway with a cup of barley tea going cold in their hands—a wrongness threaded through the woodsmoke, something chemical and deliberate beneath it. Oil. Pitch. The particular stink of a fire meant to eat paper.

They had been three days at the hermit's archive, and in those three days Sable had learned enough to be afraid in new directions. The child—Wren, though Sable hadn't said the name aloud yet, hadn't decided if naming was kindness or claim—sat cross-legged on Odile's worktable among the brass instruments, turning an astrolabe over with small, careful fingers. The astrolabe's gears should have been rusted solid. They gleamed now, bright as new coin.

Everything Wren touched came away mended. Or changed. Odile kept a saucer of milk by the table, and every morning it had turned to cream, then to something richer still, until the old woman had stopped leaving it out, muttering about variables she couldn't control.

"Odile," Sable said quietly. "That smoke."

The archivist didn't look up from the fragment she'd been transcribing all night, her pen scratching in the lamplight. She was past seventy and moved like a woman made of knotted rope, all sinew and economy, and she had not slept since she'd first laid eyes on the child. "I smell it."

"From the pass?"

"Too early for charcoal-burners." She set down her pen. Her hands were steady. Her voice wasn't, quite. "Sable. Before they arrive—and they will arrive—I need you to hear what I found. All of it. Because if I burn today, this goes into your head or nowhere."

Through the window behind her, far down the valley, Sable could see the smoke rising in a straight black column. Deliberate. Announcing itself. Wardens didn't hide their fires; they wanted people to run, because running marked you.

"Talk fast," Sable said.

Odile pulled the transcription toward her. Her script was cramped and beautiful, the hand of someone who'd spent sixty years copying dead languages by lamplight. "This is from the Third Dynasty fragments—the ones the royal academies call apocryphal. Listen." She read:

*"...and the king sat upon his throne and smiled at his lords, and his lords were glad, for the king remembered them fondly. But the queen looked into his eyes and saw winter where the summer had been, and she went to the binding-priests and asked: what wears my husband? And the priests answered: that which has always worn him. We built it to hold his burdens. It grew hungry. A crown is only ever a circle, majesty, and circles learn to feed on what they enclose."*

Sable's arms prickled. Not the tea. Not the cold. "Go on."

"The fragment breaks there. But there are others—references scattered across four centuries, always oblique, always censored. A dynasty of kings who were beloved and vacant. Edicts issued in perfect grammar by men who could no longer remember their mothers' faces. And here—" she tapped a marginal note, "—a line from a court physician, second dynasty, complaining that he was ordered to treat the king's *memory-loss as a state secret*. Memory-loss, Sable. As a symptom. Something has been eating kings for a very long time, and everyone who noticed was either silenced or wrote it down sideways where no one would look."

"And the child?"

Odile finally looked up. Her eyes were red-rimmed and fierce. "I told you the tongue is pre-dynastic binding-script. I told you the child isn't speaking it—they *are* it. But here's what I didn't say." She rose, joints cracking, and crossed to the shelf where she kept the forbidden things, the books wrapped in oilcloth against damp and discovery. She unwrapped one, thin as a hymnal. "There's a rite described here. An old one. A vessel-rite. When the binding-priests needed to store something too large for any mind to hold, they wrote it into flesh. Living sigils. The practice was outlawed so thoroughly that even mentioning it became heresy—but the *outlawing* is the tell, isn't it? You don't burn a practice out of the world unless it worked."

"You're saying Wren is a stored memory."

"I'm saying Wren is a *piece* of something. A remainder. Leftover script, cut loose when the main text was bound." Odile's voice dropped. "And someone wants the remainder destroyed badly enough to send soldiers into the Thornfells in deepwinter. Which means the thing doing the wanting is still hungry, and still wearing its crown."

Outside, the wind shifted, and beneath the woodsmoke Sable caught another scent: horse. Iron. Oiled leather. Many men.

They were closer than the smoke suggested.

* * *

Captain Corvess stood at the tree line and watched her squad torch the archive, and felt something in her chest go quietly wrong.

She had served the Crown for nineteen years. She had burned smugglers' dens and heretic chapels and once, in the border war, a granary full of grain she knew children would have eaten, and she had slept after each of them, because that was the job and the job was the kingdom and the kingdom was worth sleeping for. She was good at compartmentalizing. It was the warden's true weapon—not the sword, not the writ, but the ability to put a thing in a box and close the lid.

But this order wouldn't fit in the box.

*"An unbound vessel,"* the directive had read, in the Crown Marshal's own cipher. *"Locate and destroy. No capture. No interrogation. No survivors within the structure."*

No capture. In nineteen years, Corvess had never once been ordered to destroy rather than take. Prisoners were intelligence; intelligence was power; power was the entire architecture of the Crown. You did not burn libraries containing prisoners. You did not issue kill-orders for *things*, because things did not have vessels, and vessels implied—

"Captain?" Sergeant Dain, beside her, young and eager, watching the flames climb the archive's eastern wall. "The hermit's not in there. Tracker says footprints heading upslope, two sets. One small."

One small. Corvess had read the supplementary briefings. The vessel was believed to be a child. She had assumed a figure of speech, some cultist's term for a relic. Then she'd seen the sketch the informants provided: a child of perhaps eight years, barefoot in snow, unmarked by cold.

"The hermit's a witness," Dain said. "Orders say no witnesses."

"Orders say no witnesses *within the structure*," Corvess corrected, and heard how thin the distinction sounded, and hated that she'd made it. "We take them alive until someone tells me otherwise in writing."

Dain frowned. "The Marshal's cipher—"

"Is not present, Sergeant. We are in the field. Field judgment applies." She said it flatly, and Dain swallowed whatever else he'd been going to say.

The archive burned beautifully, which was part of the obscenity of it. Four hundred years of accumulated knowledge—grammars of dead tongues, star-charts, herbals, dynastic records the royal academies had declared redundant—going up in orange and black while her squad warmed their hands at the edge of the heat. Somewhere inside, glass was shattering in sequence as shelves collapsed. Corvess found herself cataloguing the losses without meaning to. That was the heresy growing in her, she supposed: she had spent enough years around seized contraband to know what knowledge looked like when it died.

A soldier emerged coughing, carrying an armload of salvaged books—he'd gone in against orders, some private impulse—and Corvess should have reprimanded him. Instead she took the top volume from his arms, a slim thing with a scorched cover, and opened it, and read half a page about a ceremony called *the unbinding*, and closed it again before anyone could see her face change.

"Captain," the tracker called from upslope. "Fresh prints. They're moving for the high pass."

Corvess put the book under her cloak. "Then we move. And Sergeant—" she met Dain's eyes, "—if we find the child, we find out what it knows *before* anything burns. Am I understood?"

Dain hesitated a half-second too long. "Understood, Captain."

In the box, something pressed against the lid.

* * *

Sable ran with Wren on their back, and the mountain tried to kill them both, and lost.

The high pass above Odile's archive was a goat-track in summer and a death sentence in deepwinter—a knife-edge of granite with wind-scoured ice and a drop on either side that the eye refused to measure. Sable had chosen it precisely because of that. Wardens were soldiers, and soldiers moved in columns, and columns did not do well on knife-edges. One pursuer at a time. Even odds, or better.

But the storm-sense made running its own torment. Every gust spoke to Sable whether they listened or not—pressure fronts like shouted arguments, temperature gradients like whispers, the ion-taste of coming lightning crawling along their teeth. They had spent eleven years learning to keep that sense folded shut, a door bricked over inside themselves, and now they had to hold it open just wide enough to navigate while the whole sky screamed through the gap.

*Left,* the wind said. *Ice. Trust me.*

Sable trusted it. Eleven years of exile had taught them exactly one skill worth having: how to survive being right about terrible things.

Wren's arms were locked around their neck, and the child weighed almost nothing, and made no sound at all—which was somehow worse than crying. Once, halfway up the switchback, Sable felt small cold fingers tap their shoulder in a rhythm. Three taps. Pause. Three taps. Sable realized, with a lurch, that it matched the cadence of the binding-script Odile had chanted over the transcriptions. The child was humming. Without voice. Keeping time with a language older than walls, the way other children hummed nursery songs.

*What are you?* Sable thought, and did not ask, because there was no time and because the answer might break something in them that needed to hold.

Behind and below, maybe half a mile back, the wardens had reached the base of the pass. Sable felt them the way you feel a stone dropped into a still pond—a wrongness in the air-pressure, disciplined bodies moving in rhythm. And something else. A thread of working-magic, faint and controlled, probing the wind itself. A bloodhound-mage, or near enough. Reading the weather for tracks.

So much for invisibility. So much for eleven careful years of erasing yourself from every record, every ledger, every living memory outside these mountains. Sable Marchetti, storm-caller, the person who had once held a village's dying hope in their hands and failed to save a single soul with it—that person was supposed to be dead. Had arranged their own death meticulously: false grave, forged coroner's writ, a name surrendered like a debt paid off.

And now the Crown's hounds were sniffing up a mountain after them anyway, because magic found Sable whether they wanted it or not. It had found them in the shape of a barefoot child in a blizzard, and it would keep finding them, and the only question left was how many people burned before Sable stopped running.

They crested the pass as the sun broke through—a hard, white, merciless light—and below them the archive's smoke still rose, a black finger pointing accusingly at the sky. Odile had stayed behind. Had insisted, pressing the transcriptions into Sable's hands with a grip like iron wire: *"Someone has to mislead them, and I'm the only one who can lie convincingly about where you've gone. Besides—" that fierce, red-rimmed smile, "—they'll want me alive for questioning. Old women make poor kindling and excellent hostages. Go."*

Sable had gone. They were still deciding whether that was cowardice or obedience to the only order that mattered: *keep the child safe.*

Wren tugged their collar. Pointed back down the pass.

The wardens had stopped at the treeline. Forming up. Not pursuing onto the ice.

Because they didn't have to. Because the bloodhound-mage had already read the wind, and the wind had already told them everything, and somewhere in the lowlands ahead—every road, every ferry, every border town where Sable owed debts and owned favors—the net was already closing.

Sable stood on the knife-edge with the orphaned sigil on their back and the stolen truth in their satchel, and let themselves, for one breath, feel the full weight of it. Then they turned north, toward the border towns, toward the debts, toward the people whose lives they'd saved and never spoken to since.

Time to start collecting.

* * *

Corvess found the hermit sitting calmly in the ruins of her own life, sorting charred pages into piles as though tidying a pantry.

The old woman didn't flinch when the squad surrounded her. Didn't beg. She looked up at Corvess with the mild irritation of someone interrupted at work and said, "You're late. Most of it's ash by now."

"Odile Vane," Corvess read from the warrant. "You're charged with harboring contrabant texts and an unbound vessel. You'll come with us to—"

"I'll come with you nowhere. My knees are seventy-two years old and my testimony is worth more than your warrant, so you may as well hear it standing." The hermit brushed ash from her skirts. "Ask your questions, Captain. I can see you have them. You've been holding that book under your cloak since the burning started, and you haven't reported it, which means you've read it, which means you have questions the Marshal's briefing didn't answer."

Corvess's hand went to her cloak before she could stop it. Stopped. Behind her, she heard Dain shift uncertainly.

"You're mistaken," she said.

"I am many things, but rarely mistaken about readers." Odile Vane folded her scorched hands. "Here is what will happen, Captain. You will take me to your superiors, as your orders demand. They will question me. I will tell them a version of events. And somewhere between here and the capital, you will open that book again, and you will read the rest of it, and you will begin—slowly, unwillingly, the way all honest people do—to wonder who gave the order to burn, and why, and what exactly it is that a crown eats to stay hungry for thirty years."

The wind moved through the ruined archive, lifting ash in gray spirals. For a moment neither woman spoke.

"That's sedition," Corvess said at last.

"That's a bibliography," said the hermit. "Sedition requires believing something. I'm only offering you sources."

Corvess signaled her squad forward. They bound the old woman's hands with cord, gently, because she offered her wrists herself, and marched her down toward the lowlands. And all the way down, through the blackened trees and the falling snow, Corvess kept her hand pressed flat against her cloak, over the shape of the salvaged book, feeling it like a coal that would not cool.

Two sets of prints had led up the pass. The tracker swore the smaller set vanished at the crest—as if the child had simply stopped touching the earth.

Corvess filed that away too, in the box, next to the kill-order that specified destruction instead of capture, next to the phrase *no survivors within the structure* applied to a building that had contained one old woman and forty thousand pages of dead languages.

The box was getting full. She was starting to worry about the lid.

And far to the north, in a border town called Graywater, a ferryman named Brakka woke from a dream of storms to find an old debt knocking at his door.
