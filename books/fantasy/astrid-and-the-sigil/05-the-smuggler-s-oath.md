## 5. The Smuggler's Oath

Sable had a list of names written nowhere, which was the only safe place to keep one.

They kept it the way other people kept prayers—recited in the dark, tested against forgetting. *Brakka, who owes a crossing. Liss, who owes a face. Hume, who owes a war.* Three names, three towns strung along the border like beads on a fraying cord, and between them and Sable lay nine days of road with a child who hummed in a dead language when they slept.

Wren rode in the cart's bed among barrels of salted pike, wrapped in Sable's spare cloak, watching the Thornfells shrink behind them with an expression Sable was beginning to recognize as attention. Not the blank stare of a child watching scenery. The fixed, patient look of someone reading.

"Stop that," Sable said, without turning around.

The humming stopped. Then, after a moment, it resumed—lower, as if Wren had decided the instruction applied only to that particular tune.

Sable exhaled through their nose and let it go. Nine days of road. Nine days to decide whether the names on the unwritten list were still alive, still reachable, and still the kind of people who could be asked to die for a child they'd never met.

There was a fourth question underneath, the one Sable didn't recite: *still the kind of person who could be asked to remember what I did for them, and forgive it.*

The road dropped out of the mountains through country that smelled of pine rot and old snow, and on the third evening the trees opened onto the Graywater—wide, brown, and slow as poured honey, with Brakka's ferry moored at the near bank like something that had given up on swimming.

* * *

Brakka was bigger than Sable remembered, which was saying something, because Sable remembered him as a man who had to turn sideways to enter his own wheelhouse. He sat on the deck mending a net with hands like cured hams, and when he looked up and saw who stood at the top of the ferry ramp, the net slid from his fingers into the water and he made no move to retrieve it.

"No," he said.

"I haven't asked anything yet."

"You don't have to. I know that walk." He hauled himself up, joints cracking like green firewood, and glowered down from a height most men needed a ladder to reach. "That's the walk of someone about to ruin my perfectly quiet life. I buried you, Marchetti. I *drank* at your grave. It was a good funeral. Best I've been to."

"The eulogy was too long."

"It was *accurate*." Brakka's eyes went past Sable's shoulder to the cart, to the small figure sitting up among the barrels, and something in his heavy face shifted—the look of a man who had carried drowning children out of rivers before and knew the weight before he lifted it. "Whose is that?"

"Complicated."

"Nothing's complicated. Things are simple and then we lie about them." He scratched under his jaw. "You look terrible. You look like weather that's decided to happen to one person."

Sable had rehearsed this. They'd rehearsed it for nine days, standing watch while Wren slept, composing the speech that would make the asking clean: *the Crown has wardens in the field, hunting a child; I mean to take the child somewhere the Crown can't follow; I need a ferryman, a forger, and a soldier; the pay is nothing and the odds are worse.*

Instead what came out was: "They burned Odile Vane's archive."

Brakka went still. He and Odile had history—everyone on the border had history with Odile; she'd forged half their marriage contracts and corrected the other half in red ink. "Burned it. All of it?"

"What the wardens didn't take, yes."

"And you're going to do something stupid about it."

"I'm going to do something necessary about it. The stupid part is incidental."

Brakka looked at the child again. Wren looked back, unblinking, and Sable saw the moment it landed on Brakka—the moment he understood that the child was the *why*, the whole why, the reason a dead woman was standing on his ferry asking for a crew. He'd lost a daughter to the Graywater's spring flood, years back. Sable had never once mentioned it. Had never needed to.

"I have a wife," Brakka said finally. "Two sons. A ferry."

"Yes."

"If I do this, and it goes wrong, they're not just widowed and orphaned. They're *contaminated*. Wardens don't stop at the guilty. They stop at everyone the guilty ever loved."

"I know."

"And you're asking anyway."

"I'm asking anyway." Sable held his gaze. "I'd rather ask than owe you the lie of a clean grave, Brakka. You've had one of those from me already. They don't hold water."

The ferryman was quiet a long time. The river moved beneath them, patient and brown, carrying things nobody claimed.

"Three days," he said at last. "I have cargo contracted. I deliver it, I settle my accounts, I tell Maren a story she'll choose to believe, and I meet you at Hollowford crossing with the barge and my good arm. If you're not there by the new moon—"

"I'll be there."

"—then I'll know you died of something else, and I'll drink at *that* funeral too." He bent and pulled his net from the river, wringing it out. "Marchetti. The child. When it looks at me like that—"

"Yes?"

"Nothing." He shook his enormous head. "Get off my ferry. You're making it depressing."

* * *

Hollowford was a town built on the difference between two tax jurisdictions, which made it a town built on Liss.

She worked out of the back of a chandler's shop, behind a door with three locks and no handle, and when Sable knocked the rhythm they'd agreed on a decade ago—a smuggler's knock, four and two—there was a long silence and then a voice like a knife being drawn slowly: "Dead people don't know the knock. Who taught you?"

"You did. The night you were too drunk to remember teaching me."

Another silence. Then the locks turned, one, two, three, and Liss stood in the gap—small, sharp-faced, ink to the second knuckle and a smudge of it along her jaw like a bruise she'd chosen. She looked at Sable the way she looked at suspected counterfeit coin: thoroughly, and with visible disappointment in the result.

"Everyone said you were dead," she said.

"Everyone says that. It's very restful. May I come in?"

"No." But she stepped back to let them pass, which was Liss's way of saying yes in a language only Liss spoke fluently. The room beyond was papered in documents—not hung, *papered*, layers of it, edicts and writs and passports in six hands and four kingdoms, a wall of other people's official truths. Wren stopped just inside the door and stared at it with their head tilted, and Sable saw the child's lips move, silently, tracing something.

Liss noticed too. "Your kid's reading my wall."

"Not my kid. And it's not reading." Sable hesitated. "It's recognizing."

"Recognizing what? That's a royal trade charter from Vell. That's a border pass sealed by the Marcher Lord. Those are real. I keep them real because—" Liss stopped. Sable watched her stop, watched the forger's mind do what it always did, which was take a wrongness apart to see what made it wrong. "Why are you here, Marchetti? The real answer. I can smell the rehearsed one on you."

So Sable gave it to her plain, standing in the papered room with Wren's hand curled in theirs: the archive, the wardens, the binding-script, the fragment Odile had salvaged about a king whose memories were eaten by something wearing his crown. Liss listened without interrupting, which from Liss was a form of awe, and at the end she was silent for so long that Wren tugged Sable's sleeve and pointed at her, concerned.

"I forged documents for the palace," Liss said finally. "Did you know that? Three years. Invitations, decrees, the pretty stuff they hand out at festivals. Good work. Clean work. Paid for my shop, my locks, my—" She laughed, one short note with nothing in it. "You know what I never noticed? What I never *once* noticed?"

"Tell me."

"I never read them. Not really. You don't, in my trade—you read the *form*, the hand, the seal, the spacing. The words are just cargo." She turned to her wall of truths and put her palm flat against the royal charter, and Sable saw that her hand was shaking, very slightly, the way it never shook over her work. "Thirty years of edicts, Marchetti. Every one of them passes through hands like mine. Every one of them carries a seal. And if someone wanted to write a spell into law—" she looked at Sable, and her sharp face had gone pale under the ink smudge, "—the law is the one document nobody ever *reads*."

"That's not why I came."

"No. But it's why you'll be glad you did." Liss pulled a satchel from under her workbench and began filling it with a forger's essentials: pens, inks, blanks, a seal-stamp kit that Sable pretended not to recognize as stolen. "I'm not coming because I owe you. I'm coming because if the kingdom's a hostage, I've been the one holding the pen. I want to see the ransom note." She paused at the door, looking down at Wren. "Does it eat?"

"It eats."

"Good. I work better when other people are eating. It's like having an audience."

* * *

Hume was the hardest, because Hume had earned the right to say no.

They found him—or he found them—two towns over, in a border garrison town called Marrow's Rest where the taverns served deserters' prices to deserters' faces and everyone pretended not to know why the beer was cheap. He was sitting alone in the corner of the Drowned Ox with his back to the wall, a habit that outlived the war the way a scar outlives the wound, and when Sable walked in, he set down his mug with the deliberate calm of a man who has decided not to be surprised.

"I heard a rumor," Hume said. "That a dead smuggler was buying rounds in Hollowford. I said, that's impossible, because I carried that smuggler's body off a battlefield myself and buried them with full honors, and dead people stay bought." He looked up. His face was a map of old campaigns, every line a road he'd walked away from. "So either the rumor's wrong, or the honors were wasted."

"The honors were lovely. I appreciated the folded flag."

"I *stole* that flag."

"I know. That's why it meant something." Sable sat down across from him. Behind the bar, the keeper found urgent business in the cellar. In Marrow's Rest, you learned when a conversation needed privacy, and you provided it. "I need a soldier, Hume."

"No."

"You haven't heard—"

"I don't need to hear. I know the sound of it. It's the same sound as the last time, at Kessrin Ford, when you stood in the rain and told me the plan was 'technically survivable.'" He turned his mug a quarter-turn on the table, aligning it with the wood grain, a sergeant's tic. "I deserted, Marchetti. Do you understand what that means? Not *left*. Deserted. There's a difference. Leaving is a thing you do. Deserting is a thing that's done *to* your name. They wrote it on me. If I'm seen holding a pike next to you, every garrison from here to the capital has a paper with my face on it and a number under it, and the number is what I'm worth to the man who brings me in."

"Yes."

"And you're asking anyway."

"It seems to be the week for it."

Hume was quiet. Outside, the garrison drums sounded the change of watch, and Sable watched the sound move through him like water through a lock—the shoulders squaring, the hands going still, ten years of trained reflex answering a call he'd sworn off. That was the thing about Hume that Sable had never been able to explain to anyone who hadn't served: he hadn't deserted the *work*. He'd deserted the *orders*. The work still lived in him like a tenant who paid no rent and could not be evicted.

"Why a soldier?" Hume asked at last. "For what? You're a smuggler. Smugglers run *from* soldiers. That's the whole business model."

"The child is hunted."

"Children are hunted all the time. It's the century's chief product." But his eyes had gone to the doorway, where Wren stood wrapped in the too-big cloak, waiting with a patience no child should own. "That one's different."

"That one's different."

"How?"

Sable weighed it. Lied to Hume and the lie would calcify; told him everything and he might do the arithmetic of danger and walk out the door, and Sable would have no one to blame. In the end they chose the truth, because Hume had carried their body off a battlefield and deserved the weight of it.

"The Crown wants them destroyed. Not captured. Destroyed. And the child is the only key to freeing a king who's been a prisoner in his own head for thirty years."

Hume's mug stopped turning.

"I served in the palace guard," he said quietly. "Before Kessrin. Before the ford. You know that."

"I know you don't talk about it."

"I stood post outside the king's chambers for two years. And I'll tell you what I told the tribunal, when they asked why I ran." He looked at his hands—soldier's hands, blunt and scarred, hands that had done their share of the century's chief product. "The king smiled at me every morning. Every single morning, warm as fresh bread, remembered my name, asked after my mother. And every single morning his eyes were like the far side of a locked door. Like a house where the lights are on but nobody's home, and the lights are on *for show*." He looked up. "I deserted because I couldn't swear an oath to whatever was wearing him. If you're telling me there's a man inside that house after all—"

"I'm telling you there's a door."

Hume sat with that. The tavern noise resumed around them, the keeper reappearing with studied innocence, and for a while there was only the drumbeat of the changing watch and the small sounds of Wren investigating a bowl of complimentary pickled eggs with the gravity of a scholar.

"I have conditions," Hume said finally.

"Name them."

"I don't kill children. I don't burn villages. And when this is done—whatever's done—I get to go back to the palace gates I ran from, and stand there, and be seen standing there." His jaw worked. "A man should face the thing he wrote on his own name. Even if it's a wall. *Especially* if it's a wall."

Sable extended their hand across the table. "Agreed."

Hume took it. His grip was careful, the grip of a man who knew exactly what his hands could do and rationed it accordingly, and Sable felt the oath pass between them—not spoken, because smugglers and deserters didn't waste words on oaths, but settled, the way a load settles on a cart when the ropes are finally tied right.

* * *

They left Marrow's Rest before dawn, three adults and a child and a cart of salted pike, and the road unrolled gray and cold ahead of them toward Hollowford crossing, where a very large ferryman would be waiting with a barge and a story his wife would choose to believe.

Wren rode between Liss and Hume, and Sable, walking at the cart's side, listened to the strangest conversation they'd heard in ten years of silence: Liss teaching the child the names of forgeries on her wall, Hume correcting the child's grip on a stick they'd decided was a pike, and Wren—Wren *answering*, in that dead pre-dynastic tongue, in tones that rose and fell like questions, like jokes, like the beginnings of something that would have been laughter in any living language.

And the child was laughing. Sable heard it—heard the shape of it, at least—and felt something in their chest that had been boarded over for a decade shift on its nails.

It was Hume who noticed it first, because sergeants notice things. He rode up beside Sable at the morning's midpoint, and his voice was low, pitched under the wind.

"There's dust on the back road. A lot of it, moving fast. Mounted." He glanced at Sable. "Could be a merchant train."

"Could be."

"Could be the reason you needed a soldier."

Sable looked back the way they'd come. Far off, where the road bent around a hillside, a smudge of dust hung in the cold air, and beneath the wind—faint, almost imagined—Sable's skin prickled with the old storm-sense, the sense they'd sworn off, reading the air like a page.

*Iron,* it said. *Many riders. And under the iron, something that crackled. Wardens.*

"Brakka's at Hollowford," Sable said. "We make the crossing before they do."

Hume nodded once, and turned back to the cart, and his hand found the stick-pike at his side—not a habit anymore, Sable thought. A decision.

Behind them, the dust cloud grew.
