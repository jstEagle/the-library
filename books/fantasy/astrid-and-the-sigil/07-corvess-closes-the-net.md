## 7. Corvess Closes the Net

The cells beneath the Wardenhouse at Graywater Ford were cut into the riverbank itself, so that the water could be heard through the stone—a low, patient whisper, like the fortress itself murmuring secrets it would never say aloud. Captain Ilse Corvess had always found the sound comforting. Tonight it sounded like counting.

She descended the wet steps with a lantern in one hand and the prisoner's file in the other, and she made herself read the file again before she went in, because that was discipline, and discipline was the only prayer the Wardens kept.

*Name: Tam Vosk. Trade: ferryman's hand, unlicensed. Apprehended: three nights past, attempting to move contraband writs across the Graywater crossing. Notes: talkative under pressure.*

The last line was the important one. Talkative men were not brave men, and Corvess had no patience for wasting a night on bravery.

She pushed open the cell door with her shoulder. The room beyond was six feet of damp stone and a stool, and on the stool sat Tam Vosk, a broad, soft-bellied man in his forties with river mud still dried in the creases of his knuckles. They'd taken his boots. Standard practice. Bare feet made men honest faster than any question did.

"Captain." He tried to stand, thought better of it, and settled for sitting up straighter, which achieved nothing except to make him look like a dog trying to seem taller than it was.

"Master Vosk." She set the lantern on the floor between them, so his face was lit from below and hers was lost in shadow. An old trick. It worked because men believed tricks mattered more than they did. "You've been here three nights. You've told my sergeant about your wife, your debts, and a boil on your left hip. You have not yet told me what I want."

"I don't know what—"

"You were carrying forged travel writs." She let the file drop onto her knee with a slap of wet paper. "Good ones. Liss-quality, if you know the name, and I think you do, because you flinched. Forged writs mean a forger. A forger means a buyer. And the buyer paid you in *this*—" she drew a coin from her coat pocket and held it up, letting the lantern light catch the worn stamp, "—which is old coin, Master Vosk. Pre-dynastic minting, or close enough that a man who ferries smugglers would notice. Old coin comes out of the mountains."

Vosk's throat moved. The river whispered through the wall behind him, patient as ever.

"The mountains," he said carefully, "are full of things."

"They are." Corvess crouched, bringing her face down into the lantern light, which was worse for him than standing over him; men hated being looked at level. "And one of those things is a name. A name from before the war, before the Purges, before the Wardens wore gray instead of red. A storm-caller who died, officially, eleven years ago, in a rockslide that no body was ever recovered from, because the rockslide was a story and stories don't leave bodies." She watched his eyes. "Say the name, Master Vosk, and walk out of this cell tonight with your boots on and my report saying you cooperated fully."

Silence. Long enough that the river filled it, whispering its numbers.

Then Vosk laughed—not defiance, something sadder. "You know what's funny, Captain? Everyone who owes that person a debt says the same thing when we're drunk. Says: *if they come asking, tell them nothing, because telling is how they die.*" He rubbed his bare feet together against the cold. "But everyone who owes that debt also says the second thing. Says: *but if the Wardens come asking, tell them, because lying to the Crown gets your family burned.*"

Corvess said nothing. That was the trick beneath all her other tricks: silence was a door, and frightened men walked through it on their own.

"Sable," Vosk said finally, barely above the water's voice. "Marchetti. The storm-caller. Alive. In the Thornfells, past the pass the maps don't show. That's all I know, Captain, on my mother's grave. I never met them. Nobody meets them. You just find that the road was easier than it should have been, or the fever broke when it shouldn't have, or the warden patrol got called away the night your brother needed it to—and then a year later there's a note under your door asking for a favor, and you pay it, because what else do you owe a miracle?"

Corvess stood. Her knees ached; she was forty-one and the field took its toll. "Sergeant Orla will bring your boots," she said. "You'll be released by morning. Your cooperation will be noted."

Vosk exhaled like a man surfacing. Then, as she turned: "Captain. One more thing. Free of charge."

She paused at the door.

"The person who bought the writs wasn't Sable. Sable doesn't buy writs—they don't need them, the roads bend around them. It was someone traveling *with* them. Someone small." His voice dropped, and something in it made the hair rise along her forearms, some animal instinct older than her rank. "A child. My cousin saw it at the Graywater market, two weeks back. Barefoot. In the snow. Just... walking through the blizzard like it was a summer meadow. And every dog within a street started howling at once, and then stopped, all together, like something had told them to."

Corvess looked back at him. "A child."

"A child," Vosk agreed, "that my cousin swears looked at him and said a word in a language that made his teeth ache. He's been drinking since. Trying to drown the word out." He smiled without any humor in it. "He can't remember the word, Captain. But he remembers that it *meant* something. Meant something big. Like the word was bigger than his head could hold."

Corvess left him to the river's whispering and climbed the stairs two at a time, because her hands wanted to be busy and her mind wanted to be anywhere but where it was.

* * *

The dispatch came up from the capital by fast courier the next evening, sealed in black wax with the sigil of the Office of Unbound Things—that branch of the Wardenship so secret most wardens believed it was a rumor, a story told to new recruits to keep them from asking questions about the third floor of the Citadel, which had no windows and received no visitors and, according to the cooks, ordered food for twelve and fed no one anyone had ever seen.

Corvess broke the seal alone in her office and read the orders twice.

The first part was expected. *Confirmed intelligence places the fugitive Marchetti, designation STORM-UNBOUND, alive in the Thornfell region. All assets redirected. Capture priority absolute.* Fine. Clean. A hunt was a hunt; she'd run down deserters and rogue hedge-mages for fifteen years and slept fine after.

The second part was not fine.

*Re: the vessel accompanying the fugitive. Designation SIGIL-HOST, status UNBOUND. Do not capture. Do not transport. Destruction on contact is authorized and required. No report of the vessel's nature is to enter written record. Burn this directive after reading.*

She read it a third time, because the first two times she must have misread it.

*Destruction on contact.*

They were sending her—the woman whose service record contained four commendations for prisoner recovery and exactly zero kills of noncombatants—an order to kill a child. Not capture. Not contain. *Destroy*, in the language the Office used for contaminated livestock and plague-pits. And beneath it, the instruction that curdled everything else: *no report of the vessel's nature is to enter written record.* Which meant the Office already knew what the child was. Knew it well enough to be afraid of the knowledge itself. You didn't burn information unless the information was dangerous to *you*.

Corvess sat very still in her chair and listened to the wind off the Graywater worrying at the shutters, and she performed the exercise her first commander had taught her, twenty-three years ago, back when the Wardens still wore red and she'd been young enough to think the color suited her.

*An order is a tool,* Commander Ashe had said, turning a whetstone slowly along a blade. *Tools are made for jobs. When a tool arrives broken—when it cannot do the job it claims—you do not swing it anyway. You look at it. You ask who sent it broken, and why.*

"What job is this?" she asked the empty office.

No answer. The wind, the shutters, the distant river. Somewhere below, her sergeants were drilling the new intake, and their voices rose in the cadence of the Warden's Litany, the one every recruit learned before they learned to ride:

*The Crown watches, therefore we watch.*
*The Crown judges, therefore we judge.*
*What the Crown wills, we have already done.*

She had recited it ten thousand times. It had always felt like armor.

Tonight, for the first time, it felt like a muzzle.

* * *

She filed the directive. Not burned—filed, in the locked drawer beneath her maps, beneath the false bottom she'd installed years ago for documents she couldn't destroy and couldn't obey, a drawer that now held three items including this one. Three in fifteen years. She told herself the number proved her steadiness. A captain who defied orders weekly was a traitor. A captain who had questioned only three orders in fifteen years was simply thorough.

Then she wrote her operational report, and here she committed her fourth small treason, though she dressed it in perfect obedience: she reported the confirmation of Marchetti's survival, the mountain routes, the smuggler network, all of it accurate and complete. Of the child, she wrote only: *Secondary presence reported, nature undetermined, assessment pending.*

Assessment pending. It bought her days. Maybe a week. The Office's couriers moved fast but their patience moved slow, and by the time they pressed her for the vessel's disposition, she would either have found the child—or found a reason, a good bureaucratic reason, why destruction was strategically inferior to capture. She was good at reasons. Fifteen years of writing reports had taught her that the truth was never killed outright in the Wardenhood; it was just reclassified until it suffocated quietly in an archive.

She wondered, sealing the report, when she had become a person who thought in those terms. She couldn't point to the day. That was the horror of it. Corruption of conscience didn't arrive like a storm; it arrived like weather, so gradual you only noticed when you realized you'd been breathing it for years.

* * *

The captured smuggler's cousin—the one who'd seen the child—was easy enough to find. Drunk, terrified, and desperate to unburden himself to anyone in a gray coat who'd listen, which was its own kind of damning. Corvess interviewed him in the taproom of the Ferry Bell with a cup of small beer she didn't drink, and he talked for an hour, and by the end of it her hands were steady and her thoughts were not.

"He looked right at me," the cousin said, for the fourth time. "Right through me. And he said—I told the ferryman I forgot the word, but that's a lie, Captain, I didn't forget it, I *can't* say it. Every time I try, my mouth won't. But I remember what it meant." He leaned close, breath thick with ale and fear. "It meant *you are counted*. Like—like being weighed. Like something opened me up and read me and put me back, and I came up short. I came up short, Captain, and it knew, and it was *sorry*. A child, sorry for me. What kind of child is sorry?"

"What did you feel," Corvess asked quietly, "when it happened? Truly."

The man stared into his cup. "Safe," he said, and began to cry. "That's the worst of it. I've never felt so safe in my life. And then it walked on into the snow, and I've been cold ever since. Two weeks. Can't get warm. Doctor says there's naught wrong with me." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand. "You're going to kill it, aren't you. I heard the garrison talking. Word came down from the capital."

Corvess said nothing, which was answer enough, and the man nodded slowly, unsurprised, and finished his beer, and she left him weeping softly in the taproom while the fire cracked and spat and gave less heat than it should have.

Outside, snow was beginning again. She stood in the courtyard of the Wardenhouse and watched it fall through the lantern light, each flake brief and exact, and she thought about a barefoot child walking through a blizzard like a summer meadow, and dogs falling silent at a command no one heard, and an Office with no windows ordering food for twelve.

*Unbound vessel.* The phrase from the directive surfaced again, unwelcome. She knew the old theology behind it—every warden did, from the history modules they made recruits sleep through. Before the dynasties, magic hadn't been cast. It had been *written*, in a language that predated ink, and a spell written in living flesh was called a binding, and bindings were the reason the first kings had burned the archives and salted the scriptoriums. Because a bound thing didn't serve its writer. A bound thing *became* the writing. And a kingdom that contained even one true binding was a kingdom with a second author in it.

So the Office feared the child. That much was rational. That much she could almost salute.

But you did not *destroy* a binding. Every fragmentary text that survived the purges agreed on that, and Odile Vane—the mad archivist up the mountain, whom Corvess had once raided and once, shamefully, warned an hour in advance—had said it to her face across the smoke of her own burning shelves: *"You cannot unmake what is written in flesh, Captain. You can only move it. And moving it means someone must carry it."*

Destruction on contact. As if flesh could be burned without the writing going somewhere. As if the Office didn't know that.

Unless the Office did know. Unless destruction was precisely the point—not to erase the sigil, but to *loosen* it. To crack the vessel and let whatever was written inside scatter into the world, into the wind, into whoever stood nearest when it broke.

Into whoever carried out the order.

Corvess looked down at her hands, gloved in gray leather, steady on the pommel of her sword, and felt the cold the drunkard had described settle into her bones and refuse to leave.

* * *

At midnight she climbed the tower to the signal platform, ostensibly to check the beacon stores, actually to breathe air that didn't smell of ink and wax. The Thornfells stood up against the stars to the north, black on blacker, and somewhere in that darkness was a dead woman who wasn't dead, a forger whose work Corvess had been confiscating for a decade with mounting private admiration, and a child who made grown men weep with the memory of feeling safe.

Her orders were simple. Find them. Kill the small one. Bring the large one in chains.

She had followed simple orders for fifteen years, and the kingdom was peaceful, and the king was beloved, and the third floor of the Citadel fed twelve meals a day to no one, and somewhere in the back of her mind, filed beside the drawer with its false bottom, a voice that sounded unpleasantly like Odile Vane asked: *And what, Captain, do you imagine they eat?*

Below her, the garrison bell rang the hour. Two of her riders were already saddled in the yard—she'd given the order an hour ago, before she'd let herself admit she was giving it: a sweep of the high passes, starting with the hidden roads, the ones the maps didn't show. Officially, a reconnaissance in force. Unofficially—

Unofficially, she intended to find the storm-caller first. Before the Office's own hunters arrived; there were always hunters after a directive like this one, quiet ones, windowless ones. She wanted to see the child with her own eyes. She wanted to stand in front of the thing she'd been ordered to destroy and learn whether her conscience was cracking, or merely waking.

And if it came to a choice—if the choice came down the mountain at her with its teeth bared—she wanted to be the one holding the sword.

The snow thickened. North, the peaks vanished entirely, and Corvess turned from the railing and went down to saddle her own horse, leaving the beacon dark behind her, and the river below the walls whispering its patient count into the night.
