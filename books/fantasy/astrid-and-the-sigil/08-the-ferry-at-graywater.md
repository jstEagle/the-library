## 8. The Ferry at Graywater

The Graywater earned its name honestly. Where the Thornfell rivers came down off the ice they ran white and quicksilver-bright, but here in the valley's lap they slowed, gathered their silt and their secrets, and turned the color of old pewter—wide, flat, and patient as a thing that had drowned better people than us.

Brakka stood at the rail of his ferry with one boot up on a coil of rope, squinting across the water like he could read what waited on the far bank.

"Something's wrong," he said, for the third time since we'd come down out of the tree line.

"Everything about you is wrong," Liss said. She was sitting on an overturned crate, re-inking her travel writs because the morning damp had feathered the edges. "Your face is wrong. Your boat is wrong. That smell is definitely wrong."

"That smell is fish," Brakka said with dignity. "And I'm telling you, no one's working the east landing. No carts. No gulls. Gulls don't lie, Marchetti."

I looked where he was looking. The far bank was a smear of alder and pale shingle, and it was true there were no gulls wheeling over the landing, no smoke from the ferryman's hut, no cart tracks in the mud. My storm-sense, that old suppressed second sight I'd spent nine years pretending I didn't have, stirred behind my breastbone like a dog hearing its name. The air over the east bank was wrong too—too still, too dry, holding its breath.

Wardens didn't hide well. They were trained to be seen; fear was half their armor. But whoever had emptied that landing knew how to wait.

"We could go around," Hume said. He'd been quiet all morning, working the strap of his sword-harness like it owed him money. "Upstream there's a ford at the old mill. Adds two days."

"Two days is enough for them to close every crossing between here and the capital," I said. "We take the ferry. We take it now, we take it fast, and if anyone on that bank is wearing gray, Brakka puts us mid-river and keeps us there."

Brakka spat over the side. "Mid-river I can do. It's the keeping-them-there part you'll want to be polite about."

Wren was sitting in the ferry's shadowed stern, knees drawn up, watching the water with that unnerving stillness children sometimes have when they're listening to something adults can't hear. Since Odile's archive had burned, since the night we'd run through the smoking pines with ash in our hair and a salvaged fragment of pre-dynastic script sewn into my coat lining, the child had been quieter than ever—which for Wren meant something, given they'd never spoken a word aloud in any tongue the living used. They spoke constantly in other ways. The milk soured when they were frightened. Hume's nightmares had stopped the week after we'd pulled him off a prison transport, and he'd never asked why he'd started sleeping through the nights again, just looked at Wren once, long, and touched his forehead like a man doffing a hat.

Now the child's fingers moved against their knee, small and restless, tracing shapes. Binding-script. Always binding-script.

"It'll be all right," I told them, and crouched so we were eye to eye. "You stay behind me. Whatever happens, you stay behind me, and you keep your hands still."

Wren looked up. Their eyes were the gray of the river, and something moved in them—not fear exactly. Recognition. Like they'd read this day already, somewhere, in a language older than walls.

Then they reached out and pressed one small palm flat against my chest, over my heart, and left it there a moment before pulling away.

Static crawled up under my ribs, faint as a struck match.

* * *

The first hundred yards of crossing were ordinary. The river took us, the current shouldered the hull, and Brakka worked the sweep-pole with the easy competence of a man who'd been bribing, smuggling, and occasionally drowning his way across this water for twenty years. Liss finished her inking and blotted the writs. Hume watched the banks. I watched the sky.

That was how I saw it first. Not the wardens on the bank—the wardens I'd have felt anyway—but the birds. A flock of fieldfare coming down the valley, hundreds of them, and as they crossed above the east landing they rose all at once, a single convulsive motion, like a sheet snapped by wind. Birds don't break formation for nothing.

"They're on the bank," I said. "All of them. Get low."

Hume was moving before I finished. Twenty years of soldiering doesn't unlearn itself; he had Liss by the collar and both of them down behind the cargo crates before the first crossbow bolt came out of the alders with a sound like a wet branch snapping. It buried itself in the mast a hand's width from Brakka's ear.

"Graycoats!" Brakka bellowed, with the affronted tone of a man whose river had been personally insulted. "On my water!"

They came out of the treeline then, and my stomach dropped, because it wasn't six of them or ten. It was thirty at least, in column, warden-gray cloaks and lacquered half-masks, and behind them a wagon with a cage on it, and behind the cage—

Behind the cage stood a woman on a black horse, and even at three hundred yards I knew the set of her shoulders. Corvess. The warden captain who'd burned Odile's archive, who'd hunted us through three valleys, who had looked at me across the burning archive yard with eyes that did arithmetic instead of hating. She raised one arm, unhurried, and the column split into two wings that began to run along the bank, pacing us.

"They can't follow onto the water," Hume said. "Right? Tell me they can't follow onto the water."

"Wardens don't swim," Liss said grimly. "But they don't have to. Look at the masthead."

I looked. Above us, the sky over the river had begun to change. Not clouds—there were no clouds—but a pressure, a darkening, like ink dropped in water. The air went cold enough to ache. And I understood, with a lurch, what I should have understood on the bank: they hadn't come to fight us. They'd come to *flush* us. Drive us onto open water where we couldn't run, and then—

A warden at the center of the line lifted something on a pole. Not a weapon. A standard—a banner of gray silk stitched with the Crown's sigil, and around the sigil, in thread so fine it was nearly invisible, a ring of characters I recognized with a sick lurch of the gut. Binding-script. Stitched law. Stitched spellcraft.

The pressure above the river *clenched*.

The storm came down out of a clear sky.

It wasn't weather. Weather has rhythm, has appetite, has moods you can read like text if you've spent your childhood learning to. This was a hammer blow shaped like weather—a fist of wind that hit the ferry broadside and laid it over until the gunwale kissed the river, until Liss screamed and the crate she'd been sitting on went overboard, until Brakka threw his whole body against the sweep-pole and shouted words I was fairly sure were prayers in a smuggler's dialect. Rain came sideways, hard as flung gravel. Lightning walked the water in jagged white legs.

"Hold!" I heard myself shouting. "Get to the deck boards, hold the child—"

Wren was not behind me.

The child was standing at the rail, barefoot on the pitching deck, hair whipping, arms out—and they were *screaming*. No sound came out. Their mouth was open wide and nothing came from it but the storm answered anyway, because of course it did, because Wren was a sigil given flesh and terror had found the only voice they had. The lightning bent toward them. The rain circled them like a congregation. Every lash of the false storm fed into the small body at the rail and came back out doubled, tripled, a feedback loop of panic and power, and the river beneath us began to stand up in ridges.

"BRAKKA!" I roared. "GET US TO SHORE OR GET US UNDER, EITHER ONE, BUT MOVE!"

"I'M TRYING—" The pole snapped in his hands. The current, whipped mad by the warring storms, had the ferry now, spinning it like a leaf in a sluice.

A wave came aboard. Not a splash—a wall, green-gray and solid, and it took Liss off her feet and swept her toward the rail, and Hume caught her wrist at the last possible instant and held, boots skidding, teeth bared, a man being slowly winched overboard by the river itself.

And Wren kept screaming without sound, and the sky kept answering, and somewhere inside me a door I had nailed shut nine years ago began, splinter by splinter, to come open.

* * *

Nine years ago there had been a village called Lowmere, and a summer storm that I had called up without meaning to, the way a sleeper grinds their teeth. I had been angry and seventeen and full of power I didn't understand, and the storm had come when I whistled for it, and it had not stopped when I asked. Eleven dead. My mother's house gone to kindling. My name struck from every record in three duchies—I'd done that myself, paid a forger in Vell's Crossing to erase Sable Marchetti so thoroughly that even I sometimes doubted she'd existed. Storm-caller. Storm-killer. I had sworn, kneeling in the mud of what used to be home, that I would die dry.

I had kept that oath for nine years. Through exile and hunger and the long gray anonymity of mountain passes, I had kept my hands closed around the lightning and never let it out.

Now Liss was going into the river. Now Wren was tearing themselves apart at the rail. Now thirty wardens paced us along the bank and Corvess sat her black horse in the rain and watched, and the false storm overhead ground against the true storm rising out of a terrified child, and the Graywater stood up in waves like a thing waking hungry.

There was no version of this where I stayed dry.

"WREN." I fought my way along the tilting deck, hand over hand, the rain hitting like thrown stones. "Wren, look at me."

The child turned. Their eyes were full of lightning now, literally, actual light crawling in the gray, and it broke something in me to see it—that much power in something so small, that much fear wearing that much power like a coat too heavy to shrug off.

"You're not the storm," I said. I grabbed their shoulders. Static bit through my sleeves, crawled up my arms, tried to stop my heart, and I held on. "Listen to me. You are NOT the storm. The storm is just weather. You're the one telling it what to be. So tell it."

Their mouth shaped a word. No sound. But I felt it land in my chest like a dropped anchor: a character of the old script, searing through me, meaning something like *help* or maybe *home* or maybe both at once.

"Yes," I said. "Yes. But not like this. Like THIS—"

And I opened my hands, and called the storm.

It is difficult to describe what it feels like after nine years of starving yourself, to eat. The power came up out of the river and the sky and the deep places under the mountains where storms sleep between seasons, and it came *to me*, recognizing me, the way a dog recognizes the hand that fed it even years later, even after the beatings. It poured into my bones and my old scars lit up like fuse-lines and I heard myself make a sound I hadn't made since Lowmere—not a word, not quite a laugh, something older than either.

But I didn't call it to destroy. That was the whole difference, the whole nine years distilled: I called it the way you calm a spooked horse. I put my will around Wren's panic-storm like a hand around a candleflame, and I said, in the only language that mattered, *enough, enough, little sister, enough*—and the two storms met above the ferry and I felt them argue, felt Wren's terror thrash against my steadiness, felt the child feel me, really feel me, for the first time. Their eyes widened. The lightning in them flickered, uncertain.

*Together,* I pushed at them, wordless, the way you push a thought through static. *Not mine. Not yours. Ours.*

The storms braided.

For one impossible moment the whole valley went silent—the rain hung, the waves stilled, the wardens on the bank froze mid-stride—and then the braid of our two weathers came down once, gently, deliberately, a single controlled stroke of lightning that hit the warden standard and vaporized it, silk and sigil-thread and all, leaving the pole smoking and thirty gray-cloaked soldiers standing in a circle of fused glass.

Message sent.

Then I turned everything else inward, downward, into the river, and told the Graywater to lie down. It did—grudgingly, the way Brakka did anything anyone told him, but it did. The waves sank. The ferry righted. Hume hauled Liss over the gunwale and they collapsed together on the deck boards, gasping, soaked, alive.

Wren swayed. I caught them before they fell, and the child went boneless against me, spent, breathing in great shuddering hitches—and then, muffled against my shoulder, barely audible under the settling rain, they made a sound.

Not script. Not silence.

A sob. Their first fully human sound, and it was grief and relief in equal measure, and I held them through all of it while Brakka, miracle of miracles, got the sweep-pole braced and began, stroke by stubborn stroke, to bring us toward the west bank.

* * *

We grounded in the reeds a mile downstream of the landing, hull groaning, everyone shaking. The false storm had died with its standard. The real weather was just weather again—gray drizzle, honest and stupid.

Corvess had not followed. From the ridge above the reeds I saw the warden column regrouping, and saw her sit her horse at its head, looking at us across the water. Looking, I thought, at me specifically. At the woman standing ankle-deep in a flooded ferry with lightning scars lit along both arms like brands.

She knew what I was now. Which meant the Crown would know within the week. The name Sable Marchetti, so carefully erased, so carefully dead, was about to be exhumed and posted in every garrison from here to the capital—with the word *storm-caller* attached, and everything that word would drag behind it.

"There's no going back after this," Hume said quietly, beside me. He said it without accusation. He said it the way a man confirms a route on a map. "Whatever you were hiding as. That's finished."

I looked down at Wren, asleep against my chest despite everything, one small fist knotted in my ruined coat. Then at Liss, wringing out her ledger of forged identities and finding, miraculously, most of them legible. Then at Brakka, who was bailing and complaining in equal measure.

"No," I agreed. "Finished."

And it was Liss, elbow-deep in sodden paper, who went suddenly still, holding one document up to the gray light with an expression I didn't like at all.

"Sable," she said. "This writ—it's a palace courier's pass, thirty years old, from the royal chancery. I copied its seal last month for a job in Vell's Crossing." Her finger traced the watermark, and her voice dropped. "There's writing in the fibers. Under the seal. The same characters Wren draws."

The river whispered against the hull. Somewhere upstream, thirty wardens were carrying news of us back to the capital—and the capital, I was beginning to understand, had been casting a spell on itself for thirty years, one edict at a time.

"How many documents have you forged from chancery originals?" I asked.

Liss looked up at me, and for once the forger had no lies ready.

"All of them," she said. "Every single one. And Sable—they all say the same thing."
