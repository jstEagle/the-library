## 9. Liss's Ledger of Lies

Liss had learned forgery the way other children learned prayers: at her mother's knee, in a room that smelled of lamp oil and fear.

Her mother had been a clerk in the Chancery of Writs in Vellamere before the fall—before the audit, before the accusations, before the rope. She'd taught Liss two things above all others. First: *every hand has a signature it doesn't know it's signing.* The way a man crosses his sevens. The way a woman lets her ink pool at the end of a downstroke. The small honest habits that betray even the most careful liar. Second: *the best forgery isn't a copy. It's a document so ordinary no one ever looks twice.*

Liss was eleven when her mother died, and she had been making documents no one looked twice at for twenty years since. Bills of lading. Pardons. Death certificates for people very much alive. Once, memorably, a papal dispensation for a smuggler who wanted to marry his own cousin and needed God to sign off on it. She could do a warden captain's seal in her sleep, and had, once, actually done it in her sleep, waking with ink on her cheek and a perfectly serviceable transit pass drying on the table.

So when Sable Marchetti put six blank writs of passage on the table of the safehouse above the dyer's shop in Marrowgate and said, "Make us nobody," Liss had almost laughed.

Almost. Because the child was watching her from the corner, sitting very still on a crate with their hands folded in their lap like a small solemn magistrate, and there was something about being watched by those eyes—old eyes, patient eyes, eyes like wells you couldn't find the bottom of—that made laughter die somewhere in her chest.

"Six identities," Sable said. "Names that don't trip anything. Trades that explain why we're on the road. Writs current enough to pass a checkpoint but not so fresh they raise questions about who issued them."

"You want documents that are old enough to be trusted and new enough to be valid," Liss said. "That's not a writ, that's a marriage."

"That's why I'm paying you what I'm paying you."

That was true, at least. Sable paid in crown silver, actual minted coin from thirty years back, which was either a collector's treasure or evidence of something Liss didn't want to think about yet. She took the coin. She took the job. And she took the stack of reference materials Sable produced from an oilcloth wrap—genuine edicts, genuine writs, genuine letters of conscription spanning three decades, acquired she did not ask how—to use as her models.

It was the third night, working by lamplight while the others slept, that she found it.

* * *

Forgery, done properly, was mostly reading.

Anyone could draw a seal. The craft was in understanding what you were copying—the rhythm of the language, the weight of the paper, the particular bureaucracy that produced it. A writ issued by the Border Office used different phrasing than one from the Interior; a forgery that confused the two would fool a farmer and hang its bearer at the first warden post. So Liss read. She read everything Sable had brought her, cover to cover, the way a musician reads a score, until the kingdom's paperwork sang in her head in all its official voices.

The edicts were the strangest part.

Royal edicts came from the palace chancery under the king's own seal, and they had a house style—of course they did—but it was a style that had *drifted* over the years. Liss noticed it the way she noticed everything, without meaning to, the patterns assembling themselves behind her eyes while she worked on other things. The edicts of King Aldric's early reign, thirty years past, were plain enough: declarative sentences, clean syntax, the voice of a young monarch finding his feet. *Let it be known that the tolls of the Graywater shall be remitted for the space of three years.* Straightforward. Almost warm.

By year ten, something had shifted. Not the meaning—the edicts still said sensible things, sensible things about grain tariffs and garrison rotations and the licensing of river pilots. But the *sound* had changed. The sentences grew longer. They wound. A single edict might run four hundred words to announce something that needed forty, and the extra words weren't decoration exactly, they were... structural. Repetitions with slight variations. Phrases that circled back on themselves. Sentences that began mid-thought as if continuing an argument started somewhere else.

She'd seen bureaucratic bloat before. Every aging administration suffered it. She told herself that was what this was, and kept working.

Then she made the mistake of copying one.

It was an edict from year nineteen—a routine thing, a revision of weights and measures for the eastern markets—and because her commission required her to produce a matching letter of implementation over a lesser official's name, she transcribed the full text by hand, slowly, the way her mother had taught her, feeling out the rhythm of each sentence so her forged companion-document would breathe in time with it.

Halfway through the second page, her pen slipped.

Not her hand—her pen. It dragged, just slightly, just for a moment, as if the ink had thickened or the nib had caught, and when she looked down she saw that instead of the word *measures* she had written a small dense knot of strokes that meant nothing in any alphabet she knew. A sigil. Angular, closed, precise, like a knot tied in wire.

She stared at it. Her first thought was fatigue. Her second thought was that she'd seen that shape before.

She went back through the stack. Edict after edict, year after year, holding each page up to the lamp at an angle, looking not at the words but at the *texture* of them—at the places where the ink sat heavier than sense required, where a flourish curled a half-beat too long, where a repeated phrase varied in ways that served no rhetorical purpose she could name. And once she knew what she was looking for, she couldn't stop seeing it.

There. In the grain tariff edict, year twelve: the same knot, buried in the descender of a *y*. There again in the garrison rotation of year fifteen, hidden in the crossing of a *t* that crossed twice. Year nineteen, the one she'd copied herself, the shape surfacing through her own hand like a fish rising in a pond. Year twenty-two. Year twenty-five. Year twenty-eight. The last edict in the stack, issued only months ago, announcing the anniversary festival in the capital—she held it to the light with hands that had begun, embarrassingly, to shake, and there it was, rendered in the looping tail of the final flourish, so integrated into the calligraphy that a reader would see only elegance, only style, only the handsome hand of the palace chancery.

Every edict. Thirty years. The same sigil, hundreds of times, thousands of times, woven into the legal fabric of the kingdom so finely that no one had ever—

No. That wasn't right. Someone had written these. Someone had *designed* this. Which meant someone had also, presumably, read them, checked them, sealed them, distributed them, posted them in market squares and read them aloud to crowds. Thousands of clerks. Tens of thousands of readers. And not one of them had ever seen it, because it wasn't meant to be seen. It was meant to be *read*—by something that wasn't a person, or by persons who never knew they were reading.

Liss set the edict down very carefully, the way you set down a snake you're not sure is dead.

Behind her, a floorboard creaked. She didn't jump; twenty years of illegal work had burned the startle reflex out of her. But her hand did drift, casually, toward the knife under her blotter, before Hume's voice said, "You're up late," and she let the knife go.

The sergeant stood in the doorway in his shirt, gray-haired, hollow-eyed—he always looked half-awake, though Liss had noticed he slept less since the child had quietly done whatever the child had done to his nightmares. He nodded at the papers spread across her table. "Progress?"

"I don't know." Her voice came out strange. Flat. "Hume. You served in the palace."

Something moved behind his eyes—that flinch he always had when the palace came up, the flinch of a man who'd left part of himself inside a place he couldn't reenter. "A lifetime ago."

"When you stood guard. When the edicts came through for posting. Did anyone ever—" She stopped. Started again. "Did anyone ever check the text? Against the drafts, I mean. Word for word."

He frowned. "The chancery sealed them. Sealed documents aren't questioned. That's rather the point of seals." He came closer, drawn despite himself, the way soldiers are drawn to anything that looks like trouble. "Why?"

Liss turned the anniversary edict toward him and pointed at the final flourish. "What does that look like to you?"

"A fancy tail on the *R*."

"Now look at the grain tariff. Year twelve." She laid it beside the first. "And the measures edict. Year nineteen."

He looked. His frown deepened. He was not a forger, not a reader of hands, but he was a sergeant, and sergeants know drill formations when they see them, even drawn in ink. "Those are the same," he said slowly. "That's... the same mark. Three times."

"Thirty years, Hume. Every edict the crown has issued in thirty years carries that mark, hidden in the lettering. Hundreds of copies of each. Posted in every town square in the kingdom. Read aloud at every market day." She heard her own voice climbing and forced it down. "Do you understand what that means? It means every law this country obeys is also a spell. Or half of one. Or a thousand repetitions of one. It means when the herald reads a tariff schedule to a crowd of farmers, he's reading *liturgy*, and he doesn't know it, and neither do they."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below them, in the dye works, a vat bubbled; the smell of mordant rose through the floorboards, sharp and chemical.

"That's madness," Hume said at last, but his voice had no conviction in it. He was staring at the edicts the way a man stares at a door he'd walked past every day of his life and only now notices is slightly ajar. "The king wouldn't—"

"The king signs what the chancery puts in front of him," Liss said. "You know that better than I do. Who writes the edicts, Hume? Who holds the pen?"

And there it was again, the flinch, deeper this time. "I never saw the king write anything," he said quietly. "I stood post outside his study for two years. He was in there every morning. Hours. And I never once heard a pen scratch paper." He rubbed his face with both hands. "I told myself the study was soundproofed. I told myself a lot of things."

From the corner of the room, where the child slept—or seemed to sleep—curled under a wool blanket on their crate, came a sound.

Liss turned. Wren was awake. She couldn't say how she knew; the child hadn't moved. But the quality of the dark over there had changed, the way air changes before lightning, and when Liss looked at the small pale face half-buried in the blanket, the well-deep eyes were open and fixed—not on her, not on Hume, but on the edicts spread across the table. On the sigils.

Wren lifted one small hand and traced a shape in the air. Slowly. Deliberately. The angular knot, the closed wire-loop, drawn from memory, drawn with the ease of someone writing their own name.

"You know it," Liss whispered. "You've *seen* it."

The child shook their head. Then, with the terrible patience that was somehow worse than any answer, they touched their own chest—right over the heart—and then pointed at the mark on the page.

Hume made a small sound. Liss found she had stopped breathing entirely.

"It's not a spell *about* something," she said, and her voice sounded far away to her own ears, a voice speaking from the bottom of a well. "It's not a ward or a curse or a compulsion. It's a signature. It's the Crown signing its work. Every law, every edict, every proclamation for thirty years—it's all one document. One long continuous casting, renewed with every proclamation, fed with every reading." She looked at the pages, at the elegant harmless lettering, at the whole vast machinery of ordinary governance humming along underneath the sigil like water under ice. "We're not subjects of a kingdom. We're the medium it's written on."

The child watched her with those bottomless eyes. And Liss thought, with a horror that arrived not as a shout but as a quiet settling, like snowfall: *my mother hanged for forging a letter worth a hundredth of one of these. My mother died for moving ink. And whoever holds the pen in that palace has been moving the entire country.*

She thought of every document she'd ever forged, every seal she'd ever faked, and wondered—with a cold new nausea—how many of them had carried the mark too, passed along through the templates and exemplars she'd copied from, the corruption propagating itself through the world's paperwork like rot through a beam. She thought of the papal dispensation. She thought of the death certificates. She thought of her own name, Liss, signed at the bottom of a hundred lies, and whether any of those signatures had ever been read by the thing that read everything else.

"Get Sable," she said to Hume. Her voice was steady now. Steadier than she felt. Twenty years of forgery had taught her that much: whatever you're feeling, your hand stays level, because the hand is the thing that gets examined. "Wake everyone. And bring me every scrap of official paper in this building. Writs, licenses, tax notices, playbills, prayer cards—I don't care what it is. If it was printed or penned anywhere near the capital, I want it on this table."

"Why?" Hume asked. "What are you going to do?"

Liss pulled a fresh sheet toward her, dipped her pen, and looked at the sigil glowing faintly in the lamplight like a brand beneath the skin of the page.

"What any good forger does," she said. "Find out how it's made. Because if the Crown can write law as spellcraft, then law can be unwritten the same way—and I am going to learn the hand that wrote it if it kills me."

In the corner, Wren hummed—three low notes, soft as breath, a fragment of melody that made the lamp flame stand perfectly still—and reached out one finger toward the oldest edict in the stack, the one from the king's first year, the one whose lettering was still clean.

The one that carried no mark at all.

Whatever the Crown was, it had a beginning. And beginnings, Liss knew—her mother's whole creed, the forger's first commandment—were where the alterations showed.
