## 10. Hume's Desertion, Revisited

The fire had burned down to its red bones by the time Hume finally spoke.

They were camped in a hollow below the Graywater road, three days out from the ferry fight, and the crew had arranged themselves in the old soldier's geometry without anyone ordering it: Sable nearest the horses, Liss with her ledger on her knees even now, Brakka snoring with the shameless depth of a man who had sold nothing lately and was proud of it. Wren sat closest to the coals, wrapped in a blanket two sizes too big, watching Hume with those eyes that never quite blinked when they should.

Hume had been polishing the same boot buckle for an hour. It gleamed. It had gleamed an hour ago. His hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

"You all think I ran because I was a coward," he said.

Liss looked up. Sable didn't move at all, which was worse.

"I ran because I wouldn't swear," Hume said. "There's a difference. Small one. But it's mine."

* * *

He'd been twenty-two when he took the palace oath, a farm boy from the Loamlands with shoulders like doorframes and a head full of banners. The King's Own, they called it. Two hundred men in white and gold, sworn to stand between the crown and every harm in the world. His mother wept at his commissioning. His father got drunk enough to say he was proud and meant it.

The first year was parades. The second year was boredom dressed as ceremony—corridor watches, gate rotations, standing very still while nobles swept past pretending not to see him. He learned the palace the way you learn a river: where it ran shallow, where the currents pulled, which doors opened onto rooms no edict ever mentioned.

It was in his third year, on the night rotation of the Amber Gallery, that he first saw the king up close.

Not at distance. Not across a throne room with trumpets drowning everything human. Close. Ten feet. The king had come walking through the gallery at midnight—no escort worth the name, just two quiet attendants—and stopped before a painting of his own coronation.

"Beautiful, isn't it," the king said.

Hume hadn't known he was being addressed. He'd snapped to attention so fast his neck cracked. "Your Majesty."

The king turned. He was smiling. That was the thing Hume could never make anyone understand afterward—the king was *smiling*, warm as harvest, the kind of smile that made you want to smile back, made your face do it without asking permission.

"It's beautiful," the king said again, "isn't it."

And Hume had looked at the painting, and looked at the king, and understood in one cold drop down his spine that the question wasn't a question. There was nothing behind it. No curiosity, no vanity, no man checking whether his likeness flattered him. The words came out of the king's mouth the way water comes out of a pump: someone had worked the handle.

"Yes, Your Majesty," Hume said.

"Wonderful," said the king, still smiling, and walked on, and his eyes passed over Hume like lamplight over a wall—touching everything, holding nothing.

Hume stood his post until dawn. Then he went to the barracks and asked the sergeant-major, carefully, casually, what the king was like off-duty.

"The king?" The sergeant-major didn't look up from his cards. "The king is gracious. The king is beloved. Ask a better question or don't ask."

* * *

After that, Hume started watching. It's what soldiers do; it's what kept their grandfathers alive. And once he watched, he couldn't stop seeing it.

The king agreed with everyone. That was the first sign. A monarch who agrees with everyone is a monarch who is managed, and Hume had assumed—everyone assumed—that the management was the Privy Council, the chancellors, the great machine of state grinding its slow gears. But the councilors fought each other like cats. They disagreed about tariffs, borders, marriages, grain. The only thing they never disagreed about was whatever the king had already smiled and assented to. As if the decisions weren't being made in any room at all.

The second sign was the memory. Or the lack of it.

A chamberlain once referenced, in the king's presence, a drought relief the king himself had ordered six years prior—a famous act, coins from the royal purse, songs written about it. The king smiled and said, "How generous of whoever arranged that." The room laughed politely. Hume did not laugh. He had seen men come back from campaigns with holes in them like that, shell-shocked lads who'd lost whole years to the guns. But this wasn't trauma. This was tidiness. The gaps in the king were too clean, like a house stripped by professionals before the auction.

The third sign—the sign that ended it—came on the eve of the Renewal Oath.

Every guard swore it. Every year. Hand on the crown's sigil, words older than the dynasty itself, renewed each anniversary of the coronation. Routine. Hume had taken it twice already without a thought.

But that year, the week before, they moved the ceremony. Moved it into the undercroft beneath the throne hall—a place guards weren't posted, a place Hume had never had reason to enter. And they brought in priests he didn't recognize, in vestments of no order he knew, gray-robed and soft-spoken, and the sigil they set on the lectern wasn't the enameled badge from the armory. It was older. Black metal, worn smooth, and when the torchlight crossed it the light seemed to bend wrong, the way heat bends above summer roads.

"We swear to the crown," the lead priest said, "and to what wears it."

That was the liturgy. Hume had recited some version of those words every year of his service and never heard them properly until that moment, in that cold stone room, with that black thing drinking the torchlight. *To what wears it.* Not the man. Never the man.

They went down the line, one by one, hand on the sigil. Hume watched each guard touch it and rise looking—normal. Relieved, even. Lighter. Like men coming out of a confessional.

When his turn came, he put out his hand.

And stopped.

He couldn't have told you why, then. He could tell you now. It was the same instinct that makes a man not drink from a stream when the deer carcass is upstream. Something in him, some animal ledger older than oaths, weighed that black metal and wrote *do not*.

"I'm ill," he heard himself say. "This morning. Beg pardon."

The priest smiled at him. It was the king's smile. Exactly the king's smile, on a stranger's face, and Hume understood that he had just seen something he was never supposed to see, and that the priest knew it, and that the smile was a door closing.

"Of course, sergeant," the priest said gently. "You'll take it tomorrow."

Hume deserted before dawn. Climbed the laundry chute with his boots around his neck like a thief in his own garrison, went over the wall into the dark, and ran until running became walking and walking became a new life with a forged discharge and a name spelled slightly wrong.

He told himself for eleven years that he'd run from a superstition. A bad feeling in a basement. Men desert over less.

Then he'd watched a barefoot child walk through a blizzard speaking a language dead a thousand years, and watched wardens burn an archive to find her, and heard Sable Marchetti say the word *Crown* like a man naming the thing that killed his family—and every buried night in the Amber Gallery came up out of the ground at once.

* * *

"So that's it," Hume said to the fire. His voice was steady now, which surprised him. "I saw a hollow man in a golden cage, and I ran, and I let myself call it wisdom. Eleven years I've been proud of that night." He shook his head slowly. "Proud. Of leaving him in there."

"He was already gone," Liss said quietly. "One guard wouldn't have—"

"One guard," Hume said. "Two hundred guards. Each of us the one guard. That's how it works, isn't it? That's how it always works." He looked around the circle. "You want to know the worst part? The part I've never said out loud? When I climbed that wall, I looked back. From the ridge above the city. Whole palace lit up like a festival, and him in the middle of it, smiling at everyone, agreeing with everyone, empty as a drum. And my feeling—" his jaw worked— "my feeling was *good riddance*. Not poor bastard. Not somebody help him. Good riddance, glad it's not me. I left a man buried alive and felt lucky."

Wren shifted. The blanket rustled. When Hume glanced over, the child was looking at him with an expression far too old for the face wearing it—not pity, exactly. Recognition. The look of one forgotten thing finding another.

Hume huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. "Look at that. Even the babe pities me."

Wren reached out and touched his wrist. Just that. Two small fingers, brief as a moth landing.

The nightmares didn't come that night. Hume slept like the dead for the first time since Graywater, and if he dreamed at all, he dreamed of a corridor full of light and a door he'd locked behind him swinging open.

* * *

Morning came gray and clean. Hume was up before anyone, sharpening a knife that didn't need it, and when Sable emerged from the treeline with an armful of kindling, Hume was waiting.

"I'm going back," he said.

Sable crouched by the fire ring, arranging sticks with more care than they needed. "Back where?"

"You know where. Through the gates I jumped off of. If we're doing this—if we're really walking into the capital and prying that thing off the throne—you'll need someone who knows the inside. Guard rotations. Which walls are thick and which are theater. Where the undercroft stairs run." He sheathed the knife. "I know the palace, Marchetti. Better than I know my own mother's kitchen, and I know her kitchen better than I know myself."

Sable looked up. In the flat morning light, the storm-caller's eyes had that weather-reading quality Hume had noticed and never commented on—like they were always checking the horizon behind your face. "You understand what you're offering. Corvess has our scent. The capital will be the deepest warded ground either of us has ever stood in. And you're a deserter with a face the King's Own drilled beside for three years."

"Aye. All true." Hume grinned, and it was an ugly, honest grin. "But here's what else is true. For eleven years I've been a man running from a door. Every job, every border town, every card game—I could feel that door at my back. You don't outrun a door. You only get to choose whether you go back through it closed or open." He nodded toward Wren, asleep again by the ashes, small hands curled loose. "That child was made to be forgotten. So was he, near enough—hollowed out and smiled at while something wore him like a coat. Somebody forgot them both. I'm done being somebody."

Sable was silent a long moment. Then: "You could die there."

"I could die anywhere. I nearly did at Graywater, and that was a river." Hume stuck out his hand. "Swear me proper, then. None of your smuggler's oaths with loopholes. The real one. The one I wouldn't take in that cellar."

Sable took the hand. Their grip was dry and strong and faintly warm, warm the way air goes before lightning. "No crown," Sable said. "No crown and no thing wearing it. We swear past both—to the people under them. Can you swear to that?"

"To the people under them." Hume tasted the words. They fit his mouth better than any oath ever had. "Aye. To the people under them."

* * *

They broke camp within the hour, and it was Liss, riding up alongside Hume on the switchbacks, who changed the shape of everything.

She'd been scribbling in her ledger against her knee for two days straight—he'd caught her at it by firelight, muttering, cross-referencing—but today she reined in beside him with an expression he'd last seen on sappers examining a bridge they'd been ordered to cross anyway.

"Hume. Your Renewal Oath. The one in the undercroft." Her voice was low, pitched under the wind. "You said the words every year before that one too. Barracks version. Standard text."

"Hand on the sigil, swear to the crown and what wears it. Yearly. Why?"

"Because I've spent thirty years forging official documents, and official documents are just oaths with ink on." She turned the ledger toward him, and her finger was trembling—not with fear, he realized, but with the particular fury of a craftsman who has found a flaw in someone else's perfect work. "Every royal edict for the last three decades carries the same seal flourish. Same curl, same tail. I always took it for house style. It's not house style, Hume. It's a sigil. The same black-metal geometry as your undercroft lectern, drawn fine enough that nobody sees it unless they draw seals for a living." She met his eyes. "It's not just the king. It's not just the guards. Every law, every writ, every pardon and tax levy and border pass in this kingdom for thirty years—it's all been written as spellcraft. The whole country signed the oath without knowing it. We're all standing in that cellar."

Hume looked at the page. At the little curling flourish repeated down decades of edicts, patient as frost.

Somewhere behind them, Wren began humming—an old, strange melody, notes that seemed to arrive slightly before they were sung—and the horses, which had been shying at the switchback wind, went calm all at once, ears forward, as if listening.

"That child," Hume said slowly, "was made to be forgotten."

"And I think," said Liss, closing the ledger with a snap like a trap setting, "somebody in this kingdom just decided we shouldn't be. There's a vault under the ice where Odile Vane kept her deepest holdings—and if she survived that fire, that's where she'll be. We need to get there before Corvess does."

Hume turned his horse up the trail, back toward the mountains, back toward everything he'd fled—and found, to his astonishment, that his feet wanted to go.
