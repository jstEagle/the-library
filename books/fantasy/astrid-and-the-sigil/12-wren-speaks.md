## 12. Wren Speaks

The vault breathed cold.

Wren felt it the way they felt everything now—not on skin, exactly, but underneath it, in the place where words lived before they became words. The ice above them was older than any kingdom. It pressed down through a hundred feet of blue-black glacier, and beneath that pressure, the script in Wren's chest stirred like a sleeper turning over.

They sat cross-legged on a floor of ancient stone, watching Odile Vane work.

The old archivist had cleared a circle of frost from the wall with her bare hands, breath steaming, fingers blue to the second knuckle, and she had not stopped talking since they'd descended. She talked the way some people prayed—continuously, desperately, as if silence might let something terrible catch up with them.

"—and here, do you see, here is the first dynasty's seal, and *here*—" her fingernail traced a groove in the stone, "—is where someone tried to chisel it out. Someone who understood. Too late, of course. It's always too late by the time anyone understands."

Sable stood at the edge of the lantern light with their arms crossed, and Hume leaned against the doorway with his sword across his knees, and Liss was copying everything into her ledger by candlelight, her tongue between her teeth. Brakka had not come down. Wren had watched him linger at the top of the ice stairs, looking back the way they'd come, and had felt something small and cold turn over in their belly—not fear, exactly. Recognition. They knew what it looked like when a person's feet were already walking away.

But mostly Wren watched Odile, because Odile was reading them.

Not their face. Their *script*.

"Come here, little one," Odile said, and patted the stone beside her. "I won't hurt you. I've spent forty years learning not to hurt things like you."

*Things like you.* Wren crossed the room. The cold came up through their bare feet—they still didn't wear boots if they could help it; cold had never been an enemy—and they knelt where Odile pointed, in front of the carved wall.

"Now," Odile said softly. "When I touch this line, I want you to tell me what happens. Not with your mouth. With whatever you use instead."

She laid two fingers against the groove.

The world *rang*.

It wasn't sound. It was the thing under sound, the wire beneath the bell. Every glyph in the wall lit faintly gold, then dimmed, and Wren felt the vibration travel up through their knees, their spine, settling behind their sternum where the oldest marks lived—the ones even Sable hadn't seen, the ones that only showed when Wren slept or bled or dreamed too hard.

And the wall answered them.

Not in light. In *memory*.

Wren gasped—a small, punched-out sound—and suddenly they were standing somewhere else: a corridor of white stone, tall windows, a boy running. A boy with dark hair and a gap in his smile, laughing over his shoulder, holding something cupped in both hands like a secret—no, like a bird, no—

Like nothing. His hands were empty. He was laughing at nothing, running toward nothing, and Wren knew him the way you know your own reflection in a window you weren't expecting.

*I know you,* Wren thought, and the thought came out in the old tongue, the dead tongue, the one that was not dead but sleeping, and the corridor shattered into snowfall and they were kneeling on frozen stone again with four faces staring down at them.

"Wren." Sable crouched, close enough that Wren could see the storm-grey flecks in their eyes. "What did you see?"

Wren opened their mouth.

Nothing came. Nothing ever came. There was a door inside them where common speech should have been, and behind the door was all the language in the world, stacked floor to ceiling, and none of it fit through the crack.

So they did what they always did. They reached for the script.

Their hand moved without permission, finger tracing shapes in the frost on the floor: *boy. white hall. empty hands.*

Liss read it upside down and went pale. "That's the palace. That's the *palace*, the great hall before the fire of 811, that's—it's in every history book, it's—"

"The king," Hume said quietly. He hadn't moved from the doorway, but his voice had changed, gone rough. "As a boy. Before."

Silence. Even Odile stopped talking.

Wren looked at their own hand, at the frost-marks already melting, and felt the truth rising in them like water filling a boot-print. They had known something was wrong with them for as long as they had known anything—which was perhaps three years, perhaps thirty, time worked strangely when you were made rather than born—but knowing a thing and being able to say it were separated by that locked door, and the door had never once opened.

Until now.

Because the vault was pressing on them. Because the whole mountain was a held breath, a ritual half-finished, waiting, and rituals *pull*. Wren could feel it tugging at the seams of themselves, patient as tide, saying: *come apart, come home, be used.*

"No," Wren said.

Everyone turned.

It wasn't a word so much as a shape made of air, cracked and rusted, like a hinge that hadn't swung in a thousand years. But it was common-tongue. It was *speech*.

Odile's hands flew to her mouth. Liss dropped her pen. Sable went very still, the way they went still before lightning.

"I said no," Wren said again, and their voice was strange and small and theirs, scraped raw from disuse, and they hated how it wobbled and loved that it existed. "The vault wants me. I can feel it. It wants me to—to open. To be the door." They looked down at their own chest, at the place where the oldest glyphs slept. "And I don't want to be a door yet. I want to know what I am first."

Nobody spoke. Snow ticked against the ice far above them, a sound like slow applause.

Then Odile began, very carefully, to cry.

"Oh, you poor *child*," she whispered. "You magnificent, terrible, brave little thing. Do you have any idea—you spoke. You *chose* to speak. Binding-script doesn't choose. It executes. It obeys. And you just—" she wiped her eyes with a frost-burned knuckle, "—you just told a thousand-year-old ritual to wait its turn."

"It can wait," Wren said. Each word cost more than the last, like drawing water from a very deep well, but the well was not dry. The well had never been dry. The door had been locked from the *outside*, all this time, and the vault's pressure was rattling the lock loose. "I want to know. Please. Tell me what I am."

Odile looked at Sable. Sable looked at Odile. Some whole conversation happened between them in the space of a breath, the way it did with people who had traveled together long enough, and then Sable nodded once and sat down on the cold stone beside Wren, close, shoulder almost touching shoulder. Warmth. Wren leaned into it a fraction of an inch.

"Then we do this properly," Odile said, and pulled a battered journal from her coat, one of the few things salvaged from the burning archive, its edges charred brown. "From the beginning. And child—if you need to stop, you stop. Understanding shouldn't be a knife."

* * *

The beginning, it turned out, was a king nobody remembered.

"There was a monarch before the first dynasty," Odile said, tracing the charred pages. "Before crowns meant anything. The histories call him the Shepherd King, which is a polite way of saying we don't know his name, because his name was the first thing taken." She glanced up. "He was young. Younger than Sable. He had enemies—he always has enemies, that's the curse of thrones—and those enemies couldn't kill him. So they did worse. They hired binders, the old kind, the kind who wrote spells in living language instead of ink, and they built a thing. A crown. Not metal. A *pattern*. A parasite woven from stolen memory, fed year after year, reign after reign, growing fat on kings."

"But he died," Hume said. "Obviously. That was centuries ago."

"He died," Odile agreed. "Or the thing wearing him died, which may amount to the same. But here's what the fragment says, the piece we saved from my archive, the piece the wardens burned a library to destroy." Her voice dropped. "A binding cannot be perfect. Every spell leaves leavings. Scraps. When the binders took the king's memories—his childhood, his name, his mother's voice, the color of his first winter—they didn't hold all of it. Something fell through their fingers. A remainder. A piece of the pattern too small to matter, too true to erase."

Wren's hands had gone cold, and not from the ice.

"They discarded it," Odile said gently. "Left it in the high mountains, unformed, unfinished. A scrap of a king's childhood, given just enough flesh to persist. Sleeping in the snow for a thousand years until—" she spread her hands, helpless, "—until it woke up. Until it walked out of a blizzard to a storm-caller's cabin, barefoot, speaking the language it was written in."

The vault was very quiet.

Wren looked at their own hands. Small hands. Chapped at the knuckles. One thumbnail bitten short. They turned them over slowly, and the script rose faintly along the veins, gold-green, patient, and for the first time they understood what they were looking at.

Not tattoos. Not curses.

*A boy's memories.* Written on them like a letter that had been folded and folded and folded until it became a person.

"That's why I know the palace," Wren said. Their voice was getting stronger, word by word, the well giving easier. "That's why I know the boy in the white hall. He's not—I'm not—" They frowned, working it through, feeling the truth settle into them like a stone finding its place at the bottom of a stream. "I'm not a copy of him. I'm a *piece* of him. The part they threw away. The part that laughed at nothing in the corridor because he was happy, once, before they hollowed him out."

"Yes," Odile whispered.

"And the king now. The one in the capital." Wren looked up at Sable. "He's still him. Underneath. Isn't he? The Crown eats his memories but it can't eat the part that remembers being happy, because that part isn't in his head anymore." They touched their own chest. "It's here. Walking around. Getting snow in its boots."

Sable made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been grief. "Getting snow in its boots," they repeated softly. "Yes. I suppose that's you."

"So the ritual." Liss had stopped writing; her pen hovered, forgotten. "The unbinding. The vessel, the door, the hand—Odile, you said it needs a vessel willing to hold thirty years of returned memory. And a door. And—"

"A door," Odile said, and her eyes went to Wren, and everyone's eyes went to Wren, and Wren felt the weight of it land on their small shoulders like the glacier overhead.

"The vault wants me to open," Wren said. "That's what it's been pulling at. I'm the door because I'm the leftover seam. Where the binding was torn, I'm the tear." They considered this. It didn't frighten them, quite. It was more like hearing the last line of a story they'd been telling themselves in their sleep. "If I open, the memories go through me. Into the vessel. Into—" they looked at Sable, and their throat tightened, "—into you."

"Into me," Sable said quietly.

"And then into the king. Home. All of it home." Wren hugged their knees. "And it will hurt you. I don't know how I know that, but I know it. Thirty years is heavy. Maybe it takes things from you on the way through. Maybe—" they stopped, because the next part was too big, and they had only had a voice for an hour and didn't trust it with big things yet.

"Maybe it takes everything," Sable finished, calm as a frozen lake.

"Sable—" Liss started.

"We knew the price might be steep when we started," Sable said. "We just didn't know who'd pay it."

"No," Wren said.

Everyone looked at them again. Wren unfolded from their huddle and stood, and the script along their arms brightened, and the vault's pressure pushed against them like wind against a shutter, and they pushed back.

"You keep deciding things for me," Wren said. "All of you. Sable decided to take me in. Odile decided what I am. Liss writes about me in her ledger like I'm cargo. Hume stands in doorways protecting me like I'll break." Their voice cracked and they let it crack. "I was *made* to be forgotten. That's the first thing I ever was—a thing someone threw away because it was too small to matter. Well. I'm not going to spend the rest of myself being small, and I'm not going to be thrown into a ritual either, like a coin into a well, like something to be *used*." They stepped forward, into the middle of the lantern light, into the middle of all of them. "If I'm the door, then I open the door. Me. When I choose. And I'm choosing now."

The words rang off the ice.

"Do you understand what you're volunteering for?" Sable asked, low.

"Do you?" Wren shot back, and had the deep satisfaction of seeing the storm-caller blink. "You're going to carry thirty years of a stranger's life through your body and maybe lose yours. I'm going to open a seam in a thousand-year-old spell. Neither of us knows what happens after. So don't stand there looking at me like I'm the fragile one." They lifted their chin. "I walked out of a blizzard to find you. Barefoot. I think I'm allowed to walk into a throne room the same way."

For a long moment, nobody moved.

Then Hume, from the doorway, began—quietly, gruffly, unmistakably—to laugh. "Gods preserve us," he said. "It talks like you, Marchetti. It *talks like you*."

"It always did," Sable murmured. "It just didn't have the words." They looked at Wren for a long moment, and something passed across their face—grief and pride tangled together so tightly Wren couldn't have said which was which—and then Sable bowed their head, formally, absurdly, like a knight to a queen. "Then it's agreed. Door, vessel, hand. All volunteers."

"All volunteers," Odile echoed, wiping her eyes again.

Wren exhaled, and the exhale shook, because the moment they stopped pushing, the enormity of what they'd just done caught up with them, and their legs went wobbly, and Sable's arm was there, steady around their shoulders, warm through the cold.

"Hey," Sable said, quietly, just for them. "Scared?"

"Very," Wren admitted.

"Good. Brave means scared and doing it anyway. Anyone who tells you different has never done anything brave."

Wren leaned into them and let the shaking happen, and above them the glacier groaned and settled, and the vault's pull eased—not gone, never gone, but gentler now, as if the old ritual had heard them and, grudgingly, agreed to wait its turn.

* * *

They climbed back up through the ice stairs as the light outside turned the color of pewter. Liss was tallying supplies in a low, worried voice—*three days of hardtack, half the lamp oil, Brakka's ferry money gone with the ambush losses*—and Hume was arguing with her about whether salt pork counted as food or punishment, and it was ordinary, it was blessedly ordinary, and Wren listened to all of it with their new voice folded away inside them like a bird in a pocket.

At the top of the stairs, the gray daylight hit them, and Wren stopped.

Brakka was gone.

Not gone-gone. His bedroll was still there, his pole still leaned against the rocks. But his tracks led away from camp, down the southern slope, alone, and they were fresh—fresh as in *this morning*, fresh as in while the rest of them had been underground learning the shape of the end of the world.

And beside his tracks, half-buried in the snow where he'd stood a long time before leaving, was a scrap of paper weighted with a stone.

Liss reached it first. She read it, and Wren watched the blood leave her face in real time, watch the pen fall from her fingers into the snow.

"What is it?" Sable said. "Liss. What is it?"

Liss held up the paper with a hand that wasn't steady. On it, in Brakka's blunt ferryman's scrawl, were only five words:

*They have my family. Forgive me.*

And below that, smaller, as if written after a long pause:

*Corvess knows the pass you're taking.*

The wind came down off the glacier and carried the paper's corner upward, snapping it like a small flag of surrender, and far to the south, past the tree line, past the foothills, a thin thread of smoke rose from the direction of Thornhollow—too straight, too deliberate, too much smoke for a chimney.

Signal smoke.

Wren looked at Sable, and Sable looked at Wren, and neither of them needed the old tongue to understand what passed between them.

The hunt had found them after all.
