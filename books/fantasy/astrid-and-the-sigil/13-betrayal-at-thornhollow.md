## 13. Betrayal at Thornhollow

The trouble with trusting a man was that you had to keep doing it long after the reasons ran out.

Sable felt it first as a wrongness in the air above Thornhollow—a flatness where there should have been texture. Storm-sense was a language they'd spent ten years refusing to speak, but the mountains still spoke to them whether they answered or not, and tonight the sky over the hollow read like a page with something written on it in invisible ink. Pressure stacking in the west. A cold front moving against the wind's grain. And beneath it all, faint as a scar under paint: the hum of warden wards, tuned and waiting.

They were being herded into a bowl.

Sable reined up at the head of the column and let the others draw level on the narrow track. Below them Thornhollow opened out—a cup of dark pines around a frozen millpond, the old drover's inn a smudge of chimney smoke at its center. It was the last safe crossing before the high passes. Odile's maps said so. Brakka's memory of the route said so. Brakka had walked them into this valley with the easy confidence of a man who knew every stone by its first name.

"Trouble?" Hume asked quietly. The sergeant had learned to read Sable's silences the way other men read weather.

"Maybe nothing." Sable didn't believe their own voice. "Maybe everything."

Wren stirred in the pack-basket behind Sable's saddle, small hands finding the belt at their hip. The child had gone quiet in the way that meant listening—not with ears. Static prickled along Sable's spine where Wren's forehead rested between their shoulder blades, faint as a moth against glass.

Liss rode up on the left, breath steaming, the ledger she never stopped writing in strapped across her chest like armor. "You've got your storm-face on," she said. "I hate your storm-face."

"My what?"

"The face you make when you're about to ruin my evening." She squinted down into the hollow. "Inn's got lights. Too many lights for a drover's rest in dead winter."

Sable counted them. Seven windows lit. A drover's inn this far off the king's road would keep two, maybe three burning—one for the common room, one for the kitchen, one because someone always forgot. Seven meant either a wedding or an occupation.

"Brakka," Sable called back down the line.

The ferryman came plodding forward through the snow, big shoulders hunched into his coat, his face doing something complicated when he saw theirs. He'd been doing complicated things with his face for two days. Sable had told themselves it was worry for his family—Brakka had a wife and three daughters in Graywater, and Graywater was a day behind them and full of wardens now.

"How many ways out of this valley?" Sable asked him.

"Two," Brakka said. "The way we came, and the pass north."

"And if someone wanted to close both?"

The pause lasted half a heartbeat too long. Half a heartbeat is nothing, most days. Tonight it was a confession shouted from a mountaintop.

"I'd not know anything about that," Brakka said.

Hume's hand moved toward his sword. Sable put out one finger without looking—the smallest gesture—and the sergeant froze. Down in the hollow, one of the seven lit windows went dark. Then another. Someone inside was counting the same stars they were.

"You sold us," Sable said. Not loudly. There was no need. The night was cold enough to carry a whisper a mile.

Brakka's jaw worked. Snow gathered on his shoulders and he didn't brush it off, as if some part of him had already decided to stand very still and let the weather bury whatever happened next. "They have my girls," he said finally. "Corvess's wardens pulled them out of school in Graywater eight days ago. Held them in the ferry house. Fed them, even. Polite as priests." His voice cracked down the middle. "Then a captain I never seen sat them at a table and showed them a drawing of me, and asked them did they know the man who carried Mama's letters to the mountain folk. My Lira, she's seven. She told them everything she knew because she thought it was a game."

The words fell into the snow between them and lay there.

"They gave me a choice," Brakka went on. "Lead the storm-caller into Thornhollow, and my girls go home. Refuse, and—" He spread his huge hands, empty. "There's no refuse. You understand? There's no word for it. They only ask you the question once, and after that every answer is yes."

Sable looked at him for a long moment. Anger came up in them like floodwater—hot, ancient, familiar. This was the shape betrayal always took: not a knife in the dark but a friend's tired eyes in the firelight, asking you to understand. Ten years ago Sable would have called the storm down on him for less. Ten years ago Sable had called storms for much less, and a village had paid the bill.

"Wren," Sable said quietly, "hold on."

And then the world came apart below them.

* * *

It began with light. Every window in the inn flared white at once—not lamplight but ward-light, sigils igniting in the glass like frost catching fire—and the whole bowl of Thornhollow lit up blue-white as a lightning strike held still. Figures poured from the treeline on three sides. Wardens, a dozen or more, in gray lacquered coats, crossbows leveled up the slope, and behind them the bloodhound-mage Sable had been feeling in their teeth for a week: a gaunt figure whose shadow moved wrong, sniffing the air with a face that wasn't using its nose.

"Ambush!" Hume roared, already dragging Liss off her horse as the first bolts came up the slope. They struck snow and bark in stuttering lines. One took Sable's mare through the neck and the animal screamed and went down and Sable rolled clear, hauling Wren's basket with them, hitting the drift behind a granite shelf as the night filled with shouting.

"Down! All of you down!" Sable pressed Wren's head into the snow beside them. The child's eyes were enormous, reflecting the ward-light, and static crawled over their skin in visible waves now—fear leaking out of them into the world. The temperature dropped so fast Sable's breath turned to falling ice.

"Liss!" Sable shouted. "The writs—burn them if you have to, just stay—"

A bolt took Liss through the shoulder.

She made no sound at first, which was worse than screaming. She simply folded, the ledger spilling from its strap, pages fanning across the snow—weeks of forged identities, travel writs, the pattern she'd found stitched through thirty years of royal edicts, all of it whitening with blood. Hume got his body over hers and started swearing with the fluency of a man who had done this before, tearing open her coat, pressing cloth into the wound while bolts hammered the rocks around them.

Sable stood up.

Later, they would not remember deciding to. They remembered the cold coming up out of the ground to meet them, and the sky answering, and the old language rising in their throat like water finding a crack in a wall. They had sworn off this. They had buried this. But magic finds you whether you want it or not—it had found them in a blizzard wearing a child's bare feet, and it found them now wearing a dozen crossbows and a burning girl.

"Enough," Sable said, and the storm heard.

Clouds that weren't there five minutes ago boiled over the rim of the hollow. Wind hit the warden line like a door thrown open, flattening snow in a white wall, snapping trees at the waist. Crossbowmen staggered, shields up, and Sable walked down the slope into the gale with lightning walking ahead of them—striking the ground deliberately, left and right, herding rather than killing, each bolt a sentence in a grammar of warning. *Go. Go. I am giving you the chance I was never given.*

The bloodhound-mage stood fast. Its wrong-shadow stretched toward Sable across the snow, and Sable felt it tasting them, reading the signature of their power, and understood with cold clarity that it now knew exactly who they were. That price was already paid; hiding was already over. So Sable gave it something else to taste—a full-throated scream of thunder that cracked the ice on the millpond from shore to shore—and the mage's composure broke at last, and it ran with the rest.

In ninety seconds it was finished. Wardens scattered into the treeline. The inn's false lights died. The wind eased, grudgingly, like a dog told to heel, and snow began to fall straight down into sudden silence.

Sable stood in the middle of the hollow, sparks still crawling their arms, breathing like a bellows, and looked at what they'd done. No bodies. Not one. The realization shook them more than the fighting had. Ten years ago there would have been bodies.

Then they remembered Liss, and ran.

* * *

Hume had her propped against a fallen log, the wound packed and bound, her face gray as tallow. The bolt had gone through the meat below her collarbone—bad, not mortal, the kind of wound that killed you slowly instead of quickly if fever set in. Her ledger lay soaked beside her. She was trying to gather the pages with her good hand, fingers clumsy with cold and shock, and she was crying without any noise at all.

"It's all right," Sable said, kneeling. "The work survived. You survived. Both things are true."

"Some of it's ruined." Her voice was a thread. "Three months of patterns, ink running—" She laughed, wet and thin. "I forge documents for a living and I can't keep my own ledger dry."

"Hume, get her on a horse. We're at the north pass within the hour." Sable tore strips from their own coat, added them to the bandage. "Odile will have willowbark and spiderweb-glass. She'll live."

"She'd better," Hume muttered, gathering Liss up with a gentleness that sat strangely on his soldier's frame. "I've gotten attached to the annoying ones."

Which left the hollow, and the inn, and Brakka.

He hadn't run. Sable found him standing where they'd left him, in the open, snow to his knees, hands empty and raised—not in surrender, exactly. In offering. As if to say: here I am, do what the story requires.

Wren stood beside Sable now, having refused the basket, small hand gripping two of Sable's fingers. The child looked at Brakka with those too-old eyes, and static whispered between them and Sable's skin, and Sable understood that Wren could feel what Sable felt—that the child was a mirror, and whatever Sable chose next would be written into them somewhere deep.

Kill him. The thought arrived honestly, and Sable let it stand in the doorway of their mind without inviting it further. He sold you. Liss has a hole in her. Hume carried her bleeding through a killing field. The law—if the Crown's law meant anything—would call him a traitor twice over, to them and to the wardens both, once this was reported. Justice, the clean kind, the kind with rules, would put a blade in him and call it arithmetic.

But the Crown wrote law as spellcraft. Liss had proven it, page by page. Every edict for thirty years bearing the same subliminal sigil, the whole kingdom a hostage humming a tune it couldn't hear. If Sable executed Brakka here in the snow, they would be enforcing a law written by the thing in the throne room—or inventing their own, which amounted to the same arrogance with a different signature. Either way, they would be becoming a small crown themselves: a power that decided whose life balanced whose.

"You knew the risk when you took us through," Sable said. "So did I. That's the difference between us and them—we choose our prices. They assign them."

"Sable—" Brakka's voice broke. "My girls—"

"Are safe. Corvess keeps her word; I've read enough of her kind to know it. Your daughters go home because you kept your end, and you keep your life because I keep mine." Sable stepped closer, close enough to see the frost in his eyelashes, the terror and the shame tangled together like rope. "But hear me, Brakka of Graywater. Mercy isn't the same as forgetting. If your path crosses ours again carrying their colors, I won't be the one who decides. Do you understand? Next time it'll be the storm, and the storm doesn't weigh anything."

Brakka nodded. Then, awkwardly, hugely, he knelt in the snow—not to Sable, Sable realized, but to Wren, bowing his head the way fishermen bowed to an angry sea. "Little one," he said hoarsely. "Forgive an old ferryman. I carried you across the river and I sold the road after. That's not how a ferryman dies. That's not how anyone should be remembered."

Wren studied him. Then the child reached out and touched his bowed head, and static jumped—gentle, deliberate—and something passed between them that Sable couldn't read. When Brakka rose, his face was wet, and he looked lighter, as though a debt had been transferred somewhere he couldn't follow.

"Go home to your girls," Sable said. "Tell them stories about the mountains. Leave us out of them."

He went. They watched until the dark took him, and Sable found that the anger had drained out with the watching, leaving something quieter and harder underneath—not forgiveness, not yet, maybe not ever, but the refusal to carry his weight up the mountain with them. They had enough weight.

* * *

They climbed through the night. Hume rode with Liss wrapped in his coat, talking to her in a low steady stream—nonsense, mostly, army jokes and complaints about the cold—because he'd learned somewhere that wounded people stayed on the far side of death better if someone kept talking to them. Sable led, Wren against their back, following Odile's map by moonlight and storm-sense both.

Near dawn they halted in a sheltered fold below the pass, and Sable changed Liss's dressing. The wound looked angrier than Sable liked, the flesh around it hot.

"Fever by tomorrow," Sable murmured.

"Then we'd better reach your archivist by tomorrow." Liss managed a ghost of her usual smirk. "Besides—I figured something out. While I was lying in the snow getting shot." She fumbled one-handed at her breast pocket and drew out a single page, protected there by chance or instinct: the salvaged fragment from Odile's burned archive, the one describing a king whose memories were eaten. Blood had spattered across it, and where the blood had dried, the old binding-script beneath had darkened, risen, become visible—as if the page had needed feeding to confess.

"What am I looking at?" Sable asked.

"The fragment's a copy. Odile said so herself—she copied it from an older text." Liss's eyes were fever-bright and absolutely certain. "Look at the margins. Under the blood. Those aren't scribbles, Sable. They're corrections. Whoever copied this was comparing it against the original—and the original says the unbinding doesn't take one vessel." Her hand trembled, and not from the cold. "It takes two. A vessel to hold the memories, and a door to open the way. The copyist crossed that part out. Someone, a thousand years ago, decided the world shouldn't know it takes a child."

Behind Sable, very softly, Wren began to hum.

The binding-script, in a lullaby's rhythm, rising into the freezing air—and every hair on Sable's arms stood up, because the child was humming the exact passage Liss had uncovered, note for note, as if remembering it.

"We have to get to Odile," Sable said. "Now. Before they regroup. Before the bloodhound picks up our trail again."

Above the pass, the eastern sky had gone the color of a healing bruise, and somewhere beyond it, in a vault beneath the ice, an old woman was waiting—with answers, or with the last piece of a trap a thousand years in the setting.
