## 14. Corvess Defects

The order came at dawn, written in the Crown's own hand and sealed with wax the color of dried blood.

*By decree of the Throne: the village of Ashenmere harbors sigil-contaminated families. Purge it. Leave nothing that can remember.*

Corvess read it three times by lamplight in her tent, while outside her warden company broke camp in the gray half-light of the high valley. The paper was fine stock, heavy as a funeral shroud. The seal was genuine. That was the worst part—there was no forgery to blame, no clerk's error to hide behind. Somewhere in the capital, something wearing the king's authority had dipped its pen and written *burn children*, and the machinery of the realm had carried the words to her tent without a single hand trembling along the way.

She had filed away contradictions before. The order to destroy the vessel rather than capture it. The requisition ledgers that never quite matched the wardens' actual deployments. The way her superiors' eyes went flat and distant when she asked who, precisely, was *contaminated*, and by what, and how anyone could tell. She kept these things folded small in the back of her mind like contraband letters, and every morning she told herself she would examine them later, when there was time.

There was no more time. Ashenmere was two hours downstream, forty households, a mill, a shrine with a cracked bell. And her lieutenant, Dain Ferro, was already standing at the tent flap with the kindling question on his face.

"Captain? The men are asking about the objective."

"Tell them we move in twenty minutes," Corvess said.

Ferro saluted. He was thirty-one years old, ambitious, devout in the way the Crown encouraged—devotion to the throne rather than any god, because gods could be argued with and thrones could not. He had been her second for four years. He had once carried a wounded recruit eleven miles through snow on his back. Corvess looked at him and thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *he will do it. He will burn them all and sleep well after, and he will be promoted for it, and I will have taught him how.*

"Captain?" he said again. "Is there a problem with the orders?"

"No," Corvess said. "No problem at all."

Her voice did not shake. She noted that too, filed it away. Twenty years in the wardens had built her into someone whose voice never shook, and she was only now beginning to understand what else that construction had been built to hold down.

* * *

They marched down the valley in column, sixty wardens in black-lacquered half-plate, breath steaming, boots crunching frost. The Thornfells rose on either side like the shoulders of something sleeping. Corvess rode at the head and did not look at the mountains, because she knew what was in the mountains now—the smuggler Marchetti, alive; the vessel, alive; the whole rotten scaffolding of her last six months of orders creaking under the weight of questions she had stopped asking out loud.

Ashenmere appeared around a bend of the river road: smoke from chimneys, honest smoke, breakfast smoke. A dog barked somewhere. A woman was hanging washing on lines strung between two houses, white sheets snapping in the cold wind, and Corvess thought absurdly that those sheets would be the first thing to catch.

"Halt the column," she said.

Ferro rode up beside her. "Captain?"

"I want the village elders brought to me before anything else. Names, household rolls, evidence of contamination. We don't purge on suspicion alone—that's not doctrine, that's panic."

"It's what the decree says." Ferro's tone was careful, the tone of a man handling something explosive. "Purge it. Leave nothing that can remember."

"The decree also says the Crown protects the realm," Corvess said. "I've read both halves. Bring me the elders."

He hesitated. She watched him weigh it—her rank against his certainty—and saw him decide, wrongly, that this was a test of her nerve rather than his obedience. He wheeled his horse and went. Behind her, the column waited in the road, sixty faces watching their captain argue with a piece of paper, and Corvess understood that whatever happened in the next hour, they would all remember this morning for the rest of their lives.

The elders came: a miller with flour in the creases of his knuckles, a grandmother leaning on a hoe, a shrine-keeper still holding the broom he'd been sweeping the steps with. They stood in the road in front of sixty armed wardens and did not run, which took a courage Corvess doubted she could have matched.

"Are there sigil-touched among you?" she asked. "Families who've taken in strangers this season? Children born strange?"

The grandmother spoke first, no fear in her at all, just the flat patience of a woman who had buried a husband and two sons and outlived every argument winter had ever started. "We took in no one. But there's a family up past the mill—the Aldous girl's boy draws marks in the dirt sometimes. Marks nobody taught him. The priest from Graywater called it contamination and wanted him drowned, and we called it a child drawing in dirt, and we were right, and here you are anyway."

"How old is the boy?"

"Six."

Sixty wardens. Forty households. One six-year-old who drew in dirt. Corvess looked at the decree in her saddle case and felt the paper's weight change—not heavy now but hot, like a coal passed hand to hand, something that would burn whoever held it longest.

"Captain," Ferro said quietly, at her shoulder. "The decree doesn't ask for the boy alone. It says purge. Contamination spreads through association. That's standard doctrine."

"I know what standard doctrine is, Lieutenant. I helped write two of its revisions."

"Then you know better than I do why we're here." There it was again, that careful tone, and beneath it something harder. "With respect, Captain—you've been different since Graywater. Since the smuggler got away. The men notice. I notice. If there's a problem with your commitment to the mission, the proper procedure is to report it, not to stall in the field."

Corvess turned in the saddle and looked at him fully. "And if the mission is wrong?"

"Then it isn't ours to judge." He said it without heat, almost gently, the way you'd correct a child reaching for a knife. "That's the entire point of the Crown, Captain. Somebody has to decide so the rest of us don't have to carry it."

"Somebody decided," Corvess said. "I read what they decided. Sixty of us, one village, one boy who draws in dirt. You want me to give that order so you don't have to carry it. Fine. Then carry this instead—" she raised her voice so the whole column heard it, "—wardens! Form ranks facing the village. No torch is lit until I say the word. No torch is lit until every one of you has looked at those washing lines and that mill and that woman's face, and told yourself honestly that this is what you swore to protect."

Silence, except the wind snapping the sheets.

It lasted perhaps ten heartbeats. Then Ferro dismounted, drew his sidearm—not the sword, the short truncheon-rod wardens used for crowd discipline—and struck the nearest brazier stand, tipping coals onto dry roadside grass. Flame jumped up yellow and eager.

"By authority vested in me as acting field commander," Ferro announced, "in the event of a commanding officer's incapacity or dereliction—"

"You'll need more than a speech," Corvess said, and drew her sword.

* * *

What followed was not, strictly speaking, a fight. Corvess had spent twenty years learning exactly where authority lived—in the voice, in the bearing, in the assumption that everyone else would obey—and she spent all of it in ninety seconds. She put herself between the fire and the village. She named names. "Vessen, you carried Tam Orla off the ice at Redford—would you burn her mother's house? Cade, your own sister lives in a river town; if the Crown wrote her name on a list tomorrow, is that doctrine too?" She did not threaten. She did not plead. She simply stood in the road with her sword drawn and made each of them look at the arithmetic, and arithmetic, unlike orders, could not be un-read.

Eleven men lowered their weapons. Nine wavered. The rest stayed with Ferro—but stayed badly, shifting, not meeting her eyes, and she knew that most of them would find reasons to be slow.

Ferro came at her with the rod and then the sword, and he was good—he'd learned from her—but she had twenty years on him and grief on her side besides, and grief, properly channeled, is just precision with the temperature turned up. She beat his blade wide, hooked his ankle, put him in the frozen mud with her knee on his chest and her point resting in the hinge of his jaw, right below the ear, where a quarter inch of steel ends everything.

"Yield your command," she said. "Formally. In front of witnesses."

"You're a traitor," he said. His eyes were wet—with rage, she thought, or maybe with relief. Maybe some part of him had been waiting months for someone to make disobedience possible. "They'll hunt you. There's nowhere in the nine provinces—"

"They'll hunt me either way the moment I refuse an order. The only question left is what I am when they start." She pressed the point just enough to dimple skin. "Say it, Dain. Relinquish command. Live. Go home. Grow old. Forget my name."

For a long moment he lay there breathing hard, frost and mud on his face, and she saw the war behind his eyes—the Crown's voice saying *somebody decides so you don't have to carry it*, and underneath it, faint but persistent, the memory of a wounded recruit on his back in the snow, eleven miles, because carrying people was the whole reason he'd ever put on the black.

"I relinquish command," he said, and the words came out of him like a splinter leaving flesh. "Field command passes to—"

"To no one," Corvess said, standing, sheathing her sword. "To Wardens Vessen and Cade jointly, with one order: march back to Graywater garrison, report the decree verbatim, and let the garrison commander choke on it in writing. If the Crown wants Ashenmere burned, let it send someone willing to sign his own name to it."

She turned to the elders. The grandmother was watching her with an expression Corvess couldn't parse—something between gratitude and the look you give weather.

"You should go," Corvess told her. "Not today, not in panic. But within the month. Take the boy somewhere the lists haven't reached. There are places in the high valleys—" she stopped herself. She had been hunting people in the high valleys all autumn. "There are places," she finished.

"And you, Captain?" the miller asked. "Where will you go?"

Corvess looked north, up the valley, toward the passes, toward a smuggler she'd chased across three provinces and a child made of a language older than walls.

"Toward the people I was ordered to kill," she said. "Apparently I'm contaminated too."

* * *

She rode out of Ashenmere with four wardens who chose to follow her—Vessen, Cade, a scarred tracker named Pell, and a young signal-mage called Ives who could scry a map's wards and, more usefully, could tell her exactly which of the documents in her saddle case were trapped. Because that was the other thing. That was the thing she'd realized at midnight over the decree, the contradiction she could finally unfold all the way:

She had maps. Twenty years of maps. Garrison schematics, patrol rotations, supply routes—and once, years ago, as a reward for service she still didn't understand the purpose of, a full survey of the palace interior. Not the public floors. The warded interior. The vault levels beneath the throne. She had carried that document through four postings and never once wondered why a warden captain needed to know that the palace's third sublevel had seven sealed doors and a cold shaft that appeared on no architectural record.

Now she wondered. Now she thought about a king thirty years beloved and thirty years vacant, about edicts written in blood-colored wax, about orders that destroyed evidence instead of gathering it, and the whole shape of it assembled itself in her mind with the terrible click of a lock turning.

The Crown wasn't hunting the vessel to contain a threat. It was hunting the vessel to prevent a cure.

"Captain," Ives said, riding close as they climbed into the passes. "The decree parchment. It's warded. Everything from the palace is—it's why we can't copy official documents. But I've been reading the weave, and—" the young mage hesitated, the way people hesitate before saying something that can't be unsaid. "There's a subliminal binding in the ink. Every decree carries it. It's meant to make obedience feel like relief. Like putting down a weight."

"Does it work?"

"On most people. Yes."

"And on you?"

Ives looked at the mountains, at the road, at the small figure of Corvess riding ahead of four defectors toward certain designation as traitors. "I think," Ives said slowly, "it works until you actually read the words. Until you make yourself see what you're agreeing to. After that it just feels like someone pulling on a door that's already locked."

Corvess nodded. Ahead, the pass narrowed, and the wind coming down from the high snowfields smelled of storm—of ozone and rain that hadn't fallen yet. Somewhere in those heights was a cabin, a smuggler, a child. Somewhere behind her was a garrison that would report her name by nightfall, and hunters by week's end, and her own face on the lists she had spent her life enforcing.

She found, examining herself the way she'd examine a battlefield, that she felt lighter than she had in years. Not happy. Lighter. The weight Ives described, the one she'd been carrying folded small in the back of her mind for months—all those contradictions, all those orders she'd swallowed without tasting—was gone, because she had finally set it down on the far side of a line, and the line was behind her.

"Pell," she said. "You tracked the Marchetti smuggler through the Thornfells last spring. Can you find them again?"

The tracker grinned, gap-toothed and unrepentant. "Captain, I can find anyone in these mountains. Question is whether they'll thank us for it."

"They won't," Corvess said. "But they need what I'm carrying more than they need to shoot me." She touched the saddle case with its twenty years of maps, its survey of the vault beneath the throne, its seven sealed doors drawn in a dead surveyor's careful hand. "And I need what they know. So we ride carefully, we announce ourselves loudly, and if the storm-caller asks why a warden captain just burned her own career in a village road—"

She paused. Below them, far behind, a thread of smoke was rising from the direction of Graywater garrison, where Vessen and Cade would be delivering her report, where her name was even now being entered into the ledgers of the damned.

"—tell them the truth," she said. "Tell them I read the decree. All the words. And once you've truly read it, there's no unreading it—and I'd rather hang as myself than serve as something hollow wearing my name."

She spurred her horse into the pass, into the smell of coming storm, and the five of them climbed together toward the people they had been sent to destroy—carrying, in wax-sealed cases and guilty hearts, everything the Crown most feared arriving.
