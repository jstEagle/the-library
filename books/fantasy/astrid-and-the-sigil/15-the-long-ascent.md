## 15. The Long Ascent

### Sable

The pass above Thornhollow had no name on any map Sable had ever seen, which was precisely why they had chosen it. Maps were how the Crown thought. Every road, every bridge, every ferry crossing was a sentence in a language of control, and for thirty years that language had been written in more than ink. So they climbed where nothing was written.

The air up here had teeth. It came off the icefields in long, sawing gusts that found every gap in wool and leather, and Sable walked at the front of the line reading it the way Odile read her salvaged fragments—not word by word but in the grammar underneath. A pressure drop in the west, sour and metallic: snow within the day, heavy. A high thin whine through the notch ahead: wind funneling, which meant the notch was narrow, which meant cover from pursuit but no room to fight. And beneath it all, faint as a scar under paint, something else—a wrongness in the weather's handwriting, as if someone were editing the sky behind them.

Sable stopped and let the caravan bunch up. Six figures and two mules on a shelf of gray stone with a drop on their left that went down far enough to make silence feel appropriate.

"Problem?" Hume asked. The sergeant had gone quiet since Thornhollow, quieter even than his usual, and he carried his pack like a man who expected to set it down permanently soon.

"Maybe." Sable turned a slow circle, eyes half-closed, letting the storm-sense spread out of them like spilled water. *Suppressed*, they reminded themselves. *Reading only. You don't call anything. You just listen.* But listening had its own cost; the storm always listened back, and somewhere in its vast indifferent attention Sable could feel a small hot point of focus, deliberate and directional, like an eye.

"There's a mage on our trail," they said. "A good one. They're not following our tracks—they're following *us*. Reading what we do to the air."

Liss pulled her collar higher. "So we do less."

"You can't do less than existing." Sable opened their eyes. "But we can be boring. No fires above the treeline. No singing—" a glance at Wren, who had the grace to look abashed, "—no humming, no whistling, no magic of any kind, including the kind you don't know you're doing. We move until dark, then we stop moving. Understood?"

Wren nodded. The child walked barefoot still, even now, even here; boots lasted a day on those feet before the laces rotted or the soles split along seams that hadn't existed when Liss bought them. Corvess had stopped commenting on it around the third morning. Some things you filed away with the other contradictions.

They climbed. The notch narrowed as promised, walls rising on both sides until the sky was a bright ragged ribbon overhead and the mules had to be led single file, breath steaming in ropes. Sable counted paces without meaning to—an old habit from the years when counting had been the only thing between them and thinking—and at four hundred they heard it.

Not behind them. Ahead.

A sound like a bell struck underwater, rolling down the notch, and every hair on Sable's arms stood up beneath three layers of wool. They knew that sound. Everyone who had ever been hunted by the Crown's wardens knew that sound. A bloodhound-mage casting a seeking-cadence, and casting it *ahead* of them, which meant either there were two hunters or—

"They've cut the pass," Sable said. "Down. Everybody down."

### Liss

Liss had survived eleven years as a forger in the border towns by cultivating one talent above all others: knowing when a document was about to become worthless. A writ the moment the seal changed. A passport the moment the border moved. An identity the moment someone said your name too loudly in the wrong tavern.

She felt that same cold certainty settle over her now, crouched behind a fallen slab of granite while the underwater bell rolled through the notch again, closer, patient. Somewhere above them, a warden bloodhound was tasting the air their little company had disturbed, and every hour of climbing, every careful boring fireless mile, was becoming worthless by the minute.

Beside her, Brakka checked the mules' harness with hands that didn't quite tremble. Since Thornhollow the ferryman had been trying very hard to be useful, the way guilty men do—volunteering for watches, carrying double loads, laughing a beat too fast at jokes. Liss knew what he'd done. She suspected everyone knew what he'd done, and that the only reason Brakka still drew breath was that Sable had looked at him across the ashes of their burned supplies and said, in a voice like a door closing, *"Go home to your family, Brakka. That's the only reason you're walking away."*

He hadn't gone home. He'd followed them into the mountains like a dog that had bitten its master and couldn't decide whether to flee or grovel.

"He knows we're here," Brakka whispered. "The hound-mage. He'll have our scent written down already. There's wardens' waystations on this stretch—I served a cargo run through here once, there's a station at the—"

"If you say another word about waystations," Liss said sweetly, "I will forge documents making you legally a mule, and Hume can lead you by the bridle."

Despite everything, Brakka almost smiled. Then the bell sounded a third time, and it did not roll past them down the notch. It stopped. Directly overhead.

Snow sifted down from the rim, fine as flour. Against the white glare at the top of the wall, a silhouette: robed, hooded, arms raised in the posture of a caster holding a note. Even at that distance Liss could see the frost crawling outward from the figure's feet in a perfect circle, a signature written on the mountain itself.

"Hume," she breathed. "Corvess. Company."

What happened next happened fast and mostly sideways. Corvess came up the rocks like the professional she'd spent fifteen years being, crossbow already shouldered, and fired one bolt that shattered against a shield of hardened air with a sound like a cracked bell. The bloodhound-mage looked down at them—all of them, cataloguing, memorizing—and Liss watched Corvess's face do something complicated, because she recognized the silhouette. Everyone in the warden service knew Mage-Seeker Vell by reputation. Corvess had probably drunk with him.

Vell raised one hand, almost courteously, and spoke a word that made the whole notch ring.

Then the sky fell.

Not rain. Not the clean whiteout Sable had promised was coming. This was a *curtain*—snow driven sideways in a solid roaring wall, temperature dropping so fast the mules screamed, visibility collapsing from fifty yards to five to none. A blizzard called down deliberately, aimed, thrown like a net over six fugitives and two animals on a ledge with a cliff on one side.

"We can't stay here!" Hume bellowed, hauling a mule's head away from the edge. "We'll freeze before midnight!"

"And if we run," Corvess shouted back, "we run blind off a mountain! He's herding us—he wants us moving, panicked, scattered!"

Through the white roar, Sable's voice cut low and level, the voice of someone who had once guided caravans through worse than this and sworn never to do it again. "There's a cave. Two hundred yards back, south side, below the shelf—I felt it in the stone this morning, an echo-hollow. We make for it on rope. Everyone tied to everyone. Nobody lets go of anybody."

Liss got her wrists bound to Brakka's and hated every second of it, and then the world became nothing but white violence and the pull of the rope and the desperate animal arithmetic of putting one foot in front of the other foot in front of the other foot, and somewhere in the middle of it she lost all track of time, direction, and dignity, and then there was rock overhead instead of sky, and silence except for wind moaning at the cave mouth like something locked outside.

They were alive. All six, both mules, shivering, iced to the eyebrows, alive.

Outside, faint through the storm, the underwater bell rang once more—satisfied, unhurried—and began to circle.

### Hume

Hume had frozen before. Properly frozen, the palace-guard winter exercise at Cold Spur, the year he was nineteen, when three recruits had lost fingers and one had lost his nerve and deserted, and the sergeant had said: *the cold doesn't kill you, son, it just asks you questions until you answer wrong.* He'd remembered that all his life, and he remembered it now, sitting against the cave wall with his knees drawn up, feeling the questions start.

*Why are you here?* asked the cold. *You deserted once already. You could have stayed deserted.*

Because I saw the king, he answered, in the privacy of his own skull. Because I stood post outside the solar at midwinter and His Radiance smiled at me through the doorway and there was nobody home behind the smile. Because whatever wears him has been wearing him for thirty years and somebody has to walk back through the gates I ran from.

*And you think six ragged souls and a mute child will manage what armies haven't?*

No, Hume admitted. But armies serve the Crown. That's rather the problem.

Across the cave, Corvess was rationing the cold-rations with a quartermaster's precision, and Brakka was doing something useless to the mules, and Liss was asleep sitting up, which took a special kind of talent, and Sable sat nearest the entrance with their eyes closed, listening to the storm the way other men listened to conversation.

And Wren—

Wren was worried. Hume could see it in the set of the small shoulders, the way the child kept looking from face to face in the failing light, cataloguing them the way the bloodhound had catalogued them from the ridge. Counting breaths. Counting shivers. The child's lips moved constantly, shaping silent syllables in that dead pre-dynastic script, and Hume had learned enough in the past weeks to know that wasn't prayer. It was maintenance. Wren read people the way Sable read weather.

The cold asked its next question: *How long before the little ones starts answering wrong?*

Because that was the thing none of them said aloud. Wren didn't shiver properly. Wren's body burned warm—had burned warm since the night it appeared barefoot in a blizzard—but warmth was fuel, and fuel ran out, and the child had been spending itself for days: mending axles, smoothing tempers, keeping Hume's nightmares at bay without ever being asked. Hume had slept dreamless for a week straight and had been too grateful to wonder what it cost.

"Wren." He kept his voice gentle. "Come sit by me, little one. Share the wall."

Wren crossed the cave and settled against his side, light as a bird, and Hume wrapped his cloak around them both and pretended not to notice that the child was humming.

It started so quietly he almost missed it beneath the wind. A thread of melody, wordless, rising and falling in patterns that repeated without ever exactly repeating—the binding-script, sung instead of spoken, worn soft as a lullaby. Hume knew he should tell them to stop. Sable's rule. No magic, nothing the hound could taste.

But the cold in Hume's bones loosened. The questions stopped. Around the cave, one by one, the others stopped shivering—not warmed, exactly, but *held*, the way a hand holds a candleflame in wind. Even the mules went quiet. Even the storm at the mouth of the cave seemed to lean in and listen, its roar flattening into something almost like respect.

Sable opened their eyes.

For a long moment the exile and the child regarded each other across the fireless dark, and Hume saw Sable weigh the rule against the result, and saw the rule lose.

"Keep humming," Sable said softly. "Quietly. If the hound hears, let him hear a lullaby."

Wren hummed on. And Hume, sergeant of a broken guard, deserter twice over, slept without dreams in the arms of a living spell, while outside the whiteout sang its one long note over the roof of the world.

### Corvess

Corvess took first watch because she didn't trust herself to sleep.

She sat near the entrance with her back to the rock and her crossbow across her knees and watched the whiteout grind past the cave mouth, and she made herself think about Mage-Seeker Vell, because thinking about Vell was safer than thinking about the rest.

She had known him twelve years. He'd taught her the counter-signs for hostile castings; she'd taught him how garrison soldiers actually talked when officers weren't listening. He'd named his daughter after her, once, drunk, and laughed about it for a month. And today he had thrown a killing blizzard over her and four civilians and a child, not to capture—to *herd*, toward the waystation at Gray Crag where the burn order would be waiting, because the orders said the vessel was to be destroyed, not taken, and Vell followed orders the way glaciers follow gravity.

*Destroyed.* She turned the word over again, looking for the version of it that made sense. Contaminated, the edicts said. Unbound vessel, sigil-bearing, existential threat to the realm. She had read the dispatches herself, had signed the routing slips, had never once in fifteen years asked what happened after the routing slips. Institutions were built so that no one had to ask. That was what institutions were *for*.

Then she'd met the vessel. The vessel was eight years old and hummed lullabies in a dead language and had mended Sergeant Hume's sleep without being asked, and Corvess had killed men for less cause than that child had shown kindness.

Behind her, the humming threaded on, soft as breath. Brakka snored. Liss muttered forgery numbers in her sleep. Sable sat motionless, and Corvess caught them watching her across the dark.

"You're going to ask me," Sable said quietly, "whether Vell will keep circling or go for reinforcements."

"I'm going to ask you why you let me keep my crossbow."

"Because you haven't used it on us. Yet." Sable's voice held no accusation, which was somehow worse. "And because when it comes time to walk into the capital, I'll need someone who knows the wards from the inside. Someone the wards remember fondly."

"That's not trust. That's inventory."

"It's both. Most things are." Sable leaned their head back against the stone. "Sleep, Captain. The child's holding the cold off, but the child can't hold your conscience off. That part you have to carry yourself."

Corvess looked out at the storm. Somewhere in that white violence, Vell was circling, patient as winter, writing their names in his seeking-cadence for whoever came next. She thought about her lieutenant Dain, who would follow any order with a smile. She thought about the village at Lowmark, on the list, sigil-contaminated, scheduled.

"When we reach the capital," she heard herself say, "there's a gate warden named Ferro, third watch, east post. He owes me his life. He can get us through the festival crowds unsearched." She paused. "Consider it inventory."

Above the wind, very faint, Wren's lullaby changed key—and Corvess, who had spent fifteen years learning to hear what official words were actually saying, heard beneath the melody a second pattern, urgent and thin, like a warning stitched into a song.

The child had stopped humming to them.

The child was humming *at the storm*.

### Wren

*Cold is a hunger,* the storm said, in the old tongue, in the true tongue, the tongue Wren had been made of before anyone gave them a name. *Cold is patient. Cold has been sent.*

*I know,* Wren hummed back. *I know you were sent. I'm sorry.*

*The one who rides the wind tastes of iron and orders. He will circle until the white dies, and then he will see the smoke of nothing, because you have made no smoke, and then he will call the others, and the others will come with fire.*

Wren curled smaller inside Hume's cloak and kept the melody moving, feeding it outward, wrapping each sleeping shape in a loop of the script the way a mother wraps a child in a blanket. *How long does the white last?*

*Until dawn. Perhaps past dawn. The iron one is strong but the mountain is stronger, and the mountain owes me nothing and you nothing, and it will do what it likes.* A pause, vast and glacial. *Little remainder. Little left-over piece. Why do you protect them? They will forget you. They forget everything. That is what they are.*

Wren thought about Sable's eyes closed, listening. About Hume's arm around them, heavy and warm and trusting. About Liss's sleeping mutter, Brakka's shame, Corvess's straight back and crooked heart.

*That's why,* Wren hummed. *They forget. So someone has to remember.*

The storm considered this for a long time, the way mountains consider things.

*When the white dies,* it said at last, *the iron one will not be alone. He has called down the pass for his brothers. Three days, if the weather serves you. Less if it doesn't. And little remainder—* the wind dropped to almost nothing, almost tender— *they are not coming to take you anymore. The order has changed. I tasted it in him. They are coming to unmake you, and unmaking leaves nothing to carry, and nothing to remember.*

Wren kept humming. Inside the cloak, Hume's heart beat slow and steady, and the others breathed, and the lullaby held them all in its loops, and Wren sang the warning into the script itself, folding it into the melody where only one person in the world might someday read it:

*Three days. They're coming to erase me. Wake up. Please wake up.*

And far across the cave, Sable's eyes opened in the dark.
