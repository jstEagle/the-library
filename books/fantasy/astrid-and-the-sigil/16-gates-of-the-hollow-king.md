## 16. Gates of the Hollow King

The capital announced itself three days before they reached it.

Sable felt it first in their teeth—a low, grinding hum beneath the road, like a millstone turning somewhere far off. Then Wren felt it too. The child had been riding on Hume's shoulders for an hour when they suddenly went rigid, small fingers tightening in his hair, and said, in that flat pre-dynastic cadence that was slowly giving way to common speech: *"The city is singing."*

"It's the festival," Liss said from the wagon bench, not looking up from her ledger of forged lives. "Coronation Day. Thirty years since His Radiance took the throne. They've been building to it for a month—lanterns, processions, the whole gilded circus."

"That's not what I mean," Wren said.

Sable reined in beside the wagon and let themselves listen. Beneath the ordinary noise of the world—the wind off the plains, the creak of harness leather, Brakka's mule complaining about its load—there it was: a pulse, vast and slow and patient, radiating out of the capital like heat off a forge-stone. Not sound exactly. Pressure. The same pressure Sable had felt once before, years ago, standing at the edge of a storm cell so large it had its own weather, its own gravity, its own intentions.

Something in that city was breathing. And everything within fifty miles breathed with it.

"Everyone's feeling it," Corvess said quietly. She rode at the rear, as she always did, positioned where she could watch the road behind them. Old habits. "They call it the King's Blessing. Pilgrims come from the coast provinces just to stand inside it. They say it feels like being loved."

"Do you feel loved?" Hume asked her.

Corvess was silent for a long moment. "I feel *held*," she said finally. "There's a difference."

*

They came down off the last ridge at dusk on the eve of the anniversary, and the capital opened before them like a wound full of light.

Sable had seen cities before. They had grown up in one, before the mountains, before the storm, before the long erasure of themselves. But they had never seen anything like this. The walls rose white in the failing light, and every stone of them was lit from within—not torchlight, not lamplight, but a soft golden glow that seemed to seep out of the masonry itself, as if the whole city were a lantern with a candle burning at its heart. Above the walls, spires climbed into the dark, hung with banners of silver and blue, and above them all, on the central mount, the palace blazed like a second sun refusing to set.

And over everything, that hum. That patient, tidal pull.

"The wards," Corvess murmured, drawing up beside Sable. Her face, half-lit by the distant glow, looked older than it had a month ago. "Every gate, every wall, every street-corner shrine. I helped maintain the outer ring myself, my first year in the wardens. We were told we were protecting the city." She paused. "Now I look at it and I think: this isn't a shield. It's a net."

"Can you get us through it?"

"I can get four of us through it," she said. "Five, if nobody breathes wrong."

Liss's answer to the problem of five fugitives lay folded in oilcloth inside the false bottom of the wagon: five sets of papers, five histories, five small paper lives. A wool merchant from the eastern valleys and his wife. Their factor. Two hired guards, discharged veterans with honest faces and unremarkable records. Liss had spent nine days on them, working by candlelight, and when she'd finished she'd held the merchant's travel writ up to the light and said, with something like grief, "He's more real than I am."

Now she distributed the documents in the shadow of a roadside shrine, checking each face against each name one last time.

"You're Aldous Vey," she told Sable. "Wool. Boring wool. You have opinions about dye lots. If anyone asks, complain about the roads—the roads are always a safe complaint." She pressed the writ into Sable's hand and held on a moment longer than necessary. "Aldous has a wife. Try to remember you're fond of me."

"I'm fond of you," Sable said. "That part won't need forging."

Liss snorted, but her ears went pink, and she turned away to fuss with the mule's harness that did not need fussing with.

Brakka would not be coming. This had been decided without ceremony, weeks after Thornhollow, when he'd knelt in the mud outside a waystation and asked—voice cracking—for the chance to carry supplies as far as Graywater and no further, because his family lived two days' ride from the capital and if the Crown ever learned his face among theirs, his children would pay for it. Sable had looked at him a long time. Then they'd shaken his hand and told him the truth: that forgiveness wasn't theirs to give or withhold, only to practice. He'd wept into his beard and loaded the mules in silence, and at the Graywater fork he'd stood watching them go until the road bent him out of sight.

Hume would be coming, though nothing on earth could have stopped him. He wore his old sergeant's bearing like armor polished for a final battle. "Thirty years I've been ashamed," was all he'd said, when Sable tried once to talk him out of it. "Tomorrow I get to spend it. That's not a cost. That's a purchase."

And Wren—

Wren sat between Liss and Sable on the wagon bench as the queue for the Festival Gate crawled forward, wearing a new coat Liss had bought in a market town, hair trimmed, face scrubbed, looking for all the world like a merchant's child bored past endurance. Only their eyes moved constantly, reading the city the way Sable read weather. Cataloguing. Listening.

*"It's louder here,"* they said softly, in the old tongue, then caught themselves and repeated it in words anyone might overhear: "It's loud here."

"What is?" Liss asked, smiling brilliantly at a passing guard.

Wren pressed a small hand flat against their own chest. "Everything. It's all... leaning. Like grass under wind. The whole city leans toward the palace, even asleep. Even the stones."

Sable felt it too, and said nothing, because there was nothing true that could be said aloud in a queue full of pilgrims singing coronation hymns.

*

The Festival Gate took an hour.

They passed through in a river of celebrants—farm families in their best clothes, coastal pilgrims with shells braided into their hair, children carrying paper lanterns on sticks, everyone flushed and laughing and half-deafened by their own joy. The guards at the gate barely looked at faces; they looked at papers, and Liss's papers were beautiful. One of them glanced at Aldous Vey's wool merchant's writ, stamped it without interest, waved the wagon through with a jerk of his thumb, and returned to arguing with his fellows about which taverns would still have wine by midnight.

Inside the walls, the city swallowed them whole.

Sable had expected splendor. What they hadn't expected was the *uniformity* of it. Every street was decorated identically—silver bunting, blue ribbons, lanterns spaced with mathematical precision. Every shrine held the same arrangement of candles. Every shop window displayed the king's profile in some form: painted, carved, stitched, cast in cheap tin for pennies. And everywhere, everywhere, that gentle golden light, pouring from the stones themselves, washing all colors toward warmth, smoothing every edge.

"It's beautiful," Liss whispered, and there was real wonder in it, and underneath the wonder, horror. "Sable. Look at the people. Look at their *faces*."

Sable looked. The crowd flowed around them, and every face wore the same expression—not happiness exactly, not the ragged varied thing that happiness actually was, but something smoother. Contentment like still water. A woman selling roasted chestnuts smiled at a customer with lips curved in perfect serenity while her eyes tracked nothing at all. Two old men argued cheerfully about a card game, and their argument had the texture of a performance, warm and weightless, leaving no marks.

"They're not drugged," Corvess said quietly. She had gone pale. "I'd know. I've seen warden pacification work. This is—" She stopped. Started again. "This is voluntary. They love this. It's the Blessing. Everyone inside the wards feels the king's contentment, and the king's contentment is—" she searched for the word and found none adequate, "—*enormous*. It fills them up. Why would anyone fight it? From the inside, there's nothing to fight."

"And the ones who can't be filled?" Sable asked.

Corvess didn't answer. She didn't have to. Both of them had seen the warden reports, back when Corvess had still been bringing them: the quiet lists of the resistant, the melancholic, the *unresponsive*, relocated to provincial postings, to hospitals, to places with high walls and no festivals.

Hume walked close to Wren and did not stop walking close to Wren, one hand hovering near the child's shoulder the entire way, as if the crowd might reach out and take them.

*

They lodged at the Gilded Antler, an inn Corvess knew from her warden years—clean, expensive enough to be unremarkable, patronized by factors and merchants who wanted comfort without questions. Liss paid in coin whose provenance she declined to discuss. The innkeeper gave them rooms on the third floor facing the courtyard, away from the street, and told them the Procession of Radiance would pass the cathedral square at midday tomorrow, and that good positions went early, and that His Radiance was expected to appear on the balcony at dusk for the Lighting of the City, as he did every year.

"He's kind," the innkeeper added, warming to a favorite subject. "You'll see. Last winter my daughter took fever, and I wrote to the palace—not expecting anything, mind—and within a week there was a physician at my door, paid from the privy purse. He does that. He *cares*." The man's eyes shone with simple, uncomplicated devotion. "Best king in a hundred years, they say. Maybe the best ever."

When he'd gone, Hume sat down heavily on the end of the bed and put his face in his hands.

"I served that man," he said through his fingers. "Palace guard, six years. I've stood three feet from him. He remembered my name. He remembered my *wife's* name, asked after her shoulder, the one she hurt in a cart accident—he'd heard about it from someone, some file, some servant, and he asked, and he meant it, or something in him meant it, and I would have died for him that day and every day after." He lowered his hands. His eyes were wet and furious. "And the whole time he was empty. A lamp with the flame taken out, still glowing. Still *warm*. Gods, Sable, he was so warm. You wanted to stand near him forever. That's how it works, isn't it? That's why nobody's ever—"

"Yes," Sable said. "That's how it works."

Because Sable had read Odile's salvaged fragment until the words were burned into them: *the Crown feeds not on suffering but on love, for love is memory at its most concentrated.* A parasite that made itself beloved. A thief who stole the house by making the house adore being robbed.

Wren sat on the windowsill, knees drawn up, looking out at the blazing city, and said nothing at all.

*

Midday came, and the Procession of Radiance filled the cathedral square from wall to wall.

They stood packed among ten thousand celebrants, and Sable understood now why Corvess had insisted on this—why seeing the king mattered, why it couldn't be skipped or imagined or secondhand. Some things had to be witnessed to be believed, and some things had to be believed before they could be fought.

The bells began first—all of them, every tower in the city, a sound like the sky cracking open in joy. Then the procession: ranks of palace guard in silvered mail, the High Clergy in vestments of woven light, honor cohorts of wardens marching with ceremonial pikes, and down the center of it all, drawn by eight white horses that stepped as if time were optional, a carriage of glass and gold.

And in it, the king.

Sable had prepared themselves for a monster. They had spent weeks imagining the Crown's host as something to brace against—a tyrant's face, a predator's stillness, cruelty dressed in majesty. They had rehearsed their hatred the way a soldier rehearses a sword drill, so that it would come without thinking when it was needed.

None of the rehearsal helped.

The King of Vhalen was beautiful. That was the first thing, and it was unbearable. He was perhaps fifty-five, with silver threading dark hair, a face like something a sculptor would weep over—strong jaw, kind mouth, eyes the color of deep water. He wore white and gold, simply cut, without ostentation, and he stood in the open carriage and raised one hand to the crowd, and the crowd made a sound that Sable felt in their sternum, a sound like ten thousand hearts breaking open at once.

He loved them. That was the second unbearable thing. His smile was radiant and effortless and utterly genuine, and it swept across the square like sunlight across water, and wherever it touched, people wept with happiness. He turned his hand in a slow benediction. He mouthed words of blessing. He was, by any measure the world could apply, the finest king a nation had ever had.

And behind his eyes there was nothing.

Sable saw it because they had spent a lifetime reading what people hid, and because they were looking for it, and because at forty feet, with the sun full on his face, there was no missing it once seen: the king's gaze moved across the crowd and touched nothing. It was the gaze of a man looking out of a window at scenery he could not quite see. His smile was perfect because it had been worn so long it had become architecture. Somewhere behind those deep-water eyes, behind thirty years of radiant, beloved, immaculate performance, a man was locked in a room the size of his own skull, screaming, or worse—no longer screaming, no longer able to remember there had ever been anything to scream about.

The carriage rolled closer. Ten feet. Five.

For one moment—one terrible, suspended moment—the king's eyes passed over Sable.

And stopped.

Not recognition. Nothing so useful. But something flickered there, deep down, in whatever flooded ruin remained of the man: a hitch, a stutter, a single dropped stitch in thirty years of seamless weaving. His smile faltered by a degree invisible to anyone else in the square. His head turned a fraction, tracking the plain wool merchant with the forgettable face as the carriage carried him past, and his brow creased with an expression that had no business on a king's face on his holiest day—

It was grief. Raw, bewildered, bottomless grief, surfacing for less than a heartbeat like a drowning man's hand breaking water, and then the tide of the Crown closed over it and the smile resumed, radiant and vacant, and the carriage rolled on toward the cathedral, and the crowd roared its love, and Sable stood in the roaring heart of it with tears running down their face that they had no memory of starting.

"Sable." Liss's voice, very close, very quiet, her hand gripping their sleeve. "What happened? What did you see?"

"He saw me," Sable said. "Or—something in him did." They wiped their face with the back of their hand, roughly, like a man angry at weather. "Liss. He's still in there. Whatever the Crown is, whatever it's eaten, he's still in there, and he knows. Some buried piece of him *knows*, and it's been drowning alone for thirty years, waving at a country that worships the thing holding him under."

Around them the celebration swelled toward evening. Lanterns were being lit by the thousand. Somewhere ahead, on the palace balcony, the Hollow King would raise his hand at dusk and light the city, and the city would love him, and the Crown would drink, and grow, and wait patiently for the next reign, and the next, and the next.

Wren tugged Sable's sleeve. The child's face was grave and old and certain.

"Tonight," Wren said. "While the whole city is looking at him. That's when the wards are thinnest. That's when we go in."

Above the rooftops, the sun touched the western hills, and the first lantern of the Lighting of the City bloomed gold against the coming dark.
