## 22. Astrid and the Sigil

The woman who called herself Astrid woke before dawn, as she always did, though she could not have said why. Some habits outlive their reasons.

Wren was already awake—Wren was always already awake, sitting cross-legged on the flat rock above the camp with the frost steaming off their shoulders, watching the east go from ash to rose. Wren never seemed to feel the cold. The caravaneers had stopped remarking on it months ago; mountain folk learned quickly which questions were not worth asking.

"Storm coming," Wren said, without turning around. "Three days. Out of the northwest. It'll miss us if we take the low fork past Graywater."

Astrid climbed up beside them and looked where Wren looked, at a sky that showed nothing but clean winter blue. "You're sure?"

"You're sure," Wren said. "I'm just the one who says it out loud."

That was the arrangement between them, more or less. Astrid read the weather in her bones—in the ache behind her teeth, in the way her fingers itched when pressure dropped—and Wren translated it into words a caravan master would trust. She had tried once to explain what it felt like: that storms were legible, that a building thunderhead had grammar, that she could skim a front like a letter from an old friend whose face she'd forgotten. Wren had listened with that particular stillness of theirs and then said, *Yes. That's how I remember things I never saw.*

They made an odd pair and the road knew it. A tall woman with storm-grey eyes and no history anyone could find, and a sharp-eyed child of eleven or so who spoke like a grandmother and hummed tunes no fiddler could learn. They guided caravans through the Thornfells for coin, and they were good at it—uncannily good, luck-wrapped good, the kind of good that made superstitious drivers leave extra offerings at the roadside shrines. Not to any god in particular. Just, you know. In case.

* * *

The caravan they were walking now belonged to a wool merchant named Doss, a fat, cheerful man who paid well and talked endlessly, and by the third day he had worked his way around to the story everyone asked for eventually.

"They say there was a smuggler," he said, huffing up the switchback beside Astrid while Wren ranged ahead, kicking stones off the trail edge with great seriousness. "Years back. Storm-caller, some said. Hid out up here after doing something terrible—or something wonderful, depends who's telling it. And there was a child." He glanced sideways at Wren, then back, grinning. "And a king."

"There are always kings in stories," Astrid said.

"Not like this one. This king woke up, they say. After thirty years of sleeping with his eyes open. First thing he did was burn the warden rolls and open the archives. There's folk in the capital now reading laws out loud to see if any of them *hum*." Doss laughed at his own joke, then sobered. "My cousin swears he saw the king himself, at the anniversary festival last spring. Standing in the crowd like any man. Crying, my cousin says. Just standing there crying at a festival, happy as anything."

Ahead, Wren had gone very still.

Astrid felt it too—that half-buried feeling, the one that lived under her sternum like a coal banked in ash. It stirred whenever the story came up, and the story came up everywhere now. The Smuggler and the Sigil. The Storm Over the Spire. The King Who Wept. Minstrels had gotten hold of it and improved it, the way minstrels do; in the southern versions the smuggler was a dashing man with a scarred jaw, and in the coastal ones the child spoke prophecies, and in one truly shameless ballad everyone died except the horse.

"What happened to them?" Doss asked. "Smuggler and the child? Nobody seems to know. One day they're in every song, next day—nothing. Like the mountain swallowed them."

"Mountains do that," Astrid said.

But her hand had drifted, without her noticing, to the hollow of her own chest, pressing lightly, as if checking whether something was still there.

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of a ridge Astrid knew without knowing how she knew it—a sheltered fold of stone with a spring that didn't freeze, exactly the sort of place a person would choose if that person had spent years learning every secret fold of these mountains. She stood looking at it for a long moment, snow ticking softly against her hood.

"I've been here before," she said.

Wren looked up from the fire they were coaxing alight. "Probably."

"No. I mean—" She turned a slow circle. "I mean I chose this spot before I knew why. The way I chose my name."

Astrid. She had found it a month after leaving the capital, surfacing in her mind like a fish rising to bread: not remembered, exactly, but *recognized*, the way you recognize a melody from a room away. It had come attached to a feeling—warmth, and grief, and something fiercely proud—and she had put it on like a coat that fit perfectly and smelled faintly of someone else's smoke.

"You said names chosen that way are the truest ones," she said to Wren. "Did I say that? Or did you?"

"You did." Wren poked the fire. Sparks climbed. "You say a lot of true things. You just don't remember saying them."

"And you keep them for me."

"Somebody has to." Wren said it lightly, but their eyes, when they lifted, were old and careful and full of a tenderness that made Astrid's throat tighten for reasons she couldn't name. "It's not a sad thing, Astrid. You gave away more than anyone ever gave. What came back is yours. All of it. Even the parts that don't make sense yet."

"The storm," Astrid said quietly. "When I call it—the way it feels right, the way it *answers* me—I know that cost me something. I can feel the shape of the thing that's missing. Like a tooth. My tongue keeps going to it."

Wren was quiet a moment. Then: "Do you want the story again? The real one?"

Astrid sat down across the fire. This was their ritual, though she hadn't known until recently that it was one. Once or twice a week, usually on the hard nights, usually when the wind made sounds like voices, Wren would tell her a piece of it—not the minstrels' version, the true one, or as true as a child could make it who had been there for most of it and pieced together the rest from a deserter's letters and a ferryman's drunken confessions and an archivist's furious marginalia.

"Tell me about the cabin," Astrid said. That was her favorite. She never knew why. "The blizzard one."

So Wren told it.

"A knock at midnight," they began, in the cadence of a tale often told. "There was a woman living alone in a cabin above the tree line, in a valley so far off the maps the mapmakers apologized for the blank space. She had erased herself so thoroughly that even her debts had forgotten her name. She wanted it that way. She'd sworn off the storm, because the last time she'd called it, it had taken everything she loved and left her standing in the wreckage holding the lightning like a knife she couldn't put down.

"And then, in the worst blizzard in twenty years, someone knocked on her door."

"Wren," Astrid said. "In your telling, is the child afraid?"

Wren smiled. Firelight caught the sharp planes of their face and made them, for a moment, look like someone else entirely—someone older, or younger, or both. "No," they said. "That's the whole point of the story. Everyone in it is afraid of everything. The wardens are afraid of the Crown. The Crown is afraid of being unbound. The king is afraid of his own memories. The smuggler is afraid of herself. Everybody, afraid. Except the child, barefoot in the snow, who wasn't afraid of anything, because the child was made out of remembering, and you can't be frightened of a thing when you're made of its answer."

Astrid watched them over the flames. Her chest ached again, that banked-coal ache.

"What happened to the child?" she asked, though she asked it every time. "At the end. When the memories went home."

"They went with the smuggler," Wren said. "Because the smuggler forgot them—gave up every memory of the child to carry the king's memories home—and the child decided that wasn't allowed to be the end. So the child carried the remembering instead. For both of them." Wren shrugged, elaborately casual, the way children do when a thing matters too much to say plainly. "Some people think that was foolish. Giving your whole heart to someone who doesn't know you anymore."

"What do you think?"

"I think," Wren said, "that love isn't a memory. Memories burn. Love's the thing that does the burning." They fed another branch to the fire. "Besides. The child got something out of it."

"What?"

"A name. A road. Somebody to walk it with." Wren looked up, and their eyes held hers, and for one suspended moment Astrid felt the whole shape of it press against the inside of her ribs—the cabin, the blizzard, small cold hands, static crawling up her arm like a greeting. Then it sank back into the ash, and she was only a tired guide by a fire, with an odd ache and a good companion.

"Sleep," Wren said gently. "Long climb tomorrow. And you'll want your strength."

"Why?"

Wren grinned, and it was the grin of a smuggler's crew, of a ferryman, of a forger, of every ragged soul who had ever walked this road. "Because tomorrow we pass the ruins of an archive, and I want to show you where Odile Vane hid the good brandy. And because three days from now, at Graywater, there's a ferryman who owes somebody a debt—and I've decided it's time we collected."

* * *

Astrid slept, and dreamed, as she sometimes did, of a spire against a green-lit sky, and of a storm that did not destroy but *opened*—and in the dream she was not afraid, because a small voice was humming beneath the thunder, keeping time, keeping faith, singing the world's oldest language like a lullaby.

She woke at dawn with tears drying on her cheeks and no idea why, and found Wren sitting on the flat rock, watching the east go from ash to rose.

"Good dream or bad one?" Wren asked.

"Both," Astrid said. "The best ones usually are."

She climbed up beside the child and looked out over the Thornfells—range beyond range, white and blue and endless, all of it legible to her now, every wind written plain. Somewhere behind them lay a capital with a waking king and a burned warden-roll and laws being read aloud to see if they hummed. Somewhere ahead lay roads that needed guiding, and people who needed getting through, and a life that was, against all odds and all prices, still going.

"Tell me another story," she said.

Wren leaned against her shoulder, small and warm and utterly unafraid.

"Once," Wren said, "there was a smuggler who swore off storms, and a sigil who refused to be forgotten, and a king who slept thirty years with his eyes open—and none of them knew, yet, that the ending was only the door propped open so the beginning could walk through."

Below them, the caravan stirred. Bells rang. The road went on.

And Astrid—who had chosen her name from a half-buried feeling, and her road from a full heart—shouldered her pack and led them into the morning, while the child who remembered for both of them told her, step by step and year by year, exactly who she had been:

and let her decide, fresh each day, who she would be.
