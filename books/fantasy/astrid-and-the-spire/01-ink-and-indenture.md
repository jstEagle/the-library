## 1. Ink and Indenture

The candle had burned down to its last knuckle of wax, and Astrid Galloway counted the hours the way other people counted coins: by what remained.

Three fingers of tallow left. Perhaps four hours of light. Enough, if the wind stayed off the mountain and the draft through the scriptorium's warped shutters kept its opinions to itself, to finish the last eleven pages of the *Vellum Heresies* before the bell for prime. Eleven pages, four hundred and forty lines, and every one of them a small act of treason performed in a fair, obedient hand.

Astrid dipped the quill, touched it to the blotter to shed the excess, and wrote.

*And it was said among the first priests that the crown was not a symbol but a mouth, and that every oath sworn before it was a meal.*

The words went down clean and black, the ink still warm from the warming stone at her elbow. She did not let herself think about what she was writing. That was the trick of it, the discipline the abbot prized in his best scribe: the hand copies, the mind wanders elsewhere. She thought instead about the ledger. Her ledger. The little book of columns she kept in the margin of her memory because keeping it on paper was the one crime the monastery would actually punish.

*Two hundred and eleven days,* she calculated, watching the letters take shape beneath her nib. *At the standard rate for a journeyman scribe, minus room, minus board, minus the candle-tallow and the ink and the vellum, minus the interest the bursar adds each quarter with a smile like an apology—* two hundred and eleven days, give or take a hard winter, until the debt that had owned her since she was fourteen years old was paid down to nothing.

She had been copying the *Vellum Heresies* for six nights. The abbot's instruction had come through the sub-prior, as his instructions always did, delivered in the passive voice that let everyone pretend the words had assembled themselves: *A text is to be reproduced. Discretion is observed. The client is known to the office.* A client. Somewhere south of the pass, some collector with more coin than conscience wanted a heretical text in a fine modern hand, close enough to the original to pass, new enough to deny. And Thornfell, drowning in its own debts, had said yes.

Astrid did not judge them for it. Judging required a vantage point she didn't have. The monastery owed money to wool factors and wine merchants and, through three tangled intermediaries, to the crown itself, and the crown's collectors had begun to smell blood in the water this year. The abbot sold what he had. What the abbot had was a scriptorium full of hands that could reproduce anything, and a scribe who could memorize a page in a single reading and recite it back, word-perfect, a month later.

That last part was not in any inventory. That last part was Astrid's, and she guarded it the way she guarded everything else that was hers: silently, completely, and with the constant low hum of fear that one day it would be noticed.

Because it was not a thing a scribe should be able to do. It was not a thing a *person* should be able to do, if the catechisms were to be believed, and Astrid had copied enough catechisms to know what the catechisms said. *The gift is the wound the world suffered when man first knelt. The gifted are the scar tissue. Scars are cut away.*

She had never told anyone. Not the novice who shared her cell, not Maren Ostwick down in the valley, who had taught her the smuggler roads and never asked why a temple scribe needed to know them. Not anyone. For nine years the gift had lived in her like a coal banked under ash, and she had fed it nothing but small, secret uses—lighting a candle with a thought when her hands were full, holding a line of text in her mind's eye so perfectly that copying it was less transcription than tracing.

And every small use was a coin from a purse she couldn't afford to open.

The wind found the shutter and worried it. Astrid rose, crossed the cold flagstones, and wedged a sliver of slate into the frame, and while she stood there she let herself look out—just for a moment—at the pass.

Thornfell Pass cut through the mountains like a scar through a face, and in the pre-dawn dark it was a blacker black, a absence of stars where the peaks should have caught them. Somewhere down in that dark was the road. The switchbacks, the shrine at the third bend where travelers left offerings and smugglers left letters, the frozen stream crossing where the ice sang if you stepped wrong. Astrid knew all of it. Not from maps—Thornfell's maps were pious fictions—but from walking it, eleven times now, carrying letters and packets and once a small locked box that had hummed against her ribs like a trapped wasp the whole way down.

Eleven runs. Eleven payments against the debt, each one worth a season of copying. That was the arithmetic of her life: the monastery's ink for the monastery's ledger, the smugglers' coin for her own. Two ledgers, two hands, one person, and neither ledger knew the other existed.

She went back to her desk. The candle flame leaned as she sat, and she steadied it without thinking—a small pressure of will, the way you'd steady a cup—and the flame stood straight again, and she pretended she hadn't done it, the way she always pretended.

*And the first priests bound their king with words, and the words took root, and in time the words were all that remained of the man.*

Her nib paused.

It was a strange text. Astrid had copied scripture, saints' lives, herbals, a treatise on siege engineering that had made the sub-prior sweat. She knew the flavor of forbidden things, and most forbidden things were forbidden for boring reasons—politics, property, the bruised egos of dead bishops. This was different. The *Vellum Heresies* read like a warning written by someone who had checked the arithmetic. It spoke of oaths the way a merchant spoke of debts: as things that accumulated, that fed something, that *matured*. And there was a phrase that had snagged in her memory on the first night and had not let go, the way a fishhook doesn't let go:

*The crown endures because the crown is fed. Thirteen turns of the moon make a feeding permanent. Count the moons.*

Count the moons. She had counted them, of course, the way she counted everything—compulsively, silently, in the margins of her mind. The moon was in its eleventh turn of the current cycle. She had noted it the way she noted everything and filed it under nothing, because there was nowhere to file it. A scribe with a heretical thought and nowhere to put it was just a woman with a stone in her shoe.

She shook the thought off and wrote on.

* * *

Prime came with the bell, and the bell came too soon, as bells always did.

Astrid finished the final line as the first stroke rang—the *Vellum Heresies* complete, eleven pages of treason in a hand so obedient it could have signed itself to the catechism—and blotted the page, and stacked the quires, and bound them in the plain gray boards the client had specified. Then she sat for a moment in the gray light with her hands flat on the finished work, feeling the specific exhaustion of the night's labor, which was not the tiredness of the body but of the self, of all the selves she had to be before the day properly began.

There was the scribe-self, who went to the morning chapter with ink still under her nails. There was the debtor-self, who bowed to the bursar and accepted the quarterly accounting with a grateful face. There was the smuggler-self, who existed only in the valley and only after dark. And underneath all of them, folded small as a moth in a folded letter, there was the self that was none of these—the one that could hold fire in the palm of her attention and make it behave. That self got no hours of the day. That self only got the margins.

"Brother Aldous says you were at the candles all night." Novice Petra stood in the scriptorium doorway, cheeks pink from the cold, a basket of fresh vellum on her hip. She was sixteen and still believed the monastery was a place rather than a machine. "He says the client work is bad for your eyes."

"Brother Aldous is welcome to do the client work himself," Astrid said, "and I will say a novena for his eyes."

Petra giggled and set down the basket, and Astrid felt the familiar pang—the one that came whenever the novices were kind to her, the pang of *I am lying to you by existing here*—and smothered it. Nine years of practice. She was very good at smothering.

The morning chapter passed in the usual liturgy of accounts. The abbot read the debts. The debts were many. The crown's collector had written again, and the abbot read the letter twice, and the second time his voice did something almost human at the edges, and everyone studied their hands. Astrid studied her hands too. They were good hands, a scribe's hands, and they were the only property she had ever owned outright, and she thought: *Two hundred and eleven days. Two hundred and ten after today.*

After chapter, the bursar caught her in the cloister walk, breath steaming, ledger under his arm like a weapon he was tired of carrying.

"Galloway. A word."

She had known this was coming. The client's courier had arrived two days ago with the payment and a second request, and second requests were always delivered in person, and in person always meant her.

"The letter," the bursar said, without preamble, when they were out of earshot of the brothers. He didn't say *the smuggled letter* or *the letter for the pass*. Nine years had taught them both the grammar of plausible deniability. "It goes down tonight. The usual arrangements."

"The usual price?"

"The usual price." He hesitated, and then, because he was not entirely a machine either, he added, "Galloway. The crown's men were in the valley last week. Asking after roads, after couriers. The abbot thinks it's about the taxes. I think—" He stopped himself, the way men stopped themselves when the next word was a word that could hang them. "Be careful. Be quick. And be nothing, if you can manage it."

"I'm always nothing, Master Bursar."

He gave her a look that was almost paternal and entirely weary, and went off to his ledgers, and Astrid stood in the cloister with the frost making lace of the flagstones and felt, not for the first time, the shape of the trap she lived in: too useful to free, too dangerous to keep, too tired to run.

*Two hundred and ten days,* she thought. *After tonight, two hundred and nine.*

She could endure anything that had a number attached to it. That was the whole secret of her survival. Numbers had edges. Longing didn't.

* * *

The letter went into her coat an hour after compline.

It was a good letter, in the sense that it was unremarkable: a single folded sheet, sealed with plain wax, addressed in a merchant's hand to a name in the low country. The client's letter, presumably, or a letter about the client—Astrid had long ago stopped wondering what moved through the pass in her coat. She was a road, and roads don't ask their travelers' business. What mattered was the weight of it, the small stiff rectangle against her sternum, and what it bought: forty crowns against the debt. Forty crowns was a month of copying. Forty crowns was a month of her life, purchased in one cold walk.

She went down through the monastery's postern gate with the letter against her heart and the mountain wind in her teeth, and the gate-brother didn't look up from his prayers, because the gate-brother never looked up, that being the arrangement.

The first mile was the worst. It was always the worst—the stretch where the monastery was still at her back, close enough that its windows were a scatter of candlelight, close enough that running felt like something a person could still do. Astrid walked it every time with the same argument running in her head, the argument she'd been having with herself for nine years:

*You could just keep walking.*

And the answer, every time: *And go where? With what? The debt follows. The debt is the one thing in this kingdom that will never lose track of you.* Debts were written into the crown's rolls. Debts outlived their debtors. She had copied enough of the law-books to know that a runaway debtor could be taken in any town, claimed by any creditor, bound over to any work. The mountains were full of people who had run, and most of them were running still, in circles, in harness, in worse.

So she didn't run. She walked. She walked the smuggler road that Maren Ostwick had taught her, the one that ran below the pilgrim road and above the stream, the one that existed on no map because Maren had erased it from every map she could reach—a woman after Astrid's own heart, though Astrid hadn't known it at fourteen, when a wool-trader with quick eyes and quicker hands had found a starving, sold, half-frozen child on the pass and taught her, first, how to survive it, and later, how to own it.

*The road remembers your feet if you let it,* Maren used to say. *Don't let it. You remember the road. That's the difference between a courier and a corpse.*

Astrid remembered the road. She walked it now in the dark without a lantern, because a lantern on the smuggler road was a confession, and her eyes had long ago learned the dark the way her mind had learned texts: completely, and against all expectation. The shrine at the third bend rose out of the blackness, a hump of old stones with its offering-niche, and she did what she always did—pressed two fingers to the cold stone, an old habit, half piety and half apology—and went on.

It was at the fifth bend, where the path narrowed above the frozen stream, that the moon came out from behind the clouds.

It was nearly full. It lay along the snowfields like a spilled coin, and the whole pass went silver and blue and suddenly, terribly visible, and Astrid stood still in the middle of it and let herself look, because there was no one to see her look.

*Eleven turns,* she thought. *Two more, and the cycle closes.*

And the thought arrived unbidden, the heretical phrase rising in her memory with the terrible fidelity of her gift, word-perfect, a month old and fresh as ink:

*Thirteen turns of the moon make a feeding permanent. Count the moons.*

She counted them. She couldn't help it; counting was what she was. Eleven. Two remaining. And the crown's men in the valley, asking after roads. And the crown's collectors pressing the monastery. And the client in the south who wanted the *Vellum Heresies* copied *now*, this season, in a fine modern hand.

Astrid stood in the moonlight on the fifth bend with a smuggled letter against her heart, and for the first time in nine years she felt the numbers in her head shift of their own accord, the columns sliding, the sums rearranging themselves into a shape she didn't like.

It was nothing, she told herself. Coincidence was the mortar of the world; she copied enough chronicles to know that history was mostly coincidence wearing a crown. It was nothing. She had a letter to deliver, a payment to collect, two hundred and nine days to serve.

She walked on down the pass, and the moon followed her, one turn closer to full than it had been the night before, and the letter lay against her sternum like a second heart, beating its small, stiff, secret beat.

She did not know—could not have known—that three hundred miles away, in a locked room in the royal map-house, a man was being questioned about the marks he had made on a seal-line, and that his answers were already burning.

But the road knew. Roads always know before their walkers do. And the road, that night, tilted its shoulder ever so slightly downhill, toward the valley, toward the low country, toward everything that was coming for her—
