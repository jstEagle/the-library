## 2. The Proclamation

The bell woke Astrid before the candle did.

That was wrong. Thornfell's morning bell rang at the seventh hour of the night, and it always woke the candle first—Brother Anselm going down the dormitory row with his taper, touching flame to wick after wick until the whole long room glowed like a field of stubble wheat. Astrid had counted on that ritual for six years. The bell meant the bell; the light meant the light. In a life where everything else was ledgered and owed, those small certainties were the only property they owned outright.

But this morning the bell came alone, ragged and urgent, rung by someone who did not care about rhythm, and the dark stayed dark.

Astrid sat up in the narrow cell, heart already quickening, and listened. Boots in the corridor. Not the shuffling tread of brothers shuffling toward prayer—these were hard heels, many of them, moving with the particular purposefulness of people who had been told to move quickly and were enjoying it. From the courtyard below came a sound Astrid knew from market days in Thornfell village: the flat slap of a proclamation board being set against stone.

They dressed in the dark, fingers finding each buckle by memory, and slid the day's copying—the heretical *Testament of the Nine Veils*, three-quarters finished, worth half a year of their debt if it reached its buyer intact—into the false bottom of their writing box. Then they went down to learn what the world wanted now.

* * *

The courtyard was full of snow-light and breath. The brothers stood in their gray ranks, hoods up against the cold, and behind them, at the edge of the flagstones where the mountain began its climb into pine, stood a dozen riders in crown colors—oxblood and gold—and among them, mounted, a crier with a voice like a slate being scraped.

"By decree of His Majesty," the crier read, "and for the preservation of the realm's peace, all persons possessing the unsanctioned gift are hereby declared subject to discovery, registration, and disposition. All abbots, priors, rectors, and heads of household are commanded to surrender the names of such persons within their charge. Reward is offered for accurate report. Penalty—" here the crier paused, and Astrid watched him find the line again with a small professional cough, "—penalty for concealment is forfeiture of charter, goods, and standing."

*Disposition.* Astrid turned the word over the way they'd once turned over an unfamiliar glyph in a smuggled text, looking for the trick of it, the meaning hidden under the meaning. There was no trick. Disposition was a word chosen because no one liked the older words. Burning. Drowning. The deep cells under the capital where people went in and did not come out, or came out changed, docile, oath-marked.

Beside them, old Brother Anselm made a sound like a man swallowing a stone. Anselm had taught Astrid their letters, back when Astrid was nine and newly indentured, sold to the monastery by an uncle who had signed the debt contract with a flourish he probably believed was elegant. Anselm's hands shook now. They had shaken for years. But they had never shaken like this.

"The gift-hunt," whispered Sister Maren—no, not Maren, that was someone else, someone from another life, another road; it was Sister Odile. Astrid's mind kept sliding sideways this morning, reaching for names that weren't there. "They haven't called a hunt since the old king's grandfather."

"They're calling one now," said Astrid, quietly, and felt every eye in the gray ranks flick toward them and away again, quick as fish.

That was the thing about living in a monastery of scribes: everyone could read, which meant everyone could read you. Six years of careful nothing—of keeping their shoulders round and their eyes down and their gift folded up inside them like a letter sealed with wax—six years, and still the brothers looked at Astrid a half-beat too long when candles guttered without wind. Still they paused outside the scriptorium door when Astrid worked late, as though drawn by something they couldn't name and didn't want to.

Abbot Corvane came out onto the cloister steps then, robed in black wool despite the cold, his face arranged into its usual expression of patient sorrow, as though the world were a difficult manuscript he had been asked to edit. He raised one hand and the courtyard quieted.

"Brothers. Sisters. You have heard the crown's decree." His voice carried beautifully; it always had. "I want you to understand that I will do everything in my power to protect this house. Everything." A pause, measured to the width of a hair. "Thornfell has stood four hundred years. It will stand four hundred more. Go to your offices. Pray for wisdom."

He looked at no one in particular while he said it, which meant he was looking at everyone, which meant he was looking at Astrid.

* * *

The archive burned at noon.

Not all of it—that would have been madness even for Corvane, who loved the collection the way a miser loves coin—but enough. Astrid watched from the scriptorium window as the abbot directed three novices to carry armload after armload out of the east vault: the apocrypha shelf, the sealed case of pre-Reformation liturgies, the entire cabinet of texts on gift-lore that Astrid had spent four years cataloguing under innocuous titles. *Meditations on the Inner Flame.* *On the Governance of the Soul.* Books that were, in truth, manuals of the gifted arts, bound in calfskin and mislabeled by monks dead two centuries who had known exactly why such labels mattered.

The pyre was built in the kitchen yard, out of sight of the road, and Corvane himself set the torch to it, and the smoke rose straight up in the windless cold like a column holding up nothing.

Astrid stood at the window with their coat already on.

They had not decided to put it on. Their body had decided, some older and cleverer part of them, the part that memorized smuggler roads and counted debt margins and knew how to be invisible in a room full of people who owned them. The coat was heavy winter wool, cut generously, the kind of garment a scribe might plausibly wear to fetch ink from the storeroom. Inside its lining, between wool and a layer of oiled canvas Astrid had sewn there two winters ago for exactly this kind of contingency—though they had told themselves then it was for smuggling letters, not books—there was a space the size of a psalter.

One book. That was all the space allowed. One book out of four hundred burning.

Choosing was the worst of it. Astrid went down through the cloister with their heart hammering a smuggler's rhythm—*quick, quiet, don't look at the guards*—and stood near the pyre in the crowd of grieving, frightened brothers, and let their eyes move across the stacks waiting their turn at the flames. The *Nine Veils* was safe in their writing box upstairs; that was cargo, not treasure. What burned today was knowledge, and knowledge was the only inheritance Astrid had ever been permitted, borrowed at the price of indenture, and they could save exactly one volume of it.

Their hand closed on a slim book in a plain binding: *The Unbinding of Oaths,* attributed to a heretic named Vael whose name had been scraped off every other copy in the realm. Astrid had catalogued it themselves, five years ago, and had read it eleven times since, and had never understood why it made their fingertips ache—until this morning, watching the proclamation riders sit their horses like men who fed on something, until this moment, holding it, feeling it hum against their palms like a struck tuning fork.

It hummed because it was about them. Or about whatever they were. The thing they had spent six years pretending not to be.

"You."

Astrid's blood went to ice water. But it was only Brother Anselm, beside them, staring into the fire with wet eyes, and his hand came down over theirs—over the book, hiding it, shielding it, in one motion, casual as a blessing.

"Storeroom needs an inventory," Anselm said loudly, to no one, to everyone. "Take your time about it, child."

Then he walked away, and did not look back, and Astrid understood that this was the last kindness the monastery would ever give them, and that Anselm knew it too.

* * *

Corvane summoned the senior household to his study at vespers, and Astrid was not senior, but Astrid was the best scribe in the house, and the abbot had never in six years been able to resist using the best tool available. So Astrid sat in the corner with pen and inkwell, recording, while the abbot laid out the future of Thornfell like a merchant laying out cloth.

"The crown's commissioner arrives Thursday," Corvane said. "Captain Veyra, of the Oathwardens. She will want names." He spread his hands, and his rings caught the lamplight. "And I intend to give her accurate ones."

Silence. Prior Lucan's jaw worked. "Father Abbot—the brothers who—"

"I am aware of what I am proposing, Prior." Corvane's voice was gentle, which was how they all knew it was final. "I am proposing that this monastery survive. Four hundred souls under this roof, and perhaps—" he paused, delicately, "—perhaps four or five whose natures place them outside the decree. Weigh it. I have weighed it. I weighed it before noon."

Astrid wrote. *Commissioner Veyra. Names, accurate. Four hundred souls. Four or five.* Their handwriting did not shake, because they had trained it not to, because a scribe whose hand shakes is a scribe whose work gets checked, and Astrid's work could never afford checking. But inside, something was coming apart with a sound like wet paper tearing.

Six years. Six years of fourteen-hour days at the copying desk, of memorizing roads in the dark, of carrying letters sewn into hems down passes where a caught courier loses fingers—six years of buying back, coin by coin and page by page, a freedom the monastery had priced at ninety-one marks, twelve shillings, and change. The ledger lived in Astrid's head to the last farthing. Forty-three marks remained. Two years, maybe less, if the smuggling runs stayed good.

And now the abbot would sell their name to the crown for whatever he could get, and the debt would die unpaid, and Astrid would die with it—or worse than die, *dispositioned*, whatever soft horror that word was built to hide.

"We should discuss compensation," Prior Lucan said carefully. "For the house. If we are to—"

"Per name, the commissioner offered ten marks." Corvane said it the way he announced feast days. "Plus a stipend of grain through the winter, provided our cooperation continues."

Ten marks. Astrid's pen stopped. Ten marks per name, and there were perhaps five names, and Astrid did the arithmetic the way they did all arithmetic—instantly, treacherously, helplessly—and arrived at fifty marks, which was more than the remainder of their own debt, which meant that Astrid Galloway was worth more to Thornfell as a betrayal than as a servant.

The ink sat in the well, black and patient. Astrid thought: *I could write it wrong. I could record that he said eight marks, sow confusion, buy a day.* And then thought: *he'll read my transcript tonight, and he reads everything, and if he catches me he'll know I know.*

So they wrote *ten marks* in their beautiful, obedient hand, and hated every letter of it, and understood with total clarity that the institution which owned them had just finished pricing them, and that the price was fair, and that fairness had nothing to do with anything.

* * *

Night. The monastery slept badly; Astrid heard coughing in the brothers' wing, footsteps, a door opening and closing twice. Everyone was doing sums tonight. Everyone was counting names.

Astrid packed by feel and moonlight through the frost-furred window. The *Nine Veils* into the writing box's false bottom, along with the buyer's token—a pewter medallion stamped with a nine-pointed star—and the letter for the Hollowmere run, sewn into the coat's other hem, because a courier who abandons cargo abandons reputation, and reputation was the only currency that crossed borders freely. The *Unbinding of Oaths* went inside the coat itself, against their chest, where its faint humming warmth sat like a second heartbeat.

Forty-three marks owed. It occurred to Astrid, folding their spare shirt, that running meant the debt never closed—that Uncle Aldous's contract would pass to the crown's collectors, that they would be hunted as a debtor and a fugitive and a gifted, three leashes instead of one. It occurred to them, and they found—to their own surprise—that the thought weighed almost nothing. Somewhere between the pyre and the word *disposition*, the debt had stopped being a chain and become simply a number, and numbers could be outrun. Chains could not.

What hurt was smaller and sharper. The desk by the south window where the light came in clean from second hour to fifth. Anselm's dry voice correcting their Greek. The smell of the archive—vellum and cedar oil and cold stone—which was the closest thing to a home smell Astrid had ever owned. They had hated Thornfell for six years, properly, righteously, the way you hate a creditor. They had not known until tonight that hatred and love could share a roof, and that losing both at once left a hole shaped like neither.

At the door they stopped, and went back, and did one last thing: took the fresh transcript of the evening's meeting off Corvane's desk, and beneath the official record, in a hand disguised as a brother's shaky scrawl, added a single line:

*The abbot sells names. Ask him what he sold forty years ago, and who paid for his crozier.*

It was true—they had found the old receipt in the archive's deepest drawer during a catalogue, a bill of sale for a gifted novice, signed C.C., dated the year of the last hunt—and it was a knife, and knives were the only inheritance Astrid intended to leave in this house.

Then they went out through the infirmary window, which stuck, and gave, and remembered them kindly, and dropped into snow that came up past their knees, and began to climb.

Behind them Thornfell lay silvered and silent under the thirteenth-of-the-year moon, smoke from the afternoon's burning still ghosting from the kitchen yard, and ahead the pass ran black and secret, a road written in no honest map, a road that existed only in the memory of people who had needed it.

Astrid walked into the mountains with a stolen book against their heart and forty-three marks of debt riding on their soul, and did not look back, because looking back was a luxury of the free.

Somewhere below, a dog began to bark. Then a second. Then, from the direction of the gatehouse, a horn—one short note, uncertain, questioning.

The hunt had not waited for Thursday.

Astrid broke into a run.
