## 4. Smoke Over the Pass

The candles told her first.

Astrid had been awake since the third bell, hunched over a fresh quire of vellum in the scriptorium's coldest corner, copying out the *Lamentations of the Veiled Choir* for a merchant in Lowmarsh who paid by the page and never asked where the pages came from. The work was steady, mindless, the kind of copying that let her count her debt in her head while her hand moved on its own. Eleven years, four months, and some days she could not bear to tally. The ledger margin beside her elbow held the running total in her own small cipher: *owed to Thornfell, less remitted, less coin sent down-mountain with the salt carts.*

Then, without her touching them, every candle in the room stood up straighter.

It was subtle at first—a brightening, as if someone had opened a shutter onto snowlight. Astrid looked up from the *Lamentations* and watched forty flames lean toward her like a congregation turning to hear a reading. The wax hissed. Shadows swung across the vaulted ceiling, and for one long breath the whole scriptorium breathed light instead of air.

"No," she whispered, and pressed her palm flat against the desk, against the wood her forearm had polished thin over eleven years. "No, no, not now."

But the gift had never once asked her permission. It lived under her sternum like a second heart, banked and buried, fed on nothing, starved on purpose—and tonight it had woken ravenous. The candles flared again. Two of them guttered blue at their bases, burning too hot, drowning in their own melted wax. Down the row of desks, Brother Anselm snored on, oblivious, his ink-stained fingers curled around a cold cup of barley tea.

Astrid closed her eyes and did what she had done since she was nine years old and a candle had leapt into her hand at her mother's funeral: she built the box. Dark oak, iron-banded, lid shut tight, buried deep. She put the light inside it. She put the warmth inside it. She put the terrible sweet wanting—the way fire loved her, the way she loved it back—inside it, and nailed the lid down with breath after slow breath until the candles sagged back to their ordinary yellow and the shadows settled like birds coming home to roost.

Her hands were shaking. Her heart was not. That was the worst part. Some traitorous chamber of her chest felt *fed*.

She was still pressing her palms against the desk when she heard the dogs.

Not the monastery's own hounds—those she knew, old Barley and his dull-witted sons, who bayed at geese and slept through thunder. These were thinner voices, sharper, coming up the pass road from the valley in that disciplined chorus she had only ever heard described in smugglers' warnings: *when the king's dogs sing in rounds, you are already counted.* Maren Ostwick had taught her that, years ago, over a mug of burnt chicory in a waystation below the snowline. Maren, who took her letters and her coin and never asked why a scribe knew so much about which roads the crown's riders avoided.

Maren, who owed her a favor Astrid had been saving like a coin bitten to test its metal.

Brother Anselm woke with a snort. "Dogs," he said stupidly, and then, hearing them properly, went gray. "Those aren't ours."

"I know." Astrid was already moving, sweeping the *Lamentations* into a drawer, dragging her sleeve across wet ink. "Wake the abbot. Wake everyone. Go."

He went. Astrid did not follow—not yet. Instead she stood in the middle of the darkening scriptorium, listening to the dogs sing closer, and made the calculation she had been making in the margins of her life for eleven years: what could be carried, what must be burned, what would damn her if it was found.

The archive was three floors down. The proclamation had come a month ago—criers in every market town, the gift-hunt declared, names to be surrendered to the crown's captains. Abbot Corvane had burned the open shelves within the week, a great public pyre of heresy and herb-lore, inviting the valley to watch him feed the flames. But Astrid had seen what he kept. She had copied the inventory herself. There was a locked case beneath the infirmary stairs, and in that case, wrapped in oilcloth, lay books whose titles she knew by heart because she had written them out in secret ledgers for buyers who never gave their names.

One of them was hers now. Had been hers for a month, hidden inside the false bottom of her writing desk, sewn there with thread pulled from her own hem: *On the Unbinding of Oaths*, author unknown, its margins full of a dead hand's annotations about crowns and the things that wear them. She had told herself she kept it for sale value. She had known she was lying.

She took it now, along with her cipher ledger, her needle, her knife, and the small purse of coin that represented four years of copied psalms sold twice. Into the lining of her coat went everything. The coat was heavy. It felt like putting on another person.

* * *

By the time she reached the cloister garth, the monastery was awake and unraveling. Torches moved along the walls. Novices clutched one another in doorways. And beyond the gatehouse, down the switchbacks of the pass road, torches answered—many torches, riding in formation, with the black-and-gold pennons of the crown snapping above them.

Abbot Corvane stood on the chapel steps in his fur-trimmed cope, which he wore even at this hour, even for this. He looked, Astrid thought, exactly as he had looked the day he'd appraised her mother's debt and named its terms: calm, reasonable, and already counting.

"Inside," he told the gathered brothers and sisters. "All of you. Kneel in the chapel and pray. If any rider asks you questions, answer honestly and briefly. We have done nothing wrong." His gaze crossed the courtyard and snagged, just for a moment, on Astrid. Just for a moment too long. "We have done nothing wrong," he repeated, softer, and Astrid understood, with a cold clarity that felt almost like relief, that he was rehearsing.

She slipped along the shadowed arcade toward the stables, and that was where Sister Hedwig found her—old Hedwig, who ran the infirmary and watered her medicinal poppies with wine and pretended no one noticed.

"You're leaving." It wasn't a question. Hedwig pressed a bundle into her arms: bread, dried plums, a twist of paper that smelled of feverfew and something sharper. "For pain, if you need it. For sleep, if you can't run anymore. Don't take both at once."

"Hedwig—"

"Don't." The old woman's hands were steady; her voice was not quite. "I've watched you count your debt in your sleep for eleven years, girl. Whatever's coming up that road, it isn't worth more than you are. No debt is." She glanced toward the pass road, where the torches had begun to climb the last switchback. "Go through the charcoal burners' track. The riders will take the main road to the gate. The burners' track is ugly but it drops you at the waystation shrine by dawn if your legs hold."

"How do you know the burners' track?"

Hedwig smiled with all the guile of a woman who had spent forty years smuggling brandy in medicine bottles. "Because I filled this monastery's sickroom before you were born, and I know which roads bring sickness and which carry it away. Now go. And Astrid—" She caught her wrist, grip surprisingly strong. "Whatever they say you are, whatever the books say you should be afraid of becoming—you were a child lighting candles at a funeral. Remember that. Not a monster. A child."

Astrid's throat closed. She nodded, once, and ran.

* * *

The charcoal burners' track was everything Hedwig had promised and worse: a goat-path stitched into the mountainside, half scree, half root, dropping through pine shadow so thick the night seemed to have substance. Astrid climbed down it with the book digging into her ribs and her lungs full of resin and fear, and behind her, above her, Thornfell began to burn.

She saw it first as a glow through the trees—an orange stain spreading across the low clouds. Then came the smell, hot pitch and burning thatch, and then, drifting down the mountain with obscene clarity, the sound: shouting, the crash of timber, a bell rung wrong, over and over, panic instead of hours.

They're burning the archive, she thought. No—they already burned the archive. They're burning the *proof*. Or the hunters were, or the abbot was, or it didn't matter, because fire was doing what fire always did in her life: taking the record, taking the past, taking the version of events that might have saved someone.

Her chest tightened. Under her sternum, the banked thing stirred, sniffing the smoke like a hound catching scent.

*No,* she told it, climbing faster. *Not now. Not here.*

But grief is a kind of permission, and terror is a kind of invitation. Somewhere above her a window burst, and a fountain of sparks climbed into the sky, and Astrid's gift rose to meet them like water finding its level. Light bloomed along the whole length of her body. She felt it pour out through her skin, her fingertips, her open mouth—and ahead of her, in the pitch-dark of the burners' track, every frost-rimed branch lit silver. Every stone glowed. The path unrolled before her, luminous, mapped in her own radiance, and for three staggering steps she could see everything—the drop on her left, the roots at her feet, the waystation shrine two miles below, glowing faintly through the trees like a coal wrapped in cloth.

Then she wrestled it down, gasping, and the dark rushed back, and she stood shaking on the mountainside with tears freezing on her cheeks.

*I lit my own way out,* she thought. Eleven years of burying it, and the first time it ever truly served her, it happened by accident, in flight, wearing her like a lantern wears a flame.

She laughed—one cracked, disbelieving sound—and put her hand over her sternum as if she could feel the thing beating there.

"Thank you," she whispered, to it, to herself, to no one. Then she kept moving, because the dogs had stopped singing, and silence from hunting dogs meant they had found what they were looking for.

* * *

She reached the waystation shrine an hour before dawn, legs gone to water, coat soaked with sweat that froze in the wind. The shrine was a drystone hut no bigger than a byre, sacred to no god anyone currently admitted to worshiping, used by smugglers as a cache point and by travelers as a place to swear oaths they didn't mean. Inside: a cold hearth, a shelf of left-behind offerings, and the wall of scratched initials and tally marks that smugglers called the honest ledger, because unlike the crown's ledgers, nobody lied on it.

Astrid found her own mark there—*AG*, cut shallow the day Maren had first walked her down this track, thirteen years old and furious at the world. Below it, Maren's mark, and next to that, in Maren's blunt hand, a phrase Astrid had never dared ask about: *the thirteenth turn comes for us all.*

She built a fire in the hearth with hands that would not stop trembling, and here was the thing, the shameful, wonderful thing: she didn't strike flint. She simply looked at the kindling, reached into the box in her chest, and *asked*—and a curl of flame rose off her fingertip like a tame bird and set the tinder alight.

It took less than a heartbeat. It cost her nothing. It felt like being seen for the first time.

Astrid sat back against the cold stone and watched the fire catch and spread, and let herself feel both things at once, because there was no one alive to see her do it: terror, still, humming in her teeth—the dogs, the torches, the abbot's rehearsed innocence, the knowledge that her name was in someone's ledger being weighed against coin. And underneath the terror, rising through it like heat through floorboards, exhilaration so fierce it was nearly indistinguishable from joy.

She was free. Not freed—there was no writ, no manumission, no debt forgiven. Free the way a bird is free when the cage door breaks rather than opens: bleeding, probably, unfed, certainly, but *out*. Out under the actual sky, with the actual wind coming off the glacier, carrying the smell of her burning home down-valley behind her.

Home, she thought, and tested the word, and found it had already rotted through. Thornfell had never been home. It had been a ledger with a roof.

What was in her coat was home now. What was under her sternum was home. Whatever waited down these roads—in Lowmarsh, with Maren, in the places the maps didn't admit to—she would build it out of contraband and candlelight.

She ate Hedwig's bread. She drank snowmelt from the shrine's stone basin. When the eastern sky went the color of a healing bruise, she got up, marked the honest ledger with today's date and a single word—*out*—and stepped onto the smuggler's road heading south, away from the smoke, away from the pass, away from everything she had been owned by.

She had gotten perhaps a mile when she saw, far below on the valley road, a single rider moving fast—not in formation, not with pennons, but alone, bent low over his horse's neck with something pale fluttering from his saddlebag like a wing. Half-burned papers, she would learn later. Charts. A mapmaker running from the same crown that was running her down, though she couldn't know that yet.

All she knew, watching him dwindle along the road she herself would have to take, was that the road south had just become a great deal more complicated—and that her feet, traitorously, were already carrying her after him.
