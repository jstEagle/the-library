## 5. Two Fugitives

**Astrid**

The road above Thornfell did not exist on any map the crown had ever printed, and that was precisely why Astrid loved it.

She had learned it at fourteen, hunched over Maren Ostwick's kitchen table while the old fence traced routes in spilled tea: *the shepherd's path keeps to the north face, girl, where the snow lies longest and the hunters' dogs lose the scent. When the path splits at the split stone, take the low way, even if the high way looks kinder. The high way is where they wait.*

Astrid had taken the low way.

Two days from the burning scriptorium, her legs had stopped shaking and started merely aching, a deep bone-tiredness that felt almost like accomplishment. Her lungs burned with cold mountain air. Her coat—abbot's gray, stolen from the laundry line, too big in the shoulders—held one book against her ribs, wrapped in oilcloth, warm as a second heart. *The Canticle of Unmaking.* The only copy. She had felt it there through every mile, through the night she'd spent wedged in a hollow beneath a fallen fir while a hunting horn sang somewhere below her, through the morning she'd eaten snow because she was too afraid to light a fire.

She should not have lit the candles. That thought came back to her in loops, always ending in the same place. She should have held it down. She had spent eleven years holding it down—every flicker, every warmth in her fingertips, every time the ink on a page seemed to glow for her alone. She had held it down the way one holds one's breath, and then the hunters had kicked open the scriptorium door and she had exhaled, and forty candles had leapt alight at once like a congregation rising to sing.

*Gifted,* they would say. *The scribe. The quiet one with the ink-stained fingers.*

She crested the ridge where the low way bent toward the Widow's Notch and stopped, because someone was already there.

A man sat on the split stone.

He had not heard her—she was certain of it, she had been quiet as falling snow—but there he was, a lean figure in a travel-stained coat, sorting through papers with the frantic care of a man counting coins he could not afford to lose. His hood was down. Dark hair, wind-tangled. A leather tube of maps slung across his back like a sword.

A hunter. It was the first thing she thought, and the thought arrived with her heartbeat already climbing. Hunters traveled in pairs, though. Hunters had dogs, and this man had no dog, only the papers, and he was muttering to himself as he sorted them—*no, no, that one's gone, that one's gone too*—in a voice frayed to threads.

But hunters could be alone. Hunters could be tired. Hunters could sit on the split stone of the low way, exactly where Maren had said they waited.

Astrid's hand found the scribe's knife at her belt. It was a poor weapon, made for trimming quills, but it was the only one she had. She stepped back, meaning to slip away down the scree, and her heel turned on a stone.

The crack of it was very small. It sounded enormous.

The man's head came up. For one suspended moment they looked at each other across the length of the ridge—two figures against a sky the color of pewter, the wind pulling at them both—and then he was on his feet, papers scattering, one hand diving into his coat.

Astrid ran.

She heard him behind her, boots on stone, and she ran the way Maren had taught her—not away from him but along the contour of the ridge, letting the slope carry her, cutting through the stunted pines where a taller man would have to duck and slow. Her breath sawed in her throat. The book thumped against her ribs. *Don't light,* she told the warmth gathering in her palms, the fear trying to become fire. *Don't. He'll see. He'll know.*

She burst from the pines onto a shelf of bare granite and the ground simply ended—a drop of twenty feet into a snow-choked gully, beautiful and absolute.

She turned. The man stood at the tree line, hands raised, palms out, empty.

"I'm not—" He was breathing hard. "I'm not with them."

"They all say that." Her knife was out now. It looked ridiculous in her fist, a quill-trimmer, a bookbinder's tool, and she saw him see that too.

"Put it away before you hurt yourself."

"Give me one reason."

He hesitated. Then, slowly, keeping his left hand raised, he drew something from inside his coat with his right and held it out: a sheet of paper, half of it blackened to lace at one edge, the surviving portion marked with fine ink lines she knew at once, in her bones, as a map.

"I'm Sef," he said. "I was the king's cartographer. Eleven days ago I was in irons for it. I've been walking since." His eyes, gray and exhausted, did not leave her face. "And you're the scribe from Thornfell. Aren't you. The one who lit the candles."

The wind moved through the pines. Somewhere far below, water ran under ice.

"How do you know that?" she said.

"Because the hunters are talking about nothing else." He lowered his hands at last, slowly. "Because I've been following the story of you up this mountain for two days, hoping to find you before they did." He glanced at the knife again. "And because if I were one of them, I'd have let you run yourself off that ledge by now."

* * *

**Sef**

She did not put the knife away, but she did stop pointing it at his throat, which Sef counted as progress.

They sat on opposite sides of the gully's rim, the snow between them like a border neither would cross, and she watched him the way a cat watches a hand that might feed or strike. He had seen that look before—in the interrogation room, in the mirror, in the weeks since, in every stranger on the road. It was the look of someone deciding how much of themselves it was safe to spend.

He sorted his papers while he talked, partly because his hands needed the work and partly because he wanted her to see them: the surviving charts, the half-burned seal-line surveys, the fragments he had pulled from the fire in the archive while the building came down around him.

"They took me because of this." He held up the oldest sheet, its edges scorched, its center immaculate. "Every royal document in the kingdom carries the king's seal. And every seal, when it's pressed, leaves a line—a pressure line, here, along the wax. It's been identical for forty years. The same hand. The same weight. Kings age, kings change, but the seal-line doesn't, because it's the *office*, not the man, and the office has a weight all its own." He turned the sheet so the firelight—no, the gray daylight—caught it. "Fourteen months ago, the line changed."

She frowned. "New seal."

"There is no new seal. Same matrix, same wax, same press. But the line is different. Heavier, at the end, like—" he searched for it, "—like the weight of it isn't the king's anymore. Like something else is leaning on the stamp." He had said this to the Lord Inquisitor and watched the man's face go smooth and pleasant and terrible. "I marked the anomaly. I marked eleven more across the year. And then I made the mistake of asking the archivist whether any other reign had shown drift in the seal-line."

"And?"

"And the archivist reported me. That's the thing about oaths, scribe. They're not just promises. They're *listening*. Everyone bound to the crown is a window into it, and the crown looks back through them." He said it flatly, because he had stopped being able to say it any other way. "I burned my own quarters to cover the escape. Half the charts burned with them. The ones that survived all point the same direction."

"Where?"

"Thornfell." He watched her flinch. "The monastery holds the old binding texts. The ones the crown spent three centuries buying up and burning. Whoever bound the king's line the first time—whenever that was—they wrote down how it was done. And someone at Thornfell has been copying them."

The wind gusted. She pulled the too-large coat tighter around herself, and Sef's eyes caught on the shape beneath the oilcloth at her ribs, the rectangular weight of a book, and he understood.

"You have one," he said. "That's why the hunters want you alive."

"They want me because I lit forty candles with my mind," she said, but her hand had gone to the book, protective, and she knew he'd seen it, and something in her jaw set like she'd been caught and had decided not to care. "The book is mine. I copied it. I paid for it in eleven years of my life."

"I'm not here to take it."

"Everyone is here to take something."

He could not argue with that. He looked out at the gully, at the snow lying blue in the shadow of the far wall, and told her the rest—the part he had told no one, the part that had made the Lord Inquisitor's face go smooth. About the thirteenth moon. About what the old texts said a binding became, if it was fed long enough. About oaths as food.

When he finished, she was silent for a long moment. Then she said, "You're mad."

"Probably."

"You're asking me to believe the king is dead, or worse, and that some—*thing* is wearing him, and that it eats loyalty out of people like marrow out of bone, and that in—" her lips moved, counting, "—six weeks it becomes permanent, and that a book I copied by candlelight for a debt I never agreed to is the only instruction manual left in the world."

"Yes," Sef said. "When you say it all at once, it does sound mad."

"Good. Because I'm not going to believe you." She stood, brushing snow from her coat, and he saw how thin she was under the stolen gray, how the trembling in her hands was cold and fear and something else, something banked like a coal. "I'm going to the Widow's Notch, because the low road passes through it and the Notch comes out on the salt road, and the salt road is the only way off this mountain that hunters don't watch. You can come or not. But if you come, you walk behind me. And if your map and my memory disagree—"

"They won't," Sef said.

She stopped. Looked back at him.

"The low road," she said. "The split stone. The north face where the snow lies longest." Her eyes had gone narrow and bright. "Where did you get that route? It's not on any chart I've ever seen. It's not on *any* chart."

"It was on this one." He held up the half-burned sheet, and for the first time in eleven days he felt something that was not fear: a small, stubborn, unreasonable hope. "Burned, but not gone. I've been walking it from memory. The memory of a map I drew from a smuggler's confession, twenty years old, taken from a prisoner who died in the cells."

"Whose confession?"

"A woman called Ostwick. Maren Ostwick."

The change in her face was extraordinary and brief—a whole weather system passing in the space of a breath—before she shuttered it. "Maren taught me the roads herself," she said. "Every one. When I was fourteen."

"Then we've been walking the same road twice," Sef said, "once in ink and once in blood. And neither of us has been caught yet."

* * *

**Astrid**

They walked, and she made him walk behind her, and for three hours neither of them spoke.

It was not the comfortable silence of the scriptorium, where silence had been a shared devotion, forty scribes breathing ink and beeswax in common. This silence was a negotiation, conducted in the language of footfalls: his a half-step back and to her left, always, never crowding, never falling so far behind that she had to wonder where he was. She noticed that. She noticed it the way she noticed everything now, her senses flayed open by fear, and she resented how carefully he moved, because it made him harder to hate.

The Notch came at dusk, a blade-cut between two shoulders of the mountain where the wind funneled through with a sound like a choir holding one note forever. Snow lay in the cut in long blue drifts. And there, half-buried at the entrance, was the cairn—three stones stacked by some traveler long dead, the marker Maren had made her memorize: *when you see the three brothers, the hard part's done.*

Astrid stopped and looked at the cairn and felt, absurdly, like weeping.

"Ostwick's route," Sef said quietly, behind her. "The cairn's on the chart. Someone added it in a different hand. Older ink."

"She added it herself. She went back and marked it after—" Astrid stopped. After what? After the year Maren had walked it, whatever year that had been, carrying whatever Maren had carried. There was so much about Maren she had never asked, because asking cost coin in that world, and Astrid had never had coin to spare. "It doesn't matter. It's here. Your dead prisoner's confession and my memory agree."

"That's the second time today the map and the memory have matched," Sef said. He came up beside her—she let him, which surprised her—and stood looking at the Notch with an expression she was learning to read: the look of a man whose whole life had been the business of putting the world on paper, confronting the possibility that the world had been lying to him in a very specific and deliberate way. "The seal-line, the routes. Everything the crown erased, someone kept. In tea spilled on a table. In a smuggler's confession. In a book copied eleven times over by candlelight." He glanced at her. "They burned the archive, but they didn't burn it out of the world. It's still in people. In you."

"Don't," Astrid said.

"I didn't mean—"

"You meant exactly that. That I'm a *vessel* now. That my eleven years of indenture were actually a *gift* to the cause." Her voice came out low and shaking, and she hated it. "I didn't choose any of this. I was sold to Thornfell at nine years old for a debt I never saw the ledger of. I copied heretical texts because the abbot told me to and memorized smuggler roads because Maren paid the abbot to let her teach me and I never once, not once, got to decide what my own hands learned. So don't tell me it's in me like it's a *blessing*. It's in me like a scar is in me. Like a chain."

The wind took her words and flung them down the Notch. Sef said nothing for a while. When he spoke, his voice was rough.

"I know what it is to have your work turned against you," he said. "I drew maps my whole life so people wouldn't get lost. And the last thing I did with them was help a man hunt other men." He looked down at his hands, ink-stained too, she noticed. Ink-stained the same as hers. "I'm not asking you to be a vessel. I'm asking you to walk with me as far as the salt road. After that—" he shrugged, and it was a real shrug, defeated and honest, "—after that you can disappear, and I'll hope the hunters are slower than you are."

Astrid looked at him for a long moment. The light was going. His face was half shadow, and there was a burn scar along his jaw she hadn't noticed before, old and thin, and his eyes were the eyes of a man who had been in irons eleven days ago and had not yet stopped being surprised to see the sky.

"The salt road," she said at last. "Then we find Maren. She has a house below the timberline at Coldharbor. If anyone can hide us, she can. If she's still—"

She stopped. Sef waited.

"If she's still there," Astrid finished, in a different voice.

They went down into the Notch together, the wind screaming its one held note around them, and Astrid walked first because she had decided to, not because she had to, and the difference felt like a door opening in a wall she had stopped seeing.

Behind them, far down the valley, a hunting horn sounded. Once. Then, after a pause too long to be an echo, again.

Sef's hand found her sleeve. "They've picked up the trail."

"Then we move," Astrid said, and thought of the book warm against her ribs, and of Maren's tea-spilled table, and of the thirteenth moon rising somewhere beyond the mountains, six weeks away and climbing. "And this time, cartographer, we don't stop to talk."
