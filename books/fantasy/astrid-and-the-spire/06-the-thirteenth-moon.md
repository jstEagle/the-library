## 6. The Thirteenth Moon

The fire had burned down to a red heart by the time Sef finally spoke, and even then he did not look up from the chart he was mending.

He had been mending it for two days. That was the truth of it—two days of rice paste and thin strips of linen, of tweezers made from split goose quills, of hands that had once redrawn coastlines for a king now trembling over a scrap of vellum no bigger than a prayer book. The chart had come out of the fire at Thornfell half-consumed. Its western third was gone entirely, eaten to a lace of char. What remained was enough. It had always been enough. That was the problem with knowing things: you could not unknow them, and you could not unburn them either, though he had tried both.

Astrid sat across the fire, sharpening a knife they had bought in Lowmark with coin Sef suspected was stolen. They sharpened it the way they did everything—efficiently, without pleasure, as if even violence were a chore on a ledger. *Debt* was the word Sef would have used, if he were the sort to psychoanalyze smugglers. Everything about Astrid Galloway moved like someone paying down a balance: quick, precise, incurious about anything that didn't reduce the sum owed.

"The moon," Sef said.

Astrid's knife paused. "What about it."

"It's waxing." He turned the chart toward the light, angling it so the surviving corner caught the orange glow. "It'll be full in four days. Then it wanes. And on the turn after next—" he tapped the vellum, once, "—it will be the thirteenth moon since the king last appeared in public."

"I know how moons work."

"Do you?" He hadn't meant it cruelly, but it came out that way, the way most true things did when they'd been sitting in his chest too long. "Then you know we have six weeks."

"Six weeks until what?"

Sef set down his tweezers. His hands wanted something to do, so he gave them the wine skin instead, and drank, and found the wine tasted of smoke and cheap plums and the particular sourness of a story he had told no one, not whole, not ever.

"Until it stops being him," he said.

---

He told it badly at first. He knew that even as the words came out. He was a cartographer; he thought in lines and elevations, in the clean grammar of *here* and *not-here*, and what he was trying to describe had no edges. It was like being asked to draw fog.

"You've seen the king," he began. "At the Procession of Banners, three years ago. Everyone saw him."

"I saw a man on a horse at a distance," Astrid said. "I was copying punishment psalms that week. We got an hour out of doors."

"Then you saw what everyone saw. A tall man, grey at the temples, wearing the circlet. Waving with the right hand—he always waved with the right hand, there are forty years of portraits showing it. And three years ago, at that procession, I was standing close enough to see the hand." Sef turned his own right hand over, studied the palm as though the memory were written there. "It waved left-handed. The fingers bent wrong. Like a puppeteer testing strings he hadn't pulled before."

"And you reported this."

"I drew it." He laughed, and hated the sound. "That's worse. You don't understand what the map rooms are. You think we make maps. We make *records*. Every surveyor's mark, every seal-line, every boundary stone—I was trained to notice what doesn't belong. And the king's seal—" he pulled the ruined chart into his lap again, careful as a priest handling relics, "—the royal seal is drawn onto every charter, every treaty, every grant of land. It's a line of oath-script wound around the crown device. Do you know what oath-script is?"

"Temple scribe," Astrid said dryly. "Guess."

"Then you know it's not decoration. Oath-script is binding language. It's how vows are made to hold—marriage oaths, fealty oaths, the coronation vow itself. Every subject born under the crown swears something into it, whether they know it or not. Your grandfather swore. Your mother swore. The tithe-oaths, the levy-oaths, the little promises at every border crossing—*so help me the crown*—they all feed the same line." He traced it with one fingertip, hovering above the charred vellum. "For six hundred years that line has been a ledger of what the kingdom owes itself. And three years ago, I noticed the ledger was being *read*."

The fire popped. Somewhere down the valley a fox screamed, high and human, and fell silent.

"Being read," Astrid repeated. Not a question. A test. Sef had learned that tone from them—the flat voice they used when they were weighing whether a thing deserved their belief, and he understood suddenly that they weighed everything, that trust for Astrid Galloway was a currency counted out in farthings.

"There's a way of writing," Sef said, "that the old priests used. Binding script. It's in the oldest cartographic conventions—why do you think maps were holy objects once? Why do you think drawing a border wrong was heresy? Because a map is an oath made visible. And whatever sits on the throne now reads oath-script the way you read your ledger margins. Fluently. Hungrily." He looked up. "It's eating them, Astrid. The oaths. All of them. Six hundred years of inherited promise, poured into one vessel."

Silence. Astrid set the knife down across their knee, blade flat, deliberate—a gesture Sef had catalogued as *patience running out*.

"So the king is dead," they said, "and a—what? A demon? A ghost? Some creature out of a heresy I copied twice and burned twice—is sitting on the throne signing treaties with a borrowed hand. And you're the only man in the kingdom who noticed because it waved with the wrong hand."

"Yes."

"That's madness."

"It's also why the crown wants me dead," Sef said quietly. "Which I'd suggest is evidence in my favor."

---

But Astrid still didn't believe him. Sef could see it—not disbelief exactly, but the refusal to let belief land, the way a person refuses to step on ice they can hear cracking. They banked the fire instead. They checked the horses. They walked the perimeter of their little hollow below the ridge where the smuggler road bent north, and came back, and sat down, and said nothing for a long time.

It was the chart that broke them. Sef hadn't planned it that way; he'd simply reached for it when the cold got into his knees, meaning to put it away, and Astrid said, "Let me see it."

"Not the front. The burn-through."

They took it the way they took everything—with competent, ungentle hands—and turned it toward the firelight, and Sef watched their face while they read.

He watched the color leave it.

Because the back of the chart was where he'd kept his real notes. Marginalia, in his own cramped surveyor's hand, dated and cross-referenced the way the map rooms demanded: dates of the king's public appearances against lunar turns. The frequency of royal audiences, declining month over month, in a curve that matched nothing natural. The harvest ledgers—grain quotas rising, granary records sealed, whole districts written down as *rendered unto the crown* in oath-script that predated the dynasty's own conventions. And at the bottom, in ink Sef had mixed himself from oak gall and ash because he trusted no other hand near it:

*The binding completes at the thirteenth turn. After that, no unbinding. The oaths will be permanent. The kingdom will be a mouth that cannot stop swallowing.*

Astrid read it twice. Their lips moved on the second pass. Sef realized they were checking his arithmetic—and that the arithmetic was right, and that this was the moment the ice cracked, because Astrid Galloway might distrust men and gods and crowns, but they did not distrust numbers.

"Six weeks," Astrid said. Their voice had gone very quiet. "You wrote this three years ago."

"A year ago. The observations go back three."

"A year ago." They lowered the chart. In the firelight their eyes had a shine to them that was not quite tears and not quite fury, and Sef thought: *there it is*, the thing under all that weary competence, the engine of them. "A year ago you knew. A year ago you ran. And the archive at Thornfell—the books I spent seven years copying, the ones the abbot burned—they were about this. Binding script. Unbinding rites. That's why the crown hunted them. That's why the hunt is happening *now*."

"They're clearing the field," Sef said. "Everything that could name it, everything that could loose it. The gifted, the old priests, the texts. It's not paranoia, Astrid. It's pest control. It's making sure that when the thirteenth moon comes, no one left alive remembers there was ever another way to swear an oath."

Astrid stood up. Walked to the edge of the hollow. Stood looking north, where the mountains went black against a sky already salted with stars, and when they spoke again their voice was steady in the way that Sef had learned meant the opposite of calm.

"My debt," they said. "Seven years of copying, and the abbot sold my name anyway. I thought it was greed. I thought it was just Thornfell being Thornfell." A breath. "It was orders. The monastery wasn't saving itself—it was feeding the hunt. And every text I memorized, every road I mapped in my head to pay off a debt that was never going to end—" they turned, and the starlight caught the hard line of their jaw, "—none of it was ever mine to buy free of. It was a cage they built around a person who could read the lock."

Sef said nothing. There was nothing to say that the night wasn't already saying better.

"So six weeks." Astrid came back to the fire and crouched, forearms on knees, close enough that Sef could see the ember-light moving in their eyes. "And you have half a chart, and I have a head full of forbidden roads, and between us we have—" they gestured at the dark, at the two tired horses, at the whole indifferent wilderness, "—this."

"We have more than that." Sef reached into his coat and brought out the thing he had been carrying against his ribs since the map rooms, wrapped in oilcloth, warm from his body. He unwrapped it slowly. Inside lay a second piece of vellum, small, intact, unburned—the one thing he had saved whole. On it, in faded brown ink, was a list of names. Priests' names. And beneath them, a place-name rendered in the old convention, half-erased but legible to anyone who had spent years reading the grammar of borders:

*Hollowmere.*

"The priests who bound the crown the first time," Sef said. "If it can be bound, it can be loosed. If anyone alive remembers how—it's them."

Astrid stared at the vellum a long moment. Then they laughed—one short, sharp note with no humor in it at all—and rubbed a hand across their face.

"A smuggler road through country nobody maps, to find priests the crown erased, to fight a thing wearing the king's face, in six weeks." They looked at Sef across the dying fire. "Do you know what Maren Ostwick would charge for a job like this?"

"Everything," Sef said.

"Everything," Astrid agreed. And then, horribly, they smiled—the first real smile Sef had seen from them, crooked and exhausted and lit from underneath by something that dread had not managed to kill. "Good. I'm tired of things I can't afford."

They kicked dirt over the embers, and the dark came down complete, and above the black teeth of the ridge the moon rose fat and white and mercilessly, beautifully full—counting, Sef thought, counting down.

* * *

They rode north before dawn, following a road that existed only in Astrid's memory and Sef's worst fears, and somewhere past the second river ford Astrid reined in and pointed east, where the sky had begun to bruise with light.

"Smoke," they said.

Sef saw it a moment later: a thin grey thread standing up off the horizon, straight in the windless morning, too deliberate to be a chimney and too steady to be a wildfire. It rose from the direction of the low country behind them. From the direction, though he could not have said how he knew, of everything they had left burning.

"Hunters?" he asked.

"Worse." Astrid's face, in the first grey light, had gone still and old. "That's the direction of Lowmark. We sold the horses' winter shoes there two days ago. Paid with coin, gave our descriptions to a farrier who talks to everyone." They turned to Sef, and their voice dropped into the flat, clipped register of a smuggler naming a price. "Someone's burning the places we've been. Working backward along our trail, town by town. Which means they're not tracking us anymore, Sef."

"Then what are they doing?"

Astrid nudged their horse forward, toward the smoke-stained horizon, and answered without turning around.

"Closing the net."
