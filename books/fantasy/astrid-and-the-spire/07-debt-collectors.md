## 7. Debt Collectors

The name on the ledger was hers.

Astrid knew it before the peddler said a word, the way you know weather coming in your knees. He sat across the fire at the waystation below the pass, warming his hands, talking too much the way tired men do, and somewhere between the price of salt and the state of the roads he mentioned that Thornfell had opened its books. That the abbot, in his wisdom, had sold what he could not burn.

"Names," the peddler said, and spat into the coals. "Gifted names. The crown pays by the head now. Twenty crowns, I heard, and more if the name comes with a face and a road."

Astrid kept her eyes on her cup. Steam rose off it in a thin gray thread, and she watched it instead of watching him, because watching him would have been the same as confessing.

"They say one of them was a scribe," he went on. "Little mouse of a thing, ink to the elbows. Lit up every candle in the scriptorium the night the hunters came, so bright they saw it from the valley. Abbot Corvin wept when he gave the name over. Wept like a man selling his own daughter."

*Like a man settling accounts,* Astrid thought. *Weeping is just interest.*

She paid for her tea with smuggler coin—Maren Ostwick's coin, worn smooth from a hundred hands—and left before the fire burned down. Behind her the waystation door groaned shut, and she did not look back at it, because looking back was a habit of people who still had somewhere to return to.

Sef was waiting where she'd told him to wait, in the stand of black pines above the road, his half-burned charts spread across a flat stone like a patient awaiting surgery. He'd weighted the corners with pebbles. Even now, even hunted, he could not bear to see a map curl.

"Well?" he said.

"She's real," Astrid said. "Veyra. The captain. The peddler talked about her like other men talk about storms—half fear, half admiration. She doesn't burn villages. She reads them." Astrid crouched beside the stone, tracing the pass with one finger. "She'll have my name by now. Bought and paid for. And mine comes with something the others don't."

"What?"

"A debt ledger. Fifteen years of entries. Every road I copied, every letter I carried, every errand the monastery ran through the pass. My whole life is written down in columns, Sef. Veyra won't have to hunt me. She'll only have to read me."

Sef was quiet for a moment. In the dark his face was all angles and shadow, the face of a man who had spent years making himself accurate and been punished for it. "Then we make sure the reading ends badly," he said. "Wrong turns. False trails. Give her a story so tidy she believes it."

"I know a place," Astrid said, and hated how much she knew it, how the knowledge sat in her like swallowed stones. "There's a stretch of the old salt road where the mountain has been trying to die for a hundred years. The rock is rotten there. Maren used to route caravans around it in spring melt, never through. But the crown couriers go through, because their maps say it's safe."

"My maps say it's safe," Sef said slowly.

"Your maps are the king's maps. And the king—" Astrid stopped. Saying it aloud still felt like stepping off a ledge. "The king isn't the king. Maybe his maps lie too."

They looked at each other across the flat stone, across the ghost-lines of roads neither of them could walk openly anymore, and Astrid felt the strange cold clarity that had been growing in her since the night every candle in the scriptorium had answered her fear. Grief and purpose, braided tight. You could not pull one without the other coming loose.

"The rockslide," she said. "Two travelers, dead in the fall. A scribe and a cartographer, fleeing north. Let Veyra find exactly what she was promised."

* * *

They spent two days preparing, and Astrid learned that fraud, done properly, is its own kind of scholarship.

Sef taught her how a cartographer thinks: what a searcher looks for first, second, third. A dropped map is worth ten confessions, but only if it's *his* map—so he drew one fresh, an honest copy of his own hand, marked with the passes they'd actually walked, and then aged it with ash water and folded it wrong on purpose, the way a frightened man folds things. Astrid contributed her scribe's arts: a journal, written in her true hand, entries trailing off mid-sentence three days back, the last line a smear where a raindrop—or a tear—had fallen. She wrote herself frightened. It was not difficult. She only had to stop pretending she wasn't.

The hardest part was the blood.

"You don't have to watch," Sef said, knife out, forearm bared over the rocks at the slide's upper lip.

"Yes I do," Astrid said. "If I'm going to kill myself on this mountain, I want to meet the body."

He cut shallow and let it drip onto the scree, onto the broken slate that would carry the scent down to whatever dogs Veyra ran. His blood was very red against the gray stone, absurdly vivid, and Astrid found herself thinking of wax seals, of oaths signed in the old way, and pushed the thought away before it could grow teeth.

Her own turn came after. She cut along the heel of her palm, where the skin was already calloused from fifteen years of pens, and pressed her hand flat against the rock as if taking its pulse. The mountain was cold under her palm. Colder than any living thing should be, and yet she felt it—the faintest tremor, deep down, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

And beneath her hand, unbidden, the gift stirred.

It was small, this time. Contained. A candle-flame of warmth that ran from her palm into the stone, and for one breath the mountain *leaned toward her*, the way a horse leans toward a known voice. The scree above the cut shifted with a sound like a long exhale. Somewhere far below, a pebble clicked against another pebble.

Sef went white. "Astrid."

"I didn't mean to."

"I know. That's what frightens me." He wrapped his hand in linen, moving carefully, the way men move around wounded animals. "You asked a dying mountain to fall, and it said yes. Do you understand what that means? Everything alive answers you. Everything. Including things that are falling."

Astrid looked at the slope, at the wound they'd made in it, and felt the truth of his words settle into her chest like a swallowed coal. She had spent fifteen years believing her gift was nothing—a flicker, a trick, candles and hearth-coals. But candles were only small fires, and fires were only small mountains, and everything in the world that burned or broke or bloomed was apparently listening for her voice.

"Then we'd better be gone before it decides to give me more than I asked for," she said.

* * *

They laid the false camp in the lee of the slide itself, close enough that any searcher would have to climb past the fresh-fallen rock to reach it. A bedroll each. The doctored journal. Sef's planted map, tucked into a saddlebag with the strap torn. Cold ashes from a fire built the proper coward's way—small, shielded, doused in haste. Two tin cups, one chipped. Astrid arranged the cups with a care that surprised her: side by side, handles turned the same way, the way she and Sef had drunk together every morning for a week without ever once discussing it.

"There," she said quietly. "That's us. That's the truest picture of us anyone will ever find."

"It's a lie."

"It's the truest lie I've ever told." She stood, brushing slate dust from her knees. "Everything else about my life was a lie told honestly. This is the reverse. I think I prefer it."

They climbed out the back way, the smuggler's way, a route that existed on no crown chart because Maren Ostwick had paid three men to keep it off every chart ever drawn. It was a goat track, mostly, switchbacking up through the bones of the mountain, and Astrid climbed it with her body remembering what her mind had tried to bury: the weight of contraband letters sewn into her coat lining, the specific silence of moving through places you're not supposed to be. Fifteen years of being owned had at least taught her how to be invisible. She intended to be excellent at it.

From the high shoulder, near dusk, they looked back.

The salt road wound below them like a gray ribbon someone had dropped. And on it, small with distance but unmistakable, came riders. Six of them, moving in the disciplined spread of professionals, and at their head a figure in a storm-gray cloak who did not ride like a soldier. She rode like a reader—pausing at intervals, bending in the saddle, consulting something.

"Veyra," Sef breathed.

Astrid watched the tiny figure stop at the mouth of the cut. Watched her dismount. Watched her stand for a long moment, head tilted, reading the mountain the way Astrid read a page—top to bottom, hunting the flaw in the text.

Then the captain raised one hand, and her riders fanned out along the slide's edge, and began to climb.

"They'll find the camp within the hour," Sef whispered. "The blood, the journal. By tomorrow she'll report two bodies recovered, or two fugitives lost in the fall. Either way, the hunt for us ends."

"Either way," Astrid echoed. But she did not move. She stood on the shoulder of the mountain and watched Captain Veyra climb toward the false corpses of Astrid Galloway and Sef the cartographer, and what rose in her was not triumph.

It was grief.

It came sideways, the way grief does, disguised as something smaller. It was the chipped tin cup. It was the smell of size and lampblack, the scriptorium's particular darkness at dawn. It was Abbot Corvin's cough in the corridor, the scratch of forty pens, the margin of the debt ledger where she had once, in a childish hand, written *paid in full* next to an entry and been beaten for the presumption. It was the archive burning, and the one book against her ribs even now, wrapped in oilcloth, warm from her own body. It was the whole small, cruel, familiar world that had owned her, that had sold her, that she would never—

Never what? Never see again? Never be allowed back into?

Never *be*, again. Because Astrid Galloway, scribe of Thornfell, debtor, mouse, was dead on a mountainside as of tonight, officially, irrevocably, twenty crowns' worth of dead. Whatever walked away from this shoulder was something new and unnamed, and names cost, and she had just spent the last one she had.

Sef touched her arm. "We should go. If she's half as good as you say, she'll check the high routes too."

"In a moment." Astrid wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist, roughly, angry at the tears for arriving without permission. "Let me finish burying her."

Below, a rider's shout drifted up, thin with distance. They'd found the camp. Somewhere down there, Veyra was holding Astrid's forged journal, reading her invented fear, believing—in a moment, any moment now—that she had closed the account.

*Rest well, Captain,* Astrid thought. *The ledger says I'm dead. For once, the ledger and I agree.*

She turned from the view and climbed, and the mountain took them in, fold after fold, the way it takes in everything eventually.

* * *

Three days later, in the high country above the tree line, the mountain called in its debt.

Astrid felt it first as a wrongness in the wind—a held breath, a pause between verses. Then the ground spoke, a low grinding note she felt in her teeth before she heard it with her ears, and Sef shouted something that the wind tore apart, and the slope above the goat track moved.

Not much. Not the killing torrent she had begged from it weeks ago. Just a tongue of scree, a few tons of rotten slate deciding, all at once, to come down.

But the track was narrow, and the drop below was long, and there was nowhere to be.

Astrid did not think. Thinking was for people with time. She threw herself at Sef, both hands out, palms flat against the broken rock of the slope, and she *spoke*—not words, nothing so orderly—to the mountain, to the falling stone, the way you shout at a rearing horse, the way you grab a child's sleeve. *No. Not him. Not here. Settle.*

The gift went out of her like a flood through a broken door, vast and cold and ancient, and for one suspended heartbeat she was the mountain: she was ten thousand years of patient pressure, she was frost-wedged seams and sleeping roots, she was enormous and slow and utterly indifferent to two small warm lives on a path. And then—because it was her voice, however small, however borrowed—the indifference bent. The scree shuddered. Hesitated. Poured itself into the empty bend of the track behind them instead of across it, a roaring curtain of stone that sealed the road they'd come by and missed them by the width of a prayer.

Silence. Dust, hanging gold in the alpine light. Sef on his back, staring at her, breathing in ragged pulls.

"You stopped a rockslide," he said finally. "With your hands."

"With the mountain's hands." Astrid looked down at her palms. They were bleeding where the slate had bitten, and shaking, and beneath the shake she could still feel it—that vast attention, receding now, like a tide going out. Like something that had noticed her, and would remember. "I asked it to spare us and it... listened. Sef, it *listened*. Do you know how long I've been talking to things that couldn't hear me? Candles. Coals. Ink drying. All those years, whispering, and nothing ever whispered back."

"And now everything does." He got up slowly, dusting slate from his shoulders, and looked at the wall of fresh-fallen rock blocking the track. His expression was complicated—gratitude braided with something very like alarm. "You realize what you've done. Veyra's report says we died in a slide. And you've just..." He gestured at the sealed road. "...confirmed it. Spectacularly. No one will ever dig through that looking for survivors. As far as the world knows, the mountain finished the job."

Astrid laughed. It came out cracked and wet and startled her with its sound—the first laugh since the scriptorium, maybe the first honest one in fifteen years. "So we're dead twice over. Once for the crown, once for the mountain."

"Dead people travel light," Sef said. "No names to carry. No debts." He paused, and something gentler moved through his exhaustion. "You're free, Astrid. Actually free. Doesn't it feel—"

"No," she said, before he could finish, because the word rising in her wasn't *free*, it was *unmoored*, and she didn't trust her voice with it yet. She looked back the way they'd come, at the wall of stone where the road used to be, and past it, down through the haze of distance, to where Thornfell must be—its bell tower, its smoke, its forty pens scratching in the dark. The life she could never return to. The life that had never truly been hers.

What she felt was not freedom. It was the feeling of a book whose final page has been torn out: the story continues, but the ending is gone, and she would spend whatever remained of her life wondering what it would have said.

"It feels like being dead," she said quietly. "Ask me again when I've learned to like it."

They went on, north, along a track that no longer appeared on any map—crown or smuggler or otherwise. Behind them the mountain kept its silence, keeping its secret, and somewhere far below, a woman in a storm-gray cloak was writing two names into a ledger with a steady hand, closing accounts that were, though she could not yet know it, very far from settled.

By evening they reached the tree line, and by full dark, the first lights of the low country showed ahead—scattered, warm, human. Somewhere among them waited a dying fence named Maren Ostwick, a confession, and a debt of a different kind entirely.

Astrid squared her shoulders and walked down into the lamplight, a dead woman carrying a living book against her ribs, toward whatever the next account demanded.
