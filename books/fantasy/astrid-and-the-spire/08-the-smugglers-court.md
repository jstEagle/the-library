## 8. The Smugglers' Court

The rain found them an hour before Saltmere did, coming down the valley in a grey wall that turned the smuggler road into a riverbed and the world into rumor. Astrid walked with her hood up and her hand pressed flat against the satchel at her hip, where Maren's letter rode against her spine like a second heartbeat. Behind her, Sef picked his way over the wet stones with a cartographer's caution, one hand out for balance, the other keeping the oilskin-wrapped charts dry against his chest.

"You said she'd be expecting us," he called over the rain.

"I said she'd be expecting *me*." Astrid didn't turn. "Whether she's still—"

She didn't finish it. Some sentences you leave half-built, the way you leave a wall unfinished when you're not sure the house will stand.

Saltmere wasn't on any map Sef had ever drawn, and Astrid knew that pleased him in a way he'd never say aloud. It was less a village than an argument between the mountain and the river—a scatter of slate-roofed houses clinging to the valley's throat, chimneys smoking sideways in the wind, a single bridge over white water. The kind of place the crown's criers never reached, where the king's face on the proclamations was just another stranger's face, and nobody knelt to strangers.

Maren Ostwick kept her court above the cooper's shop, at the top of a stair that smelled of wet iron and tallow. Astrid climbed it with her heart climbing faster, and at the top she stopped, because the door stood open, and the room beyond was wrong.

She had pictured it a hundred times on the road. Maren at her ledger desk, sharp-eyed, sharp-tongued, a pot of barley tea already steeping because Maren Ostwick missed nothing, least of all the hour a tired girl would come dragging up her stairs. She had pictured the old woman's voice first, dry as slate: *You're late, Galloway. You're always late.*

Instead the room was dim. The ledgers were closed. And Maren lay propped in the great bed by the window—her bed, the one no one was ever permitted to sit on, the one she'd once claimed she'd die in her boots before—wrapped in blankets that had gone grey with age, her hands folded on the coverlet like two birds that had forgotten how to fly.

"Maren."

The old woman's eyes opened. They were still sharp. Whatever the sickness had taken, it hadn't taken that.

"There she is," Maren said. Her voice was smoke and gravel, thinner than Astrid remembered, but the shape of it was the same. "Three days I've been dying, and you pick *now* to come up my stairs. Sit down before you drip on my floor."

Astrid sat. The chair by the bed was already there—already placed, she realized, angled toward the pillow, as if Maren had been waiting for someone to fill it. As if she'd known.

"You got my letter," Astrid said. "The one from Thornfell. Before the raid."

"Got it. Read it. Burned it." Maren's mouth twitched. "That's how you know I read it."

Sef hovered at the doorway, rain still running off him, and Maren's eyes slid past Astrid to take his measure in one long, unblinking look. Astrid watched her do it—the old habit of it, the fence's arithmetic, weighing a man's coat and his boots and the set of his shoulders and arriving at a number.

"Royal work," Maren said. "The ink on his fingers. The way he stands like someone's always about to take the chair out from under him." She looked back at Astrid. "You're running with crown property now."

"Disowned crown property," Sef said.

"They all say that." But she jerked her chin at the stool by the fire. "Sit, mapmaker. You're letting the cold in."

* * *

They dried by the stove while the rain worked at the roof, and Maren's neighbor—a silent, broad-backed woman called Dagny who seemed to have appointed herself nurse—brought barley tea and dark bread and left without a word. Astrid ate because her body demanded it, but her eyes kept going to the bed, to the ledger of Maren's face, reading the entries there: the hollows under the cheekbones. The breath that caught and restarted, like a cart on a bad axle. The hands that had once counted coins faster than any tax clerk in the kingdom now trembled when they lifted the cup.

"How long?" she asked, when Dagny had gone.

Maren shrugged, which in her was a whole oration. "The physician in Lowmark says spring. The physician in Lowmark also says I should stop drinking gin, so you can weigh his opinions accordingly." She sipped her tea. "Winter, if I'm honest. Maybe less. The cough's down in the deep places now."

"You should have sent for me."

"Sent for you *how*? A letter to Thornfell? *Dear Astrid, come home, I'm dying, bring your abbot's permission and your debt ledger.*" Maren snorted, which turned into a cough, which she rode out with her jaw set like a woman refusing to be interrupted even by her own body. "You had your own walls to climb. And now you've climbed them, I see. Run clean out of the monastery with a hunted cartographer and a stolen book in your coat." Her eyes flicked to Astrid's chest, to the shape beneath the wool. "You still carry it like a novice. Against the heart. Anyone frisking you finds it in the first three seconds."

"I'll remember that."

"You won't. You never did." Maren set down the cup. For a moment she just looked at Astrid, and the sharpness in her gaze went soft at the edges, the way a blade goes soft when it's been wrapped in cloth too long. "The proclamation reached Saltmere two days ago. Gift-hunt. Names to be bought, bounties to be paid. Dagny's boy already asked me if I'd heard anything about a scribe from Thornfell." She let that sit. "I told him the crown's coin spends the same whether it comes from honest hands or dirty ones, and he'd best decide which hands he wanted."

"What did he say?"

"He said he'd think on it." Maren's mouth flattened. "Which means half the valley is thinking on it, and the other half already wrote. You can't stay here, girl. Not more than a night, maybe two. I'm sorry for it. I'd have given you the attic and a false name and my own boots if the world were a kinder place."

"I know."

"But you didn't come up those stairs for shelter." Maren's eyes narrowed, and Astrid saw the fence surface fully now, the woman who had spent fifty years knowing what people wanted before they knew it themselves. "You came because you're carrying something, and I don't mean the book. You came because you've heard a word. Hollowmere."

The name went through the room like cold air through a cracked window. Sef's head came up from his tea. Astrid's hands went still in her lap.

"You know it," she said.

"I know what it costs to know it." Maren leaned back against her pillows, and for a moment she looked very old, and very tired, and very much like someone who had been carrying a weight for so long she'd forgotten it was a weight and not simply part of her spine. "Sit properly, both of you. This is a long story, and I've only got the breath for telling it once."

* * *

She told it in pieces, the way old smugglers tell everything—out of order, with the names changed where names didn't matter and the truth left bare where it did.

She told them about the roads first. How she'd learned them from her own mother, who'd learned them from hers, a line of Ostwick women running salt and letters and fugitives over the Thornfell passes since before the passes had names. How the crown's maps showed three roads through the mountains and the truth was there were eleven, and the eight the crown didn't know were the eight that mattered.

She told them about the priests. How, in the years before the purges, before the gift-hunts, there had been an order that kept the old bindings—oath-keepers, the crown called them, though the crown hadn't consulted them in a hundred years. How they'd lived at Hollowmere, in the high valley past the Salt Road, and how they'd been scattered when a king three generations back decided that anyone who could unbind an oath was more dangerous than anyone who could swear one.

"Exiled," Maren said. "Not destroyed. The crown never could find the valley, and the valley never wanted finding. But they kept one thread to the low country. One runner. One fence who could move a letter without the crown's eyes touching it." She looked at Astrid. "You know what a fence does, girl. I don't move goods. I move *trust*. And for forty years, I was the trust between the crown's enemies and the priests who could unmake what the crown had made."

Sef set down his cup. "The binding," he said quietly. "On the king."

Maren's eyes went to him, then to Astrid, and something passed over her face that Astrid had never seen there before—something that looked, impossibly, like fear.

"So you do know." She exhaled, long and slow. "Then you know why the roads matter. And you know why I can't tell you the way to Hollowmere."

Astrid's stomach dropped. "Maren—"

"Because the crown has been hunting that road for three generations, and every soul who's walked it carrying a crown's secret has been followed, and followed, and followed, until the priests sealed the valley tighter than a miser's purse." She held up a trembling hand before Astrid could speak. "I'm not refusing you, girl. I'm telling you the price has gone up. Everything's got a price. You learned that in a monastery, of all places, so don't look at me like that."

"Name it."

Maren was quiet for a long moment. The rain filled the silence, drumming on the slate roof, running in the gutters, and beneath it Astrid could hear the old woman's breath working, the rattle in it now that she listened for it, the sound of a bell with a crack in it.

"Under the bed," Maren said finally. "The third board from the wall. There's a box. Bring it up."

Astrid knelt and worked the board loose with her fingernails. The box beneath was cedar, small, older than she was, its hinges green with age. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that crackled like frost, lay a bundle of letters tied with black cord, and beneath them a single sheet of heavy paper, covered in a cramped, slanting hand.

"That," Maren said, when Astrid had risen with it, "is my confession. Forty years of it. Every name I passed, every road I opened, every oath I helped carry over the mountains—including the ones I carried for the crown when the crown paid better and I told myself the priests would never know." Her voice had gone very flat and very even, the voice of a woman reading from a ledger she'd memorized long ago. "There are names in there that would hang half the smuggler court if it fell into the wrong hands. There are names in there that would hang *me*, if I had time left to hang."

"Maren—"

"Carry it to Hollowmere." The old woman's eyes were bright and wet and absolutely steady. "Give it into the hands of their oracle herself, and no one else. Not a priest, not a runner, not a pretty voice in a doorway. The oracle. And when you've done that, when you've put my forty years into her hands, then they'll hear what you've come to say. That's the price. That's the only price I'll take."

"Why?" Sef asked. It wasn't challenge; it was genuine confusion, and Astrid loved him a little for asking it plainly. "Why does a confession buy a road?"

"Because trust isn't given, mapmaker. It's *paid*." Maren turned back to Astrid. "Because I've spent forty years being the door between the crown and the priests, and a door that's only ever opened one way is just a wall with hinges. Because when I'm in the ground, the only person left alive who knows how the low country and Hollowmere spoke to each other will be a dead woman, and dead women's debts get collected by whoever finds the paper." She reached out, and her hand—light as a bird, light as ash—closed around Astrid's wrist. "Because you're the last person I'll ever trust with anything, Astrid Galloway, and I would rather my truth die in the right hands than live in the wrong ones."

Astrid looked down at the box in her lap. The letters. The confession. The weight of forty years, tied with black cord.

"I'm not a priest," she said. "I'm not even—Maren, I'm a debtor. I'm a scribe with a price on her head and a gift I can't control, and you're asking me to carry the one document in the kingdom that could burn everything you built—"

"I'm asking you to do what you've always done." Maren's grip tightened, and for a moment her strength came back, all of it, the whole iron spine of her. "Carry what matters. Over the passes. Through the weather. Past the hunters. That's all a courier ever does, girl. The only difference is what's in the bag." She released Astrid's wrist and fell back against the pillows, breath rattling, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped to almost nothing. "And one thing more. When you give it to the oracle—when you've done that—ask her about the Ostwicks. Ask her what the name means to the order. She'll know. She's known for forty years, and she's never once sent word, and I want to know—" the rattle in her breath caught, "—I want to know if I was forgiven before I go asking forgiveness myself."

* * *

They left at first light, because Maren insisted, because the valley was already talking, because staying was a luxury the rain couldn't buy them. Dagny packed them bread and cheese and a skin of gin "for the cough or the cold, whichever kills you slower," and Maren watched from the window, propped up in her grey blankets, as Astrid buckled the satchel with the confession riding against her spine.

"Third board from the wall," Astrid said. "The box. I'll bring it back empty."

"You'll bring nothing back." Maren's voice was thin in the cold morning air. "You'll send word by the roads I taught you, and it'll say one word—*paid*—and I'll die a settled woman. That's all the return I want on forty years of freight." She paused. "Astrid."

Astrid looked up. The old woman's face was pale in the grey light, and for a moment—just a moment—the fence was gone, and the ledger was closed, and what looked down from the window was simply an old woman, dying, looking at the girl she'd taught to read a mountain the way other people taught children to read a prayer book.

"You were the best thing I ever smuggled," Maren said. "Out of that monastery. Over those passes. Into the world. Don't you dare let the crown collect you."

Astrid stood in the mud of the lane with the rain just beginning again, fine and cold, and found she had no words that were large enough. So she did the only thing she could think of: she pressed her fist to her heart, the old courier's sign, the one Maren had taught her at fourteen on the first road they'd ever walked together—*the load is safe, the road is true*—and held it there until Maren raised her own trembling hand and returned it.

Then they went up out of the valley, north along the river, toward the Salt Road and the high passes, with forty years of confession riding against Astrid's spine and the thirteenth moon swelling somewhere above the clouds, unseen but counting down all the same.

Sef didn't speak until the bridge was behind them and the valley had folded itself away.

"She said ask about the Ostwicks," he said. "What do you think she meant?"

Astrid walked a dozen steps before she answered, and when she did, her voice came out strange to her own ears, quiet and rough.

"I think," she said, "that Maren Ostwick has spent forty years being a door, and doors don't get to ask what's on the other side of them." She tightened the satchel strap. "I think we're about to find out what she was carrying."
