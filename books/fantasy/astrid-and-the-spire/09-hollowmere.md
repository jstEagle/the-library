## 9. Hollowmere

The road to Hollowmere did not exist.

Sef had known this the way he knew most things—by paper. In the royal cartography office, in the years before his disgrace, he had drawn the northern reaches of the Thornfell range a dozen times, and every version showed the same blank: a fold of grey mountains above the salt cliffs, unmarked, unlabeled, annotated only with the surveyor's dry note *barren scree; no water; no value*. He had copied that note faithfully, because faithful copying was what a royal cartographer was for. It had not occurred to him then that a kingdom might have roads it paid its mapmakers never to see.

Now he walked one of them, and understood why the crown had wanted them erased.

The path climbed through fog so thick it seemed less like weather than like intention—a deliberate blinding, laid over the mountain by hands patient enough to wait out centuries. The stones underfoot were fitted, not scattered. Someone had paved this track and then let the moss take it back, keeping just enough of it alive to be passable to those who knew where to put their feet. Astrid knew. Of course Astrid knew; Maren Ostwick's confession-letter was sewn into the lining of their coat, and Maren's roads lived in Astrid's memory the way scripture lives in a priest's.

"Left at the split birch," Astrid said, without turning. Their voice came muffled through the fog. "Then you stop following the trail and start following the water."

"There's no water."

"There will be." A pause, and then, softer, almost apologetic: "There always is, if the priests built it. They liked their priories to sing."

Sef didn't ask what she meant. He had learned, in the weeks since the pass, since the rockslide, since the first kiss neither of them had mentioned again—that asking Astrid Galloway to explain something before they were ready was a good way to receive silence instead of an answer. So he kept walking, one hand on the cold leather tube slung across his back where the half-burned charts rode against his spine, and listened.

And then he heard it.

Water—but wrong. Too regular. It fell somewhere ahead of them in long measured threads, each drop striking stone at the same interval, so that the mountain itself seemed to keep time. Beneath it, fainter, a second sound: voices. Not conversation. Repetition. A phrase spoken over and over by many throats slightly out of phase, the way rain sounds like a word if you listen long enough.

Astrid stopped. Sef felt them stop more than saw it—the fog swallowed everything past three arm-lengths—and when they spoke again their voice had changed. Smaller. Younger.

"I used to copy their hymns," Astrid said. "In the archive. There was a fragment—eleven lines, half of it water-damaged. I thought it was about a river. I transcribed it forty times before I realized it wasn't a hymn at all." A breath. "It was an instruction manual."

"For what?"

"For putting something down," Astrid said, "and making sure it stayed down."

* * *

The priory announced itself the way a wound announces itself under clothing: not by appearing but by changing everything around it. The fog thinned all at once, as though parted by a hand, and there it stood—Hollowmere, sunk into a bowl of mountain like a ship settled on the seafloor.

It was built downward. That was the first thing Sef's eye refused and then accepted. Where Thornfell monastery had strained toward heaven with its bell-tower and its gilded spire, Hollowmere turned its back on the sky entirely: tier upon tier of pale stone descending into the earth, courtyards open to the clouds but walls windowless, roofs low, every line of the architecture an argument for looking down. Water threaded the whole structure—channels cut into the stair-rails, fountains at every landing, all of them falling at that same metronome interval. The singing came from below, from whatever depth the priory considered deep enough.

At the top gate stood a single figure. Old, robed in undyed wool gone the color of ash, hands folded. Waiting. As if they had been standing there since dawn. As if they had known.

"You'd be the letter," the priest said, when they came close. His eyes were closed. Blind, then—or pretending, which in a place like this amounted to the same courtesy. His face turned toward Astrid with unsettling precision. "Maren Ostwick's letter. She promised it to us thirty years ago. We had begun to think she meant to die owing us."

"She died owing everyone," Astrid said. "She said you'd understand."

"We do." The blind priest inclined his head, and something in the gesture was so genuinely mournful that Sef felt his suspicion loosen a notch. "Come down. All the way down. What you carry doesn't keep well at altitude."

"What we—" Sef started.

"Both things," the priest said mildly. "The letter. And the girl who lights candles without touching them. Word travels faster than fugitives, children. It has longer legs."

* * *

They descended for the better part of an hour.

Sef counted the tiers as they went—nine landings, nine gates, each one opened by a different silent brother or sister who touched the stone in some sequence too quick to follow. Priests passed them in the corridors, and every one of them was blind. Not old-blind, not all of them; some were young, faces smooth, moving through the dark halls without hesitation, without lanterns, without any need of light at all. Sef found himself watching their hands. Cartographers learned early that people lied with their faces and told the truth with their hands, and the hands of these priests were never still—they read the walls as they walked, fingertips trailing the stone, and Sef slowly understood that they weren't navigating.

They were listening to it. The whole priory was a text, and they were reading it with their skin.

"They gave up their eyes," Astrid murmured, when the corridor finally emptied. "That's the price of the binding arts. You can't unmake a thing while you're still looking at the world it sits inside. You have to look inward instead." A pause. "I read that too. In another fragment. I thought it was poetry."

"Everything you thought was poetry turns out to be weapons-grade," Sef said.

Astrid huffed something that was almost a laugh. It died quickly. They had both been quiet since the rockslide—quiet in the specific way of two people carrying an unspoken thing between them, setting it down carefully each night, picking it up again each morning. Sef told himself the silence was practical. There would be time after Hollowmere, after the ritual, after the entity wearing the king's face had been dragged off the throne it was eating its way into. After, there would be time to say—

He didn't finish the thought. He had learned not to finish that thought.

The last stair ended in a chamber that took his breath and held it.

It was round, and vast, and hollow—hence the name, he thought distantly, hence the whole name—and its floor was a single disc of black stone polished to the depth of a well. Water ran around its rim in a channel, falling at intervals, keeping the time the mountain kept. And rising from the center of the black floor, carved in relief so shallow it could only be read from here, from directly above, was a map.

Sef's heart stopped, started again, and then began to pound.

Not a map of the priory. Not of the mountain. Of the kingdom—the whole of it, coastlines and rivers and the spine of the Thornfells, rendered in stone with a fidelity that made his professional soul ache and his professional pride curdle with shame. Every royal chart he had ever drawn was a child's sketch beside this. And at the center of it, where the capital sat, where the palace sat, where the throne room sat, the stone was worn smooth. Polished. Burnished by touch.

Someone had been touching that spot for a very long time.

"The founders drew it before the city grew," said a voice behind them. "They drew the throne room first, actually. Everything else was added afterward, to give it context."

Sef turned. The blind priest from the gate had followed them down—or had been waiting here; with these people there was no difference. But he was not alone. Beside him stood a figure that made even the ash-robed brothers pause in their passing.

She was ancient in a way that had nothing to do with years. Her robe was white, unstained, unworn, and her face was smooth as the worn stone of the throne-room mark—not young, but *erased*, features softened as though a thumb had smudged them. And her eyes—her eyes were not closed. They were simply gone. Lids sunken over emptiness, healed shut decades ago, the skin there fine and pale as the underside of a leaf.

"This is Sister Vael," the gate-priest said. "Our oracle. The last of the binders who sealed your king's grandfather's crown. She was there, at the first binding. She remembers what it cost."

Vael's head turned toward Astrid. Only Astrid. And when she spoke, her voice was soft and terrible, the voice of someone speaking from the bottom of a well.

"Come here, little candle. Let me read what you are."

* * *

Astrid went. Sef watched them go—watched them cross the black map of the kingdom, boots ringing on the stone, stepping over rivers and provinces and the worn-smooth ghost of the throne—and something in his chest clenched hard enough to hurt. He had spent six weeks telling himself that what he felt for this scribe was respect, gratitude, the ordinary bond of fellow fugitives. Standing here, watching Astrid walk toward a blind oracle across the carved body of the kingdom, he admitted to himself, briefly and privately, that he was lying.

Vael took Astrid's hands in hers. Her fingers were cool. She turned the palms upward, traced the lines of them, and then—gently, questioningly—pressed her own palm flat against Astrid's sternum, over the heart.

She went rigid.

For three full seconds the oracle of Hollowmere did not breathe. Then she exhaled, a long shuddering sound, and stepped back, and her ruined face turned slowly until it faced the ceiling, the sky, the world above—as if checking that it was all still there.

"Well?" the gate-priest asked quietly.

"It's true," Vael said. "The old kind. The first kind. Unbinding." Her voice dropped, and Sef heard something in it he had not expected: fear. Real fear, in a woman who had bound a crown and given her eyes for it. "Do you know how rare this is? Do you know how many of them there have been? I met one, in my novitiate. One, in eighty years. The gift that doesn't add but subtracts. That finds the knot in a thing and loosens it." Her head swung back to Astrid. "You could walk into a binding and it would come apart in your hands like wet rope. Oaths. Wards. Curses. Contracts written in blood and sealed in stone. All of it. Loose."

"That's what we came to hear," Sef said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "Can it be done? The entity on the throne—it's completing its binding at the thirteenth turn. Six weeks. Can she unmake it?"

Vael was silent for a long moment. Around the rim of the chamber, the water fell and fell and fell, keeping its patient time.

"Yes," she said at last. "Yes. It can be done. The rite survives—I am the rite, what's left of it; it lives in me now the way a song lives in the last throat that knows it. She could do it. She is perhaps the only soul alive who could do it." She raised one hand, and the priests along the walls bowed their heads. "But hear the shape of it, children, because I will only say this once, and I want no one leaving this chamber able to claim they misunderstood me."

The temperature of the room seemed to drop. Or perhaps it was only that every blind face had turned toward them at once.

"A binding is a debt," Vael said. "That is the first law and the last law. Nothing is held without cost; nothing is fastened without payment. When we bound the crown, three generations ago, we paid in the only coin strong enough—an oath freely given, by blood that had the right to give it. The heir of that age knelt on this very floor and swore herself to the seal, and her blood went into the circlet, and the thing beneath the crown slept for sixty years." Her empty eyes fixed on Astrid. "To unbind it, the seal must be answered in the same coin. Not taken. Never taken—stolen blood seals nothing, it only feeds the thing worse. It must be *given*. Willingly. By an heir of the royal line. A drop, freely offered, at the moment of unbinding—or the rite fails, and fails catastrophically, and the thirteenth turn completes, and the entity becomes permanent, and every oath in this kingdom belongs to it forever."

Silence. The water kept falling.

"And if there's no willing heir?" Sef heard himself ask. His mouth was dry. "If the princess is drugged in the palace and the king is a hollowed-out puppet and the whole royal line is either dead or owned—what does the rite cost then?"

Vael's ruined face turned toward him, and for a moment he had the obscene feeling that she was seeing him—seeing all of him, the burned charts and the treason and the kiss on the salt road and every cowardly unfinished thought.

"Then it costs the caster everything," she said gently. "Gift and life together. The unbinding takes what the seal lacks. If no heir's blood answers, the caster pays the whole of the debt alone." She paused. "And I should tell you, little candle, before you grow brave in the wrong direction—your gift is worth more than your life. Gifts like yours come once a century. Lives come cheap. Promise me you'll remember which is which."

Astrid said nothing. And Sef, watching their face in the grey light of the hollow chamber, saw something settle there—something quiet and resolved and utterly unbearable—and his stomach dropped through the black map of the kingdom, through the stone, through the mountain, down and down.

Because he knew that look. He had seen it on the faces of men reading their own death warrants, and he had seen it on maps: the look of someone who has already chosen the route, and marked it, and folded the chart away.

An heir's willing blood. Six weeks. A princess locked in a palace of wax and poppy-milk, whom none of them could reach, whom none of them had ever met.

"So we need Ilseva," Sef said aloud, because someone had to say it, because saying it made it a plan instead of a prayer. "We need the princess. Alive, awake, and willing."

Vael smiled then, and it was the saddest thing Sef had ever seen.

"Yes," she said. "And child—by the time you reach her, I suspect she'll need you rather more than you need her. The dreams have started. Even here, even blind, I feel her reaching." Her hand drifted out, unerring, and came to rest on the worn-smooth stone at the center of the map. On the throne room. "Something in that palace has begun to whisper her name."
