## 10. Princess Ilseva

The poppy came in a silver cup at dawn, as it always did.

Ilseva had learned to hold it in her mouth the way she held everything else in this palace—carefully, patiently, without swallowing. She sat by the window with the cup balanced on her knee and let the morning light find her face, because the light was honest even when nothing else was. It came through the east glass in long amber bars and lay across the floor like something spilled, something precious that no one had bothered to gather up.

Below, in the courtyard, the swans were being fed. She could hear the wet slap of their wings on the water of the ornamental pool, the shrill complaints of the chamberlain's dog, the ordinary music of a kingdom that believed itself whole.

She tipped the cup against the window ledge when the guard turned his back, letting the bitter liquid run down into the crack between the stones where a small green weed had taken root. The weed drank it every morning. The weed had grown fat and glossy and strange, its leaves curling like fingers, but it was still a weed, still growing, still reaching for the light.

*So am I,* Ilseva thought. *Whatever they're feeding me, I'm still reaching.*

"Your Highness." Marta, her lady-in-waiting, stood in the doorway with a tray of sweet buns and that particular softness in her voice that was meant to be kindness and had become, over the past year, something closer to management. "You should eat before your father's audience."

"My father isn't holding an audience today," Ilseva said. "He's holding a *receiving*. Audiences are when he listens."

Marta's smile flickered—the smallest tremor, a candle flame disturbed by breath—and then steadied. "Of course. Forgive me."

Ilseva ate one bun to keep them satisfied, chewing slowly, tasting nothing. Everything tasted of nothing now, or of copper, or of the faint sweetness that clung to the inside of the silver cups no matter how many times they were scoured. She had stopped asking for honey. Asking was a door, and doors opened onto corridors where other people made decisions about what you were allowed to want.

She was nineteen years old, and she had not left the palace grounds in fourteen months.

* * *

It had begun, she thought now, with the hands.

Her father had always had restless hands. He drummed them on the council table during tedious petitions; he folded them behind his back on the battlements; he had held her mother's hand at the funeral so tightly that Ilseva, standing beside them both, had seen the knuckles go white as river stones. His hands were never still. A king who was never still, he used to say, is a king who is paying attention.

But the man who sat the throne these days—her father's face, her father's grey-shot beard, her father's voice pitched exactly as memory required—had still hands. They rested on the arms of the throne like objects set down and forgotten. When he gestured, the gesture arrived a half-beat late, as though someone were remembering to move him the way one remembers to water a plant.

And his eyes. That was worse. Her father's eyes had been quick and warm and slightly amused, always, even in grief. These eyes were patient. There was no other word for it. They looked at people the way a ledger looks at numbers.

The first time she had said *Father, are you well?* he had smiled, and the smile had been correct in every particular, and she had felt cold all the way down for three days afterward.

The second time, she hadn't asked. She had watched instead. Watching was safer. Watching was a skill, and like all skills it sharpened with practice, and Ilseva had fourteen months of practice now, and the poppy had taught her the rest: how to be underestimated, how to be soft-voiced and slow-eyed and dismissed from rooms, how to sit in corners embroidering things she never finished while the men of the court spoke over her head as though she were furniture.

They gave her the draught to make her docile. What they had not counted on—or perhaps they simply had not cared, which amounted to the same thing—was that a docile princess is invisible, and an invisible princess sees everything.

She saw that the king no longer visited the royal crypt, though he had gone every seventh day of her childhood to stand before her mother's sarcophagus.

She saw that he no longer touched anyone. Not the queen's sister, not his old hunting companions, not Ilseva herself. Once, when she had kissed his cheek at midwinter, he had gone rigid beneath her lips like a man braced against cold water.

She saw that the oaths had changed. This was the thing she understood last and best, the thing that had finally turned her fear into certainty. Every noble house renewed its oath of fealty each spring, kneeling in the throne room, hand on the crown's own reliquary. She had knelt there herself at fifteen, reciting words older than the walls around her. But this spring, the ceremony had been different. Longer. Quieter. And afterward, the lords had come out of the throne room looking—not frightened, exactly. *Emptier.* Like houses after a moving-out, swept and bare and waiting for whoever came next.

Old Thane Aldric had wept in the corridor afterward, quietly, without knowing why. Ilseva had seen him wipe his eyes with the back of his hand and stare at his own wet fingers as though they belonged to a stranger.

Something was drinking her kingdom. Something that wore her father the way a hand wears a glove.

* * *

In the afternoon they brought her the dispatches to seal.

This was another courtesy that had curdled into control. As the king's only child, she had once handled correspondence freely—signing minor decrees, sealing letters of condolence, the pleasant busywork of a heir learning her trade. Now the stack arrived each afternoon already written, already addressed, requiring only her stamp in pale violet wax, and a guard named Oskar stood by the desk while she worked, watching her hands the way a falconer watches a hawk.

Today's stack was thin. Condolences to a merchant family in Lowmark. A pardon for a poacher. And at the bottom, sealed in black wax rather than the palace red—a hunter's dispatch, marked with the sigil of the Gift-Hunt: a closed eye above crossed staves.

Ilseva's stamp hovered over it.

She had learned the seals' grammar months ago. Red wax for domestic matters, green for foreign, gold for the treasury. Black was for the hunt, and the hunt's dispatches never passed through her hands—they went directly from the Lord Marshal's office to Captain Veyra's couriers. Someone had made a mistake. Or someone wanted them seen passing through the princess's hands, so that later, if questions arose, her mark would be on them.

Either way, it was in front of her, and Oskar had turned toward the window where a cart was rattling through the gate below.

Ilseva lifted the letter's flap—just the corner, just enough—with the tip of her sewing scissors, and read.

*To Captain Veyra, commanding the Gift-Hunt, southern circuit. Report received from Thornfell monastery. One scribe fled the raid, name entered in the debt-ledger as Astrid Galloway. Gift manifest: light, uncontrolled, possibly of the old kind. The Abbot has surrendered four additional names. Pursue along the smuggler roads. The Crown requires the gifted delivered alive. Note: the old kind is worth ten of the common sort. Do not damage.*

*By order of His Majesty's hand.*

His Majesty's hand. She knew her father's handwriting better than her own—it had written her birthday letters for sixteen years, had annotated her lessons, had once drawn a lopsided duck on the margin of a treaty to make her laugh during a dull state dinner. This was not his hand. It was *almost* his hand, the way the man on the throne was almost her father: correct in every particular, and wrong in the way that mattered most. The letters leaned too uniformly. The flourishes were identical, one to the next, like stamps rather than strokes. Her father's writing had moods. This writing had none.

*The old kind,* the dispatch said. *Worth ten of the common sort.*

She pressed the flap closed and held the letter for a moment longer than necessary, feeling the paper's texture, the faint grit of the black wax. Somewhere south of here, in the mountains, a scribe with light in her hands was running for her life, hunted by men carrying orders forged in her father's name by whatever wore him.

Oskar cleared his throat. "Highness?"

"I'm deciding whether the wax suits the gravity," Ilseva said mildly. "Black is so final, don't you think?"

"The Lord Marshal prefers it for the hunt."

"So he does." She set her stamp down, pressed, and lifted it. The closed eye bloomed in violet wax, her own insignia nested inside the hunt's, and she thought: *there. Let my mark be on it. Let them see it later and know I saw.*

Because that was the beginning of rebellion, she understood now. Not swords, not midnight rides, not any of the ballads' nonsense. Rebellion began with a girl at a desk refusing to look away. It began with reading what you were given to seal. It began with the decision, made quietly, in a sunlit room, that you would know things—that knowledge would pass through you and leave a residue, would accumulate, would become weight, and that one day the weight would be enough to tip something over.

Marta took the dispatches away. The door closed. Ilseva sat alone with the afternoon light going long and orange across the floor, and her heart was beating very fast, and her hands were perfectly steady.

* * *

That night she began the second thing.

Under her mattress, wrapped in oilcloth, she kept a growing collection: a stub of pencil, three sheets of good paper stolen from the chancery stores, a length of charcoal, and a small notebook bound in grey leather that had belonged to her mother. In it, in a cipher of her own devising—numbers standing for letters, letters for numbers, keyed to the dates of her mother's funeral, because grief was the one thing in this palace no one had thought to weaponize yet—she wrote down everything.

The names of the lords who emerged emptied from oath-ceremonies. The dates. The increasing frequency of the black-wax dispatches. The courier schedules she had memorized from her window: the hunt's riders left by the south postern at dawn and dusk, changing horses at the Millbridge relay, riding hard toward Thornfell and the mountains beyond. The fact that the king had not spoken her childhood name in over a year—*Liss*, only her mother had ever called her Liss, and if her father were anywhere inside that body, that word would have escaped him by now, the way real things escape.

Tonight she added the dispatch. *Astrid Galloway. Light, uncontrolled, possibly of the old kind. Worth ten of the common sort. Do not damage.*

She wrote it twice, then sat looking at the words until the candle guttered low.

*The old kind.* She didn't know what it meant. But she knew what the priests of the old rite had been before the crown dissolved their priories—her tutor, before tutors became forbidden luxuries for a princess being kept soft, had told her stories of the binding-priests, the ones who had crowned the first kings, the ones whose oaths ran deeper than dynasties. If there was an *old kind* of gift, there had been an *old purpose* for it. And if the thing on the throne was collecting gifts the way a miser collects coin—

Then somewhere in the mountains was a person who might matter very much indeed. A scribe. A debtor. Someone the crown considered property, which meant someone the crown had never imagined could become a threat.

Ilseva blew out the candle and lay in the dark listening to the palace breathe—the footfalls of the night watch, the creak of the banner poles on the roof, the distant sound of the fountain in the inner court that her father had built for her mother because she loved the sound of water.

*Liss,* she thought into the dark. *Say it. Say it once, and I'll know you're in there, and I will tear this kingdom apart to get you out.*

The dark said nothing. The dark had been saying nothing for fourteen months.

But tomorrow the dusk courier would ride south, and Ilseva had decided something tonight, lying awake with her mother's notebook under her pillow and defiance settling into her bones like new-made bone: she was going to write a letter of her own. Unsigned. Unsealed. Slipped into the courier's satchel between Veyra's orders and the chancery's routine reports, where no one would notice one more piece of paper among the dozens.

A warning, maybe. Or a question. Or simply a sentence, addressed to a fugitive scribe she had never met:

*You are not running from the crown. You are running from the thing that stole mine. Keep your light burning. Help is coming—from inside the walls.*

It was madness, of course. If the letter were found, if her cipher were broken, if Oskar or Marta or the Lord Marshal traced one line of ink back to her hand—

Ilseva smiled at the ceiling, and it was her father's smile, the quick and slightly amused one, the one she had inherited honestly.

Let them come. She had spent fourteen months learning to be invisible, and invisibility, she had discovered, was not the absence of power. It was power, coiled and waiting, gathering weight in the dark.

At dawn, the poppy came in its silver cup, and Ilseva carried it to the window, and fed it to the weed.
