## 12. The Salt Road Ambush

The salt road smelled of old money and older lies.

Astrid had known it as a line on Maren Ostwick's charts—a thin scratch of chalk running south from the Greywater fords, past the abandoned brine-works, toward the low country where smugglers paid no tithes and asked no names. But no chart had prepared her for the smell of it: mineral and brackish, the ghost of a sea that had never existed this far inland, rising from the cracked pans on either side like breath from a sleeping mouth. The salt crusted everything. It whitened the stones of the ruined works, furred the dead reeds, glittered in the wheel ruts like a road paved with ground bone.

"Keep to the ruts," Sef murmured beside her. He walked with his charts rolled tight in one hand, though he no longer needed to consult them; three days on the salt road had burned its turns into him the way they were burned into her. "The pans look solid. They aren't. There's brine under the crust, and a man who breaks through in this cold won't climb out again."

"I know," Astrid said. "Maren showed me the map when I was fourteen. She said the salt keeps its dead."

He glanced at her, and in the flat grey light his eyes were the color of the pans themselves. "Did she say anything else about the dead?"

"That they're patient." Astrid adjusted the strap of her pack, where Maren's confession lay wrapped in oilcloth against her spine, a small hard weight she had carried for a week and would carry to Hollowmere if it killed her. "That they don't mind waiting."

Behind them, the others strung out along the ruts: two of Maren's people, a scarred woman named Bree and her silent nephew Tam, who carried the supplies and had not spoken a full sentence since Greywater. The sky overhead was the color of dirty pewter, low enough to touch, and the wind came off the pans carrying salt that settled on the lips and in the corners of the eyes until everything tasted of preservation. Of things kept too long.

Astrid had not slept properly since the shrine road. Since the kiss.

She could still feel it—brief, salt-stung, Sef's hand cupping her jaw as if she were something breakable, which she had never been and resented being treated as. She could still feel her own answer to it, the way she had leaned in before her mind caught up with her body, before six weeks of shared roads and shared fires and the slow, dangerous accumulation of trust crystallized all at once into want. And she could still feel the moment after, when he had pulled back with an expression like a man reading his own death warrant, and said, *We shouldn't have—* and she had said, *No,* and they had walked the rest of the day three feet apart, speaking only of water and mileage.

*Love as vulnerability.* That was what the old texts called it, in the margins, in the heretical hands Astrid had copied by candlelight for seven years. The heart is a door, one of them had said, and every door can be forced. She had thought it was poetry then. She knew better now. She had a door in her chest that Sef had opened without meaning to, and through it she could see everything she stood to lose, and the seeing made her slower, softer, more afraid than she had been when she was alone and owned.

The gift stirred under her breastbone, restless, answering her fear the way it always did. She pressed it down. It was like holding a hand over a candle flame—possible, for a while.

* * *

The brine-works came up on them an hour past noon: a sprawl of collapsed sheds and rendering houses, their timber bleached white as driftwood, salt-hung chimneys leaning against the sky at drunken angles. The ruts ran through the middle of the ruin, and the ruin offered a hundred places for an ambush and no way to know which.

Sef stopped at the edge of it, and Astrid saw him do the thing he always did—turn his head slightly, as if listening to the land itself. He had told her once, on the shrine road, that a mapmaker learned to read terrain the way priests read scripture: every fold of ground a sentence, every sightline a verse. "This place is wrong," he said now, quietly. "The ruts are fresh. Nobody's worked these pans in thirty years, but the ruts are fresh."

Bree unslung her crossbow without being told. Tam set down the supply pack and drew a short blade, his face as blank as the salt flats.

"Riders," Bree said. "Two, maybe three, holding back on the southern rise. Been there since the ford, if I'm honest. I didn't like the way the birds were sitting."

Astrid's stomach dropped. "Veyra?"

"Could be. Could be bounty men. Could be honest travelers with bad manners." Bree's mouth twisted. "On this road, I'd bet on the first."

"We split," Sef said, and his voice had the calm of a man who had rehearsed this. "The works funnel the road—if they hold the far side, we're meat in a chute. We go through the sheds, north side, and cut across the pans to the old towpath. Bree, Tam—draw them if you can, then follow. Astrid—"

"No." The word came out of her before she could dress it up. "We don't split. Splitting is how they win. Maren taught—"

"Maren taught you roads, not war." He said it gently, which was worse. "Astrid. If they take all of us, Maren's confession burns with us. If they take half—"

The first crossbow bolt took Tam through the throat.

There was no warning she could have given, no sound, no shouted challenge—just the flat *thock* of a bolt finding flesh and the boy folding sideways into a salt-crusted wall, his blade ringing off the stones, his mouth working around words that would never come. Bree screamed—a sound Astrid felt in her teeth—and threw herself behind the wall, and the world came apart into noise and motion.

Riders poured off the southern rise. Not two, not three—six, seven, in the grey cloaks of the hunt, crossbows leveled, fanning through the ruin with the drilled ease of men who had done this many times. Astrid saw the lead rider's face and her blood went to ice: a woman, hard-featured, with a scar through one eyebrow and eyes that held no hatred at all, which was the worst kind of eyes to find leveled at you. Captain Veyra. The name the smugglers spoke like a curse. The name Maren had spat on the day she sent them out.

*Run,* Astrid thought. *Run, run—*

But Sef had frozen. He was standing in the open, in the middle of the ruts, staring at the lead rider with an expression Astrid had never seen on him—not fear, something older and more terrible, recognition. And a second bolt was already loosed, already flying, already aimed at the center of his chest.

She didn't decide. There was no decision. There was only the bolt, and Sef, and the door in her chest flung so wide open that everything behind it came through at once.

The gift went out of her like a shout.

Every grain of salt in the ruin answered. Astrid felt it—felt *all of it*, the whole white field of it, the pans and the crust and the crusted walls, a million million crystals each one a small hard seed of light—and she pulled, and they came. The air filled with salt. It rose off the ground in a howling white sheet, a storm with no wind behind it, and the bolt meant for Sef's heart struck the swirling cloud and shattered, its head ground to powder mid-flight. The riders reeled, blinded, horses screaming and throwing their heads. Salt scoured the sheds. Salt hissed against steel. The whole ruin became a blizzard of it, and through the white roar Astrid heard her own voice, high and cracked, saying words she did not know in a language she had never been taught, the old language, the *unbinding* language, and she could not stop.

She could not stop.

That was the thing the texts had never said clearly enough, the thing she understood now with the gift pouring through her like a flood through a broken weir: it wasn't a tool. It was a hunger with her name on it. It wanted to unmake everything—the bolts, the riders, the walls, Sef, herself—and it was only her, small Astrid Galloway, debtor, scribe, liar, holding the leash with both hands and her heels dug in, that kept it pointed at the sky.

She got the leash. Barely. She wrenched the storm sideways, up, spent it against the low pewter clouds, and the salt came down again as gentle as snow, sifting over the ruin, over the bodies, over everything, white on white.

The riders were gone. Scattered, blinded, fleeing—she saw them distantly, shapes coughing and staggering back toward the rise. But she saw, too, the one who had not fled. Veyra, on her knees in the settling white, had shielded her face with her arm, and now she lowered it and looked straight at Astrid across forty feet of salted ground. Looked at her the way a scholar looks at a page she has been searching for all her life.

"There you are," the hunt captain said. Her voice carried, hoarse and clear. "The unbinding kind. *There you are.*"

Then she was up and moving, and Bree was dragging at Astrid's arm, screaming her name, and the world resumed.

* * *

They ran north across the pans, because there was nowhere else. Bree carried Tam's blade; they had not been able to carry Tam. Astrid heard Bree weeping as she ran, a raw silent weeping, the kind that costs too much to make noise, and she thought: *I did that. My door was open and he walked through it and died.* The salt crust cracked under her boots like old ice. Behind them, faintly, a horn sounded—two notes, a pause, two notes. Signaling. Telling the world where they were.

"She'll send riders to the towpath," Sef gasped. He had hold of Astrid's hand, or she had hold of his; she could not remember which of them had reached first. "The pans will slow her horses but not her. We need—"

"I know where we need." Astrid's voice came out scraped. "There's a brine-cave. Half a mile north. Maren's map. It goes down under the pans and comes up past the ford road. We can lose her under the salt."

They found it: a fissure in the crust, a black slot in the white earth breathing cold air. They went down into it with Bree last, pulling the pack after, and the horn's two notes faded above them like a bird passing over.

The cave was old workings, timbered in places with beams furred white as coral, and it ran in a long descending gallery lit by nothing until Bree found a candle stub and lit it with hands that shook. In the small trembling light they stopped, three fugitives in a throat of the earth, and the adrenaline drained out of Astrid all at once and left her shaking worse than Bree.

She sat down on a salt-encrusted beam and put her head in her hands.

She had saved him. The bolt had shattered a foot from his heart, and he was alive, and he was crouching in front of her now, saying her name, and she had saved him. And in saving him she had lit a beacon visible from the southern rise to the capital. *There you are,* Veyra had said, and her voice had not been the voice of a hunter closing on prey. It had been the voice of a woman finding a thing she'd been ordered to find, and being afraid of it.

"I killed Tam," Astrid said. Her voice sounded like someone else's.

"No." Sef's hand found her shoulder. "Veyra's bolt killed Tam."

"My gift brought us here. My gift made us visible at Greywater, at the shrine, everywhere we've—" She looked up at him, and the candlelight made his face strange, hollow-eyed, beautiful, unbearably dear. "Every mile we've gained, I've paid for in miles lost. They're not hunting the confession, Sef. They're not hunting you. They're hunting *me*, and I keep leading them straight to everything I love."

The silence in the cave was total. Salt ticked and settled somewhere in the dark, patient as its dead.

"You think I don't know what it costs," Sef said at last, quietly, "to be the reason someone else bleeds. I marked the king's seal-line because it was true, and the truth got my colleagues taken, and I have walked with that for a year. I know what it is to be a door things come through." He exhaled. "Astrid. What you did back there—"

"Was a beacon."

"Was the most terrifying thing I've ever seen," he said, "and I have seen the entity that wears the king's face." A ghost of a smile, there and gone. "You pulled a storm out of the ground with your grief. Do you understand that no one else could have? The oracle said the unbinding kind. She said it like it was a curse. But Astrid—the entity feeds on oaths, on binding, on everything that holds. You are the one thing in this kingdom that comes to unmake it. Veyra knows it. The thing on the throne knows it. That's why they're hunting you. Not because you're a beacon." His hand tightened on her shoulder. "Because you're the only key."

*The only key.* The words went into her and did something she didn't want them to do, which was make her feel less like a weapon and more like a person. She hated how easily he could do that. She hated that she was sitting in a hole in the earth with salt in her hair and a dead boy's blood on Bree's sleeve and all she could think about was the kiss on the shrine road, and how she had regretted it, and how she regretted regretting it.

"Sef." His name came out before she could stop it, the way the gift came out, uncontrolled.

He went very still. "Don't."

"Why not?"

"Because every time I look at you I start wanting things I can't afford." His voice cracked on *afford*, and he laughed, a small broken sound. "I'm a disgraced cartographer with a bounty on his head, and you're—the key to the kingdom, apparently—and in six weeks either we save the world or we die in the attempt, and I keep thinking that if I let myself—"

Astrid kissed him.

It was not like the shrine road. On the shrine road it had been a spark, an accident of proximity. This was a decision, made in candlelight under a mountain of salt, with the horn still echoing in her ears and Tam's blade wrapped in Bree's arms, and it was a *bad* decision, and she made it anyway, because the texts were right: the heart is a door, and she was so tired of guarding hers. He made a sound against her mouth like surrender. His hand came up to the back of her neck, salt-crusted and shaking, and for a handful of heartbeats the cave was the only safe place in the world.

Then she felt it—the gift stirring, answering her joy the way it answered her fear, rising under her breastbone like a second heartbeat—and she broke away so fast she nearly fell off the beam.

The candle flame had turned white. The salt on the walls around them was glowing, faintly, a ring of small cold lights, and Sef was looking at her with his lips parted and his eyes wide, and Astrid pressed her hands flat against her knees and forced her breath down and the light guttered and died, all of it, everywhere, until only the candle burned.

"There," she said. Her voice was wrecked. "That. That's why not."

"Astrid—"

"Every feeling I have, it *uses*." She was on her feet now, backing away, needing the dark between them. "Fear, joy, you—it doesn't care what it is. It takes it and it spends it and it *shines*, and everything for ten miles sees it shine. I can't—Sef, I can't love you and keep us alive. Not both. Not with this in me."

He stood slowly. In the candlelight his face was very controlled, the cartographer's face, the face he wore when a map told him something he didn't want to know. "So that's the ledger, then," he said. "That's the price."

"That's the price."

He looked at her a long moment. Then he nodded, once, and turned away, and began checking the gallery north with the candle, measuring their route to Hollowmere in silence, and Astrid sat back down on the salt beam and wrapped her arms around her knees and did not weep, because weeping would glow.

* * *

They walked the under-road for hours, single file, Bree leading now because grief had made her hard as her nephew's blade. Somewhere above them the horn sounded again, twice, patient, and then faded as they went deeper, and Astrid told herself the salt was swallowing the sound.

Near midnight the gallery opened into a chamber where an old brine-pool lay still as a mirror, and in its black surface Astrid saw the ceiling scattered with points of light—salt crystals catching the candle, a false sky, a sky made of preserved things. She stopped and looked up at it and thought about keys, and doors, and the entity that fed on everything that held.

Bree, ahead in the dark, said quietly: "There's a marking on the wall here. Fresh. Cut, not written."

Sef brought the candle. The light found a symbol chiseled into the salt-hardened stone: a circle, broken by a single vertical line, and beneath it, small and precise, the mark of the hunt.

"Veyra knows the under-roads," Sef said slowly. "She's not chasing us. She's *herding* us." He looked back at Astrid across the black mirror of the pool, and his face in the candlelight was grim. "This passage doesn't go to Hollowmere, Astrid. It goes exactly where she wants us to go."

The candle guttered. In the dark, far ahead, something in the under-road began, very softly, to glow.
