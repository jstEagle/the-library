## 13. Confession at the Shrine

The road to Hollowmere forgot itself on purpose.

That was the first thing Astrid understood about it, walking the last mile behind Sef with Maren's confession wrapped in oilcloth against her chest. Every smuggler road she'd ever learned announced itself—a blaze on a birch, a cairn of three stones, a bend that smelled of pine resin where the pines had been cut a generation ago. This one did the opposite. It unmade its own markers. The blazes had been chiseled off and the wood left to weather gray; the cairns were scattered so carefully they looked like accidents; and twice the path doubled back through a streambed in a way that would have convinced any honest traveler they'd lost it entirely.

Only someone taught by Maren Ostwick could have read the negations. *Where there is no stone, look up. Where the moss is wrong, go right.* The old woman had drilled her on these inversions over winter fires, laughing through her pipe smoke: *"A hidden road is just a loud road wearing a lie, girl. Learn to hear the silence."*

So Astrid heard it now—the deliberate hush of a place that had spent centuries refusing to be found—and when the trees finally opened onto the hollow, she stopped as if she'd walked into glass.

The priory sat in a bowl of mountain meadow gone gold with late summer, ringed by cliffs that held the light like cupped hands. It was smaller than she'd imagined from Sef's descriptions: a squat chapel of fieldstone, a bell tower with no bell, a cloister walk whose roof had half-fallen and been left to fall the rest of the way, wild roses claiming the arches. A garden sprawled where a courtyard should be—beans and squash and sunflowers grown tall and unruly, tended by figures in undyed wool who moved among the rows without hurry.

No walls. No gate. No guards. After weeks of looking over their shoulders for Veyra's hunters, the openness of it felt almost obscene.

"They don't need walls," Sef said quietly, reading her expression. He was thinner than he'd been at the pass, his cartographer's hands nicked and stained, but his eyes still did that thing she both loved and resented—seeing everything, drawing it, filing it away. "The road is the wall. Anyone who arrives here was meant to."

"Or desperate enough to read silence," Astrid said.

"That too." He touched her elbow, brief and warm. "Come on. She's expecting you."

*She*. The blind oracle. Astrid's stomach tightened around the bundle. They had come to Hollowmere for answers about her gift, about the ritual, about the impossible price the priests kept circling. But first—first there was this. A dead woman's last request, heavier than any debt ledger Astrid had ever copied.

They crossed the meadow through a corridor of sunflowers taller than their heads, and the smell of them—green and bitter-sweet, dust and pollen—was so violently alive that Astrid's throat closed. Maren had smelled like pipe smoke and tallow and dried yarrow. Maren had died eleven days ago in her bed above the counting house in Ostwick's Crossing, holding Astrid's hand hard enough to leave marks, saying only: *Hollowmere. Give it to the oracle herself. No one else reads it. Swear it.*

Astrid had sworn. She was beginning to understand that swearing things was how her whole life went wrong.

* * *

The oracle received them in the chapel's side room, a space barely larger than a scriptorium cell, lit by one high window that threw a blade of afternoon light across the floor. The woman herself sat on a plain bench with her hands folded—old, older than Maren had been, her face seamed like cracked glaze, her eyes bandaged with white linen that had once been very clean and was now the color of old honey.

"You are the scribe," the oracle said before either of them spoke. Her voice was dry and precise, like a quill on good vellum. "And you are the mapmaker. Sit. Both of you. You've brought me something I have waited forty years to hold."

Astrid's skin prickled. "How did you—"

"The road told me. Three sets of feet, two of them heavy with grief, one of them carrying paper that smells of tallow and yarrow and another person's whole life." She extended her hands, palms up. "Give it to me yourself, she said. As if there were any other way. As if I would let a confession like hers pass through intermediate hands."

Astrid knelt—she didn't decide to kneel, her body simply did it, the way you kneel at a graveside—and laid the oilcloth bundle in the oracle's palms.

The old woman unwrapped it slowly. Inside were two things: a letter sealed with plain wax, and beneath it, flat and heavy, a small book bound in green leather gone black at the corners with handling. Even before the oracle touched it, Astrid knew what kind of book it was. She had spent seven years copying books. Some objects carry their purpose in their weight.

"A ledger," the oracle said softly. "Of course. That woman thought in ledgers to the end." She broke the seal and unfolded the letter, tilting it toward the window though her bandaged eyes could never find the words. "Read it to me, scribe. My fingers are not what they were. And then—" her voice caught, just slightly, the first crack in all that precision, "—then we will see whether you can forgive us."

"*Us*," Astrid repeated. "Forgive who?"

The oracle only held out the letter.

Astrid took it. The handwriting was Maren's—she knew it instantly, the cramped forward slant, the way the old woman crossed her t's like little knives. Reading it aloud in that small stone room, hearing Maren's voice rise out of the page after eleven days of silence, was like pressing on a bruise to prove it still hurt.

*Mother Superior,* it began. *If you are reading this, then my granddaughter has done as I asked, which means she is braver and more foolish than I raised her to be, and I am dead, which serves me right.*

The word landed like a dropped stone.

*Granddaughter.*

Sef made a small sound beside her. Astrid didn't stop reading. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her, because nothing inside her was steady; everything inside her was a rope pulled tight enough to sing.

*I was born Maren Galloway, third daughter of Edric Galloway, unbinding-gifted, of the line the crown hunted in the last purge. I escaped the harvest because my father burned our family record and scattered us. I changed my name. I built a life out of fences and false roads and other people's secrets, and I told myself it was enough. Then my son married, and his wife bore a child, and the child was gifted—I saw it when she was four years old, lighting candles with her temper, and I knew what was coming for her.*

*I could not protect her openly. The gift-hunters of that era were thorough, and the Galloway name was already ash. So I did the cruelest clever thing I have ever done. I manufactured a debt. I bought her parents' debts quietly, folded them into one sum, and sold the child's indenture to Thornfell monastery under a fabricated name—Galloway struck from every paper, replaced with a name I invented from nothing. I paid the monastery to keep her ignorant of her own blood. I paid the abbot to burn any record that might connect her to the purged lines. I made her an orphan on paper so the hunters would never look for her family, because I had already erased it.*

*It worked. Gods forgive me, it worked for twenty years. She grew up owned and ignorant and safe. And I watched her count down her debt in the margins of other people's holy books, and I let her believe she was paying for a crime her grandfather committed, when the truth is the debt was mine, and the crime was love, and the price was her whole life.*

*I am dying now, and the hunt has started again, worse than before, and my cleverness has run out of road. Tell her everything. Tell her the debt was manufactured. Tell her the name Galloway is hers. Tell her I am sorry in every language I know, and I know several, and none of them is large enough.*

*One more thing, and this is the important one. The debt papers. When she was indentured, I kept the original contract—the true one, with my seal on it, the one that proves the whole arrangement was fraud. It is sewn into the binding of the green ledger. She will know what to do with it. She always was better with paper than I ever was.*

*Tell her the roads I taught her were the inheritance I could give openly. Everything else was stolen from her, and I stole it, and I would do it again, and that is the most damning sentence I have ever written.*

*Maren Ostwick. Born Maren Galloway. Grandmother of Astrid.*

Astrid finished reading. The silence afterward had texture, weight, a pressure against the eardrums like deep water.

Her hands were shaking. She looked down at them—at the ink stains ground so deep into her knuckles they seemed part of her skin, seven years of copying other people's truths while her own was locked in a stranger's ledger—and she heard her own voice say, very calmly, in the tone she used for reading difficult passages aloud:

"She sewed it into the binding."

"Yes," the oracle said gently.

Astrid took the green book. Her fingers found the spine, found the slight ridge beneath the leather where thread puckered, and she did what she had done ten thousand times in the scriptorium—she fetched the small knife from her kit, and she opened the binding along the seam with the care of a surgeon and the fury of a gravedigger.

The pages inside were Maren's confession ledger—decades of entries, names and sums and sins—but that wasn't what made Astrid's vision blur. Folded flat and stitched beneath the endpaper, yellowed and creased and bearing a wax seal stamped with a sigil she recognized from her father's one surviving letter home—a stag rampant above three stars—

There it was. The original contract. *Indenture of one child, name withheld per purchaser's instruction, term of service until debt of 340 crowns discharged.* Signed below in a hand Astrid knew better than her own: the abbot's. And countersigned, in Maren's slanting knife-crossed letters, with the note in the margin that undid twenty years:

*Paid in full at signing. Sum advanced by M.O. Consider the remainder interest on a mother's cowardice.*

Paid in full. At signing. There had never been a debt. There had never been anything to count down. All those years of candlelight and cramped fingers and counting in margins—*312 remaining, 298 remaining, 281*—all of it arithmetic performed on a number that had been zero since the day she was born.

Astrid laughed. It came out of her like something breaking, one harsh bark of sound that frightened even her, and then she couldn't stop, and then she was crying, kneeling on the cold stone floor of a hidden chapel with her grandmother's fraud open in her lap, laughing and crying in the same breath because the alternative was screaming and there were hunters in the mountains and no time for screaming.

"Astrid." Sef's hand on her shoulder. Warm. Steady. Not pulling her up, just anchoring her while the storm went through.

"She named me," Astrid said, when she could speak. "In the letter. She signed it—she signed it *grandmother of Astrid*, like it was a title, like it was—" Her voice fractured. "I spent seven years being nobody's daughter. Nobody's anything. A name invented from nothing, she says. She *invented* me. And the whole time—"

"The whole time you were the last of a line the crown was afraid of," the oracle said. Her bandaged face turned toward Astrid with terrible accuracy. "Yes. And now you understand why the debt had to be manufactured, and why it must make you angry, and why your anger is correct. Rage is the honest response to a theft this size. Do not let anyone—including her ghost, including me—talk you out of it."

Astrid wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "You knew. You said you'd waited forty years to hold her confession. You knew what was in it."

"I knew Maren Galloway when we were both young and the world was burning around people like us. I knew she survived by becoming someone else. I suspected what she'd done, because Maren never could resist a clever cruelty dressed up as protection." The oracle's folded hands tightened. "I let her do it. That is my sin in this ledger, scribe. We who hid the gifted taught them to hide so well they hid from themselves. We taught your grandmother to erase you, and called it mercy."

The candles.

Astrid became aware, distantly, that every candle in the side room had flared—not the wild flare of panic, not the involuntary blaze that had betrayed her at Thornfell, but a low, sustained brightening, like held breath. Her gift, responding to her rage the way it always had, but shaped now, contained, *hers*. The oracle turned her bandaged face toward the light and smiled faintly.

"There it is. The unbinding gift. Anger and candlelight. Your grandfather could boil rivers with his temper, they said." She reached out and touched Astrid's wrist, her fingers cool and certain. "Feel it. That fire is not Thornfell's. It is not the debt's. It is not even Maren's, though she carried its shadow her whole life. It is yours, Astrid Galloway. It was yours before anyone lied to you, and it will be yours after. Whatever you choose to do with it at the thirteenth moon—that will be the first truly free choice of your life. Make it as yourself."

Astrid looked down at the contract in her lap. The abbot's signature. Maren's marginal confession. Three hundred and forty crowns that had never existed, purchased with a child's entire childhood.

Slowly, deliberately, she held the corner of the parchment to the nearest candle.

The flame took it hungrily. Old wax-sealed vellum burns beautifully—it curls and browns and lifts its ghost-image upward before it goes—and Astrid watched the abbot's signature blacken and blister and become nothing, watched the number *340* curl into ash, watched the last physical proof of her bondage return to the air it had always been borrowed from.

"You'll want the ledger kept," Sef said quietly. Nodding at the green book. "As evidence. For Veyra's second ledger. For whatever comes after."

"Yes." Astrid's voice was rough but level. "The ledger stays. The lie burns." She set the green book down with care, the way you set down something that has become sacred by surviving. "Maren gets to be guilty in the record. But I don't have to be owned in it anymore."

The oracle inclined her head. "Then you are ready to hear what the gift costs. And what it offers. And why the priests of Hollowmere bound the crown in the first place—and what your grandfather's line was originally meant to do with that fire of yours."

Astrid rose. Her knees ached. Her eyes stung. Somewhere in the garden outside, the sunflowers moved in a wind she couldn't feel, and the blade of window-light had crawled across the floor to rest at her feet like a path being drawn.

"What do you mean," she said slowly, "*originally meant to do?*"

The oracle's smile was old and sad and full of forty waiting years.

"Sit down, Astrid Galloway. Your family's story is longer than your grandmother's guilt. And it begins with the crown."
