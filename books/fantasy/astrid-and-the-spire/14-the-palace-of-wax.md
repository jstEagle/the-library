## 14. The Palace of Wax

The palace of Veylmark smelled of honey and tallow, and Ilseva had spent nineteen years believing that was simply what wealth smelled like.

She knew better now. She had known for eleven days—since the night the dispatch crossed her lap, since the name *Veyra* surfaced from the hunter's ledger like a drowned body rising in still water—and the knowledge had changed the smell entirely. Now the honey-sweetness of a hundred burning candles read to her as what it was: wax, poured over everything, sealing every seam, filling every crack where something true might once have lived. Her father's palace was a thing dipped again and again until its shape remained but its substance did not.

Tonight she would find out how deep the dipping went.

The household slept in the way the household always slept now—early, heavily, obediently. Since her father's *indisposition*, as the chamberlain called it, the servants moved through the halls like figures in a shadow play, their footsteps softened, their voices pitched to a murmur that never quite reached the walls. Even the guards at the west stair changed watch with a strange punctuality, as though they too were wound on some great silent mechanism. Ilseva had begun to wonder whether the poppy syrup the physician pressed on her each evening—the *calming draught*, he called it, *for your grief, Highness*—was also being pressed on everyone else, in smaller doses, in the wine, in the bread.

She had stopped drinking hers three nights ago. She poured it into the fern by her window, which had grown lush and faintly nodding, and she had begun, for the first time since the funeral-that-was-not-a-funeral, to dream.

That was how she learned the throne room was sealed.

Not officially. Officially it was *closed for restoration*, the same phrase that had closed her mother's apartments, the old chapel, and the eastern gallery where the portraits of the first six kings hung. But dreams did not concern themselves with official phrases. In her dreams she walked corridors she had run down as a child chasing her father's cloak, and the doors stood open, and behind them the throne room blazed with light that was not light, and something sat upon the throne wearing her father's posture the way a thief wears a stolen coat—correctly, carefully, and yet not quite *in* it.

She woke from the third such dream with her heart hammering and the taste of honey in her mouth, and she understood that the dream was not a dream. It was a summons. Or perhaps only an echo—her father's echo, faint as a voice shouted up from the bottom of a well, reaching her because no one had thought to dose her dreams.

*They think I am asleep,* she thought, dressing in the dark, in the plain grey wool of a minor attendant, her hair braided tight and hidden beneath a servant's coif. *They think I am soft wax, ready to take any impression they press into me.*

Let them think it. Wax held impressions beautifully. And tonight she intended to press back.

* * *

The servants' corridor behind the state rooms had been her secret since she was seven, when her nurse's son Torv had shown it to her in exchange for half a honeycake. Torv was gone now—dismissed, along with half the old household, in the tidy weeks after her father's *retirement from public life*. But stone remembered even when people were made to forget, and Ilseva's fingers found the third sconce from the corner, pressed, and felt the panel give with the same reluctant sigh it had given twelve years ago.

Inside, the passage was exactly as narrow as childhood had made it seem vast. She went by touch, one hand on the left wall, counting doorways by the breath of colder air that came through each. Past the council chamber, where voices murmured even now at midnight—regency business, they called it, though the regency council had not invited her to a single session. Past the map room, where she heard paper rustle and someone weeping very quietly, and did not stop, though her chest tightened. Past the chapel, where the incense had been allowed to go stale, which no priest of the Nine would ever permit, which meant there were no priests anymore, or none who mattered.

And then the last door, the one at the passage's end, and beyond it—silence so complete it rang.

The throne room's inner door was barred from without; she could feel the bar's weight through the wood when she leaned her forehead against it. But wax seals, she had learned this month from a smuggled book of heraldic law hidden inside her mattress, were not merely decoration. They were bindings. Old law, older than the dynasty: *what is sealed with intent may be opened with intent.* The great seal pressed into the wax across the door's edge bore her father's face—or the face that wore her father's coins and proclamations—and around it ran a ring of script she did not recognize, fine as spider silk, dark as dried blood.

She should have been afraid. She registered the fact distantly, the way one registers cold: *this is the place where fear would be.* Instead she felt the dream-pull again, stronger here, that faint well-bottom voice, and she set her palm flat against the seal.

*Father,* she thought—not prayed, she had stopped praying to anything the crown claimed to speak for—*if you are anywhere in there, I am coming.*

The wax did not melt. It *yielded*, softly, like skin under a too-long pressure, and the ring of script flickered once—a sickly amber, the color of candlelight seen through flesh—and went dark. The bar on the other side lifted itself with a groan and clattered to the marble.

Ilseva stood very still for a long moment, listening to her own heartbeat.

Then she pushed the door open and went in.

* * *

Moonlight came through the high windows in pale blades, and by it she saw what the candles would have shown her anyway, because the throne room was full of candles—hundreds of them, thousands, ranked on every tier and step and sill, all of them burned down to stubs, all of them cold. Cold, and yet the air was thick with the smell of fresh tallow, as though they had all been snuffed moments before she entered. As though the room itself had blown them out to receive her in the dark.

The throne stood at the far end beneath the banner of the nine-pointed crown. And in it—

Her breath left her in a sound she did not recognize as her own.

He was thin. That was the first obscenity: her father, King Aldric, who had once needed two tailors and a running joke about his waistcoats, was thin as a winter branch, sunk into the cushions of the throne like a man settling into his own grave. His hair had gone white at the temples—he was fifty-two, it should not have gone white—and his hands lay in his lap, palms up, fingers slightly curled, in the attitude of a man who has surrendered something and cannot remember what.

But his eyes were open. And they were *his* eyes. Whatever else had been done to him, whatever hollowing had scooped him out, those were her father's grey eyes, and they found her across forty feet of moonlit marble, and they filled—with such slow, staggering effort, like water rising in a cracked vessel—that Ilseva's knees nearly failed her.

"Ilseva," he whispered.

Not *Highness*. Not *daughter* in the formal court fashion. He said *Ilseva*—and more than that, he said it the way no one had said it in eleven years, since she was eight and feverish and he had sat by her bed all night telling her the story of the cartographer's daughter who mapped the moon. He said it with the little upward lilt he had always given it, *Il-SEH-va*, the lilt of a private joke between them, because he had named her after a river on a map he'd loved as a boy, a river that split and rejoined itself and could never truly be divided.

"Papa." The word broke her cover, her caution, everything. She crossed the floor at a run, skirts gathered, and dropped to her knees before the throne, and took his hands—and cried out, because they were *cold*, cold as the candle stubs, cold as wax, and so light. When had her father's hands become light?

"Hush," he breathed. His lips barely moved. "Hush, little river. Listen. There is so little—" A shudder went through him. For a moment his eyes lost focus, slid sideways, and when they returned to her they were wrong—smooth, pleasant, courteous, the eyes of the man who dined with the council and signed the proclamations and patted her hand at public audiences. Then they fought their way back. Back to her. "There is so little of me left that I must not waste it. Listen."

"I'm listening. I'm here, Papa, I'm—"

"The thirteenth moon." His fingers twitched in hers, trying to grip, managing only a flutter. "When it turns, it will not need me anymore. It will not need *anyone*. Do you understand? All the oaths—all the sworn things, every vow ever sworn to the crown, going back nine generations—it has been drinking them, slowly, and at the thirteenth turn it drinks the last draught and the cup is sealed forever. And everyone who ever swore—" His breath caught. "Everyone bound. Everyone *owned*. It will hold them all, and they will kneel, and they will smile while they kneel, and they will call it love."

The horror of it arrived not as a thought but as a physical sensation, cold climbing her spine. She thought of the guards changing watch with clockwork perfection. Of the chamberlain's murmuring regency council. Of herself, nineteen years of inherited oaths, daughter of the crown, *bound before she could walk.*

"How," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "How do I stop it?"

"You can't." The words fell like stones. "You mustn't try. It watches me watching you, do you see? Every moment I fight it spends me faster. If it learns you know—if it learns anyone outside knows—" His gaze darted to the dark doorway, the dark windows, the darkness everywhere, as if the palace itself might be listening. Perhaps it was. Perhaps the whole palace of wax was one great ear. "There are others," he whispered, fast now, urgent, spending himself like a spendthrift. "A scribe. From Thornfell. Gifted—the old kind, the unbinding kind. And a mapmaker, Sef, my Sef, I trusted him with—" Another shudder, longer. His eyes went smooth again, and for three heartbeats he smiled at her blankly, kindly, terribly, and said, in a warm and reasonable voice, "You shouldn't be awake, little one. You'll catch cold."

Then his fingers pressed hers—one hard squeeze, deliberate, agonized—and the grey eyes came back.

"Find them," he breathed. "Before the twelfth moon rises. After that there is no time. Promise me. Swear it—but swear it to *me*, Ilseva, not to the crown, never to the crown, swear nothing to the crown—"

"I swear it to you," she said. "To my father. To Aldric, son of Maren—" 

She stopped. The words had come from somewhere below memory, some nursery rhyme or genealogy lesson, and her father's face changed—changed utterly—with something that was almost laughter and almost weeping.

"You know," he whispered. "Some part of you already knew."

"I don't understand—"

"No time. No time to explain. Ask the priests at Hollowmere. Ask them what the crown harvested. Ask them—" His head jerked back against the throne. His mouth opened and what came out was not his voice. It was many voices, layered, harmonized, gentle as a choir and wrong as a choir of the drowned: *"Daughter. You are weary. Return to your chambers. All is well. All is sworn. All is loved."*

Ilseva rose. She made her legs rise, made her face smooth, gave the darkness and whatever watched through it the curtsy of an obedient, sleepy child—and walked backward toward the door, keeping her eyes on her father's face, refusing to turn her back on him even as every instinct screamed to run.

At the threshold she paused. The moonlight had shifted; a blade of it lay across the throne, across his sunken cheek, and in that silver light she saw what the dark had hidden: tracks on his face, old ones, cut deep and long dry. He had wept, here alone in the dark, for weeks. And no one had come. No one had known. The whole palace had dipped itself in wax over his living body and called it peace.

"I will come back for you," she said aloud, quietly, clearly, letting the words carry to whatever listened. Let it hear. Let it learn that the king's daughter was only a girl who missed her father and wandered at night. "I promise, Papa. Hold on."

His lips moved. No sound came. But his eyes—his eyes said it, plain as print:

*Little river. Split and rejoin. Never be divided.*

* * *

She did not go back to her chambers. She went to the map room instead, where the quiet weeping had been, and found the door unlocked and the room empty except for one guttering lamp and a young archivist asleep facedown on a chart of the southern coast, tears dried in pale tracks on his cheek, a half-burned fragment of vellum clutched under his hand as though he'd tried to save it from a fire.

She eased the fragment free. It was royal cartography, she realized—the seal-line of the kingdom itself, the great boundary survey, marked with annotations in a small precise hand. Anomalies, someone had called them, once, and been destroyed for it. Along the border of the seal, in that dead cartographer's careful script, ran a single repeated notation:

*Oath-flow reverses at each new moon. Thirteen turns to saturation. Source: the throne.*

Below it, smaller, almost illegible, scorched at the edge: *If found—Thornfell. Find the scribe.*

Ilseva stood in the lamplight holding the piece of a burned map, and outside the window the waning moon hung over the city like a coin spent in advance, and she counted—twelve nights until the twelfth turn, twenty-six until the thirteenth—and felt the last of her childhood drain away through the floorboards like wax through a mold.

Her father had told her to find a scribe. The map had told her where.

By dawn she had a horse, a servant's cloak, and a lie prepared for the chamberlain about a pilgrimage of grief. By full dark she was through the south gate, riding north toward Thornfell and the mountains, with the burned chart against her heart and her father's whisper in her ears:

*Swear nothing to the crown.*
