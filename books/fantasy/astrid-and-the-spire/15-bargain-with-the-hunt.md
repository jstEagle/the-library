## 15. Bargain with the Hunt

*Veyra*

The trap had taken three days to build, and Captain Sera Veyra hated every hour of it.

Not because the work was poor. The work was excellent. The Salt Road narrowed at Graywater Ford where the cliffs leaned in like conspirators, and she had seeded the gorge with her best riders, closed both ends with felled pines, and set watchers on the high shelf where no fire could be seen from below. When the fugitives came through—and they would come through, because Maren Ostwick's old smuggling routes all funneled here eventually, and Astrid Galloway had learned those routes at Maren's knee—the net would close clean as a purse string.

She hated it because for three days, while her soldiers sharpened and waited, she had sat by her own fire with two ledgers open on her knees.

The first was the crown's ledger, bound in black calfskin, stamped with the seal she had served for nineteen years. It held the names of the gifted she had taken: forty-one entries in her own hand, each one dated, each one delivered to the palace with a receipt signed in wax. She had always been proud of that ledger. Complete. Honest. A soldier's book.

The second ledger was newer. She had bought it in a market town for two coppers, cheap paper, a cover the color of dried blood. On its first page she had copied out the record she'd found in the palace annex, sealed behind a false drawer in her own commission file: *Ostwick line, oaths harvested, third generation. Veyra line, oaths harvested, second generation.*

Her family. Her grandmother's name, entered in the same hand that had entered the king's own vassals. Taken decades before the proclamation, before the hunt had ever been declared—taken quietly, without receipts, without wax, without anyone to sign for them. Her grandmother had not been gifted. Her grandmother had simply owned land the crown wanted, and the oath-binding had made certain the deed passed cleanly into royal hands, along with every memory of how it had been lost.

Veyra had read that page eleven times. Each time, the first ledger grew heavier in her lap, until she could hardly lift it.

Now she stood on the high shelf above Graywater Ford, watching the gorge through falling dusk, and told herself the same thing she had told herself for three days: *You are still a captain of the crown. You are still owed your pay. Whatever you do next, you do it as one.*

Below, in the blue dark, a lantern kindled in the fugitives' camp. One flame, then a second, then a small scatter of them—a fire built low, guarded, the camp of people who had learned to sleep afraid.

"Signal on the east rim," said Corporal Dann, beside her. "They've posted a watch. Good eyes on them."

"They'll have seen us by morning regardless," Veyra said. "Galloway knows these roads better than we do. If I wanted them dead, they'd already be dead."

Dann looked at her sideways. He had served under her for six years and knew when his captain was speaking to herself as much as to him. "And what is it you want, Captain?"

Veyra closed the black ledger. She did not open the red one. She carried it against her ribs like a splinter working toward her heart.

"I want the truth," she said, "and I want witnesses when I present it. Come morning, we're not taking prisoners. We're taking an audience."

* * *

*Astrid*

Astrid woke to birdsong that was wrong.

She lay still in the bracken, Sef's bedroll a warm shape beside hers, Ilseva breathing softly beyond the fire's ash, and listened. The dawn chorus over Graywater Ford should have been thick—thrush and wren and the rude opinions of jackdaws. Instead there were only crows, and too many of them, all perched on the eastern rim of the gorge in a row like mourners who had come early to a funeral.

Crows that didn't startle. Crows that stayed put while a horse shifted beneath them.

"Hunters," she breathed.

Sef was awake before she finished the word. He didn't sit up; he simply opened his eyes and went still in the way he did, the cartographer's stillness, reading terrain the way other men read scripture. His gaze tracked up the cliff walls, along the fallen pines choking the north end of the ford, across the too-neat arrangement of deadfall at the south.

"A net," he murmured. "Both ends closed. They've had days."

Ilseva stirred. In the weeks since Hollowmere she had shed something of her palace softness—her hands were cracked now, her braid practical—but she still woke like a princess, all at once and entirely, eyes clear before her head left the ground. "How many?"

"At least twenty," Astrid said. "Maybe more under canvas. And the watch positions—" She pointed without lifting her arm, marking them in the air. "There, there, and on the high shelf. That's a captain's dispositions. That's Veyra."

"You're sure?"

"She took Maren's roads apart once before, years ago. Maren said she fought like someone reading a letter she'd already written." Astrid's mouth tasted of copper. "We're in the letter."

They crawled back from the rim and crouched in the bracken, three fugitives and their small hoard: the burned chart, the oracle's warning written in Sef's careful hand, the vial of Ilseva's blood that the priests had blessed and Astrid had sworn never to need. Below them the gorge ran silver with morning mist. Above them, somewhere on that shelf, sat the woman whose ledgers held Astrid's name in ink that could not be erased.

"We can't go south," Sef said. "The deadfall's watched and it's a killing ground if they loose arrows into it. North is worse—the ford narrows, single file, perfect ambush. Climbing out means the rims, and the rims mean skylining ourselves against the dawn."

"Then we don't climb," Astrid said slowly. Something was itching at her, some wrongness in the picture below. "Look at the camp. Look at it properly."

Sef looked. So did Ilseva. The hunters' fires burned small and disciplined. Their pickets faced outward. Their horses were saddled but not harnessed.

"They haven't come down," Ilseva said.

"They've had us boxed for a day at least—maybe three, if those pines fell as long ago as they look. Veyra doesn't wait. Maren used to say the only mistake Veyra ever made was patience, and she'd cured herself of it." Astrid's heart was beating strangely, hard and slow, like a fist knocking on a door from inside. "She's waiting for something. Or she's decided something."

"Captains don't decide things," Sef said. "Crowns decide. Captains obey."

"Then why hasn't she?" Astrid got to her feet, brush whispering against her coat, and before either of them could stop her she stepped out onto open rock at the gorge's edge, hands empty and raised, the rising light catching her face.

Below, a horn sounded—one long note, uncertain, almost startled. Along the rims, crossbows rose.

Astrid stood in the open and shouted down into the gorge, her voice cracking on the cold air:

"Captain Veyra! You've read your second ledger—I know you have! Come down and read ours!"

* * *

*Veyra*

She let them sweat for an hour. It was good tactics, and besides, she wanted to see them.

They came down to the ford's stony middle under escort, walking slow, hands visible. The Galloway scribe first—younger than the reports suggested, sharp-faced, eyes that moved over Veyra's soldiers the way a locksmith's fingers move over a lock. Behind her, the disgraced cartographer, half-burned charts wrapped in oilcloth across his back like a wounded wing. And behind him—

Veyra had expected to feel contempt for the princess. What she felt instead was a cold shock of recognition. Ilseva walked like her father had walked, before. Before whatever lived in the throne room had eaten everything except the shape of him.

"Captain." The Galloway girl stopped ten paces off, on the flat stone where the ford ran shallowest. Her voice was steady. Her knuckles were white. "You could have killed us in our sleep. Three days you've had us in a sack. So either your orders changed, or you changed them. Which?"

Bold. Insolent. Exactly what Maren Ostwick's letters had promised, back when Maren had been a thorn in the crown's side and Veyra had been young enough to find that admirable instead of inconvenient.

"Corporal Dann will search you," Veyra said. "Cooperate, and this stays civil."

Dann went through them gently, by her instruction. From the scribe: a scribe's kit, a knife, a folded letter bearing the seal of Hollowmere. From the cartographer: the oilcloth packet, which Veyra unrolled herself on a flat rock.

Half-burned charts. She had seen copies of these in the palace annex—the ones that hadn't burned, the ones Sef Ryland had marked before his arrest. Seal-lines. Oath-threads rendered in cartographic convention, the dynasty drawn as a watershed, tribute flowing downhill toward a single confluence. And at the confluence, where the king's seal should have anchored the whole system, a mark Veyra had been trained never to make and so had never seen before: an anomaly symbol, repeated, circled, annotated in a cramped hand. *Feeds. Grows. Thirteenth turn.*

"You marked the king's seal-line as a foreign body," Veyra said quietly.

"I marked it as what it is." Sef's voice was hoarse, a man who had been interrogated recently and thoroughly. "I was the royal cartographer, Captain. I mapped the oath-roads for eleven years. I know what the king's line looked like. This"—his finger came down on the anomaly mark, gentle as touching a wound—"is not the king. It wears the oath-network like a coat. And every oath in the kingdom feeds it. Including yours."

"Including mine," Veyra said.

"Yes."

She reached into her coat and drew out the red ledger. Held it up between them. The morning light caught the cheap cover, the color of dried blood.

"Second generation," she said. "My grandmother's name is in the annex records. Harvested thirty-one years ago, along with her land title. No receipt. No wax. No one accountable. I served the crown for nineteen years believing my family's loyalty was a choice." She let that hang in the cold air between them. "It was a harvest."

The scribe's breath caught. The princess went very still, in the way of someone hearing a door open in a house she thought empty.

"So," Veyra continued, and heard her own voice flatten into parade-ground cadence, the voice she used when feeling was a luxury the formation couldn't afford. "Here is what happens now. You will give me proof. Not charts—charts I could have forged myself, and so could you. Proof I can carry into the palace and put in front of the regency council and the garrison commanders and every officer whose loyalty currently runs through that thing on the throne. Proof that survives being called treason."

"And then?" Ilseva asked. Her voice was her father's voice, and it cost Veyra something to hear it.

"And then I open the gates," Veyra said. "The river gate, the night of the twelfth moon's turn. My garrison holds it. Forty men who trust me, or trusted me this time last week. I can put you inside the palace walls ahead of the thirteenth turn, with the entity unaware, if such a thing can be unaware." She rolled the charts closed and held them out to Sef. "But I don't spend forty lives on a story. Bring me proof, and the gate opens. Fail to bring it, and I burn this conversation out of every memory it touched and hunt you to the coast. Those are my terms."

"That's not a bargain," the scribe said. "That's a ransom."

"It's a ledger entry," Veyra said. "Debit and credit. I've spent nineteen years on one side of the book, Galloway. I'm learning to keep the other."

Silence, except the ford running shallow and bright over its stones. The scribe looked at Sef. Sef looked at the princess. And Ilseva—Ilseva stepped forward, out from behind her protectors, and looked up at Veyra with eyes that had her father's gray in them.

"What proof would convince you?" the princess asked. "If you were still the woman you were last week?"

Veyra almost smiled. Almost.

"The priests of Hollowmere bound the crown once," she said. "Their oracle will know what proof exists. Get it. Bring it to the river gate by the twelfth turn. And Princess—" she hesitated, then made herself finish, because a ledger entry half-written was worse than none, "—if your father is alive in there, as the chart suggests, then understand what you're asking my garrison to fight for. Not a rebellion. A rescue. Say it that way, and men follow. Say anything else, and they hesitate, and hesitation dies at gates."

Ilseva nodded slowly. "A rescue."

"A rescue," Veyra agreed. Then, to the scribe: "One more thing. The monastery sold your name. I bought the contract myself—it's in my first ledger, the debt, the price on your head. As of this morning I'm striking it." She drew the black ledger from her coat, opened it to the page, and in front of all of them, in front of her own corporal, drew a single line through *Astrid Galloway* and wrote in the margin, in her precise captain's hand: *Contract voided. Witnessed.*

The scribe stared at the struck name as though it were a wound closing. "Why?"

"Because debts that were manufactured shouldn't be honored," Veyra said, "and I have thirty pages of manufactured debts in this book, and I'm done collecting them." She closed the ledger. "Twelfth moon, river gate. Don't be late. Gates that open once don't open twice."

* * *

*Astrid*

They watched the hunters break camp and march north in good order, and only when the last rider topped the far rim did Astrid realize she had stopped breathing somewhere in the last quarter hour.

"They struck my name," she said again. It didn't sound more real the second time. "Out of the ledger. In front of witnesses."

"Mm." Sef was re-rolling the charts with hands that weren't quite steady. "Nineteen years of service, undone by one page in an annex. Be careful, Astrid. That's how it starts."

"How what starts?"

"Believing the crown can be reasoned with." He tied the oilcloth cord and looked up at her, and his eyes were tired and fierce at once. "It can't. But it can be *shocked*. There's a difference, and the difference is proof. We have six weeks to find something that shocks a woman who has spent her life refusing to be shocked."

"The oracle," Ilseva said quietly. They turned to her. She stood at the ford's edge with the water running bright past her boots, and the morning wind pulled at her plain cloak as if it remembered her palace silks. "Hollowmere bound the crown once. The priests who did it—they'll have left records. Instruments. Maybe even the original binding itself." She turned, and Astrid saw that her hands had curled into fists at her sides. "And if they don't have proof, then we go into the palace and we take my father out of that room, and everyone in the kingdom can look at his hollowed face and draw their own conclusions."

"That's not proof," Sef said. "That's a corpse walking."

"No," Ilseva said. "That's my father. And I am done letting the word *proof* stand between me and him." She started up the bank toward the road, then paused, not looking back. "Twelfth moon. River gate. We'll be there. All of us—including him, if I have anything left to say about it."

Astrid exchanged a glance with Sef. Six weeks. Hollowmere lay nine days' hard walk west, through country that was getting less safe every day the hunt ran on. And somewhere behind them, in a cheap red ledger against a captain's ribs, forty-one names were waiting to become testimony—or ammunition.

"Well," Astrid said, and was surprised to hear a ghost of laughter in her own voice, ragged but real. "Maren always said the dangerous part of any road isn't the bandits. It's the moment you realize the person hunting you might be right."

She shouldered her pack. The ford glittered. Somewhere west, under stone and silence, the oracle waited with whatever truth the old priests had buried alongside their order—and Astrid Galloway, debt-free for the first time in her life, went to collect it.
