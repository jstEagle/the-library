## 16. What the Gift Costs

The oracle's chamber smelled of cold ash and old honey.

Sef had expected incense, perhaps—some ceremonial sweetness to match the blind woman's reputation. Instead the Hollowmere priory kept its oracle in a round room beneath the chapel floor, walled in riverstone that wept moisture in slow beads down the mortar lines. A single brazier burned at the center, and around it sat cushions gone flat with decades of kneeling. The air tasted of things long finished burning.

Her name was Sister Ondine, though she had not answered to it in years, if the priests' hushed corrections were anything to judge by. She was small and folded like a letter someone had read too many times, her hands resting palm-up on her knees as though waiting to receive something. Her eyes were the pale gray of river fog, and they did not track. They did not need to.

She had been listening to Astrid for an hour.

Not speaking—listening. That was the unsettling part. Sef had watched oracles work before, in the capital, in the gilded annexes where the Cartographers' Guild kept its own tame seers, and those women and men performed. They spoke in riddles on schedule, priced their prophecies by the syllable. Sister Ondine simply sat with her face turned toward Astrid Galloway the way a flower turns toward light it cannot see, and listened to something beneath the scribe's voice, beneath even their breath.

Astrid sat across the brazier from her, hands in their lap, spine straight with the particular discipline of someone who has spent years being watched while copying other people's words. Sef knew that posture. He'd worn it through three days of interrogation in the palace undercrofts before the rope on the north wall had been conveniently frayed by a sympathetic guard whose name he still meant to repay.

"You carry it quietly," Ondine said at last. Her voice was dry paper, but there was warmth buried in it, deep down, like a coal at the heart of a dead fire. "Most of your kind burn loud. Candles leaping. Fires answering. Yours is—" she tilted her head, listening to something Sef could not hear, "—yours is ink."

"Ink," Astrid repeated.

"Written things want you. Words remember your touch." The blind face turned slightly, a compass finding north. "You are the unbinding kind. The kind the crown has spent two hundred years trying to breed out, buy out, burn out. And you have come to me asking how to break an oath-entity loose from a stolen throne." She smiled without much humor. "You ask expensive questions, little scribe."

Sef shifted against the wall where he stood, arms crossed, charts—the surviving half-charts, water-stained and scorched at every edge—rolled tight against his chest like a shield. "Can it be done? That's all we need to know. Can the binding be broken before the thirteenth turn?"

"Everything can be done," Ondine said mildly. "The question is what it costs."

The brazier popped. Somewhere above them, muffled through stone, one of the exiled priests began vespers, his voice thin and cracked and utterly indifferent to beauty.

"The entity wearing your king's face," Ondine continued, "is old. Older than the dynasty it wears. It does not rule by force—it rules by *agreement*. Every oath sworn to the crown, every knee bent, every 'so help me' whispered over a signed treaty, feeds it. It drinks inheritance itself. Father to son, mother to daughter, the whole long chain of promised loyalty, and it grows fat on the taste." Her hands turned over, palms down now, pressing against her knees as though holding something down. "It is nearly finished eating. One more moon and the binding completes, and then no ritual on this earth will pull it free, because there will be nothing left of the man it replaced to pull toward."

"So we break it now," Astrid said. "Before the thirteenth turn. How?"

Ondine was quiet for a moment. When she spoke again, her voice had changed—lower, slower, each word placed like a stone in a stream crossing.

"There is a rite. The old priests knew it; fragments survive in our archive, and I have carried the whole of it in my head since I was younger than you. It requires three things. A caster of the unbinding gift—that is you. A vessel to hold what is cast out—that is the crown itself, gold and circlet, made for exactly this purpose by men who feared their own kings. And blood." She paused. "Willing blood. An heir's willing blood, freely given, not taken. The oath-chain runs through royal veins, and only a hand on that chain can open the last link."

"Ilseva," Sef said quietly.

"If the princess consents," Ondine agreed. "And that is a problem for another day. Today I must tell you the rest, and the rest is the part no one wants to hear."

The vespers above them ended. In the silence that followed, Sef became aware of the water beading down the stones, each drop striking the floor with a small, patient sound, like a clock counting something.

"The unbinding rite does not use your gift, Astrid Galloway. It *consumes* it. All of it. Every thread of the magic in you goes into the casting, and when the rite ends, the well is empty. Not blocked. Not sleeping. Empty. You would live the rest of your life as any ordinary person lives—as I live." She touched her own fog-gray eyes briefly. "I paid a different price for my knowing, but I know what it is to spend a self and receive change back."

Astrid said nothing. Their face did something complicated and closed, the way it always did when anyone came near the subject of the gift—the flaring candles, the ink that moved for them, the whole secret life they'd hidden under a scribe's gray robe for years.

"And if the caster hesitates?" Sef asked. Because someone had to. Because Astrid wasn't asking, and the silence had a shape to it that frightened him.

Ondine turned her blind face toward him, and for a moment he felt seen straight through, catalogued, filed among her thousand certainties.

"If the caster hesitates—if the gift holds back, if any part of them grips what they are giving away—then the rite takes the rest instead. The body. The life. The unbinding demands totality: either everything you are beyond the ordinary, or everything you are entirely." She folded her hands again. "There is no partial payment. There never is, with old magic. It keeps honest books."

*It keeps honest books.* Sef thought of ledgers—of Astrid counting debt in margins, of Veyra and her two columns, of Maren Ostwick's confession carried like a dead coal across half a mountain range. Everyone in this story was paying something. He had begun to suspect the currency was always the same.

"Thank you," Astrid said.

Just that. Two words, level and courteous, the voice of a scribe acknowledging receipt of information. Then they rose, bowed to the oracle with the precise depth owed to a superior, and walked up the stairs out of the oracle's chamber without looking back.

Sef lingered.

"Sister," he said. "What didn't you tell them?"

The blind eyes found him with unnerving accuracy. "You care for them."

"That's not an answer."

"No," she agreed, and almost smiled. "But it's true, and truth is the only coin I have left." She leaned forward, and the brazier light moved on her face like water. "I told them everything the rite requires. What I did not tell them is what I heard, sitting here, listening beneath their voice. Your scribe has already decided which price to pay. They decided before they asked me. Perhaps before they ever left Thornfell." She settled back. "They intend to hesitate, cartographer. On purpose. At the final moment, they mean to grip the gift and hold it—and let the rite take their life instead, believing that death is cleaner than emptiness."

The words went into Sef like a blade slid between ribs—slowly, so he felt every inch.

"Why?" His voice came out wrong. "Why would anyone—"

"Ask yourself what they told you about their debt. About the monastery. About the years of copying forbidden texts for the institution that sold their name to hunters." Ondine shook her head slowly. "That child has spent a lifetime being owned. Being priced. Being told their worth in another's ledger. And now you offer them a miracle: a gift that makes them *theirs*, finally, wholly—a self no abbot or captain or crown can appraise. Do you understand? To give that up is to go back to being nothing. To be emptied is, to them, a form of surrender. Death at least dies *whole*."

Sef stood very still. The water dripped. The brazier breathed its small heat against his shins.

"They'll do it," he said. "They'll actually do it."

"Unless someone teaches them, between now and the twelfth moon, that an empty cup can be filled again by other means. That belonging chosen is worth more than belonging inherited." Ondine turned her face away, back toward whatever she listened to in the dark beneath the world. "Unless someone loves them loudly enough to make survival seem less like loss. Go. You have six weeks and a stubborn heart. Spend both wisely."

He climbed the stairs two at a time.

* * *

He did not confront Astrid that night. He wanted to—he rehearsed it, pacing the narrow cell the priests had given him, composing speeches and discarding them, imagining the exact pitch of voice that would crack Astrid's resolve open like a seal. But every speech sounded, in his own ears, like an argument, and arguments gave the arguer somewhere to retreat to. Astrid Galloway had survived an institution built on arguments. They could out-wait any debate he started.

So he watched instead.

Watching was a cartographer's discipline. You did not stare at the mountain; you noted where the snow clung, where the scree had slipped, where the meltwater ran. You assembled the truth from details the mountain didn't know it was showing you.

He noticed that Astrid stopped writing letters home—to Maren's memory, to the smuggler contacts, the small correspondence that had sustained them on the road. The last unsent letter sat folded in their pack, and after the second night in Hollowmere it disappeared altogether, and Astrid's hands looked strangely idle in the evenings, the way hands look when they've given up a habit they loved.

He noticed that Astrid began giving things away. Small things, so small anyone else would have missed it: a bone stylus pressed into the hands of a novice priest who admired it; the good wool socks, worn soft, left folded on Ilseva's borrowed cot when word came that the princess had escaped the palace and was making her own way east with Veyra's covert help; a packet of dried apricots set beside Sef's door with no note, because Astrid knew he liked them and had never once said so aloud.

An inventory of a life being quietly disbanded.

And he noticed—this on the fifth night, coming back late from the archive where he'd been redrawing the palace approaches for the twelfth time—that candlelight was leaking under the door of the scriptorium cell Astrid had been given, at an hour when everyone else slept.

He should have knocked. He told himself later that he should have knocked. But the corridor was dark and the door was old and had shrunk in its frame, leaving a finger-width gap at the bottom, and through that gap he saw what he saw.

Astrid sat at the cell's small desk, and before them lay not a book but a sheet of good paper, and on the paper, in their careful scribe's hand, they were writing.

Sef could not see the words. But he could see the ink.

It moved.

It gathered itself off the page in fine black threads, rising into the air like smoke deciding to be silk, and it wove itself around Astrid's wrists, their throat, the crown of their head—a living harness of written light, delicate and total. And Astrid sat inside it with their eyes closed and their expression—this was the part that would keep Sef awake for the rest of his life, however many of them remained—*peaceful*. Not afraid. Not grieving. Peaceful, the way a person looks stepping through a doorway they have been walking toward for years.

They were practicing.

He understood it all at once, with the sick clarity of a map suddenly revealing its scale. They weren't saying goodbye to the gift. They were learning to hold it—to grip it, exactly as Ondine had described—at the moment of casting. Rehearsing the hesitation that would kill them. Making sure that when the moment came, their hands would not shake, their will would not falter, and the rite would take everything cleanly rather than leave them hollowed and alive and *ordinary*.

They had chosen death over emptiness, and they were drilling it like a soldier drills a sword-form, alone, at night, by their own moving light.

Sef put his hand against the doorframe and did not push the door open. His breath came shallow. Through the gap, the ink-harness pulsed once, twice, gentle as a heartbeat, and Astrid's lips moved silently—counting, perhaps, or praying, or reciting some private ledger of debts and payments in which their own life figured as a sum already entered, already approved, awaiting only the date.

He stepped back from the door. The floorboard behind him creaked—softly, but in a priory at midnight, softly was enough.

The light under the door went out instantly.

For three long breaths Sef stood in the dark corridor, heart hammering, listening to the silence on the other side of the wood. Then, quietly, he said:

"Astrid. Open the door."

Nothing. The kind of nothing that was listening.

"I saw the ink," he said. "I know what you're doing in there. And I'm not going away, so you may as well save us both the pretense."

A long pause. Then the latch lifted, and the door opened onto darkness and the faint smell of lamp oil and iron-gall ink, and Astrid stood in it, robe pulled close, face composed into the mild courtesy of a scribe interrupted at work.

"You're up late," they said.

"We need to talk about the rite."

"What about it? The requirements are clear. Caster, vessel, willing blood. We have all three, or we'll have them soon enough, once Ilseva arrives." Astrid's voice was perfectly steady. That steadiness was the worst thing Sef had ever heard. "Is something unclear?"

"Yes." He kept his voice low, fierce, pitched under the sleeping priory's hearing. "What happens to *you*."

"Nothing that isn't necessary."

"Astrid—"

"Don't." The word came out sharp, and for a fraction of a second the composure cracked and he saw underneath it—not fear, worse than fear, a kind of *certainty*, bedrock-hard, the certainty of someone who has done their sums and trusts them. "Don't make this a scene in a hallway, Sef. Please."

"Then let me make it a scene in your cell." He stepped past them, into the dark, because he had learned on the road that Astrid yielded ground to force of presence more readily than to argument. "Ondine told me. After you left. She told me what she heard under your voice."

Something flickered across Astrid's face—the first true movement he'd seen there all night. "Did she."

"She says you plan to hesitate. On purpose. That you've decided dying whole is better than living empty." He laughed, and it came out bitter. "Do you know how that sounds? From the outside? You've spent your whole life being told what you're worth by other people's ledgers, and the moment you finally own something of your own, you—you *return it*. Like it's still someone else's property. Like you're still paying a debt."

"It's not like that."

"Then tell me what it's like."

Astrid was silent for a long time. Outside, wind moved through the priory pines, and somewhere a shutter knocked gently at its frame. When they finally spoke, their voice had dropped its armor, and what was underneath was quiet and tired and terribly young.

"When the hunters came to Thornfell," they said, "and every candle in the scriptorium lit at once—I was terrified. You know that. But you don't know that in the middle of the terror, running through the pass with the smoke behind us, I was *happy*. For the first time in my life, the thing I am wasn't a liability to be hidden or a debt to be worked off. It was mine. It got me out. It's getting us to the throne room. It's the first thing I've ever had that nobody gave me and nobody can repossess." Their hands curled at their sides. "And you're asking me to hand it over anyway. To finish the rite and wake up tomorrow as plain Astrid Galloway, scribe, no gift, no—no *self*—just another person the world gets to price. I'd rather die owning myself than live as collateral again. Is that so hard to understand?"

"Yes," Sef said. "Because you're wrong."

"Oh?"

"About what emptying costs. You think it gives you back to the ledgers. It doesn't." He took a step closer, close enough to see the ink stains on their fingers even in the dark, the ones that never fully washed out. "Listen to me. If you die in that throne room, you know what happens? The kingdom writes your story. Veyra's council, the regents, the priests—they'll decide what you were. *The scribe who died breaking the crown.* Noble, tragic, useful. They'll put you in their histories and their oaths and their monuments, and you'll belong to them forever, more completely than you ever belonged to Thornfell. Dying doesn't free you, Astrid. Dying is the last surrender. It's handing them your ending the way the monastery handed them your name."

"That's not—"

"But if you live—" his voice caught, and he pushed through it, "—if you live, empty, ordinary, *free*—then you get to choose what you are next. Nobody's ledger. Nobody's prophecy. You could teach. You could walk the roads. You could—" he stopped himself before the word *stay* could escape, because it was too large for this dark cell and this fragile hour, "—you could do anything. Emptiness isn't surrender. It's the first blank page you've ever been given. And you of all people should know what a blank page is worth."

Astrid stared at him. In the darkness their eyes held a light that had nothing to do with candles—something older, moving, the gift itself looking out through them, weighing him the way it weighed all written things.

"You believe that," they said at last, wonderingly. "You actually believe emptiness could be a beginning."

"I map things," Sef said. "Blank spaces are where I live."

For a moment—one suspended, trembling moment—he thought he had reached them. Their shoulders lowered a fraction; their breath changed; the hard certainty in their face softened into something questioning, something that might, with time and gentleness, become a different decision.

Then the bell in the priory tower began to ring.

Not vespers. Not matins. The alarm pattern—three strikes, pause, three strikes—and beneath it, running footsteps in the cloister, and a novice's voice cracking high with urgency:

"Riders on the pass! Crown colors! They're asking for the fugitives by name!"

Astrid moved first, snuffing the last trace of ink-light, sweeping the practice papers off the desk and into the brazier's cold ashes. The conversation, the confession, the trembling edge of a changed mind—all of it vanished into the practiced efficiency of the fugitive they had become.

"Sef." They were already pulling on their coat, already the scribe-soldier, already armored. "Whatever we were saying—it keeps. Get your charts."

He stood frozen for one heartbeat longer, staring at the cold brazier where their rehearsal had gone up—or rather, where it hadn't, because ash kept nothing, and neither, he feared, did Astrid Galloway's resolve.

*A blank page,* he thought desperately. *Please. Take the blank page.*

Then the bell rang again, and he ran to fetch his maps.

Down in the courtyard, torches bloomed along the priory wall, and the gates groaned open onto the night, and far up the mountain road, pinpricks of lantern light descended through the pines—too many lanterns, moving with the disciplined patience of people who had all the time in the world and knew exactly whom they had come for.

At their head rode a figure Sef recognized from half-burned dispatches: tall, straight-backed, carrying herself like a drawn blade.

Captain Veyra had found them.
