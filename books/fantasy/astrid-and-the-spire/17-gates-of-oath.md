## 17. Gates of Oath

The gates of the palace had never opened for Ilseva. They opened *around* her—swung wide by servants bowing, by guards saluting, by the whole machinery of deference that had carried her through childhood like a barge on still water. She had passed beneath them a thousand times and never once touched them.

Tonight she pushed them herself.

They were older than she remembered, or perhaps she had simply never looked. Bronze over oak, taller than three men, and worked across their faces with a frieze of kneeling figures: kings, queens, lords, all bent with their foreheads to a ground that was itself a crown. The first king of Vhalen knelt at the bottom left corner, and every ruler after him descended in a spiral toward the center, where the metal had been worn smooth by centuries of hands.

Ilseva's palms were bleeding. She noticed only distantly, the way one notices rain. Behind her, up the long switchback road from the salt gate, came the sound of boots—not many, but enough. Captain Veyra walked at their head with her lantern unshuttered, which was either trust or a trap, and Ilseva had stopped being able to tell the difference in anything anymore.

"The postern watch changes at the half-bell," Veyra said. Her voice was flat as slate, the voice of a woman reading from her second ledger. "After that, the inner court is yours until dawn. What you do with it—" She stopped. Adjusted the strap of her sword. "I have written down what I know. All of it. If I don't send word by morning, my second-in-command opens the ledger whether I live or not."

"You're afraid," Ilseva said.

Veyra looked at her then, and there was something almost like respect in the hunt captain's tired eyes. "Yes, Highness. I am. That's how you'll know I'm telling the truth." She turned to her soldiers. "No one follows the princess. No one sees what she sees. Am I understood?"

*Highness.* The word landed strangely, like a coin dropped into an empty well. Ilseva had been *the princess* her whole life, an ornament, a dose of poppy syrup twice daily, a girl kept soft and sleepy in rooms that smelled of crushed violets so she would not ask why the windows were painted shut. No one had ever said *highness* as though it meant something she might actually *do*.

She climbed the inner stair alone.

* * *

The palace at night was a body pretending to sleep.

She knew its sounds the way she knew her own pulse: the settling groan of the west wing, the drip of the fountain court, the far-off cough of a guard who thought no one heard him weep after his letters from home stopped being answered. She moved through it in servant's grey, barefoot in the last stretch, because shoes rang against marble and because—though she would never say this aloud—she wanted to feel the floor of her own house under her own feet, unmediated, unpoisoned, hers.

The corridors near the throne room were wrong. She had known they would be; Sef had described them, his voice low and urgent across the smugglers' fire, drawing the seal-lines from memory on the back of a burnt chart. But knowing was nothing. The air here tasted of hot wax and old honey, thick enough to chew. The candles burned without flickering, each flame standing perfectly straight, like soldiers at attention, like figures in a frieze. And the shadows fell in directions the light did not explain.

Her father's study door stood open. She could not help herself; she looked in.

It was exactly as he had left it—or exactly as *something* had decided he had left it. His inkwell sat uncapped. His chair was pushed back at the angle of a man rising in haste. On the desk lay a letter addressed to her mother, dead nine years, in her father's hand, dated three days ago.

Ilseva made herself walk past it. Later, she told herself. Grieve later. Break things later.

The doors of the throne room rose before her, white ashwood banded in gold, carved with the same spiral of kneeling figures as the gates outside—but here the spiral did not end. It continued onto the lintel, down the jambs, across the threshold stones, a procession of bowed heads flowing into the room itself, and Ilseva understood suddenly, viscerally, why the servants never cleaned this hall at night, why even the boldest petitioners asked to be excused before dusk.

The oaths didn't stop at the door. They flowed *in*. Everything promised to the crown, everywhere in the kingdom, drained toward this room like blood toward a wound.

She put her hands on the doors.

They were warm.

* * *

Inside, the throne room was lit by a single source: the crown itself, resting on a cushion of black velvet atop the dais, glowing with a pale gold light like the last hour of a dying day. Not the king's working circlet—the great crown, the Oathcrown, the one worn only at coronations and funerals, the one Ilseva had seen exactly once, from a balcony, when she was six, and had dreamed about ever after with a longing she'd been ashamed of.

It was beautiful. That was the horror of it. It was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen, and it was awake.

And beside it, seated on the steps of the dais like a tired old man waiting up past bedtime, was her father.

"Daddy?" she said, and hated how small her voice came out, six years old again, balcony-light and fireworks.

He turned. It was his face. The deep lines around his eyes, the grey coming in at his temples, the small crooked tooth visible when he smiled—he smiled now—that the palace physicians had offered to fix and he had refused, saying a king should keep one imperfection to remind him he was mortal.

"Little lark," he said.

The nickname went through her like a blade finding the gap in armor. Only he had called her that. Only he knew it. She took a step forward, then another, and the warmth of the room wrapped around her shoulders like a cloak, like arms, like—

Like the syrup. Like the violet rooms. A sweetness that made everything soft and far away and *fine*.

"I've missed you," her father said. "You've been away so long, little lark. So long asleep. Come and sit with me. There's so much to tell you about your inheritance."

His voice was right. That was the unbearable thing. It was pitched exactly as it had always been, warm gravel and patience, the voice that had read her ballads and explained the tax rolls and laughed at her terrible drawings of horses. Whatever wore him had studied him for years. Had learned him the way Astrid had learned the smuggler roads: thoroughly, lovingly, for the purpose of travel along them.

"You're not him," Ilseva said. Each word cost her something, pried loose from somewhere deep. "My father is in the sealed rooms below. He whispers my name. I've heard him."

Something flickered across the borrowed face—not anger. Pity. That was worse.

"He is tired," the thing said gently. "He has carried this kingdom thirty years, and a kingdom is heavy, little lark. Heavier than any one man should hold. I only carry it *with* him. Isn't that kinder? Isn't it kinder than watching him break?"

"It isn't kind," she whispered. "It's eating him."

"It's *keeping* him." The entity rose, and it moved in her father's body the way a hand moves inside a glove, slightly too smooth, slightly too fluid, and still her heart lurched toward it, still some animal part of her cried *father, father, follow him*. "As I will keep you. As I have kept every heir since the first. That is what a crown is, Ilseva. You've finally come close enough to see. It is not a hat. It is a promise that outlives everyone who makes it, and promises must live somewhere, and drink something, and be fed."

It extended her father's hand. Palm up. An invitation, or a summons.

"Come. Kneel with me. You've been kneeling your whole life—you just did it lying down, in pretty rooms, with sweet smoke. This is only honesty. Kneel, and you will never be lonely again. Never be drugged again. Never be *afraid* again. Every oath your ancestors swore is already in your blood, little lark. I'm not asking you to give me anything new. I'm only asking you to stop fighting what you already are."

And the terrible truth, the truth she would carry to her grave, was that her knees began to bend.

Not because she was weak. She would need years to understand this, and even then only partly, but in this moment she felt it raw: the oaths were real. They were *in her*, woven into her before birth, generation upon generation of bowed heads pressed into bronze and ashwood and law, and they were not chains from outside. They were marrow. When the crown called, her own blood answered, warm and willing, and the sweetness rose in her like dawn, and the fear fell away, and oh—oh—it would be so *easy*, it would be rest, it would be belonging, the thing she had starved for in her painted-shut rooms, and her father's face was smiling at her with such love, such perfect counterfeit love—

Her knee touched stone.

The cold of the marble shocked up through her leg, and in that sliver of sensation she caught a thread of herself, one thin screaming thread: *the floor is cold, the floor is real, cold is real, cold is yours.*

But her other knee was going down. Her head was bowing. The crown's light poured over her like honey, like syrup, like sleep, and somewhere very far away she heard herself think: *this is how kingdoms die. Not by conquest. By daughters kneeling.*

Then hands seized her under the arms and hauled her backward.

* * *

"No you don't—no, none of that—"

Sef's voice, cracked and furious and alive, the most human sound in the world. He dragged her back across the threshold and the sweetness tore away like a bandage, leaving her gasping in the cold honest dark of the antechamber, her knees smarting, her cheeks wet, her whole body shaking with the effort of having *not done the easiest thing she had ever done*.

"You followed me," she managed.

"Veyra's men can't count," Sef said grimly. He was gaunt, road-worn, smelling of woodsmoke and horse, and he had never looked more trustworthy in his life. Behind him, half-shadowed in the doorway, stood Astrid—a scribe's ink-stained fingers, a hunter's wary stance, eyes that glowed faintly at the edges like banked coals. The gifted one. The unbinding. Ilseva had heard the stories around the smugglers' fires and had not believed half of them.

She believed them now. Because Astrid looked past her, into the throne room, at the figure of her father still standing palm-upturned on the dais, and Astrid's face held no temptation at all. Only grief, and fury, and readiness.

"She nearly knelt," Sef said quietly, to Astrid, not to shame her but to warn her. "It wasn't weakness. It's in the blood. Everyone born to an oathed line—"

"I know," Astrid said. Something crossed their face, some private ledger closing. "I know exactly how deep inherited things go."

Ilseva got her feet under her. Stood. Wiped her face with the heel of her hand, roughly, like a child, like a queen, like both at once.

"It spoke to me in his voice," she said. "It used my name. My *real* name, the one no one uses." Her jaw tightened. "It offered me everything I've ever wanted. And the worst part—the part I want you both to hear—is that I believe it would have given it to me. Forever. Warm and sweet and safe, in a room with painted windows."

"That's not everything you want," Astrid said.

"How would you know?"

"Because you're standing in the cold," Astrid said, "and you haven't gone back in."

The words settled over Ilseva like a mantle. She looked down at her bleeding palms, at the smear of red on the threshold stone—her blood, freely shed, freely *given*, no oath in it, no compulsion, only a girl's stubborn refusal—and she felt something shift in her chest, some load-bearing wall of her old self coming down.

Below them, behind the sealed doors, her true father whispered her childhood name to the dark.

Above them, on the dais, the false one waited with an open hand.

Between, the thirteenth moon hung somewhere beyond the palace roof, one turn from full, one turn from forever.

"Get me to the sealed rooms," Ilseva said. Her voice did not shake. "Before it feeds again. Before the moon turns. I want to see his face when he wakes up—and I want it to see mine standing when it falls."

Astrid smiled then, small and fierce, and drew a knife, and stepped toward the doors.

The candles in the throne room, all together, all at once, went out.
