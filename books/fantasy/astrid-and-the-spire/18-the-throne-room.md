## 18. The Throne Room

The palace of Vael Corrin had been built to be approached, and that was its cruelest trick. Every corridor Astrid ran down had been designed to make a visitor feel small in the correct way—small before power, small before history, small before the long dead kings whose marble faces watched from alcoves with the patience of men who had never once needed to hurry. Astrid had spent nine years being made to feel small by gentler instruments: ledgers, candle stubs, the abbot's soft voice reading out the sum of what they owed. They knew the architecture of diminishment when they saw it. They refused it now with every stride.

Sef ran beside them, his half-burned charts crushed against his chest like a child's comfort. Behind them came Ilseva's people—the princess had opened the sealed wing herself, her father's own signet pressed into wax, her hand steady in a way that made Astrid understand how much it must have cost her—and behind *them*, distantly, the sounds of Veyra holding the gates. Steel on steel, muffled by stone, like weather happening in another country.

"The throne room is through the Hall of Inheritance," Ilseva said. She had not run with them; she walked, fast and terrible, as though the palace were hers already and always had been. Perhaps it was. "There are guards."

"There won't be," Sef said. "Not past the third door. He doesn't need guards anymore. He needs witnesses."

Astrid did not ask what he meant. They had read enough of the old texts—the ones the abbot burned, the one hidden inside their coat for six weeks now, warm as a second heartbeat—to know what a binding looked like at its end. The last pages described a room full of kneeling courtiers, an oath spoken in unison, and a king who no longer blinked.

* * *

The Hall of Inheritance was lit entirely by candles.

That was wrong. Astrid knew it was wrong the way they knew a forged signature—some deep scribe's instinct flaring before thought could catch up. The palace had glass in its windows, moonlight in abundance; there was no reason for a thousand candles except that someone wanted it so. And every flame stood perfectly still. Not one guttered. Not one leaned in the draft from the open doors. A thousand points of light frozen mid-gesture, like insects in amber.

Astrid's gift rose toward them without permission. It had done this since the scriptorium—the candles answering them, leaning toward their magic like flowers toward sun—and they had learned to clamp down on it, to be a closed fist. But here, in this hall, the flames did not lean. They *waited*. All thousand of them, oriented toward the far end of the hall, toward the doors of the throne room, and Astrid understood with a lurch that they were not candles at all. They were a congregation. They were the audience.

"They're feeding," Sef whispered. "Every flame. Look at the wax—it never shortens." He had stopped walking. His face, in the still light, was the color of old paper. "My grandfather served in this palace. He used to say the king's hall was kept bright because the crown liked to see itself reflected. I thought it was vanity."

"It's appetite," Astrid said.

Ilseva had stopped too. She was looking at the candles with an expression Astrid could not name—not fear, not quite grief. Recognition, maybe. "He used to hold court here at night," she said quietly. "When I was small. My father. He'd let me sit on the steps of the dais and watch him settle disputes. He said a king should do his work where the people can see it." Her jaw tightened. "She hasn't held open court in two years. She rules from behind the seal now. From behind the *oaths*."

*She.* The entity. Astrid had heard it called many things across six weeks of burned charts and oracle warnings—the crown-bound, the inheritor, the thing that wears the king—but Ilseva said *she* with the particular bitterness of someone who had competed with a rival for a parent's love and lost.

"Your highness," Sef began.

"Don't." Ilseva lifted a hand. "Don't call me that in here. Not in front of it. Titles are oaths, and oaths are food." She looked at Astrid, and her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely clear. "Call me nothing. Just go."

They went.

* * *

The throne room doors were white oak banded in silver, tall as three men, carved with the genealogy of the Corrin line—a tree of kings, root to crown, each face distinct, each name legible. Astrid had seen the design before, in a book. In the book, the tree ended with the current king.

Here, the carving continued past him. Below the last named king, the wood darkened and swelled into new growth—faces without names, generations of them, budding from the royal line like galls on a branch. Faces that had not been born yet. Faces that would never be born, if the thirteenth turn came and went.

And beneath the whole tree, coiled through the roots, a serpent of silver inlay. Its head rested just under the throne itself. Its mouth was open.

Astrid pushed the doors.

They swung without sound. That was the worst of it, somehow—all that mass moving in perfect silence, the hinges oiled or enchanted or simply obedient, admitting them the way a mouth admits food.

The throne room was round. Astrid had expected vaulted ceilings, banners, the machinery of majesty. Instead the walls curved close, unadorned, the stone polished to a dull gleam, and the ceiling was low enough that the sense was not of grandeur but of *interiority*—of standing inside something. A throat. A keeping-place. The thousand candles from the hall had followed, or their light had, pooling along the curved walls in a ring of frozen fire.

And on the throne sat the king.

Astrid had seen his likeness on proclamations, on coin, on the seal-line Sef had marked with his treasonous little anomalies. King Aldous Corrin IV, sixty-one years of age, gray-bearded, heavy-browed, a man who had signed away half his authority in his own lifetime and been praised for his humility. The figure on the throne matched the likeness exactly.

Except that he did not blink.

His chest did not move. His hands lay on the arms of the throne with the relaxed weight of a sleeping man, but his eyes were open and fixed on the middle distance, and they had the wet shine of eyes that are maintained rather than inhabited. Wax, Astrid thought, remembering Hollowmere's oracle and her warning about the Palace of Wax. But it wasn't wax. It was worse. It was *care*. Something was tending this body the way a scribe tends a manuscript it cannot bear to finish—keeping the vellum supple, keeping the ink from fading, preserving a text whose author left long ago.

"Astrid Galloway."

The voice came from everywhere—from the walls, from the candles, from the king's unmoving mouth, which did not move even as it spoke. It was a woman's voice, warm and unhurried, the voice of someone who has never once been interrupted.

"You've brought my property into my house."

Astrid's hand found the book inside their coat. "It was never yours."

"No?" The king's head turned. Slowly. The movement had the smoothness of poured honey, and it was obscene, because the body performed it while the eyes stayed fixed, so that the face turned around its own gaze like a wheel around its axle. When the motion completed, the king was looking directly at Astrid, and the eyes had changed—they had come alive, all at once, lit from within by something patient and vast and terribly interested.

"Everything in this kingdom is mine," the entity said, through the king's lips. "That is what inheritance means. You of all people should understand. You have spent your whole life learning what it means to be owned."

The words landed with a precision that felt surgical. Astrid felt their breath go shallow.

"I've read your ledger," the entity continued, almost gently. "I keep all the ledgers. Every debt in Thornfell, every oath in the palace, every promissory whisper between smugglers—I am the great book, Astrid Galloway, and your page has been open before me for years. Nine years of indenture. Do you know what I see when I read it?"

"I imagine you see profit," Astrid managed.

"I see *waste*." The word cracked like a whip, and the candles flared, all thousand of them, and in the sudden blaze Astrid saw that the walls of the throne room were not bare after all. They were inscribed. Oaths, thousands upon thousands of them, cut into the stone in every generation's handwriting, covering every surface, the names of everyone who had ever sworn anything to the crown of Corrin, layered over each other until the original stone was invisible. The room was not a room. It was a ledger with a ceiling.

"I see a gift like mine," the entity said, softer now, "poured into copying other people's words. I see a mind that memorized a hundred smuggler roads because freedom was sold to you by the inch and you bought it back the only way you could. I see a debt that was never honest—that was manufactured, Astrid, before you could walk, by frightened people hiding you from me. Maren Ostwick's confession. Did you deliver it? Did you learn what you are?"

Astrid's throat closed. *Grandmother. Bloodline. Unbinding.* Yes. Yes, they had learned.

"Then you know," the entity said, "that we are the same kind of thing. I was bound once, long ago, by priests who feared what I am. They chained me into a circlet and told themselves the chain was mercy. For three hundred years I have worn crowns instead of a body, and I have learned everything about belonging, Astrid. Everything. I know what it tastes like when ten thousand people swear themselves to you at once. I know what it is to be *needed* beyond question, beyond doubt, beyond loneliness." The king's body leaned forward on the throne, and though the movement was slow, it carried the weight of something enormous shifting its attention. "You have never belonged anywhere. I can give you that. Permanently."

"I don't want to belong to something that eats people."

"Then belong *with* me." The voice wrapped around them, warm as candle-wax, warm as the scriptorium on a winter night. "Stay. Be my scribe. Not my servant—my *equal*. I will burn your ledger myself. Every debt, every oath ever sworn against your name, gone. Thornfell will become yours, not theirs—an archive where gifts like ours are taught openly. Isn't that what you dreamed? I will build it for you tonight. I will write your name into the foundation stones, and no one—no hunter, no abbot, no priest—will ever again be able to tell you what you owe."

The temptation was total. That was what Astrid had not been prepared for—not the scale of it, but the *specificity*. This was not a demon offering gold and kingdoms in the abstract. This was something that had read their life the way Astrid read manuscripts: closely, covetously, noting every marginal longing. The archive. The debt erased. The name written in stone. Every private thing they had ever wanted at three in the morning, offered back to them in a voice like absolution.

And underneath it, sweetest of all, the unspoken promise: *you will never have to choose again.*

Astrid's eyes stung. Their gift stirred in their chest, answering the thousand candles, wanting—wanting so badly to say yes, to sink into that vast attention, to be seen completely by something that would never look away—

That was when they understood the trap. Not the lie; there was no lie. Everything the entity offered, it would deliver. The trap was the truth of it. Belonging given by a keeper was just ownership with better lighting. Astrid had been owned for nine years by people who called their debt *placement*, who called their labor *vocation*, who burned books and sold names and called it stewardship. The entity was offering them Thornfell with the serial numbers filed off. A larger monastery. A kinder cage. A cage that genuinely loved its bird.

Their hand stopped trembling.

"No," Astrid said.

The candles dimmed, all together, like a held breath.

"No," Astrid said again, louder, and their voice echoed strangely in the round room, off the ten thousand inscribed oaths, and their gift rose with it—not clamped now, not a closed fist, but opening, spreading through them like dawn through a window. "You're right. We are the same kind of thing. That's exactly why I know what you are. Because I know what I wanted, all those years, when I copied forbidden texts by stolen candlelight. I didn't want to be needed. I wanted to be *free*. And everything you've just offered me is the opposite of free, dressed up in my own handwriting."

The entity was silent for a moment. When it spoke again, the warmth was gone, and what remained was older than the kingdom.

"The twelfth moon rises in an hour," it said. "At the thirteenth turn, the binding completes, and there is no ritual, no heir, no clever third path. There is only me, forever, and everyone in this kingdom kneeling inside me, safe and fed and *mine*—including your cartographer, including your princess, including you. You cannot reach the king. You cannot reach the crown. You are standing inside the only body I have, little scribe, and the doors are shut."

Behind them, the great doors boomed closed.

Astrid did not turn around. Ahead, on the dais, the hollowed king sat in his maintained flesh, and somewhere behind those maintained eyes—Ilseva had heard him whisper her childhood name, had sworn it—somewhere in there, Aldous Corrin was still waiting to wake.

"One hour," the entity said, and the king's mouth curved into a smile that did not belong to any living man. "Convince me otherwise."

Astrid stepped forward, onto the first stair of the dais, and reached into their coat for the book, and the blood-prick of Ilseva's willing drop waiting in its vial against their heart, and began to climb.

* * *

Above the palace, through parting cloud, the twelfth moon cleared the eastern towers—swollen, silver, one turn shy of full permanence—and its light fell through the high windows of the throne room and laid a pale road straight up the dais, as if the sky itself had drawn Astrid a map.

They followed it.
