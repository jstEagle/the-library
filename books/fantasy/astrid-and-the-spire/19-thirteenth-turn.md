## 19. Thirteenth Turn

The throne room smelled of tallow and old honey.

That was the first thing Astrid noticed—how wrong the smell was. A royal hall should have smelled of stone and cold air, of torch pitch and the ghost of a thousand petitions. Instead it was sweet, cloying, like wax left too long in the sun, like a candle burning down inside its own skin. The walls themselves had gone soft at the edges. She could see it where the moonlight fell through the high windows: mortar gone glassy, stone sweating, the whole palace slowly being swallowed from the inside out by something that wore architecture the way the thing on the throne wore a king.

Twelve moons hung in the vaulted ceiling above the dais. Not painted. *Grown*—pale discs of nacre that had seeped up through the gold leaf over years, each one a little fuller than the last, arranged in an ascending arc that ended above the empty thirteenth place. Astrid's gift stirred against her ribs when she looked at them, the way a dog stirs when it scents something buried.

*Thirteen turns,* Sef had said, weeks ago, on a frozen road with his half-burned charts spread between them like evidence of a crime. *And then it's not wearing him anymore. It's being him. Forever.*

Below, in the great doors they'd come through, she could hear Veyra's voice—low, clipped, giving orders to hunters who no longer knew whose side they were on—and Ilseva's, sharper, refusing to be left behind. Good. Let them come. But slowly. Slowly enough for Astrid to do what she'd come to do.

She walked down the length of the hall alone.

Her boots made no sound. That should have frightened her; instead it felt like all those years of copying forbidden texts by candlelight, learning to move through the scriptorium after hours without waking the abbot's dog. She had spent her whole life practicing silence for someone else's benefit. Tonight the silence belonged to her.

The entity sat on the throne with her father's body folded neatly beneath it, hands resting on the armrests, head tilted with an expression of mild, patient interest—the look of a man listening to a long petition he has already decided to grant. Up close, the wrongness resolved into details. The king's face was right. That was the horror of it. Every line was correct: the deep crease between the brows, the scar through the left eyebrow that Ilseva had described to them in a whisper, the gray coming in at the temples. It was a portrait done by someone who had studied the original for decades and loved it in the way a counterfeiter loves money.

Only the eyes were wrong. They caught the moonlight and held it too long, the way wax holds a flame.

"Scribe," it said. Its voice was the king's voice, warm and gravelled, and hearing it made Astrid's throat tighten with a grief that wasn't hers. "You've read so many of my oaths. I know your handwriting. You copied the Thornfell covenant three times, didn't you? Beautiful work. Such even strokes." It smiled with the king's mouth. "You have been bound your whole life. Debt. Indenture. Ink under your fingernails and never once a page of your own. I could unmake all of it. One word from me and every ledger in this kingdom burns."

Astrid stopped at the foot of the dais, three steps below the throne, exactly where a petitioner would stand.

"I know," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "That's what you offered Veyra, isn't it? And the abbot. And every priest who ever helped bind you. You don't threaten people. You *relieve* them. You take the weight off, and then you own the shape of their shoulders forever."

"It is not ownership. It is *rest*." The entity leaned forward, and the movement was fluid in a way the old king's body should not have permitted. "Every oath is a debt, scribe. Every inheritance is a chain passed hand to hand. I am simply the collector who forgives. Kneel, and your debt dies tonight. Your gift stops flaring in the dark and betraying you. You stop running. You go home."

"I don't have a home."

"You will. I will build you one."

"There it is." Astrid laughed—a small, cracked sound that echoed strangely in the sweet-smelling air. "You know what Maren Ostwick told me, dying, on her filthy wonderful floor? She said: *girl, anyone who offers to erase your debt is telling you what they're afraid you'll buy free.*" She took one step up. "You're afraid of me."

Something flickered behind the king's eyes. For half a heartbeat the wax-sheen thinned, and Astrid saw—not the entity, but *through* it: a hollowed man far beneath, deep as a well, and at the bottom of the well two lips moving around a name.

*Ilsa.*

Ilseva's childhood name. The true king, alive down there in the dark, whispering for his daughter.

"I'm not afraid of a scribe," the entity said, but the warmth had cooled by a degree. "I am patient with one."

"Patience runs out in—" Astrid glanced up at the twelve moons, and at the thirteenth place, bare and waiting. "Four hours. Maybe five. Then you don't need patience anymore. So let's not waste it pretending this is a negotiation."

She climbed the second step.

The air thickened. She felt it press against her skin like wet wool, felt the hall itself lean toward her—all those harvested oaths soaked into the stone, generations of kneeling, the accumulated weight of a kingdom's inherited promises. This was what the oracle had tried to warn her about. Not the entity's power. The *building's*. A throne room was nothing but oath given form, and she was standing in the deepest reservoir of it in the world.

Her gift rose to meet it.

It had always been like this, since the night the candles lit themselves in the scriptorium—her magic answering pressure with light, terror with illumination. Unbinding, the blind oracle had called it. The old kind. The kind that doesn't add to the world but *opens* it, finds the seam in any binding and slides a finger in. All her life she'd thought the gift was the problem. Standing here, drowning in a kingdom's worth of false choice, she finally understood: the gift had never been a debt at all. It was the only thing about her that had always been free.

The entity stood.

The king's body unfolded from the throne with terrible grace, and the twelve moons overhead brightened, and the honey-smell became a roar of sweetness, and Astrid felt the full weight of the thing turn its attention on her like a lighthouse beam.

"You will kneel," it said, and it was no longer entirely the king's voice. There were other voices in it now, layered, ancient, *hungry*—the voices of every oath it had ever eaten. "Your grandmother knelt. Your mother knelt. The debt was written before you were born, child. It is *in* you. You cannot refuse what is already mine—"

"No," Astrid said. "See, that's where you've made your mistake. The same one everyone makes."

She reached into her coat and drew out the book she'd saved from the abbot's fire—the heretical text, the one she'd carried against her heart through smoke and mountains and ambush, the one whose margins she had filled with her own handwriting across a thousand miles of road. She had finally, somewhere on the salt road, understood what it actually was. Not heresy. *Instructions.* Written by the last priests who had bound the crown the first time, back when binding meant containment instead of feeding.

"The debt was manufactured," she said. "Maren's confession proved it. My bloodline was bound to keep me small, to hide what I am, so no unbinding-gift would ever grow up next to your cage. You didn't inherit your power over me. You *forged the paperwork.*" Another step. One more between them. "And forged things can be broken. That's the whole of my art, creature. I have spent ten years copying bindings. I know exactly how they fail."

The entity struck—not with a fist but with the hall itself. The floor heaved. Oath-pressure crashed down on her like a wave, a thousand voices at once: *kneel, obey, honor thy father, serve the crown, the crown endures, the crown—*

Astrid opened her gift.

Light poured out of her—not candlelight this time, not the panicked flare of the scriptorium, but something deliberate, a blade of it, and where it touched the falling weight of oaths they *unraveled*. She heard them go, thread by thread, old vows sighing apart like rope cut under tension. Somewhere in the dark of the well beneath the entity, a hollowed man gasped.

The entity screamed with a hundred throats.

"You cannot—" it snarled, the king's face splitting along lines that faces shouldn't split along, wax showing beneath, "—you cannot hold all of us—"

"I'm not holding you," Astrid said, through her teeth, shaking, blazing, more alive than she had ever been. "I'm *offering you better accommodations.*"

Because that was the third path. The one she'd found at three in the morning, scribbling in the margin of the heretical text while Sef slept beside the fire. The ritual required a willing heir's blood—that was the price the oracle named, the impossible requirement, the trap of sacrifice. But the priests who wrote the text had assumed the blood would pay the caster's cost. They'd never imagined an heir who gave it freely, not to save herself, not to save her father, but because she *chose* it. Willingness wasn't currency. Willingness was a key. And a key, turned in the right lock, didn't spend anything at all.

It only opened a door.

The great doors boomed open behind her. Ilseva came up the aisle at a run, skirts torn, Veyra's hunters fanning out behind her with weapons they weren't sure they should draw—and Ilseva didn't slow, didn't hesitate, didn't kneel. She reached the dais, took in the split-faced thing wearing her father, and Astrid watched her flinch and *keep walking*, which was braver than anything Astrid had done in her life.

"Astrid." Ilseva's voice shook. Her eyes did not. "Tell me what you need."

"Your blood. One drop. Given, not taken—you have to mean it, Ilseva, it has to be a choice, or it's just another oath and it'll feed him."

"What does it choose?"

"Not sacrifice." Astrid held out her hand, palm up, the way you offer feed to a strange horse. "Inheritance. He's spent sixty years eating what your family owed. Give him something your family *chose* instead. Something no one can collect. It'll be the first thing he's ever tasted that he can't digest."

Ilseva stared at her. Then at the thing on the throne, which was watching them both now with twelve moons' worth of hunger and, for the first time, something adjacent to fear.

"He'll be in there," Ilseva whispered. "My father. When it's done. He'll still be in there?"

"That's why we're doing it this way."

Ilseva bit her thumb open on her own teeth and pressed the bead of red into Astrid's palm.

It was the smallest thing. A drop of blood, warm, ordinary, smelling of iron and life. Astrid closed her fingers around it and felt it blaze like a coal—because willingness, freely given, was the one substance in the world the entity had never consumed, and it burned him the way sunlight burns something that has lived sixty years in a cellar.

"Now," Astrid breathed, and drove her gift through the drop of blood like a needle through cloth.

She stitched him in.

Not into the king—into the *crown*. The circlet sat on the entity's brow, gold and old and heavy with every harvest it had ever overseen, and it was the one binding in the room older and deeper than anything else, the master-oath, the root-thread. Astrid found its seam the way she'd found seams her whole life—in ink, in contracts, in the fine print of her own indenture—and she pulled the entity into it, all of it, the hundred voices and the honey-hunger and the patient centuries, winding it down into the gold the way you wind a snarl into a knot.

The entity fought. Of course it fought. The hall shrieked, the moons overhead cracked one by one like eggs, the sweet smell curdled into something burnt and animal—but every ounce of its strength was borrowed from oaths, and Astrid had spent the last hour unbinding them, and it swung at her with arms that were coming apart into light.

"*Sef!*"

He was already moving. She'd heard him come in with the others—of course she had; she always knew where he was now, the way you know where your own heartbeat is—and he crossed the hall with the case slung off his shoulder, and from it he drew not a weapon but a tool: the cartographer's breaking-iron, the one used to crack frozen map-cylinders, absurd and perfect, the only instrument in the kingdom made for opening sealed things.

Sef Vane, disgraced royal cartographer, who had marked the anomalies on the seal-line knowing it would cost him everything, raised the iron over the circlet with the entity's face inches from his own, wearing the expression of a man correcting an error on a map.

"For the record," he said, "your borders were *wrong*."

He brought it down.

The crown shattered.

There was no sound at first—or rather, there was every sound at once, compressed into a single white instant: a kingdom's worth of released vows exhaling together, twelve moons bursting into drifting pearl-dust, the honey-stink tearing away like a bandage off a wound. Astrid felt the entity go—felt it compress, howl, and pour down into the broken gold like water into a cracked cup, contained at last in the one vessel that could hold it, because a shattered crown was a promise that had failed, and failed promises make excellent prisons.

Then the sound arrived, all of it, and the windows blew outward into the night, and the dust of twelve moons rained down silver on everything.

Astrid fell to her knees on the dais.

Not from the effort—from the *emptiness*. The pressure was gone. Sixty years of harvested oath-weight, lifted off the hall, off the city, off the whole bowed spine of the kingdom, and the absence of it was so vast she felt she might float away. Her gift guttered low, exhausted, but present. Whole. Hers.

And on the throne, in the ruin of pearl-light, a man moved.

He moved like an old man, like a man waking from a fever of sixty years, stiff and blinking and *breathing*, really breathing, chest rising unevenly. His eyes—his own eyes, brown and human and terrified—found the girl standing over him with blood on her thumb.

"Ilsa?" King Aldric whispered. His voice was rust and ruin. "Ilsa. You got so tall."

Ilseva made a sound Astrid would remember for the rest of her life—half laugh, half sob, wholly undone—and caught her father as he slid from the throne, and held him, and the three of them were kneeling together in the wreckage of the crown when the twelfth moon's last dust settled around them like snow.

* * *

Afterward—minutes or hours, Astrid couldn't have said—she stood at the top of the palace steps with Sef, looking out over a city that had begun, tentatively, to light its windows again. Somewhere below, Veyra was restoring order with the terrifying competence of a woman who had just burned one ledger and opened another. Somewhere inside, a princess was sitting with a hollowed-out king, feeding him broth, asking him questions he could only answer a few at a time.

Sef's hand found hers. Ink-stained and map-scarred and steady.

"You didn't lose it," he said quietly. "The gift. The oracle said—"

"The oracle was working from the old text." Astrid watched a lamp flare in a window across the square, and felt her own magic answer it faintly, like a greeting. "They all assumed the price had to be paid. Nobody thought to ask whether the heir's blood was a payment or a *permission*." She exhaled, long and slow, and realized her hands had finally stopped shaking. "Turns out the whole ritual ran on consent. Always did. He just never let anyone try."

"And you figured that out in a margin."

"In a margin," she agreed. "Where all the best truths live."

Below them, the city bells began to ring—unscheduled, unsanctioned, joyous, the sound of a kingdom discovering it could make noise again. And in the doorway behind them, footsteps: a guard, breathless, saluting clumsily, saying that the regency council demanded answers, that Captain Veyra was asking for the scribe and the cartographer by name, that there was already talk of coronation, of succession, of Ilseva, of *duty*—

Astrid looked at Sef. Sef looked at Astrid.

"We should probably tell them," he said, "that we don't do duty anymore."

"After," Astrid said, and pulled him toward the servants' stairs, because she had spent her whole life bound to buildings, and she was not going to spend her first free hour indoors.
