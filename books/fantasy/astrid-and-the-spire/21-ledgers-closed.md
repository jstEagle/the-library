## 21. Ledgers Closed

The road to Thornfell smelled of rain and old ash.

Astrid had walked it a hundred times in the years of her indenture—always with her head down, always counting steps, because counting was what debtors did. Counted pages copied, counted candles burned, counted coins owed against coins earned against the interest that grew like mold in a damp cellar. She had known every stone of this road by its weight under her boots and never once by its beauty.

She knew it by its beauty now.

The pass opened before them in the grey-gold light of late morning, the mountains shouldering up on either side like patient giants, the pines dark and dripping after three days of spring storms. Somewhere below, a stream ran loud with snowmelt. The air tasted cold and clean and utterly unowned.

"You're doing it again," Sef said behind her.

"Doing what?"

"Counting."

She laughed—a sound that still surprised her when it came out of her own mouth, unguarded, unbilled. "I'm not counting. I'm measuring."

"Measuring what?"

"How far it is." She turned to look at him. He had his map case slung across his back out of habit even though they carried no maps that needed hiding anymore, only the new ones he was drawing, honest ones, with the smuggler roads inked in plain daylight beside the king's roads as if they had always belonged there. His hair had gone shaggy since the palace. There was ink on his knuckles and a fading bruise along his jaw where a piece of the shattered circlet had caught him, and he was looking at her with that expression she was learning to read—the one that meant he saw her counting and loved her anyway.

"It's four miles to the monastery gate," she said. "I used to know it to the step. Two thousand eight hundred and some. I counted them every time because—" She stopped. Started again, more honestly. "Because if you count the steps, the road belongs to someone else. You're just walking through it. It's easier that way."

"And now?"

"Now it's four miles," she said. "And it's mine to walk however slowly I please."

They walked it slowly. There was no reason not to. Veyra's dispatch had gone ahead of them by fast rider—an official letter, sealed with the hunt captain's own signet, declaring Thornfell monastery cleared of all charges, its debts annulled by decree of the regency council, its surviving scribes offered amnesty. Ilseva had added a line in her own hand at the bottom: *Come home and build something better. —I.*

*Home.* Astrid turned the word over as they climbed. For six years Thornfell had been many things—prison, workplace, the walls between her and the hunters—but never once had anyone called it home, least of all her. A debtor did not have a home. A debtor had an assignment.

The monastery appeared around the last bend the way it always did: squat and grey, hunched into the mountainside like a creature trying not to be noticed. But it was changed. Half of it was changed, rather—the east wing where the archive had burned still stood black-gutted, its windows empty eyes, its roof beams charred fingers reaching at the sky. Sef had suggested they raze it entirely. Astrid had said no. She wanted it standing exactly as it was, a ruin inside the living walls, so that no one who studied there could ever forget what fear had cost.

But the rest of the monastery was alive in a way it had never been in her time. Smoke rose from the kitchen chimney. Laundry hung between the cloister posts. And on the old training green where novices had once drilled calligraphy in silence, a dozen people were gathered in a loose, noisy circle—some of them exiled priests from Hollowmere in their undyed wool, some of them smugglers from Maren Ostwick's old network who had drifted in over the winter with nowhere else to go, and some of them children, village children, farm children, sitting cross-legged in the grass with their hands held out in front of them, watching small flames flicker above their palms or pebbles tremble in the air or, in one girl's case, nothing at all yet, which Brother Callum kept insisting was perfectly normal at her age.

Brother Callum looked up and saw them coming, and his whole face went the way a window goes when the shutters are thrown open.

"They're here," he bellowed, to no one and everyone. "They're here!"

* * *

The first weeks were chaos, and Astrid found that she loved the chaos with a fierce and private tenderness.

There was so much to build. The regency council, still finding its feet with Ilseva refusing the crown and the old dynastic oaths dissolved into so much legal parchment, had granted Thornfell a charter—the first of its kind in the kingdom's history—an institution where the gifted would be taught openly, without oath, without binding, without ledger. *The Galloway Archive,* the charter named it, and Astrid had argued for a week to have her name struck from it and lost, mostly because Ilseva had learned exactly how stubborn she could be when cornered and had simply refused to answer any letters on the subject until Astrid gave in.

So it was hers. Hers and everyone's. That was the point of it, she kept telling the council in her letters. A gift was not property. It could not be owned, taxed, inherited, or sold. It could only be tended, like a fire, like a garden, like anything alive.

The library rose first, built into the shell of the burned wing so that the new shelves stood where the old ones had died. Sef drew the plans himself, staying up half the night over them, muttering about load-bearing walls and light—he was obsessed with light, with getting the reading desks positioned so that no candle would ever be needed before dusk. "You spent six years ruining your eyes by tallow-light," he told her, "copying books that were going to be burned anyway. I am done watching you squint." The south wall would be glass, he decided. All of it glass. Let the sun do the work.

Astrid spent those weeks doing the thing that frightened her most, which was not magic. Magic, she had discovered, came easily now that she had stopped strangling it—it rose in her like breath, warm and bright, and the young students watched her light a candle with her mind the way she had once watched the abbot process in his gold vestments, with awe and a little terror and the dawning thought that maybe, someday, that could be hers too.

No. What frightened her was the teaching itself. Standing in front of people. Saying *this is how it works* out loud, in daylight, where anyone could hear and write it down.

The first lesson she ever gave, she gave with shaking hands. Eleven students sat before her in the unfinished library among the smell of cut timber and wet plaster, and she stood at the front holding a single sheet of paper, and her voice came out smaller than she intended.

"When I was your age," she said, "I hid my gift for six years. I lit candles with it in secret and put them out again before anyone saw. I told myself it was nothing. I told myself it was dangerous. Both of those things were lies other people taught me." She looked down at the paper and realized she didn't need it. She set it aside. "Here is the truth. Your gift is yours. Not the crown's. Not the temple's. Not your family's. Yours. And the first thing we are going to learn in this room is what that means—and the second thing is that it doesn't mean whatever you want it to mean, because gifts have weight, and weight has consequence, and consequence is the whole rest of this curriculum."

One of the smuggler children—a wiry boy called Tam who had come down from the salt roads with his aunt—raised his hand. "What happens if we don't want it?"

The question landed in the room like a dropped coin, ringing.

"Then we will teach you what it costs to refuse it," Astrid said quietly, thinking of the oracle at Hollowmere, thinking of the choice she had almost made in the throne room, thinking of the third path she had found only because two people had refused to let her take either of the false ones. "And then we will help you decide. Freely. That's the difference between this place and every place that came before it. Nobody decides for you here. Not ever again."

Tam nodded slowly, gravely, the way children nod when they understand more than they can say. Above his palm, quite without his noticing, a pebble had begun to turn slow circles in the air.

Astrid smiled until her face hurt.

* * *

On the evening the last shelf was set, Sef found her in the ruined east tower.

She had gone up there alone, as she sometimes did, to stand in the gutted room where the archive had burned. The abbot had fed the heretical texts to the flames one armful at a time while the brothers sang, and Astrid had stood in this exact spot with one book hidden inside her coat, feeling her heart hammer against its cover. Now the room was open to the sky, floored in soft grey ash that had long ago become soil, and someone—she suspected Brother Callum—had scattered wildflower seed across it. They were blooming. Cornflowers and poppies, blue and red against the blackened stone, growing straight out of the ashes of everything they'd been forbidden to read.

Sef came up the stair quietly, but she heard him; she always heard him now, the way you hear weather coming.

"The last shelf's in," he said. "Callum wants to bless it. I told him you'd want to do the honors instead."

"He can bless it. Blessing is his trade." She crouched and touched one of the poppies, careful not to break the stem. Its petals had the texture of tissue paper and the color of a struck match. "I keep expecting someone to stop me. Did I tell you that? Every morning I wake up in a room nobody owns, walk to a desk nobody assigned me, and I keep waiting for the hand on my shoulder. Six years of indenture and my body still hasn't learned that it ended."

"Does it help," Sef said, "when I remind you that you ended it? Not them. You. You bound that thing into the crown with your own hands. Nobody gave you that. You took it."

"I had help." She looked up at him. The evening light came through the empty windows and caught him full in the face, gilding the ink stains and the scar and the tired, hopeful lines around his eyes. "I had a mapmaker who lied to a king for the truth. I had a princess who bled willingly for a stranger. I had a hunt captain who chose her second ledger. I had a dead grandmother I never knew I had, who bought my freedom twenty years before I was born and paid for it with her life." Her voice caught, and she let it catch; that was another thing she was relearning, letting sounds leave her body without charging herself for them. "Nobody ends a thing like that alone. That's the whole lesson, isn't it? That's what the entity never understood. It fed on oaths—on duty passed down like a debt. And the thing that killed it was people choosing each other. Fresh. Voluntary. No inheritance required."

Sef crossed the ash-and-flower floor and sat down beside her, their shoulders touching. Below them the monastery glowed with lamplight, and beyond it the mountains went blue into dusk, and somewhere a student was practicing—badly, joyfully—making a lantern flame dance in stuttering rhythms like a heartbeat learning its own pace.

"So," he said.

"So."

"What will you do with your freedom?"

It was, she realized, the first time anyone had asked her that question outright. Maren had asked it once, near the end, in a voice like dry leaves—but Maren had known the answer already, had been steering her toward it since before Astrid could walk. The oracle had asked it too, blind eyes shining. But Sef asked it like a man standing at the edge of an unmapped country, wanting to know what lay there, wanting to draw it.

"I used to think freedom would feel like flying," she said. "That's what I imagined in the scriptorium, copying other people's words by candlelight. One day the debt clears and I walk out the gate and I just—rise. Off the ground. Like the gift finally gets to be what it is." She shook her head. "It doesn't feel like flying. It feels like ground. Solid ground, everywhere, in every direction. It turns out I never wanted to fly. I wanted to stand somewhere and have it be mine."

"And is this somewhere?" He gestured—at the ruin, the flowers, the lamplit windows, the whole stubborn, rebuilding world of Thornfell.

"This is the first somewhere." She leaned into him. "But I'll tell you a secret about freedom that nobody warned me about. It's enormous. It's so much bigger than escaping. When I was bound, I only ever wanted one thing—to be free of the debt. That was the entire horizon. Now the horizon's gone, and there's just... land. Everything I could want. Everything I'm allowed to want. And wanting things, it turns out, takes courage. More courage than the throne room did. In the throne room I only had to refuse. Out here I have to choose."

"What do you choose?" he asked softly.

Astrid was quiet for a long moment. Below, the lantern flame steadied at last into a clean, bright burn, and a child whooped with triumph, and the sound floated up the tower like something released.

"I choose this," she said. "The archive. The students. The glass wall Sef insists on and the flowers in the ashes and Callum's terrible blessings. I choose letters that travel openly and roads drawn honestly and a kingdom that has to learn, slowly and painfully, what it did. I choose all of it." She turned her head and kissed him, unhurried this time, nothing regretted, nothing snatched back. When she pulled away, she was smiling. "And I choose mornings. With you. Unbilled."

"Mornings," he repeated, tasting the word. "Unbilled." He laughed, low and wondering, and pulled a folded paper from his coat—she recognized the heavy cream stock of the regency council's correspondence before he even opened it. "Then you should see this. It came with the rider today. I was waiting for the right moment."

She unfolded it. Ilseva's handwriting, sharp and clear:

*Astrid—The council has recovered documents from the old reign. Among them, a registry of the harvested: every person whose oath the entity consumed, going back two centuries. Thousands of names. Some of them may be recoverable—Hollowmere believes the bindings left traces, and that a gift like yours might read them. Might wake them. I don't know if it's possible. I only know that if anyone can find out, it's the founder of the Galloway Archive. Come to the capital when your walls are ready. Or don't, and tell me to burn it. Either way, the choice is yours. That's rather the point of everything now, isn't it? —I.*

Astrid read it twice. The poppies glowed red in the last light. Below, the lamp in the library window burned steady and bright, and somewhere in the dark mountains a thirteenth moon that would never rise was already forgotten, and the road down from the pass lay open in every direction, unmapped, unwalked, hers.

"Sef," she said slowly, "how do you feel about drawing a very large map?"

He grinned. "How large?"

"Two centuries large."

He was already reaching for his pens.
