## 22. The Road We Keep

**Astrid**

The new moon made the pass a rumor of itself.

Astrid walked it anyway, boots finding the old smuggler road by memory and by Sef's hand in hers, because some knowledge lived deeper than sight. A year ago she had known this road as a thief knows it—in fragments, in the dark, in the space between one lie and the next. Now she knew it whole, and knowing it whole felt like a different thing entirely.

"Left fork ahead," she said.

"You said that before the last left fork."

"And I was right about that one too."

Sef made a sound that was not quite a laugh, more like air escaping a man who had spent too long holding himself together and was only now learning he could stop. She felt it through their joined hands, small and warm. The night smelled of pine resin and cold stone and, faintly, of woodsmoke from the shepherd's hut two valleys over—the first hearth they'd pass on the way down to Lowmark, where a widow named Bess waited for a letter from her son in the coastal garrisons. An honest letter. A letter written for no reason except that a mother missed her boy.

Delivering letters instead of secrets. Astrid still turned the phrase over sometimes, testing whether it would dissolve like so many other things had dissolved: the monastery, the debt ledger with her name scored through it, the crown that lay now in pieces in a regency vault while Ilseva argued law with men twice her age and won.

The road bent. Astrid took the left fork without slowing, and Sef followed, and below them the valley opened like a held breath finally let go.

* * *

**Sef**

He had mapped this pass eleven times in his life. Eight of those maps were lies—not his own lies, but the crown's, roads erased or rerouted at some minister's whim, passes closed on paper that stayed open under the stars. He had drawn falsehoods so long they'd worn grooves in him. Even after Thornfell, even after the throne room, even after watching Astrid bind an ancient hunger into a circlet of gold and shatter it with a cartographer's hammer—he had caught himself, more than once, reaching for the official version of a road instead of the true one.

Unlearning was its own kind of journey. Slower than the mountains. Steeper.

Tonight he carried a new map, half-finished, inked by lamplight in the loft above the Hollowmere priory where they'd slept three nights running because the exiled priests fed them too well and asked nothing back. This map told the truth. Every smuggler cut, every shepherd's shortcut, every ford that drowned in spring and every one that didn't. He'd marked the widow Bess's cottage with a small square and the letters *B.O.* in his own cramped hand, because Maren Ostwick had taught Astrid the roads and Astrid had taught him that a road was only ever as honest as the people who kept it.

Maren was dead eight months now. Her confession sat delivered, her grave swept, her name carved plain on a stone in Hollowmere's yard: *She kept the roads when no one else would.* Sef had never met her alive, but he felt he knew her anyway, the way you know a country through its maps. She was in every line of Astrid's stride.

"Astrid." He stopped where the path narrowed above the drop. "Look down."

Below, the valley floor was a scatter of lights—Lowmark, and beyond it the coast road, and beyond that the dark seam of the sea. Somewhere out there, ships carried the news of a broken crown to kingdoms that had traded with the old one for a century. Somewhere out there, other thrones sat under other moons, and Sef did not let himself think about what might be wearing those faces. One war at a time. One moon at a time.

"It's beautiful," Astrid said, "and you're stalling."

"I'm *savoring*. There's a difference."

"There is not, and we'll lose the tide window if we don't make the low road by dawn." But she leaned into his shoulder as she said it, and stayed there longer than the view required, and Sef thought: *this is what I burned my charts for. Not the kingdom. This.*

* * *

**Astrid**

The letter weighed almost nothing.

That was the strange part, every time. In the old days everything Astrid carried had weight—the forbidden texts sewn into her coat lining, the debt tallied in ledger margins, the gift coiled in her chest like a second heart she couldn't confess to. Now she carried a page of ordinary ink saying *I am well, the posting suits me, tell Tamsin the ring fit after all*, and it felt heavier than any of it, because it mattered honestly. No one had paid her to carry it. No one had threatened her if she failed. Bess had simply pressed it into her hands at the market stall with flour still on her knuckles and said, *You're the ones who walk the passes, aren't they? My Tam's at Greywatch. If it's no trouble.*

No trouble. As if carrying a mother's love across forty miles of mountain could be called trouble.

They reached the low road as the sky went from black to the grey of wet slate. Astrid lit the lantern with a thought—a small, easy flicker, the gift moving through her now like breath instead of like a struck match—and Sef shook his head with the same fond exasperation he always did.

"One day you'll use flint like a normal person."

"One day you'll admit the lantern trick has saved us an hour of fumbling at least six times."

"Seven," he admitted. "But who's counting?"

*I am,* she almost said. *I count everything. It's what they made me.* But that wasn't true anymore either, or not only true. She counted because counting was how she loved things—counting the miles between rest stops, the coins set aside for winter, the days since the twelfth moon rose over the palace and everything changed. Counting was just another word for paying attention, and she intended to pay attention to this life with everything she had.

Greywatch appeared mid-morning, a garrison town all whitewash and salt wind. They found the barracks, found a young soldier with Tam's same crooked nose, and handed over the letter without ceremony. He read it standing in the yard, and his face did something complicated and young, and then he looked up and said, "You came all this way? For a letter?"

"For a letter," Astrid agreed.

He stared at them like they were mad, or holy, or both. Astrid supposed both had been true of her once, in different ways. Now she was neither. Now she was a woman on a road with ink-stained fingers and a man who drew true maps, and that was enough. That was, she was discovering, the entire point.

* * *

**Sef**

On the road out of Greywatch, Astrid stopped at the crest of the first hill and turned back to look at the town, and Sef watched her watch it, and understood she was doing the thing she did at the end of every delivery—memorizing it. Filing it somewhere safe. He used to think it was habit from her smuggling years, cataloguing exits and sympathies. Now he knew better. She was keeping a ledger of her own, and every entry in it was proof.

Proof that she belonged to the world, and the world to her. Not inherited. Chosen. Road by road, letter by letter, chosen.

"My grandmother walked this stretch," Astrid said quietly. "Maren. Before she ever went to ground in Lowmark. She told me once—well, she told the confession, and the priests told me—that the roads were the only honest thing in her life. Everything else was performance. But a road doesn't care who you are. It just goes where it goes, and you either know it or you don't."

"She'd have liked the new archive," Sef said. "Open shelves. Anyone can learn the passes."

"She'd have hated it for a month and then secretly taught half the students herself." Astrid smiled, and the wind coming up off the sea pulled at her hair, and Sef thought about the question he'd been carrying since Thornfell, the one he'd asked in the scriptorium with dust still settling from the shattered crown: *What will you do with your freedom?*

She'd answered then that she didn't know. She answered differently now. He could see it in the set of her shoulders, the way she faced the open road south without flinching.

"So," he said. "Freedom. Still deciding what to do with it?"

"Still deciding." She glanced at him sideways, eyes bright with something that was mischief and promise in equal measure. "There's a road east of here nobody's mapped properly. Runs along the cliffs past the old salt works. The fishermen say parts of it fell into the sea twenty years ago and parts of it are fine, and nobody knows which is which."

"That sounds like a terrible idea."

"It sounds like *work*," she corrected. "Honest work. Dangerous, badly paid, unglamorous work. Walking roads and writing them down true, so the next person doesn't break their neck on a lie." She held out her hand, palm up, the way she had in the throne room when she'd asked Ilseva for a single willing drop of blood—not taking, asking. Choosing. "Come on, cartographer. The moon's dark and the roads are waiting."

Sef looked at her hand. At the road beyond it, unwritten, unmapped, going wherever it went. At the whole enormous honest world.

He took it.

* * *

**Astrid**

They walked south along the coast as the sun climbed, two figures on a road that belonged to no king, no abbey, no ledger. Behind them Greywatch faded into haze; ahead the cliffs ran on, white and wild, stitched with the ghosts of old paths waiting to be found again and named truly.

At the top of the long rise, where the land dropped away toward the shining water, Astrid paused one last time and counted—as she always counted—the things she carried. A half-finished map. A lantern she could light with a thought. A name that was hers alone, given by no institution, owed to no one. And beside her, matching her stride for stride, a man who had chosen her over every inheritance the world had offered him.

The debt was paid. The road was open. The moon was new, and everything under it was theirs to begin.

She took the first step down, and Sef followed, and the road—honest, at last, in both directions—went on.
