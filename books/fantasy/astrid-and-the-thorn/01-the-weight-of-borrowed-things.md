## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Things

The caravan road to Hollowbridge ran along the spine of the Thornfell mountains like a scar that had never quite healed, and at dusk it filled with the kind of people who carried things worth taking.

Astrid Joubert had learned this the way he learned most things—by watching, by waiting, by letting the world forget him while it went about its business. He sat now on a boulder above the third switchback, where the road narrowed between two walls of granite furred with lichen the color of old bruises, and he counted travelers the way other men counted coins.

Below him, the evening caravan strung itself out in a line of lanterns and creaking axles: six wagons, four outriders, a knot of pilgrims walking with their hoods up against the mountain wind, and one merchant riding alone near the rear on a dun mare too fine for the company he kept. That last detail interested Astrid. Merchants who rode apart from their guards were merchants with something to hide, and men with something to hide kept their minds loud. Loud minds were easy to read from a distance, and easier still to pick clean up close.

He waited for the light to fail another degree. Dusk was his hour—not night, when honest thieves worked with knives and shadows, but that thin blue interval when the world grew uncertain of its own edges. In dusk, a man could brush against a stranger's shoulder and be forgiven. In dusk, no one trusted their own eyes enough to doubt them.

The merchant reached the switchback below him. The dun mare picked her way over loose stone, and the merchant swayed in the saddle with the boneless ease of a man three cups into his cups, humming something tuneless and off-key.

Astrid slid down the scree without sound. Ten years of practice had taught his body to move the way water moves—present everywhere, noticed nowhere—and by the time the mare's hooves found the flat stretch below the bend, he was already walking beside her, a tired traveler with a traveler's slouch, close enough to smell the wine on the merchant's breath and the road dust in his coat.

"Evening," Astrid said. "Fair warning—the ford ahead's running high."

The merchant blinked down at him, squinting against the last light. "Ford? There's no—" He waved a vague hand. "There's a bridge. Stone bridge. I crossed it coming up."

"Not tonight there isn't." Astrid let concern crease his face, genuine as anything he owned. "Flash flood off the high snows this morning. They've got a ferry running, but you'll want to know before your horse finds out the hard way."

"Ferry." The merchant said the word as though it were an insult personally delivered. He fumbled at his belt pouch. "What's the toll?"

"No toll. Just thought you'd want to know." And here came the moment, the small ordinary gesture that made everything after possible: Astrid reached up and steadied the man's stirrup as the mare sidestepped on the loose stone—a helping hand, nothing more, palm flat against the leather, knuckles brushing the man's boot.

Skin to skin would have been better, but leather conducted well enough when the mind behind it was drunk and grateful and thinking about nothing but ferries and tolls.

Astrid closed his eyes for half a heartbeat and *listened*.

It was not hearing, exactly. It was more like standing in a dark house with all its shutters open, feeling the warmth of every room pour out around him. The merchant's mind spilled over its windowsills: worry about the ferry, worry about the toll, a dull resentment toward a brother-in-law in some lowland city, and beneath all of it, gleaming like coins at the bottom of a well—

*There.*

Astrid took a slow breath and lifted.

The first memory came away the way a ripe plum comes off the branch: a summer evening, a girl named Liss with flour on her hands, standing under an apple tree behind a bakery, and the kiss—clumsy, sweet, tasting of stolen dough and cider. The merchant had been seventeen. He had thought about that kiss eleven thousand times since, and each remembering had polished it smoother, until it shone.

Astrid drew it out gently, careful not to tear the roots of it, and set it into the hollow locket at his throat, which was not a locket at all but a sliver of thorn-wood carved into a shape that remembered things so he didn't have to. It hummed faintly against his collarbone, warm as a swallowed coal.

The second memory was harder—a mother's face, seen through the eyes of a boy of six, from below, looking up: gray eyes, a laugh line still shallow then, a smudge of ash on one cheekbone. She was singing. The song had no words the boy knew, only a rising melody like water climbing stones. Around the memory clung older sediment: her voice going quiet, her chair standing empty, the particular silence of a house learning to live without someone.

That one hurt to take. Most of them did, if you did it right. A thief who felt nothing tearing memories out left ragged holes, and ragged holes got noticed. But Astrid had a second reason, one he'd never said aloud to anyone: he liked to know what he was carrying. Every memory he stole passed through his hands, and for the length of a breath he lived inside it—the apple tree, the ash-smudged cheekbone, the love and grief braided together—and then he put it away and became himself again, whatever that was.

The merchant swayed in the saddle. His humming faltered, resumed, faltered again. When Astrid glanced up, the man wore the expression of someone who has walked into a room and forgotten why: a small, puzzled frown, already smoothing over. By morning he'd remember the kiss and the face as things half-glimpsed in dreams. He'd feel a strange tenderness toward a bakery he couldn't name. He might spend years chasing the ghost of a song.

*Never take what they can't live without,* Astrid reminded himself, the way he reminded himself every time. *Take the ornaments, not the walls.* A first kiss, dimmed by forty years of marriage to someone else. A mother's face, already worn soft by mourning. These were things the man could lose and survive. Barely. Probably.

He told himself that. He told himself that a great deal.

"You're sure about the ferry?" the merchant called after him, already forgetting why he'd asked.

"Last one crosses at moonrise," Astrid said, and touched two fingers to his brow, and walked backward off the road into the rocks, and was gone.

* * *

Hollowbridge announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke and wet wool, tallow and tar, the river's green rot underneath it all, and threaded through everything the sharp mineral tang of the mountain springs that gave the town its name. The town clung to both banks of the Greywater where the river cut through a gorge, its houses stacked like drunks leaning on each other, its bridges—seven of them, counting the two nobody admitted to—strung across the dark water like beads on a black cord.

Astrid came in by the smuggler's stair, a goat-path of steps hacked into the gorge wall, because the front gate meant questions and questions meant names and names were the one thing Astrid Joubert refused to keep or give. He moved through the lamplit lanes with his hood up and his shoulders rounded into anonymity, past a fishwife arguing prices, past two children playing a game with knucklebones and chalk lines on a doorstep.

He gave the children a wide berth. Not because children's memories were worth less—they were worth more, vivid and unpolished, a buyer's delicacy—but because of the rule, the first rule, the rule that had teeth in it: *never steal from children.* Children needed everything they had. Their memories weren't ornaments; they were load-bearing walls. He'd watched what happened once, long ago, in a village whose name he'd paid good coin to forget, when someone else had broken that rule. He still dreamed about the empty-eyed boy sometimes. He'd stopped trying to stop dreaming about him.

The fence he wanted kept shop above a chandler's on Tallow Row, behind a door painted the exact grey of every other door on the street, marked only by a candle-snuffer scratched into the wood at knee height where only those who knew to look would ever look.

Astrid knocked twice, paused, knocked once.

The door opened on a chain. One eye appeared in the gap, brown and bloodshot and shrewd. "You're late," said Odile Vasseur.

"The road was long."

"The road is always long. That's why they pay me what they pay me." The chain rattled off. "In. Wipe your boots. If you track river mud on my floor again I'll start charging you rent."

Odile's shop was a chandler's front, honestly run as far as any inspector ever bothered to check—candles in ranks, wicks in drawers, the whole place smelling of beeswax and patience. The real business happened in the back room, which held a single table, two chairs, a lamp turned low, and a wooden box lined with velvet that had, in its day, held crown jewels. Now it held smaller treasures.

Astrid sat and unlaced the thorn-wood locket from his throat. Setting it on the table was always a small relief and a small loss, like setting down a pack he'd forgotten he was wearing.

"Two items," he said. "First kiss, male, age seventeen, orchard setting, high quality—the man's replayed it until it glows. Second item's a mother's face, childhood vantage, with an attached grief. The grief's included whether you want it or not; they grow together."

Odile opened the box and lifted out a pair of silver tweezers, absurdly delicate for the work they did. "Grief attachments depress the price."

"They also authenticate it. Nobody counterfeits grief. Too much work."

She grunted, which from Odile was agreement. She bent over the locket and began, with the tenderness of a midwife, to draw the memories out—one by one, setting each into its velvet slot, where they lay glowing faintly like embers under ash. Astrid watched them go with the practiced detachment of a man handing over a stranger's letters. The apple tree. The gray eyes and the ash-smudge. The song like water climbing stones.

He did not keep them. That was the second rule, the rule under all the others: *never keep what you take.* A thief who kept souvenirs stopped being a thief and started being a magpie, and magpies ended up hoarding themselves into madness, buried under glittering rooms full of other people's lives. Astrid had seen that too. He'd seen nearly everything, in ten years of this trade, except the inside of his own locked past.

"Buyer in Lowmarket wants the kiss," Odile murmured, mostly to herself. "Widower. Rich. Collects young love the way other men collect hunting trophies." Her mouth twisted. "I don't ask. You don't tell. That's the arrangement."

"That's always been the arrangement."

"And the mother?" She looked up. In the lamplight her face was all angles and suspicion, but her eyes were older and softer than she'd ever admit. "Same buyer keeps asking after maternal memories. Pays obscene. I don't like what that says about him."

"Then sell it to the schoolhouse. The one on Bell Lane. They buy childhood memories for the orphans—give them something to dream about besides the fever ward."

Odile snorted. "Charity. From you. The mountains must be thawing."

"I'm selling, not giving. Take my fee out of it."

"Your fee." She counted coins onto the table with the reluctance of a woman plucking her own feathers. Then, as his hand moved to sweep them up, she added, lightly, too lightly: "Anything else come through your hands lately? Anything strange?"

Astrid paused. "Strange how?"

"Strange how I mean, which is none of your business until it pays." She leaned back, and the lamplight caught the silver in her hair, more of it than there'd been last season. "There's a whisper going round the low markets. Somebody's buying *old* things. Not memories—older than memories. Names, they're saying. Birth-names. Soul-titles, if you believe the Thornfell talk, and I pay people not to make me believe Thornfell talk." She fixed him with the bloodshot eye. "Prices are mad. Buyers won't give names. And the ones who broker for them wear grey, and they don't haggle, and they don't smile, and I don't like them in my town."

Astrid gathered his coins slowly. Gray cloaks. He'd heard the phrase before, in fragments, in the corners of other people's sold memories—a flash of grey fabric at a funeral, a rumor of riders who paid in old coin, a child's nightmare of tall figures smelling of winter. He'd filed it all under *mountain nonsense*, the way sensible people filed away tales of the Thorn Court, the court of thorn-crowned dead that supposedly ruled the deep places beneath Thornfell, keeping souls the way misers kept heirlooms.

Everyone knew the stories. No one sensible believed them.

"Old coin," Astrid said. "How old?"

"Older than the kingdom. Older than the *last* kingdom, the brokers say." Odile shrugged, elaborate in her indifference. "Probably counterfeiters with a theme. Anyway. None of my trade touches theirs, and none of yours should either. Stick to kisses and mothers' faces, Joubert. They're honest theft, at least."

Nothing was honest theft. But he nodded, pocketed his fee, and stood to go.

At the door, Odile spoke again, and this time there was nothing light in her voice at all. "One more thing. Since you're standing there anyway." She turned the lamp wick up a fraction, studying him. "You look tired. Not road-tired. *Vault*-tired. How long has it been since you went through your own stock? Counted what you actually own?"

"I own what's in my head," Astrid said. "That's the point of the rules."

"Mm." Odile looked at him a moment too long, the way she looked at goods she suspected were misrepresented. "There's a storm coming down off the high passes tonight. First real one of the season. You'll want to be behind your own door before it breaks."

"I'm always behind my own door."

"Everyone thinks that," Odile said, "right up until the knock comes."

He laughed—it came out rusty, rarely used—and let himself out into the street, where the wind had indeed begun to rise, dragging clouds down the gorge like smoke poured from the peaks. The first drops hit the cobbles fat and cold. Above the town, the Thornfell summits had vanished into cloud, and somewhere up in that drowning white, lightning flickered without sound, once, twice, like something signaling.

Astrid pulled his hood low and started up toward his cabin on the ridge, telling himself the unease crawling up his spine was only the weather.

Three hours later, soaked to the bone and half-frozen, he would open his door to the storm and find a small barefoot girl standing on his threshold, unafraid, unhurried, looking up at him with eyes the color of deep water—and she would speak, in a language older than any kingdom, older than the last kingdom, older than the old coin in the gray cloaks' purses.

And at her throat, on a cord of woven hair, would hang a pendant of black thorn-wood that hummed—when Astrid's fingers brushed it, later, trembling—the exact same note as the locket that held everything he had ever stolen.
