## 2. A Knock at the Door

The storm came down the Thornfell passes like something with a grudge.

Astrid heard it coming for an hour before it arrived—a low grinding in the high country, the sound of wind finding every hollow in the mountains and filling them with its voice. He had learned to read that sound the way other men read weather-glass or the aching of old scars. It meant shutters barred, fire banked low, and no travel until the mountain finished its tantrum.

He was counting his evening's take when the knock came.

Three coins' worth of memory sat in the little lead box on his table: half of a wedding day, a sailor's knowledge of knots and stars, and—his favorite of the lot, though he would never keep it—a baker's whole childhood in a city called Vell, all warm stone and flour dust and a grandmother humming off-key. He was weighing which of these the fence in Hollowbridge would pay best for when the sound reached him through the timber walls: *knock, knock, knock.* Small knocks. Patient ones.

No one knocked on Astrid Joubert's door. That was rather the point of living where he lived.

His cabin sat above the tree line where the smuggler paths braided together and frayed into nothing, three days' hard walk from Hollowbridge and two from Maren's croft, perched on a shelf of granite below a face of black cliff that swallowed the light by mid-afternoon. He had chosen it for its loneliness the way other men chose wives for their beauty. In eleven years, the only visitors had been those who already knew to come—the occasional courier, a lost poacher he'd turned around with a stolen sense of direction, once a gray-cloaked rider who had stared at the cabin for a long time before riding on. Never a knock. Never at night. Never in a storm.

He set down the lead box and took up the knife instead. Old habit. The blade was more ritual than defense—he had never needed steel, not since he'd learned what else could be taken from a man—but rituals kept the hands steady.

*Knock, knock, knock.*

The same small rhythm. Unhurried. As if whoever stood outside had all the patience of stone.

Astrid crossed the single room, lifted the bar, and opened his door onto a wall of rain.

Wind hit him like a shoulder, driving sleet past him into the room, guttering the fire. He squinted into the dark and saw nothing at first—just the storm, the wet gleam of granite, the pines below bent double under the gale. Then he looked down.

A child stood on his doorstep.

She could not have been more than eight years old, small enough that the storm seemed to bend around her rather than through her. She was barefoot on the frozen stone. Her dress was some rough-spun thing, gray as river fog, soaked through and hanging off her thin shoulders, and her hair—dark, dripping, plastered to her cheeks—framed a face that was watching him with an expression he had never seen on any child, and rarely on any adult.

She was not afraid. She was not crying. She was not shivering, though the cold out there could kill a grown man in an hour. She simply looked up at him, rain running off her chin, and waited with the composure of someone who has been standing in doorways for a very long time and expects, eventually, to be let in.

"Gods' teeth," Astrid said. "Where is your mother?"

The girl tilted her head. And then she spoke.

The language that came out of her was nothing Astrid knew—and Astrid knew languages the way a miser knows coins. He had bought and sold a dozen tongues over the years: caravan pidgin, the trade-cant of Hollowbridge's docks, the formal High Speech that nobles paid handsomely to forget they'd ever spoken drunkenly in front of witnesses. He knew how languages sat in a mind, how they tasted when you lifted them out.

This was none of them. The sounds were older than sounds should be—consonants like roots cracking stone, vowels that seemed to come up from beneath the ground rather than out of a throat. They were not harsh. That was the strangest part. They were patient and green somehow, the way moss is green, the way deep caves are green in dreams. Listening to her felt less like hearing speech and more like overhearing the mountain talking to itself in the dark under the world.

She said perhaps six words. She pointed past him, into the cabin, at the fire.

"No," Astrid said automatically. Then, because she only watched him, unblinking, rain streaming off her: "You can't be out here. Where did you *come from?*"

She pointed down. Not at the path—at the ground itself. At the mountain.

Astrid felt the first true cold of the night then, and it had nothing to do with the weather. There was no settlement below. There was nothing below but mine-shafts, sealed and collapsed, and deeper than those—

He stepped aside. He would tell himself later that it was practicality: a dead child on his doorstep was a problem, a live one indoors was merely an inconvenience. But the truth was simpler and worse. Something in her voice had gotten under his skin the way a splinter gets under a nail, small and invisible and impossible to ignore.

"Come in, then," he said. "Before the mountain decides otherwise."

* * *

She walked into his home the way water fills a bowl—completely, without hesitation, as if she had always been meant to be there.

Astrid barred the door behind her and turned to find her standing in the middle of the room, dripping a puddle onto his floorboards, turning slowly in place. Her eyes moved over his things with an odd gravity: the copper kettle, the stack of books he'd bought with a scholar's discarded love of reading, the lead box on the table. When her gaze passed over the lead box, it stopped. Stayed. Moved on.

"You're soaked through," he said. "There's a blanket on the—no, of course."

Of course she didn't understand. He dug out dry wool—a shirt too big for her, a blanket that smelled of cedar—and she submitted to being dried with the same unnerving patience, holding still while he rubbed warmth back into her arms. Her skin was cold, but not deathly so; cold the way river stones are cold, as though the temperature were simply what she was made of. Under his hands he felt her pulse, slow and even, slower than any child's pulse had a right to be.

Her feet, he noticed, were unmarked. Barefoot through a storm, over scree and frost, and not a scratch, not a chilblain, not a crack. He looked at her feet for a long moment and decided not to think about it, which was a skill he had refined over many years.

He put bread and cheese in front of her. She studied the bread first, turning it over in both hands, sniffing it, pressing her thumb into the crust. Then she ate—not greedily, not daintily, but with the focused attention of someone performing a task she intended to learn properly. When she got to the cheese she paused, held a piece up toward the rafters, and said something in that root-and-stone voice, brief and almost reverent, as if offering thanks to whatever lived up there.

Astrid sat across from her and watched, and the unease in his chest cracked open another degree, and something else—something he disliked intensely—seeped into the gap.

Curiosity.

He had rules. He had built his whole life on rules, because rules were what stood between a man with his particular gift and the abyss. *Never steal from children.* First rule, oldest rule, the one he'd carved deepest. *Never keep what you take.* Second. *Never look too long at your own reflection in another man's memories.* Third, and hardest.

And now here was a child in his house who spoke a dead language and came from underground, and every instinct he possessed was leaning toward her like a plant toward light.

"Don't," he told himself quietly, and the girl looked up, as if he'd spoken to her.

"I wasn't—" He stopped. Rubbed his face. "You don't understand me. Good. Keep it that way."

But she was looking at him with those dark, unhurried eyes, and after a moment she set down her bread, reached up, and drew something out from the collar of her ruined dress.

A pendant.

It hung from a cord of woven hair—hair too coarse and dark to be hers—and at the end of it swung a single thorn, black and glossy, longer than his thumb, curved like a falcon's talon. It was not wood. It was not iron. It was not anything Astrid could name, and he had spent years handling objects that carried weight beyond their substance: love-tokens, grief-locks, saints' knucklebones sold out of forgotten reliquaries. He knew the feel of things that remembered being alive.

The thorn pendant hummed.

Not audibly—or not only audibly. He heard it faintly, a tone at the very bottom edge of hearing, like a struck bell heard through a hillside. But he *felt* it clearly, the way he felt memories waiting to be lifted: a vibration in the air between himself and the object, a resonance that recognized something in him and leaned toward it.

Astrid went very still.

"Do not touch that," he said to himself this time, firmly, the way one speaks to a hand drifting toward a stove.

He touched it.

Later he would try to explain to himself why—curiosity again, that crack widening into a fissure, or perhaps something older than curiosity, some groove worn into him by a road he couldn't remember walking. His fingers closed around the thorn and the hum climbed through his bones, and for one vertiginous instant the cabin, the storm, the child—all of it fell away like a mask slipping, and he was somewhere else.

Somewhere dark and vast and rooted. A hall whose pillars were trees grown wrong, spiraling inward instead of up. A throne of braided thorn. Voices in that same green, patient language, speaking words of protocol and price, and himself—someone—standing before the throne, knowing the words, knowing the *etiquette*, bowing at precisely the correct depth because he had been taught, because he had *practiced*, because—

The vision snapped shut.

Astrid found himself on his knees beside the table, the pendant still clutched in his fist, his heart slamming, the fire burned down to embers he had no memory of burning down. Across from him the girl watched him with an expression that was, for the first time, not entirely unreadable. If he had to name it—and he was very good at naming expressions; he'd bought and sold enough of them—he would have called it recognition. Or grief. Or the particular sorrow of watching someone fail to remember something important.

He opened his hand. The thorn lay in his palm, inert now, ordinary-looking, and utterly alien.

"That craft," he said hoarsely. His voice sounded wrong in the empty room. "I know that craft."

He knew soul-bargains the way a locksmith knows locks: professionally, intimately, from the outside. Every fence in Hollowbridge had handled the residue of one at some point—a merchant who'd sold twenty years of his life for a fleet that sank anyway, a widow who'd traded her grief and gotten a hollow where her love used to be. The work had a signature. Soul-work left seams, like bad stitching, and Astrid had spent a decade learning to feel those seams with his gift the way a blind man reads faces.

The pendant was stitched from that same thread. Older, finer, done by masters—but the same craft, the same grammar of binding a soul to a vessel and setting terms.

And beneath the recognition, underneath the professional appraisal, was something else. A familiarity that had no business existing. The feeling of walking into a house you've never entered and knowing exactly which stair creaks. The feeling of a name on the tip of the tongue that will not come, will not come, will not—

He searched himself for the memory of why. That was the natural thing, the obvious thing. He had a vault inside his own mind—he was, uniquely among his kind, also his own customer—and when something felt familiar without reason, a man went looking for the reason.

He found the vault exactly where he had left it. Sealed. Locked. And behind the lock, when he pressed against it the way he'd pressed against it a thousand times over eleven years, the same blank wall, smooth and total, that had always been there. Whatever he had put away, he had put away completely. Even the keyhole was gone.

Except—tonight, with the hum of the thorn still fading in his bones—for the first time in eleven years, the wall didn't feel empty behind it. It felt *occupied*. Something stirred back there, slow and enormous, like a shape shifting in a locked room, and settled again.

Astrid withdrew from his own mind as gently as a thief leaving a house where the dog has just lifted its head.

The girl was still watching him. Rainwater had pooled beneath her chair and she had not moved from it, and outside the storm threw itself against the cliffs as though trying to get in and finish whatever it had started.

"All right," Astrid said finally, to her, to himself, to the mountain. "You speak to roots and I speak to thieves, and neither of us understands the other. But you're not leaving tonight, and I'm not sleeping, so we may as well be honest with each other." He nodded at the pendant lying in her small palm. "That thing is a claim-mark. I've seen its like twice, and both times the person wearing it belonged to somebody else." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and made his voice as gentle as he knew how—which was gentler than most people expected from him. "Who do you belong to, little one?"

She considered him for a long moment. Then she answered, in that language older than kingdoms, six soft syllables that rose at the end like a question, like a name offered in hope.

He didn't understand the word. But he understood the shape of it, the way it was spoken—carefully, tenderly, the way one says a name one has been carrying a long way and is afraid of dropping.

And deep behind the sealed wall in his own mind, something stirred again, and pressed close against the lock, and *listened*.
