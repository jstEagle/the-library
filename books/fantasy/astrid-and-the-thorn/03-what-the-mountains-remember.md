## 3. What the Mountains Remember

The storm had spent itself by morning, leaving the world rinsed and dripping, the pines heavy with last night's rain. Astrid stood at his window with a cup of barley tea going cold in his hands, watching Wren sleep.

She had taken the cot without argument—had, in fact, curled into it like something returning to a shape it remembered, knees drawn up, one fist beneath her chin. The thorn pendant lay against her collarbone, rising and falling with her breath. In the gray light it looked less like jewelry and more like a splinter that had worked its way under her skin and stayed.

He had not slept. He had sat up most of the night turning the pendant's hum over in his mind the way a man turns a coin he suspects is clipped. It had sung against his fingers—a low, fibrous vibration, like a plucked root—and something in him had answered before his mind could object. Not recognition exactly. Recognition would have been simpler. This was closer to an old scar aching before rain: the body knowing what the head refused to name.

Wren stirred. Her eyes opened—not the startled snap of a child waking in a strange place, but a slow unfurling, green as new leaves, taking in the room, the fire, him, in that order, as though she were cataloguing rather than fearing.

"You slept," he said, though she could not understand him. He was finding he talked to her anyway. "That's more than I managed."

She sat up and pressed her palm flat to the floorboards. Held it there. Then she looked at him and made a sound—not a word, but a note, low and woody, the sound a cabin makes when wind finds a crack in its walls. She said it again, pointing at the floor, then at him, patient as a teacher.

"You're naming things," Astrid realized aloud. "You're learning my words."

She pointed at the fire next. *Fff.* At the kettle. At him again, and this time she waited, eyebrows raised, until he sighed and supplied, "Astrid."

"Ast-rid." She shaped it carefully, tasting each syllable like a berry she wasn't sure was safe to eat. Then she smiled—the first full smile he'd seen from her—and it went through him like a splinter of its own, somewhere behind the ribs.

He set down his tea. "We're going to see someone," he told her. "Someone who can tell me what you are. What was done to you."

Outside, the mountains stood white-capped and indifferent, and somewhere beneath them, he could not shake the feeling, something ancient had begun to turn its attention toward his door.

* * *

Maren lived two valleys east, in a house that grew out of the hillside rather than standing on it, its turf roof furred with moss and its walls built of stone so old no one remembered who had quarried it. The walk took most of the day. Wren made it barefoot.

Astrid had offered her his spare boots three times—at the trailhead, at the river crossing, at the long scree slope above it—and three times she had declined with a small, polite shake of the head, stepping over stones sharp enough to lame a mule as though they were moss. By midday he stopped offering. There were things about the girl that arguing with would be like arguing with the weather.

She walked differently in the wild places, too. In the cabin she had been watchful, contained, a creature in a cage it didn't yet know the dimensions of. But on the mountain paths she loosened. She touched things—bark, lichen, the split trunk of a lightning-struck pine—and at each touch she paused, head tilted, listening to something Astrid couldn't hear. Once she stopped at a fallen birch and crouched beside it for so long he nearly left the path, and when she rose she was crying, silently, wiping her cheeks with the backs of her hands before turning back to him as if nothing had happened.

"What did it say?" he asked, before he could stop himself.

She considered the question with grave seriousness. Then she laid her hand on his chest, over his heart, and made the falling note again—the one from the night she'd arrived, the sound of things letting go.

He hadn't asked her anything else after that.

They reached Maren's at the hour when the light goes long and gold and every shadow stretches out like something reluctant to let go of the day. Smoke curled from the chimney. Herb baskets hung drying under the eaves, filling the yard with a smell like summer being saved for winter—yarrow and sage and something sharper underneath, something medicinal and dark.

Maren was in her garden when they came up the path, kneeling between rows of comfrey and valerian, her gray hair bound back with a strip of red cloth. She was deaf—had been since a fever in her girlhood, though the fever had given as much as it took, opening in her a way of reading the world through her hands that no hearing person ever quite trusted and none who knew her ever doubted. She felt their footsteps through the ground before she saw them; Astrid had watched her do it a hundred times, her palm flat to the soil, her face changing as she read the tremor of approach.

She looked up now, and her expression did what it always did when she saw him: softened first, then sharpened. Softened because she liked him—he was fairly sure she liked him. Sharpened because she had never once believed a single thing he said about himself.

*Astrid.* She signed it, the old thieves' cant they'd developed over years—one hand flicking from her ear outward, *the man who takes sounds away*, which was her private joke about his gift and one he'd never found funny enough to tell her so.

"Maren." He signed back, clumsy as always. *I need your hands.*

Her eyes went past him then, to Wren, and everything in her face changed.

He watched it happen—watched the warmth drain out and something older take its place, something he had only seen once before, years ago, on the face of a dying smuggler in a snowbound pass who had looked up at the mountains and whispered a name Astrid had never let himself remember. Maren rose from her garden slowly, wiping her hands on her apron, and did not sign anything at all.

Wren, for her part, had gone very still. She stared at Maren with an intensity that bordered on hunger, and then—before Astrid could stop her—she crossed the yard and held out the pendant. An offering. A question.

*Don't,* Astrid started to sign.

Maren took it.

* * *

Inside, the herb-witch's house smelled of dried things and earth and, faintly, of smoke from a wood that had burned a very long time ago. Maren lit lamps even though the day wasn't done. She sat Wren at her table, gave her a bowl of honeyed milk that the child accepted with grave courtesy, and then she took Wren's small hand in both of her own, turned it palm-up, and closed her eyes.

Astrid knew what she was doing. He had seen her read a hundred people this way—skin speaking to skin, memory passing through touch like current through wire. He had felt it himself once, early on, before he learned to guard against it: Maren's hands on his temples, and the strange sensation of being *read*, of his own past rising in him like sediment in shaken water.

But he had never seen her recoil.

It happened fast. One moment she was still, reading; the next she had dropped Wren's hand as if it had burned her and pushed back from the table hard enough to topple her stool. She stood with her back against the wall, chest heaving, one hand pressed to her mouth, staring at the child with an expression Astrid had no name for. Horror was close. Grief was closer.

"Maren—" He moved toward her. She flung up a hand. *Stop.*

Wren had begun to cry—silent tears, no sound, the weeping of a child who had learned long ago that sound brought nothing good. Astrid knelt beside her instead, awkward, his hand hovering over her shoulder without landing. "It's all right," he said uselessly, in words she couldn't understand, while across the room Maren fought for breath.

It took the witch a long moment to compose herself. When she finally returned to the table, she did not sit. She signed as she spoke aloud, out of habit, though Astrid was the only one present who needed either—and her signs were sharp, truncated, angry in a way he had never seen from her.

*What have you brought into my house?*

"I don't know. That's why I came."

*You know.* Her hands cut the air. *You know better than anyone living. Look at her neck.*

He looked. The pendant lay against Wren's skin, and now, in the lamplight, he saw what exhaustion and storm-dark had hidden from him: fine red lines radiating from it across her collarbone, thin as hair, like the marks a thorn leaves when it catches flesh and holds. They were not wounds. They were older than wounds. They were the marks of something that had been holding on for a very long time.

"It's a claim-mark," he said slowly. "Soul-bargain collateral."

*Soul-bargain.* Maren's mouth twisted around the word as if it tasted of ash. She pulled out a chair and sat, heavily, and for a moment she simply looked at Wren, who looked back with those leaf-green eyes, still crying without sound, still holding out her milk bowl as though offering to share it might fix whatever she had broken.

Then Maren reached across the table and took the pendant itself, holding it at arm's length, and Astrid saw her hands begin to shake.

"Put it down," he said quietly.

She didn't put it down. She read it—lips moving, eyes shut, her whole body rigid—and when she finally released it, she did so gently, laying it back against the child's chest with a tenderness that contradicted everything else in her face. Then she looked at Astrid, and what she signed next, she signed slowly, so there could be no mistake.

*This is Thorn Court work. The court beneath the mountain. The dead kings with crowns of briar. This child's soul was sold to them, body and all, and the price has come due. The body is borrowed, Astrid. Borrowed things get called home.*

"How long?"

*Not long. Weeks. Perhaps less. The mark is deepening—I can feel it drinking her.* She paused, and her hands hesitated in a way he had never seen them hesitate. *There is more. You will not like it.*

"There's a surprise."

*Whoever sold her used a craft I have felt only once before in my life.* She reached out and took his wrist—not reading, just holding, her grip fierce. *Yours.*

The word landed in the silent room like a stone down a well.

"That's not possible," Astrid said. His voice sounded far away to him, coming from someone else's mouth. "My craft is theft. Memory-work. I don't deal in souls. I've never—"

*I know what you do. I have known you twelve years.* Her signs slowed, became deliberate, each one placed like a stone in a wall. *And I am telling you that the signature on this bargain is written in a hand I would know anywhere, because I have felt it under my own palms. The same grammar. The same weight. The same cold, careful, self-erasing precision. Whoever sold this child learned the craft from the same well you drank from, Astrid. Or—*

She stopped. Her hands hung in the air, unfinished.

*Or,* she began again, and this time the signs came slower, heavier, *or there is no second crafter at all. Or the bargain was made by someone who knows your magic from the inside. Someone whose memories you carry. Someone you—*

She broke off again, and this time she did not resume. Instead she reached out and touched his face, briefly, the way she might touch a wound to judge its depth.

*When did you last open your own vault, Astrid?*

"I don't have—" He stopped. The lie died in his throat, because it was a lie and because Maren's hands could feel lies the way her ears could not hear them. "I don't remember."

*No,* she agreed softly. *You don't. That is precisely the problem.*

Wren tugged at his sleeve. He looked down. She held up the honeyed milk, offering him the last swallow, her face solemn and wet and trusting, and Astrid felt something shift in his chest—a fault line settling, a weight finding its angle of slide.

Dread, he thought. So this is what it feels like when it takes root. Not a thunderclap. Just the slow knowledge that the ground beneath you was never solid, and that you yourself may have dug it hollow.

"We leave tonight," he said. "Before dark. Before anything follows us here."

*Nothing needs to follow you,* Maren signed grimly. *Look at her neck again. The court doesn't send hunters for what already belongs to it. It only waits for the debt to ripen.*

She caught his arm as he gathered their things—her grip urgent now, her signs quick. *Hollowbridge. Vess. If any record of the bargain survives, she will know where it sleeps. And Astrid—* she hesitated, then finished, *—whatever you find in yourself, however bad it is, come back and tell me. Don't do what you always do. Don't lock it away and call it mercy.*

He promised her nothing. Promises, in his experience, were just debts with better manners.

Outside, the light had failed while they talked, and the eastern peaks had gone the color of iron. Wren took his hand without being asked—small, warm, calloused along the palm in ways a child's hand should not be—and together they started down the darkening path toward the valley, toward Hollowbridge, toward answers that were already beginning to feel less like answers than like excavations.

Behind them, in the doorway of the turf-roofed house, Maren stood watching, one hand pressed flat against the doorframe as though she could read the whole mountain through it.

And far below them both, deeper than roots, deeper than the oldest stone, something patient felt the debt grow ripe—and began, at last, to stir.
