## 4. The Girl Who Speaks in Roots

Before there were words, there was the dark of the soil.

Wren remembered it the way a seed remembers winter: not as an event but as a condition, a long and patient waiting. She had been *held* once—she could not say when, or by whom, only that the holding had been warm and close and smelled of loam and iron—and then she had been *poured*, like water from one vessel into another, and the vessel she woke in was small and aching and had hands at the ends of its arms that did not know what they were for.

The body was borrowed. She knew this the way roots know which way is down. It fit her badly, the way an old coat fits a different shoulder. Its lungs were too shallow. Its heart beat too fast, a bird trapped in a cage of ribs, and sometimes when she lay still at night she could feel the seams of it, the places where she had been stitched into flesh that was never grown for her. The seams pulled. The seams sang a thin, sour note that no one else seemed to hear.

But the cabin—the man's cabin—was built of things that spoke kindly.

The walls were pine, felled young, still carrying in their grain the memory of wind. When she pressed her palm flat against them she felt the long slow shudder of trees remembering storms, and it comforted her, because storms end. Trees know this. Nothing that grows believes any weather is permanent.

* * *

The man was harder to read than the wood.

He moved through his own house like a fox through unfamiliar territory: soft-footed, watchful, always leaving himself two ways out. He did not touch her more than he must. When he set bread before her on the first morning—he called it *bread*, a round blunt word, a word shaped like the thing itself—his fingers hovered near her wrist as though he wanted to steady her and had decided against it.

She looked at the bread. It was brown and cracked along its crown and it breathed warmth into the cold room, and she understood suddenly that fire lived inside it, that someone had caught fire and fed it flour and the fire had turned the flour into this: this softness, this smell, this *bread*.

"Bread," she said. Her voice came out wrong, rusty, a hinge unused for years. "Bread."

The man went very still.

She learned later that stillness was his language, that he said most of his truest things by not moving. But in that first moment she read it as fear, and fear she recognized, because fear grew in people the way bindweed grew in fields—silently, underground, and then all at once everywhere.

"It's yours," he said. "Eat."

*Yours.* Another word to plant. She took the bread in both hands and broke it and the steam rose up and touched her face like something alive, and she ate, and the eating was a kind of falling, a sweet collapse, her whole borrowed body saying *yes, yes, yes* with a hunger that frightened her because it was not really hers. The body hungered. She only lived in it.

The man watched her eat without eating himself. His eyes were gray, the gray of stone under water, and they held a door in them, and the door was shut, and behind the door something paced.

* * *

At night she dreamed in the old way.

Not pictures. Not stories. She dreamed in *growing*. She dreamed of a garden where every plant was a moment, and the gardener had gone away, and the moments were going to seed untended. She dreamed of thorns—not cruel ones, not yet, just thorns as they are before anyone decides what they mean: a plant's way of holding its ground. She dreamed of hands catching her out of a great fall, hands that were warm and certain, and then the hands opening, letting go, her name—her real name, the one made of green and dark water—falling away from her like a leaf let loose in autumn.

She woke with tears on the borrowed face and did not know whose grief they were. Perhaps the body's. Bodies grieve on their own schedule; they keep their own accounts.

The fire in the hearth had burned low. She crept to it, barefoot on boards that murmured under her feet—*he cut us, he planed us, he built us a roof, we do not mind*—and crouched beside the coals. Fire was the strangest thing in this world. It was the only thing here that grew downward and backward, that ate its own history, that needed to be given things or it died. She held her palms toward it and felt it consider her, the way a dog considers a stranger.

"You'll burn," said the man's voice behind her, quiet. "If you get too close."

She did not turn around. "It's lonely," she said. The words came easier now; each day the mouth loosened, each day more of her poured down into the borrowed throat. "It eats and eats and everything it eats is gone."

The silence behind her stretched. Then he came and sat beside her, an arm's length away, careful as ever, and put a split log on the coals, and the fire flared up gold and grateful.

"That's one way to see it," he said.

"How do you see it?"

He was quiet so long she thought he would not answer. The fire worked at the log. Outside, wind moved through the pines in long slow breaths, and the cabin breathed with them, and somewhere under the mountain—far under, deeper than roots went—something old turned over in its sleep, and Wren felt it turn, the way you feel a wagon pass on a road miles distant, a tremor in the soles of your feet.

"I don't," the man said finally. "See it, I mean. I take things out of my head so I don't have to look at them." He said it flatly, the way a man reports weather. "You'd think that would make a person light. It doesn't. It makes them hollow. Hollow echoes."

She turned and looked at him. In the firelight his face was all planes and shadow, a cliff-face at dusk, and behind his eyes the shut door strained on its hinges.

"What's your name?" she asked.

Something crossed his face—quick, involuntary, like a bird's shadow over snow. "Astrid," he said. And then, softer, as if the word cost him: "I think it's Astrid."

*I think.* As if a name were a coat found in a ditch, worn because it fit well enough. She reached out—slowly, the way you reach toward a wild thing—and laid her fingers on the back of his hand.

His skin said many things. Skin always did; it was the honest part of a person, the part that couldn't lie fast enough. His skin said: *no one has touched me gently in years.* His skin said: *I am braced for this to become a theft.* His skin said, underneath everything, faint as a root-voice under frozen ground: *please.*

She took her hand back. Some doors you do not knock on. You wait beside them until they open on their own, the way flowers open, which is to say: toward whatever light is offered.

"Astrid," she said, tasting it. It was a hard word, a word with stones in it. A word for carrying.

"Go back to sleep," he said gruffly, and did not look at her, and fed the fire another log so it would be warm between them and the dark.

* * *

Days braided themselves together after that.

She learned the cabin the way she learned everything: by touching, by listening with her palms and the soles of her feet and the fine hairs on her arms. The house told her its whole biography. The axe-nicks in the doorframe where a taller man had once marked heights—*not him*, the wood said, *someone before, someone gone*. The kettle's dented belly, which hummed a note of complaint whenever it heated. The floorboard by the window that sighed under weight, an old wound in the wood that had healed crooked and never stopped talking about it.

And Astrid. She learned Astrid in fragments, because he kept himself in fragments, a book with pages torn out.

She learned that he counted things when he was uneasy—steps, breaths, the cracks in the ceiling—counting as other men prayed. She learned that he slept facing the door with a knife under his pillow, and that his hand found the knife-handle in sleep the way a child's hand finds a blanket. She learned that when she sang—not songs, she had none, only sounds, the vowel-moans of growing things, the hum the earth makes in spring—he stopped whatever he was doing and stood in the middle of the room with his eyes closed, listening like a starving man outside a lit window, and afterward he never spoke of it.

One afternoon she followed him out to the woodpile and watched him split logs, and the rhythm of the axe entered her, and she began to hum, and the humming became words without her deciding it—old words, root-words, the language she had spoken before the pouring, the language older than any kingdom, the language the mountains themselves had been sung in when they were only soft stone dreaming under the sea.

She didn't know what she was saying. That was the terrible thing. The words came up through her like water through a well-rope and she could feel their shapes but not their meanings, meanings having been taken, packed away, sold perhaps, the way everything about her had been sold—

The axe stopped mid-swing.

Astrid stood with the blade buried in the block and his face gone white as birch bark, and when he spoke his voice was strange, scraped hollow. "Where did you learn that?"

She shook her head. "It learns me," she tried to say, and heard how little sense it made, and hated the borrowed tongue for being too narrow, a riverbed trying to hold the sea.

He pulled the axe free. His hands were shaking. He set the axe down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off, and walked past her into the house without a word, and through the wall she heard him moving fast, drawers, the scrape of a chest, and then nothing, a silence like held breath.

That night he did not eat. He sat by the fire turning a small object over and over in his fingers—a pendant, black-thorned, hung on a cord—and the pendant hummed when his thumb crossed it, a note she felt in her teeth, in the marrow-seams of her borrowed bones. A claiming-note. She knew it the way cattle know the sound of the slaughter-gate.

He was looking at the pendant. He was looking at her. The door behind his eyes rattled in its frame.

* * *

She remembered the fall again that night, and this time she held onto it instead of letting it dissolve.

Falling. Not through air—through *arrangement*, through the order of things, tumbling out of wherever souls are kept into the cold country of bodies. And hands. Warm hands, sure hands, catching her at the last moment, cradling her—no. Cradling something. Someone small. A crying thing wrapped in wool, and snow coming down fat and silent, and a voice above her saying words she could not then understand but now, now the meaning rose in her like sap:

*Take her instead.*

Her own voice? No. A man's voice. Young. Breaking on the words like ice breaking underfoot.

*Take her instead. Take me out of it. Take anything—*

And the hands, the warm sure hands, tightening once—holding on, holding on, holding—

Letting go.

Wren sat up in the dark with her heart slamming and the seams of her body singing, and across the small room she saw Astrid sitting upright too, awake, staring at the wall as if the wall had spoken. Neither of them moved. The fire ticked. Under the mountain, far below, the old thing turned again in its sleep, closer now, or they closer to it.

"Astrid," she whispered.

He didn't answer. But in the morning, when she came to the table, there was bread waiting for her, and honey, and he had built the fire high, and when she sat down he pushed the honey toward her across the wood—an offering, small and awkward, a branch laid across a gap.

"Eat," he said. "Then we're going to see someone. A woman who can... read things." His jaw worked. "Read me, maybe. Whatever's in me that I can't open."

Outside, the wind had changed. It came down off the high passes carrying a smell Wren knew from the deepest part of herself, older than her stolen name: wet stone, and turned earth, and thorns.

Something under the mountain had begun to walk.
