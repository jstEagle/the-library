## 5. Smuggler's Road

The gray cloaks came on the third morning, when the frost still held the grass stiff as wire.

Astrid saw them first as a wrongness in the treeline below the cabin—a stillness too deliberate to be deer, a patience too cold to be wolf. Three figures where the pines grew thin, standing among the shadows the way stones stand in a river: not moving, not needing to. They wore cloaks the color of wet ash, and where their faces should have caught the dawn light, the light simply went somewhere else.

He had been packing for an hour already. He'd woken before the birds with a taste like old iron in his mouth and the certain, animal knowledge that the mountains had turned their faces away from him. Now he understood why. The Thorn Court had noticed its property walking around in borrowed skin, and it had sent collectors.

"Wren." He kept his voice low, the way you speak near a sleeping animal. "We're leaving. Now. Don't ask questions yet—I'll answer them on the road, and some of them I may never answer, and you'll have to make your peace with that."

The girl looked up from the hearth, where she had been arranging ash into spirals with one finger—a habit she'd developed in the two nights under his roof, drawing patterns that made his teeth ache to look at, all spirals within spirals, the geometry of roots. She didn't ask questions. That was the strange mercy of her: she had learned, somewhere in whatever life had made her, that questions were a luxury of the unclaimed. She simply rose, took the thorn pendant in her fist, and waited.

Astrid slung the pack he kept packed in every season—dried fish, a flint, a coil of waxed rope, three vials of poppy tincture, and a false-bottomed box that had once held memories worth a house in Hollowbridge and now held nothing but the shape of what he'd sold. He banked the fire. He took one last look at the cabin he'd built with hands that wouldn't tell him why they'd chosen that particular hollow, that particular slope, that particular angle of escape toward the western passes.

The cabin knew things about him that he didn't. That was the shape of his whole life.

Then he took Wren's hand—her fingers cold as a streambed—and stepped out the back into the bracken, and the fugitive life he had chosen, or that had chosen him, closed around him like a door.

* * *

He did not take roads. Roads were for people with names.

Instead he took the smuggler's veins, the hidden arteries of the Thornfells that no magistrate's map had ever recorded because the men who'd cut them had been paid in silence and buried in it. There was a path behind the cabin that looked like nothing—a fall of scree, a seam of shadow between two boulders—that opened after forty yards of crawling into a ledge wide enough for two, running west along the shoulder of Crag Fell with the whole world dropping away to the left. Astrid had learned these ways the way you learn a mother tongue: without remembering the learning. His feet knew where the loose stones were before his eyes found them. His hands reached for handholds he hadn't seen. It should have been comforting, that deep knowledge in his own body. Mostly it felt like being followed by a stranger who wore his shape.

Wren moved like water finding its level. She made no sound on the scree—none, which was wrong in a way he couldn't name, because even goats made noise on scree—and she read his hesitations before he had them, turning left when he turned left, freezing when he froze. Twice he caught her looking back the way they'd come, and both times her face held no fear at all. Only recognition. As if she'd been hunted before, in some country older than fear.

They climbed through the morning. The frost gave way to a thin, singing rain, then to cloud, then to the particular gray nothing of the high fells where the world ends a few feet from your face and you walk inside your own skull. Astrid navigated by memory and by the wind, which came from the west here with a smell of peat and old snow, and by the cairns the smugglers had raised generations ago, waist-high stacks of stone that no honest eye would mark as anything but the work of frost.

It was at the third cairn that he felt them again.

Not saw. Felt. The gray cloaks had a quality he'd only met once before, years ago, in a memory he'd lifted from a dying man on the coast road—a memory of deep-sea diving, of the pressure of fathoms pressing in from all sides. That was what the pursuers were: pressure. A slow, patient weight on the air behind them, growing by degrees, never hurrying. They didn't need to hurry. The mountain was theirs, and everything that walked on it was walking on their palm.

"They're below us," Wren said.

Astrid stopped. In three days she had spoken perhaps twenty words, and each one arrived in her strange, root-grown accent, vowels rounded like river stones. "You can hear them?"

"I can feel where they've been." She pressed her palm flat against the cairn. "Stone remembers weight. They walked here before dawn. They are walking our path behind us, and they do not tire, and they do not eat, and they do not stop to sleep." She lifted her hand and looked at it as though it had betrayed her. "The stone is afraid of them. I didn't know stone could be afraid."

Everything about her frightened him, and everything about her made him want to stand between her and the world. He was beginning to suspect those two things were the same feeling.

"Then we don't take the path they expect," he said. "We take the one nobody expects, because nobody in their right mind takes it." He looked north, toward the black seam in the mountainside that the cloud had been hiding all morning. "The old mine at Graywether. It goes through the shoulder of the fell and comes out in the next valley. It also hasn't been worked in sixty years, and the last crew that tried to reopen it came out the far end as a rumor." He heard his own voice doing what it always did—making a bad road sound like a clever choice, wrapping terror in the dry cloth of professionalism. It was the voice he used at fences, at borders, at the moments when the truth would get him killed. "There's a saying about it among the carter folk. *The mountain keeps its own.*"

"What does that mean?"

"It means I'd rather take my chances with a collapsing mine than with things that don't sleep." He crouched to her level, and for a moment—just a moment—he let the mask slip, because she was a child, and because his rules had never included lying to children. "Wren. It may come down while we're inside. I need you to stay behind me, hold the hem of my coat, and if I say *flat*, you drop and you don't move no matter what you hear. Can you do that?"

She looked at him with those eyes the color of water over slate, and she reached up and touched his cheek with one cold finger, the way you'd touch a tree to ask it something.

"You are afraid of the small dark places," she said. "Not of the falling. Of the smallness."

Astrid's throat closed. He had never told her that. He had never told *anyone* that; he hadn't known it himself until the words left her mouth and settled into him like a stone into still water, and he recognized the truth of it the way you recognize your own handwriting.

"Then we'd best be quick," was all he said.

* * *

The mine's mouth was a wound in the hillside, timbered with beams gone soft as bread, half-swallowed by a curtain of creeping vine that had gone black with the season. The air that breathed out of it smelled of rust and standing water and something older underneath—something mineral and patient, the smell of the deep rock that has never seen the sun and resents the suggestion.

Astrid lit the storm-lamp he'd carried for exactly this, though he hadn't known it was for exactly this, and led Wren into the dark.

The first mile was almost kind. The main adit ran straight and dry, the rails of the old ore-carts still down the center, furred with moss. Their footsteps rang on iron and stone. Wren kept her hand fisted in his coat hem as instructed, and Astrid kept the lamp high, and the dark pressed close around the little bubble of their light, and he counted his breathing the way he'd taught himself to do in small places—four in, four held, four out—because the walls were close and the mountain was old and the mountain, he could not help feeling, was awake.

Then the first tremor came.

It was not an earthquake. It was smaller and worse than that: a single deep shudder through the rock, like something vast turning over in its sleep, followed by a silence so complete he could hear the water thinking in the walls. Dust sifted down through the lamplight, golden and slow.

"That wasn't us," Wren whispered.

"No." Astrid had stopped walking. His skin had gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with the mine. He knew that shudder. He had felt it once before, in a memory he'd siphoned from a Hollowbridge miner—a memory of the *knocking*, the deep percussions that came before a collapse, the mountain clearing its throat. "They're behind us. And they're not digging. They're—" He stopped, because saying it would make it true. "They're asking the mountain to move for them."

The second knock came, closer, and with it a sound that was not a sound: a pressure against his mind, faint but unmistakable, like a fingertip drawn down the inside of his skull. *Come out. Come out, little thief. The goods you carry are not yours.*

"They know where we are," Wren said. Her hand had gone tight on his coat. "They've always known where we are. The running is just—" she searched for the word, found it, disliked it, "—*courtesy*."

"Then let's be discourteous." Astrid took the lamp off its hook and did the thing his body had been begging him to do since the first tremor: he ran.

The mine became a maze and the maze became a nightmare. The main adit gave onto a junction of galleries, and Astrid took them at a dead sprint, choosing turns by instinct he didn't examine—left, right, down a chute of rubble on the seat of his trousers, up again, Wren flowing behind him like a shadow with a heartbeat. The knocking came faster now, and with each impact, rock fell somewhere in the dark behind them: great sloughing booms, the mountain shedding its skin. Dust thickened until the lamp's beam was a solid yellow rope. Somewhere a timber groaned the long, splintering groan of a dying thing.

And then the passage ahead simply ended—not in wall, but in collapse, a fan of fallen rock from floor to ceiling, old and new at once, as if the mountain had been saving that blockage for exactly this moment.

Astrid skidded. Turned. Behind them, down the gallery they'd come, the lamplight caught it at last: a figure in the dust-haze, gray-cloaked, unhurried, its face a smooth absence where the light refused to land. And behind it, faintly, two more, and behind *them* the sound of the mountain coming apart in slow, deliberate sections, the court's collectors unzipping the fell like a coat.

Wren pressed against his back. The pendant at her throat had begun to hum, a thin black note he felt in his molars.

There was no way through. There was no way back. And Astrid Joubert, who had built his whole life on the principle of never being anywhere a thing could corner him, stood in a dead-end gallery sixty feet underground with a child behind him and death walking toward him at the pace of something that had never once needed to hurry.

*Well,* he thought, with the strange clarity that arrives at the bottom of things. *This is the room you made. You just never let yourself look at the walls.*

The lead gray cloak raised one hand. Not a threat—a summons. The pressure against Astrid's mind swelled, and beneath it he heard, or almost heard, a voice like dry roots: *Give back what is the court's, and walk away, nameless one. We have no quarrel with the empty.*

*The empty.* The words went into him like a blade finding a gap in armor. They knew what he was. They knew he had hollowed himself. And they thought that would make him easy.

Astrid set down the lamp. He turned to Wren, and he put both hands on her shoulders, and he said, very calmly, in the voice he used for the worst bargains: "Whatever happens in the next minute, don't let go of my coat. And don't look at my face."

Then he did the thing he had sworn, in whatever year it was, in whatever life it was, never to do.

He reached out with his gift—not to lift a memory, not to skim the bright surface of a passing mind, but to *take*. He seized the gray cloak's mind the way a drowning man seizes a rope, and he went straight past the surface, past the shallow silt of orders and patrol-routes, down to where the court kept its servants, and he found what he was looking for, because every mind has it, because fear is the one thing no power ever manages to remove.

And he ripped it out.

He took the gray cloak's fear the way you'd tear the keystone from an arch.

The effect was instant and terrible. The figure did not cry out—its kind perhaps could not—but it *shuddered*, the whole smooth absence of its face cracking like lake ice, and the pressure in the air collapsed, and the thing staggered, and behind it the other two staggered with it, linked, all three of them suddenly, horribly *human* in their emptiness. Because fear, Astrid understood in that flashing instant, was the only thing holding them in their shapes. The court's servants were hollow too—hollower than he was—and what he'd stolen was the wall that kept the hollowness from showing.

The gray cloak stood swaying in the dust. And then, with a sound like green wood splitting, it came apart. Not fell—*unmade*, unraveling into ash and threads of gray light that the mine's dead air took and dispersed, and the mountain, freed of whatever will had been prying at its joints, settled with a groan into silence.

Astrid stood in the settling dust with a stranger's fear burning in his chest like swallowed coal, and it was *vast*, that fear, it was a cathedral of it, and he understood in one white-hot moment that the gray cloaks were afraid of the court the way slaves are afraid of the house, and that he had just made an enemy of the house.

"Up," he said hoarsely. "There'll be a ventilation shaft. There's always a ventilation shaft. Old mines breathe if you know where to put your ear."

He put his ear to the rock in four places before he found it—a thread of moving air, cold and clean, behind a slab of fallen timber. He dug with hands that bled and didn't care. The shaft was narrow, near-vertical, ribbed with old iron rungs, and it was the smallest dark place he had ever willingly entered, and Wren went first because she was lighter and because he could not have borne to send her second and could not examine why.

They came out through a fox's earth on the far side of Graywether Fell, into evening, into rain, into a green valley that smelled of life after the mineral dark. Astrid lay on the wet grass with his heart slamming and a dead thing's fear still echoing in the rooms of his mind, and Wren knelt beside him and put her small hand flat on his sternum, over the hammering, and left it there.

"You gave it away," she said. "Its fear. You carry it now. I can feel it in you—it's too big, it doesn't fit, it's like—" she frowned, hunting, "—like wearing a giant's coat."

"I know," Astrid said.

"Why would you do that? For me?"

He looked at her—at this child he had known four days and could not remember knowing at all, this girl whose face kept snagging on something at the very bottom of him, some hook set deep below the waterline of his memory. And he heard himself say the truest thing he'd said in years:

"Because I've spent my whole life taking things from people, Wren. It was time to find out what happens when I give one back."

She studied him for a long moment. Rain beaded in her dark hair. Then she said, in her root-and-river voice, words that turned his blood to snowmelt:

"The court will not send three, next time. The court will send the thing that *wears* the gray." Her hand pressed harder against his chest. "And it knows your smell now, Astrid-of-the-cabin. It knows the shape of your hollow. We cannot run anymore. Running is a courtesy, and we have used ours up."

Astrid sat up. The valley lay below them, and far to the west, past the fells and the black line of the forest, a smudge of smoke and lamplight against the coming dark: Hollowbridge, the smuggler city, the town where everything could be bought and everything could be sold, and where a man might find out what he had sold without knowing it.

He had a fence there who owed him. He had a broker who traded in secrets. And he had, though he could not have said how he knew it, a signature somewhere in that city that matched one on a contract that had sold a child's soul.

"Then we don't run," Astrid said, and got to his feet, and held out his hand to her. "We go to Hollowbridge. And we find out who I was before I made myself empty."

Wren took his hand. Behind them, deep in Graywether Fell, the mountain gave one last long shudder—like a door closing, or like a throat clearing to speak.

They walked down into the valley, and the rain came with them, and the dark came on.
