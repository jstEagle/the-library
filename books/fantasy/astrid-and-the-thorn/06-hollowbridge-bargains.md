## 6. Hollowbridge Bargains

Hollowbridge announced itself the way a fever announces itself: first a warmth at the edges of things, then a sweat of noise and light, then the full delirium.

Astrid felt it before he saw it—the city's hum rising through Wren's small hand where it gripped his, through the soles of his boots, through the old scar tissue along his ribs that always ached when too many minds crowded close. A thousand thoughts bleeding into the air like heat off summer stone. Greed and grief and gossip, all of it simmering in the crooked streets below the gorge.

He stopped on the last bend of the smuggler's road, where the pines broke open and the whole city spilled out beneath them: houses stacked on houses, black timber and pale plaster, bridges lacing the ravine like stitches in an old wound. Lanterns were coming alight along the waterline, gold coins dropped into dark water one by one. The famous bridge itself arched overhead—hollow, as its name promised, a tunnel of shops and secrets with the river running loud beneath it.

Wren stood beside him, her breath fogging in the mountain cold, and stared down at it all with an expression he was beginning to learn how to read. Not wonder exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of someone seeing a place they had dreamed without knowing they could dream.

"You'll stay close," he said. "You don't speak to anyone. You don't touch anything anyone offers you. And if I say *run*, you run, and you don't wait to see why."

She looked up at him. In the last days she had learned perhaps thirty words of the common tongue—*bread*, *fire*, *cold*, *no*—and she spent none of them now. She simply took hold of two fingers of his hand, deliberately, the way she did everything, and held on.

Something in his chest pulled tight, like a knot drawn snug. He ignored it. He had gotten very good, over the years, at ignoring the knots.

They went down into the city together.

* * *

Hollowbridge smelled of tar and frying onions and wet wool, of tallow smoke and the green rot of the river, and underneath it all—a smell only people like Astrid caught—the faint copper tang of traded memories, leaking from the memory-houses along Sable Row like perfume from a cracked vial. He kept his hood up and his mind shut tight behind the practiced walls of his craft: a door barred, a window shuttered, nothing offered, nothing taken. In a city like this, an open mind was an invitation and a wallet left on a table both.

The gray cloaks had not followed them across the high passes—or if they had, they wore other shapes now. But Astrid had spent three days watching treelines and feeling the itch between his shoulder blades, and the itch had not gone away. It had only changed address. It lived in the city now, somewhere. Things that served the Thorn Court did not stop because their quarry reached crowds. Crowds were simply more places to hide a knife.

He moved through the evening streets the way water moves through cracks: no hurry, no hesitation, every turn chosen three steps early. Down Cutpurse Stair. Across the Weepers' Bridge, where beggars with genuinely weeping eyes—half of them bought, half of them real—held out their bowls. Through the fish market, where the day's catch lay silvering on beds of ice and the gulls screamed their ownership from the rigging of moored boats.

Wren kept pace at his side, silent as a shadow given shoes. Once, passing a stall of dried herbs, she slowed and touched a bundle of rowan with one fingertip, and the leaves—all of them, the whole dried bunch—shivered though there was no wind. The vendor frowned at it, puzzled, and Astrid drew Wren onward before the man could frown at anything else.

"Later," he murmured. "Everything, later."

He didn't know if that was a promise or a prayer. Probably both. Most promises in Hollowbridge were.

Vess kept her offices above a chandler's shop on Candle Lane, which was either a joke or a warning; with Vess it had never been possible to tell the difference. The stairs were narrow and smelled of beeswax, and at the top a door of black oak bore no sign, no knocker, only a small brass plate polished mirror-bright, in which whoever arrived was invited to examine their own face and decide whether they wanted what waited beyond badly enough to knock.

Astrid looked at himself in the little mirror. A lean, weathered man, forty-some years and lying about five of them, gray coming in at the temples like frost claiming a field one furrow at a time. Tired eyes. A face built for being forgotten, which was the point of it, which had cost him more than he cared to count.

Beside him, reflected small and solemn, Wren studied his face too—and then, unexpectedly, reached up and pressed her palm flat against the brass over his reflection, as if testing whether the man in the metal was solid.

"He's solid enough," Astrid said dryly. "More's the pity."

He knocked.

* * *

The door opened before the second knock landed, which meant Vess had known he was coming, which meant Vess always knew, which was the entire architecture of her power.

"Joubert." Her voice was smoke and ledger-ink. She stood in the doorway in a robe the color of dried blood, her iron-gray hair coiled and pinned with what appeared to be actual pins made of actual keys. Her eyes—pale, quick, unsettlingly kind for a woman who sold secrets the way fishwives sold herring—went from him to the child and back again. Something flickered behind them. Calculation, yes, but also something older and softer that she would have denied under torture.

"You look like a man who has been dragged behind his own life," she said. "Come in before the lane does. Half of Candle Lane is for sale and the other half is buying."

The room beyond was Vess entire: shelves of labeled jars (some holding spices, some holding letters, some, Astrid knew, holding memories sealed in wax and salt), maps pinned three deep on every wall, a stove ticking with heat, and a cat—an enormous, disreputable orange beast named Magpie—sprawled across the only comfortable chair as though the chair owed it money.

Wren stopped just inside the threshold. Her nostrils flared. Her gaze went around the room slowly, and Astrid saw her do the thing she did—see the world as growing things and old stone—and saw her arrive at some conclusion about Vess's rooms that he would never be able to ask her for. Whatever it was, it made her shoulders come down from around her ears. She crossed to the stove and sat on the floor beside it, cross-legged, close enough to warm her hands and far enough to bolt if she needed to.

Magpie opened one eye, assessed her, and poured himself off the chair to drape instead across her lap. She froze. Then, with the gravity of a queen accepting tribute, she set her hand on his orange bulk.

"Well," said Vess. "That cat has bitten two fence-magnates and a bishop. Sit down, Joubert, before I decide you're not worth the tea."

He sat. The tea came anyway—black, bitter, cut with something piney that was probably worth more than the cup. Vess poured two cups and then, after a pause that pretended to be carelessness, a third, smaller one, which she set within Wren's reach without comment. The girl wrapped both hands around it and breathed the steam like a sacrament.

"Now." Vess settled into her chair and folded her hands. "Three years you've been silent. Three years, and then a note under my door written in your hand but hurried, asking about soul-contracts, Thorn Court seals, and brokers who deal in *names*. You'll forgive me if I skip the pleasantries and go straight to the part where I tell you this conversation frightens me."

"It should," Astrid said. "It frightens me more."

"Good. Misery loves company, and company pays better." But her eyes went soft again, briefly, toward the child by the stove. "The girl?"

"Wren."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know what you asked." He drank his tea. It scalded pleasantly. "I'm not ready to answer it. Maybe after tonight I will be. That's rather the problem."

Vess watched him for a long moment. Outside, the river muttered under the street, and somewhere below a chandler's bell rang the hour, and Magpie began to snore against Wren's knee with the deep contentment of a creature who had never once doubted his place in any room.

"Tell me what you have," Vess said at last. "Not what happened. What you *have*. Paper, wax, thread—I can work with objects. Stories I have to charge extra for, because stories lie."

Astrid reached inside his coat and brought out the thing he had carried for six days now, wrapped in oilcloth against his skin like a second heart: the scrap of contract Maren had torn free of Wren's pendant's inner casing, the fragment bearing the court's seal and the seller's mark.

He unwrapped it on the table between them.

Vess did not touch it at once. That was the difference between Vess and every other broker in Hollowbridge—she understood that some objects read *you* while you read them. She leaned close instead, lamplight pooling in the hollows of her face, and studied the fragment the way a jeweler studies a stone for flaws.

"Soul-bargain parchment," she said quietly. "Vein-cured. You don't see this outside the deep courts anymore; the material alone is worth a house in the Terrace district. The seal—" she tilted the scrap, and the thorn-crown sigil seemed to writhe slightly, the way seals of the Thorn Court always did when observed too directly, "—is genuine, and old. Not freshly struck. This contract was made years ago, Joubert. Years. Which means whatever claim is walking around wearing that child has been maturing in the dark a long time."

"I know." His voice came out flat. "Maren told me as much. What I need is the seller's mark. There." He indicated the small sigil at the fragment's lower edge, half-torn, a looping cipher struck through with a single descending line. "Every soul-broker signs their work. Guild habit. Vanity. Leverage. Call it what you like—but marks can be traced to hands, and hands can be found."

"Mm." Vess produced a lens from somewhere in her robes—a disc of smoky crystal banded in silver—and held it over the mark. For a long time she said nothing. The stove ticked. The river ran. Wren's hand stilled on the sleeping cat.

Then Vess set down the lens, and looked at Astrid, and her expression had gone carefully, professionally blank—which, from Vess, was louder than shouting.

"What," Astrid said.

"Give me your hand."

"Why?"

"*Give me your hand.*"

He gave it. She turned it palm-up on the table, took the lens, and held it above his bare palm the way a physician reads fever. Then she took his ink-stained right hand instead, turned it over, and tapped one long fingernail against the callused side of his forefinger, where a writer's callus rises.

"When you write," she said, "when you sign anything—you hook your letters. Did you know that? Everything you write leans forward, like a man walking into wind. And your flourishes—" she traced a shape in the air, "—always break downward. One descending stroke, struck through the body of the letter. Like a blade going in. I've seen a hundred of your notes over the years. I could pick your hand out of a library of ten thousand."

The room got very quiet. Even the river seemed to lower its voice.

"Look at the mark again," Vess said softly.

He looked. The looping cipher, half-torn. The single descending line struck through it like a blade going in.

His own handwriting leaned into the wind. His own flourishes broke downward.

"No," he said.

"Joubert—"

"No." He was on his feet without deciding to stand, the chair legs shrieking on the boards, and Wren's head came up sharply, and Magpie woke with an offended yowl. "That's—that's coincidence. Thousands of brokers. Hundreds of styles. A loop and a stroke—"

"Astrid." Vess almost never used his given name. It landed like a cold coin dropped down his collar. "I've spent twenty years buying and selling the truth in this city, and I am telling you, freely, unpaid, because you once carried my sister's confession out of a burning house and never told a soul you'd done it: *that mark is yours.* Or it is your twin's, or your ghost's, or your father's—but I have read contracts until my eyes bled, and I am telling you that whoever signed that child away wrote with your hand."

The words went into him and kept going, past the places where words usually stopped, down into the locked dark where he kept the things he refused to carry.

And the terrible thing—the thing he would remember afterward, in the sleepless nights that followed—was that some part of him, some cellar-deep part, was not surprised. Some part of him had been waiting for this the way a wound waits for rain. When Vess said it, something in him clicked into place with a sound like a lock turning, like a debt being recognized, and the click *hurt*, and the hurt felt horribly like homecoming.

Wren was looking at him. She had gone still in the way she went still when the world spoke to her in its older language. Slowly, she rose, leaving the cat aggrieved, and crossed the floor, and stood beside the table looking down at the contract fragment, and then up at his face.

She reached out and touched the mark with one finger. Then she touched his hand.

Then she looked at him with an expression he had no words for, in any tongue, and he understood that she knew too. Had maybe always known, in the root-and-stone way she knew things. Knew that the man who had taken her in, fed her, sung to her in the fells, fled with her through collapsing mines—

Knew that his hand was on her bill of sale.

"I didn't," he said. His voice cracked in the middle like river ice. "Wren, I swear to you, I don't remember—I don't—"

But even as he said it, he heard the shape of the sentence, heard what it confessed: *I don't remember.* Not *it wasn't me.* Every thief in Hollowbridge knew the difference between those two statements. Only one of them was a defense.

"There's more," Vess said quietly. "Isn't there. There's something you're not telling me, because you've only just realized it yourself."

He stared at the fragment. At the seal. At the mark that was his hand's own signature wearing a stranger's cipher.

"I keep my own past locked," he said. The old explanation, the professional's explanation, the one he'd given Dagny and Maren and himself a hundred times. "Behind doors even I can't open. It's how the craft stays clean. You can't sell what you won't look at."

"And what's behind those doors, Astrid?"

"I don't know."

"No," Vess agreed, and her pale eyes were terribly gentle. "But I think the doors might."

Silence. The stove ticked. Wren's hand still rested on his, small and warm and unbearably trusting, and he could not bear it and could not pull away, and so he did neither—he simply stood there, a man suspended between the person he believed himself to be and the signature on the table.

"The Thorn Court doesn't deal in paper alone," Vess said at last, briskly, because grief was bad for business and business was how she survived grief. "Contracts need brokers. Brokers need records. If your hand signed that sale, there are copies—there are *always* copies—and there are other fragments of the transaction scattered where the court couldn't burn them. Payment records. Witness accounts. The name of whoever the bargain was *for*." She paused. "Because soul-sales are never simple, Joubert. Nobody sells a child for coin. There's always a trade buried in the trade. Something given. Something spared. Someone else who walked away whole."

The words rang in him like a bell struck in an empty church.

*Take her instead.*

The thought surfaced from nowhere—from the locked dark, from behind the doors—and was gone again before he could seize it, leaving only the cold wash of its wake. Four syllables. He didn't know where they'd come from. He didn't know whose voice had said them.

But he knew, with the same horrible certainty as the signature, that they were his.

"Vess," he said, and his voice sounded like a stranger's. "How much do you charge to find everything? All of it. Every copy, every record, every trace of that contract and the hand that wrote it. I want the whole transaction laid out on this table if it takes every coin I have and every favor you owe me."

Vess studied him for a moment. Then she nodded, once, like a woman closing a ledger.

"For you? Nothing." She raised a hand before he could speak. "Don't argue—it isn't charity, so don't insult us both. It's insurance. Because if the Thorn Court comes to Hollowbridge looking for that child, they will come to *me* eventually, everyone does, and I intend to be standing next to you when they do. Misery loves company." The ghost of her old smile. "And company pays better. Go home, Joubert. Go wherever you're hiding. And while you're there—"

She glanced at the contract fragment, at the mark that was his hand, at the child whose fingers were still curled around his.

"—open one of those doors," Vess said. "Whatever's behind them, it's already loose. Better you meet it on your feet than asleep."

* * *

They left by the back stair, down through the chandler's shop, out into Candle Lane where the lanterns burned low and the river talked to itself beneath the streets.

Wren walked beside him, close, her shoulder nearly touching his coat. She had not spoken—she had almost no words yet to speak with—but several times on the walk down from Vess's rooms he had felt her glance slide up at him, quick and searching, and each time he had pretended not to notice, and each time the pretending had cost more than the last.

At the mouth of Cutpurse Stair, where the city fell away below them in tiers of lamplight, she stopped. Tugged his sleeve. When he looked down, she pointed—at his hand, at the one that had signed, the one that had fed her, the one that had held hers on the mountain road—and then pressed that hand flat against her own chest, over her heart.

Then she pointed at him, and shook her head. Firmly. Twice.

*I know,* the gesture said, in the language older than kingdoms. *I know what your hand did. And I know it isn't you.*

He did not deserve it. That was the plain arithmetic of it, the simplest sum he had ever worked. And yet he stood there in the cold and let a mute child absolve him, because he was not strong enough to refuse, and because some starving thing in him swallowed the absolution whole.

"We'll find the truth," he said hoarsely. "All of it. Even if it's mine."

Above them, on the heights of the Terrace district, a bell began to ring—not the hour, not the alarm, but the third thing, the rare thing, the toll that Hollowbridge used only when someone important died or someone important arrived. The sound rolled down through the tiers of the city, and dogs answered it street by street, and Astrid felt the itch between his shoulder blades sharpen into something with edges.

Somewhere in Hollowbridge, the gray cloaks had arrived.

And somewhere behind the locked doors of his own skull, something stirred at the sound of the bell, and turned over in its sleep, and began—slowly, patiently, like a thorn pushing through frozen ground—to wake.
