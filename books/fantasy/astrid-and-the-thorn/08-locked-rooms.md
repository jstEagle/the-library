## 8. Locked Rooms

The key was small enough to lose in a coat seam and heavy enough to feel like a stone in the palm.

Astrid had carried it for three days now, since Dagny had pressed it into his hand over her scarred kitchen table, since Wren had fallen asleep against Maren's shoulder with the thorn pendant still humming faintly against her collarbone like a trapped bee. Three days of carrying it, and he had not once put it near the lock.

He told himself it was caution. Caution was a language he spoke fluently; it had kept him alive through twenty years of roads that ate travelers and fences who smiled too widely. But caution had a taste, and this was not it. This tasted like the moment before you opened a letter you already knew was bad news. Like standing at the edge of a frozen lake, testing the ice with your weight, knowing—knowing—that the crack would come.

The cabin was quiet in the way only his cabin could be quiet: not silence but held breath. The fire ticked in its grate. Snow came down past the window in fat, unhurried flakes, erasing the world one inch at a time until there was nothing left but the room and him and the small iron box on the shelf above the hearth.

Wren was asleep in the loft with Maren's woven charm tucked under her cheek. He could hear her breathing if he listened—the slow, even rhythm of a child finally safe enough to sleep deeply—and he found himself listening the way other men listened for wolves.

*Take her instead.*

Dagny's voice, three days old, still hanging in the air of his skull. *You hoard yourself behind locks even you can't open, boy. What are you so afraid you'll find?*

He had answered her then with something dry and deflecting, some smuggler's coin of a joke, and she had let him get away with it because Dagny always let him get away with things she shouldn't. That was her particular cruelty, maybe. Her particular love. She gave people enough rope and then sat watching to see whether they'd hang themselves or climb.

He took the key out of his pocket again. Set it on the table. Poured himself a finger of pine-spirit and did not drink it. The spirit sat in the cup like a dark eye.

*You don't have to do this tonight.*

But that was the trouble with nights like this one—with snow sealing the passes and no caravans to work and nothing between him and his own thoughts but firelight. On the road he was fine. On the road he was all reflexes and edges, lifting memories from merchants like plucking apples, clean and quick, never looking at what he took longer than the selling required. It was in stillness that the locked rooms made themselves known. Not as doors, exactly. As absences. As the shape of a thing missing, the way you felt the gap where a tooth had been, tongue worrying the space again and again.

There were gaps in him. He had always known there were gaps. A man who sold memories for a living learned to recognize the surgery marks in others, and he had long ago catalogued his own: the smooth blank stretch where a childhood should have been, the years between—he'd counted once, sitting up nights with a candle—between what he knew of himself and what he could prove. He remembered learning the theft-gift, or rather he remembered *having* it, fully formed, the way you remember having hands. He remembered Hollowbridge, the fences, the first terrible winter when he'd sold a memory just to eat and wept afterward without knowing why. He remembered everything that mattered about being Astrid Joubert, memory-thief, loner, careful man.

What he did not remember was becoming any of it.

And now there was Wren, asleep upstairs, speaking a language older than kingdoms, wearing a soul-bargain around her neck like a yoke, and every instinct in him said *run*, and every instinct also said, quieter, in a voice that sounded uncomfortably like his own younger voice: *you know this craft. You know this seal. You know the shape of the hands that made it.*

Because he did. When Maren had recoiled from the pendant, when she'd traced the bargain's working through touch and gone gray as ash, Astrid had looked at the thing—not the girl, the *thing*—and felt recognition settle over him like snowfall. Slow. Silent. Total.

Someone had sold that child's soul with a skill he knew intimately. Someone who worked memory the way he worked memory. And the mountains held perhaps four such souls, and two of them were dead, and one was Vess, who dealt in information and never in flesh.

Which left the arithmetic pointing somewhere he refused to look.

Astrid drank the pine-spirit in one swallow. It burned going down, a small honest pain, and he welcomed it the way you welcome anything true.

"Right," he said aloud, to the empty room, because saying it aloud made it a decision instead of a drift. "Right."

* * *

The vault was not a place. That was the first thing people never understood about his kind of craft. They imagined cellars, iron doors, dusty shelves—a thief's fantasy of treasure rooms. But memory did not live in meat the way coins lived in purses. Memory lived in pattern, in the webbing of light between thoughts, and a worker of the gift could fold a pattern closed the way you folded a letter, seal it the way you sealed a letter, and hide it not in a place but in a *shape*—a shape so familiar, so constant, that the mind walked past it ten thousand times without ever seeing it.

His vault was the sound of rain on the cabin roof.

That was the trick of it, and he'd known it the way he knew his own hands: without remembering learning. Every rainstorm, every night of water drumming overhead, his mind passed across the sealed folds hidden in that rhythm and read them as weather. Nothing to see here. Only rain. Only the ordinary percussion of a roof doing its work.

He knelt by the hearth, the key flat on his palm, and waited.

It took an hour for the rain to come. Of course it did. The sky, which had been dropping fat dry flakes all evening, chose this night—the one night—to hold its breath, and Astrid knelt on cold flagstone with his neck craned toward the rafters like a man praying to a god of carpentry, listening for water.

When it finally came, it came gently. A whisper first, then a patter, then the full soft language of it, and Astrid felt the familiar shapes shift in his awareness—the roof opening like a book, the rain-letters rearranging into something legible, the folds surfacing one by one like drowned things rising.

There were seven.

He had never known the number before. Knowing the number changed something. Seven sealed folds, seven locked rooms, and each one hummed against his attention with a different weight, the way keys on a ring have different weights, the way griefs do. Six of them were shut tight as fists. But the seventh—

The seventh trembled.

Not broken. Not decayed—seals didn't decay; they were cut in pattern, not in time. Trembling the way a door trembles when someone stands on the other side of it. Or the way a word trembles when it has been waiting years in your mouth.

The key grew warm in his palm. Not hot—warm, the way a stone is warm if a hand has been holding it a long while. Dagny's hand. Three days in his pocket, and before that however many years in hers, and before that—

Before that was the locked thing itself, wasn't it. The key's history was part of the cargo.

"Don't think," he told himself quietly. "Thinking is how you talk yourself out of doors."

He set the key against the seventh fold the way you set a key against a lock: blind, by feel, trusting the teeth to find their tumblers. There was a moment of resistance—the deep, muscular reluctance of a thing sealed well—and then the key turned with a sensation he had no honest word for, somewhere between a click and a bone setting, and the fold opened.

Memory poured in like cold water finding a crack.

* * *

Snow.

Snow falling through a darkness that was not night—or was night, but a night with no stars, a night under trees, a thick canopy-dark where the snow came down in slow spirals and landed without sound on—on his shoulders. He was outside. He was standing. His feet hurt with cold in a way that meant he had been standing a long time, and there was a bundle in his arms.

A bundle wrapped in grey wool, the good wool, the wool you saved, and it was heavy the way nothing so small should be heavy, and it was crying.

A crying infant. Thin, furious, exhausted crying, the cry of a creature that has cried past hunger and past cold into pure insistence: *I am here. I am here. I am here.* The sound went through him like a blade through wet canvas, and he understood dimly, with the part of the mind that stands apart even inside a memory, that this was not an ordinary child's cry. There was a resonance in it. A harmonic underneath, like a bell heard through a wall. Something in the cry answered the mountains themselves.

His arms ached. He was swaying, rocking the bundle, an automatic motion, and his breath came out in white flags, and he was looking down at the small red face screwed tight with fury and life, and he was saying—

He was saying words. He felt his own mouth move. The memory gave him the sound of his own voice as if from a little distance, muffled by snowfall, worn thin:

*"Take her instead."*

And another voice answered. A voice like dry leaves and door-hinges, a voice with courtliness rotted inside it, a voice that made the trees lean away:

*"The bargain is struck, then. The name for the soul. You understand what you surrender?"*

*"I understand."*

His own voice again. Steady. Steadier than a man holding a crying infant in the snow had any right to be. Steady the way a man is steady when he has already done the crying, earlier, alone, and wrung himself out, and come to the meeting place with nothing left but resolve.

*"Then give it here, memory-thief, and be quick. The gate will not stay open past the third bell."*

Arms reached out of the dark—arms in grey, long-fingered, thorn-scarred—and the bundle began to lift away from him, and the infant's cry spiked, and Astrid felt his own hands tighten, felt the whole body of his younger self seize with a refusal that lasted one heartbeat, two—

And then the memory tore.

Not ended. Tore—ripped jaggedly forward, the way a dream tears when you fall, and the next thing was different: the same snow, the same dark under the trees, but his arms were empty. Empty and aching with the ghost-weight of a child no longer there, and his knees were in the snow, and he was making a sound he did not recognize as coming from a human throat, and far off in the black between the trees a door was closing—a door of thorn and root, a door that sighed shut like a book—and the last thread of the infant's cry came through the closing gap, thin as wire:

*I am here. I am here. I am—*

Gone.

* * *

Astrid came back to himself on the hearthstone with his face wet and his hands shaking so badly the key chattered against the floor.

The fire had burned low. The rain still whispered on the roof. Somewhere above him, Wren turned over in her sleep, and the floorboard creaked under her small shifting weight, and the sound of it went through him like a spear.

*Take her instead.*

His voice. His own voice, saying it, in snow, in the dark, to a thing with a rotten-courtly voice, while he held a crying infant whose cry rang with the same harmonic he had heard—God help him, he had heard it—in the pendant around Wren's neck. In Wren herself, on the nights when she slept badly and murmured in her root-language, the sounds carrying that strange undertone, that bell-through-a-wall resonance he had noticed and filed away and refused to examine.

He had been there. At the sale. Not nearby—*present*. Holding her. Speaking the words. Making the bargain.

And then he had torn the rest away. He could feel the tear now, raw as a wound's edge, the memory ripped off mid-moment by his own hand—his own craft, his own skill, turned inward with a violence he had never used on any traveler. He had not merely locked this room. He had gutted it, left it bleeding, sealed the doors, and thrown the key to an old woman in Hollowbridge with instructions he could not remember giving.

*For when I'm brave enough.*

"Oh," Astrid said. It came out very small. "Oh, you coward."

He knelt on the cold stone and understood, in the way you understand a thing all at once and forever, that guilt had arrived ahead of understanding. He did not know yet who the infant had been—whether Wren, whether kin, whether some other stolen child entirely. He did not know whom he had been saving when he said *take her instead*, or what price the court had named, or what *name for the soul* meant—whose name, whose soul. He did not know why he had erased himself so thoroughly, or what else lay in the six remaining folds, or which of them the Thorn Court itself had bought.

He knew only this: a child had cried in his arms in the snow, and he had given her away, and then he had stolen the memory of giving her away so that he would never have to carry it. He had done to himself what he did to merchants on the road at dusk. He had lifted the unbearable thing and sold it into the dark and walked away lighter.

Except he hadn't walked away lighter. He had walked away hollow, and the hollowness had a shape, and the shape was a grey bundle in the snow, and the shape had just climbed his stairs on bare feet and fallen asleep under his roof.

Above him, the floorboard creaked again.

Astrid rose on unsteady legs. He stood a long moment at the foot of the stairs with one hand on the rail, listening to the small sounds of her breathing, and the guilt in his chest was so large and so specific that it seemed to bend the firelight around it.

Six folds remained. Six locked rooms, and somewhere among them—the arithmetic was merciless now, it pointed like a compass needle—one of them had been bought by the Thorn Court itself. Which meant that to learn the whole truth, to learn what he had traded and why and what the court still held of him, he would have to go where the court kept its heirlooms.

Down. Beneath the mountain. Into the dark where souls hung like lanterns in a thorn-crowned hall.

He looked at the key in his palm. Then, slowly, deliberately, he crossed to the shelf above the hearth, moved aside the small iron box that held nothing but flint, and set the key beneath it where the firelight would find it every morning.

"No more locks," he said to the empty room. His voice cracked on it. "No more."

Upstairs, Wren murmured in her sleep—a thread of that older-than-kingdoms language, rising and falling—and beneath the murmur, clear as a bell through a wall, the harmonic sang.

Astrid stood in the dark and listened, and for the first time since the storm had brought her to his door, he let himself hear it fully: the infant's cry and the sleeping child's dreaming voice were the same song.

Whatever he had surrendered in the snow, it had not stayed surrendered. It had come back to him barefoot, through the storm, knocking at his door.

And the Thorn Court would want it back.
