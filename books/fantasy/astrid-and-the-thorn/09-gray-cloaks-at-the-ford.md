## 9. Gray Cloaks at the Ford

The river speaks before Wren sees it.

She hears it the way she hears everything now—not with her ears first, but with her roots, the deep invisible threads she has begun to understand run out of her always, into soil and stone and every living green thing. The river's voice is cold and silver and full of stones turning over in their sleep. *Come down,* it says. *Come down, small sister, and I will carry you where walking cannot.*

Wren does not have words for most of what she feels. She is learning them—*bread*, which means warmth and softness and the crust that crackles; *fire*, which means the orange animal that lives in stone circles and eats wood and purrs; *Astrid*, which means a shape that stoops and a voice like a door opening in a house she thought was empty—but the river needs no words. The river is old language. The river speaks the way she spoke when she first fell through the world, all current and weight and no edges.

She stops at the top of the bank and lets it pour into her.

Behind her, Astrid's footsteps falter. She has learned to read his footsteps the way he reads roads: the quick light ones mean they are safe for now; the long deliberate ones mean he is thinking; the ones that stop entirely mean danger, or memory, or both.

"Wren." His voice comes low. "What is it?"

She turns. The morning is gray and thin, mist still tangled in the pines like wool caught on thorns. Astrid stands three paces back with his hand already inside his coat, where the knife lives, where the stolen things live. His face is doing the thing she has begun to hate: the closing, the shuttering, the man becoming a wall. He has been more wall than man since the mine, since he tore the fear out of the gray-cloaked one and came away shaking as if *he* were the one who had been robbed.

Wren points down the slope, through the trees, to where water flashes between the trunks.

"The ford," Astrid says. He does not relax. Nothing makes him relax anymore. But something in him tilts, calculating, and she watches him do the arithmetic of distance and pursuit and daylight the way other men count coins. "It's the fastest crossing. Which means it's the obvious crossing. Which means—" He stops himself. Looks at her. Looks at the river. "You hear it too."

Not a question. He has stopped pretending not to know what she is. Wren nods once, slow, the way he nods, because she has learned that from him too: how to be a person by copying one.

"Then we cross fast and we don't linger," he says. "Stay close. If anything happens—if I say *run*—you run. You don't wait for me. You don't look back. You go."

Wren looks at him for a long moment.

Then she shakes her head.

He exhales through his nose, that sound that is almost a laugh but has forgotten how to finish becoming one. "Of course you don't listen. Why would you start now?" But there is something underneath the words, something warm and wounded, and Wren roots into it without meaning to, the way she roots into everything: *he does not want to be left alone in the dark. He has been left alone in the dark so long he has built a house there.*

They go down together.

* * *

The ford is a wide shallow place where the river spreads itself thin over pale stones, chattering, showing off. On the far side the land climbs into dark fells, and beyond the fells, somewhere, Maren's cottage, and herbs, and hands that heal. Wren can feel the witch's garden even from here, a faint green hum on the edge of her senses, rosemary and feverfew standing in their rows like patient soldiers.

Astrid crouches at the water's edge and tests the current with two fingers. "Knee-deep here, maybe thigh in the middle. Cold enough to stop your heart if you stay in too long, but we won't." He glances up at her, and for a moment—not a wall, just a tired man with frost-colored eyes—"You've never crossed running water, have you? Not really crossed it. Not in this body."

Wren hasn't. This body has known only roads and rooms and the inside of fear. But the river knows her. The river has been calling her *sister* since they crested the ridge, and sisters do not drown each other.

She steps past him and puts her foot in the water.

The cold bites, sharp as teeth—and then, instantly, unaccountably, it doesn't. The current parts around her ankle like a crowd making way. The stones beneath the surface shift, settling themselves flat and firm under her sole, an arrangement of the riverbed so precise it can only be courtesy.

Astrid stares. "That's not—that's not supposed to happen."

Wren takes another step. The water bows. Fish gather in the eddies beside her calves, not fleeing, watching, their mouths opening and closing in silent speech she almost understands: *the little queen walks among us, the little queen, the root-born, the thorn-kin—*

*Astrid!* she tries to say, and what comes out is the old language, the falling language, syllables like seeds splitting open, and the river rises half an inch along both banks in answer, delighted, attentive, a dog hearing its name.

"Don't," Astrid says sharply. "Don't call attention—we don't know who's listening—"

Someone is listening.

Wren feels it before she hears it: a wrongness in the ground, upstream, where the pines grow thick and black. It is not the wrongness of wolves. Wolves are honest hunger. This is a wrongness like frost in spring, like a nail driven into a living tree—the feeling of something dead that refuses to stop moving.

She grabs Astrid's sleeve.

He follows her eyes upstream and goes very still, the stillness of prey animals and professionals. And then Wren sees them too, sliding between the trunks with the unhurried grace of things that have never once needed to hurry: four figures in gray, cloaks the color of ash and fog, faces beneath their hoods smooth and dim as river-worn stones. They do not walk so much as *arrive*, again and again, each step a small erasure of the distance between.

"Thorn Court," Astrid breathes. "Here. Already." His hand closes around hers—cold fingers, steady grip—and pulls her back toward the near bank. "Water. We go back, we find another crossing, we—"

The lead figure raises one hand.

The river stops.

Not slows. *Stops.* The whole broad shining length of the ford goes rigid and silent, every ripple frozen mid-curve, the white noise of it cut off so completely that Wren's ears ring in the sudden hush. Mist hangs motionless above the surface. A fish hangs suspended beneath it, mouth open around a bubble that will not rise.

The river screams.

Wren feels it as a vibration through her rooted self, through the soles of her feet, through her bones: the river screaming against the hold on it, straining like a horse reined hard, and the gray cloaks stand on the bank with their stone faces and their raised hand and they are *holding* the river the way a thief holds a stolen purse.

*Ours,* says a voice that is not a voice, that arrives inside her skull like a splinter. *The vessel returns. The court thanks the carrier. Set down the cargo, thief, and perhaps you keep your hands.*

"They're not taking her." Astrid's voice has gone quiet and flat, the voice he uses for prices and threats. He steps in front of her, one arm out, barring her behind him like a gate. "Whatever she is to you, whatever contract, whatever seal—you don't touch her."

*You misunderstand your position,* the splinter-voice says, almost gently. *You are not an obstacle. You are packaging.*

The gray cloaks come down to the water.

And the river breaks free.

Later, Wren will try to explain it and fail, because there are no words yet in her borrowed mouth for what happens next. But in the moment she only knows this: the hold on the river snaps like a rotted rope, and the river *rises*—not in a wave, not in a flood, but in a shape, a towering curling shape with arms and intention, and it comes off its bed with a roar that shakes pinecones from the branches, and it does not strike the gray cloaks so much as *refuse* them, hurling them backward across the bank like dolls, like litter, like things the world had simply decided it was done carrying.

One of them catches itself, stone face cracking along the jaw. Another loses its hood and beneath is nothing but woven shadow and a pair of pale lights where eyes might have been. The splinter-voice shrieks—a real sound now, ugly, furious—and the remaining cloaks regroup with terrible patience, drawing blades of black iron that drink the gray light.

"Wren!" Astrid shoves her toward the far bank—*toward*, not away, and some buried part of her notes that he has changed the plan, changed it for her, *run* now means *run forward, run with me, run where I can reach you*. "Cross! Now! I'm right behind you!"

She believes him. That is the terrible, wonderful thing. She believes him the way the river believes gravity.

She runs into the frozen-still water and the moment her feet touch it the river wakes fully, roaring back to life around her legs, and instead of dragging her it *carries* her, the current cupping her like two hands, hurrying her across the stones while the battle erupts behind her.

She should keep going. She told herself she would keep going. Astrid said *right behind you* and right behind you means you run and you don't look back—

She looks back.

Astrid has not followed. Astrid is on the near bank with his knife out and his coat flaring, and he has stepped *between* the gray cloaks and the river, between them and her, one thin mortal man placing himself in front of four servants of a court older than kingdoms. One of the black blades flashes. He twists—not fast enough. Wren sees the blade catch him high on the side, see the dark bloom spread across his shirt, sees him stagger and *keep his feet*, keep his body between them and the water, keep his arm out pointing at her even as his knees begin to fold.

*Run,* his mouth shapes. No sound. Just the shape of it. *Run.*

No.

The word rises in Wren from somewhere deeper than language, deeper than the falling place, deeper than the thorn pendant suddenly blazing against her chest like a captive star. Heat pours down her sternum, through her ribs, into the ground, and the ground answers—all of it, the whole valley, every root and stone and sleeping seed—and the river turns as one creature and comes back for him.

The gray cloaks raise their hands to stop it.

The river is not stopped. The river is *hers*, or she is the river's, and kin does not obey kin's enemies. Water slams into the nearest cloak and unwinds it like thread. Black iron sinks and stays sunk. The splinter-voice screams something about law, about claim, about *contract*, and the river answers by tearing the last two agents off the bank and grinding them against the stones until the stone faces crack like eggs and the shadows inside spill out and dissolve in the current, hissing, undone.

Then it is quiet. The river settles. The mist begins, slowly, to move again.

And Astrid is on his knees at the water's edge with blood spreading under him and his eyes fixed on her with an expression she has no word for except that it is the opposite of the wall, the opposite of closed—it is a door blown open, a man looking at something he cannot price, cannot sell, cannot steal, cannot understand, and does not want to.

Wren wades back to him through water gone gentle as bathwater. She kneels in the shallows. Her hands find the wound—hot, wet, frightening—and press down the way she has seen pressed, instinct outrunning knowledge. Blood wells between her fingers, red as rowan berries, red as the flower she sometimes dreams of and cannot name.

"You came back," Astrid says. His voice is fraying at the edges. "I told you to run. You came *back*."

Wren looks at him. At this ridiculous, shuttered, bleeding man who stood between her and the dark with nothing but a knife and a lie about being right behind her. Who steals memories and keeps none. Who feeds her bread and teaches her words and builds walls she can feel him building, brick by brick, against ever belonging to anyone.

She leans forward and presses her forehead to his, the way the old language says *mine*, the way it says *chosen*, the way it says *family*—and feels him go utterly still, utterly undone, a man receiving something he had convinced himself was never coming.

"I know," he whispers, to the words she didn't say in any tongue. "I know. Me too."

His eyes roll back. The river holds him upright until she can get her shoulder under his arm.

Across the water, on the torn and empty bank, something glints among the scattered gray cloth: a scrap of parchment, weighted with a seal of black wax stamped with a crown of thorns. The wind lifts it, carries it across the still water, and lays it at Wren's knee like an offering—or a summons.

Written in ink that moves like living shadow, in a script she somehow reads without knowing how:

***THE DEBTOR IS KNOWN TO US. COME AND SETTLE.***

Wren looks at the words. Then at Astrid's gray face. Then north, toward the mountain, where the fells climb dark against the sky.

The pendant cools against her chest, quiet now, waiting.

She gets her arm around him, and begins, inch by inch, to pull him up the far bank—toward Maren, toward help, toward the debt and the mountain and whatever waits beneath it.
