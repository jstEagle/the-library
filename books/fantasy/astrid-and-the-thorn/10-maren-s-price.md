## 10. Maren's Price

The rain found them before the road did.

It came down in gray sheets off the fells, turning the smuggler's path into a riverbed, and Astrid walked through it with Wren's small hand locked in his and his own blood thinning to pink water beneath his coat. The wound was in his side, low, where the gray cloak's blade had slipped past his guard at the ford. Not deep. Deep enough. Every step sent a slow pulse of heat up through his ribs, as if someone had pressed a coal against him and left it there.

Wren had not spoken—she never spoke—but she had not let go of him either. That was its own kind of language. When he stumbled on the wet stone, her grip tightened; when he righted himself, it loosened just enough to say *good, again, keep going*. She navigated by some sense he couldn't name, tugging him left when the path forked, pulling him down when the wind screamed over the ridge. The mountains liked her. He was beginning to understand that they liked her the way a hearth likes its own fire.

Maren's cottage sat in a fold of the hills where no cart track went, walled with turf and old stone, roofed in living heather. Smoke rose from it crooked, bent sideways by the wind like a man leaning into a gale. Astrid had been here twice before in nine years of living half-dead in these mountains—once for a fever, once for something he didn't let himself think about—and both times the cottage had seemed smaller than he remembered, as if the land itself were trying to hide it.

He hammered on the door with the heel of his fist.

The door opened before the third blow landed. Maren stood in the gap, silver-haired, broad-shouldered, her eyes the flat pale gray of a blind woman's—which they were, and weren't. She looked at him the way she always did: not with her eyes but with her hands, which came up already reading the air between them.

Then her fingers found his sleeve, and stopped.

Her whole body went still. Stillness, on Maren, was louder than shouting.

*You're bleeding,* she signed, sharp and fast. *Inside. Now. Both of you.*

* * *

The cottage smelled of yarrow and smoke and something older underneath, a green smell like the inside of a hill. Maren sat Astrid on a stool by the fire and cut his shirt away from the wound with a knife that had seen more years than either of them. Wren hovered at the edge of the firelight, watching everything with those dark, root-dark eyes of hers, and when Maren reached for a basin, the girl fetched it before the witch could turn. When Maren pointed at the shelf, Wren brought down the jar of rendered fat and the bundle of dried moss without being told which was which.

Maren paused. Looked at the jars in the child's hands. Looked at Wren.

*She knows my shelves,* she signed slowly, one hand talking to Astrid while the other kept pressure on the wound. *Better than I do.*

"She knows growing things," Astrid said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Everything that grows."

*And you know how she got that way.* It wasn't a question. Maren's hands moved with the clipped economy of a woman who had spent a lifetime saying things once. *I felt it the day you brought her here first. Felt it through the floor.* Her fingers curled, an old gesture he'd learned meant *thorns*. *That pendant. You still carry it.*

"It's around her neck."

*I know where it is. I can feel it humming from here, like a wasp in a jar.* Her mouth thinned. *Hold still.*

She packed the wound with moss and sewed it with thread waxed in pine resin, and Astrid bore it the way he bore most things—quietly, counting his breaths, letting the pain be a thing happening to a body rather than a self. But halfway through, Maren's hand flattened against his skin above the stitching, and her head tilted, and for a long moment she simply read him. That was how it worked with Maren. Touch was her tongue, and memory was what she tasted.

When she pulled back, her face had gone strange.

*There's blood in your past,* she signed. *Not yours.*

"I know whose."

*Do you?* Her hands hung in the air, unfinished. Then she shook her head, silver hair swinging, and turned back to her work. *Later. First we stop you leaking.*

* * *

It took until well past dark. The fire burned down to coals; Wren fell asleep sitting upright in the corner, her back against the wall, her chin dropped to her chest, one hand closed around the thorn pendant even in sleep. Maren spread a blanket over her without touching the pendant itself—Astrid noticed that. The witch gave that little black thorn a wide berth, the way you'd step around a sleeping adder.

When it was done, Maren washed her hands, dried them, and stood in front of Astrid with her arms crossed. In the firelight her shadow went up the wall huge and hunched, like something out of the oldest stories.

*My price,* she signed.

Astrid nodded. He'd known this was coming since the needle first bit. Maren never healed for coin—coin could be stolen, and stolen things soured in her house. She took payment in the only currency she trusted. Memory. A memory of his choosing, siphoned out clean, given freely.

"Name it," he said.

*Your choosing,* she repeated, and her pale eyes fixed on him with terrible gentleness. *That's the price. You choose. And I'll know if you choose badly.*

"There's no such thing as choosing badly. They're only memories."

*No.* She crouched so they were eye to eye, and her hands moved slow now, each sign deliberate as a seed pressed into soil. *A memory is not a stone you hand over. It's a room in your house. Give away the wrong room and you'll walk through your own life knocking on walls, wondering why nothing fits. I've taken memories from men who chose carelessly. I've watched them forget why they loved their wives, because they sold the wrong afternoon. Choose something you can afford to lose, thief. If such a thing exists in you.*

Astrid looked at the fire. He thought about his rules—the ones he'd lived by for nine years, carved into him like grooves in a doorframe. Never steal from children. Never keep what you take. Never open the vault behind his own eyes, the one he'd locked so thoroughly that even the key's shape had gone strange to him.

He had a lifetime of memories and almost none of them were safe to give. The useful ones were leverage. The painful ones were ballast—he suspected, in some wordless way, that if he started unloading them he might capsize entirely. And the happy ones—

The happy ones were few. That was the truth of it. Nine years of professional loneliness, of lifting other people's joy out of their heads and handing it to fences, of keeping nothing. There were perhaps a handful of afternoons in all that time that he would have called warm.

One of them rose in him now, unbidden: three summers ago, or four. A market day in Hollowbridge, before Vess's debts and Dagny's scolding had curdled into routine. Rain threatening all morning and never falling. He'd bought a paper cone of roasted chestnuts from a woman with flour on her apron, and eaten them on the bridge with his feet dangling over the brown water, and an old dog had come and leaned against his leg—not begging, just leaning, the way dogs do when they've decided you're tolerable—and he'd sat there until the light went gold and then copper, sharing chestnuts with a stranger's dog, wanting nothing, owing nothing, being no one in particular.

It wasn't much. That was exactly why it would do.

"This one," he said, and held it out the way he'd held out a hundred stolen memories for sale—cupped, offered, detached.

Maren took his face in both hands.

Her palms were dry and warm and smelled of yarrow, and her touch slid into him with the ease of long practice. He felt her move through the outer rooms of his mind the way a housekeeper moves through a hall—respectful, quick, touching nothing she hadn't been given. She found the afternoon on the bridge. He felt her pause there, felt her read it whole: the chestnuts, the dog, the gold light going copper.

Then she took it.

It should have hurt. Taking always hurt the ones he stole from—a flinch, a gasp, a hand pressed to the temple. But this was given, and given things leave differently. There was a sensation like a page turning. Like setting down a weight he hadn't known he carried.

And then—

Nothing. Not even the nothing of absence. Just smooth, unbroken continuity, as though the afternoon had never existed, as though the space where it had been had always been exactly this size and shape and full of exactly this much emptiness.

"That's it?" he heard himself ask.

Maren released his face and sat back on her heels. She was crying. Silently, the way deaf people sometimes cry, without any change in her breathing, tears simply falling as if her eyes had decided independently of her.

*That's it,* she signed. *Do you know what I just took?*

"A market afternoon. Chestnuts. A dog."

*A dog,* she repeated. Her hands trembled. *Astrid. You described it perfectly. You described every detail—the apron, the bridge, the color of the light—and you said it like a man reading an inventory. Like a fence appraising goods.* She wiped her cheeks with the heel of her hand. *You didn't notice it leaving. Not really. You noticed the transaction.*

The fire ticked. In the corner, Wren slept on, her fist tight around the thorn.

"I paid," Astrid said. "That's all it is. I've done it a thousand times from the other side. People sell me their worst days and walk out lighter. It's mercy work, half the time—"

*Is it?* Maren's signs came fast and hard now, cutting the air. *When you take a bad memory, the man remembers losing it. He feels the hole and he's grateful for the hole. But you—you just handed me one of the few good hours you have left, and you can't even feel the shape of where it was. Do you understand what that means?*

He didn't answer.

*It means you've been doing this to yourself for years,* she signed. *Every memory you ever sold, every one you locked away—you've worn the channels so smooth that giving yourself away doesn't even register. You're a man standing in a house with fewer rooms every year, telling himself the house feels spacious.* Her hands dropped. *I shouldn't have taken it. I knew, the moment I touched it, that it was one of maybe five warm hours you own. I took it anyway, because a price is a price, and because I wanted you to see it happen. To watch yourself do it.*

"Why?" His voice came out flat. "Why would you want that?"

*Because in three days you're going to stand in front of the Thorn Court,* she said, *and they are going to offer you a bargain that sounds exactly like this one. Painless. Reasonable. A fair price. And you will pay it smiling, because paying is the only way you know how to be worth anything to anyone.* She stood, joints cracking, and pulled a blanket from the chest. *Learn what it costs first. Learn it in a cottage, with soup after, instead of under a mountain with thorns in your mouth.*

She threw the blanket at his chest.

*Sleep. You'll take the watch at moonrise. And Astrid—* her hands hesitated, then finished, quiet as ash settling— *the dog's name was Bracken. The woman's name was Odile. You kept the details and lost the warmth. That's how it always goes with you. Don't let the court make a habit of it.*

* * *

Moonrise came gray through the smoke-hole. Astrid sat the watch with his back to the wall and his knife across his knees, listening to the two breaths in the room—Maren's deep and even, Wren's quick and light as a bird's.

The wound pulsed. Sewn shut, packed with moss, but pulsing, a reminder written in heat: *you bled for her, you'd bleed again, and you still don't know why.*

Except he was starting to know why. Starting to feel the edges of it, the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue. The signature Vess had shown him, flowing off his own pen without thought. The fragment behind the lock—snow, a crying infant, his own voice saying *take her instead*. The way the lullaby had risen out of him whole, note-perfect, a song he had no right to know.

He looked across the dying fire at Wren. In sleep her face lost its wariness and became simply a child's face, round-cheeked, chapped lips slightly parted. The pendant rose and fell with her breath, and in the ember-light the black thorn gleamed like a splinter of midnight.

*Take her instead.*

Instead of whom?

The question turned over in him, patient as water wearing at stone. He had spent nine years believing his past was a locked room because he'd wanted it locked—that whatever waited inside was too heavy to carry. But Maren's hands had read something else in him tonight. Blood in his past, not his own. And grief, she'd signed afterward, grief worn down to the grain, the kind that stops feeling like weather and starts feeling like climate.

He was so tired of climates.

Near dawn, Wren woke. She didn't cry out or startle; she simply opened her eyes, found him in the gray light, and crossed the room to sit beside him, shoulder to shoulder, the way she'd begun doing. For a while neither of them moved. Then she reached out and touched his stitched side—gently, fingertips hovering just above the bandage, asking permission even now—and when he nodded, she rested her palm there, flat, careful.

Warmth spread under her hand. Not healing, exactly. More like spring coming early to one small patch of ground.

He looked at her sharply. She looked back, unblinking, and something passed between them that needed no language at all: *you bled; I mend; we are not even, because we are family, and family doesn't count.*

"You shouldn't waste that," he said quietly. "Whatever it is. You'll need it for yourself."

She shook her head once, firm as a root taking hold.

And it was then, in the gray hour before sunrise, that hoofbeats sounded on the trail below the cottage—many horses, moving fast, and among the jingle of tack a sound that raised every hair on Astrid's neck: the dry rattle of thorn-wood, clicking against saddle leather like bones.

Wren's hand clamped over the pendant. Through the smoke-hole, far down the valley, Astrid saw them: riders in gray, six of them, fanning out along the stream with the unhurried precision of hunters who already knew where the fox slept.

But they weren't riding toward Maren's door.

They were riding toward Hollowbridge. Toward Dagny. Toward everyone who had ever sheltered them—and ahead of the riders, moving faster than any horse, went a single figure on foot, wrapped in a cloak the color of wet slate, carrying a sealed letter bound in black thorn-thread.

Astrid was on his feet before he'd decided to rise, the loss of the chestnut afternoon already forgotten, the wound screaming, his mind doing the only thing it had ever truly known how to do:

Counting exits. Counting debts. Counting the people he could not afford to lose.
