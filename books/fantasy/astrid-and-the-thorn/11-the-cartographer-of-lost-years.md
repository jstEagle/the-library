## 11. The Cartographer of Lost Years

Vess kept her shop above a tannery in the stinking quarter of Hollowbridge, where the smell rose through the floorboards like an argument no one could win. Astrid climbed the stairs with Wren's small hand wrapped in his, and the girl pressed her sleeve over her nose and made a face that was almost—almost—a child's expression. In the weeks since the ford, since the river had risen like a living thing to shield her, she had begun to look less like something the mountain had misplaced and more like a girl.

That frightened him more than the gray cloaks had.

The door at the top of the stairs was papered over with maps. Old ones, new ones, maps of places that did not exist and one, he noticed, of the Thornfell passes drawn from below—which was impossible, unless you were Vess, who collected impossibilities the way other people collected debts. He knocked twice, paused, then once more: the rhythm they'd agreed on six years ago, when he'd sold her his memory of a harbor town and she'd promised him, in exchange, that she would never sell anything about him to anyone else.

"Late," said the voice behind the door.

"You're open."

"I'm always open. That's different from being awake." The door swung inward on a room lit by oil lamps hung from fishhooks, every surface buried under charts, ledgers, brass instruments, and jars of ink ground from things Astrid preferred not to ask about. Vess herself sat cross-legged atop a desk that had clearly surrendered its original purpose years ago. She was small and sharp-faced, her gray hair bound in copper wire, her fingers stained black to the second knuckle. She looked at Wren first, then at him, and her mouth thinned.

"So it's true," she said. "You've got the court's cargo under your arm."

"She's not cargo."

"No?" Vess slid off the desk. "Then why do the smugglers' taverns have a price on your head that would buy this whole street? Why did three of my runners come back gray-eyed and empty this week? Astrid." She said his name the way you'd tap a cracked bell. "I owe you. I know what I owe you. But I need you to understand what's coming down that mountain after you."

Wren tugged his sleeve. She looked up at Vess and signed something—her hands moving in that flowing root-language Maren had taught him to half-read—and then, when Vess only stared, she pointed at the maps on the walls, then at her own chest, then made a slow spiral downward, toward the deeps.

Vess went very still.

"That's what I thought," she said quietly. "She wants to know where the rest of you is."

* * *

He hadn't meant to tell her everything. He'd meant to tell her nothing, in fact, and to leave Hollowbridge by dawn with a map of the smuggler roads south. But Vess fed them—dark bread, hard cheese, tea brewed from pine needles—and somewhere between the bread and the tea he heard himself describing the vault behind his own eyes: the locked rooms, the sealed doors, the years of himself he had pried loose and sold off one piece at a time to fund a life with no name attached to it.

"You sold yourself," Vess said. Not horrified. Professional. "In pieces."

"To stay invisible." He turned his cup. "A man with no past can't be traced. No grudges follow him. No debts. I sold my childhood to a collector in Corvane. My mother's face went to a woman in the river towns who'd lost her own mother and wanted the shape of one. A decade of winters went to—" he stopped. "It doesn't matter who. What matters is that I kept the important things locked, and even I don't have the key to most of them anymore."

"But Dagny has a key."

"Dagny has *a* key." His jaw tightened. "One door opened. Snow. A crying infant. My own voice saying *take her instead.*" He looked at Wren, who was watching him with those dark, patient eyes, and made himself go on. "There are more doors. And if the court is hunting me because of whatever I did—if the bargain that put her soul under the mountain came out of my own hidden past—then I need to know all of it. Every fragment. Before they take her."

Vess was already pulling parchment toward her across the littered desk. "How many?"

"How many what?"

"Fragments. Doors. Count them."

He closed his eyes and did what he had never done: walked the corridors of his own mind deliberately, counting the sealed rooms the way a thief counts locks before a job. "Eleven," he said. "Seven are gone. Sold, spent, scattered. Four left."

"Four left where?"

"That's the problem. I sold them through fences. Through brokers. Some through you."

"Not through me." Vess's voice had an edge. "I told you—I never sold a thing about you. But I *bought* records. Ledger entries. Debts of the memory-trade pass through this city like blood through a heart, and I've been mapping them for twenty years because knowing where everyone's secrets sleep is worth more than gold." She dipped her pen. "Give me the shapes of them. Not the contents—you couldn't give me the contents if you tried, they're locked—but the weight. When you sold each one, how heavy was it? What season? What did your hands feel like afterward?"

It was the strangest work he had ever done: sitting in a lamplit room above a tannery, describing the *texture* of his own amputated memories to a woman with ink-black fingers while a mute child watched him with the patience of standing water. But Vess was brilliant at it, in her terrible way. Each detail he gave her, she matched against her ledgers—the trade records of a dozen fences, the auction notes of collectors dead and living, the whispered inventory of the memory-markets that ran beneath Hollowbridge like a second city.

By the time the lamps burned low, she had covered three sheets of parchment with a diagram that looked less like a ledger and more like a star chart: four points, connected by inked lines, annotated in her cramped hand.

"There," she said, tapping the first point. "Corvane. The collector Aldous Renk—he bought your childhood winter, the one with the well and the dog, and he still holds it. He's old, he's vain, and he keeps his collection in a warded strongroom under his house. Retrieval difficulty: moderate. You've stolen from worse."

"The second?"

"A shrine in the Graywater marshes. A cult of the Drowned Lady bought nine years of your youth—your apprenticeship, whoever trained you, all of it. They feed memories to their goddess. Retrieval difficulty: high, because the goddess sometimes *answers*, and what answers isn't friendly." She moved her finger. "Third. This one surprised me. A pawnshop in Lowmarket, here in Hollowbridge. Your seller didn't even bother hiding it. Whoever fenced that fragment wanted it findable."

Astrid stared at the mark. "Findable by whom?"

"By you, I'd guess. Someone wanted you to be able to buy yourself back." Vess leaned back and studied him with those flat, unblinking eyes. "Which brings us to the fourth. And Astrid—I want you to hear this part sitting down."

"I'm fine where I am."

"The fourth fragment never entered the mortal trade. There's no fence, no collector, no ledger entry. It passed straight from your seller's hands into—" she tapped the lowest point on her chart, where the inked lines spiraled down into a blank space she had shaded with charcoal until it looked like a hole in the page, "—the Thorn Court's own vault. Beneath the mountain. It's the oldest entry in any record I have, and the seal on it matches the pendant that child is wearing."

The room seemed to tilt. Wren's hand found his without either of them looking.

"Three retrievable," he said finally. "One held by the dead."

"Three retrievable, if you move fast. Renk is dying—that's why his collection matters to him, he thinks memories keep a man alive past his hour. The Drowned Lady's cult floods their shrine at each dark moon; after next dark moon, whatever's sunk stays sunk. And the pawnshop—" Vess smiled without warmth, "—the pawnshop closes when its owner decides to close it, and its owner has been asking after a memory-thief with your description for two days now. The gray cloaks aren't just hunting you in the fells, Astrid. They're buying up the market. They mean to own every piece of you before you can reach them."

"Why?" The question tore out of him raw. "Why does a court of thorn-crowned dead care about the discarded scraps of a smuggler? They have souls in heirloom cases. They have kingdoms of the buried dead. What am I to them?"

Vess looked at Wren. Then at him. Then, very carefully, she rolled up the chart and tied it with wire.

"I map things," she said. "I don't interpret them. But I'll tell you what the shape looks like to someone who's been reading ledgers for twenty years." She set the roll in his hands. "Every sale you ever made—every fragment you cut out of yourself—they all trace back to a single origin date. One transaction, older than all the others, that funded everything else. And that transaction isn't in any mortal ledger, because it wasn't a mortal sale. Whatever you sold *first*, Astrid, you sold to the mountain. Everything else was just you spending the change."

* * *

They left before dawn, when Hollowbridge was all fog and fish-rot and the gulls were screaming over the rooftops like creditors. Vess walked them down as far as the tannery door, which for her was a ceremony of affection.

"The pawnshop first," she said. "Lowmarket, sign of the Brass Owl. Go today, before the cloaks do. Then Corvane if you can reach it. The marshes last—or never, honestly, the marshes will kill you as quick as the court will." She hesitated, and then did something he had never seen her do: she took his hand in both of hers, ink-stained and cold. "When you get them back—all of them—you're going to be someone you don't recognize. Are you ready for that?"

"I spent ten years making sure I'd never have to answer that question."

"Well." She released him. "Answer it anyway."

Wren stopped on the threshold and turned back. She signed to Vess—slowly, formally, the way Maren had taught her that thanks was given—and Vess watched the girl's hands with an expression Astrid had never seen on her face before, something soft and lost, like a woman reading a letter from someone she'd mourned.

"What did she say?" he asked, when they were down in the fog.

Vess's voice followed them, thin through the morning air. "She said: *when you find the missing piece of yourself, bring it home too.*"

The road north out of Hollowbridge climbed through bracken and gorse, and Astrid walked it with the chart against his ribs and Wren's hand in his, and he did not say aloud what was sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone:

Three fragments waited in the world of the living. One waited in the dark beneath the mountain, in a vault belonging to things that wore crowns of thorn. And the oldest debt of all—the one that had paid for everything—was the one he could not remember making.

The Brass Owl's shutters would be opening soon. Somewhere behind them, in the fog, gray cloaks were already asking their questions.

He tightened his grip on the child's hand and walked faster.
