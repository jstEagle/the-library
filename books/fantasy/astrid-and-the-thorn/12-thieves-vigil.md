## 12. Thieves' Vigil

### Dagny

The stew was the same recipe Dagny had cooked for forty years in Hollowbridge's back rooms, and it had never once tasted like forgiveness until tonight.

She ladled it thick—barley, black root, a knuckle of salt pork she'd been saving for a bad day—and set the bowl in front of Wren, who regarded it the way pilgrims regard relics. The girl had learned words now. *Bread. Fire. Astrid.* She said them carefully, like coins she wasn't sure were legal tender. Tonight Dagny taught her another.

"Stew," Dagny said, tapping the bowl's rim.

"*Stew,*" Wren echoed, and something in her face opened like a window in a walled house, and Dagny had to turn away and pretend to mind the fire because forty years of thieving had not prepared her for that.

Astrid sat across the room, cleaning a knife he did not need to clean. He'd been doing it since sundown. The blade gleamed like a chapel floor. His arm was bandaged where the river had tried to take him, and he held it stiffly, the way men do when they've decided pain is a private matter.

"You're going to bleed through that by morning," Dagny said.

"It's fine."

"It's seeping."

"Dagny."

"Don't *Dagny* me, boy. I changed your bandages when you were fourteen and stupid, I'll change them now that you're twenty-six and stupider." She crossed the room, took his arm without asking—the only way to treat Astrid Joubert, who flinched from gentleness like other men flinched from blades—and unwound the linen. The wound beneath was angry but clean. Maren's work. Maren's salve, smelling of pine and old women's knowledge.

He watched her re-wrap it with the expression of a man being handed something he didn't know how to hold.

"Why are you doing this?" he asked quietly. "All of you. Vess is drawing maps in your back room. Maren came down off her mountain. You're feeding a child who eats like a sparrow and talks like a landslide. None of you owe me anything."

Dagny tied off the bandage harder than she needed to.

"No," she agreed. "We don't."

* * *

Later, when Wren slept curled against the stove like a cat who had finally decided the house was hers, and Vess scratched away at her charts, and Astrid stood at the window watching the fog eat the lamplight of Hollowbridge, Dagny took out the thing she had kept.

It was small. A tin whistle, dented, cheap, the kind sold at fairs to children who would lose them by autumn. It had lived in her lockbox for twelve years between a false bottom and a dead man's signet ring, and she had never once played it.

She set it on the table beside Astrid's elbow.

He looked at it. She watched him look at it, and watched the moment it became familiar—not remembered, never that, his own past was a locked house even to him—but *familiar*, the way a scar itches in cold weather.

"What's this?"

"You tell me."

"I don't—" He picked it up. Turned it over. His thumb found the dent near the mouthpiece, and Dagny saw his breath change. "There was a boy," he said slowly. "In the winter. In the market square. He was crying because—because someone had taken it, or broken it, or—" He frowned, pressing at the memory the way you press a bruise to learn its shape. "I don't remember. Why don't I remember?"

"Because you took it out of yourself and sold it, most likely, along with everything else that ever made you soft." Dagny sat down across from him. "But I remember, boy. I was there. A boy of eight, sobbing in the snow because cutpurses had taken his whistle and his mother's coin purse both, and a skinny thief of fourteen chased them down an alley twice his sense and got the whistle back and gave the boy every coin in the purse besides. Then he walked past my stall white as whey with a split lip and said, *don't tell anyone.*"

"That doesn't sound like me."

"It sounds exactly like you. That's the whole trouble with you." She pushed the whistle toward him. "You've spent twelve years building a man out of rules and locked doors, and underneath it there's still the fool who ran down an alley for a stranger's brat. Keep it. Play it badly. Give it back to the boy someday if you find him—he'll be twenty now, probably a fishmonger, probably terrible at it."

Astrid held the whistle like it might go off.

"Dagny. Why did you really come? To the mountain, to Maren's, here. You hate leaving Hollowbridge. You told me once you'd only leave for your own funeral, and only then to make sure the mourners weren't lying."

She looked at the sleeping girl. At the thorn pendant hung by the hearth, humming its low note like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing.

"Forty years ago," Dagny said, "I was caught lifting a purse in Thornfell's shadow, and a gray-cloaked thing offered me a bargain, and I was clever enough to say no and lucky enough to run fast enough. Most aren't. Most say yes, because the things in the cloaks know just what to whisper." Her voice went flat and old. "I've spent forty years pretending that didn't happen. And then a child walks out of that same mountain wearing a soul that isn't hers, and I look at her, and I think: *there but for a fast pair of legs.*"

"So this is penance."

"This is *company*, you idiot." She stood, joints cracking like green wood. "Nobody walks into that mountain alone. Not because they can't. Because we won't let them." She paused at the door to her room. "And Astrid—you gave me more than a whistle story once. When my shop burned, when I had nothing, someone left a purse of crowns on my sill and I never learned whose. I always suspected. I never asked. That's the kind of family we are, you and I. We give in the dark and pretend it was the wind."

She left him sitting with the whistle, turning it over and over, listening for a memory that wasn't there anymore.

### Maren

Maren could not hear the storm, but she felt it in her teeth, in the floorboards, in the way the candle flames leaned all one direction like grass under wind. Storms spoke a language she understood better than any spoken one. Everything true was felt first in the body.

The cabin above Hollowbridge was Dagny's sister's, empty ten years, and it smelled of dust and camphor and, faintly, of the past. Maren had claimed the table by the second night and covered it with her things: mortar and pestle, jars of willow bark and feverfew, the long needles she used for stitching flesh, and the bowl of clean water in which she read what ears could not.

Wren sat across from her, watching her hands.

The girl had taken to Maren quickly, Maren thought, because Maren's world was already shaped like hers—a world of touch and pressure and meaning carried in skin. When Maren signed, Wren watched. When Maren pressed her palm flat to the table to feel the storm, Wren pressed hers beside it, and their hands nearly touched, and neither pulled away.

Tonight Maren was making the salve for Astrid's arm. Yarrow, comfrey, pine pitch, beeswax. Wren handed her jars before she reached for them, having learned the order of things after two days, and Maren allowed herself the luxury of surprise. Children who had been sold did not usually learn generosity so fast. Sold children learned hoarding, hiding, the arithmetic of scarcity. This girl handed things over like the world was a place where handing was safe.

*Mine did not,* Maren thought, and her hands stilled on the pestle.

Her daughter had been thirty years gone now. Taken by fever, not by courts—there was no one to rage against, which was its own particular hell—and Maren had spent those thirty years on her mountain learning every root that delayed death and none that reversed it. She had met Astrid eleven years ago, when he came stumbling up her path half-dead with a wound he refused to explain, carrying a satchel of stolen memories he offered as payment, and she had refused the payment and healed him anyway, and he had been offended for a full year.

Why had she come down for him now?

She knew why. She had known since the night she touched the pendant and seen the Thorn Court's claim coiled around the girl like a vine around a sapling. But there was another reason, one she had not said aloud, and she set down the pestle and took Wren's hand and turned it palm-up, asking permission with a raised eyebrow. Wren nodded.

Maren traced, slowly, in the girl's palm, the signs: *I had a daughter.*

Wren's brow furrowed. She didn't have all the signs yet. So Maren took the girl's hand and laid it against her own throat, where the pulse beat, and then against her own cheek, letting her feel the wetness there, and Wren's face did the opening thing again—that window in the walled house—and the girl reached up and, very solemnly, wiped the tear away with her sleeve, the way you'd wipe dirt from a stone you meant to keep.

Then Wren did something unexpected. She pressed Maren's hand flat against her own chest, over her heart, and held it there, and through the contact Maren felt what the girl could not say: a vast slow green patience, roots deep in dark stone, and threaded through it, unmistakable, a small fierce tenderness aimed at her, at Dagny, at Vess, at the wounded man asleep in the next room.

*Family,* the feeling said, in the wordless way growing things say it. *This. Family.*

Maren laughed—a rusty sound, rarely used—and signed *yes* against the girl's palm, over and over. Yes. Yes. Yes.

Later, applying the fresh salve to Astrid's arm while he sat too still and let her, she caught his eye and answered the question he'd been carrying since the mountain.

"You're wondering why I'm here," she said. "You're wondering what you paid me, or what you're going to owe."

"Maren—"

"When you came to me eleven years ago, bleeding, you had a stolen memory in your pocket you couldn't sell. A woman's memory. Her last afternoon with her son before the fever took him. You'd lifted it from a dying man in Hollowbridge who wanted to forget, and no fence would buy it because grief has no market price." She smoothed the bandage flat. "You brought it to me instead. You said, *someone should keep this who'll honor it.* I have kept it eleven years. I take it out on hard nights. It is the closest thing I have to hearing his voice." She met his eyes. "You thought you were a thief who sold everything. You have been a keeper all along. You just kept forgetting to count what you'd kept."

Astrid looked at his bandaged arm as though it belonged to someone else.

"The court below keeps souls in vaults," Maren said, packing her jars. "So do you. The difference, Astrid, is what you do with the key."

### Vess

Vess worked best at night, when Hollowbridge's noise sank to a murmur and the maps could breathe.

She had covered Dagny's sister's table in paper now—the descent route, three attempts, each failure annotated in her cramped hand. The Gate of Thorns opened only to a debtor; that much the oldest smuggler songs agreed on. What lay beyond, no song survived to tell. She drew the mountain's known tunnels in brown ink and the unknown in blank space, and the blank space was most of the page, and she hated it the way she hated all unfinished sentences.

Astrid appeared at midnight with two cups of Dagny's terrible tea and set one at her elbow without comment. They had worked like this before, years ago—two insomniacs bent over contraband and cartography—and the ease of it frightened her more than the mountain did.

"Your third route is wrong," he said.

"My third route is *bold*."

"Bold and wrong. The flooded gallery on level four—if the water still runs where it ran when I was nineteen, you'd drown before the second junction."

"If you were nineteen. You remember that?"

His jaw tightened. "No," he admitted. "But my hands do. Watch." He took her pencil and, without looking at any reference, sketched the gallery from memory that wasn't memory—a smooth confident line, a side chamber, a drainage channel, marks for timber props rotted at specific intervals. He stared at his own handiwork like a man reading a letter from a stranger. "I've been down there. Before. I don't remember going."

"You've been everywhere down there," Vess said quietly. "That's what I've been trying to tell you all night. Look." She turned her master sheet toward him. Overlaid on the mountain's tunnels, in red ink, was a line she'd plotted from his unconscious sketches over the past two days—a wandering thread that avoided every collapse, every guard post, every dead end, threading the mountain like a needle. "Every map you've drawn from instinct matches passages no living smuggler has charted. Astrid. You haven't just been in that mountain. You've been *home* in it."

He was quiet a long time. The candle flames leaned in the draft.

"There's something else," Vess said, and pulled a slim ledger from her coat. "When you asked me about the sale contract's seal, I told you the signature style matched your hand. I've done more digging since. The broker who witnessed the original bargain—a creature called Sallowfen, keeps accounts in a den under the tannery row—I bought five minutes with his ledger at considerable cost." She tapped a line of entries. "Twelve years of sales, Astrid. Not Wren's memories. *Yours.* Sold in fragments, buyer after buyer, always through intermediaries, always at prices just high enough to fund your next disappearance. You financed your own erasure. Piece by piece. On purpose."

"Why would anyone—"

"And here's the entry that matters." She turned the ledger fully toward him and watched his face as he read. "The final fragment. The largest. Your name, your origin, the whole of what you were before. It was never sold to any collector." She let the silence sit. "It was purchased by the Thorn Court itself. Twelve years ago. The same season the girl was sold to them."

Astrid sat down slowly, the way men sit when the ground has moved.

"They've been holding it," he said. "All this time."

"Or wearing it," Vess said. "The court deals in souls, and a name is the smallest soul there is. Either way—it's below. Behind the gate. Along with her." She gathered her papers, aligning their edges, a ritual for steadiness. "One more thing, and then I'll let you not sleep in peace. You asked me once, years ago, why I trust you—Vess trusts no one, you said, it's famous. And I never answered." She looked at him across the candlelight. "When I was new to Hollowbridge, seventeen and stupid, I sold information to a man who turned out to be a court agent, and the gray cloaks came for me, and everyone I'd ever dealt with suddenly couldn't remember my name. Except one thief who walked into their safehouse, stole the evidence of my debt right out of their strongroom, and left a single copper coin on the empty shelf—payment, he said later, so the theft wouldn't be a lie. I never forgot that. Even if you did."

Astrid stared at her. "I don't remember that either."

"No," Vess said softly. "But the coin was yours. I've kept it twelve years. It's sewn into this coat, over my heart, and tomorrow it goes back down that mountain with us." She rolled the maps and bound them with cord. "Sleep, Astrid. Tomorrow we buy back what you sold. All of it."

* * *

He left for the back room. Vess remained at the table, and in the quiet she heard—through the wall, thin and wavering and badly played—a dented tin whistle attempting a lullaby, stopping, starting again, determined as water against stone.

From the hearth, the thorn pendant hummed one note lower than it had the night before.

Vess noticed. Vess wrote it down. The mountain, she suspected, had begun to listen back.
