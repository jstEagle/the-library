## 13. The Second Fragment

Hollowbridge at night was a city of held breath.

Astrid had always thought of it that way—the smuggler capital built on a gorge so deep the river below was only a rumor of sound, its houses stacked like crooked teeth on either rim, connected by bridges that swayed when the wind came up from the dark. Lanterns hung everywhere and lit nothing fully. Every shadow here had a tenant. Every door had two prices: one for what it sold, one for pretending it hadn't.

He moved through the lower warrens with Wren's absence pressing against his ribs like a second heartbeat. She was safe—Dagny's word on that, and Dagny's word was the closest thing Hollowbridge had to scripture—but *safe* was a relative term in a city where the Thorn Court's gray cloaks walked openly now, their presence no longer rumor but weather. He'd seen two of them at the fish market that morning, standing motionless among the stalls while vendors made a show of not looking at them. They didn't buy anything. They never bought anything. They simply stood, and the crowd bent around them like water around stones, and Astrid understood that they were waiting for him to make a mistake.

So he wouldn't make one. He would make a theft instead.

The collector's house clung to the gorge's northern wall three bridges out from Vess's territory—a narrow, vertical thing of black timber and salt-stained glass, owned by a man named Corvane who had made his fortune buying what other people could not bear to keep. Grief-memories, mostly. Last words. Final afternoons. The faces of the dead, preserved in amber so the bereaved could visit without bleeding. It was, Astrid had always privately admitted, a kinder trade than his own. Corvane kept what people brought him willingly. Astrid took what people would have given anything to hold forever.

But Corvane also bought from fences, and fences bought from thieves, and somewhere in the last eleven years a piece of Astrid Joubert's own mind had passed through those salt-stained doors and been shelved like a jar of pickled grief.

Vess's map lay folded against his chest, drawn in her precise, spider-fine hand: *Corvane's vault, third cellar, iron cabinet, drawer marked with a white thorn.* She'd underlined the thorn twice and written beneath it, in letters small enough to require trust: *He knows what he has. He'll have warded it. Don't be clever, Astrid. Be quiet.*

He intended to be both.

* * *

The house had seven ways in, by Astrid's count, and six of them were traps.

He'd spent the afternoon watching it from the window of a tea house across the lane, a cup of something bitter going cold in front of him while he memorized the rhythms of the household. Corvane himself was a widower of sixty-odd years who kept late hours and earlier ones, sleeping perhaps four hours a night in a room on the fourth floor. Two servants, a mother and daughter, who moved through the house like water moving through pipes—predictable, essential, unnoticed. A dog, old and half-blind, that slept in the kitchen and would smell him before it saw him unless he gave it something better to smell. He'd bought a strip of dried liver from a butcher two streets over for exactly this purpose.

The seventh way in was the chimney of the cold hearth in the third cellar, which vented through a flue so narrow that a grown man should not have fit—and did not, quite, fit comfortably, which was why Astrid waited until full dark, stripped off his coat, oiled himself down with tallow until he smelled like a candle rather than a man, and went down into the dark feet-first, arms above his head, breathing in shallow sips.

The descent took the better part of an hour. Brick scraped his shoulders raw. Twice the flue narrowed and he had to exhale everything, go limp, and let gravity drag him through a gap that felt like being born in reverse. Soot filled his ears, his eyes, the creases of his knuckles. Somewhere above him the house settled and groaned, and once—only once—he heard footsteps cross a floor directly overhead, unhurried, and stop, and move on.

Then the flue opened, and he dropped the last six feet into a hearth that smelled of forty years of dead fires, and crouched in the ashes of the third cellar listening to his own heart argue with the silence.

The vault room was exactly as Vess had described it, because Vess was never wrong about rooms. Low ceiling. Salt-fogged windows too narrow for a cat. Shelves upon shelves of memory-jars, each one glowing faintly with the color of what it held—grief ran blue-white, love ran gold, terror ran a sickly green—and along the far wall, the iron cabinet with its rows of small drawers, each labeled in Corvane's careful hand.

He found the white thorn mark on the fourth row, third drawer from the left.

And stopped.

Because the ward was not what he'd expected. He'd braced for blood-locks, for sigil-work, for any of the dozen crude protections a collector might buy from a hedge-witch. What he found instead was a thread of magic so fine he almost missed it—a single filament of power stretched across the drawer's seam, thin as spider silk, humming with a frequency he knew.

Knew *intimately*. The way you know your own voice played back to you.

It was soul-bargain craft. The same craft Maren had recoiled from when she touched Wren's pendant. The same signature that lived in the seal of Wren's sale contract, the one Astrid had copied without thinking, his hand moving through shapes his mind had never learned.

Someone had warded this drawer using the same magic that had sold a child to the court of thorns.

His hands were steady. That was the horror of it—they stayed steady, absolutely steady, as if steadiness itself had been carved into him alongside whatever else he'd cut away. He reached out and let his fingertips rest on the filament, and closed his eyes, and did the thing he had done ten thousand times on ten thousand roads: he reached into the magic and *took*.

The ward unraveled like a song losing its note. No alarm. No flare. Just a soft exhalation of power that tasted, on his tongue, of winter and pine sap and a name he couldn't pronounce.

The drawer slid open without a sound.

Inside, nested in wool, sat a single memory-jar. It was smaller than most—memories compressed by age or by the weight of what they carried—and it glowed a color Astrid had never seen in Corvane's inventory, in anyone's inventory. Not gold, not blue, not green. A deep, warm amber shot through with veins of silver, like honey holding lightning.

His own handwriting was on the label. He recognized it the way you recognize a scar: *For the man I will not remember being. Do not open until you are brave enough.*

Dagny's key, warm in his pocket since the night she'd pressed it into his hand, seemed to pulse against his thigh.

"Right," Astrid whispered to the empty cellar, and his voice came out sanded rough. "Brave enough."

He lifted the jar. And because he was still, despite everything, a professional, he left in its place a forgery—one of his own stock memories, a merchant's forgettable afternoon of haggling over silk, decanted into a matching jar and labeled in a fair imitation of Corvane's hand. If the collector ever noticed the swap, he would assume a rival fence. He would never, ever guess the truth: that the thief had come to steal back a piece of himself.

The climb up the flue was worse than the climb down. Everything is, on the way back.

* * *

He didn't open it in the cellar. He didn't open it on the bridges, or in the alley behind Dagny's lodging house, or in any of the hundred half-dark places where a man might reasonably crack a stolen memory and pour it into his skull. He carried it up four flights of stairs to the rented room Dagny had secured for them—two beds, one window, a lock she'd called "decorative"—and he sat on the floor between the beds with his back against the wall and the jar in his lap, and for a long time he simply looked at it.

Wren was asleep in the far bed, curled small beneath a quilt Dagny had bullied out of a neighbor, her breathing slow and even, the thorn pendant rising and falling on her chest. In sleep she looked younger than her years and older than her body, both at once, the way the drowned must look drifting under clear water. Three days ago she had spoken her first full sentence to him—not in the root-language, but in trade-tongue, cracked and rusted from disuse: *"You came back."* As if coming back were a thing that required proving. As if everyone else who had ever been responsible for her had demonstrated otherwise.

He thought about the fragment he'd already recovered. Snow. A crying infant. His own voice saying *take her instead.* He had stopped there, that night in Dagny's kitchen, hands shaking too hard to go deeper, guilt arriving ahead of understanding like cavalry ahead of an army.

Now the army was here. Now he had to look at what it had come to tell him.

"You can put it down," said a voice from the doorway, and Astrid nearly went through the ceiling.

Dagny stood leaning against the frame, gray-haired and gap-toothed and wrapped in a shawl the color of dishwater, holding two cups of something steaming. She was seventy-one years old and had once picked the lock on a duke's pleasure barge mid-river during his own wedding feast, and she still moved quieter than anyone Astrid had ever known, including himself.

"How long—"

"Since you started staring at it like it was going to bite." She crossed the room, set one cup on the floor beside him, lowered herself onto the edge of the other bed with the elaborate care of old joints, and nodded at Wren. "She sleeps like the dead. Go on."

"Dagny."

"I know what you're going to say." She said it around a mouthful of her own drink. "You're going to say you should do this alone. You're going to say if it's bad, you don't want witnesses. You're going to say—" she pitched her voice down into a fair imitation of his flat murmur— "*it's my past, Dagny. My mess.*" She snorted. "Boy, I've watched you carry other people's memories out of their heads for fifteen years like you were emptying chamber pots. Polite. Efficient. Never a drop spilled on yourself. Tonight somebody finally hands you your own filth and you want privacy." Her eyes, sharp and brown and utterly unsentimental, held his. "No. Whatever's in that jar, you'll come out the other side of it needing to know someone saw you. That's not weakness. That's just true."

Astrid looked at her. Then at Wren, asleep, trusting, wearing a pendant that hummed with the craft of whoever had sold her.

Then he pulled the stopper.

* * *

Memories taken by force come in like weather. Memories freely given come in like guests. But a memory returning to its original owner—

That was different. That was a door in his own house opening onto a room he'd forgotten he owned, and every object inside exactly where he'd left it, and dust on nothing, because the room had been waiting.

Snow. He was standing in snow. Not falling snow—*falling* snow, the fat slow flakes of high Thornfell in the weeks before solstice, when the passes closed and even smugglers went home. He was younger. He could feel it in the body he wore: less scar, more hunger, hands that hadn't yet learned all the tricks they would learn. Twenty-three, maybe twenty-four. Standing in a circle of pines above a frozen lake, and the snow was landing on his shoulders and not melting, and in his arms—

In his arms was a newborn.

Tiny. Red-faced with fury at the cold, wrapped in a blanket that was far too thin, her fists punching at the air as though she could fight the whole sky. Black hair plastered wet to her skull. And when she opened her eyes—newborn eyes, dark, unfocused, furious—they fixed on his face with an intensity no newborn should have possessed, and Astrid felt, flooding up through the recovered memory, the exact shape of what he had felt in that moment:

Not affection. Not yet. Something older and more frightening. *Recognition.* The feeling of meeting someone you had already agreed to die for, before you'd ever seen their face.

There were others in the clearing. He turned—younger-Astrid turned—and saw them. Three figures in gray cloaks, faces shadowed beneath their hoods, standing in the snow without disturbing it. And between them, forward, a fourth figure: tall, robed in a weave of living briar that shifted and bloomed and died and bloomed again, its face a smooth oval of pale wood grain, featureless except where two knots of darker wood served as eyes.

An envoy of the Thorn Court. He knew that now the way he knew the ward-craft—as knowledge that predated his knowing.

And the envoy spoke, and its voice was the sound of branches creaking in wind, and it named its price.

The memory gave him the words whole, uncorrupted, in the envoy's own voice and in his own understanding: *"The child's soul is owed. The debt was signed in root and ink. But the Court is not without mercy, little thief. We will take the vessel and release the soul to wander—or we will take a bargain of equal weight. A name. A future. All the years of one man, freely given, sealed and shelved. Choose."*

And younger-Astrid—cold, terrified, holding a baby that was not his, that had never been his, that had been handed to him in the night by a dying woman whose face the memory mercifully blurred beyond recovery—looked down at the furious scrap of life in his arms and heard himself say:

"Take her instead."

*Take her instead.* His own voice. Offering the court not the child's soul but her *body*—the failing borrowed vessel, the mortal flesh that could not contain what she was—so that her soul might remain free, unclaimed, able to grow, to live, to someday walk out from under the mountain on her own feet.

*"Take her instead,"* he had said, *"and take me. My name. My future. Whatever she'll need to stay free—I'll pay it. All of it. Now, and later, however long it takes."*

And the envoy's wooden face had done something that might have been a smile, and it had said: *"Spoken like a debtor. The Court accepts. We will wear your years kindly, Astrid Joubert."*

And he had felt them lift his name out of him like a splinter drawn whole from the flesh, and he had felt the bargain seal itself in root and ink, and he had looked down at the baby one last time and whispered—already feeling the words slide away from him even as he spoke them—

"Forget me. Please. Forget me, and live."

* * *

The jar was empty in his hands. The room came back: lamplight, rain against the window, Dagny's face very close to his, her rough hand gripping his shoulder hard enough to bruise.

He was crying. He noted it distantly, the way he noted weather in another country. His face was wet and his throat was wrecked and he could not remember starting.

"Astrid." Dagny's voice, gentler than he'd ever heard it. "Talk to me. What did you see?"

"I didn't sell her." The words came out broken, half of them drowned. "Dagny, I didn't—I *saved* her. Or I tried. Her body was dying, the court was going to take her soul, and I—I traded myself. My name, my whole future, everything I was going to be. I paid it in installments. Eleven years of installments." He laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "Every piece of myself I've sold off, all these years, funding my invisibility—I wasn't hiding. I was *paying*. I just forgot what for."

"And the girl?" Dagny's eyes flicked to Wren. "Why does the court still want her, if you paid?"

"Because a bargain that size doesn't finish in one lifetime." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, uselessly; more tears replaced the first. "I paid my name. I paid my future. But the contract isn't closed until the debt is *discharged*, and the court decides what discharge means. They've been wearing my discarded self like a mask, Dagny—an avatar walking around in the shape of the man I erased. And Wren—" his voice caught— "Wren grew up. The body I gave them failed, like bodies do, and her soul came looking for shelter, and it found *me*. It found the one person in the world who'd already ruined himself trying to keep her free."

Silence. Rain. Wren's slow breathing.

Then, softly, from the far bed: "You sang."

Astrid froze.

Wren was awake. He didn't know how long she'd been awake—how much she'd heard. She sat up in the nest of quilts, the pendant glowing faintly at her throat, and her dark eyes were enormous and wet and fixed on him with that same newborn intensity he had just remembered, unchanged across eleven years, across erasure, across everything.

"In the snow," she said. Her trade-tongue was still cracked, still rusted, but she pushed through it. "When I was new. Before the court took my body. You held me and you were afraid and you *sang*, badly, to keep me quiet. I remember it from inside. It's the oldest thing I have." Her chin trembled. "I've been looking for the song my whole life. I didn't know it was a person."

Astrid opened his mouth and found nothing in it.

Dagny rose, silent, and slipped out of the room with the wisdom of old women and old thieves, and then it was just the two of them, the debtor and the debt, the thief and the thing he'd stolen himself back to save.

"The court has the last fragment," Astrid said at last, hoarse. "The final payment. Whatever's left of me is in their vault, under the mountain. And as long as that contract stands open, they'll keep sending the gray cloaks. They'll keep hunting you." He looked at his hands—steady, always steady, traitorously steady. "So I'm going to go down there and close it. However they demand. Whatever it costs."

Wren slid off the bed. She crossed the floor barefoot, the way she'd arrived at his cabin in the storm, unafraid, and she took his tear-wet face in her two small hands the way no one had touched him in fifteen years, and she said:

"Then I'm coming. You paid for me once." Her grip tightened. "Nobody pays twice alone."

Outside, far below in the gorge, the river murmured its one endless secret, and somewhere in the dark of the mountain, something ancient turned a page in a ledger of names, and waited.
