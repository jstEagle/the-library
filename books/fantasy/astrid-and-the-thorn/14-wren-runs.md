## 14. Wren Runs

The memories come like water through a cracked wall.

Wren is sitting by the fire in Dagny's house, holding a bowl of barley stew she has learned to love—the way it steams, the way the grains swell soft against her tongue, the way warmth pools in her belly like a stone left in the sun—when the first fragment breaks loose.

It arrives not as a picture but as a smell: crushed yarrow and cold iron, the scent of a room she has never entered in this body. Then a sound, thin as a reed: someone singing. A lullaby in no language she knows, and yet her bones know it. Her ribs hum with it the way the thorn pendant hums when Astrid touches it.

The bowl slips.

Dagny catches it before it hits the floor—old thief's reflexes, faster than her years—and says something sharp and kind in her gravel voice. Wren does not hear it. She is falling again, the way she fell at the beginning, the way she fell before the beginning, down through dark and root and stone, and hands caught her—

*Hands caught her and then let go.*

That is the part that always comes back wrong. The hands that caught her are the same hands that let go, and she has never understood how both can be true, and now the fragments are surfacing faster, more of them every day, pushing up through the soil of her mind like pale shoots after rain, and each one carries a weight she has no words for because the words were sold too.

She remembers—no, *she almost remembers*—a door. A door of woven thorn, breathing. A voice like frost forming on glass, saying words that were numbers, prices, terms. She remembers being small and warm and held, and then being small and cold and *counted*.

She remembers snow.

Snow on a woman's face. Snow melting on eyelashes that might have been hers once, in the body she was born to, the body that failed or was taken or was traded away—she cannot tell which, and the not-knowing is a thorn working deeper under her skin with every hour.

"Wren." Dagny's hand on her shoulder, rough and steady. "Girl. Look at me."

Wren looks. Dagny's face is seamed like old bark, her eyes brown and shrewd and unafraid, and for a moment the fragments quiet. This is what people are, when they are not memories. This is what the world is, when it is only stew and firelight and an old woman's grip.

But then the pendant at her throat pulses—once, twice, like a second heart—and beneath it she feels the pull. The court's claim, tightening. Maren had said it plainly, pressing her palm flat against Wren's sternum until the vibration of her voice carried up through bone: *the mountain is reeling you in, little fish. Line and hook.*

And tonight the line pulls hard.

It starts in her feet. An ache, deep in the soles, as if the ground itself has grown teeth and is gently, patiently biting. The direction of it is unmistakable: north and down, toward Thornfell's black spine, toward the gate she dreamed of last night—a gate of living thorn, opening like a mouth.

She stands. Her legs decide before she does.

"No," Dagny says, reading her the way she reads everything, in the language of bodies. "No, you sit right back—"

But Wren is already moving, and the terrible thing, the thing she will remember with shame later, is that some part of her *wants* to go. The pull is not only a hook. It is also a homing. Somewhere under the mountain is everything that was taken from her—her name, her years, the body that fit—and the court whispers to that hunger in a voice older than kingdoms: *come home, come home, we kept it all for you, nothing here above ground will ever fit you again.*

She believes it, just for a moment. That is enough.

The door is heavy but she is desperate, and desperation is its own strength. Cold air floods in. Snow swirls in the lamplight. Behind her Dagny shouts, and somewhere in the house Astrid's footsteps start, quick on the floorboards, calling her name in his careful mouth-shape of it—*Wren*, two syllables he taught himself from watching her lips.

She runs into the storm.

* * *

The fells take her the way the sea takes a swimmer: gradually, then completely.

Up here there are no roads, only sheep-tracks and the memory of paths, all of it smothered under snow that comes sideways on a wind with knives in it. Wren runs anyway. Her bare feet—she never could learn shoes; they feel like coffins for toes—find purchase on stone and frozen turf, and the cold should hurt but instead it sings to her, because cold is what the mountain speaks, and she is kin to the mountain whether she wants to be or not.

Trees fall away behind her. The world opens into a high white moorland where the wind strips everything down to essentials: rock, snow, sky, breath. She can see for miles and see nothing. The clouds have swallowed the moon. Her world shrinks to the circle of her own struggling light.

And still the fragments rise.

Running shakes them loose like fruit from a branch. She skids around a cairn and suddenly she is remembering a garden—not this harsh fell-country but a walled garden somewhere green, and a woman kneeling in it with dirt on her hands, showing her how to press a seed into the earth. The woman's face is a smear of light, sold away, but her hands remain: strong, patient, humming the lullaby. *Mama,* Wren thinks, in the word Astrid gave her, the first word she ever stole back for herself, and the thought hurts so much she stumbles.

She goes down hard on the frozen ground. Pain blooms in her palms. She kneels there, gasping, snow gathering on her shoulders, and feels the pull in her feet strengthen—as if her falling pleased it, as if the mountain tasted her weakness through the soles of her feet and pulled the line home.

Get up, she tells herself. Get up get up get up.

She gets up.

She doesn't know why she's running anymore. Away from the pull, or toward it? The distinction has worn thin. All she knows is that staying still means drowning, and motion means something, even if it's only the something of choosing.

The wind changes.

She feels it before she hears it—a new note in the wind's chord, a wrongness, like a string detuning mid-song. The cold takes on texture. Direction. It flows *around* things now, around shapes moving through the snow behind her, and when she turns she sees them: three gray cloaks, pale as fog given ambition, gliding across the moor without disturbing the snow.

They do not walk. They *arrive*, over and over, each arrival closer than the last.

The pendant at her throat flares hot. Wren's whole body clenches around the heat of it, and in the flare she feels the court's voice again, clear now, intimate, almost tender:

*You ran from the ones who keep you safe, little soul. Come. We will hold you where nothing can be stolen again.*

And God help her, some starving corner of her leans toward that promise like a plant toward a window.

No. *No.* She thinks of stew. Of firelight. Of Dagny's bark-seamed face and Maren's humming hands and Astrid—Astrid who touches her pendant like it might bite him and touches *her* like she might break, Astrid who sings badly to himself when he thinks no one listens, Astrid whose eyes go strange and far away whenever she asks about before, but who came for her in the mine, who bled at the ford, who looks at her sometimes with an expression she has no word for except the shape of it, which is the shape of grief wearing the mask of duty.

She turns and runs south, away from the mountain, away from the promise, and the gray cloaks flow after her like spilled ink finding its way downhill.

They are faster. Of course they are faster. The snow parts for them and fights her. Within a hundred heartbeats they have her flanked, three pale shapes converging, and the nearest one raises a hand and the wind itself bends to its will, shoving her, spinning her, driving her to her knees in a drift.

The cloak looms over her. Beneath the hood there is no face—only a smooth gray blankness, like a stone worn featureless by centuries of weather, and yet she feels it looking at her, appraising her, the way a merchant appraises goods.

A gray hand extends toward the pendant.

Wren screams. No words—words were sold—but sound, raw and rising, and the mountain answers.

The snowfield shudders. Beneath her knees she feels it: a great slow pulse rolling up out of the deep rock, the heartbeat of Thornfell itself, and where it passes the ice groans and splits and *rises*. Walls of white leap up around her, higher than a man, higher than two men, curling like waves deciding to stand still. The gray cloak recoils, its hand snatched back as a spike of ice lashes out where its fingers had been.

Wren stares at her own hands, buried to the wrist in snow that is now leaning toward her like wheat toward sun.

*I did that,* she thinks. *Or it did. Or I am it.*

She doesn't know which answer frightens her more.

The three cloaks regroup beyond the ice walls, patient as winter. More are coming—she can feel them now, faint cold spots moving through the dark, five, seven, more than she can count, converging on the place where the mountain's power just announced itself like a beacon. She has shown them where she is. She has shown them what she is.

The walls begin, slowly, to melt. Whatever rose in her is ebbing, and the ebb leaves her hollowed and shaking, empty as a scraped bowl.

She runs again, but there is nowhere to run. The fells stretch white and trackless in every direction, the storm thickens, and her legs are failing now, the borrowed body burning through its last reserves. She crests a rise and stumbles down into a shallow dell ringed with stones, and there she stops, because there is no more stopping to be had anywhere else, and turns to face the gray shapes flowing toward her out of the dark.

Three. Five. Eight. They spread across the rim of the dell like frost creeping across a window.

Wren stands in the center of the stones, shaking, and does the only thing left. She plants her bare feet on the frozen ground and reaches down, down, into the deep places Maren showed her, into the root-language, the stone-language, the old tongue that lives in her marrow, and she *pulls*.

The ground answers sluggishly. Too slow. The cloaks descend the slope, unhurried, inevitable, and the pendant burns at her throat and her vision sparkles at the edges and she thinks, absurdly, of the stew going cold on the table, of Dagny catching the bowl before it fell, of hands that catch things—

Hands that catch, and hands that let go, and how can both be true—

And then, through the storm, threading between the wind's knives, she hears it.

Singing.

A man's voice, ragged and off-key and half-swallowed by the gale, singing a lullaby in no language she knows—and her bones know it. Her ribs know it. It is the song from the garden, the song from the snow, the song the smeared-faced woman hummed with dirt on her hands, and it is coming from Astrid's throat, and he cannot possibly know what it is, except that he must, he must, because he is singing it *to her*, across the moor, loud enough to crack his voice, loud enough for the dead to hear.

The gray cloaks falter. Just slightly. Just enough.

He comes over the rise at a dead run, coat flying, hair plastered with snow, and he does not stop or slow or hesitate—he charges straight into the space between Wren and the nearest cloak, arms spread, planting himself like a fence post driven into bedrock, and keeps singing. His eyes find hers over his shoulder. In the storm-dark they are very bright.

*Go,* he mouths. *I'm here. Go.*

She does not go.

Instead she does the bravest and most foolish thing she has ever done in either of her lives: she runs forward and presses herself against his back, fists knotted in his coat, and adds her voice to his. She sings the lullaby in the old tongue, the root-language, the language older than any kingdom, and where their two voices tangle—the human and the ancient, the bought and the borrowed—something happens to the air. It thickens. Warms. The storm slackens around them as if listening.

The gray cloaks stop at the edge of the dell.

They stand in a crescent, eight featureless faces turned toward the singing pair, and Wren feels their attention shift—feels the appraisal change, the way a buyer's expression changes when an item proves more costly than expected. The lead cloak tilts its blank head. And then, in a voice like frost forming on glass, it speaks—not to her, but to Astrid, in words that make the pendant scream with cold against her skin:

*"Debtor. You were warned. The collateral walks to us on its own feet now, or we take it from your cooling hands. You have until the moon clears the cloud. Bring her to the gate—or bring nothing, and we will come for what remains of you."*

The cloaks do not leave. They simply cease, the way mist ceases when the sun finds it—one moment arrayed on the slope, the next absorbed into the gray air, gone.

The wind resumes its ordinary violence. The storm closes over the space where they stood.

Astrid stops singing. His shoulders are heaving, and Wren realizes he is crying—silently, hopelessly, the tears freezing in the lines of his face—and that he is gripping her wrists where they clutch his coat hard enough to bruise, as if letting go would mean losing her to the dark forever.

Slowly, he turns. He looks down at her. His mouth works, shaping words, abandoning them, shaping others. Finally he lifts one trembling hand and touches the pendant at her throat—gently, the way you touch a wound that is also a treasure—and then presses that same hand flat against his own chest, over his heart.

*Yours,* the gesture says. *Mine. Ours.*

Wren's answer is to bury her face in his coat and sob—all the fear, all the fragments, all the sold and stolen years, pouring out of her in the old tongue and the new silence alike. He holds her. Above them the clouds begin, grudgingly, to tear, and a sliver of moon shows through, silvering the dell, silvering the stones, silvering the tracks of the gray cloaks already filling with snow.

Somewhere down the southern slope, lantern-light bobs through the storm—Dagny, Maren, Vess, coming for them both, shouting names into the wind.

Astrid cups Wren's face in his cold hands and makes her look at him. His eyes are red-rimmed and fierce and terrified and certain, all at once, and when he speaks his voice is hoarse from singing and steadier than it has any right to be.

"We don't run anymore," he says, and though she barely knows his words, she knows *this* one, has known it since the mine, since the ford: *we*. Us. Together.

Then he looks north, toward the black spine of Thornfell, where the gate of thorns waits behind miles of ice and stone, and his jaw sets in a way she has learned to read as well as any language.

"Tomorrow," he says, "we knock."

Above the mountain, the moon slides free of the clouds—pale, full, and counting down.
