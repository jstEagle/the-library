## 15. The Gate of Thorns

The mountain stopped pretending to be a mountain on the third day.

Astrid had known it was coming—he had walked this ridge once before, though the memory of it lived behind a locked door he could not yet open—but knowing did nothing to soften the wrongness of it. The peaks ahead should have been stone and snow like all the others. Instead they were thorned. Black spines rose from the rock in ranks, some thin as needles, some thick as watchtowers, all of them curved inward toward a single point below the summit where the air itself seemed to gather and wait.

*The Gate of Thorns,* Maren had written to him once, years ago, in her quick signing hand: *the door that opens only for those who owe it.*

He owed it. He had always suspected as much. Now he was walking toward the proof with a child's hand in his and three unlikely allies at his back, and the proof was growing out of the earth like a wound that had decided to become architecture.

Wren felt it before any of them. She stopped in the middle of the trail, barefoot on frost-bitten scree—she refused boots, had refused them since Hollowbridge, and Dagny had finally stopped arguing—and tilted her head the way she did when she was listening to something underground.

"It's singing," Astrid said. Not a question. He had learned to read her silences the way other men read weather.

She nodded slowly. Then she made the gesture she used for *hungry*—one hand pressed flat against her sternum, fingers spreading like roots—and after a moment's thought, added the gesture for *me.*

His stomach dropped. "It isn't hungry for you," he said, and hated how unsteady his voice came out. "Not while I'm here."

Wren looked up at him with those eyes the color of deep water over old stone, and she did not believe him, and he could not blame her. The pendant at her throat—the thorn-cast thing that had hummed under his fingers the first night she arrived on his doorstep—was glowing now. Faintly. A green-gold light like sap catching sun, pulsing in time with something far beneath their feet.

Behind them, Vess swore softly. She had not sworn in two days, which Astrid knew meant she had been saving it up.

"That," she said, "is the ugliest door I've ever seen, and I once sold directions to a gate made entirely of teeth."

"Teeth at least have the decency to be honest," Dagny said. The old thief stood with her weight on her good leg, her bad one braced against a boulder, and she was looking at the thorned peak with an expression Astrid recognized from thirty years ago, from the first job she'd ever run him through: the flat, measuring stare of a woman calculating odds she already knew were bad. "Doors like that don't want visitors. They want *debtors.* There's a difference."

"I know," Astrid said.

"Do you?" She turned to look at him then, and her eyes—gray as slate, gray as the cloaks of the things hunting them—held his without mercy. "Because I've watched you walk into every bad room in these mountains for three decades, boy, and you always walk in like a man who's already paid. You don't walk like a debtor. You walk like a man who thinks if he apologizes nicely enough, the world will let him off."

Astrid said nothing. The wind came down off the ridge and carried the smell of the thorns to them—not rot, exactly, but the memory of rot, the way a cellar smells years after something died there. Wren's small hand tightened around his fingers.

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen spine, a black thorn as long as a ship's mast lying across the trail like a felled god. Maren built the fire with her quick deaf hands, efficient and wordless, and Vess spread her maps across a flat stone, weighting the corners with pebbles. Wren sat close to the flames and held her palms out, and where the firelight touched her skin, Astrid could almost see through it—could almost see the shape of something older and greener underneath, waiting.

He sat beside her and did not look away. He owed her that much, at least. Probably more than that. Probably everything, if the fragments were true—if the crying infant in the snow, if his own voice saying *take her instead*, if the newborn in his arms while a court envoy named a price. He had two recovered memories now and a third waiting behind the mountain's teeth, and every hour the picture grew clearer and worse.

"Astrid." Vess's voice, low. She tapped the map. "Come look at this before you sleep, because I won't repeat myself."

He went. The map showed the final approach: a single pass between two thorned ridges, narrowing to a cleft maybe twenty feet wide, and beyond it the gate itself—a seam in the mountain's face, tall as a cathedral door, framed in living thorn.

"They'll come through here." Vess traced the pass with one lacquered fingernail. "The gray cloaks lost us at the ford, but they haven't lost us. They're behind us by half a day, maybe less. They know this is the only way in. They'll be waiting."

"Then we go fast," Astrid said.

"You can't outrun them uphill with a child and a witch who won't leave her herbs behind." Vess glanced at Maren, who was watching them both with the particular stillness of someone reading lips by firelight. "Someone has to hold the pass."

"No," Astrid said immediately.

"I didn't say me."

"You were about to."

Vess smiled, thin and sharp. "I was about to suggest we draw lots like civilized criminals. But since you brought it up—"

"*No.*" He heard his own voice crack against the word. He lowered it. "Nobody stays. Nobody gets left on this mountain for those things to find. We've lost enough—" He stopped. He had been about to say *I've lost enough,* and the truth was he couldn't remember what he'd lost, only that the losing had left hollows in him shaped like people.

Maren touched his arm. When he turned, she signed with slow deliberation, making sure he caught every word:

*You cannot protect everyone by refusing help. That is how you ended up alone in a cabin with locks on your own head.*

He flinched. She saw him flinch, and her hands kept moving, gentle and merciless:

*Dagny has been holding passes since before you were born. Ask her what she wants. Don't decide for her. You've stolen enough choices in your life.*

The old guilt moved through him, familiar as breath. He crossed the camp to where Dagny sat sharpening a knife that had no business being as sharp as it was, and he crouched down beside her, and he asked.

Dagny looked at the pass for a long moment. Then she looked at Wren, asleep against the fallen thorn with the pendant's glow rising and falling against her collarbone like a second heartbeat.

"There was a night," Dagny said, "about twenty-two years ago. A woman came to my door in Hollowbridge with a baby wrapped in a blanket that smelled of snow, and she paid me a month's rent in coin she must have stolen from a bishop, and she asked me to hold something for her. Not the baby. Something else. A key." She turned the knife over in her hands. "I never asked whose key. I never asked what it opened. That woman had eyes like yours, boy, and a way of asking that made you feel like refusing would break something in the world. So I held it. And when you finally came for it last month, I gave it back, and I didn't ask then either."

"Dagny—"

"Let me finish." Her voice was gravel and patience. "I've spent my whole life keeping other people's secrets in safe places because I'm good at it and because nobody ever asks a fence to come along on the adventure. Well." She sheathed the knife. "This time somebody's asking. This time I get to choose what I keep safe. And what I choose is that girl, and you, and that deaf witch who makes a stew that reminds me of my mother, and that insufferable map-seller who cheats at cards. I choose to stand in a pass and make sure the things chasing you arrive too late to matter." She smiled, and it was the smile she'd worn the first day he met her, when she'd caught him picking her pocket and instead of breaking his fingers had taught him how to do it better. "So don't you dare take this from me, Astrid Joubert. It's the first honest work I've been offered in forty years."

He knelt there in the firelight and found that he could not speak. His throat had closed around something enormous.

Wren stirred in her sleep. The pendant pulsed.

* * *

They reached the mouth of the pass at dawn, and the dawn was wrong.

Light came up behind the eastern peaks the way it always did, but the thorned summit drank it. The shadows lay toward them instead of away, long violet fingers pointing down the trail like accusations, and the cold that came out of the pass was not mountain cold. It was older. It smelled of vaults and sealed letters and the inside of coffins built for people who intended to wake up.

Wren rode on Astrid's back, her arms around his neck, her cheek against his shoulder. She had not spoken—she never spoke; her voice had been part of the price, or perhaps it had simply never belonged to the body she wore—but as they entered the pass she began, very quietly, to hum. The lullaby. Their lullaby, the one he'd sung to her on the high fells, the one that had bridged the gap between her language and his. Except she wasn't humming his melody. She was humming the version underneath it, the root-melody, the one the lullaby had apparently been copied from a thousand years ago by someone who had heard it drifting up out of the ground.

The thorns on either side of the pass leaned toward the sound.

"Keep singing," Astrid murmured.

She did. And the thorns listened, and the terrible cold eased by a single degree, and they climbed.

An hour in, Vess called a halt at a place where the pass widened into a shallow bowl—a natural choke point, walls rising sheer on three sides, a scatter of boulders offering cover. She looked at it the way a carpenter looks at a joint that fits.

"This is where she stops," Vess said.

Astrid's jaw tightened. But he had seen it too. Beyond this bowl the pass narrowed to a slot barely wide enough for two, and anyone trying to fight through that slot while carrying a child might as well lie down in front of the gray cloaks and apologize.

Dagny was already unpacking. She worked with the unhurried economy of a woman setting up a market stall: knives placed where her hands would find them, a coil of wire, three vials from Maren's satchel, a length of fuse-cord from Vess's pack. The boulders above the slot had been sitting there since the world was young. All they needed was encouragement.

"How long?" Astrid asked.

"Long enough." Dagny didn't look up from her work. "Go. All of you. The gate won't open for the rest of us anyway—it opens for debtors, and there's only one debtor in this company, whatever pretty stories we tell ourselves about found family."

"Dagny." His voice came out rough. "When this is done—when she's free—I'm coming back down this pass, and you're going to be here, and you're going to complain about my cooking for another thirty years. That's the debt I'm putting on *you.*"

For the first time since he'd known her, Dagny Winterbourne-of-no-fixed-address, terror of three smuggling rings and one tax collector, looked briefly, dangerously close to tears. She covered it by cuffing him on the shoulder hard enough to stagger him.

"Get out of my pass," she said.

Maren embraced him quickly, fiercely, her hands signing against his back faster than he could read—*come back, come back, come back*—and then she took up position behind a boulder with her satchel of burning powders and her calm, terrifying competence. Vess squeezed his arm once, said "Don't die; I've invested too much in you to write you off," and vanished up the trail with Wren's hand in hers.

Astrid lifted Wren onto his back. As they climbed away from the bowl, he looked back once.

Dagny stood alone among the boulders, small and gray-haired and utterly unafraid, testing the fuse-cord in her hands. Seeing him look, she raised one finger—not a wave, a warning: *eyes forward, boy.* 

He obeyed. He climbed. Behind him, faint and fading, he heard the first sounds of the gray cloaks arriving at the mouth of the pass, and Dagny's voice ringing off the stone, clear and profane and magnificent, greeting them the way she greeted all unwelcome guests.

Then the pass bent, and she was gone from sight, and there was only the climb and the cold and the child on his back, humming the oldest song in the world.

* * *

The gate filled the end of the pass like a held breath.

It was not built. That was the thing Astrid's mind kept snagging on as they approached. Doors are built; this had been *grown*—a seam in the living rock framed by two thorn-trees so vast they were less trees than towers, their branches interlocking overhead into a lattice that filtered the daylight into green-black stripes. Between them, set into the mountainside, stood the door itself: dark wood shot through with veins of iron-gray thorn, carved—or grown—with figures that seemed to move when he wasn't looking directly at them. Kings. Beggars. A woman holding a swaddled bundle. A man with his hands empty and open.

Beneath the lintel, in a script older than any kingdom, older than the language Wren spoke, ran an inscription. Astrid couldn't read it. But Wren slid down from his back, walked forward until the thorn-lattice cast its bars across her small body, and translated it the only way she could—by pressing her palm flat against the door.

The pendant blazed. The veins in the wood lit green-gold, tracing the inscription letter by letter, and Astrid felt the words land in his mind the way memories landed when he stole them: not learned, but *remembered.*

**THE DOOR OPENS TO DEBT ALONE. NAME WHAT YOU OWE AND TO WHOM, AND ENTER.**

And beneath it, smaller, almost an afterthought, almost cruel:

**THE COURT KNOWS ALL NAMES. LIES ARE INTEREST BUT PRINCIPAL IS OWED.**

Silence, except for the wind moving through the thorn-lattice with a sound like slow breathing.

Wren turned and looked at him. Her expression was the one that undid him most completely—not fear, not pleading, just a complete and patient faith that he would do whatever needed doing, a faith he had done nothing to earn except refuse, again and again, to leave her.

He stepped forward and put his hand on the door beside hers.

The wood was warm. It pulsed under his palm like a sleeping animal, and somewhere far below, something vast turned its attention toward him with the weight of centuries.

*I owe it a soul,* he thought. *I owe it a child. I owe it—*

But the door did not want thoughts. The inscription had been specific. **NAME WHAT YOU OWE AND TO WHOM.**

To whom.

The court knew all names. Lies were interest. Which meant the name he spoke had to be true—not the name the world knew, not *Astrid Joubert*, smuggler and thief, the convenient fiction he had worn for twenty years. The door wanted the name underneath. The name he had buried so deep that even he could not dig it out, the name he had traded away in a snowlit room while a newborn screamed and an envoy smiled—

He didn't remember it. That was the horror of it. He stood at the door of the Thorn Court with his hand on the wood and reached for his own name and found a smooth blank wall where it should have been, a locked room inside a locked room, and behind him half a mile back Dagny was buying his passage with her freedom, and in his arms' reach a child was running out of borrowed time, and the door would not open for a man who did not know what he owed or to whom.

*Think.* He closed his eyes. The fragments he had recovered rose in the dark: snow, a crying infant, his own younger voice saying *take her instead.* And beneath those, surfacing now like bodies rising in thawing water, edges of the third memory—the one still held in the court's vault—the one he had to reach:

A candle. An envoy's gloved hand extending a contract. His own voice, younger, steadier than he had ever been, speaking words of bargain in the court's own protocol. And at the start of it, before the terms, before the price—the envoy saying, courteously, the way one reads a signature aloud:

*"Then let the record show. Speak it for the seal."*

And his own answer. Not spoken in the memory—he had taken that piece of himself away before he could hear it again—but the shape of it remained, the shape of his own mouth forming it, and his body remembered what his mind had burned.

Astrid opened his eyes.

The door waited. Wren waited. Somewhere below, the court waited, patient as roots.

He spoke his name.

It came out of him like a splinter drawn from flesh—small, ancient, painful, and unmistakably his. Three syllables in a tongue that predated the kingdoms, the tongue Wren spoke, the tongue of the court itself, and the moment the last syllable left his lips the mountain shuddered. The thorn-lattice overhead rattled. The veins in the door blazed from green-gold to white, and the great dark wood swung inward without a sound, opening onto a stairway that descended farther than the eye could follow, lit by a light that came from nowhere and everywhere, and the smell that rose out of it was the smell of the cellar, the memory of rot, the deep places where heirlooms are kept.

Wren's hand found his. Her grip was fierce. The pendant glowed like a captive star.

Somewhere behind them, far down the pass, a muffled boom rolled through the stone—Dagny's boulders falling, the chase delayed, the seconds purchased—and Astrid Joubert, who had erased his own name to become no one, stepped through the Gate of Thorns as someone, owing everything, to collect his debt.

The door closed behind them like a held breath released.

And from the darkness below, courteous and cold, a voice that wore his own erased name like a mask said: *"Welcome home."*
