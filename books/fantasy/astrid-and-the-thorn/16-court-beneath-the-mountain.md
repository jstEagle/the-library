## 16. Court Beneath the Mountain

The gate closed behind them the way a mouth closes—slowly, deliberately, with the patience of something that had all the time the world had ever owned.

Astrid felt it seal against his back like a second spine. The passage beyond the Gate of Thorns was not a tunnel so much as a throat, its walls ribbed with roots grown pale from centuries without sun, and the air tasted of cold iron and old honey. Somewhere far below, water dripped in a rhythm too regular to be accidental. Counting. Keeping time. The mountain kept time the way a miser keeps coins: carefully, and only for itself.

Wren's hand found his in the dark. Small, warm, gripping hard enough to hurt. He was grateful for the pain. It gave him something to hold onto besides the memory he'd spoken aloud at the gate—*Astrid Joubert*—his own name rolling out of his mouth like a stone he'd been carrying under his tongue for fifteen years.

The name had opened the door. He still didn't know what it had opened in him.

"Stay behind me," he murmured. "If anything happens—if anything *moves*—you run back the way we came and don't look for me. Promise me."

Wren's answer was to grip his hand harder. She had learned enough of his language now to refuse him in it. *No*, she signed against his palm, one finger pressing the word into his skin, and then, in her own tongue of roots and stone, she said something long and low that he felt in his teeth rather than heard. He had stopped translating her. Some things didn't need it.

They descended.

The passage widened by degrees, and with every step the roots in the walls grew more deliberate—no longer the blind groping of buried things but *arranged*, braided into columns, woven into arches, trained into shapes that might have been decoration or might have been script. Astrid touched one as they passed and snatched his fingers back. The root was warm. It had a pulse.

They were walking through something alive, and the something knew they were there.

Light came next—not torchlight, nothing so honest—but a greenish glow rising from the floor like memory of summer, and then the passage opened all at once, the way a held breath opens, into a hall so vast that Astrid's eyes refused its dimensions for the first several heartbeats.

He had stolen the memory of this place once. He had not known it until now.

* * *

It came back to him in pieces, the way all his stolen goods came back—secondhand, already worn smooth by another mind's handling. A courtier's memory, lifted from a gray-cloaked agent on a mountain road three years ago, filed away and forgotten because it had seemed like nothing: a fragment of etiquette, a set of forms of address, the choreography of a court he'd assumed was legend.

The hall was real. The hall had *rules*. And the memory knew them.

Columns of black thorn rose into darkness overhead, each one hung with what he first took for lanterns. Then his eyes adjusted, and his stomach turned over, and he understood that they were not lanterns at all.

They were souls.

Each one hung in a cage of braided thorn no larger than a birdcage, and each one burned with a light that was unmistakably, horribly *personal*. Some burned white and steady. Some flickered. One, near the entrance, guttered the way a candle gutters when it has nearly nothing left, and Astrid found himself walking toward it before he knew he'd moved—some thief's instinct, some rescuer's reflex, he no longer knew which parts of himself were which.

The caged light flared as he approached. And inside it, briefly, he saw a face. Not a face floating in the flame—a face *remembered by* the flame, a life compressed to its own final image: a young man on a riverbank, laughing, a fish on a line, sunlight on water. The soul's last kept moment. The thing the court had chosen to display, the way a collector displays the most flattering portrait.

"Astrid." Wren's voice—her real voice, rusty and small and almost never used—stopped his hand an inch from the thorn. "Don't. They want you to touch."

He withdrew his fingers. "You can speak here."

"I can speak where they can hear me." Her eyes, in the green light, were the exact color of the glow. "They gave me back my words so I could beg properly. That's how it works. That's how it *always* works." Her jaw tightened around something older than her borrowed body. "Don't give them a reason to add you to the wall."

*The wall.* He looked up. The columns went up farther than he'd thought. The cages went on farther than he'd thought. Thousands. Tens of thousands. A hoard of selves, each one burning, each one *kept*—and the court below did not even trouble to look at them. That was the worst of it. Not cruelty. *Curation.* These were heirlooms, and heirlooms are not loved. They are dusted.

"Come on," he said, and his voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect. "We have an appointment."

* * *

The throne room—if that was the word—lay at the heart of the hall, and it was smaller than he expected, which he understood was deliberate. The court did not need grandeur. Grandeur was for kings who feared their subjects. The Thorn Court feared nothing that still breathed.

They sat in a semicircle of living thrones, nine of them, grown from a single root system that must have gone down into the dark of the world like the taproot of creation. Each throne held a figure, and each figure wore a crown of thorn so ancient it had gone the color of old bone, and each figure was—

Different. That was the trap of them. One was a woman of gray bark with eyes like rings in a tree's heartwood. One was a tall shape like a heron made of shadow and briar. One was almost human, almost kind, a grandmother's face with frost in its wrinkles. Astrid's stolen memory supplied the protocol and nothing more: *do not count them aloud, do not name them, do not meet the eyes of the one in the center until addressed.*

The center throne was empty.

He made himself bow—the full courtly depth of it, hand over heart, palm turned outward to show it empty of iron, of salt, of theft. The memory guided his body like a puppeteer's hand, and he let it, and the motion felt horrifyingly natural, as if his spine had done this before in some life he couldn't reach.

"We come under debtor's right," he said, and the words came out in a language with no living name, old as root and stone, and he did not stumble once. "One soul held in pledge. One debt outstanding. I am come to negotiate terms of release."

Silence. The kind of silence that has texture. Then the heron-shape moved its head, slow as a season changing, and its voice came from everywhere the thorns grew—which was everywhere.

*"A debtor. How novel. They so rarely come down of their own legs."*

"They rarely know they're debtors," Astrid said. "You make sure of that."

*"We make sure of nothing. We only keep what is given."* The bark-woman's voice was drier, rustier, like wind through a dead hedgerow. *"The child was given. Properly. With seal and signature and price paid in full. The law of thorns is not cruel, thief. It is only exact."*

"Then let's be exact." He kept his hands loose at his sides, his breathing even, his eyes fixed just left of the frost-grandmother's face. Every instinct he owned was screaming at him to run, and he set each scream aside like a tool he'd need later. "I've read the contract. Through an intermediary. The seal is valid. The signature—" he paused, and made himself say it "—the signature is mine."

The court did not stir. But every thorn in the hall, every cage, every pale root in the walls, went very slightly *still*, the way a house goes still when it listens.

*"Is it,"* said the heron.

"You know it is. You've had fifteen years to study it. You've had fifteen years to *wear* it." He took a breath. This was the part he'd rehearsed on the road above, in the dark, while Wren slept against Dagny's side. "I'm not here to dispute the debt. I'm here to buy it out. Name the price for the girl's soul, freely and fully released, her name struck from your rolls, her claim dissolved root and branch. I'll pay it."

*"Will you,"* said the frost-grandmother, and her smile was the kindest thing in the room, and therefore the most terrifying. *"Thief. What could you possibly have that we want?"*

And Astrid Joubert—who had spent fifteen years selling other people's yesterdays, who had stripped merchants of first kisses and soldiers of their mothers' faces and never once flinched—felt the answer rise in him before he could stop it, and knew, with a thief's cold clarity, that they had brought him here precisely to say it.

"Me," he said.

* * *

The court let the silence stretch until it sang.

Then the center throne—the empty one—began, very slowly, to grow. Thorns curled up from the seat like fingers unfurling. A shape assembled itself out of shadow and briar, and Astrid watched it with the detachment of a man watching his own funeral, because he knew what it was building. He'd known since the gate. Maybe since the pendant. Maybe since a snowy night fifteen years ago that he'd locked away so thoroughly he'd needed a key and a stranger's kindness to find the first splinter of it.

The shape finished. It stood. It wore his face.

Not a copy. Not an approximation. *His* face—younger, unlined, the face he'd had before he'd taught it to be forgettable—crowned with thorn, dressed in traveling clothes he half-remembered owning, and smiling the smile of a man who has just made a bargain he is proud of.

"This is what you sold," the court said, with its nine voices braided into one. "This is what we have kept. A name. A future. Fifteen years of the man you might have been—and we have worn him well, thief. He has walked your mountains. He has signed your contracts. He has, on occasion, been more useful to us than a hundred agents." The thing with his face tilted its head. "You erased yourself so thoroughly that even you cannot say what is owed. But we can. We kept the receipts."

Wren's hand was shaking in his. He squeezed it once: *steady.*

"Then name it," Astrid said.

*"The final fragment,"* the court said. *"The last piece of you in our vault. The night of the bargain, whole and uncut—every word, every price, every reason. You have seen two splinters of that night. You have not seen how it ends."*

"And if I take it?"

"Then the girl goes free." The frost-grandmother leaned forward, and her kindness was a knife laid flat on the table, hilt toward him. "Her soul released, her name struck from the rolls, her body hers again—aging as mortals age, free as mortals are free. All of it. In exchange, you read the memory. All of it. And then you *keep* it. We do not take it back. We do not erase it. You will walk out of this mountain knowing exactly who you were, exactly what you did, and exactly what you gave up to be the man who stands here now."

Astrid stared at them. At the cages burning overhead. At his own younger face, wearing its thorn crown, wearing its patient smile.

"And the catch," he said, because there was always a catch, and thieves who forgot that died poor or died caged.

"No catch." The heron spread its wings of briar, and the gesture was almost courtly. "Only this, which is not a catch but a fact: the memory is heavy. Heavier than any you have stolen, because it is *yours*, and yours was never a light thing to carry. Some men read such a memory and are destroyed by it. Some men read it and are *made*. We are curious, thief, which you will be. We have been curious about you for fifteen years."

The frost-grandmother's voice dropped to something almost tender. "You came down here to ransom a child with your memories. We are offering you the same bargain you intended to make—only we ask for less. One memory. One night. One truth." Her smile deepened. "Mercy, thief. Dressed in exactly the clothes you'd have dressed it in yourself. That is how you know it is mercy."

*That is how you know it is a trap,* said every instinct he had left, and for once, Astrid listened to his instincts and did not obey them, and did not refuse them either.

He looked at Wren. She was watching the thing with his face with an expression he'd never seen on her—old grief meeting old anger, a girl who remembered being sold looking at the shape of the selling. And beneath it, carefully hidden, carefully guarded, something that might have been fear *for him*.

"Will it hurt her?" he asked the court. "The reading. The keeping."

"The child feels nothing but freedom," the court said. "That is the whole of the law."

"Then I'll read it."

The hall exhaled. Every thorn in every wall shivered once, like a forest in wind, and the thing with his face stepped aside, and behind the empty throne a door opened in the living rock—a small door, a vault's door, breathing out air that smelled of snow and iron and a crying infant's first breath.

"Walk," said the court, with nine voices, almost gently. "Read. And thief—"

He paused at the threshold.

*"When you reach the end of that night,"* the heron said, and for the first time its voice sounded less like a season and more like a man, *"you will understand why we kept the mask. And you will understand why, of all the souls on our wall, hers was the only one we never let gutter."*

Astrid stepped through the door, and it closed behind him like the gate had—slowly, deliberately, with the patience of something that had already won.

The vault was dark. Then a light bloomed, small and white, hovering at the height of a man's heart: the memory, waiting, whole.

He reached out—and stopped.

Because the light was humming. The same note, the same pitch, as a thorn pendant in a storm-lit doorway, on the night a barefoot child had knocked on his door and changed the shape of his life.

The memory of the night he'd sold her was humming *her* song.

Astrid Joubert closed his hand around it, and the dark took him.
