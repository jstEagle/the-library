## 18. The Mask That Walked

*Astrid*

The throne room of the Thorn Court was not a room. It was a wound in the mountain that had learned to pretend it was architecture.

Astrid felt it in his teeth as they crossed the threshold—the wrongness of stone grown rather than carved, ribs of black basalt arching overhead like the inside of some enormous throat, walls furred with thornvines that had no business living this far beneath the earth. They were white here. Pale as bone, pale as frost, and they moved. Slowly. The way a sleeper moves. The way something dreams.

Wren's hand was small and cold in his. She had not let go since the gate.

*Do not look at the dais yet,* he almost told her, and then understood the cowardice of it. He had spent twenty years teaching himself never to lie to children, never to keep what he took, never to open the locked rooms inside himself—and every one of those rules had been a door slammed against this exact moment. He looked at the dais.

The Thorn Court sat in a crescent of nine seats grown from the same black stone, and in those seats sat things that had once been kings, or claimed to have been, their crowns of living thorn sunk so deep into their skulls that crown and bone had become one argument. Their faces were old the way rivers are old—worn smooth by time but still moving, still carrying something along. Their eyes did not reflect the pale light of the hanging lanterns. They *kept* it.

And before them, at the foot of the dais, stood a figure in gray.

Not a gray cloak. Astrid knew the cloaks now—he had torn fear out of two of their wearers on the smuggler's road and still dreamed of what he'd found underneath: devotion like root systems, tangled and total. This figure wore no hood. It wore a mask.

The mask was his face.

Not approximately. Not a likeness. It was *his* face as he had worn it twenty years ago—younger by two decades, unlined, the jaw less hollowed, the mouth not yet trained into its professional half-smile. He knew it the way you know your own handwriting: instantly, intimately, with a lurch of recognition that arrived ahead of any thought. The mask's surface was porcelain-pale thornwood, grained fine as skin, and where the eyes should have been there were only shallow depressions, smooth and empty, waiting to be filled.

"Debtor," said the masked thing, and the voice was worse than the face. It was his voice. Younger. Warmer. A voice that had never stolen anything from anyone. "You return to us wearing borrowed years and a name you stole from silence. We have kept yours safe. Would you like it back?"

Behind him, Astrid heard Vess draw breath through her teeth—the sound of an information broker doing arithmetic she didn't like. Maren's hand found Wren's shoulder, steady, grounding. And somewhere above them, beyond three locked gates and a collapsing pass, Dagny was either dead or captive, and Astrid could not afford to know which.

"You're wearing my face," he said. His own voice sounded thin in the vast throat of the hall.

"We are wearing what you abandoned." The avatar tilted its head, and the gesture was so perfectly, horribly *his*—the little bird-tilt he did when listening—that his stomach turned over. "You came to our gate twenty years ago with an infant's soul cupped in your hands like water, and you begged. Do you remember begging? You were very good at it. You offered us everything. Your name. Your future. Every year you would ever live. We accepted the name and the future and left you the years, because a man who does not remember himself makes such a *convenient* neighbor." The empty eye-holes seemed to regard Wren. "We have walked among mortals in your shape since before she could walk. We signed contracts. We bought memories. We collected debts. You built your invisibility, thief, out of materials we handed you."

"That's not—" Astrid stopped. Swallowed. The truth of it was already crawling up through him, cold as groundwater. The signature style he'd copied without thinking why. The courtly protocol that had come off his tongue flawless in the antechamber, word for word, as if he'd spoken it yesterday. The way the gate had opened to him *like a door opening to its own key.*

He hadn't been bargaining with the court all these years.

He'd been *rehearsing.*

"The final fragment," he said instead, because the alternative was to scream, and screaming was a luxury for people with fewer allies behind them. "Her ransom. You offered a trade."

"We offered *mercy,*" the avatar said gently, in his gentlest voice, the one he used on frightened travelers right before he reached into their heads. "One memory for one soul. The last piece of yourself, held in our vault these twenty years, gathering interest. Take it, read it, keep it whole—and the child walks free into the morning. Is that not fair? Are we not generous?" It spread its arms, and the arms were his arms, and the sleeves were gray. "Or refuse, and watch her body finish what her bargain started. She has days left, debtor. Perhaps hours. The thorn is patient, but the flesh is not."

*Take it,* whispered something in him that sounded like his own younger voice, the voice on the mask. *Take it and be whole. You've been hollow for twenty years. One fragment and you'll remember who you were—*

"No," said Wren.

---

*Wren*

Wren had been learning words for two seasons now, and she had learned that most of them were cages.

*Bread* was warm and good but also meant the crusts Dagny cut off and saved for the birds. *Home* meant Astrid's cabin but also meant a place under the mountain where souls hung in cases like pressed flowers. *Free* was the worst word of all, because everyone said it about her, and none of them—not Maren with her speaking hands, not Vess with her maps of lost years, not even Astrid—knew what it cost.

But *no* needed no learning. *No* was older than language. *No* was the first thing she had ever said, though no one had heard it—a newborn's silent refusal as hands passed her from warmth to cold, from mother to mountain. She had been saying it for her whole life. She said it now, and the mountain heard.

The white thorns on the walls turned toward her. All of them. Thousands of pale spines shifting like grass before wind, and beneath her feet she felt the deep roots of the place shiver—not in hunger, which she had expected, but in *recognition.*

She was kin to this power. Maren had known it at the ford; the river had known it when it rose around her like a mother's arm. The Thorn Court grew from the same soil her soul had been planted in, and that meant—

That meant it could be argued with. Roots could be reasoned with. She was learning words, and the oldest word she knew was the one growing things said to each other through the dark: *enough.*

"Little vessel," said the masked man, turning toward her, and his empty face made her want to run until the world ran out. "You are nearly ripe. Come home. We have kept your case dusted."

"I am not a vessel," Wren said. Her voice came out strange, doubled, as if something spoke through her from below. "I am a *tree.* And trees do not go home to the axe."

She stepped forward, out of Maren's shadow, out of Astrid's reach, and pulled the pendant over her head.

It hummed against her palm—her mother's humming, she realized suddenly, the first sound she had ever loved, caught in thornwood like a fly in amber. The court had given it to her body as a leash. But a leash tied to a tree is just a rope, and ropes can be *grown through.*

She drove the pendant's thorn into the floor of the great hall.

---

*Astrid*

The mountain screamed.

Not the court—the *mountain*, the deep patient bones of it, as Wren's blood met the pale thornwood and something in the stone remembered what it was before it was furniture. Light went through the hall like a bell-stroke, white and total, and the thornvines on the walls surged upright, unfurling, every spine straining toward the girl at the center of the room as if toward sun.

The nine crowned things rose from their seats. For the first time, their smooth river-old faces showed expression, and the expression was *fear.*

"She is unripe," one rasped. "The contract—"

"Contracts," said another, and its voice cracked like ice on a pond, "are thorn-law. And thorn-law says—" 

"—that nothing planted may be harvested before it flowers," Wren finished, in the doubled voice, in the old language, and then, in theirs, plain and fierce and young: "*I haven't flowered.*"

Astrid understood then, with a clarity that felt like falling, what she was doing. She wasn't fighting the court. She was *lawyering* with it—in the only law it obeyed, the law written in its own roots. And every second she held its attention was a second it wasn't holding hers.

Which meant the rest was up to him.

The avatar had not moved. Through the white blaze of Wren's defiance it stood untouched, unblinking, its empty face turned toward Astrid alone, and when it spoke again the young-warm voice carried easily over the groaning of stone.

"You see what she is," it said. "Power without a self to hold it. She will burn herself out in an hour, or we will take her in a day, or she will become a court of her own and forget your name within a century—souls do, thief, it is what souls are *for.* Unless." It extended one gray-sleeved hand, palm up, and in the palm lay a shard of pale light: the final fragment, his last sealed memory, glowing like a splinter of dawn. "Unless someone holds her. Someone with roots. Someone who knows who he is."

Astrid looked at the fragment.

Twenty years of hollowness looked back. He thought of the snow, the crying infant, his own voice saying *take her instead.* He thought of the locked rooms, the key Dagny had kept for him *for when I'm brave enough.* He thought of how badly he wanted it—not the knowledge, exactly, but the *wholeness*, the end of being a man assembled from other people's leftovers, the simple animal relief of finally, finally knowing his own name.

The avatar smiled with his smile.

"Take it," it said. "Be yourself again. Be our brother. We grew you, after all—we can grow you back. And the child goes free, and you walk out of here *complete,* and all it costs is that you stop pretending you are something the court did not make."

And Astrid Joubert—who had spent his whole life taking things from others and keeping nothing, who had erased his own name rather than live inside what he'd done, who had taught himself that the only honest possession was the one you gave away—looked at the last piece of himself in the world, and refused it.

"No," he said. "I don't want myself back. I want myself *forward.*"

He reached out—not for the fragment, but for the mask.

The avatar recoiled, and for one instant the emptiness of its eye-holes filled with something that was not calm: panic, old and deep, the panic of a thing that has been wearing a stolen face so long it has forgotten it ever had another. Astrid's fingers closed on the porcelain edge of his own younger cheek, and he *pulled*, and the gift rose in him the way it always did—that cold clean reaching, the thief's touch—but this time he did not take a memory.

He gave one back.

He poured into the mask everything he had recovered: the snow, the infant, the envoy naming his price, the desperate bargain, the shame, the love, the lullaby. Twenty years of a man's life, returned to the shape that had been carved to hold it. The mask drank it in hungrily—it couldn't help itself, it was made of wanting—and as it filled, it began to *crack*, hairline fractures spidering from the empty eyes, because a shell built to contain nothing cannot survive containing everything.

"You are not me," Astrid said, through his teeth, through the light pouring out of the splitting seams. "You are what I threw away. And I am not taking you back. *Be yourself.*"

The mask shattered.

What fell out of it was not a monster. It was smaller than that, sadder than that—a shape of gray smoke and thornwood, formless, clutching at the fragments of a face it no longer had, mouthing a name that no longer belonged to anyone. The nine crowned things cried out as one, and the white thorns of the hall whipped wild, and Wren's doubled voice rang off the vaulted dark:

"*Enough!*"

---

*Wren*

Afterward, Wren would remember the chaos in pieces, the way broken glass remembers the window.

The avatar's ruin thrashing apart into smoke. The nine thorn-crowned elders descending from their dais with a sound like a forest walking. Vess shouting coordinates to doors that weren't doors yet. Maren's hands flashing *down, down, stay low* while she dragged a stunned smuggler out of the path of a sweeping vine. The whole hall convulsing as the mountain chose between its masters and its child.

And through all of it—one clear note, cutting across everything.

Dagny's laugh.

Wren spun. At the far end of the hall, where the great gates had sealed behind them, the doors stood open, and in the doorway stood a small round woman in a thief's leathers, gray with tunnel dust, one eye swelling shut, flanked by two gray-cloaked agents who were very carefully not touching her.

Because they couldn't. Because Dagny had her hand flat against the gate-thorns, and she was talking to them the way she talked to everyone—fast, foul-mouthed, and absolutely certain—and the thorns were *listening.*

"—and clause nine, you absolute shrubbery, clause *nine*: a debt collector who takes a debtor's kin hostage forfeits claim on the debtor entire! Read your own roots! I've been sitting in your cellar for two days reading your own *roots*, and let me tell you, whoever keeps your ledgers should be ashamed—"

"She speaks thorn-law," one of the crowned ones breathed.

"She speaks *contract,*" Dagny snapped, "which is more than I can say for you lot. Now. The girl hasn't flowered. The avatar's voided. The debtor's present and refusing payment—which, per clause twelve, means the debt is *in dispute*—and disputed debts go to arbitration, and arbitration—" she jabbed a finger at the nearest elder, whose crown of thorns had begun, very slowly, to wilt, "—goes to the *mountain.* Not to you. To the mountain. So unless any of you would like to argue with the bedrock you're standing on—"

The mountain answered.

It was not loud. It was worse than loud. It was a single slow pulse, deep beneath them, like a heartbeat deciding, and every thorn in the hall turned white-hot for an instant and then went dark—dark as rich soil, dark as rest. The nine elders sank back onto their thrones as if strings had been cut. The gray cloaks dropped to their knees. Somewhere far above, Astrid later swore, snow slid off a peak that had held it for a hundred years.

In the ringing quiet, Dagny limped across the hall, took one look at Wren, and gathered her into a hug that smelled of pipe smoke and tunnel dust and stubbornness.

"Told you I'd hold the pass," she muttered into Wren's hair. "Nobody said which pass."

Then she looked past Wren's shoulder, at Astrid—at the man standing amid the ashes of his own face, hollow-eyed, swaying, empty-handed, having just refused the only thing he'd ever wanted—and her voice went soft in a way Wren had heard exactly once before.

"Well," Dagny said. "That's the hard part done."

Astrid shook his head slowly. When he spoke, his voice was very quiet, and very steady, and it frightened Wren more than the elders had.

"No," he said. "Now they'll want to know what I *do* have left to pay. And Dagny—I'm going to give them everything."

---

*Astrid*

The eldest of the nine leaned forward on its throne, crown of wilted thorn dripping sap like slow tears, and studied him with eyes that kept the light.

"A dispute," it said, "requires an offer."

Astrid looked at Wren—flushed with power, alive, her bare feet rooted in the dark soil of the hall as if she had grown there—and then at the vault doors beyond the dais, behind which hung his last fragment like a lamp in a case. He thought of every memory he owned. Every memory he had stolen. Every year.

"All of it," he said. "Everything I have. Everything I am. My ransom for her soul—whole, free, and flowering wherever she chooses."

The hall went still. Even the smoke of the ruined avatar paused in its dying.

"Thief," the eldest said softly, "you do not understand what remains to you. If we accept, you will not lose a piece. You will lose the *whole*. You will stand in the morning sun tomorrow and know nothing—not your trade, not your friends, not the child you are buying. You will be a stranger in your own skin, forever."

Astrid smiled. It was, he realized, the first honest smile of his life.

"I've been a stranger in my own skin for twenty years," he said. "At least this way, I'll have company."

He opened his arms—to the court, to the mountain, to the gathering dark—and Wren screamed his name, and the forgetting came down on him like snow.
