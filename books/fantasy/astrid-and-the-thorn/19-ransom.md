## 19. Ransom

The vault of the Thorn Court did not have walls. It had roots.

Astrid stood in a chamber grown rather than built, its ceiling a lattice of black branches so dense they drank the light of the thorn-lamps and gave back only a dim red glow, like embers seen through closed eyelids. Shelves spiraled up into the dark, and on the shelves sat jars—not glass, but something older, something like amber wept from trees that had died before there were names for trees. And in each jar, a light. Some burned steady. Some guttered. Some were nearly out.

Souls. Kept like preserves. Labeled like vintages.

He had walked past them without looking, because looking was a kind of consent, and he could not afford to consent to anything in this place. Every step through the court's halls had been a negotiation he hadn't known he was entering: the doors that opened only when he spoke their protocol, the servants of woven bark who bowed at angles no spine should permit, the slow pressure of attention from the throne room above—or below; direction meant nothing here—all of it designed to make him feel small, felt, *owned*. He had answered it with stolen courtly memory, with the flawless cadences of a language of bargains he had lifted from an envoy's mind two winters ago and never thought he'd need.

It had worked. That was the worst part. The court recognized fluency. The court respected debt.

And now, before him, on a shelf of living wood at exactly the height of his own heart, sat a jar with Wren's name scratched into it in a script older than any kingdom—the same script she spoke in her sleep, the same script that had crawled up Maren's arm like frost when the herb-witch touched the pendant.

The light inside it was small. Flickering. A candle in wind.

Beside it, larger, steadier, sat another jar. Unlabeled. But Astrid knew what it held without touching it, the way you know your own hand in the dark. The final fragment. The last piece of himself he had sold away, all those years ago, to buy the silence that had kept him alive and hollow ever since.

"Take it," said the voice from the throne.

* * *

The Thorn Court did not sit upon thrones so much as grow around them. What presided over the chamber was less a figure than a congregation—a mass of crowned shapes, bark-faced and antlered, their eyes the color of sap in moonlight. They had worn many faces over the centuries. They wore one now that made Astrid's stomach turn over slowly, like a stone dropped down a well.

His face. Or close enough. The mask they had crafted from the self he'd discarded—the name he'd spoken aloud for the first time at the gate, the name that had opened this door because the door knew its debtor—it looked out at him from the center of the congregation with patient, vegetative calm.

"You may have the girl," the masked one said, and its voice was his voice, younger, unbroken by years of smoke and solitude. "In exchange for the fragment. Fair trade. One soul returned to the world. One memory returned to us. The scales balance."

The jars hummed softly around him, a chord of held breaths.

Astrid looked at Wren.

She stood beside him, small and barefoot as the night she'd arrived at his door, her hand buried in his coat pocket where she'd kept it since the gate, since Dagny's shout had faded behind them in the pass, since everything. Her pendant hung dark against her chest—for now. Her eyes went from the jar with her name on it to his face and back again, and in them was a question she had learned enough words to ask only once, weeks ago, in a cabin full of storm-light:

*Why?*

Why would anyone come this far. Why would anyone trade for a child sold by strangers. Why him.

He had never answered her properly. There hadn't been time, and then there hadn't been courage, and then there hadn't been anything left but the road and the running and the gray cloaks behind them. He owed her an answer now. He intended to give her the only kind he had left.

"No," Astrid said.

The chamber stilled. Even the lamps' red glow seemed to lean in.

"The fragment is not enough," he said, and heard his own voice come out level, which surprised him, because inside he was shaking like a leaf in the first hard frost. "You know it isn't. You've counted. You always count—that's the first rule of your protocol, the law beneath your law: *nothing given freely, nothing taken cheaply.* A girl's whole soul against one memory of mine? Your scales would tip. Your court would fracture. You'd be in violation of yourselves."

The masked one tilted its head. His own young face, wearing his own old gesture. It was like watching a ghost rehearse.

"Name your price, then," it said.

"I already have." Astrid turned to face the full congregation, and let them see him—all of him, the thief, the coward, the man who had once stood in snow and said *take her instead*, the man who had spent fifteen years buying invisibility with pieces of his own past until there was barely enough left of him to fill a jar. "Everything."

* * *

He had rehearsed this on the road down. Not the words—he'd never been able to rehearse words, words came to him the way weather came, sudden and total—but the shape of it. The architecture of surrender. Because Vess had mapped his scattered years across three kingdoms, and Dagny had pressed a key into his hand and called him a fool with love in her scolding, and Maren had healed him twice and charged him once, and somewhere in all of that he had understood the thing he'd been refusing to understand since a mute child knocked on his door:

He had spent his whole life keeping nothing. Hoarding emptiness. Locking away his past so thoroughly that even he couldn't reach it, selling memories to strangers so no one could hold him by them, keeping rules instead of people—*never steal from children, never keep what you take*—because rules couldn't leave you, rules couldn't die, rules couldn't look at you across a vault of souls and ask *why* in a language older than kingdoms.

And it had bought him nothing. It had bought him this: a hollow man in a hall of jars, negotiating with his own discarded face.

So he would spend the rest. All of it. Freely, finally, in the one direction he'd never tried.

"Every memory I own," Astrid said. "Every year I've lived. The ones I stole and never sold—the merchant's first kiss, the soldier's mother's face, the lullaby I took from a woman on the coast road whose name I never learned. The ones I bought back—snow, and a crying infant, and my own voice saying *take her instead.* The ones still locked behind Dagny's key. And the years themselves—" his breath caught, and he pushed through it, "—the years I haven't lived yet. My future. Whatever remains of it. Take the whole account. Close the ledger. Wipe the name."

The congregation stirred. Branches creaked. Somewhere far above, or far below, water dripped in a rhythm like counting.

"The court does not take futures," the masked one said slowly. "Futures are not yet ripe."

"Then take them green." Astrid stepped forward, and Wren's grip tightened in his pocket, and he gently freed his coat from her fingers and knelt to look at her, eye to eye, the way he should have done the first night. "You wanted a debtor. Here is the greatest debtor your mountain has ever swallowed. Fifteen years of interest. Compounded. Paid in full."

* * *

Wren did not understand all his words. She understood perhaps half, the way she understood half of everything said above ground—catching meaning the way her hands caught rain, some of it slipping through. But she understood the shape of what he was doing. She had watched the court's servants carry contracts written on bark, and she had watched the gray cloaks weigh souls on scales of iron root, and she knew a bargain being struck when she stood inside one.

And she knew—she knew the way the mountain knew, the way the river at the ford had known, rising to shield her—that whatever he was offering, it was the largest thing he had.

She grabbed his sleeve. Words came to her slowly, in the tongue of here, the tongue of bread and fire and *Astrid*, the words she'd hoarded like he hoarded nothing, and she spent every one she had:

"No. No. Keep. Keep you."

He smiled. It was a terrible smile, cracked down the middle, and it broke her heart more cleanly than any thorn could have.

"That's the trick of it, little bird," he said softly. "There isn't a keep-me. There never was. I made sure of that a long time ago. Everything I am is either sold or locked, and the locks are yours now too—I'm giving you the key, do you hear me? Dagny has it. Maren knows where I hid the second. Vess has the map. They'll give them back to you, piece by piece, if you want them. If you want to build me back." His voice frayed. "You don't have to. But you can. That's the ransom. Not just that I forget. That someone remembers *for* me."

Above them the congregation had gone silent in the way of forests before snow.

"The court accepts," said the masked one, in his young voice, and there was something almost like hunger in it now, something almost like joy, and Astrid understood with a lurch that this was what the court had wanted all along—not the fragment, not the girl, but *him*, whole, the debtor who had slipped their grasp once by erasing himself, delivered at last to their shelves. "The ledger closes. The name is ours. The girl walks free."

The jar with Wren's name began to glow.

* * *

Light rose from it like dawn underwater, gold going to white going to a color Wren had no word for in any tongue, the color of being *called home*, and the stopper of woven thorn unwound itself petal by petal, and the small flickering flame that was her—her true self, her sold self, the self that had been sitting in the dark on a shelf while a borrowed body walked and stumbled and learned the word for bread—rose into the air between them.

It drifted toward her chest. Toward the pendant. Toward the hollow place inside her that had ached since before she could remember aching.

And Astrid pulled her in and held her.

He held her the way he had never held anything he intended to keep, because keeping had always frightened him more than losing, and now there was nothing left to lose and so he held her with both arms and his whole ruined heart, his cheek against her hair, and he felt the light settle into her like rain into drought-cracked earth, felt her go rigid and then soft, felt her small fists close in the back of his coat.

"Forget him," the court whispered, from everywhere, from the roots, from the jars, from his own borrowed face. "Forget the snow. Forget the infant. Forget the voice that said *take her instead.* Forget the lullaby. Forget the road, the fence, the witch, the broker, the key. Forget the name."

And it began.

It began the way dusk begins—not all at once, but edge by edge, the light withdrawing from the farthest fields first. The smell of the caravan road went. The weight of the merchant's first kiss, carried fifteen years, lifted from him like a bird leaving a branch. The coast road woman's lullaby unraveled note by note, and he grasped for it and found his hands closing on air.

No. Not air. Hair. Wren's hair, under his chin, and her shoulders shaking.

Hold on to that, he told himself, though he could no longer remember why, though *himself* was becoming a rumor, a story someone else had told about a man in these mountains. Hold on to—

Her name. He still had her name. *Wren.* It floated in him like a single lit window in a house going dark, and he cupped his hands around it, around the last warm thing, and—

"Astrid!"

She was screaming it. Screaming his name, the name he'd spoken at the gate for the first time in fifteen years, the name that was even now sliding off him like a coat in summer, pooling on the floor of the vault, being gathered up by patient bark-fingered servants and folded into a jar. She screamed it in the old tongue and the new one and then in no language at all, just sound, just grief with nowhere to go, and some deep animal cellar of him that forgetting had not yet reached heard it and answered the only way it knew how.

He tightened his arms. Once. Hard. The way you press a flower between the pages of a book—so that later, much later, someone turning the pages will find it flat and faded but *whole*, will know that once it bloomed, will know that someone thought it worth saving.

"There now," he said, or the ruin of him said, the words coming out slower, softer, a stranger's kindness in a stranger's mouth. "There now. Don't cry. I don't—"

He frowned, gentle and lost, at the small fierce girl clutching his coat in a hall of glowing jars.

"I don't know you," said the man who had been Astrid Joubert. "But I think—I think I was supposed to hold on to you."

And the light of the thorn-lamps dimmed, and the court bowed its crowned heads over its newest acquisition, and Wren screamed his name into the dark one last time—

—and above them, far above, through miles of stone and root and sleeping mountain, a door of thorns swung open onto the grey light of morning, and four friends who refused to let a stranger be a stranger started down toward the sound of a child crying.
