## 20. The Man Who Forgot

The mountain let us go at dawn.

I felt it happen the way you feel a splinter leave your skin—a small, clean absence, a place where something had been and now was not. The thorn-roads unknotted themselves beneath my feet. The air above ground tasted of pine sap and woodsmoke and rain that had fallen in the night, and I stood in the mouth of the gate with Dagny's hand on my shoulder and Maren's fingers pressed to my wrist, reading my pulse like a page, and none of us spoke.

Behind us, the dark went on being dark. That was all. The court did not wail or curse or crumble. It simply closed its ledger, the way a merchant does when a debt is paid in full, and the silence it left behind was so complete I could hear the snowmelt ticking in the stones.

Astrid lay where he had fallen.

Not fallen—*settled*, the way a coat settles when its wearer steps out of it. He looked smaller than I remembered. His face was smooth in a way faces are not supposed to be, every line smoothed away, every weathering gone, as if someone had taken a cloth to him. His eyes were open. They were gray as river fog and they held nothing at all—not fear, not wonder, not even the careful blankness of a man deciding what to hide. Just nothing, going down and down.

"Is he dead?" Dagny asked. Her voice was rough as bark. She had lost blood holding the pass and her arm was bound in Maren's linen and she stood like a woman who intended to stand forever whether her legs agreed or not.

"No," Maren said. She knelt and touched his temple with two fingers, and I watched her read him the way she read everything—through skin, through heat, through the small electric truths the body cannot keep from telling. Her face did something complicated. "No," she said again, softer. "He's empty. There's nothing left in him but the body's own habits. Breathing. Blinking. The heart doing its work because no one told it to stop."

"He gave everything." My voice came out strange. Deeper than before. In the weeks since the ford, since the water rose for me and the pendant burned white against my chest, I had been growing into myself by inches—my true self, the one the court had kept folded up like a letter in a drawer. My hands were longer now. My voice cracked sometimes in the middle of words. I was aging the way a tree ages after lightning: fast, uneven, hungry. "Everything. He didn't even hesitate."

"Hesitation's for people who've got something left to lose," Dagny said. Then she crouched, groaning, and took Astrid's chin in her calloused hand and turned his face toward the light. "Oi," she said. "Thief. Wake up. You still owe me for the stew."

His eyes moved to her. There was a moment—I will remember this moment until I am old, until I am older than the court, until I am older than the mountain—when something flickered there. Not memory. Not recognition. Something more animal than either. The look of a man who has woken in a strange bed and does not yet know enough to be afraid.

"Stew," he repeated. The word came out flat, unburdened, a coin with no face stamped on it.

"That's right." Dagny's jaw worked. I saw her decide not to cry, and I saw her lose. One tear, furious, wiped away with the back of her wrist before it could be witnessed. "And you'll work it off, mind. Wood to split. Floors to scrub. I don't house idle men."

"I don't—" He stopped. Looked at his own hands as though they belonged to a stranger who had wandered into his dream. "I don't know how to do anything."

"You know how to do plenty," Dagny said. "You just don't remember knowing it. There's a difference, girl-boy, and we'll prove it to you every day until you believe it."

Maren signed something, quick and fluid, and Vess—who had come up out of Hollowbridge two days behind us with a mule and a satchel of maps and a face like a locked door—translated without being asked. *He will need shelter. He will need routine. He will need people who refuse to treat him like a ghost.*

"He'll have them," Dagny said.

Vess looked down at Astrid for a long moment. Then she reached into her coat and drew out one of her maps—the good ones, inked on waxed linen—and laid it over his chest like a blanket, tucking the edges in with a precision that fooled no one. "For warmth," she said, daring anyone to comment.

I knelt beside him. Up close he smelled of cold stone and, faintly, of thorns—the court's perfume, already fading, already becoming only the smell of a man who had been underground too long. I put my hand over his. His fingers curled around mine without intention, the way a sleeping child's do, and something in my chest tore along an old seam.

He had done this for me. This man with no name in his own head had walked into the dark carrying every year he owned and traded them away so that I could stand here under an open sky and feel the wind on my face and grieve properly, which is a thing the court never lets its property do.

"Astrid," I said.

Nothing. The word landed on him like rain on glass.

That was when I understood what I had to do. Not because anyone told me. Because I was made of the same craft that had emptied him—I was a soul once sold, once stored, once worn like an heirloom by things with thorn-crowned heads, and I knew in my marrow how memories are kept, and kept, and given back.

They were not destroyed. Nothing the court owns is ever destroyed. They were *in me*. When the ransom was accepted, when the ledger closed, every stolen year of Astrid Joubert had poured across the bargain like water finding its level, and I was the vessel standing nearest. I could feel them. All of them. A winter of them, a childhood of them, pressing against the inside of my ribs like birds in a cage too small, waiting for someone to open the door.

I could give them back. All of them. Every last one.

Or I could give them back slowly. Carefully. One at a time, the way you feed a fire when the wood is green—because a man who receives twenty years at once is a man who drowns, and I had watched drowning happen from the inside, and I would not be the cause of it twice.

One at a time. And not all. Some things a person should be allowed to set down forever.

"Astrid," I said again, and this time I put my whole self into it, the way I had learned to speak to roots and stone. "My name is Wren. You bought me back from the Thorn Court. You don't remember it, but you paid more than anyone has ever paid for anything, and I intend to make you glad of it. Do you understand?"

He blinked. Slowly. Like a cat deciding whether the hand near it means food or harm.

"...Wren," he said. Testing it. Finding no hook in himself for it to hang on. But he did not pull his hand away.

* * *

We carried him down the mountain in shifts. Dagny led, favoring her bound arm, choosing the smuggler paths by instinct so deep it lived in her feet rather than her head. Vess navigated by map and muttered correction. Maren walked beside the litter with her hand resting on Astrid's ankle, reading him hour by hour, and each time she lifted her fingers she shook her head a little less.

I walked behind. Watching. Learning the shape of what I carried.

It was stranger than I had expected. His memories did not sit in me like objects in a sack. They sat in me like rooms in a house I had inherited—furnished, lived-in, smelling faintly of their former owner. When I closed my eyes I could walk through them. A kitchen with a crooked door. A road at dusk with lantern-light coming up the hill. A woman's laugh, heard once and hoarded for forty years. A lock of hair kept in a tin. The taste of stolen bread eaten in a doorway with the specific, ferocious joy of a nine-year-old who has gotten away with it.

And underneath all of it, deeper than any room, a sealed door. Black iron. No handle on my side.

Him. The thing he had taken from himself first, before any of this—the name, the bargain, the reason. Even the court had not been able to open that door; they had only built their mask around its absence. It was the one memory that had not come to me, and I think it never would, and I think that was mercy. Some doors are locked from both sides for a reason.

On the second evening we reached the cabin.

It was exactly as he had left it—or as I remembered him leaving it, which amounted to the same thing now. Cold hearth. Two cups on the shelf, one chipped. The thorn pendant hanging on its nail by the door, dull and quiet, its long work finished. Dagny got a fire going with the efficiency of a siege engineer while Maren settled Astrid by the hearth and Vess, after a visible internal argument, hung her wet cloak on his peg rather than dripping in the doorway, claiming the peg nearest the door as a matter of practicality and fooling no one.

He woke properly that night.

I was sitting across the room, keeping the vigil I seemed to have appointed myself to, when his breathing changed and his eyes came open and found the fire. He watched it for a long time. Then he said, to the room in general:

"There's a woman in my head."

Everyone went very still.

"Not a ghost-woman," he said carefully, frowning at his own hands. "A real one. Or—she feels real. Dark hair going gray at the temples. She's laughing at something I said, except I didn't say it, because I wasn't there. Except I was." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "Why do I have her? Whose is she?"

The room held its breath. I looked at Maren. Maren looked at me and signed, slow enough for me to follow: *Tell him when he asks. Not before. A gift offered too soon becomes a burden.*

"She's yours," I said. "She was. If you want her back, I can give her to you. But not tonight. Tonight you should sleep, and tomorrow I'll teach you a game, and the game is how it works."

"What game?"

"The best kind," Dagny said from the hearth, poking the coals harder than coals deserve. "The kind with rules the other player doesn't know yet."

* * *

The game went like this.

Each morning, after chores—for Dagny had installed the doctrine of chores within two days, and Astrid, blank-eyed and obedient as a new recruit, split wood with a competence that made him frown at his own arms in betrayed wonder—each morning we would sit by the hearth, and I would take his hands in mine, and I would open one door.

Not push. Open. There is a difference, and it is the whole difference. A memory pushed into a mind is an invasion; a memory opened onto is an invitation. I had been on the wrong side of both. I knew the grammar of it the way I knew the grammar of roots: gently, gently, from below.

The first one I gave him was small on purpose. Bread.

Warm bread, torn open, butter going liquid in the creases, eaten on a dock in Hollowbridge at seventeen years old with gulls screaming overhead and salt on the wind. I felt him receive it—felt the little shudder go through him, the way a dry field takes the first rain. When he opened his eyes they were wet and he did not know why.

"Bread," he said. "Why do I care about bread?"

"Because you were happy once," I said, "and it happened to involve bread. That's all. You don't need the rest yet."

He ate breakfast that morning with more attention than I had ever seen anyone pay to a meal. He turned the crust over in his fingers. He held it up to the light.

The next day: rain on a tin roof, and the specific peace of being somewhere dry while the world was not. The day after: a dog, brown and disreputable, long dead, whose ears he used to scratch with his boot-toe while pretending to read. Each gift was small. Each one landed in him and rooted, and I began to see it happen—the man rebuilding himself the way a wall rebuilds, stone by chosen stone, except that these stones were being handed to him by someone else, and the miracle was that he kept choosing to accept them.

Because that was the part I had not anticipated. He chose. Every morning he sat across from me and held out his hands and said, "Today?" and waited, and when I gave him something he weighed it, and turned it over, and asked questions about it as though it were a found object rather than a lost self. *Was I happy here? Was I alone? Did I steal this? Should I have?*

Sometimes the answers were hard. The day I gave him the caravan road—the dusk, the merchant, the first kiss lifted from a stranger's mind and sold by nightfall—he was quiet for a long time afterward.

"I did that," he said finally. Not a question.

"Yes."

"And the person—the merchant. She missed those things afterward? Without knowing why?"

"Yes."

He looked into the fire. "I want to say I wouldn't do it again," he said. "But I don't know who I am well enough to promise that. Isn't that a strange thing? I'm ashamed of a man I haven't met."

"You've met him," I said. "You're meeting him. One morning at a time."

He nodded slowly. Then: "The woman. The one with the gray at her temples. Is she in there?"

I hesitated. Maren caught my eye across the room and gave the smallest shake of her head—not *no*, but *not yet*.

"Soon," I said.

He accepted that. He was learning to accept things, which for a man built out of other people's patience was perhaps the hardest lesson of all.

* * *

Winter came down off the high fells in earnest, and the cabin filled.

Dagny never quite left, in the way of old thieves, who treat departure as a tactical error. She claimed the chair by the window as hers by right of conquest and conducted from it a running campaign of criticism against everyone's technique—mine at the hearth, Vess's map-mending, Astrid's wood-splitting, which she declared adequate in a tone that we all understood to be the highest praise she issued. Her arm healed crooked. She refused to have it looked at again. "Character," she said.

Vess came and went between Hollowbridge and the mountains, bringing supplies and news and, once, a package containing a collector's entire inventory of purchased memories, seized through channels she declined to describe, with a note pinned to it: *These were bought from people who couldn't afford to keep them. Returning them seemed like your sort of project.* We spent a month returning them. Astrid helped. He was very good at it. He had a gentleness with the frightened ones—the ones who woke from their returned pasts weeping and grasping—that none of us could account for and all of us recognized.

Maren stayed closest. She and Astrid developed a language of their own, half sign and half gesture and wholly unnecessary, because she could read him through touch and he, strangely, could almost read her back. Some evenings I would find them sitting side by side not speaking at all, her hand on his wrist, his eyes closed, and the fire popping, and I would slip back out before they noticed me, because some conversations belong to the people having them.

And I grew. That was the strangest thread of all. The court's claim had held me at eight years old for longer than any body should bear it, and now that the claim was broken I was unfolding like a letter finally allowed to open—taller each month, my voice settling, my hands suddenly too big for gestures I'd learned small. Dagny took to calling me "longshanks" with evident satisfaction. I outgrew two sweaters by midwinter. Maren altered them without comment, and I pretended not to notice her measuring me with her eyes, guessing at the woman I was becoming, preparing wool accordingly.

Through all of it, the giving continued.

I gave him his mother's face on a morning when frost feathered the window, and he wept without sound for an hour, and afterward thanked me with a steadiness that frightened me a little, because it was the steadiness of a man who has decided to carry something rather than be carried by it. I gave him the lullaby—the one from the fragment, the one I had followed through the fells like a rope thrown into dark water—and he sang it back to me that same evening, haltingly, getting the notes wrong in places, and neither of us corrected him.

I gave him Dagny's stew. I gave him the smell of Maren's garden in late summer. I gave him the view from the pass at dawn, the one he must have seen ten thousand times, and watched him receive it like a stranger seeing a country he'd dreamed of.

But I held some things back.

I held back the worst of the guilt—the nights he had lain awake cataloguing what he'd taken, the faces of the people he'd hollowed. He would meet that man eventually, or he wouldn't; it was not mine to force. I held back the gray-cloaked terror of the mine collapse. And I held back, longest of all, the sealed black door itself—not because I could return it, but because I wanted him to understand, before he ever asked about it, that a person is allowed to have a room in themselves that stays shut.

He asked about it anyway, in early spring.

We were splitting wood—I had grown strong enough to swing the axe properly, to his visible and slightly wounded surprise—and he stopped mid-swing and leaned on the handle and said, "There's a hole in me. At the center. I can feel the edges of it. Everyone pretends they can't see me feeling it."

The axe-head rested on the chopping block. Snow dripped from the eaves. Somewhere downslope, Maren's goats were complaining about the season.

"It's the one thing I can't give back," I said. "You took it from yourself. Before all of this. Whatever's behind that door, you decided—somebody decided—that it was safer locked. And the key went with it."

"Do you know what's behind it?"

I thought about the infant in the snow. About *take her instead*. About a young man making a bargain in a language older than kingdoms, trading a future he hadn't lived yet for a child he hadn't met. I thought about how the truth of it might break him, or might build him, or might do both at once, which is usually how the important truths work.

"I know some of it," I said. "Enough to tell you this: whatever you did, you did it to save someone. And the someone was me. And I'm asking you—" my voice did that awful cracking thing it still did, betraying the girl and the woman fighting over the same throat— "I'm asking you not to go looking yet. Let me finish giving you the rest first. Let yourself be a whole man before you go opening the one door that might undo you."

He studied me for a long time. Wind moved through the pines. And then he did something I had not seen him do: he smiled. Small, crooked, entirely unfamiliar to his own face—he actually raised a hand and touched his cheek as if checking whether the expression fit—but real.

"All right," he said. "But when I'm ready, you'll tell me. Promise?"

"Promise."

He picked the axe back up. Swung. The log split clean, and he looked at the two halves with the satisfaction of a man who has just proven something to his own arms.

Then he paused, and frowned, and said, "There's a door in Hollowbridge. Blue. With a brass knocker shaped like a fish. Why do I want to knock on it?"

I laughed—really laughed, the first time since the mountain—and called up to the window, "Dagny! He's remembering the fence!"

From inside came a grunt that translated, in Dagny's dialect, to *about bloody time*, and the sound of a kettle being put on, and Astrid stood there in the falling snow with his axe and his half-smile and his growing pile of returned yesterdays, looking at me with an expression I was only beginning to learn how to read, because it was the expression of a man starting—stone by chosen stone—to build himself a self worth remembering.

And far to the north, so faint that I might have imagined it, the thorn pendant on its nail by the door hummed once, softly, like something turning over in its sleep.
