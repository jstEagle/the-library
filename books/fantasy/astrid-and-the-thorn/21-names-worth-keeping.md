## 21. Names Worth Keeping

The memories came back the way snow comes back to a mountain—not all at once, but in slow weather, drifting down from a height he could no longer see.

Wren brought them in her cupped hands like water. Each morning she would sit across the breakfast table from him, older now by strange degrees, her true self surfacing through the borrowed body the way a stone surfaces through melting frost, and she would press two fingers to his wrist and give him back one year of himself.

She never gave him more than one at a time. "You have to choose," she said, in that voice that had learned human words the way roots learn the shape of stones—slowly, patiently, around every obstacle. "That's the whole point. They gave me everything at once and it nearly unmade me. You get to do it properly."

So Astrid chose.

He chose the smell of his mother's kitchen first: tallow smoke and rosemary, bread proving under a cloth, the particular creak of the third floorboard from the hearth. It arrived whole and warm and he sat with it for an hour before he trusted himself to stand. He had forgotten that forgetting had a texture—that his past had been not an absence but a smoothness, a wall he'd run his hands along so many times he'd stopped noticing it was there. Now the wall had windows in it, and light came through, and some of the light was terrible.

He did not choose everything.

* * *

Dagny found him on the porch on the third morning of the choosing, when the frost still silvered the railing and the pines below the cabin stood black against a bruise-colored dawn. She carried two cups of something bitter and steaming and pressed one into his hands without asking, which was how Dagny did most things.

"Well?" she said. "How much of you came home?"

"More than I expected." He turned the cup in his fingers. Steam curled up between them. "Less than I feared."

"Hm." She lowered herself onto the bench beside him with the groan of old joints and old grievances. "And what did you throw back?"

He was quiet a moment. Below them, mist moved through the valley like something breathing.

"There's a memory," he said. "Of the night I made the bargain. The envoy standing in my doorway with rain running off his gray hood, and me holding Wren—holding her *body*, the newborn, while her soul was already promised away—and I remember thinking I could still say no. I remember the exact shape of the door being open behind him." His thumb traced the cup's rim. "I keep it. I keep that one."

"You're a fool," Dagny said comfortably. "Most men would burn a memory like that."

"Most men didn't make the bargain." He drank. The bitterness grounded him. "If I burn it, then some other fool down the road gets to believe that selling a soul is something that happens to people. That it arrives like weather. It doesn't. It's a door, and someone opens it, and I opened mine." He looked at her. "But there are others, Dagny. There's a whole shelf of others. Small ones. Times I stole from people who couldn't afford the loss because the rent was due. A merchant whose daughter's face I took and sold twice. I don't—" He stopped. Started again. "I'm learning the difference between guilt that keeps me honest and guilt that just keeps me small. Some of it goes back in the river."

Dagny snorted into her cup. "Look at you. A season ago you wouldn't have kept a single thing, good or bad. Wouldn't have let yourself be anyone at all." She bumped his shoulder with hers, gently, the way she'd once taught him to pick a lock without breaking it. "Your mentor's proud of you. Don't let it go to your head; pride is just another kind of hoard."

* * *

Maren came next, as she always came, walking the high path from her cottage with her herb-basket on her hip and her silence moving around her like weather. She read the changes in him the moment she stepped through the door—her hands went first to his face, then to his chest, feeling for the shape of him beneath the skin the way she felt for fever beneath the skin of the sick.

Her fingers stilled over his heart. Her brow furrowed.

*Something's missing,* her hands said, spelling against his forearm in their private grammar. *There's a room in you with nothing in it.*

"I know," Astrid said aloud, though she felt the words as vibration more than heard them. "There's a hole where a happy memory used to be. I paid it to you, years ago. For stitching up my shoulder after Gray Cloaks at the ford."

Maren's expression went strange. She set down her basket, dug through it, and produced a small clay jar sealed with wax and wound about with twine—the kind of jar she used for things too delicate to dry, petals and seeds and the last warmth of summer. She pressed it into his hands and watched him with the fierce, level gaze she used on patients who were being idiots about their medicine.

Inside the jar, folded into a scrap of linen, lay a memory. He knew it before he touched it. He knew it the way you know your own handwriting.

*Maren,* her hands said, *kept it. All these years. She never used it. She said a healer who pays herself with her patient's joy is no healer at all.* Her mouth quirked. *She said to tell you: debts should hurt going out, or they aren't debts, they're thefts.*

Astrid held the little jar and could not speak.

When he finally unfolded the linen and let the memory settle back into the place it had been carved out of, it was a summer evening, years ago—he could not yet say how many—and he was laughing. Actually laughing, head thrown back, at something Vess had said, in a room that smelled of woodsmoke and someone frying onions, and Dagny was calling them all fools from the doorway, and he had thought, in the moment itself, with the memory still fresh and unguarded: *this. This is what I stole all those memories of, from other people. This is the thing they were all remembering.*

He wept over fried onions and woodsmoke, and Maren put the kettle on, and neither of them mentioned it again.

* * *

Vess arrived with the first real thaw, when the cart-track down to Hollowbridge turned to soup and the ravens started doing their spring accounting along the fences. She came up the path in mud-splashed boots with a ledger under one arm and an expression that meant business, which for Vess was indistinguishable from affection.

"The cabin," she said, dropping the ledger on his table hard enough to rattle the cups. "We need to talk about the cabin."

"What about it?"

"It's a fortress, Astrid. It's been a fortress since before I met you. One door, barred. Shutters that lock from inside. No guest room because guests are liabilities. You built yourself a tomb with a chimney." She looked around the main room, lips pursed, and he saw it suddenly as she saw it: the bolt-plates, the sight-lines, the hidden cache under the floorboards where he'd once kept stolen years in stoppered jars. "And now you're sitting in it with three people who refuse to leave, and a child upstairs who needs a proper bed, and you're going to tell me you've been thinking about it."

Astrid leaned back in his chair. Outside the window, Wren was in the garden teaching Maren the names of plants in the root-language, Maren's hands answering in shapes, two silences conversing fluently. Upstairs, faintly, Dagny was singing an old thieves' counting song to herself while she mended—badly—a pair of trousers that belonged to no one yet but would soon belong to whoever knocked.

"I've been thinking," Astrid said, "about taking the bolts off the shutters."

Vess blinked. Then she grinned, sharp as a blade being drawn. "And the cache?"

"Empty. Going to be a pantry." He tapped the table. "And I want a second door put in on the south side. Wide enough for a cart. If someone comes here running from something—if they come here the way I once came to nobody—I want them able to get in fast."

Vess studied him for a long moment, and whatever she saw, she wrote it down somewhere behind her eyes, in the ledger she kept of everyone's true accounts. "You know what you're building," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"A haven," Astrid said. "Or the start of one. Hollowbridge has a hundred people sleeping in doorways who ran from something—the court, the wars, the collectors. People whose names got taken, whose debts got sold. I spent twenty years making myself invisible so no one could find me. I'm done being invisible. I'd rather be findable and worth finding."

Vess was quiet. Then she opened her ledger, tore out a page, and began sketching door-hinges from memory, muttering measurements. "The carpenter in Hollowbridge owes me," she said. "And you owe me dinner. And if you think I'm letting you name this venture anything sentimental, you're wrong, because I've already chosen: it's called the Waystation, and the sign will be a lantern, and lanterns mean *open*, Astrid. Lanterns mean *come in*."

* * *

They took the bolts off the shutters on a bright morning in late spring, all four of them together, Dagny working the screwdriver with curses of great antiquity, Vess holding the ladder, Maren handing up tools, Wren perched on the roof ridge like a small dark bird supervising operations. When each shutter came free of its locks and swung open, sunlight fell into rooms that had been dark for twenty years, and dust rose in the beams like startled ghosts, and Astrid stood in the middle of his own house feeling it become a different house entirely.

That was when he did the last of the choosing.

He sat at the table that evening with the returned years spread out before him—not physically, though Wren had taught him to lay them out that way in his mind, a long table set with dishes, each one a meal he could eat or decline—and he went down the line like a man dividing an inheritance.

He kept his mother's kitchen. He kept the summer evening with onions and laughter. He kept the lullaby, though he no longer needed it to find Wren; he kept it because she asked him to sing it sometimes at night, not because she was lost, but because it was theirs. He kept the fragment of snow and the crying infant and his own voice saying *take her instead*—the first bargain, the one that had bought the newborn's body a chance at life even as it doomed her soul to the mountain. He kept it because it was the truth, and the truth was the foundation he'd chosen to build on.

He kept the fear from the collapsing mine, oddly—the raw animal terror he'd ripped from a gray cloak's mind and accidentally tasted himself. It reminded him what his gift cost other people when he used it carelessly.

And he released the rest. The petty thefts. The merchant's daughter, sold twice—he could not undo it, but he could stop carrying it like a stone in his chest; instead he wrote the man's name in the front of a new ledger, the Waystation's ledger, first entry: *a debt owed, to be repaid in kind, however long it takes.* The guilt that only kept him small went into the river below the falls, and he watched the current take it, and he did not fish it back out.

What remained was not innocence. He knew exactly what he was, what he'd done, what he'd opened. But what remained was *chosen*, and it fit him the way a well-made coat fits—room to move, weight in the right places.

Wren came down the stairs as he finished. She was taller than she'd been at midwinter, her true age surfacing faster now that the court's claim was broken, thirteen going on fourteen going on the girl she should have been all along. She looked at him sitting alone at the big table with the lamp low, and she didn't ask what he'd decided. She could feel it, probably, the way she felt growing things and old stone.

"Empty plate?" she said, meaning the released memories.

"Clean plate," he corrected. "Not empty. Ready to be filled."

She considered this with the gravity she gave all new words. Then she pulled out a chair across from him—the chair that had been Vess's at dinner, that had been Dagny's, that had been Maren's, that belonged to everyone and therefore to no one and therefore to whoever needed it—and sat down, and put her elbows on the table, and looked at him with eyes the color of deep water over old stone.

"Astrid," she said. Testing it. She'd known his name since the night he screamed it into the dark of the mountain, but she still said it sometimes just to hear it exist, a name that had been erased and earned its way back. "Tomorrow, when we open the sign. What if they knock at night? What if they knock scared?"

"Then we open scared," Astrid said. "It's not complicated, Wren. Someone knocks. We answer. Everything else is furniture."

Outside, the wind moved through the pines, carrying the smell of the high fells and the far-off sea-smell of Hollowbridge, and somewhere down in the valley a lantern was already lit in the window of the world, waiting for the lost to notice it.

They hung the sign three days later. Vess had carved it herself: a lantern, and beneath it, in letters she'd made Dagny check twice for spelling, the words *ALL WHO KNOCK*.

On the fourth night, with the dishes washed and Dagny cheating at cards and Maren asleep in the chair she refused to admit was hers, there came a knocking at the new south door—soft, hesitant, small.

Astrid set down his cards. Wren was already on her feet, the thorn pendant gleaming by the hearth where it hung, and together they went to answer.
