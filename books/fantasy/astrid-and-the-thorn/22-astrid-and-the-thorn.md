## 22. Astrid and the Thorn

### Astrid

The first snow of the season came down the mountain in long gray veils, and Astrid Joubert stood at his window watching it bury the road he had once worked for strangers' memories.

The cabin was larger now. He had built the extra room himself, two summers past, with Dagny shouting measurements from a chair she refused to leave and Maren correcting his joinery with sharp deaf-mute gestures that somehow conveyed more scorn than any words could have. The new room had four beds. Three were usually full.

He still kept the old rules. He did not steal from children. He did not keep what he took. But the rules had grown quieter over the years, the way a scar grows quiet—still there when the weather changed, but no longer the whole shape of him.

On the shelf above the hearth, between Maren's dried yarrow and a chipped clay figure Wren had made at eleven (a lopsided bird, or possibly a dog; they had never settled it), hung the thorn pendant. It had not hummed in years. It hung like any dead thing hangs, dark and curled in on itself, and most days Astrid forgot it was more than a keepsake.

Tonight he did not forget. Tonight, as the snow thickened and the fire cracked low, the pendant shivered on its nail.

Not humming. Trembling. The way a held breath trembles.

Astrid set down the cup he was drying and crossed the room slowly, the way you cross a room toward something you have dreamed about too many times to trust awake. Up close he could see the thorns had unfurled, just slightly, just enough—a black knot of them loosening after seven years of sleep.

Outside, down where the road disappeared into the white, someone was knocking.

No—not knocking. Scratching. Small, persistent, the sound of knuckles too cold to make a proper fist. And beneath it, threading through the wind, a voice speaking a language older than any kingdom, older than the mountains themselves, a language Astrid had spent seven years learning word by word from a girl who had arrived at this same door barefoot and unafraid.

His hand was on the latch before he remembered being afraid.

That was the difference, he thought. That was everything. Seven years ago a knock at his door had been a threat to be assessed, a transaction waiting to happen, a stranger carrying either coin or danger. Now it was only a knock. Only someone cold. Only someone lost.

He opened the door without hesitation.

The child on his step could not have been more than six. Snow had gotten into her dark hair and melted there, and her feet—bare, blue at the toes, unafraid even now—were planted on his threshold as if she had walked out of the mountain itself. She looked up at him with eyes the color of river ice, and she spoke again: a rush of root-words and stone-words, the old tongue pouring out of her like water finding its ancient channel.

Behind him, he heard Wren's chair scrape back. Heard her cross the room fast. Felt her arrive at his shoulder, twenty years old now and tall, her breath catching once—only once—and then steadying.

"Come in," Astrid said, in the old tongue, in the plain tongue, in whatever language a cold child needed. "You're welcome here."

The girl blinked up at him. Snow caught in her lashes.

"No bargain?" she asked, in accented but careful common speech—the words clearly learned somewhere, carried somewhere, the way a seed is carried in a bird's crop. "No price?"

Astrid crouched down to her level. Behind him the pendant gave one soft chime, like a bell heard through deep water, and neither of them turned to look.

"No bargain," he said. "Just bread."

### Wren

Wren had learned, over seven years, to hear the world the way she had first learned to feel it—as growing things, as old stone, as slow green patience under everything. The cabin spoke to her constantly: the joists settling their winter complaints, the fire eating its supper of pine, the bread dough rising in its bowl by the stove with a small contented hum she alone could hear.

But the child on the doorstep rang like a struck bell.

She was kin. Not by blood—Wren had no blood-kin left, having traded hers away before she was born—but by the older kindred, the kind that ran through thorn and root and the deep places where the court's power still pooled like groundwater. This child had come out of the mountain. Wren knew it the way she knew rain coming: in her teeth, in the soles of her feet, in the pendant's sudden waking above the hearth.

And she knew something else, watching Astrid crouch in the doorway with snow swirling past him into the warm room, watching him hold out his hands to a stranger without counting the cost first.

Seven years ago, a man had stood at this same door and made a bargain that cost him everything he was. Wren had screamed his name as the forgetting took him mid-embrace, had felt his memories pour out of him like light from a broken jar, had carried his blank-eyed body up out of the mountain on her own back because no one else could and because grief makes oxen of us all.

She had given pieces back since then. Not all. Never all. Some things he was better off without—the vault of guilt, the ledger of stolen years, the memory of his own voice saying *take her instead* over a crying infant in the snow. She had asked him, once, whether he wanted that one back, and he had thought for a long time and said no, and then said yes, and then said no again, and finally said: *let me keep the part where I chose right. Let the rest go.*

So she had. That was the arrangement they had built together, the two of them, season by season: she returned what served him, and released what didn't, and between them they assembled a man who was not the man who had sold her soul, and not a stranger either, but someone new—someone who had chosen, freely and with full knowledge, to be Astrid Joubert.

Now she watched that man lift a freezing child over the threshold as though she weighed nothing, and she felt the oldest thing in her—the thing that had fallen once and been caught by hands that then let go—finally, fully, let go of its anger.

"Maren will want the hot stones," she said, already moving. "Dagny will want to interrogate her. Vess will want to know who sent her." She smiled despite herself. "In that order."

The child, wrapped in Astrid's own cloak, looked around the great room—at the four beds visible through the open door, at the herbs hanging from the beams, at the lopsided clay bird on its shelf of honor—and asked, in the old tongue:

"Is this a court?"

Wren laughed. She couldn't help it. The sound came out of her bright and startled, and the fire flared a little, the way it always did when she laughed, and outside the snow went on falling on a world that had, against all odds and all bargains, let them keep each other.

"No," she said, kneeling to the child's level, mirroring Astrid without meaning to. "It's better than a court. It's a home. Nobody here owns anybody."

The child considered this with the gravity of the very young and the very old, which are the same gravity.

"Then why does the door have a lock?"

"To keep the weather honest," said Dagny's voice from the stair, gravelly and half-asleep. The old thief descended with a candle, one eye open, her gray braid coming loose. She looked at the child for a long moment, and Wren saw her do the arithmetic she always did—value, risk, exit routes—and then saw her put all of it aside, as she had learned to do, as they had all learned to do, one kindness at a time.

"Well," Dagny said. "She'll want stew. They always want stew."

### Astrid

Later, when the child slept in the bed nearest the fire—Maren had seen to her feet with hot stones and yarrow salve, clucking her silent disapproval at whoever had let a little one walk the high passes barefoot—Astrid sat across from Wren at the table and told her what he had felt at the door.

"The pendant woke before I heard her. Like it was expecting her."

"It was." Wren turned her cup slowly on the wood. Firelight moved in her eyes. "I've been dreaming about the mountain again. Not the bad dreams. The other ones—the ones where the deep halls are emptying out. Court by court, soul by soul, walking up into the light." She looked up. "I think the Thorn Court is dying, Astrid. Or changing. Or maybe both—I don't think it knows the difference. And everything it ever claimed is being let go. Everything it ever kept."

"Including her."

"Including her." Wren glanced toward the sleeping child, whose face had gone slack and peaceful in the way of children who have finally stopped running. "She fell out of the mountain the way a seed falls out of a split pod. No claim left on her. No debt. Just—" Wren's voice caught, very slightly, on the word. "Just lost."

Astrid nodded slowly. Outside, the storm pressed against the shutters, and inside the stew pot breathed its steam, and the pendant hung above the hearth doing something it had never done in all the years Wren had owned it.

It was blooming.

They both saw it at the same moment. One of the black thorns had split along its length, and from the split came a single white flower, small and perfect and impossible, opening petal by petal in the warmth of the room as though it had been waiting seven years for exactly this temperature, exactly this company, exactly this night.

Neither of them spoke for a while.

"Do you remember," Wren said at last, "what you told me the first time I asked you why you helped me? Before we knew anything? Before the fragments, before the court, before any of it?"

"You said I'd regret it."

"You said—" she laughed, wet-eyed, "—you said, *because nobody should be a transaction.*"

"I was smarter before I got happy," Astrid said. "Happiness has made me simple."

"Happiness has made you *right*." She reached across the table and took his hand—the hand that had siphoned ten thousand memories, the hand that had signed a bargain in a script he copied without knowing why, the hand that had held her newborn body in the snow and chosen, and chosen, and chosen again. "There's going to be more of them, you know. As the courts let go. Children falling out of old bargains all across the mountains, all across everywhere. Looking for doors that don't have prices on them."

"Then we'd better build more rooms."

"Dagny will want a cut of the construction budget."

"Dagny will get a cut of the stew and like it."

They sat together in the lamplight, an old thief's household breathing around them—Maren's soft snoring from the herb room, Dagny's candle guttering on the stair where she'd fallen asleep mid-vigil, Vess's latest map pinned to the wall with routes marked in three colors of ink—and above the hearth the white flower opened another petal, and another, patient as spring.

Astrid found he was not afraid. That was the strangest gift of all, the one he had never managed to steal and never needed to: that a knock at the door could simply be a knock, and an open door could simply be a door, and a man could stand in the space between them and be, at last, entirely himself.

Upstairs, in the loft, the child murmured in her sleep—in the old tongue, in root-words and stone-words—and the sound drifted down through the floorboards like something planted, like something beginning to grow.

### Wren

Spring came early that year, the way it does when the ground has decided to forgive.

Wren walked the high fells with the child—Lark, they called her now, because she sang in a language no living tongue should know and because names, Wren had learned, should be given freely or not at all. Lark was growing into her power the way Wren had once grown into hers: sideways, suddenly, in bursts. Last week she had made the garden peas climb a week early just by humming at them. Maren had pretended to be annoyed and had cried silently into her herbs for an hour.

Below them, the cabin smoked peaceably. Dagny was on the porch arguing with a supply cart driver about the price of salt, which was how Dagny expressed affection. Vess had ridden out at dawn with maps and would return in three days with news and no explanation. The extra rooms were full—two of them, now, with others who had wandered down out of the changed mountains: a boy who spoke only in birdsong, an old man who had been a pawn in a bargain for sixty years and was learning, bewildered, to want things.

Wren sat down on a warm stone and Lark folded beside her, and for a while they just breathed the thin bright air together, kin of the thorn, daughters of the deep places, free.

"Do you still miss it?" Lark asked, in the old tongue. "The mountain. The court. What you were."

Wren thought about it honestly, because Lark deserved honesty.

"I miss the stone," she said. "Stone doesn't lie and doesn't hurry. But I don't miss being kept." She picked a stem of new grass and twirled it. "Do you know what I figured out? The court thought keeping things made them precious. Sealed halls, locked vaults, souls on shelves. But nothing kept is worth anything. It's only worth something when it can leave and comes back anyway." She nodded down at the cabin, at the smoke, at the small figures on the porch. "Like them. Like us."

Lark considered this with her grave old-young eyes. Then she reached into the collar of her shirt and drew out the pendant—Wren's pendant, given freely last autumn, thorn-black and quiet—and held it out on her palm.

Above the hearth, Wren knew, its twin was blooming white. Here on the fells, in the open sun, this one stirred too, unfurling a single bud like a held breath finally let go.

"They're waking up everywhere," Lark said. "All the thorns. All the old claims. They're turning into something else." She looked up. "What are they turning into?"

Wren watched the bud open—one white petal, then another, small and perfect and absurd against the black—and she thought of a man who had erased his own name to save a stranger's child, and of friends who had refused to let him be a stranger, and of a door opened without hesitation in the middle of a storm, and of every kindness she had forgotten she'd received and every one she'd learned to give.

"Invitations," she said.

Far below, faint on the wind, someone was singing the lullaby—their lullaby, snow and cradle and take-her-instead, transposed now into something warmer, something with an ending. Astrid's voice, rough and off-key and entirely his own, drifting up the fells to call them home.

Lark stood, and pulled Wren up, and together they started down through the new grass toward the smoke and the song and the open door—

—and behind them, on the warm stone where they had sat, the wild thorns that ringed the high fells bloomed white, all at once, as far as the eye could see.
