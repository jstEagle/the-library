## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width by the time Astrid Vane reached the bottom of the day's column, and the wax smell had gone sweet and heavy in the counting room, the way it always did when she worked past midnight. She did not mind. The guild's ledgers preferred to be read at night. She was fairly sure of this, though she would never have said so aloud — that the books of the Guild of Impossible Things had preferences, moods, small appetites. But she had kept their accounts for six years now, and six years is long enough to learn that a ledger which records impossible things must itself be a little impossible.

She dipped her pen. The ink was black as a closed eye.

*One debt of silence, paid in full — three coins and a name forgotten.*

*One promise of rain, honored late — penalty assessed, one season of sorrow, remitted for good service.*

*One shadow, returned to its owner, slightly stretched — no charge.*

Her hand moved with the ease of long habit. This was the work nobody envied her and everybody needed: balancing the accounts of bargains that should never have been struck. A farmer who had sold his grief to buy his son back from drowning. A widow who had pawned the memory of her wedding day to pay off a curse laid on her house. A soldier who traded ten years of his life for one perfect hour. The guild took these things in, weighed them, lent them out again at interest, and Astrid made sure the numbers came home.

It was honest work, if you squinted. It was also, she knew better than anyone, theft with excellent bookkeeping.

Because at night — not every night, but enough nights — Astrid went down into the city where the desperate lived, and she stole.

Not coins. Not silver. Memories. They were easier to lift than purses, once you knew the trick of it: a touch to the temple, a whispered word from a language she'd bought rather than learned, and a person's past slid loose like a pearl from a broken string. She took only from those who had too much memory to carry anyway — the grieving, the guilty, the haunted — and she told herself, every time, that she was doing them a kindness. Some nights she almost believed it.

The memories went into vials, and the vials went into a locked case beneath her floorboards, and the case was her real fortune, though she never spent it. You could sell memories back to the guild, or trade them in the night markets along the Sump where people bought other lives to escape their own. Astrid did neither. She kept hers the way a miser keeps gold: not to use, but to count.

Except for one vial. That one never left her sleeve.

She touched it now, absently, through the linen — a small glass philter no longer than her thumb, stoppered with red wax, warm from her skin. Her childhood. What remained of it. She had sold the whole of it at fourteen to a broker in the Salt Quarter to pay her mother's debts, and she had stolen a single measure of it back five years later, off a fat merchant who'd bought it thinking it would make him feel young again. It hadn't. It had made him weep into his wine and demand a refund, and Astrid had been waiting outside his door to oblige him.

She had never dared open it. She wasn't sure what frightened her more: that the memories inside would mean nothing to her now, or that they would mean everything.

The candle guttered. Astrid flexed her writing hand, blew a strand of hair from her eyes, and turned to the last page of the day's ledger — the old one, the deep one, bound in leather so dark it was nearly black and stamped with a sigil she had never been able to make out. This was the master ledger, the one the guildmasters consulted and she merely maintained. Most of its entries were centuries old. Most of them were written in hands long dead. And most of them, she had learned early on, were not to be read closely, because the guild paid her to keep accounts, not to ask what the accounts were *for*.

But tonight the master ledger lay open where she had left it, and tonight — she noticed it the way you notice a wrong note in a familiar song — there was a new entry.

New ink. Fresh. In handwriting that was not hers.

Astrid sat very still. No one else used this room after dark. The door was locked; she held the only key. Yet there the entry sat, in a spidery antique script, the letters tall and narrow like things standing in a corridor:

*One king's shadow, overdue.*

She read it twice. Three times. Her blood, when it finally remembered how to move, went cold all at once, the way water goes cold in a well.

There was no date. There was no debtor named. There was no amount owed, no terms, no interest accrued. Every entry she had ever recorded — every impossible bargain the guild had ever brokered — came with its particulars, because an account without particulars is not an account; it is a threat. But this line stood alone, naked, like a knife left on a table.

And beneath it, smaller, in the same dead hand, as if the writer had paused and then could not help himself:

*Interest compounding since the Founding. Payment demanded. Payment refused. Payment—*

The sentence stopped. The rest of the page was blank, but the blankness had a texture to it, a pressure, like the quiet in a house where something is holding its breath.

Astrid became aware that she had risen from her chair. She did not remember standing. The candle flame leaned toward the ledger, though there was no draft, and the shadows of the shelves swung slowly around the room like the hands of a clock deciding whether to keep time at all.

*A king's shadow.* There was only one king. There had been only one king for four hundred years — Aldric the Constant, Aldric the Undying, blessed be his reign, may it never end. His face was on every coin in her purse. His statue stood in every square in the capital, bronze and serene, gazing out over a city that loved him the way you love a mountain: without ever wondering what was underneath.

His shadow was overdue.

She thought: *it's a figure of speech. Ledgers are full of them. Shadow-debts, ghost-accounts, names for money owed in ways that aren't money.* She thought it firmly, the way you talk yourself across a high bridge.

Then she looked down at the page again, and the entry had changed.

Not much. One word. Where before it had read *Payment refused*, it now read:

*Payment refused thrice.*

Astrid's mouth went dry. She had balanced this ledger for six years. She knew its habits the way a sailor knows a ship — every creak, every settle, every mood of the wood. Ledgers did not edit themselves. Except that everything in this building was, by definition, impossible, and impossibility, she had long suspected, did not sleep so soundly as the guildmasters pretended.

She pressed her palm flat against the page, half to steady herself, half — she admitted it only to herself — to feel whether the paper was warm. It was cool. Cool and smooth and patient, like the surface of a pond at dusk, and she snatched her hand back with the sudden animal certainty that if she had held it there a moment longer, something on the other side might have held on.

"Right," she said aloud, to the empty room, because saying things aloud made them ordinary. "Right."

She copied the entry into her working book — a clerk's reflex, six years deep, and only afterward did she wonder why she'd done it, why some part of her had wanted the words in her own hand, somewhere safe, somewhere the ledger couldn't take them back. *One king's shadow, overdue.* Her copy sat on the page looking small and frightened, like a bird that had flown indoors.

She closed the master ledger. Its covers met with a sound like a door shutting in another room.

Outside the counting room's high window, the capital lay under a thin fall of snow, and above the rooftops the palace rose against the stars, its towers lit gold, its banners hanging still. Somewhere up there, King Aldric the Constant was either sleeping or not sleeping, and his shadow — whatever that meant, whatever a thing like that could owe and to whom — was four hundred years in arrears.

Astrid rolled the ledger shut, banked the candle, and climbed the stairs to her narrow room above the counting floor. She checked the lock twice. She checked the floorboard case a third time, counting vials by touch in the dark: thirty-one, thirty-two, thirty-three. All present. All quiet.

Only the little one in her sleeve was warm.

She lay down without undressing, the way she did on bad nights, and stared at the ceiling until the grey light began to come. Sleep took her finally in shallow pulls, and in the borderland between sleeping and waking she dreamed — not a scene, nothing so generous — just a voice, vast and dry as wind through a keyhole, reciting figures. Numbers going backward. A debt being counted down toward zero, or toward something worse than zero.

She woke with the word *overdue* behind her teeth and the taste of ash in her mouth.

* * *

Three days later, on a morning of hard frost, the knock came.

Astrid was at her desk reconciling a fortnight's worth of borrowed luck — the guild lent luck the way banks lent coin, and someone in the Fishmarket Ward had been spending it fast — when the sound reached her through the floor: a knocking at the guild's great door. Slow. Deliberate. Wrong.

Not the brisk triple rap of a client. Not the official boom of the watch. This was a child's knock — small fist, flat of the hand, striking the wood in a rhythm that repeated, and repeated, and did not vary, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

She was halfway down the stairs when the ledgers began to open.

Every one of them. She heard it before she understood it — the soft sigh of covers lifting, pages turning on their own in the archive room, in the vault, in the masters' private library behind the iron gate. Dozens of books, breathing open at once, and beneath the knocking a voice, high and thin and young, speaking words that were not words in any tongue Astrid knew, and yet her bones understood every syllable.

The language was older than the ledgers. Older, said something cold at the base of her skull, than the kingdoms. Older than the king.

By the time she reached the entrance hall, the guildmaster — Master Corvane, grey-robed, grey-eyed, a man assembled entirely from patience — was already there, flanked by two of the wardens, staring at the door as if it might bite. Beyond it, visible through the frosted glass in a smudge of brown and small, stood a shape no bigger than a child.

"Open it," Astrid said.

"No," said Corvane, without turning his head.

"It's a child. Alone. In this cold."

"It is *knocking in the dead tongue*, Mistress Vane." His voice stayed level, but she saw his hands, folded into his sleeves, go white at the knuckles. "Nothing that speaks that language knocks. Things that speak that language *arrive*. Lock the inner doors. Wake the archivist. And fetch me the black key."

Behind him, down the corridor, the ledgers were still opening, page after page, hundreds of years of impossible accounts turning as if searching for something — or as if standing at attention.

And through the frosted glass, through the knocking, through the old words falling like stones into deep water, Astrid felt it: a thread pulled taut between her chest and the small shape on the step. Recognition. Not of a face she knew — she had never seen the child before in her life.

Of eyes she had.

They were her own eyes, looking back at her out of a stranger's face, and they were afraid, and they had come here, to this door, in the oldest language in the world, to find her.

Corvane said, "Mistress Vane. The black key. *Now.*"

Astrid looked at the door, and at the wardens, and at the guildmaster's patient grey certainty — and she turned, and walked toward the inner doors, and did not slow her steps, and did not reach for any key at all.
