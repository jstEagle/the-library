## 2. A Knock in the Dead Tongue

Astrid had stolen memories from a hundred desperate people, and she knew what fear looked like when it decided to become something else. It became stillness. It became a woman walking very slowly toward an inner door with her hands loose at her sides and her mind running like a thief through burning streets.

Behind her, Corvane was saying something about the black key again. His voice had the particular patience of a man who has never once been disobeyed in his own guildhall, and that patience was more frightening than shouting would have been.

She reached the inner doors. She did not open them.

Instead she turned — a small pivot, casual as adjusting a ledger page — so that she stood between Corvane's wardens and the child at the threshold, and she said, "Guildmaster, with respect: no one is locking anyone away until I've balanced this account."

"Balanced it," Corvane repeated. He came down the three steps of the dais with his grey robes whispering, his face arranged into mildness. "Mistress Vane. The girl spoke a word that opened every ledger in this building. Do you understand what that means?"

"I understand that ledgers are meant to be read."

"I understand," he continued, as if she hadn't spoken, "that there are entries in those books which have slept for four hundred years, and that a child who can wake them is either a catastrophe or a key. Either way, she goes below, behind iron, until the masters convene." He lifted two fingers, and the wardens moved.

Astrid had perhaps three seconds. She spent them looking at the child.

The girl stood in the doorway with mud to her knees and river-weed in her hair, small enough that the doorframe made her look like a painting of a child rather than one. She was shivering. She was also, Astrid noticed with a lurch, standing exactly the way Astrid herself stood when she was deciding whether to run — weight on the balls of her feet, chin down, eyes doing arithmetic on the exits.

And the eyes. The eyes were hers. Not similar. *Hers* — the same grey-green, the same faint ring of darker color at the iris's edge, the same crooked catchlight. She had seen them last night in her own washbasin, and now they were looking out of a stranger's face with an expression Astrid recognized because she had worn it for twenty years: *I will bite before I beg.*

The wardens were six paces away.

Astrid did the only thing her body seemed willing to do. She crossed the hall, knelt in front of the child, and held out her hand — palm up, empty, the way you offered your hand to something wild.

"Sef," the girl had not said yet. What she had said, in that language older than any kingdom, was a string of syllables that had gone through Astrid like a fishhook. But when Astrid's palm opened between them, the girl looked at it, and then at her, and said — in accented, ragged, unmistakably learned trade-tongue:

"You have my page."

* * *

They let Astrid take her to the washroom. This was Corvane's first mistake, though he would not think of it as one; a guildhall kept its accounts clean, and a filthy child was an affront to the books. Two wardens followed at a distance, which suited Astrid fine. Distance was a currency she could spend.

The washroom was a stone room with a copper basin and one high window, built for scrubbing ink off clerks who'd fallen asleep in their work. Astrid pumped water into the basin and wet a cloth, and the child watched her the whole time with the unblinking attention of a bird deciding whether a hand held food or a snare.

"What's your name?" Astrid asked.

The girl considered. Then she said a word — the same word from the doorway, the hook-word — and it came out of her mouth like something being returned after long storage. Astrid felt it in her teeth. Behind the wall, faintly, she heard paper rustle. Every ledger in the building, opening itself again, pages turning like a wind moving through a forest of accounts.

"Stop that," Astrid whispered, absurdly.

"I can't." The girl's trade-tongue was improving even as she spoke, words assembling themselves out of the air, out of Astrid's own speech, the way a memory-thief assembled a stranger's childhood out of fragments. "It listens when I talk. It always listened. That's why they hid me."

"Who hid you?"

"The ones who wrote me." The girl touched her own chest, over the sternum, the way you'd touch a book's spine. "I am the last page. They wrote everything else first, and me last, so someone would remember how it ends."

Astrid's hands went still in the water. Twenty years of thieving had taught her to hear the shape of a lie, and this wasn't one; it was worse. It was the flat truth of a child reciting something she'd been told so often it had stopped meaning anything.

"My name," the girl said carefully, tasting each syllable, "is not Sef. Sef is the word for *remainder*. For what is left over when the sum is done." Her eyes — Astrid's eyes — came up. "You know the word. You've written it ten thousand times."

She had. In the margin of every balanced ledger, in the little column where the guild recorded what could not be collected: *séf*, remainder, the residue of impossible debts. She had never once wondered why the guild used a dead tongue for its margins.

Outside the door, a warden shifted his weight. Astrid heard the creak of his belt, the soft clink of the iron chain they'd brought for her — for a child half her size — and something in her chest closed like a fist.

"Wash," she said aloud, briskly, bookkeeper-voiced. "All of it. There's a shift in the cupboard, wool, it'll be too big but it's warm. When you're done, don't come out. Whatever you hear, don't come out."

The girl tilted her head. "What will I hear?"

"Me," Astrid said, "being reasonable."

* * *

Corvane received her back in the great hall, where the ledgers lay open on their lecterns like a congregation of sleeping birds, all of them awake now, all of them turned to pages no clerk had set. He was reading one. Astrid watched his finger stop on a line and lift, the way a man's finger lifts from a stove.

"Mistress Vane," he said, without looking up. "Tell me what you saw in the vault last month."

"I saw the vault."

"You saw the ledger of the crown's debts. You saw the entry dated in the reign of Aldric the First. One king's shadow, overdue." Now he looked up, and his mildness was gone, and underneath it was something old and hungry and careful. "You signed the acknowledgment page. Your hand is on that book, Astrid. Which means the girl's word woke *you* along with everything else."

"I'm awake," Astrid agreed. "That's generally considered a virtue in a bookkeeper."

"Do not fence with me." He descended the steps again. Up close, Corvane smelled of dust and lamp oil and, beneath both, of cold stone — the smell of the deep shelves where the guild kept the accounts it did not show its clerks. "Four hundred years ago, the crown borrowed something it could not repay, and the guild has carried that debt ever since, feeding it, keeping it quiet, keeping the interest paid. Do you imagine the masters will permit a mud-caked child to call it due? The masters convened an hour ago. The vote was unanimous. The bargain will be renewed — with a new signatory, a new vessel, a fresh page. The girl will be that page. It is already decided."

"Then it isn't a bargain," Astrid said. "It's just theft with better handwriting."

"Yes," Corvane said, and smiled, and the smile was the worst thing she'd seen all day. "Now you understand the guild."

The wardens came for the washroom door. Astrid heard the key turn — the black key, the one she'd refused to fetch, fetched by other hands — and she heard the door open onto silence, and then a warden's voice, confused: "She's not here. There's a window. It's — Guildmaster, the window's barred, it's been barred for thirty years—"

But the bars were bent outward, folded back like the petals of a flower, and the mud on the sill was already drying in the evening air.

Corvane turned to Astrid very slowly.

"She climbed past thirty-year-old wards," he said, "that you walked her past this afternoon. Interesting, that. Interesting, that the wards knew her. Interesting, that *you* knew her." He drew a breath, and when he spoke again his voice was almost gentle, which was how Astrid knew she had perhaps a minute left in this building as a free woman. "Where would she go, Mistress Vane? A child alone in the capital at nightfall. Where does a stray go?"

Astrid thought of the girl's eyes. Of her own eyes, in a washbasin, in a stranger's face. Of the vial of sold childhood hidden in her sleeve, and the entry she'd found a week ago — *one king's shadow, overdue* — and the sudden, terrible arithmetic assembling itself behind her ribs: a debt four centuries old, a family that had paid it once in memories, a child who called herself the last page.

Strays, she thought, go where they were fed.

"There's a soup kitchen on Candlemaker's Row," she said. "Run by the Sisters of the Quiet Hand. They feed anyone. I'd start there."

Corvane studied her for a long moment. Then he nodded to the wardens, and two of them took her arms, not roughly, which was somehow more insulting than roughness would have been.

"Lock her in the counting room," he said. "She can balance the night's entries while we consider her position. And send riders to Candlemaker's Row."

* * *

The counting room was a cage of shelves and lamplight, and Astrid was alone in it for exactly as long as it took the wardens' boots to fade down the corridor.

Then the high window — barred, warded, thirty years undisturbed — swung inward on hinges that had not existed an hour ago, and a small mud-streaked figure dropped to the floor among the ledgers, breathing hard, weed still in her hair.

"How," Astrid began.

"The building let me in." The girl shrugged, as if this explained anything. "It knows my handwriting. Everything in it does." She looked around the counting room with quick, hungry attention, and her gaze snagged on the lectern where Astrid's own working ledger lay open — and she went to it, and put one dirty finger on the margin, on the little column of dead-tongue notation, and read it aloud.

Every shelf in the room shivered. Ink rose off a dozen pages like startled birds and resettled into new figures. Somewhere below them, Astrid was certain, the vault ledger had just turned itself to a fresh page.

"Stop," Astrid whispered again, and this time the girl did, and looked up at her with those stolen eyes.

"They're coming for me," the girl said. "The thing wearing the king. I felt it wake when I knocked. It's been asleep inside him for a long time and my voice is the alarm bell." She swallowed. "I need the person who keeps the accounts. I need you to tell me what I owe."

"You don't owe anything," Astrid said. "You're a child."

"No." The girl shook her head, patient, terrible. "I'm a page. And pages exist to be paid." She took Astrid's hand — small fingers, mud under the nails, gripping hard. "Read me, bookkeeper. Before they lock you away too. Read me, and tell me how it ends."

Below them, faint through the floor, Astrid heard the front doors of the guildhall open, and a voice that was two voices at once ask, very politely, for the return of what belonged to the crown.
