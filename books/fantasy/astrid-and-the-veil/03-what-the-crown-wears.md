## 3. What the Crown Wears

The palace of Vael Corrin had a hundred ways to kill a thief, and Isandro Whitlock had spent three weeks learning every one of them.

Not the obvious killers — the guard rotations he'd memorized by bribing a laundry girl with silver and flattery, the dogs he'd quieted with drugged meat tossed over the kennel wall on moonless nights. It was the subtler deaths that worried him: the gallery on the third floor where portraits' eyes seemed to track you, the fountain in the east court that whispered your name if you leaned too close to the water, the door in the cellar that no servant would acknowledge existed and which all of them, somehow, avoided without ever appearing to.

He'd been hired by a man who smelled of ink and old coins, who had paid half up front and named his target with a delicacy that should have warned Isandro off entirely. *The king's private study. Nothing else. Bring me what he keeps locked in the writing desk — papers only, nothing touched by fire.* And then, when Isandro had asked the obvious question — why not simply buy such things from a courtier? — the ink-smelling man had smiled like a crack in porcelain and said: *Because no one who serves the crown can look at it directly anymore.*

Isandro had laughed at that. He was not laughing now.

He crouched in the dark behind a tapestry depicting the Founding — Aldric's ancestor driving back the sea with a raised hand — and watched the king's study door through the gap where the weave had worn thin. Midnight had come and gone. The guards outside the study had changed twice, both times falling into the same loose-limbed trance mid-stride, as though someone had reached into their heads and turned down the wicks of them. They stood at their posts now, breathing slow, eyes open and seeing nothing.

That was wrong. Everything about tonight was wrong, and Isandro's professional instinct — the animal sense that had kept him alive through eleven years of other people's locks — was screaming at him to go home, collect his partial payment, and find a new city.

Instead he waited, because the writing desk was twenty feet away, and because Isandro Whitlock had never once left a job unfinished, and because some cold, curious part of him wanted to see what kind of king needed his own guards asleep.

* * *

The door opened without sound.

Aldric came in alone — no crown, no escort, dressed in a plain nightrobe the color of wet ash. In the candlelight he looked older than his coin-portraits, thinner, the way a house looks after the furniture has been carried out of it. He crossed the room to the window and stood looking out over the sleeping capital for a long time, and Isandro, pressed into the tapestry's shadow, felt something he had no name for yet: a wrongness in the air, like the pressure change before a storm, like the hush in a room where someone has just stopped breathing.

Then the king spoke.

"Is it done?" Aldric said, in a voice like dry paper.

And before the words had finished leaving his mouth, another voice answered from the same throat — deeper, vast, resonant the way a cavern is resonant, a voice that made the candle flames lean toward it and the window glass shiver in its leaded frame.

"It is never done," said the second voice. "It is only *kept*."

Isandro's mouth went dry. The two voices had come from one body, one breath, overlapping so precisely that for a moment he thought he was hearing an echo — but echoes don't answer, and echoes don't carry *weight*. The second voice pressed against his skull like deep water. Behind the tapestry, Isandro discovered he was gripping his own wrist hard enough to bruise.

"You promised me rest," the paper voice said. Aldric's voice. The king's voice, worn thin as a psalter read ten thousand times. "You said a century. You said the borders would hold and I could sleep."

"I said the debt would hold." The vast voice did not seem cruel. That was the worst of it — it was patient, enormous, indifferent as arithmetic. "Sleep is not yours to spend, little king. You sold your name. A name is a door, and I have lived behind it longer than you have lived inside yourself."

"And my heir—"

"Will inherit the account. As you inherited it. As your father's father did, when he first signed."

Isandro saw it then — saw the king's shoulders drop, saw the whole architecture of the man sag like a puppet whose strings had briefly slackened. And in the slackness, in the terrible pause before the strings drew taut again, Aldric whispered something so quiet Isandro had to strain to catch it:

"There is a page. There has to be a page. Every contract has its last page."

The silence that followed lasted long enough for Isandro's heart to beat eleven times. He counted. Counting kept him sane.

"The ledger remembers," the Veil said at last — and Isandro did not know how he knew that name, only that the word arrived in his mind already labeled, the way you know a smell from childhood. "Somewhere in your grey city walks a page that was never spent. When I find it, the bargain renews itself, and you may stop hoping, Aldric. Hope is a tax you were never asked to pay."

The king straightened. The strings drew taut. And when he spoke again it was with one voice only, warm and kingly and utterly false, addressing the empty room as though greeting a courtier: "Yes. Of course. All is well. All is always well."

Isandro understood, with a lurch, that the performance was not for him. It was habit. The king rehearsed being a king the way a prisoner rehearses walking, so the chains won't show.

* * *

He should have run then. Any sensible thief would have run.

But the king moved to the writing desk — the desk, the very desk, iron-banded oak with a lock shaped like a coiled serpent — and opened it with a key he drew from his own mouth, from under his tongue, where no man keeps a key unless he trusts no lock and no hiding place and no one alive.

Isandro watched Aldric lift out a sheaf of papers bound in black ribbon, and watched him hold them over the candle flame, and understood in one white-hot flash of clarity what the ink-smelling man had meant by *nothing touched by fire.*

The king was going to burn them. Tonight. Whatever those pages were, whatever contract or confession or curse they held, Aldric had found a moment of slackness in his own strings and was spending it on destruction.

Later, Isandro would never be able to explain what he did next except as theft — pure, reflexive, eleven years of instinct overriding every survival signal his body sent. As the first corner of the topmost page caught light, he moved. Out of the tapestry's shadow, across the carpet in four silent strides, and his hand shot past the king's shoulder into the flame and snatched the burning page free, crushing the fire dead between his palm and his chest.

Aldric turned.

For one heartbeat the king's eyes were the king's eyes — blue, human, and so full of naked, desperate hope that Isandro felt it like a blade between his ribs. *Help me,* those eyes said, in the wordless language of drowning men.

Then the strings pulled taut. The hope shuttered closed like a shop at dusk, and the eyes that regarded Isandro Whitlock across eighteen inches of candlelit air were old and dark and patient, and they were not alone in there.

"Thief," the Veil said, through the king's smiling mouth. It did not raise its voice. It didn't need to; the word arrived in Isandro's bones. "How interesting. You are carrying something of mine."

Isandro ran.

He went through the window — third floor, rose trellis, a descent he'd planned and rehearsed until his hands knew it blind — with the page crushed against his burned palm and the vast voice following him not through the air but *inside his skull*, unhurried, almost amused:

*Keep it, then. Carry it far. Every stolen thing finds its way back to its owner, little thief. It is the only law I have never once seen broken.*

* * *

He surfaced in the Warrens an hour before dawn, bleeding from the trellis, shaking so badly he had to sit down in a doorway and put his head between his knees like a drunkard. The Warrens didn't notice. The Warrens had seen worse.

Only when the shaking slowed did he unclench his fist.

The page was scorched at one corner, curled like a dead leaf, but whole. By the grey light leaking under the doorway he read it — and read it again, because the first reading refused to be true.

It was a contract. Not in any legal hand — in a careful, looping script that looked almost like music notation, each clause set down with the tenderness of a love letter. Most of the terms blurred when he tried to focus on them, sliding off his eyes like water off oil. But the signature block was clear. Clearer than clear. It burned.

*Given freely, of my own will and name:* the line read, *I, ALDRIC, KING OF VAEL CORRIN AND THE FIRST KINGDOMS, surrender my name entire — to be held, worn, and spoken by the creditor until the account is paid in full.*

Below it, a signature. But not written in ink.

The letters were scarred into the parchment, raised like burn tissue, and Isandro knew — with the certainty of a man who has handled a thousand forged documents and knows what honest ones feel like — that the king had not signed this page. Something had signed *through* him. The name at the bottom was Aldric's, and it was also not; it writhed faintly when he looked away and looked back, the way letters do in dreams.

And beneath the signature, smaller, in different handwriting — quick, cramped, human, almost hidden in the margin — a single line that stopped Isandro's heart:

*Down payment received in advance: the memories of the Vane family, three generations, tendered by the Guild of Impossible Things.*

Vane.

He didn't know why the name landed like a hook behind his sternum. He'd never heard it before — or rather, he realized with a strange vertigo, he'd heard it a hundred times and never once *listened*, the way you can walk past a door for years without ever wondering what's behind it.

Three generations of memories, sold in advance. Sold by whom? To buy what? What family sells its own past to a thing that wears kings?

Isandro sat in the doorway of a collapsing tenement in the worst quarter of the capital, holding a scrap of royal soul in his burned hand, and felt the horror in his gut slowly cool and harden into something else. Something with edges. Something that pointed, like a compass needle, at a single question:

*Where do I find the Vanes?*

The ink-smelling man had told him one more thing, back at the beginning, when Isandro had asked where to bring the papers. He'd given an address and a warning in the same breath: *The Guild of Impossible Things, off Candlemaker's Row. And thief — whatever they offer you, whatever they show you, do not let them balance your account.*

At the time, Isandro had thought it was theater.

Now, folding the page into his shirt over his hammering heart, he stood up, and began to walk toward Candlemaker's Row, and understood that he had stopped being a thief somewhere on the palace trellis and become something else entirely — a man carrying evidence of a crime three centuries old, committed by a creature still hungry, against a family whose blood might be the only key to the cage.

Behind him, far away in the sleeping palace, a bell rang once and stopped, as if even the bells were afraid to finish.

He walked faster.
