## 5. Guild of Impossible Things

The Guild of Impossible Things did not advertise itself. That was the first thing Isandro Whitlock had learned about it, three years ago, when he'd been a hungrier thief with fewer scars: you didn't find the guild; you found yourself standing in front of its door on a night you couldn't otherwise explain. The door was unremarkable — black oak, iron hinges gone green with age, set into a wall of a street that most maps of Vael Corren simply forgot to draw. Above it, no sign. Only a small brass plate, polished by centuries of nervous thumbs, bearing a single engraved line:

*All debts considered.*

Isandro had come here twice before, to sell things that had no business existing in a city where the crown's censors burned stranger books than heretical ones. A vial of a drowned man's last breath. A key that fit no lock but opened one door in every house. Both times he'd walked out heavier in coin and lighter in certainty.

Tonight he carried a scrap of royal memory wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, and his hands would not stop shaking.

He told himself it was the cold. The streets of the Undercrofts were damp this time of year, and he'd run half the way from the palace district, ducking watch patrols, heart hammering out a rhythm like a siege drum. But the shaking hadn't started until he'd looked at what he'd stolen. It hadn't stopped since.

*One king's shadow, overdue.* He could still see the words if he closed his eyes — not from the scrap itself but from somewhere deeper, some dream he'd had as a child and never understood. He shook his head. Thieves who trusted dreams died poor or dead, usually both.

The door opened before he knocked.

Inside, the guild's entry hall smelled of tallow smoke, old paper, and something faintly metallic, like blood left too long in a copper bowl. Candlelight moved without wind across shelves that climbed into darkness overhead — thousands upon thousands of small glass vials, each stoppered, each labeled in a hand so fine it might have been done with a needle. Memories. Debts. Things people had sold because they had nothing else left to sell.

"You're bleeding on the floor."

The voice came from his left. A woman stood at a high writing desk, quill moving in quick, precise strokes, not looking up. Dark hair pinned back carelessly, ink stains to the second knuckle, a ledger open before her the size of a paving stone. She had the look of someone who balanced accounts for a living and found the world perpetually overdrawn.

"Occupational hazard," Isandro said. "I'm here to sell."

"Everyone is." She finished her line, blotted it, and only then raised her eyes. They were grey and sharp and missed nothing, which he disliked immediately. "You're Whitlock. You've sold here before."

"News travels."

"Ledgers travel faster." She stood, and he saw she kept one hand near her sleeve in a way that suggested there was something in the sleeve worth guarding. "What have you got?"

"Not for the front desk." He glanced past her, toward the archway at the rear of the hall, beyond which candlelight gave way to something older and redder, like the glow of a forge seen through a keyhole. "I want the masters. This is above your pay grade, bookkeeper."

Something flickered across her face — not offense. Recognition, maybe, or dread. "Everything in this building is above my pay grade," she said quietly. "That's rather the point of it. Wait here."

She disappeared through the archway. Isandro waited, counting the vials within reach out of habit — forty-one, forty-two — and trying not to think about the thing he'd seen in the king's audience chamber. Two voices from one throat. The vastness behind the man. And the scrap of memory pressed now against his sternum, warm as a live coal, whispering even through the oilcloth.

*It wasn't whispering before,* he realized. *It started after I touched her desk.*

* * *

She returned faster than he expected, and she was not alone. Behind her came an old man bent like a question mark, dressed in robes the color of wet slate, and behind him two more figures in the shadows whose faces Isandro's eyes slid off of, refusing to hold them. Guildmasters. He'd only ever dealt with clerks before. His mouth went dry.

"The fragment," said the old man. Not a request.

Isandro hesitated. In his experience, the moment you handed over the goods was the moment you lost all leverage. But the old man's eyes held the patience of something that had watched empires be negotiated away, and Isandro's hands were still shaking, and the scrap was getting warmer against his chest, and warmth from a dead man's memory was not a good sign.

He unwrapped it.

It was small — a strip of pale membrane, neither parchment nor skin, marked with lines of script that seemed to shift when looked at directly. Royal memory, torn loose from whatever vault or vessel had kept it, showing a moment frozen mid-signature: a young king's hand, a quill, a contract, and a name being written that was already beginning to blur.

The bookkeeper made a sound.

It was small — a caught breath, quickly smothered — but in the silence of the hall it rang like dropped glass. Isandro turned to find her staring at the fragment, all her professional composure gone, replaced by something raw and ancient.

"You know it," he said.

"No," she said. Then, honestly: "Yes. The handwriting. It's—" Her jaw worked. "It's my family's hand. My grandmother wrote like that. Her grandmother before her. We've been keeping these ledgers for—" She stopped herself, but the damage was done, and Isandro watched her realize it, watched her decide, visibly, that the secret was already spilled and hoarding the rest would buy her nothing. "Generations," she finished. "My family has written the guild's contracts for generations. That signature style doesn't exist anywhere else in Vael Corren. I'd stake my life on it."

"And I'd collect," said the old man mildly. "Astrid Vane. Your discretion has always been your finest quality."

"Aldric," Astrid said, ignoring him, her eyes still on the fragment. "This is King Aldric's signing hand. From the founding era. Four hundred years ago." She looked up at Isandro, and there was something terrible in her face now — not fear exactly, but the particular horror of a bookkeeper finding an entry that cannot be balanced. "Where did you get this?"

"The palace. Tonight." He told them — quickly, flatly, the way he told all his stories, stripping out anything that smelled of feeling. The burgle job, hired through cutouts by someone who wanted proof of what the king really was. The audience chamber seen through a servant's passage. The king alone at midnight, speaking to no one, and then speaking — and the second voice coming out of him, vast and patient and *hungry*, a voice like a cathedral full of dark water. The scrap of royal memory sitting in an open reliquary on the king's own desk, as if the king kept his own wound on display.

"I took it and ran," Isandro finished. "And now I'm selling it. To you. For enough coin to leave this city and never come back."

The old man turned the fragment over in his fingers, and Isandro saw the guildmaster's expression do something complicated — hunger and fear braided together, the look of a man holding both a treasure and a lit fuse.

"Do you know what this is?" the old man asked.

"A piece of the king's mind."

"It is a page," the old man said, "of the original contract. The bargain struck between the first Aldric and the entity we call the Veil. The crown's name, surrendered. Its shadow, worn. Every king since has been a coat the Veil puts on each morning." He smiled, and it was the worst smile Isandro had ever seen, because it was gentle. "You haven't stolen a trinket, Master Whitlock. You've stolen a clause."

* * *

They put him in a side room while the masters convened, which he understood perfectly well was also called locking him up, and which he tolerated because the alternative was the street, where whoever had hired him was presumably still looking, and where the fragment's warmth was spreading through his ribs like a slow fever.

The room held a table, two chairs, and a window of thick bottle glass that showed nothing but lamplight. Isandro paced. Counted. Paced.

The door opened an hour later — he judged by his pulse, which had become unreliable — and Astrid slipped inside, closing it softly behind her. Up close, in better light, he could see the exhaustion under her eyes and the fury banked behind it like coals under ash.

"They're voting," she said.

"On what?"

"On whether to return the Veil's property to it." She laughed, short and bitter. "Or worse. Whether to *renew*. The original contract expires, Whitlock. Did you know that? Four hundred years, and the debt comes due. The Veil must either release the crown—" her voice curdled on the word *release*— "or the guild can broker a renewal. New terms. New payment. The masters have wanted that for three centuries. A fresh contract means new clauses, and new clauses mean power. Power over kings. They don't intend to free Aldric. They intend to re-sell him."

Isandro sat down slowly. "And the vote?"

"Tied, last I heard. Which is why I'm here." She pulled out the chair opposite him and leaned forward, and he saw now that her hands were trembling too, and that she had stopped bothering to hide it. "You recognized the handwriting mattered to me. So here is what matters to me: my family wrote that contract. Generations of Vanes, penning the guild's bargains, and I never asked why our hand was chosen. I think I'm about to find out, and I think I'm going to hate it. There's an entry in the oldest ledger — I've seen it, I balance that ledger every day — *one king's shadow, overdue*. And next to it, in the payment column, a line item I've never been able to source. *Paid in kind: memories, one bloodline, in perpetuity.*" Her voice cracked, just barely, and she steadied it. "I think that bloodline is mine. I think everything my family ever sold — everything *I've* sold, working nights, stealing from desperate clients to survive — I think it all flows back into that contract. I'm not the guild's bookkeeper, Whitlock. I'm part of its accounts."

The lamp guttered. Neither of them moved.

"So," Isandro said at last, because someone had to say something and terror had struck him mute. "What are you proposing? We steal it back? The fragment? Walk into the masters' vault and—"

"I'm proposing that you and I are the only two people in this building who want the same thing," Astrid said. "You want to leave with your skin. I want to burn that contract. And we are both now holding secrets the other one needs." She extended her hand across the table — ink-stained, steady now, deliberately steady. "Mutual blackmail. The foundation of every lasting alliance I've ever read about."

He took it. Her grip was cold and strong.

"One condition," Isandro said. "When this is done — however it ends — you tell me what my hire was really about. Who wanted proof of the king. Because I have a bad feeling I wasn't hired to expose the Veil." He swallowed. "I was hired to deliver the fragment to someone. And I have an even worse feeling about who."

Astrid's eyes narrowed. "Who?"

Before he could answer, the door burst open, and one of the faceless guildmasters filled the frame, its features still refusing to resolve, its voice like pages turning in an empty room.

"The vote is concluded," it said. "Renewal carries. And the masters wish to inform the thief that his buyer has been found." A pause, silk over stone. "It is already on its way here. Wearing the king."

Behind Isandro, he heard Astrid whisper a word in a language older than any kingdom — the same word, he realized with a chill, that the feral child had been screaming in his dreams ever since he touched the fragment.

*"Sef,"* she breathed. "They have the girl."
