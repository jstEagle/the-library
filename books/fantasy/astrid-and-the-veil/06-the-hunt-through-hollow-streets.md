## 6. The Hunt Through Hollow Streets

The bells of the capital rang wrong that night.

Astrid heard it from the guild's third-floor window, where she stood with Sef's small hand locked in hers and a satchel of stolen ledgers digging into her shoulder. The bells had always rung in sevens — seven strokes for the hour, seven for the watch, seven for the king's eternal health. Tonight they rang in eights, and the eighth stroke was not bronze at all but something wetter, deeper, like a knuckle cracking inside the world's wrist.

"They know," Isandro said behind her. He had his coat on inside out, guild colors hidden, and he was already pulling the window's iron latch. "The masters sent word within the hour. I watched the runner go up Coronet Hill with a sealed case. That's an invitation, Astrid. And things like *that* don't knock."

Sef tugged her hand. The girl looked up with those ledger-gray eyes and said something in the dead tongue — three syllables, soft as ash falling — and every candle in the room guttered toward her as if bowing.

"I know," Astrid told her, though she didn't, not really. "I know. We're going."

They went down the back stair while the guild slept its guilty sleep, past the counting rooms where the impossible accounts sat balanced and rotten, past the door of the guildmaster's study where light still burned — he was awake, then; he was writing to *them* — and out into the alley where the fog came off the river thick enough to chew.

Coronet Hill glowed against the sky. Not torchlight. Something paler, colder, like moonlight that had been taught to march. It came down the hill in a column, unhurried, and even at this distance Astrid could see the shape at its center: a tall figure in royal white, walking with the stiff grace of a marionette whose puppeteer had grown impatient.

The king had come for his page.

"Underways," Isandro breathed. "Now. Run."

* * *

The city above was built on the bones of three older cities, and the guild kept keys to all of them — keys Astrid had copied years ago, back when copying keys was simply what she did instead of sleeping. She took them down a coal chute behind a tannery, into the dark that smelled of old smoke and older stone, and the last thing she saw of the surface world was the pale column on Coronet Hill bending, turning, following.

"It doesn't run," she panted, hauling Sef over a fallen beam. "Did you notice? It *walks*. Like it knows it can't lose."

"That's worse," Isandro said. "Things that run can be outrun. Things that walk—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to.

The underways were brick throats and flooded cellars, aqueducts dead for four hundred years, stairs that went down and kept going after any sane architect would have stopped. Astrid lit no lamps. Sef didn't need one. Wherever the girl walked, the dark thinned around her like frost off glass, and Astrid realized with a lurch that the child was *glowing* — faintly, wrongly, the way a page glows when someone is reading it by firelight.

"Sef." Astrid crouched, took the girl's face in her hands. "When we stop, I need you to talk. Whatever comes into your head, say it. In your language. Can you do that?"

Sef considered her with terrible gravity, then nodded once, and spoke — and the word she said made the wall beside them shudder. Old mortar sifted down. Somewhere in the brick, something that had been enchanted shut for centuries sighed open like a man finally allowed to die.

Isandro stared. "What did she just do?"

"She unmade a warding," Astrid said. Her voice shook. "That warding was laid by the first kingdoms. Older than the crown. Older than the Veil, maybe." She looked at the gap in the wall, the black passage beyond, air moving through it like breath. "She's not just carrying the contract, Isandro. She's *reading herself aloud*. And everything written before her has to make way."

"Then let's find somewhere for her to read," Isandro said grimly, "before whatever's walking down Coronet Hill finds us first."

* * *

They went deeper. The passage Sef had opened led them through the drowned chapel of a dynasty no historian remembered, past a font full of black water that reflected stars though no stars could reach this far underground. Twice they heard it above them — through grates, through cracked vaulting — the sound of the pale column passing over the city streets, and beneath it, threaded through the king's own footsteps, that second voice. Vast. Patient. Reciting.

"It's counting," Isandro whispered, flat against the cold stone. "Listen. It's counting *streets*."

It wasn't counting streets. Astrid understood that with a sick drop of her stomach. It was reciting the bargain — clause by clause, century by century — and every clause it finished, the bells above rang their wrong eighth stroke, and somewhere in the fabric of the capital another enchantment stitched to Aldric's name pulled tight.

"We can't outrun a recitation," she said. "It's already everywhere its words are."

"So we don't outrun it." Isandro's eyes caught the glow of Sef's skin, and there was something in his face Astrid hadn't seen before — not the thief's calculating gleam, not fear. Something rawer. "We outrun *it*. The Veil wears the king because the bargain binds the name Aldric. But the bargain lives in her." He nodded at Sef. "If she reads ahead of it — if she gets to the end of the contract before it does—"

"The contract ends in a vault under dragon-fire," Astrid said quietly. "I've balanced the guild's accounts long enough to know where the original debt is kept. And I know how fast a child walks."

"Then we carry her."

"No." Sef's voice — her real voice, thin and clear and edged with that other, older music — stopped them both. She pointed down the flooded gallery, to where the water lay perfectly still despite their splashing. "No carry. *Walk.* The words walk. I walk better."

And she stepped out onto the water.

It held her. Of course it held her — the chapel had been warded by kings who were still men, and wards were only words, and words moved aside for her. She turned back and held out one hand to Astrid and one to Isandro, standing on black water under a roof of forgotten prayers, and Astrid thought: *this is the moment. This is the moment I will remember when everything else is gone.*

She took the hand. It was warm. It was the warmest thing in the world.

* * *

They crossed the drowned chapel and three cities' worth of bones beyond it, and Sef walked them across every barrier as if the underworld were a book opening for her page by page. A gate sealed with blood-oaths dissolved at a syllable. A bridge of chained ghosts — some poor guild experiment, centuries abandoned — lay down its chains and let them pass, and the ghosts, Astrid would swear to her grave, *thanked* her.

But the eighth bell followed them. Down and down it came, muffled now, patient now, and each stroke was closer than the last.

They surfaced at last in the cisterns beneath the Tanner Quarter, a cathedral of dripping pillars where the city's rain went to die. Astrid called a halt because Sef was stumbling, because Isandro was bleeding from a fall on the ghost-bridge, because her own lungs felt full of broken glass. They huddled in the lee of a pillar, and for a moment — one stolen, human moment — nobody spoke.

Then Isandro laughed. Quietly, helplessly, the laugh of a man who has looked over too many edges.

"What?" Astrid said.

"You. Two days ago you wouldn't tell me your real name. You'd have sold my memories for a warm meal and called it bookkeeping." He shook his head. "Now you're holding a child's hand across a lake of ghosts under the feet of a god wearing a king."

"People change."

"No." He looked at her, and the honesty in it hurt more than the running had. "People don't. People get *revealed*. I've been watching you since the guild steps, Astrid Vane, and here's what I've learned: everything you stole, you stole so no one else would have to sell it. You're not a thief. You're a *vault* that learned to feel guilty."

Astrid opened her mouth to answer — to deny it, or worse, to confirm it — and the eighth bell rang directly overhead.

Not muffled anymore. Not distant. The sound came down through the cistern ceiling like a nail through a coffin lid, and along every pillar, hairline cracks of pale light bloomed and spread. The Veil had found the way down. Or it had never needed to find anything at all; perhaps it had simply been reciting its way toward them the whole time, clause by clause, and the clauses had run out.

Sef stood up. Small, mud-caked, glowing like the last unspent page of a dying kingdom, she planted herself between the pillar and the light and began to speak — not fragments now, not drawings and guesses, but whole sentences, rolling sentences, the contract pouring out of her faster and louder, and the pale light in the cracks *hesitated*.

"It's slowing," Isandro breathed. "Astrid — it's actually slowing—"

"Yes," Astrid said, and pulled both children — because that was what they were, the thief and the page, both of them children of other people's bargains — toward the far arch, where the cistern waters ran dark toward the oldest part of the city, the part built over fire. "Because it can't skip ahead. It has to hear every word. And so do we."

Above them, through the splitting stone, the king's body descended on ropes of pale light, and the vast voice behind it spoke its next clause —

— and Astrid, running, recognized the words.

They were in her ancestor's handwriting. She couldn't see it, but she knew it the way she knew her own pulse. The Veil wasn't just reciting the bargain.

It was reciting the *ledger entry*. *One king's shadow, overdue.*

And the interest had come due tonight.
