## 7. Ledger of Betrayals

They caught him in the tannery district, where the air was thick enough to chew and every shadow smelled of rendered fat and old lies.

Isandro had been careful. He'd worn a beggar's cloak over his good boots, smeared ash in his hair, walked with the hitching gait of a man whose knee had been broken once and badly set. He'd doubled back twice through the fish market, paid a guttersnipe to watch his tail, and still — *still* — they had simply been there when he turned down Tanner's Row, three figures in guild grey standing beneath the dripping awnings as though they'd grown out of the cobblestones.

"Master Whitlock," said the one in front. She was small and silver-haired, with the patient eyes of someone who had audited empires. Astrid had called her Mistress Corvane. "You've been difficult to find."

"I get that a lot," Isandro said. His hand drifted toward his knife and stopped, because the two flanking Corvane moved their hands toward their belts at exactly the same speed, with exactly the same lack of hurry, and something about that synchrony was more frightening than any drawn blade.

"You have something we want," Corvane said.

"I have a lot of things. A lovely collection of other people's secrets. A slightly damp cloak. A—"

"A fragment of royal memory." She said it the way a banker says a sum: precisely, without appetite, which somehow made it worse. "Taken from the palace vaults four nights ago. We know because the vault told us. The vault remembers everything that leaves it, Master Whitlock. It is, after all, a ledger."

The rain chose that moment to begin, a fine cold sift that beaded on the awnings above them and dripped down the back of Isandro's collar like fingers.

"And if I do have it?" he said.

"Then you will give it to us, and you will tell us where the girl is, and you will walk away rich." Corvane tilted her head. "Or you will not, and you will walk away into a cell beneath the guildhall, and we will take both anyway, and you will not enjoy the taking. We are very old, Master Whitlock. We have extracted information from men who were certain they had none."

Isandro laughed. It came out thinner than he wanted it to. "And here I thought the Guild of Impossible Things was in the business of impossible things. Charitable things. Little miracles for little people."

"We are in the business of *balance*," Corvane said. "Debts must be paid. That is all we have ever done, all we will ever do. The crown owes a debt three centuries old, and it has come due, and your little thief's conscience does not appear anywhere in the accounting." She extended one hand, palm up, smooth as a merchant's counter. "The fragment. And the location."

He should have lied. He should have given them the false address he'd prepared, the safehouse on Candlemaker's Lane that Sef hadn't seen in two days, and bought himself time to move her. He'd rehearsed it. He'd rehearsed it in the bath, in the gutter, in his sleep.

But Corvane's two companions had stepped closer without appearing to step at all, and the rain was running down his neck, and somewhere under his ribs was the memory of what he'd seen in the king's eyes — that vast thing looking out through the man's face like light through a cracked lantern — and Isandro Whitlock, who had never in his life been loyal to anything but his own skin, heard his own mouth say:

"She's in the cisterns under the old aqueduct. Saint Verity's. There's a dry chamber behind the third sluice gate. She'll be with the bookkeeper."

The words left him the way blood leaves a wound: warm, then cold, then gone.

Corvane closed her hand around nothing, satisfied. "Wise."

"There's a condition," Isandro said, and hated how quickly the words came, how eager his voice was to bargain its way back into comfort. "She comes to no harm. Whatever this renewal is, whatever ritual or contract or — she's a child. You deal with the king, you deal with the Veil, you leave the girl out of it."

"The girl *is* the contract," Corvane said gently. "That is rather the point."

"Then leave her alive in it."

"Master Whitlock." For the first time, something moved behind the silver-haired woman's patience — not quite pity, but its well-dressed cousin. "Do you imagine we want her harmed? Harm would spoil the page. We want her whole, and read, and filed. What happens after the ink dries is not our department." She turned, and her companions turned with her, three grey shapes folding back into the tannery steam. "Payment will reach you by morning. Spend it somewhere far from here."

Then he was alone in the rain, holding the stolen scrap of royal memory against his chest like a coal he couldn't put down, telling himself the oldest lie in his considerable repertoire: *it was necessary, it was necessary, anyone would have done it.*

* * *

He didn't go to collect the payment. He went to drink.

The Broken Compass was a thieves' den two streets from the river, a place where the ale was bad and the loyalty was worse and nobody asked questions because everybody had answers they didn't want traded. Isandro sat in the corner with his back to the wall and drank until the room went soft at the edges, and it didn't help, because the thing he was trying to drown kept swimming.

*A child in a dry chamber behind the third sluice gate.*

He saw her the way he'd first seen her in Astrid's keeping: mud-caked, wild-eyed, speaking that dead language that made ledgers open themselves. He saw her watching him across Astrid's table with those too-old eyes, the ones that had looked at him — he understood it only now, only drunk, only too late — the way a page looks at the hand that might yet write mercy on it.

"It's a transaction," he muttered into his cup. "Everything's a transaction. That's the whole world. That's the whole—"

The walls spoke.

Not the walls of the Broken Compass specifically. The walls of everything. It began as a vibration in the floorboards, in the cups, in the fillings of his teeth, and then it rose into hearing, and every conversation in the tavern died mid-sentence as though a hand had closed over the city's mouth.

It was the voice he'd heard in the throne room, the second voice, the one behind the king's — but now it wasn't wearing restraint. Now it was *hungry*, and it filled the stone of the city the way water fills a drowned ship.

***FOUND,*** it said. ***THE PAGE IS FOUND. THE LAST UNREAD PAGE.***

Somewhere above them, distantly, a bell began to ring — not an alarm, Isandro realized, but the great bell of the guildhall, tolling the hour that did not exist, the hidden hour, the hour the ledgers kept secret. He'd heard rumors of it in the trade. The Thirteenth Hour, when the guild conducted its true business.

***BRING THE CHILD TO THE VAULT. BRING HER BEFORE THE FIRE. THE BARGAIN WILL BE RENEWED AND THE HUNGER WILL BE FED FOR ANOTHER THREE HUNDRED YEARS.***

Three hundred years. The words went through Isandro like a blade finding the gap in armor, and behind them came images unbidden — the fragment he carried, the scrap of Aldric's memory, the moment three centuries ago when a young king had knelt before a fire and signed away his name in exchange for his kingdom's survival, and the thing that had accepted the signature had worn him ever since, decade upon decade, election upon coronation, a puppet king smiling on coins while something vast and patient pulled the strings from inside his skin.

And the guildmasters knew. They had always known. They kept the accounts of the debt, collected the interest, and now they meant to renew the loan — to feed another three centuries of a man's life into the fire, and a child along with it, a living ledger-page to be read aloud and consumed.

*What happens after the ink dries is not our department.*

Isandro set down his cup. His hands were shaking. He noticed them shaking with a strange, detached interest, the way he noticed everything now, as though he were already someone else — someone who had sold a child to save himself and was about to stop being that person, permanently, one way or the other.

The voice faded. The tavern exhaled. Conversation stuttered back to life around him, thieves and fences pretending they hadn't heard, the way people pretend not to hear the things that unmake their comfortable worlds.

Isandro stood, left a coin on the table — a rare gesture, and he'd have denied it under torture — and walked out into the rain.

* * *

The guildhall's back entrance was a sally port used by collectors who preferred not to be seen coming or going, and Isandro knew it because he'd cased the building years ago, back when the guild had merely been the richest fence in the city rather than the custodian of a three-hundred-year hunger. Two guards at the door. He watched them for an hour from a rooftop across the lane, shivering, sobering, hating himself with a thoroughness that felt almost holy.

Then he stopped hating and started planning, because shame was a luxury and resolve was a tool, and Isandro Whitlock had spent thirty years learning the difference.

He went over the wall instead of through the door, down the inside of the courtyard, across the flagstones between the patrol lines, and in through the archive window he'd once marked as the building's single blind spot. Inside, the guildhall was lit by lamps that burned without flickering, and the corridors smelled of vellum and cold iron, and everywhere — everywhere — were ledgers. Shelves of them, cases of them, chained volumes and open volumes, pages turning softly in rooms where no one stood.

He found the fragment of royal memory in his coat and held it a moment. Aldric's name, signed away. Aldric's terror, pressed flat into a scrap of stolen thought.

"You're getting this back," Isandro whispered to it. "Both of you. The name and the girl. I don't know how yet. But I've robbed better-guarded places than God, and I'm owed a miracle, and I intend to collect."

Above him, faint through floors of stone, the Thirteenth Hour tolled on, and he heard footsteps — many, purposeful, descending — and raised voices, and one voice cutting through the rest that he knew: low, furious, unmistakably Astrid Vane's.

She was shouting at the masters. She was shouting about Sef.

Isandro grinned, and it was not a nice grin, and he went up the stairs toward the sound with his knife loose in his hand and his heart hammering out a rhythm that felt, for the first time in his life, like a promise.
