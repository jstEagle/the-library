## 10. Beginning Again

The woman who came out of the dragon-fire vault was not the woman who went in.

Isandro knew this the way he knew the weight of a lockpick in his palm — not by thinking it but by feeling it, in the marrow, where certainty lived. She stood at the mouth of the tunnel with ash in her hair and soot striping her cheeks like war paint, and she looked at him with eyes that held nothing. Not coldness. Not emptiness the way a locked room is empty. Emptiness the way dawn is empty: everything burned away, everything possible.

"Do I know you?" she asked.

The question should have broken him. Instead it did something stranger — it made him laugh, a cracked and startled sound that echoed down the vault corridor and startled the pigeons roosting in the collapsed rafters above.

"Everyone knows me," he said. "I'm very memorable."

She frowned. Somewhere behind her, small feet pattered on stone, and Sef appeared — no longer feral, no longer speaking in the dead tongue of the first kingdoms, but a girl, just a girl, with mud still dried in the creases of her elbows and an expression of ferocious tenderness aimed at the memoryless woman before her.

"You're Astrid," Sef said. "Your name is Astrid Vane. You like honey in your tea but you pretend you don't. You taught me to write my letters on the floor of a locked room with charcoal stolen from the hearth. And you once told me—" the girl's voice caught, steadied— "you told me that debts are only stories we agree to keep telling."

Astrid Vane looked at the child for a long moment. Then at Isandro. Then at her own hands, which were shaking.

"I don't remember any of that," she said. "But I believe you. Isn't that strange? I believe you the way you believe a door is a door."

* * *

They took her to Isandro's rooms above the chandler's shop on Candlewick Row, because the guildhouse was no longer safe and, more to the point, no longer standing in any way that mattered.

He had gone back two days after the vault — alone, because Astrid slept like the dead and Sef refused to leave her side, and because some part of Isandro needed to see it with his own eyes. What he found was a building in its death throes. The ledgers had felt their master die. Every book in the Guild of Impossible Things had opened itself at once, the way they'd opened for Sef's voice, and what poured out of them was not ink but memory — centuries of harvested recollection, freed from their columns and margins, rising through the halls like floodwater. The guildmasters had drowned in it. Not literally, though Isandro wouldn't have mourned. Metaphorically. They sat in the great counting room amid the ruin of their life's work, old men and old women suddenly unable to remember why they had ever believed a debt could be owned, and the memories they had hoarded as collateral drifted past them like leaves on a current, going home.

One of them — Master Corvane, who had voted to renew the bargain, whose voice Isandro would hear in his nightmares until the day he died — had looked up at Isandro standing in the doorway and said, quietly, "We thought we were keeping accounts. We were keeping prisoners."

Isandro had set fire to the ledger of small debts himself. It burned green, then gold, then not at all, because there was nothing left in it to burn.

Now he watched Astrid Vane sit at his own narrow table, in his own mismatched chair, holding a cup of tea with honey she didn't know she liked, and he understood that the guild had never been a building. It had been an idea, and ideas were harder to kill, and this one was dying slowly in the woman across from him every time she reached for a memory that wasn't there.

"The king," she said. "Tell me about the king again."

"Aldric. He's real now. Actually real, for the first time in three hundred years. He woke up in the vault with three centuries missing and a kingdom to run, and apparently his first act as a free man was to weep for an hour." Isandro turned his cup on its saucer. "His second act was to send for you. He wants to thank you. Properly. With ceremonies."

"I don't want ceremonies."

"No," Isandro said. "I didn't think you would. Sef told us."

"You keep saying that. *Sef told us.*" Astrid's jaw tightened. "You talk about me like I'm someone you both knew. Like I'm a story you're checking me against."

Isandro set down his cup. This was the part he'd been dreading, the conversation he'd rehearsed a dozen times in the dark hours while she slept, and every rehearsal ended the same way: with the truth, because she deserved nothing less, and because lies were how all of this had started.

"You were," he said. "You are. Astrid — you paid for that king's freedom with everything you had. Your childhood. Your mother's voice. Every memory you had of me, and every memory you had of her." He nodded toward Sef, who was pretending very badly to read by the window. "You chose it. Freely. The Veil only accepted freely surrendered memory, and you walked into that fire knowing exactly what it would cost, and you paid it anyway, and you smiled while you did it. That's who you are. That's the person sitting in that chair, whether she remembers it or not."

Silence. The chandler's shop below sent up a thread of beeswax smell through the floorboards. Astrid looked down into her tea for a long time.

"And you," she said finally. "Did I love you?"

The question hit him like a stair that wasn't there. He'd prepared for grief, for guilt, for the ache of being forgotten. He had not prepared to be asked to define something he'd never had the courage to name when naming it was safe.

"I don't know," he said honestly. "We hadn't gotten around to finding out. There was rather a lot of running."

Something moved across her face — not recognition, nothing so kind, but interest. Curiosity, bright and unencumbered, looking at him as though he were a book she might open.

"Well," said Astrid Vane. "We're not running anymore."

* * *

Spring came to the capital the way forgiveness comes — gradually, then all at once.

They rented a house on Tallowmere Lane with a crooked chimney and a garden gone wild, because Sef put her foot down about living above a shop that smelled of tallow, and because Isandro discovered that a man who had spent his life stealing other people's homes quite wanted one of his own. Astrid relearned the world in pieces. The taste of plums (delightful). Thunderstorms (terrifying, then magnificent). Her own reflection (a stranger for a month, then, slowly, a friend). Sef taught her letters again, patiently, the way Astrid had once taught her, charcoal on the kitchen flags, and Isandro taught her the city — which bridges lied about their tolls, which baker sold day-old bread as fresh, which alleys remembered kindness.

The guild did not vanish overnight. Institutions rarely do. But the ledgers were free now, and freed ledgers travel, and one by one the debts they recorded began resolving themselves across the kingdom. A miller in the eastern provinces received back the memory of his daughter's face, taken as collateral forty years ago. A noblewoman woke from a hundred-year sleep with her name returned. The crown, under Aldric — the true Aldric, gaunt and wondering and learning to rule without a puppeteer's hand at his neck — issued a proclamation Isandro read aloud at the kitchen table while Astrid listened with her chin in her hand:

*Let it be known that no debt of memory shall be bought, sold, or held in this realm henceforth. What is given freely may be taken back freely. The books are closed.*

"They'll try again someday," Astrid said, when he finished. "Someone always tries again."

"Probably."

"Then someone will have to stop them."

Isandro looked at her. She was smiling — that particular smile, the one Sef swore was exactly the same as before, the one thing the fire hadn't touched because it had never been a memory at all. It was character. It was the grain of the wood.

"Yes," he said. "Apparently someone will."

* * *

On the first warm evening of the year, Isandro came home to find the kitchen table cleared and a blank book sitting in the middle of it.

It was a fine book — good rag paper, leather covers dyed the deep blue of evening — and it had cost Sef three weeks of saved coins and a solemn negotiation with the stationer on Bellows Street. She stood beside it now with her arms crossed, wearing the expression of a general presenting a battle plan.

"It's for you," she said. "For starting over. You need somewhere to put things."

Astrid ran her fingers over the cover. "I don't have anything to put in it."

"That's the best part," Sef said. "Everything goes in. Nothing gets left out. Nothing gets *sold*." She said the last word like spitting.

Astrid opened the book. The first page lay white as new snow, and the whole room seemed to lean toward it — Isandro in the doorway, Sef on her toes, the lamplight trembling — and Astrid Vane, who had once balanced the impossible accounts of a guild that trafficked in souls, who had burned her own childhood to unbind a king, who had walked out of a dragon-fire vault with nothing and been glad of it, picked up the pen.

She hesitated.

"What do I write?" she asked, and her voice was small, and honest, and utterly unafraid.

Sef answered for all of them. "Start with what you know."

So Astrid bent her head, and wrote, in careful letters that grew steadier with every stroke, the first line of a ledger that belonged to no guild and no crown and no creature behind any veil — a ledger with a single account, held in trust, payable to no one:

*Astrid Vane. Debts: none. Owed: everything. Beginning here.*

Below it, after a moment, she added a second line, smaller, almost shy:

*There is a boy in the doorway who says I loved him. I intend to find out.*

Isandro laughed. Sef cheered. Outside, the bells of the liberated city rang the hour, and somewhere far beneath the streets, in a vault gone cold and dark, the last ember of the last bargain guttered out forever — while above, in a kitchen smelling of honey and lamplight, a woman with an empty mind and a full heart turned the page and began.
