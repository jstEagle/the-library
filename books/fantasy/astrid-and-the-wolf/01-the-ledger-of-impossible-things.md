## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in a counting room, and I have come to know it the way a priest knows his god: intimately, dutifully, and with a low-grade dread that never quite goes away.

My name is Oscar Ishikawa, and for eleven years I have kept the ledgers of the Guild of Impossible Things.

I want to be clear about what that means, because the guild's name does a great deal of misleading work. We are not a guild of magicians or miracle-workers, whatever the brass plaque above our door in Candlewick Row suggests. We are brokers. Middlemen. The respectable face of a trade that would make a grave-robber blush. Memories, mostly — though we deal in anything the world has too much of or not enough of. Bottled storms from the Sundering Coast, sold to drought-farmers at ruinous interest. Stolen years, decanted from the young and poured into the old. Last words, first loves, the smell of a dead mother's kitchen. If it can be taken out of a head and sealed in glass or wax or ink, someone has wanted to buy it, and someone at the guild has wanted to sell it.

And someone — this is where I come in — has wanted to write it all down.

I sat at my desk on the morning this story properly begins, as I sit every morning, with my sleeves rolled to the elbow and my nib sharpened, balancing accounts that no honest bank would touch. The counting room occupies the third floor of the guildhall, a long room of dark oak and darker windows, lit by lamps that burn oil scented with rosemary because Guildmaster Corvane believes rosemary aids memory and he enjoys the joke. My desk faces the wall. This was my own request, years ago. I did not want to watch the traders come and go with their little velvet cases. I did not want to see what they carried out of people.

The ledger open before me was Volume Forty-One, *Acquisitions, Common Class*. In it, in my small, even hand, were entries like these:

*Item 1147. One (1) memory, childhood summer, river swimming, approx. four hours' duration. Acquired from: J. Merrick, age nine, by consent of guardian. Payment rendered: two silver marks to said guardian. Condition on intake: intact, warm, faintly green.*

*Item 1148. One (1) memory, wedding day, groom's perspective. Acquired from: name withheld per seller's request. Payment rendered: forgiveness of debt, one hundred crowns. Note: seller wept throughout extraction. Note struck through at seller's insistence.*

*Item 1149. Six (6) years, unallocated, drawn from debtor's estate. Quality: middling. Contains much rain.*

That last one had given me trouble. Years are difficult to itemize. A memory is a discrete thing, like a pearl; you can hold it up and describe its luster. But six years of a man's life — his twenty-third through his twenty-ninth, in this case, sold off by his creditors while he sat in a debtors' cell not knowing why he felt so hollow when he counted to thirty — six years resist tidy notation. I had written *contains much rain* and then sat looking at it for a long time, because it was true and also obscene. Somewhere in those six years there had certainly been a friend, a bad winter, probably a woman whose name he could no longer conjure. And my professional assessment, entered into the permanent record of the Guild of Impossible Things, was *middling*.

I dipped my pen. I moved on. This is the whole art of my profession, if you can call it one: dipping the pen and moving on.

You will want to know how a man comes to do such work, and stays doing it, and sleeps — mostly — at night. I have answers. They are not good answers, but they are mine, and I have polished them over eleven years until they shine like the brass fittings on the guild's front door.

First: bookkeeping is not thieving. I have told myself this so many times it has worn smooth as a river stone. The trader who sits across from a dying woman and talks her out of her daughter's face — he is the thief. The buyer who pays three crowns to feel brave for an afternoon — he is the thief. I only record what passes between them. A ledger does not reach into anyone's head. A ledger has no hands. When the Crown's assessors come, as they do twice yearly, they inspect my columns and find them immaculate, and they stamp the guild's license without once asking me whether the numbers keep me awake. They do not ask because the answer would complicate their stamping.

Second: someone must keep the accounts. This is the answer I am proudest of, which should tell you everything. Without records, I tell myself, the trade would be lawless. Sellers cheated, buyers poisoned with damaged goods, memories resold three and four times until they frayed into nightmare. My ledgers are the only conscience the guild has. Every entry is a small act of witness. *This happened. This person lost this thing. It was worth this much.* If I stopped, the numbers would simply continue in someone else's hand, and that hand might not tremble.

Third — and here my voice drops, because this is the answer I only admit at night, to the ceiling — third: I am good at it. Genuinely, shamefully good. I can look at a column of acquisitions and know which trader padded the extraction fee. I can smell a forged provenance the way other men smell rain coming. Corvane says I have a gift. He said it at my ten-year dinner, raising a glass of the good wine, the one we keep for clients, and the whole table of traders and fences and one very polite assassin raised theirs too, and I felt — God help me — I felt *warm*. Eleven years, and the guild still knows how to make me feel warm.

So much for how I live. Now let me show you how the morning went, because mornings at the guildhall have a rhythm, and rhythms are how you fail to notice the drumbeat changing.

* * *

At half past eight, Sera Dunmore came up from the vaults with the day's intake tray, and I logged it. Sera is the guild's senior extractor, a broad, calm woman with hands like a stonemason's and a voice she uses on frightened sellers the way you'd use a blanket. She sets the tray down on the corner of my desk — never the middle; she knows I need the middle — and reads out her items while I enter them.

"Two dreams," she said. "Recurring. Same seller, wants rid of both. Drowning and a house fire."

"Related?"

"He didn't say. His eyes said yes."

I wrote: *Items 1150, 1151. Two (2) recurring dreams, aquatic and combustion. Sold willingly. Seller visibly relieved.* Then, because I am the guild's conscience, I added in smaller script: *Relief purchased at cost of whatever the dreams were trying to say.* Sera watched me write it. She always watches. She has never once asked me to strike such notes, and I have never once asked her why she permits them. Between us, that is the closest either of us comes to confession.

"Anything else?" I asked.

"One oddity." She lifted a small jar off the tray, wrapped in black cloth, and set it down with more care than the dreams had gotten. "A gentleman came in before dawn. No appointment. Paid the rush fee in full, cash." She unwrapped it. Inside the jar, suspended in the pale amber of suspension fluid, hung something that was not quite a light and not quite a fog — a memory, but denser than any I'd catalogued. Ordinary memories in suspension drift, like goldfish. This one sat still, at the center of its jar, like a stone at the bottom of a well.

"What is it?" I asked.

"That's the thing." Sera rubbed her jaw. "He wouldn't say. Wouldn't sell it, either. Wanted it *stored*. Indefinitely. No retrieval clause."

"No retrieval clause?" I put down my pen. Everything in the guild gets a retrieval clause. Even the dreams. Even the rain-soaked years. People store things precisely so they can come back for them — sins they mean to reclaim when they're braver, griefs they aren't finished with. "Everyone comes back eventually."

"I know," Sera said.

"So what did you charge him?"

"Double. And I took it anyway, because the fee was triple if I asked questions and he paid that too." She rewrapped the jar. "He made me promise it goes nowhere near the sales floor. Deep storage. He said—" she paused, and I could see her deciding whether to give me the exact words, and then giving them, because Sera does not believe in softening things, "—he said, 'Keep it where nothing remembers it.'"

I wrote the entry. *Item 1152. Contents undisclosed. Storage only, indefinite term, no retrieval clause. Fee: triple rate, paid in advance. Seller departed before dawn.* And beneath it, after a moment: *What kind of man pays to be rid of a thing and then pays again to ensure no one else ever holds it?*

Sera carried the jar down to deep storage herself. I heard the vault door close behind her, three floors down, with its sound like a held breath released.

* * *

Midmorning brought the buyers, and I retreated further behind my wall-facing desk while the showroom below filled with the usual melancholy parade. I will not pretend I don't listen. The floor registers carry sound beautifully; Corvane designed it that way, so the guildmaster could eavesdrop on his own commerce, and I inherited the habit along with the desk.

A widow bought back one hour of her husband snoring beside her, having sold it two winters ago to pay for his funeral. She came up afterward to verify the transfer against my ledger, as the law requires, and stood at my desk with the little vial cupped in both hands like a candle flame, and said, "It's not much, is it? An hour of snoring." And I said, "It's the hour you asked for, madam," which was true and also obscene, and she smiled at me with wet eyes and left, and I entered the transaction in the *Returns* column and then sat very still for a moment with my hand flat on the page, as if I could press the entry flat, flatten it into harmlessness.

An old man bought a first kiss. A young soldier — guild colors on his collar, so one of ours — sold nothing and bought nothing and only stood in the corner of the showroom for a quarter hour, reading the catalogue of available stock the way starving men read menus. I watched him on the floor-register and felt my pen go dry in my fingers. He left without buying. Most of them leave without buying. The catalogue is the cruelty; the purchase is almost a mercy after it.

And then, just past noon, Guildmaster Corvane came up to my counting room, which he does perhaps once a month, and today he came holding a ledger of his own — one of mine, an older volume, Volume Thirty-Three — and wearing the expression he wears when he has found an error, which is the expression of a man about to enjoy himself.

"Ishikawa." He set Volume Thirty-Three on my desk, open to a marked page. "Walk me through this."

I looked. Entry 8921. *One (1) memory, battlefield, mass engagement, duration approx. one day. Acquired from: unnamed officer, Crown Fourth Regiment. Payment: commission, rank of captain, plus annuity. Condition: intact, hot, loud.*

"A standard acquisition," I said. "Logged, priced, warehoused. It sold eighteen months ago to—"

"To whom?"

I checked my own cross-reference, which was unnecessary, because I knew. "To the Office of the Inquisitor General. For archival suppression."

"And what," Corvane said pleasantly, "was in it?"

"Guildmaster, condition reports don't include content beyond classification. You know that better than—"

"What. Was in it."

I looked at the entry. I looked at him. Corvane is sixty-some years old, silver at the temples, a handsome ruin of a man who dresses like a scholar and negotiates like a butcher. He has never once threatened me. He has never needed to. The threat is structural; it is in the walls.

"A massacre," I said quietly. "By our side. Of civilians. The officer wanted it gone because he couldn't sleep. The Crown wanted it gone because—" I gestured vaguely at the air, at the world, at the whole apparatus of quiet that the Crown maintains and calls peace.

"Because the war ended seven years ago," Corvane said, "and the peace is a story, and stories need editors." He tapped the page. "And you edited it, Ishikawa. Neatly. Discreetly. *Condition: intact, hot, loud.* Like describing a pepper." He closed the volume and regarded me with something that in a better man might have been affection. "Do you know why I keep you?"

"Because my columns balance."

"Because your columns balance *and* your hands don't shake." He picked the ledger back up. "The Inquisitor's office ordered six more suppressions this quarter. Military engagements. All classified under *Common, Historical*. They'll route through you. I trust you'll handle them with your usual discretion."

He left. I sat with my pen uncapped and did not write anything for a long time. Then I opened the intake schedule, found the six entries, and began, because that is what I do, to prepare the pages. Ruling the lines. Printing the headers. Making clean, beautiful spaces ready to receive six massacres, each of which would be described, in my careful hand, as *intact, hot, loud*.

Bookkeeping is not thieving, I told myself. The pen has no hands.

But I noticed, as I ruled those lines, that I had begun keeping a second set of books. Not on paper. Nothing so dangerous. Just — in my head, in the place where I keep things I cannot enter anywhere: the widow's wet smile. The soldier reading the catalogue. The stone-at-the-bottom-of-a-well jar that Sera stored where nothing remembers it. A private ledger of costs, unwritten, unaudited, accruing interest in the dark behind my eyes.

I used to think that ledger made me different from the guild. Better. A clerk with a conscience, secretly tallying the true price.

It occurs to me now, writing this, that a man who keeps two sets of books is not a witness. He is just a thief with excellent handwriting.

* * *

The knock came at dusk, which was the first wrong thing. Clients come at dawn or midday; the guild's business keeps daylight hours, because fear and grief travel badly after dark. The second wrong thing was the quality of the knocking — not the client's polite triple-tap, but a fist, irregular, weakening, the knock of a man using the door to stay upright.

I crossed the counting room and opened the door on a stranger.

He was tall and starved and gray, and he had been gray for some time — not the gray of age but the gray of blood loss, his lips gone the color of ash, his left hand pressed to his right side where his coat was dark and widening. He looked at me with eyes that did the terrible arithmetic of a dying man: *this room, this clerk, this door — enough, it will have to be enough.*

"Guild closed," I said, because I am a coward with routines. "Come back—"

He fell forward against the doorframe, and with his free hand he pushed something against my chest, hard, insistent, the way you'd push a child onto a lifeboat. A scroll, wax-sealed, the seal stamped with a device I did not recognize — a crown, inverted.

"Take it," he said. His voice was gravel dragged uphill. "Off the books. Off *every* book. You understand? Nothing written. Nothing remembered."

"I'm just the clerk—"

"You're the ledger-keeper." Something that was nearly a smile moved through the ash of his face. "The one man in this rotten house who writes everything down. So listen, because I haven't time for you to write it." He sagged; I caught his arm; his weight was horrifying, like catching a falling door. "The Vault of Unremembered Kings. The map shows the road. The seal opens nothing — it *calls*. And the one who answers—" his breath caught, rattled, "—her name is Astrid. Find Astrid Vane. Tell her the wolf remembers. Tell her—"

The rest of it went out of him in a long exhale, and whatever the message was, it stayed wherever messages go when the mouth that carries them stops.

I stood in my doorway holding a sealed map and a dying man, and I did the thing I am most ashamed of in eleven years of small shames: I looked down the stairwell first. Left, then right. Checking whether anyone had seen. Because even then, even with a man's blood cooling on my threshold, some clerk-deep part of me was already assessing liability.

The corridor was empty. Below, faintly, I could hear the showroom closing up, Sera's voice, the clink of the till.

I got my arms under the stranger and dragged him inside. It took longer than it should have. I laid him out on the floor between my desk and the shelves, and I knelt there with my hand hovering over the wound, knowing pressure wouldn't matter, knowing I knew it wouldn't matter, and I said, stupidly, "Who sent you? Who's Astrid? What wolf?" — as if the dead are obliged to finish their sentences.

Then I heard boots on the stairs. Several pairs. Moving fast, in step, which clients never do and enforcers always do.

There was no time to think, which was a mercy, because thinking would have cost me the only advantage I had, which was speed. The false ledger page — every keeper keeps one, a dummy folio sewn into the binding where we log the guild's genuinely unspeakable transactions away from even internal audit — I slit the stitching with my thumbnail, folded the map small, and slid it flat against the spine of Volume Forty-One, between the recorded and the real. Then I stitched it shut with thread from my sleeve in maybe nine seconds, which is a personal record I hope never to beat again.

The door burst open as I straightened, pen already in hand, ledger already open, the picture of a man interrupted at honest work.

Three enforcers in guild gray. Behind them, unhurried, filling the doorway like weather, the broad silhouette of Vetch, the guild's chief enforcer, a man built along the lines of a dock crane and about as curious.

"Clerk," Vetch said. His eyes went past me to the body on the floor, and narrowed slightly, the way a man narrows his eyes at a discrepancy. "We're following a runner. Bleeding. Came this way."

I looked down at the stranger, and the stranger looked, in the way the dead do look — at nothing, at everything, at me — and I heard my own voice say, calm as a column summing:

"No one came this way. I've been alone all evening."

Vetch studied me. I studied my ledger. Somewhere in the walls of the guildhall, in the space between the recorded and the real, a map lay hidden with a dead man's seal upon it, and a word — *Astrid* — lay hidden in me, in the one ledger no auditor has ever reached.

Vetch grunted, ordered his men to search the room, and they turned over my shelves and my cabinets and found nothing, because they were looking for a living man and a bleeding trail, and I had already begun, without quite admitting it, to keep my books differently.

They carried the body out. Vetch paused at the door. "Odd," he said, "a clerk alone all evening. Usually there's more noise from up here. Scratching."

"My pen," I said.

"Mm," said Vetch, and left.

I locked the door. I sat down. I took out Volume Forty-One and held it in both hands, feeling the small extra thickness at the spine, the weight of a road I had no business owning.

Bookkeeping is not thieving, I told myself, one last time, in the voice of a man repeating a prayer he no longer expects to be answered.

Then I blew out the lamp, and in the dark I whispered the stranger's word to myself, once, so I would not lose it:

*Astrid.*
