## 2. A Stranger at the Door

I want it on the record — my record, the one I keep in my own head and nowhere else — that I did not invite him in.

The counting room of the Guild of Impossible Things has one door, one window with bars that cost the guild a scandalous sum to have forged from cold-iron, and one rule: nobody enters while the ledgers are open. The ledgers are always open. That is rather the point of me. So when the door banged inward at half past eleven on a night so wet the rain seemed personal, my first thought was not *a stranger* but *an interruption*, and my second was *the ink*, because a gust had come off the street and was worrying the corner of page four hundred and twelve like a dog nosing at something dead.

"Shut the door," I said, without looking up. "You're costing the guild money."

He did shut the door. That should have been my first warning. People who come to argue with a clerk do not shut doors behind themselves; they let them hang open so their outrage can breathe. He shut it gently, the way you close a lid on something, and then he crossed my floor and left a trail behind him that I did not understand until he was nearly on top of me.

It was blood. Of course it was blood. But for one absurd moment, watching it drip off his fingers onto my clean boards, I thought: *he's been carrying ink.* That is the occupational hazard of my trade. Everything looks like accounting if you stare at it long enough.

"Master Ishikawa." His voice was ruined, gravel dragged through water. He knew my name. That was my second warning, and I ignored it too, because I am very good at ignoring things; it is most of what bookkeeping is.

"You have the wrong room," I said. "Enforcers are two floors up. Grievances are three. Whatever's been done to you, there's a form."

"There's no form for this." He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough left red on his lips, and I finally put down my pen, because even a man as practiced at denial as myself cannot balance a column while someone dies on his abacus.

Let me describe him, since description is all anyone will ever have of him now, including me. Tall, or he would have been without the stoop. Grey in a way that wasn't age — grey like ash, like something burned down to its honest parts. A coat that had once been fine and had recently been used as a bandage in at least three places. And eyes that kept flicking to the door behind him with such regularity that I found myself counting the intervals. Four seconds. Five. Six. A man timing his own death against someone else's arrival.

"What did you steal?" I asked. It seemed the relevant question. In this building it is always the relevant question.

"Nothing," he said. "That's the joke. I'm the only honest man here tonight, and I'm the one bleeding on your floor."

He reached into his coat, and I confess I flinched, and I confess further that my hand went not to any weapon — I own none; weapons are an expense category I've never approved — but to the ledger itself. To the record. As though paper could stop a knife. Though I suppose in this guild it sometimes can, which is a thought I try not to have sober.

What he drew out was a roll of oilskin, no longer than my forearm, tied with cord gone soft with handling. He pressed it into my hands before I could decide whether to take it, which is a trick I have since learned: act with such certainty that the other person's hesitation becomes your opportunity. He held my wrists closed around it with both of his, and his hands were freezing, and shaking, and sticky, and I did not pull away. I'd like to say that was courage. It was shock. There is a difference, and I have spent every night since lying to myself about which one I showed.

"The Vault," he said. "Unremembered Kings. You know the name."

"I know it's a story," I said. "A fable clerks tell apprentices. Nobody trades in it. It's not in any ledger."

"It's in this one." He tapped the oilskin with one bloody finger. "The only copy. They burned the rest forty years ago and called it tidying. Listen to me, Ishikawa. Listen, because I don't have time to be believed twice." He leaned closer, and I could smell iron and river water and something older underneath, like a cellar that had never seen sun. "The guild will tell you it's merchandise. The Crown will tell you it's poison. They're both right. It's both. And it's the only thing in the world that remembers what everyone else was made to forget."

"That's not possible," I said, in the voice I use for vendors whose invoices don't reconcile. "Memory doesn't work like that. You can't erase a war. Wars are too big. Too many minds."

"One mind was big enough." His smile was terrible. "One mind held all of it, so that no one else had to. Do you understand what I'm telling you? They didn't destroy the truth. They *filed* it. Somewhere with a lock on it. Somewhere only she can open."

"She?"

And he said the word. Just the word, nothing else, the way a dying man spends his last coin on the one purchase that matters:

"Astrid."

Then the light went out of him between one breath and the next, politely, almost, like a candle pinched by a careful housekeeper. He slid down the front of my desk and came to rest against the filing cabinet, and his head tipped back, and his eyes stayed open, which I resented, because I would have to look at them while I thought, and thinking was about to become very difficult.

* * *

Here is what I did, in order, and I give you the order honestly because the order is where the shame lives.

First, I checked his pulse. Not out of hope. Out of procedure. The guild requires confirmation of death before any asset transfer, and some part of my brain — the part I hate, the part that balances columns in its sleep — had already begun classifying the oilskin roll in my hands as an asset.

Second, I looked at the door. Four seconds. Five. Six. No one came through it. Whoever he'd been running from was still running.

Third, I sat down on my stool, because my legs had informed me they were resigning, and I held the roll under my desk lamp and looked at the seal.

Wax, deep green, stamped with a device I did not recognize and recognized instantly anyway, the way you know a face from a dream. A tower with no door. A crown hanging above it, unclaimed. Around the rim, in lettering so old the alphabet had drifted: words I could not read but somehow understood anyway, the way you understand a scream in a language you don't speak.

I knew the seal. That is the sentence I keep circling. I *knew* it. Not from ledgers — I told you, it isn't in any ledger, that's the whole point of it — but from somewhere else. From the place in me where the guild puts the things it doesn't want written down. Every clerk develops one. A little dark drawer behind the eyes where we file the transactions that never officially happened. The memory bought from the mother who couldn't afford to keep her child's first word. The year purchased out of a prisoner so his sentence would feel shorter to him than to the state. The storm in a bottle, and what the storm had been doing before we bottled it, and whose valley it had been watering when we capped it.

I keep those accounts too. Off-book. In the drawer. And somewhere in that drawer, folded small, was the shape of that seal, and I do not know how it got there, and that frightened me more than the blood did.

Fourth — and here is where fear hardened into decision, the way wax hardens into a seal, quick and irreversible and shaped by whatever presses it — I heard boots on the stairs.

Guild boots. I know the sound the way a shepherd knows his dogs. Two sets, descending at the unhurried pace of men who believe the building belongs to them, which it does. Enforcers. At half past eleven. On the night a stranger dies in my counting room. Coincidence is a currency the guild does not accept, and neither, that night, did I.

I had perhaps thirty seconds. I have done remarkable things in thirty seconds before — reconciled a decade of falsified entries during a fire drill, once — but nothing quite like this. Because the obvious move was to stand up, raise my hands, and say: *gentlemen, a man came in, he gave me this, I know nothing, I am furniture.* The obvious move was to be the ledger again. Blank. Accurate. Complicit.

Instead I opened the false page.

Every counting room in the guild has one. It's not corruption exactly; it's tradition. A page sewn into the binding that reads, to any casual inspection, like a routine inventory — in my case, a reconciliation of bottled weather, quarter three, discrepancies noted and resolved. But the stitching is a seam, and the seam opens, and inside there is a space exactly as thick as two sheets of vellum, and in thirty years no auditor has ever found mine, because auditors find what they expect to find, and what they expect to find is an honest clerk who is slightly boring.

I am slightly boring. I have worked very hard at it. It is the finest craft I know.

My hands were steady. I remember being surprised by that. The oilskin roll slid into the gap like it had been measured for it, which of course it hadn't, unless you believe the dead man had, which I was beginning to. I pressed the seam flat. I smoothed the page. I picked up my pen.

The door opened on a man writing.

"Grief, Ishikawa." The tall one — Vetch, enforcer second class, a face like a shut ledger himself — took in the scene with professional slowness. The body. The blood. Me, unmoved, mid-entry. "Yours?"

"A stranger," I said. My voice came out level. I have never been prouder or more disgusted by anything in my life. "Collapsed. Dead before he said anything worth recording."

"Anything at all?"

"He apologized for the floor."

Vetch grunted. His partner, a wide woman named Brakka whose job title was officially *asset recovery specialist* and unofficially *hands*, moved past me into the room with the unhurried confidence of someone searching a place she already owned. She looked behind the filing cabinet. She looked under the desk. She looked, briefly and unpleasantly, at me, the way you look at a strongbox you're deciding whether to crack.

"He came in the front?" she said.

"The front."

"Wet through. Bleeding. Dying." She crouched by the body and turned one grey hand over, examining the palm, the fingertips, as though the answers might be printed there. "And he walked past the taverns, past the pawnshops, past six blocks of warm dry places to die, to come here. To this room. To you." She looked up. Her eyes were the color of dishwater and just as hard to see the bottom of. "Why you, Ishikawa?"

"Because I'm on the ground floor," I said, "and the stairs are steep."

She held my gaze for a long moment. I counted her blinks. Three. I have read that liars blink more. I resolved to blink less, which is the kind of thing you think when terror is running the arithmetic.

"Search him," Vetch said, meaning the corpse, and they did, thoroughly, turning out pockets, running fingers along seams, and I stood at my desk and wrote in the ledger — actually wrote, real entries, quarter-three rainfall receipts — while two guild enforcers stripped a dead man in my counting room, and the thing he died delivering lay twelve inches beneath my elbow, hidden inside a lie I had sewn years ago for reasons I could no longer entirely recall.

They found coins. They found a ferry token. They found, tucked in his boot, a scrap of paper that Brakka read aloud with a snort: *"O.I., ground floor, after midnight."*

"Well," said Vetch. "He was expected somewhere."

"By O.I.," said Brakka, and turned to look at me again, and I felt the drawer behind my eyes rattle on its hinges.

"O.I.," I agreed pleasantly, "is a common set of initials. Half the guild's creditors are named Ostler or Ivemere. If you intend to audit the city's correspondence over a corpse, I'll fetch you a form."

It was, I think, the word *audit* that saved me. Nothing empties an enforcer's interest faster than the suggestion of paperwork. Vetch muttered something about reporting it upstairs, Brakka said *report whatever you like, I'm going to drink*, and between them they hauled the body out — no ceremony; the guild disposes of unclaimed dead the same way it disposes of bad debt, quietly and at a loss — and the door closed behind them, and their boots climbed the stairs, and I sat alone in my counting room with the rain on the barred window and my heart going like a debtor's knocker.

* * *

I did not sleep. I want that recorded too, since apparently I'm keeping minutes of my own trial.

I sat until the lamps burned low and the rain stopped and the sky over the guildhall went the grey of old vellum, and I took out the oilskin roll, and I untied the cord, and I looked at what a man had carried through a wet city and a locked door and the last four seconds of his life to bring to me.

A map. Of course a map. Drawn on skin — I chose not to determine whose — in inks that shifted when the lamplight moved, so that the routes seemed to crawl, so that the vault marked at its heart seemed less like a place than like an eye deciding whether to open. Beneath the capital. Beneath everything. Down where the foundations forget what they're holding up.

And around the edges, in a cramped hand I suspected belonged to the man now lying in the guild's cold-cart, annotations. Warnings. And one line, underlined twice, that I must have read twenty times before dawn:

*The key walks. Find her before they do. Her name is Astrid.*

I am a bookkeeper. I know what it means when an entry appears in two books at once. It means someone is owed, or someone is lying, or someone is about to be collected. By morning I understood that the map had entered my ledger — the false one, the true one, the one behind my eyes — and that every power in this city keeps a book of its own, and that all of them, sooner or later, send men with boots to collect.

I could still burn it. That thought came too, sitting there in the grey light. Feed it to the stove, report the break-in, spend the rest of my life being slightly boring in a smaller town. Fear recommended it strongly. Fear has excellent handwriting and cites precedent.

But I kept hearing him say *she*, and I kept seeing that seal, and I kept feeling the shape of it in the drawer behind my eyes where the guild makes me store the things it denies doing — and I realized, with the particular nausea of a man who finds his own signature on a contract he doesn't remember signing, that I was already involved. Had been, perhaps, for years. The map hadn't come to a stranger. It had come home.

So I sewed the seam shut, and I trimmed the lamp, and I prepared my face into its usual expression of mild administrative sorrow, and I went up to answer the summons I had known since midnight would come.

Because at dawn, of course, a note arrived from the office of Guildmaster Corvane, requesting Master Ishikawa's presence regarding *a matter of irregular intake*.

Fear had hardened into a decision somewhere in the small hours, and the decision had a shape, and the shape was a lie I was about to tell a man who buys and sells what people are.

I pocketed my pen. I locked the false page. And I climbed the stairs toward the office where I would discover precisely how much of my soul the guild already held on account.
