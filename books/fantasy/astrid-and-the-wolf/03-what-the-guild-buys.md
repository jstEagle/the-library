## 3. What the Guild Buys

The summons came at dawn, which was the guild's way of saying *we know*.

Nobody in the Guild of Impossible Things is summoned at dawn for anything pleasant. Dawn summons are for clerks who have seen something they shouldn't have, or failed to see something they should. I lay in my narrow bed above the counting room and listened to the knocker-rat scratching at my door — the guild breeds them specially, little creatures with a talent for finding people who are trying very hard to be elsewhere — and I performed the small arithmetic of cowardice that had governed my life for eleven years.

Option one: answer the door, walk to the guildhall, and lie to Corvane's face.

Option two: climb out the window with a wax-sealed map hidden in a false ledger page and run until the world ran out.

I want it noted, by whatever celestial accountant keeps the books on men like me, that option two occurred to me first. It simply lost. Cowardice is not the absence of courage; it is courage that has been audited and found insolvent. I dressed, hid the map more securely — sewn into the spine of my second-worst ledger, because nobody ever confiscates a man's second-worst ledger — and opened the door.

The knocker-rat looked up at me with its little brass eyes and scratched one final scratch, almost apologetic, then scuttled away to find someone less difficult to locate.

* * *

The guildhall in Ashgate is built like a cathedral that lost its faith and took up commerce. Where other buildings put saints in their niches, we put scales. Where they rang bells for matins, we rang them for market opening. The great doors were carved with the guild's motto, which is *All Things May Be Owned*, and beneath it, in smaller letters added later by some clerk with a sense of humor history does not record: *Terms Negotiable.*

I walked through those doors with my heart going like a debtor's fist on a door, and I did what I always do in frightening situations: I counted things. Fourteen steps across the entry hall. Three guards, bored, one picking his teeth. Two braziers burning memory-scented coals — you can buy the smell of your grandmother's kitchen here, if you're sentimental and solvent. One corridor leading down to Corvane's audience chamber, which the senior clerks called the Confessional, though never where he could hear.

In the Confessional, Guildmaster Corvane sat behind a desk of black glass, and before him stood a soldier.

That was the part I hadn't expected. The summons was about me — I was sure of it, sickly sure — but there was a soldier standing at attention in front of the desk, and he was crying without any expression on his face at all, tears running down into the collar of a uniform I recognized as Crown issue, the kind worn by garrison men from the border forts. He held a small lead-lined box in both hands, the way you'd hold an offering, or a wound.

"Oscar Ishikawa," Corvane said, without looking at me. "Good. Stand there. Watch and learn."

Corvane is not what people expect when they imagine a master of thieves. People expect menace, muscle, a scar with a story. Corvane is a soft-spoken man of about sixty with ink-stained fingers and the patient manner of a village schoolmaster, and this is precisely why he terrifies everyone who understands him. Menaces threaten you. Corvane *appraises* you. Being looked at by Corvane is being entered into a ledger.

"The sergeant here," Corvane continued, "has come to sell us something. Sergeant, tell the clerk what you brought."

The soldier's jaw worked. When he spoke, his voice was flat as slate. "Third Battalion, Fort Meridian. We were sent to Hollowmere." A pause. "There was a village called Hollowmere."

"And?"

"And we burned it." The words came out of him like stones pulled from a riverbed, smooth and cold and worn blank. "Orders said the village was harboring rebels. It wasn't. It was just a village. There were children. I remember—" His voice caught, not with grief but with effort, like a man hauling on a rope. "I remember a girl in a yellow dress. She was hiding in the well house. I—"

"That will do," Corvane said gently. "You wish to forget."

"I wish to forget." The soldier said it the way other men say prayers.

I had watched transactions like this from the doorway of the counting room a hundred times. Small ones, mostly. A widow selling her grief over a dead husband because grief doesn't feed children. A drunk selling the memory of the night his wife left, and then coming back six months later to sell the memory of having sold it, because the gap bothered him worse than the loss. Ordinary commerce in ordinary sorrows. Bookkeeping, I told myself. I kept the accounts. What passed between buyer and seller was no business of mine.

But I had never stood this close. And I had never heard a massacre described in the tone of a man reading a shipping manifest.

"Price agreed?" Corvane asked me, because that was my function in the ritual. Confirm the ledger.

"Two hundred crowns," I said. My voice came out steady, which surprised me. "Plus the standard severance — the seller retains knowledge that the transaction occurred, but not its content."

"Very good." Corvane turned back to the soldier. "Sergeant. You understand that once sold, the memory becomes guild property. We may store it, study it, or dispose of it as we see fit. You will sign here, and here, and initial the clause regarding resale rights."

"Resale," the soldier repeated dully.

"You'd be amazed," Corvane said, with something almost like warmth, "how often a man sells a thing and another man comes looking to buy it back. Grief is cyclical, Sergeant. We merely provide the market."

The soldier signed. I witnessed. My pen moved across the ledger and each letter felt like a small nail going into a coffin I was building for myself, though I couldn't yet have told you whose coffin it was.

Then the extraction itself. I won't describe it fully; descriptions of extraction are against guild policy and, frankly, against decency. Suffice it to say that the soldier made a sound I have never been able to unhear, a sound like a word being pulled out of a man by the root, and afterward he stood straighter. Calmer. He thanked us. He actually thanked us, pressed Corvane's hand with both of his own, and walked out of the Confessional lighter than he'd come in, two hundred crowns richer and one girl in a yellow dress poorer.

And Corvane opened the lead-lined box, looked at what was inside — I saw only a shimmer, a curl of grey-gold light like breath on a cold morning — and smiled.

"Well now," he said softly. "Well, well, well."

* * *

He dismissed the guards. That was the first bad sign. Corvane dismisses guards only when what he's about to say is either worthless or dangerous, and Corvane never says anything worthless.

"You lied to me last night, Oscar."

Here is what I had prepared. Eleven years of keeping ledgers for dangerous men teaches you preparation the way drowning teaches you water. I had a story about the stranger: a beggar, unknown to me, who collapsed in my doorway, bled on my step, and died before I could fetch help. I had details ready — the color of his coat, the amount of blood, the exact hour — all true, all harmless, all arranged so that the truth itself would serve as my shield. The best lies are load-bearing truths.

"No, Guildmaster," I said. "A stranger collapsed at my door. I tried to save him. He died."

"Mm." Corvane turned the lead box over in his hands, once, twice, like a man shuffling cards. "And in the course of trying to save him, you searched his pockets?"

"I checked for identification. To notify next of kin."

"Naturally. And did he carry anything?"

"A knife. Some coin. Nothing else."

Corvane set the box down on the black glass desk, and the sound it made was very small and very loud. "Nothing else. No papers? No documents?"

"He was a beggar, Guildmaster. Beggars don't carry documents."

"Beggars don't bleed from crossbow bolts either, Oscar, and yet this man had a quarrel wound in him cut by Crown fletching, which means somebody with access to inquisitorial armory wanted him stopped. Beggars don't run three miles through the Rookery with a wound like that. And beggars—" he looked up at me then, and his eyes were mild and bottomless, "—beggars do not die whispering names to the guild's chief ledger-keeper. Because you reported that, of course. The name he whispered. Standard procedure. Every clerk knows that a dying man's last word may be an asset."

My mouth was dry. "He didn't speak."

"He didn't speak." Corvane nodded slowly, accepting it, and that acceptance was worse than accusation. "No. Of course not. You'd have reported it if he had. You're the most honest man in this building, Oscar Ishikawa. It's why I keep you. Everyone else in the guild lies to me professionally. You lie to me badly, which is far more useful, because I can always tell."

He let that sit. Outside, the market bell rang nine times, and somewhere below us the day's first memories were changing hands, little pieces of people being weighed and priced and shelved.

"Now." He tapped the lead box. "Do you know what this is?"

"A memory, Guildmaster. Class four, violent content, single owner, freshly extracted."

"It's a *surplus*," Corvane corrected. "The Crown pays us to take these. Did you know that? Fort Meridian has a standing contract. Every unpleasantness the army produces, we absorb. They call it morale maintenance. The soldiers sleep better, the officers file cleaner reports, and the Crown gets—" he patted the box, "—this. Do you know what the Crown does with them?"

"Stores them. In the deep archive."

"Stores some. The rest come back to us at auction. Private buyers. Old families." He leaned forward. "There is a gentleman in the Hill Ward, Oscar, who has purchased eleven memories of the same war. Eleven different soldiers' recollections of the same campaign. He sits in a room full of other men's horrors and he calls it collecting. And do you know why old families collect war memories?"

I said nothing. My hands, folded behind my back, had gone cold.

"Because wars are *assets*," Corvane said. "Grievance is capital. Somewhere in this city there are men whose grandfathers were wronged by men whose grandfathers did the wronging, and none of them remembers exactly why, but they all know it matters, and they would pay anything — anything — to hold the proof in their hands again. Proof is leverage. Leverage is price. And price, Oscar—" he spread his ink-stained fingers, the schoolmaster finishing the lesson, "—is the only prayer this guild has ever said."

Something was shifting under my feet, some floor I had stood on for eleven years quietly tilting. "You're saying we don't just keep memories. We... place them. Curate them."

"I'm saying," Corvane said patiently, "that history is not what happened. History is what someone was willing to pay to have remembered. The Crown buys forgetting. The old houses buy selective remembering. And the guild—" he smiled, "—the guild provides liquidity. We are not thieves, Oscar. Thieves steal things. We decide what things *mean*. Now."

He slid the lead box aside and drew out a sheet of paper. "The sergeant mentioned a village. Hollowmere. Ringing any bells?"

"No, Guildmaster."

"No. Nor me. Nor anyone, which is itself interesting — a whole village, garrison records confirm its existence, tax rolls list forty-one households, and not one living soul remembers it. Not even the soldiers who burned it, after we've finished with them. Forty-one households, erased so cleanly that the erasure doesn't even show." He tapped the paper. "Except in here. Except in what the sergeant sold us this morning. The last copy of Hollowmere in the world, sitting in a lead box on my desk, worth perhaps fifty crowns as a curiosity." His smile widened by a degree. "Or considerably more, to the right buyer."

The floor tilted further. I thought of the map sewn into my second-worst ledger, three floors above us, warm from my body. I thought of the dying man's lips shaping a word. *Astrid.* I had told myself I was carrying a dead man's parcel. Standing in the Confessional, watching Corvane weigh a massacre like cheese, I understood for the first time that I might be carrying something else entirely: a correction. A line item the guild had written off, and someone had died trying to restore to the account.

"Guildmaster," I said carefully, "the stranger. If he carried nothing — if there's truly nothing to find — why summon me at dawn?"

Corvane studied me for a long moment. Then he laughed, softly, genuinely, and it was somehow the worst sound of the entire morning.

"There it is," he said. "There's the honest man. Yes, Oscar, I'll be plain with you, since plainness is wasted on everyone else. Someone killed that man on our doorstep. Someone with Crown fletching. Which means the Crown believes he was carrying something worth killing for, and the Crown has asked us — politely, and with a generous retainer — to determine whether anything reached guild hands before he died." He folded his hands. "So I am asking you, ledger-keeper, keeper of my accounts, the honest man who lies badly: did anything reach guild hands?"

And I looked at Guildmaster Corvane — at the soft voice and the ink-stained fingers and the massacre in a lead box — and I understood that he already knew the answer. He was not asking to learn. He was asking to watch me choose. Whatever I said next would be entered into a ledger he kept that I had never seen, the one where Corvane recorded not what men owned, but what they were.

"He carried nothing," I said. "He died alone. I buried him myself this morning, before dawn, in the pauper's field. Unmarked. There was nothing to notify anyone about."

It was the best lie of my life, and it was mostly true, and Corvane listened to all of it with his head slightly tilted, like a man tasting wine.

"Unmarked," he repeated. "How very tidy. Well." He closed the ledger on his desk with a gentle click. "Then there's nothing to worry about. Go home, Oscar. Balance your books. And Oscar — if a beggar's ghost ever whispers anything else to you, do report it. Standard procedure."

I bowed and walked out of the Confessional on legs that belonged to someone else, past the scales in their niches, past the carved motto and its cynical footnote, out into the morning street where ordinary people were buying and selling ordinary days.

Behind me, in the dark, a massacre sat in a lead box waiting for auction, and a whole village named Hollowmere existed nowhere in the world except in the guild's inventory.

And upstairs, sewn into the spine of my second-worst ledger, a dead man's map lay against the wool and thread, patient as a debt.

I had lied to Corvane and survived. I should have felt relief. Instead, climbing the stairs to my counting room, I understood something that changed the shape of everything: Corvane hadn't believed me. He had *priced* me. And somewhere in the city, he was already setting the terms under which he would collect.

I reached my door and stopped.

The lock was scratched. Fresh scratches, bright metal showing through old patina, and the door stood open exactly one inch.

Someone had been in my counting room while I was lying to the guildmaster. Someone who knew precisely where to look.

From inside, faintly, came the sound of pages turning.
