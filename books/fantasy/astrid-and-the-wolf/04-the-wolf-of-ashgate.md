## 4. The Wolf of Ashgate

There are three kinds of people who come to Ashgate looking for me: the ones who want to hire me, the ones who want to arrest me, and the ones who want to kill me. The trick is that all three start out polite.

The man in the corner of the Drowned Rat that morning was polite in the way of a man rehearsing a eulogy. He sat with his back to the wall — sensible — and his hands flat on the table where I could see them — flatter than sensible. Nobody keeps their hands visible unless they've been told to by someone scarier than me, and there are perhaps four such someones in the world.

I finished my breakfast first. Priorities.

"You're Vane," he said as I passed his table. Not a question. People who ask questions give you room to lie; this one had been briefed.

"Depends who's asking and what they think I did."

"Corvane wants you found." He slid a purse across the table. It made the particular heavy clink of guild coin, which is to say it sounded like money that had already been spent on someone's soul. "A clerk. Oscar Ishikawa. He took something that doesn't belong to him."

"And what would that be?"

"A ledger."

I looked at the purse. Guild coin buys a lot of things, but mostly it buys silence, and I have never once managed to spend silence on bread. "Find a clerk," I said. "Bring back a ledger. That's it? No knife work?"

"The guild prefers its property returned intact." His smile was thin as vellum. "The clerk too, if convenient."

*If convenient.* In my experience, when a man says *if convenient*, he means *if inconvenient, kill him*, and when a guild says it, they mean *kill him and bill us for the shovel.*

"I'll need half up front."

He counted it out without haggling, which should have been my first warning. Haggling is how honest transactions stay honest. A man who doesn't haggle isn't buying a service; he's renting a weapon.

His name, he said, was Merrick. He wore grey like it was a uniform, and under the smell of ink and road dust there was something else — something cold and clean, like snow off stone. I'd smelled it before. I couldn't say where. That's the trouble with my life these days: half of it is missing, and the missing half keeps leaving fingerprints on everything.

* * *

Let me tell you about the gaps, since apparently we're being intimate.

Most people remember their childhoods as a long corridor of rooms. Mine is a corridor with doors painted on the wall. I know the shape of a mother — the idea of her — but no face, no voice, no kitchen. I know I was knighted because I have the sword, and the sword has a name burned into the tang: *Vigilance*. But I don't remember the ceremony. I don't remember the king's hand or the oaths or the hangover after. I know I served at a place called Cael Hold for six years because my body remembers every stair in it, every watch rotation, the exact number of paces from the gate to the well. My feet remember. My head just holds the receipt.

And then there's the wolf.

It sits between my shoulder blades, low on my spine, and it is not a tattoo. Tattoos sit *on* you; this thing is *in* the skin, grown through it like roots through a wall. Fur-textured scarring, grey going silver at the tips, shaped unmistakably like a wolf mid-leap — head thrown forward, jaws open, as if it had been caught diving into me rather than out. I found it at nineteen, in a barracks mirror, and I have never found anyone who could tell me where it came from. I asked a priest once. He looked at it for a long time and then asked me to leave, gently, the way you ask a question you're afraid of.

Some nights I dream about running. Not fleeing — running. Four-footed, snow-deep, joyous running, with something enormous pacing beside me that I love so much it hurts. I wake up with my hands curled like they're holding reins that aren't there, and my shoulder blades ache, and once — only once — I woke up standing in the yard outside the inn, barefoot in the frost, facing north, having walked there in my sleep without waking a single board.

North. Whatever's north of Ashgate has been calling me for years, patient as tide.

So yes. When a grey man offers me guild coin to hunt a thief, I take it, partly because rent is real and pride is theoretical, and partly because guild business goes strange places, and strange places are where lost things turn up. Including, if I'm lucky, myself.

* * *

I worked the thief the way I work everything: backwards.

Oscar Ishikawa, ledger-keeper, Guild of Impossible Things. The name got me nothing at first — clerks are furniture, and nobody describes furniture — until I leaned on a toll-keeper south of the city who owed me a favor from an incident involving her husband, a debt collector, and a very memorable evening. She remembered Ishikawa passing through two days ahead of me. Nervous. Alone. Carrying a satchel he wouldn't let out of arm's reach even to eat.

"He kept checking over his shoulder," she said. "Not like a guilty man. Like a man who knows exactly what's behind him and wishes he didn't."

"What's behind him?"

"That's the thing." She lowered her voice, though the road was empty. "Nothing was. Every time. Nothing at all. And he'd look anyway."

I know that look. I wear it most mornings.

I picked up his trail properly at a waystation where he'd paid for a room and slept four hours in his boots. A clerk who sleeps in his boots isn't running scared; he's running *decided*. Those are different animals. Scared men zigzag. Decided men go straight, and straight lines are easy to follow if you don't mind hard riding.

What I minded was what I found at the third stop. A dead horse — not his, someone else's, ridden hard and put down wet at the side of the road with a grey feather pinned under its saddle strap. Guild sign. And further on, at a crossroads shrine, a second marker: chalk, not feather, a small neat symbol I didn't recognize drawn inside the shrine's alcove, fresh enough to smudge.

Someone else was hunting my clerk. Someone organized, mounted, and moving faster than a man on foot could manage, which meant they were ahead of him, which meant they weren't hunting.

They were waiting.

I sat in the shrine alcove a while, working it through, and while I sat, my thumb found the wolf scar without my permission. It does that sometimes. Rubs itself like a dog working at a burr. And a thought surfaced, whole and unwelcome, the way thoughts do in quiet places:

*I know that chalk mark.*

Not recognized it. *Knew* it. Knew it the way you know your own signature — somewhere below memory, in whatever cellar of me holds the things that were taken out through the front door. My hand wanted to copy it. My hand knew the stroke order.

I didn't copy it. I sat on both hands like a child and stared at the wall, and my heart went like a rabbit's, and the shrine's little god — a fat carved fellow with a broken nose — watched me with what I chose to interpret as sympathy.

Here is what I have learned about the holes in me: they are not empty. Empty holes don't itch. These itch. Something was written there once, and scraped away, and the scraping left grooves, and every so often my life runs along those grooves like a stylus finding old wax, and letters come up that nobody living wrote.

That night I dreamed of the running again. Snow, and the great glad stride of it, and the thing beside me — and this time, for the first time, it turned its head, and it had eyes like mine, and it said, in a voice I somehow understood without hearing: *You're close. Don't let them finish the job.*

I woke up on the floor. I never sleep on the floor. I sleep like a cat, in the middle of the bed, and I have never once in my adult life fallen out of it.

* * *

I caught up with Ishikawa at a smuggler's inn called the Ferryman's Rest, three days out of Ashgate, because of course it was a smuggler's inn. Thieves run to smugglers the way wounded animals run to water: it's where the paths already exist.

I'll be honest about what I expected. I expected a rat of a man, quick-eyed and oily, the kind who talks his way into trouble and books his way out. What I found, watching him from the shadows of the common room, was a tired young man eating stew like it was a duty he was performing for the stew's benefit, sitting with his back to the wall — my wall, my seat, the audacity — with a satchel strapped across his chest like a bandolier. He looked less like a thief than like a librarian who'd wandered into a war.

And he kept checking the door. Every few breaths. Not panicked. Counting. Cataloguing. A ledger-keeper's habit, maybe — tracking entries and exits — or maybe just a man who'd learned recently that the world contains things worth counting exits against.

I gave him until the fire burned down, then followed him up to his room, because I'm subtle like a landslide when I'm tired.

He heard me on the stairs. Credit where due — most marks don't. By the time my shoulder hit the door he'd wedged a chair under the handle, which bought him exactly the four seconds it deserved, and then I was in, and he was backed against the window with a candlestick raised in both hands like a man defending a cathedral.

"Don't," he said. "Please. I haven't done anything."

"Nobody ever has." I let him see the sword. "Astrid Vane. The guild hired me to bring you in. Along with whatever's in the satchel."

Something crossed his face — fear, yes, but underneath it a flicker of something worse. Recognition. He looked at me the way you look at a word that's on the tip of your tongue.

"Do I know you?" he said.

"No."

"You're sure? Because—" He stopped himself, visibly, the way a man stops himself from stepping off a ledge. "Never mind. It doesn't matter."

But it mattered. I felt it matter, in the grooves. Somewhere under the scraped wax of me, this tired clerk with his candlestick had a page of his own, and neither of us could read it.

"Give me the satchel," I said, "and this stays civil."

"If I give you the satchel, people die."

"People die either way. That's the world. The satchel decides how many."

"It's not — it's not what you think. It's a map. To a place. And the guild can't have it, and the Crown can't have it, and honestly—" he laughed, high and cracked, "—honestly, I'm not entirely sure *I* should have it, but here I am."

"Show me."

"Why would I show you?"

"Because I'm the one holding the sword, and because if you don't, I take it, and then you'll have shown me anyway, plus a bruise."

He hesitated. And then — and this is the part I keep turning over, the part that won't sit still — he opened the satchel himself. Unstrapped it, unwrapped the oilcloth, and held out a roll of heavy paper sealed with dark green wax.

And the seal hit me like a fist.

Green wax, pressed with a sigil: a circle broken by a single vertical line, and within the circle's lower arc, small as a fingernail, the shape of a wolf.

My knees decided independently that the floor was a good idea. I sat down on his bed without meaning to, and the room tilted, and the candle flame stretched tall and still as if the air itself had stopped to listen.

I'd seen that seal before. Not on paper. Under my own hand. I could feel it — the press of wax, warm, the sigil cutting into my palm, someone else's voice saying words I couldn't reach: *by blood and consent, witness and keeper—*

"Hey." Ishikawa had crouched down in front of me. He looked terrified, but he crouched anyway. "Hey. You've seen this before."

"I don't know what I've seen." My voice came out wrong. Scraped. "That's rather the problem."

"How much do they pay you?" he said quietly. "The guild. For bringing me in."

"Enough."

"Is it enough for whatever that was?" He nodded at my hands, which were shaking. "Because I've spent a week reading documents about that seal, and none of them say what it is, except one — one line, in a margin, in handwriting older than the guild — and the line says the seal answers to blood. Specific blood." He swallowed. "Yours looks like it fits the lock better than mine does."

Outside, far off down the valley road, a horn sounded. Once. Long. The kind of horn that means *we have found what we were looking for*, and the kind of silence after it that means *and now we come*.

Ishikawa closed his eyes. "They're early," he whispered.

I stood up. My legs worked, mostly. The wolf on my back was burning like a brand, and the grooves in me were singing, and every instinct I owned — the ones I could account for and the ones I couldn't — pulled the same direction, north, always north, toward whatever waited at the end of that map.

"Here's my new price," I heard myself say. "Forget the guild's coin. You're going to tell me what that seal opens, and why my blood fits it, and then you and I are leaving through the window before whoever blew that horn finishes their supper."

"They'll follow us."

"Good." I sheathed Vigilance and swung open the shutters onto the cold. "I've been following something my whole life. It's about time it followed me back."
