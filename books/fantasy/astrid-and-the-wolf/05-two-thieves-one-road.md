## 5. Two Thieves, One Road

### Oscar

The inn was called the Drowned Rat, which told you everything you needed to know about the honesty of its clientele and the drainage of its cellar. I had been hiding there for six days, which is four days longer than a careful man hides anywhere, and I had begun to suspect that my carefulness was the thing most likely to get me killed.

Here is what I knew by then: the stranger who died on my counting-room floor had a name I never learned and a map I could not stop thinking about. The guild enforcers who came for his body asked questions with the gentle patience of men who already know your answers are wrong. And Guildmaster Corvane had looked at me across his desk of black oak and said, *Ishikawa, you have the face of a man carrying something heavy,* and I had smiled the smile of a bookkeeper and said nothing at all, because silence is the only lie that leaves no ink.

So I ran. Bookkeepers are not built for running. My boots were wrong, my coat was wrong, and my hands — hands made for balancing columns, not gripping knives — shook every time the door of the common room swung open.

It swung open now.

I did not look up. Looking up is how you die in places like the Drowned Rat. I kept my eyes on my cup of wine that tasted like vinegar that had given up on life, and I listened. The footsteps were wrong for a smuggler. Smugglers shuffle; they announce themselves with apology. These steps were even, deliberate, placed like coins in a counting tray. One set. Unhurried. Coming toward me.

A chair scraped back across from me without an invitation. Someone sat down.

"You're Ishikawa," said a woman's voice. "The ledger-keeper."

"I'm afraid you've mistaken me for someone interesting," I said, still studying my wine.

"Look at me."

There are voices that ask and voices that inform. Hers informed. I looked up.

She was not beautiful in any way a ballad would bother with, which is to say she was the kind of striking that makes rooms go quiet and then pretend they hadn't. Grey eyes, a jaw like a verdict, hair the color of wet iron pulled back without ceremony. She wore a sword the way other people wear bad news — openly, and daring you to comment. On her left forearm, where her sleeve had ridden up, was a scar. It was long, and old, and it branched, and if you squinted — and I am paid to notice things, so I squinted — it looked like something. A shape. An animal, maybe.

"Astrid Vane," I said, before I could stop myself, because recognizing people out loud is a habit ledgers give you. Then I watched her face change, and understood I had just done something very stupid. "Which is to say, I don't know who that is."

"You do, though." She leaned forward. Up close her eyes were the grey of a sky deciding whether to snow. "That's the interesting part. Everyone in this business knows the Wolf of Ashgate. Disgraced knight. Takes contracts nobody else will. Never loses." She turned her hand over on the table between us, palm up, almost an offering. "And here's what nobody knows about me, ledger-keeper. I don't remember why they call me the Wolf."

I should have run then. Instead I said, "People forget things. It's practically fashionable."

"Not like this." Her voice stayed level but something under it moved, like ice shifting on a river. "I remember my mother's kitchen. I remember my oath ceremony, every word of it. I remember eleven years of soldiering down to the smell of the tents. And then there's a gap. Two years, maybe more. No edges, no ache, no dream fragments. Just — smooth. Like someone took a knife to a loaf and the loaf grew back around the cut." She closed her fist. "You know what does that. Your guild does that."

"The guild trades in memories," I said carefully. "Voluntarily. Fairly catalogued. There are forms—"

"There are always forms," she said. "Forms are how thieves convince themselves they're merchants."

I want it noted, wherever such things are noted, that I did not flinch. Much.

"Corvane posted the contract himself," she went on. "Find the clerk who fled with stolen property. Dead preferred, alive worth double. The description was precise. Thin man, ink-stained fingers, walks like he's apologizing to the floor." She glanced down, pointedly, at my ink-stained fingers resting on my apologetic walk. "I tracked you through three towns. You tip badly and you check the exits constantly. You're either guilty or you're the most paranoid innocent man in the province."

"And if I claimed the latter?"

"Then I'd say: innocents don't pay double."

### Astrid

He didn't beg. I'll give him that. Most men, when I put the truth of their situation on the table, start bargaining with God, with me, with anyone holding a bucket. The clerk just folded his hands like I'd presented him with an invoice.

"Before we proceed," he said, "I want you to understand that whatever Corvane offered you, he intends to pay a fraction of it. He pays a fraction of everything. It's in the ledgers. I would know; I keep them."

"Convince me you're worth more alive than dead."

"That depends entirely on what you want."

"What I want," I said, "is to know who cut two years out of my head and left me walking around with the hole shaped like me."

Something happened to his face then. Not fear exactly — recognition. The look of a man who has found the entry he's been searching for and wishes he hadn't.

"Show me the contract," he said.

"Why?"

"Because I believe," he said slowly, "that I may be able to offer you a better price."

I slid the parchment across. He read it twice. His lips moved on the second pass, the way scholars' lips move, and I watched the color leave his face one shade at a time.

"This isn't a bounty," he said quietly. "This is a retrieval clause. Look at the wording. *Property of sentimental value, irreplaceable.* Corvane doesn't write like this about stolen goods. He writes like this about—" He stopped. His eyes came up to mine, and for the first time since he'd sat down across from me, the clerk looked genuinely afraid, and it wasn't for himself. "What did you lose? What's missing?"

"Two years. Maybe more. Why?"

"Because the last man who touched that map died whispering a name," he said. "And the name was yours."

The room got very small and very loud all at once. Somewhere behind me a dice game argued over a throw; somewhere ahead of me my own heartbeat counted out something I couldn't read yet.

"What map?" I said.

He hesitated. I let my hand rest on the table near my knife, and he noticed, because he noticed everything, and made a small defeated sound like a man surrendering a fortification he'd only just finished building.

"Of course," he muttered. "Of course you'd feel it. That's how these things work, isn't it. The seal calls to what it sealed." He reached into his coat, moving with the exaggerated slowness of a man demonstrating harmlessness, and drew out a folded square of oilskin. Inside was a map — old, dense, drawn on something that wasn't quite paper — and pressed into its corner, a seal in dark red wax.

I knew the seal.

I knew it the way you know a face in a crowd before you know why — before the name arrives, before the history, just the sudden drop in the stomach, the body shouting *him, her, that* while the mind scrambles to catch up. A circle broken by a wolf's head, jaws open, and beneath it a line of script in no alphabet I could read and every letter of which I somehow recognized.

The room tilted. Something surfaced in me — not a memory, not yet, more like the shadow a memory casts — snow, and a door underground, and my own voice saying words I couldn't hear, and blood on wax, my blood, red as that seal—

I came back to myself with my hand flat on the table and the clerk watching me with an expression I'd learn to know well in the weeks after: the look of a man doing arithmetic he hates.

"You saw something," he said.

"I saw a seal."

"You saw more than a seal." He folded the map away again, unhurried, infuriating. "Astrid Vane. The Wolf of Ashgate. The woman whose erased memories are, according to a dying man's last breath, the key to the Vault of Unremembered Kings." He said it lightly, testing each word like a plank over a drop. "Corvane sent you to retrieve yourself. That's either the funniest thing I've ever heard or the cruelest, and knowing Corvane, both."

"Explain. Slowly. From the beginning."

"It's a long story."

"I've killed men for shorter ones."

"Yes," he said, "but you haven't yet killed me, which suggests you're curious, which suggests I have time." He signaled the barkeep with two fingers — the first competent thing I'd seen him do — and ordered a bottle of the wine that didn't taste like regret. "My name is Oscar Ishikawa. I keep accounts for the Guild of Impossible Things. Bottled storms, traded years, borrowed luck, sold grief. Every impossible thing has a price and every price has a line in my ledger. I used to tell myself that keeping books wasn't the same as stealing." He poured two cups and pushed one toward me. "Six days ago a stranger bled out on my floor and handed me a map and a word, and now I'm telling myself different stories."

"What word?"

"You already know."

"*Astrid.*"

"*Astrid,*" he agreed, and drank.

* * *

### Oscar

Here is what I learned about Astrid Vane over the next hour, in the order I learned it:

One. She did not trust me, and she was right not to.

Two. She was smarter than her reputation suggested, which is saying something, because her reputation suggested quite a lot. When I explained the vault — the war that ended by being forgotten, the truth sealed beneath the ruined capital, the dying stranger who had chosen me, of all people, to carry the way there — she interrupted eleven times, and eight of the interruptions were the right questions.

Three. She wanted the same thing I wanted, which was to say she wanted her missing years back, and underneath that, though neither of us said it aloud, she wanted to know if she had been the thief or the robbed.

"So the guild wants the map," she said, turning her cup in slow circles. "The Crown wants the map buried. And the map apparently opens for me. Which means everyone hunting it will eventually realize they don't need the map at all." Her grey eyes lifted. "They need me."

"That is the conclusion I reached on day two," I admitted, "and it has not improved with age."

"And you came to me anyway. Sat in the worst inn in the province and waited for the woman hired to kill you."

"I didn't wait. I hid. Badly, apparently."

"Why?" she said. "Why not burn the map and vanish? You're a clerk. Vanishing is practically your trade."

I considered lying to her. I want to be honest in this account, even where I wasn't honest with her, so: I considered it, and I was good enough at it that she would likely have believed me. But there was something in the way she'd looked at the seal — that drop, that fall, that flash of a person surfacing from deep water — and I thought: *she knows what it is to have holes in herself. She is the only person alive who might understand what I carry.*

"Because a man died on my floor," I said, "and his blood is on my conscience, and the only way to wash it off is to finish what he started. Because Corvane curates history and sells it by the pound, and I've balanced his books for nine years pretending arithmetic is innocence. And because—" I stopped. Reconsidered. Went on anyway. "Because when you looked at that seal, you looked like a woman trying to remember a house she'd lived in. And I know that look. I see it every day in the guildhall, on the faces of people selling pieces of themselves to pay debts. I just never expected to see it on the person the pieces were taken *from*."

Silence. The dice game had ended; the inn had gone down to embers and drunks.

"You're a liar," she said at last.

"Yes."

"You lied to Corvane. You're probably lying to me right now."

"Almost certainly somewhere."

"And you think I should travel with you. Across half the country. Past the guild's enforcers and the Crown's inquisitors. With a man whose entire profession is taking things from people and writing down how much they got."

"When you phrase it that way—"

"I'm phrasing it the accurate way."

I spread my hands. "I can offer you a bargain instead of trust. Trust takes months; we may not have months. Here are the terms. I hold the map. You hold the sword. Neither of us turns on the other until we've seen what's in that vault — because I suspect, Astrid Vane, that what's in that vault is the answer to both our questions, and answers are worth more than either of us separately."

"And if your answer and mine turn out to be enemies?"

"Then we renegotiate at knifepoint, like civilized people."

She studied me for a long time. I have been studied by appraisers, by enforcers, by Guildmaster Corvane, who can price a man's soul to the copper. None of them looked at me the way she did — like she was reading a ledger and checking my columns against reality, line by line, looking for the elegant fraud.

"You copied the map," she said suddenly.

I blinked. "What?"

"You're a bookkeeper. Bookkeepers make copies. It's the first law of your religion. Somewhere on your person is a second version of that map, and you haven't mentioned it because you're keeping it as insurance." She leaned back, arms crossed, and I felt the peculiar shame of a fraud caught by someone who wasn't even angry. "Am I wrong?"

"...No."

"Good." She stood, dropped coins on the table for the bottle — overpaying, I noted, which contradicted everything I'd assumed about disgraced knights — and looked down at me with those winter-grey eyes. "Then we understand each other. You're a liar, Ishikawa. So am I. I lie to employers, you lie to ledgers, and we'll both be watching each other the whole road. But I want my years back, and you want your soul back, and the vault is the only place either of us is going to find them."

She slung her pack over her shoulder. At the foot of the stairs she paused, not turning around.

"Dawn," she said. "Don't be late. And Ishikawa — the copy. If I ever find out you've tried to sell it, trade it, or spend it, I won't kill you quickly, and I won't kill you at all until you've told me every lie you've ever written down. Understood?"

"Perfectly," I said. "You realize that's the most romantic threat anyone has ever made to me."

"I realize you talk too much for a dead man walking." And she climbed the stairs, boots even and deliberate, placing each step like a coin in a tray.

I sat alone with the empty bottle and the map against my ribs and the false page sewn into my coat lining, and I did the accounting, because accounting is how I pray. Assets: one map, one copy, one wolf. Liabilities: one guild, one Crown, one conscience accruing interest daily. And in the margin, in the little space where honest clerks note the figures that don't fit any column, I wrote the thing I would not say aloud even to myself:

*She remembered the seal. Which means part of her was never taken. Which means whoever cut her left the lock inside her on purpose.*

And locks, I knew better than anyone alive, are made to be opened by someone.

Upstairs, a floorboard creaked once, softly, like a question.
