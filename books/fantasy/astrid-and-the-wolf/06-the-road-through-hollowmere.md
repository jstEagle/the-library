## 6. The Road Through Hollowmere

The trouble with traveling with a memory-thief is that you start wondering which of your own thoughts are yours.

I'd been on the road with Oscar Ishikawa for four days when I caught myself doing it — standing at a crossroads sign, staring at the word *Hollowmere* carved into weathered oak, and thinking: *did I put that thought there, or did he?* He was twenty paces back, fussing with his pack straps like a man who believed fidgeting made him invisible. It didn't. It made him look exactly like what he was: a clerk pretending to be an adventurer.

"You're staring at the sign," I said.

"I'm reading the sign."

"People don't read signs for that long."

"Careful people do." He caught up, breathless from forty paces of flat road, which told you most of what you needed to know about Oscar Ishikawa's relationship with physical exertion. "Hollowmere. That's on the map."

"Everything is on the map. The map has more roads than the country does." I tapped the wax-sealed thing in his satchel, the one I'd already decided I'd be carrying by nightfall whether he agreed or not. "What's Hollowmere?"

He hesitated. That was the thing about Oscar — he hesitated *before* every lie, a little hitch, like a cart wheel hitting a rut. Once you saw it, you couldn't unsee it. "A village," he said. "On our way."

"On our way where?"

"North."

"Astrid," I said, using my own name the way you'd rap knuckles on a table, "if you say 'north' one more time as though it were an answer, I'm going to feed you to the next thing that looks hungry."

"There won't be anything hungry," he said. "There's nothing out here at all."

He was wrong about that, but not in the way either of us expected.

* * *

Hollowmere announced itself by smell before sight — woodsmoke and bread and something green, river-weed maybe, the ordinary perfume of a place where people live without trying very hard. Then the houses came up out of the mist off the mere: whitewashed, crooked, charming in the way that only places nobody wealthy has discovered can be charming.

And then the people came out too, and I knew something was wrong before I knew what.

They stood in their doorways and along their fences and watched us come, and it wasn't hostility. Hostility I know how to handle; hostility has a shape. This was worse. This was *politeness*. They watched us the way you watch strangers whose names you've been told and forgotten twice — pleasant, faintly apologetic, utterly blank.

An old woman by the well smiled at me. "Travelers! You'll stay for supper, surely. There's pie."

"That's kind of you," I said, because my mother raised me right before she presumably went on to raise other people I no longer remember. "We're just passing through."

"Of course, of course." She kept smiling. Then she said, brightly, helpfully, like a woman offering directions: "Who are you?"

"Astrid Vane. This is Oscar."

"Lovely." She nodded, satisfied, and turned back to her washing as if that settled everything.

Oscar had gone very still beside me. Stillness, on him, was louder than fidgeting.

"Ma'am," I said to the old woman, "who are you?"

"Maren," she said promptly. "Maren Ostler. I keep the well and the geese."

"And your family?"

The smile stayed exactly where it was. That was the horror of it — nothing moved, nothing flickered, the smile just hung there like a coat on a hook while behind it something reached around inside her and found nothing. "Family," she repeated, tasting the word. "Yes. I have — there are children's shoes on my doorstep. Little ones. I polish them every morning." She looked down at her hands, which were red with well water, and her brow creased with the first honest expression I'd seen on her face. "I don't know why I do that."

Behind her, further up the lane, a man was setting a fourth plate at a table set for two. He did it absently, the way you'd straighten a picture frame, and then stood looking at the plate as if it had grown there.

"Oscar," I said quietly. "What is this place?"

"I don't—"

"Don't." I grabbed his collar and walked him around the corner of the nearest house, out of Maren's line of sight, and put him against the wall. "You hesitated. Back at the sign. And now you're hesitating again, only harder. So let's skip to the part where you tell me the truth, and I decide whether to break your nose before or after we leave."

"It's not me," he said quickly. "Astrid, I swear on the ledger, this isn't guild work done *here*, recently, I mean — but yes. Yes, it's guild work. Or guild-shaped. What happened to Hollowmere—" He swallowed. "We call it harvesting."

The word sat between us like a dead bird.

"Say more," I said, "and say it slowly, because I'm counting every word against you."

* * *

He talked. We walked while he talked, because I couldn't stand still for it, and he scurried along beside me like a man narrating his own execution.

"The Guild doesn't only buy memories from people who want to sell," he said. "That's the shopfront. The pretty version. Grief taken away, bad nights erased, that sort of thing. But there's a second trade. Bulk trade. Whole ledgers of it that I balance and never read past the numbers, because—" he laughed, a horrible small sound, "—because bookkeeping isn't thieving, right? That's what I told myself. Numbers aren't memories. A column of figures isn't a stolen childhood."

"What did they take here?" I asked. "From these people?"

"Not a memory. Memories, plural. Selectively. Everything attached to—" he waved a hand vaguely at the village, the mere, the smoke, "—to a person. Or persons. You leave the name of the village. You leave the skills, the routines, the daily shape of a life, because otherwise they die, and dead harvests yield nothing. But you take the *person* out of it. Take every recollection that has a face in it, a voice, a love. Leave the shoes on the doorstep. Leave the fourth plate."

"Why would anyone do that? Who pays for that?"

"Anyone who wants someone forgotten," Oscar said, "completely, everywhere, all at once. A witness. A rival. An heir who inconveniently exists. A whole family line, if the buyer's pockets are deep enough. The village doesn't even need to know the person. Memory isn't contained in skulls, Astrid — it leaks. It lives in other people's stories about you. If your mother forgets you but the baker remembers, you still exist. So you take it from everyone within reach. You harvest the whole field so no stalk remembers the seed."

I stopped walking. Somewhere behind us a child laughed — actually laughed, a real child, running through a yard — and both its parents turned to look at the sound with the mild puzzled pleasure of people watching a dog chase a stick.

"How many villages," I said.

"I don't—"

"Oscar. How many."

His eyes went to the ground, to the road, to his own hands, anywhere but me. "In the ledgers I keep? Forty-one entries marked 'field harvest' in the last six years. Some entries cover multiple sites. I never added them up." He looked at me finally, and there it was — not the slick guild face, but the actual man underneath, the one who balanced books of stolen lives and called it honest work. "I never let myself add them up."

Forty-one entries. My head did the arithmetic anyway, because apparently some part of me was still a soldier who counted casualties. I made myself stop before I got an answer I'd have to carry.

"And the guildmaster knows."

"Corvane signs the orders."

"And you balanced the books."

"Yes." No hesitation that time. At least he gave me that much — the truth, plain, unflinched. "Every quarter. To the copper."

I hit him.

I'm not proud of it, and I am a little proud of it, and both those things can be true. It wasn't a killing blow; it was a short right hand to the mouth, the kind you give a barracks thief, and it put him down in the road dust with his ledger-satchel under him and blood starting at his lip. A goose honked at us, outraged. Somewhere Maren Ostler went on polishing shoes that belonged to children she could not name.

"You knew," I said. My voice came out low and even, which is how you know it's bad. "You sat in that guildhall with your ink and your columns and you *knew*, and you want me to believe you're different from the men who swing the knives?"

He didn't wipe the blood. He just looked up at me from the dust, and he didn't argue, and somehow that was worse than arguing. "I'm not different," he said. "I'm the part that makes it possible. I know that now. I think I always knew it and wrote it down in a column where I wouldn't have to read it."

I turned and walked. Ten steps. Twenty. The lane bent toward the north road and the mist coming off the mere, and every step said *leave him*, and every step was easy, and that ease should have warned me. Things that are easy are usually lies.

At thirty steps I stopped, because I thought about the wolf scar.

It runs from my left shoulder blade down across my ribs, old and white and shaped — the healer in Ashgate said, laughing — like a wolf mid-leap. I've had it as long as I remember having skin. I don't remember getting it. I don't remember the battle or the beast or the year it happened, and I'd built a life on top of that hole the way you build a house over a cellar door and call the floor solid. Four days ago I'd seen the seal on that map — the broken crown over the open eye — and something under my floorboards had *moved*. Something down in the dark of me had recognized it and rattled its chains, and I had spent four days telling myself it was indigestion.

If the guild could hollow out a village, it could hollow out a knight. If it could take a mother's child, it could take a woman's war.

I turned around. Oscar was still sitting in the dust where I'd left him, patiently, like a man who'd decided he deserved the road and would wait for it to finish with him. Blood on his lip. Ledger under his ribs. The most harmless-looking dangerous man in the world.

"Get up," I said.

He blinked. "Astrid—"

"You're going to teach me the ledgers. All of them. Every entry, every code, every name the guild ever filed under 'harvest.' You're going to teach me until I can read your columns in my sleep, and then you and I are going to find out what they took from me, and from everyone else they've bled, and we are going to follow that map to whatever vault is at the end of it." I crouched so we were eye to eye, and I let him see all of it — the fury, yes, but under the fury the thing that fury was standing guard over. "Not because I forgive you. Because you're the only map I've got, and maps don't get to quit."

He got up. He wiped his lip with the back of his hand and looked at the blood like it was an entry he'd have to record somewhere.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the guilt was real. Even when I was lying to myself about everything else."

"It's worth almost nothing," I said. "But it's not worth zero. Walk."

* * *

We took supper with Maren after all, because leaving felt like another theft and I couldn't stomach being the third thief to pass through Hollowmere that decade. She fed us pie and ale and asked us three times who we were, and each time she received the answer like new coin, delighted, and each time I answered like it was the first time, because for her it was.

The man with the fourth plate — his name was Aldous, he repaired wheels — sat across from us and told us, with great warmth, about the wonderful travelers passing through. Us. He told us about ourselves twice. On the third telling, Oscar's hand found the table edge and gripped it, and I watched a clerk arrive at the far border of what he could bear.

Afterward, in the loft above the stable where Maren insisted we sleep because "you seem like good folk, whoever you are," Oscar lay awake in the dark beside me, and I heard him whispering. Counting. I thought at first he was counting sheep or coins or sins, and then I caught the rhythm of it and understood.

He was reciting entries. Names. Site designations. Dates. Forty-one field harvests, spoken aloud into the dark of a harvested village, like a man planting seeds back into a burned field on the chance that anything might grow.

"Entry nine," he murmured. "Caldwell's Reach. Entry ten. The two sisters of Marrow Ford. Entry eleven—"

"Sleep," I said.

"I'll sleep when they're all said once," he whispered. "Somebody should know them. Somebody should have to hear them."

I lay back and stared at the rafters, and the wolf scar ached the way it does before storms, and I thought about the seal — the broken crown, the open eye — and the thing beneath my floorboards turned over in its sleep and settled again.

In the morning we'd take the north road. In the morning, I decided, I'd ask him about the seal, and this time I'd watch his face when he lied, and this time I'd know.

But that night, near dawn, hoofbeats came up the north road — many of them, muffled, unhurried, disciplined — and Oscar sat bolt upright in the dark with his hand pressed flat against his satchel and his face gone the color of the mist.

"That'll be for the map," he said. "Or for us. Possibly both."
