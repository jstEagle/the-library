## 7. Inquisitor Hale

I have interrogated four hundred and eleven people in my career, and I want to say at the outset that I have enjoyed almost none of it.

People assume the Crown's chief inquisitor is a man who delights in pain. The assumption is convenient for me — fear does half my work before I've asked a single question — but it is wrong, and being wrong about me has kept a great many people alive. Torture is inefficient. It produces whatever answer the sufferer believes you want, which is a different thing entirely from the truth. No, my trade is patience. I sit in comfortable chairs. I pour good tea. I ask small questions and wait for the large ones to answer themselves.

Guildmaster Corvane had been sitting in my comfortable chair for two hours when he finally lied to me in a way I could use.

"You understand," he said, spreading his hands the way merchants do when they're about to explain that the price is actually a favor, "that the Guild of Impossible Things is a licensed enterprise. We operate under charter. Whatever you imagine we've—"

"Charter eleven," I said. "Granted under the old king. Renewed annually by his daughter, who signs without reading it because she trusts me to have read it for her." I refilled his cup. He watched the tea like it might be poisoned, which was flattering; I'd have used something subtler. "The charter permits the trade of memories between consenting parties. It does not permit your people to buy a soldier's recollection of a massacre before the Crown has finished deciding what that massacre officially did not happen."

Corvane's face did something very small around the eyes. Most people's faces do large things — flinches, blanches, the whole theater of guilt. Corvane's face was a ledger too. Every entry balanced.

"I don't know what you're referring to," he said.

"Of course not." I opened the folder on the table between us. I keep folders even though I remember everything; the folder is for the other person. Watching someone look at their own signature is worth more than any question I could ask. "A sergeant of the Third Border Regiment, invalided out after the action at Cael Ford. Sold his memory of the engagement to your factor in Ashgate for forty crowns and a bottle of storm, which I'm told was a fair price, storms being scarce this season."

"The Guild buys many things from many soldiers."

"He remembers the ford as a fishing trip," I said. "He remembers catching a trout. He shows the trout to his neighbors. There was no trout at Cael Ford, Master Corvane. There were no fish at all, because the river ran red for three days, and there is no record of Cael Ford anywhere in the Crown archive, because Cael Ford is one of one hundred and six engagements that never happened, and I am the man whose entire life's work consists of making sure they continue never having happened."

* * *

Let me tell you how I came to this work, since you'll judge me either way and you may as well judge me accurately.

I was nineteen when the Peace of Ashes was signed. I say signed; nothing so clean. I stood in the ruins of the capital with my father, who had lost two sons to the war and could no longer remember their faces, because grief had eaten them the way grief does, and he said to me: *I cannot even hate them properly anymore. I've forgotten enough of what they took that hating feels like an invention.*

He meant the enemy. Everyone always means the enemy. And then the mages came through the city with their great quiet working, and my father forgot my brothers entirely, and woke the next morning a lighter man, and wept without knowing why, and lived eleven more years in a peace he could not examine because there was nothing underneath it to examine.

I was one of three hundred souls in the capital whom the working spared. Witnesses, the mages called us. Keepers. Someone had to hold the shape of the hole, or the world would heal crooked over it, the way a bone sets wrong. Someone had to know what was forgotten so that it stayed forgotten on purpose rather than by accident. Because here is the truth that keeps me awake some nights, the truth I will carry to my grave and then hand to my successor to carry to hers:

Forgetting saved millions. Not metaphorically. Not in the way poets mean. I have read the casualty projections for the war's final decade. Both sides were past victory and into annihilation; both sides were burning things that could not be rebuilt; both sides had begun, in the last year, on weapons that would not have distinguished soldier from child. The peace faction in each court arrived independently at the same impossible conclusion: you cannot stop a war whose every participant can recite its grievances. Grievance is heritable. Hatred is the most reliably transmitted inheritance in human history. So they severed the estate. They burned the ledger of the war itself — every battle, every name, every reason — and they left the survivors standing in a field of ash, whole and healthy and unable to say why they flinched at certain colors of sky.

And it worked. That's the part my critics — the few who know enough to criticize — never sit with. It worked. The kingdoms did not relapse. The children born after the Peace grew up without the war in their blood the way water carries silt. My father died content, or something adjacent to content, holding the hand of a grandson whose name he'd forgotten but whose face he loved, and if you put before him the choice — your sons' names back, and the war back with them — I know with a certainty that approaches faith what he would have chosen.

So no. I am not cruel. Cruelty requires believing your work is evil and doing it anyway. I believe my work is the single most merciful act ever performed by human beings, and I maintain it the way one maintains a dam: thanklessly, vigilantly, and with the understanding that everyone downstream lives because of a wall they never think about.

The Guild of Impossible Things is a crack in that wall.

* * *

"Master Corvane," I said, "how long have you been guildmaster?"

"Fourteen years."

"And before that?"

"Ledger-keeper. Before that, acquisition."

Acquisition. What a word. The Guild's own euphemism for theft, and Corvane wore it like a rank. I turned the folder toward him. "Your acquisition records for the past thirty years, reconstructed from sources I will not describe. Look at the pattern."

He looked. To his credit, he didn't pretend he couldn't read it.

"Memories of the border wars," I said. "Fragments. A woman who remembers a song her grandmother sang in a language that no longer exists. A farmer with a recurring dream of a city burning — you bought the dream, and now the farmer sleeps soundly and the dream sits in one of your jars. Veterans of conflicts that appear in no history book. Children's nightmares of horns and ash. You have been sweeping up the sediment of the erasure for decades, Master Corvane. Every fragment the working missed, every splinter that lodged too deep to pull cleanly — your factors have been buying it off the suffering public at market rates and shelving it."

"The Guild provides a service," Corvane said. "People are tormented by these fragments. We relieve them."

"You do. And then you keep what you relieved them of. In vaults. Catalogued." I leaned forward. "Why?"

He was silent for a long moment. Outside my window, the bells of the Palace district rang the third hour, and somewhere below a cart driver cursed another in a voice that carried, ordinary men having an ordinary quarrel that would be forgotten by supper, as quarrels should be.

"Why," Corvane said finally, "does the Crown care? The fragments are worthless. Broken pieces. No more than shards of a mirror — you cannot see anything in a shard except yourself."

"No," I agreed. "But a shard tells you where the mirror hung. And enough shards, assembled—" I tapped the folder. "There are scholars, Master Corvane. Disgraced ones, mostly, exiled from the universities for asking questions the Crown declined to fund. There are descendants of the old noble houses who inherit, along with their estates, a hunger they cannot name — a feeling that their family was *robbed* of something, some greatness or grievance owed to them. There are revisionists, romantics, fools who believe the war was glorious and its erasure a crime against history. Each of them alone holds a shard. But a man who held a catalogue of every shard ever recovered—"

"You're accusing me of intending to sell the war back to the world."

"I'm accusing you of keeping inventory," I said. "Intent can be established later. It usually establishes itself. Greed is patient but not infinitely so."

Corvane smiled. It was the first honest expression he'd produced in two hours, and I found I liked him better for it, which is a professional hazard I guard against carefully. "And what would the Crown offer," he said, "for the assurance that no such catalogue exists?"

Ah. There it was. Two hours of tea to arrive at the only sentence that mattered.

"Nothing," I said pleasantly. "You misunderstand the nature of this meeting. This is not a negotiation. Negotiations occur between parties who can each walk away. You cannot walk away, Master Corvane, because I have already read your ledgers — the real ones, the false-bottomed ones, the ones your clever clerk Ishikawa balances so beautifully. I know about the map."

The smile left his face. I confess I enjoyed that. Even a man who believes in mercy is permitted a small cruelty now and then, provided he bills it honestly.

"A stranger brought a map into your counting house three nights ago," I continued. "A dying stranger, bleeding from wounds I have spent two days identifying, and failing, which troubles me more than I show. He gave the map to your clerk and died saying a name. Your enforcers arrived minutes later and found a corpse and a clerk with remarkably steady hands. And now the map is missing, the clerk is missing, and a disgraced knight named Astrid Vane — do you know her? Of course you know her; you hired her — has also gone missing, in the same direction as the clerk." I closed the folder. "The Vault of Unremembered Kings, Master Corvane. Say it. Say it aloud so we both hear how it sounds in a warm room."

He said nothing. His hands, folded on the table, had gone white at the knuckles.

"You know what's in it," I said. "Or you suspect. Either way, you've been selling tickets in your mind for years — I saw the arithmetic move behind your eyes the moment I mentioned it. Selective truths. Curated horrors. Enough of the war to inflame the old houses, never enough to satisfy them. An auction of embers, sold as the fire itself." I stood, and he stood with me, because men like Corvane stand when power stands. "Here is what happens now. You will place every resource of your Guild at the Crown's disposal. You will find Vane and the clerk before they reach the vault, and you will bring them to me alive, because the woman is the key and dead keys open nothing. And in exchange, the Crown will permit your Guild to continue existing, which is more than it deserves, and less than I would grant if the decision were mine alone."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I burn your guildhall with everything inside it," I said, "and I do it with such thoroughness that even the ashes forget being wood."

* * *

He agreed, of course. They always agree. Agreement extracted at this altitude isn't consent, it's gravity.

I dismissed him and stood at my window a while, watching the city go about its unremembering afternoon. Somewhere down there a mother was teaching her daughter a lullaby older than either of them, neither knowing it had once been sung in a country that no longer existed, in a language that had been bought and jarred. Somewhere a veteran showed his neighbors a trout that had never been caught. It was, by every measure I trust, a good city. Peaceful. Kind, even, in the unexamined way of people who have never been given a reason not to be.

That is the thing my enemies — and I do collect them, slowly, the way a dam collects cracks — will never understand. I do not protect a lie. I protect the *absence* of a wound. The lie would be telling these people the war never happened; the truth of our arrangement is that it happened so thoroughly, cost so much, that the price of ending it was memory itself, and every soul alive paid it without being asked, and the debt is serviced daily by a handful of keepers who must remember so that no one else has to.

Someone must hold the shape of the hole. Better one man's sleepless centuries than a million graves reopened.

And yet.

I poured myself the last of the tea and thought about the dying stranger. Wounds I couldn't identify. A name spoken with his last breath — *Astrid*, the witnesses agreed, though none could say whether it was a plea or a warning. A map to a vault that opens, according to the oldest and most forbidden line in my own sealed orders, only to *her blood and her consent*. Which means the Crown has known for three hundred years that a key existed. Which means the keepers before me knew, and chose, as I choose, to leave the key walking free in the world rather than destroy her — because a key that might someday be needed is a key you tolerate, and tolerance, I am beginning to suspect, was the first crack in the dam.

She was a knight once. Before the disgrace, before the bounty work, before the gaps. She stood at the signing of the Peace of Ashes — I have seen her in the preserved memories, young and armored and gray-faced, the last witness, the one who volunteered to carry what no one else could survive carrying. She chose the forgetting. She built the lock and swallowed the key.

And now she is riding toward her own door with a thief and a stolen map, and every step she takes, fragments are coming home to roost.

I finished the tea. I sent for my horse.

If she reaches the vault, she will find the war down there in the dark — all of it, whole and waiting, every grievance pickled in its jar — and she will stand before it holding the one power in the world that frightens me, which is not the power to remember, but the power to *choose*.

No keeper has ever been given that choice. That is precisely why we are keepers.

I rode east at dusk, ahead of the Guild's slower machinery, and behind me the city lit its lamps one by one, each flame ignorant and warm, and I loved it, God help me, I loved every forgetful soul of it, which is why I could not let one woman decide for them what they were allowed to know they'd lost.
