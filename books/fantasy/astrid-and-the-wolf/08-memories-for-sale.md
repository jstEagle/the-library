## 8. Memories for Sale

There is a particular arithmetic to being broke on the road, and I have never been good at it.

In the guildhall I balanced ledgers worth more than most duchies. I could tell you, to the copper, what a decade of someone's childhood fetched at market in a bad year. I knew the exchange rate between a first kiss and a mother's face, between a soldier's courage and a baker's patience. Numbers were my native tongue, and numbers do not flinch.

But numbers, it turns out, are useless when your entire fortune consists of one stolen map, one disgraced knight who glares at you like you owe her money — which, morally speaking, I did — and eleven coppers, two of which I suspect Astrid palmed off me as tuition for trusting her.

"We need horses," she said, on the third morning out of Hollowmere, in the tone other people use for *we need air*.

"We need food," I said. "Horses are a luxury. Food is a rumor we've been chasing since yesterday."

She looked at me over the fire she'd built with the efficiency of someone who had once burned down larger things than kindling. "You're the thief."

"I'm the *bookkeeper*," I said, with what dignity remained to me. "There's a difference. Thieves take things. I record what other people took. It's a whole different sin. Much more respectable."

"Then be respectable and find us money."

The trouble was that I already knew how. That's the thing about sins — they don't require invention. They've been sitting there all along, patient as debt, waiting for you to run out of better options.

* * *

Here is what I swore off, and when, so we're clear about the shape of my hypocrisy.

In my ninth year with the Guild of Impossible Things, Corvane promoted me from copying ledgers to *acquiring* for them. There was a widow in the Saltrows whose husband had died at sea, and she wanted to forget the last year of him — the coughing, the wasting, the smell of the sickroom — and keep only their wedding day. Standard work. The guild paid her a pittance for the excision and sold the grief to a collector in Vail who liked his tragedies pre-owned.

I did the extraction myself. My hands shook the whole time, and afterward the widow thanked me, which was somehow worse than if she'd cursed me. She thanked me because she genuinely felt lighter, and I stood there holding a jar with a dead man's dying in it, thinking: *I am not a bookkeeper anymore. I am the thing the books record.*

I told Corvane I'd stay on the ledger side. He smiled the way he does — like a man agreeing to terms he intends to renegotiate later — and mostly he kept his word, because I was useful precisely because I was clean. A guild needs its clerks unsullied. It lends the operation an air of legitimacy, like a bank with a chapel in the lobby.

So: I had sworn it off. And now Astrid and I were eating roasted nettles, and the map to the Vault of Unremembered Kings was sewn into my coat lining, and every road behind us had guild enforcers on it, and roads ahead had Crown inquisitors, and neither organization was known for extending credit.

"You could sell something," Astrid said. She said it casually, which meant she'd been thinking about it a while. "From your head. You're a memory-man. You must have stock."

"I don't keep inventory in my skull. That's amateur work. Everything I've ever handled went into jars and ledgers, sealed and catalogued." I paused. "Almost everything."

"Almost?"

"There may be," I admitted, "a small personal reserve. Things I extracted and couldn't bring myself to file. Nothing valuable. A child's memory of a dog. An old man's memory of rain on a roof he built himself. Sentimental junk. The guild would call it spoilage."

Astrid stared into the fire. "You kept those."

"Somebody had to." I shrugged, and hated how defensive it sounded. "They weren't worth anything to anyone else. That's usually why they end up with me. The memories nobody bids on."

"And you'd sell those? For horses?"

"No," I said. "For horses, no. For food, maybe the dog."

She almost smiled. It was the first almost-smile in three days, and I found I'd have traded both the coppers she hadn't stolen yet to keep it. Which should have been a warning about where my ledger-keeping was headed generally.

But the point is this: even then, desperate, nettles in our teeth, I was planning to sell *my own* memories before I ever considered taking anyone else's. Old habits of conscience die hard, and mine died slowly, in installments, like everything else the guild ever traded.

* * *

The town was called Marrow's Rest, which is the sort of name towns get when they want you to lower your expectations on arrival. It sat in a fold of hills where the road bent south toward the drowned country, and it had a market, a mill, and — the reason we stopped — a notice board.

Most of the notices were the usual rural liturgy: lost sheep, missing mule, a reward for information regarding a peddler who owed half the county money. But one notice was written on good paper, in careful hand, and pinned with a brass tack that said *this household has means*.

**WANTED. A practitioner of the quiet arts. Our mother is dying. She asks to give away her last summer. Fair payment. Inquire at the house with the blue door.**

Astrid read it twice. "That's grim."

"That's business," I said, and immediately wished I hadn't, because she turned and looked at me with an expression I was going to be seeing in the dark for weeks.

"People *pay* for that?"

"The guild pays for the taking. Then sells what it takes. The family gets a little coin and an empty season; some collector in a city gets a summer he didn't earn. Everyone involved tells themselves they've done the dying woman a favor." I heard my own voice doing the thing it did in the guildhall — flat, professional, reciting rates — and made myself stop. "It's called a death-gift. It's supposed to be merciful. Dying people sometimes can't bear the weight of their own last months, and the extraction is gentler than living them."

"Do you believe that?"

"I believe it's true," I said carefully, "and also that it's what everyone tells themselves, and those are not always the same thing."

We stood there a moment. Somewhere behind us the mill turned, grinding away at something, the way mills do.

"We need the money," Astrid said finally, and it wasn't a question, and it wasn't quite permission either. It was the sound of two people agreeing to walk past a line they'd both been pretending existed.

"Inquire at the blue door," I said. "I'll do the talking."

"You'll do the lying, you mean."

"I'll do the accounting," I said. "Lying is just accounting with worse margins."

* * *

The house with the blue door belonged to a cooper named Aldous Ferrin, and his mother was dying in the back room the way people used to die before the guild made dying negotiable: slowly, honestly, surrounded by family who could not afford the remedies that might have saved her and would not have wanted them anyway, at her age, at this stage.

There were four of them. Aldous, broad and red-eyed. His wife, who kept wringing a cloth as though the cloth had personally wronged her. Two grown daughters, one of whom wouldn't look at me and one of whom wouldn't stop, as though staring hard enough might let her audit my soul and find it wanting, which, to be fair, it was.

"She asked for it herself," Aldous said, for perhaps the fourth time since I'd come through the door. He said it the way men say things they need to believe out loud. "She heard about such things, years back, a trader came through. She said when her time came near, she didn't want to spend her last days drowning in it. The pain-memories. The fear. She wants to give the summer away and keep her mind clear for — for what's left."

"What's left," I repeated. It was a phrase I'd read in a hundred transaction records. It sounded different in a kitchen that smelled of bread and camphor.

"How much?" said the daughter who wouldn't look at me.

"Aldous." I made myself use his name. Names are the cheapest honesty available, and I was going to need every scrap. "Before we speak of price, you should know what I am. I worked for the Guild of Impossible Things. Nine years. I'm not working for them now, and if they find me here, they will kill me, and possibly you, for the inconvenience. But I know the craft, and I'm the only practitioner likely to pass through Marrow's Rest this month. So you should decide whether you want a fugitive clerk's hands anywhere near your mother, or whether you'd rather wait and trust the guild's own people, who charge triple and will resell whatever I take."

The silence in that kitchen had texture. The wife stopped wringing the cloth.

"Why would you tell us that?" the staring daughter demanded. "If you're hiding from them?"

"Because you're about to trust me with your mother's last summer," I said, "and I've spent nine years watching people get trusted by professionals who never told them anything at all. Consider it a discount."

Aldous looked at me for a long moment. Then he laughed — a short, broken sound, more exhaustion than humor — and said, "God help us, you're the honestest liar I ever met. How much?"

I quoted a figure that was roughly a fifth of the guild rate and roughly double what they could afford, and then watched Aldous's face do arithmetic of its own, and lowered it, and lowered it again, until we arrived at a number that would buy us horses and food and leave the Ferrins able to bury their mother without debt. Astrid would have called it bad bargaining. I called it the first honest transaction of my career, and I intend to keep calling it that, because a man has to hold onto something.

* * *

Maren Ferrin was seventy-one years old and sharp as a new nail, and she received me in her bed like a queen receiving an ambassador from a country she disapproved of.

"So you're the thief," she said.

"Bookkeeper," I said. "But yes."

"They tell me you'll take the summer. June to September. The garden, the grandchildren, the fair at Tarnlow." Her eyes were bright and furious and terribly alive. "And you'll leave me the rest. The winter. The pain. All the parts nobody would pay a copper for."

"That's the arrangement."

"Hm." She studied me the way Astrid studied maps. "You don't want to do it."

I opened my mouth to deliver the professional reassurance — *it's painless, it's gentle, you won't feel a thing* — and found, to my considerable surprise, that I couldn't. Not to her. She'd have smelled the lie the way cats smell weather.

"No," I said. "I don't."

"Good," said Maren Ferrin. "Neither do I. Do it anyway."

"May I ask why? Truly why. Not the version you told your son."

She was quiet for a while. Outside, one of the daughters was scolding a chicken, and the ordinary sounds of a household going on around a death filled the room like water fills a hull.

"My husband died in the spring," she said finally. "Forty-eight years, and he went in his sleep, the lucky devil. And since then I've been sitting in this bed cataloguing everything. Every summer. Every fight. Every time I was cruel to him or he to me, and every time we weren't. And I've discovered something, young man, that I suspect your guild knows very well and never says aloud." She fixed me with those bright furious eyes. "*Everything weighs the same.* The best day of my life and the worst sit in me at exactly the same weight now, because I haven't the strength to carry any of them differently. And I am so tired. And the summer — the garden, the girls, the fair — that's the heaviest part, because it's the part I loved. So I'm giving you the heavy thing. Not because I don't want it. Because I can't lift it anymore, and I'd rather it go to someone who can than be crushed under it here at the end. Now." She folded her hands on the blanket, a woman settling accounts. "How does this work?"

I told her. The truth, this time, all of it: the touch, the pull, the way the memory leaves not like water but like a knot untying. That she would remember *that* the summer happened — the guild always leaves the label on the empty shelf — but not the summer itself. That some patients describe it as a room they know is there but can't enter. That it doesn't hurt, or hardly, or no more than anything hurts at the end.

She listened without interrupting, which in my experience no one in the guild's history had ever done.

"One condition," she said, when I finished. "You don't sell it. Whatever you do with it — you don't put it in a jar for some fat collector to wear like a fur coat. Swear it."

"Maren—"

"*Swear it.*"

And here is where I should record, for the honest ledger I am apparently keeping now, that I hesitated. Because the plan — the plan Astrid and I had agreed to in the blue doorway, the plan that fed us and horsed us — was to sell the extraction onward. That was the whole economy of the thing. Take from the dying, sell to the grieving-rich. I had done the sums on the walk over. I had already spent the money in my head, which is the oldest sin of all.

"I swear it," I said. "It stays with me. I won't sell it, trade it, or file it. It's yours, held in trust."

"Held in trust," she repeated, and something in her face eased, and I understood that I had just invented a financial instrument that did not exist in any guild ledger: the memory held for no one, owned by no one, carried like a debt to a creditor who was about to stop existing.

"Then do it," she said, "before I change my mind. I've changed my mind about plenty in seventy-one years. It's one of my few remaining talents."

* * *

I have performed — witnessed, recorded, enabled — hundreds of extractions. I know the theory the way a eunuch knows the Kama Sutra. But I had done it myself exactly once, nine years ago, with shaking hands, and I had promised myself never again, and here I was again, kneeling by a dying woman's bed with my palms against her temples, and her family crowded in the doorway because she'd refused to send them out ("I've hidden nothing from these people in fifty years, I'm not starting for you"), and Astrid standing apart by the window with her arms crossed, watching me with an expression I couldn't read and didn't try to.

"It's all right, lad," Maren said. "I've had worse hands on me. The midwife in '61 had fingers like fence posts."

I laughed. I shouldn't have — it broke the concentration I needed — but I laughed, and she smiled, and somehow that was the right way into it. You don't reach for a memory. You offer, and it comes, if it trusts you. That's the part the guild manuals never write down, because it can't be taught and can't be billed.

Her last summer came to me like a tide.

The garden first — beans and late roses, her granddaughter's wedding dress hanging on the pantry door because there was nowhere else safe from moths. The fair at Tarnlow: fiddle music, a rigged ring-toss she saw through instantly and beat anyway out of pure spite, her grandson asleep against her shoulder smelling of sugar and sweat. The long gold evenings when she'd sit outside because the house felt too big now, and neighbors drifting by, and the way the light came through the apple tree at seven o'clock exactly, every evening, as though the sun were keeping an appointment.

And underneath it, threaded through all of it, the knowledge — hers, constant, adult, unflinching — that this was the last time. That she was memorizing. That every golden evening was golden *because* it was numbered, and she had decided to love it harder for that instead of less, and had mostly succeeded, and had failed sometimes at three in the morning, and gotten up anyway.

The guild would have called it a premium specimen. Full sensory fidelity, high emotional valence, coherent narrative arc. I have read the appraisal language my whole career. *Valuable. Desirable. Collectible.*

It is none of those things. It is a woman loving the world on her way out of it, and it weighs —

Well. Here is the thing I learned that night, the thing I would have charged the guild a great deal to never learn:

It weighs.

When the extraction finished and I sat back on my heels with the memory settled into my own head — not in a jar, not in a ledger, *in me*, riding around behind my eyes where I couldn't catalogue it or shelve it or pretend it was somebody else's property — I understood, finally and completely, what we in the trade had been doing all along. We spoke of storage and transfer and fidelity. We priced memories like cloth, by quality and length. We never once used the word that applied.

Weight. Every memory is a weight. The guild is not a marketplace. It is a freight company, hauling the unbearable from those who cannot carry it to those who have forgotten what carrying feels like — and charging by the pound.

"Is it done?" Aldous asked softly.

"It's done," I said. My voice came out strange. "She'll sleep now. She'll be lighter. That part's true — everything I told you about that part is true."

Maren's eyes were closed. She looked, for the first time since I'd entered the house, actually rested. After a moment she murmured, without opening them: "Keep it well, bookkeeper."

"I will," I said. "It's not a job anymore. It's a debt."

"Debts," she said, drifting toward sleep, "are just love with paperwork," and I have thought about that sentence every day since, and expect to for the rest of my life, however long the guild or the Crown permits that to be.

* * *

Astrid waited until we were out on the dark road, leading the two horses we'd bought — a bay mare and an ugly gray gelding with opinions — before she said anything.

"You gave it back to them. Most of the fee."

"They needed it more."

"That's not the bargain."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

She rode in silence for a while. The hills were black shapes against a sky full of more stars than cities ever let you see, and somewhere behind us Marrow's Rest had gone to bed around its dying woman, and ahead of us lay the drowned country and the chapel on the map and whatever the vault wanted from Astrid's blood.

"You kept it," she said at last. Not a question. She'd watched my face during the extraction; she'd seen where it went.

"I swore I would."

"To a dying woman. Who'll never check."

"That's the best kind of oath," I said. "The kind no one can hold you to except yourself. Those are the only ones that count."

Another silence. Then, quietly, in a voice I hadn't heard from her before — not suspicion, not contempt, something almost like curiosity, which from Astrid Vane was practically an embrace:

"What does it feel like? Carrying someone else's summer in your head."

I considered lying. It was reflexive, the way blinking is. But I was tired, and the stars were out, and something in me had apparently decided, without consulting management, that the era of lying to Astrid Vane was entering its final season.

"Like wearing a coat that fits someone else," I said. "Perfectly. Better than my own. And knowing I'll have to give it back someday, and not knowing to whom, because—" I gestured vaguely backward, toward Marrow's Rest, toward the blue door, toward the grave that was being dug in advance in a churchyard I'd never see. "Because the owner is leaving. And the coat goes on being warm anyway. That's the part nobody warns you about. The memory doesn't die with them. It just keeps... happening. In me. Forever. The apples at seven o'clock. The fiddles. Her deciding, every single evening, to love it harder instead of less." I swallowed. "I have forty years of guild ledgers in my head, Astrid, and none of them weigh what one summer weighs."

She was quiet for a long time. When she finally spoke, she was looking at the road ahead, at the drowned country waiting for us in the dark, and her voice was low enough that I might have imagined it.

"When we get where we're going," she said, "there's a chapel. On the map. I've been dreaming about it. A drowned chapel, and an altar, and—" She stopped. Started again. "Oscar. If I ask you to take something from me. Something I can't carry anymore. Will you know how?"

The question hung in the cold air between our two horses, and I felt every ledger I'd ever kept rearrange itself around it.

"Yes," I said. "God help us both. Yes, I'll know how."

She nodded once, and spurred the mare forward, and I followed her down toward the water and the chapel and the first key, carrying a dead woman's summer against my spine like a coal that refused to cool.
