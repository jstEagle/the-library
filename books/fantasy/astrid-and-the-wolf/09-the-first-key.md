## 9. The First Key

The map called it a chapel. What we found was a lake with opinions.

"Chapels don't drown themselves," I said, standing at the water's edge while the morning fog did its best impression of nothing at all. "Somebody drowned this one."

"Somebody drowned everything around here," Oscar said. He had the map out again, the wax seal cracked and the parchment gone soft at the folds from being opened too many times. He held it the way he held everything — like it might bill him later. "Hollowmere was three days back. The village after that didn't have a name anyone could agree on. At some point you stop asking who did the drowning and start asking why the water's still warm."

I hadn't asked about Hollowmere since the night I'd punched him. He hadn't brought it up either. That was our arrangement: a fist thrown once, honestly, bought us both a long silence neither of us had earned but both of us needed.

The lake sat in a bowl of hills that should have been a valley. I knew that the way you know a room's been rearranged — not by remembering how it was, but by the wrongness of how it was now. Hills don't grow in rings. Hills don't lean inward like they're listening. Somewhere under that grey water was a town, and the town had been folded up into mountains by hands that wanted no one to know a town had ever been there.

"The map says the steeple breaks the surface at low water," Oscar said. "It's nearly dawn. If we wait—"

"I'm not waiting." I was already pulling off my boots. "Whatever's down there has been waiting two hundred years. It can wait for me to get my feet wet, but I'm not standing on this shore one more hour pretending I came all this way to admire the view."

Oscar looked at me with that expression he wore when his ledger-brain was doing sums he didn't want to show me. "Astrid."

"What."

"If something happens down there — if you touch something and it does what the map says it might do — you tell me. That's the bargain. We don't keep things from each other anymore."

"We never had that bargain," I said. "We had a bargain where you lie and I pretend to believe you, and it's kept us alive so far."

"That was the old bargain."

"Fine," I said, because lying is easier than arguing. "New bargain. No secrets."

He nodded, satisfied, and started wringing out his coat sleeves like a man preparing to be reasonable about drowning. And I turned away so he couldn't see my face, because I already knew I was going to break that bargain before the sun cleared the hills.

* * *

The water was warmer than it had any right to be, which should have been my second warning. The first was that the lake had no birds. Not silent — silent I could have worked with. Silent means something's hiding. This was *absent*, the way a house is absent of people who left in a hurry. The air over the water belonged to nothing. Even the insects had filed a grievance and moved out.

We waded in to our waists before the bottom dropped away, and then it was swimming, and then it was diving, following the broken line of a road that ran straight down into the green dark. Stones under my hands. A wall. Then the wall became a window, and the window still had its glass, and through the glass I saw pews furred with silt like the congregation had simply lain down mid-sermon two centuries ago and been very patient about it.

The steeple's bell hung above us in the gloom, and I swear — I swear on whatever's left of me — that as I swam beneath it, it moved. Not swung. *Tilted.* Like a head turning to look.

I surfaced inside the chapel through a gap where the roof had caved, gasping in an air pocket that smelled of stone and old candle smoke, which is impossible, and I noted the impossibility and put it in the pile. My pile of impossible things was getting tall enough to need its own ledger. Maybe I could borrow Oscar's.

"There," Oscar said, hauling himself up onto the flooded nave beside me, dripping, glasses fogged, hair plastered down like a drowned rat that had once been respectable. He pointed across the water.

The altar stood above the floodline on a rise of rubble, dry as a sermon. It wasn't stone. Up close it wasn't anything I had a name for — a pale material shot through with veins of silver, worn smooth in the center as if a thousand hands had rested there, except no hands had touched this place in two hundred years. The smoothness was older than the drowning. Someone had polished it with use, long ago, and then everyone who remembered using it had stopped existing.

Carved into the front of it, half-choked with mineral crust, was a wolf.

Not decorative. Not heraldic. Life-sized, or close to it, head lowered, jaws parted, carved in the act of stepping forward — and the wolf's eyes were two shallow hollows, exactly the size and shape of fingertips pressed into soft wax.

My scar woke up.

I call it a scar because that's what I told healers, and what I told myself, all these years. A pale shape over my ribs and shoulder, running up the side of my throat, that ached before storms and burned near certain kinds of magic and had never, not once, in fifteen years of bounty work, behaved like any wound I'd ever taken. Wolves bite and let go. This thing had never let go. It was less like a mark than like a hand that had closed around me from the inside and forgotten to open.

Now it pulled. Now it *pulled*, the way a compass needle pulls, and the direction was the altar.

"Astrid?" Oscar's voice came small across the water. "Your face just did something."

"My face is allowed."

"That's not — the map says the vault answers to blood and consent. Blood and *consent*, those are the words, and I've been turning them over for weeks because consent isn't a lock mechanism, consent is a *person agreeing*—" He stopped. He was looking at the altar, and then at me, and I watched him do the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since Ashgate. "Astrid, what are you—"

"Don't." I was already climbing the rubble. "Don't do the sum out loud. I want to see if I can bear the answer first."

The silver-veined surface was warm under my palm. Warm like Hollowmere's water. Warm like something that had been holding heat for someone expected.

And the world came apart along a seam I hadn't known was there.

* * *

Here is what I will say for memory: it doesn't knock.

There was no white light, no gentle tide of recollection, none of the soft-focus nonsense the ballads like. There was a sound like a bell struck underwater, and then I was standing in a field of wheat outside a city called Vael Corath, and the wheat was burning, and I was nineteen years old and my name was not Astrid Vane.

My name was Astrid Vane. That's the horror of it — the name survived. Everything else had been cut away and the name left behind like a hook in a fish's mouth, and all these years I'd been swimming against the line without knowing there was a fisherman.

In the burning wheat I knelt beside a boy of maybe twelve who was trying to gather the grain into his shirt with both hands, saving harvest from fire, because he was twelve and that was the biggest idea his mind could hold. An arrow took him through the throat and he looked at me — at her, at the girl I had been — with pure bafflement, the way you'd look at weather. On the ridge behind him stood soldiers in the livery of the Crown, and below, in the burning rows, farmers fought back with scythes, and both sides were starving, and both sides thought the other had started it, and I knew — I knew with the whole weight of what I was — that neither had. That the war had no first battle. That it had grown out of the ground like rot, fed on three generations of grievances each side of which was true, every crime answered by a crime answered by a crime until the ledger itself was the enemy.

I was there for the Sack of Vael Corath. I was there for the winter the river froze with bodies and they named the ice-bridge the King's Road out of bitter joke. I was there when the last fortress fell and the victors discovered there was nothing left to win, only land full of people who would hate their neighbors' grandchildren forever, because grief that old doesn't die, it just waits for a reason.

And I was there — this part I felt in my body, in the room with the drowned altar, my knees going into the floodwater — when the delegates of both crowns met in a vault beneath the ruined capital with a third party neither crown had invited. Merchants. Ledger-keepers. People who understood that wars end when they become unprofitable, and that this war had become unprofitable for everyone except the people selling arms to both sides, and even they were running out of buyers.

They had made something. A working. Not a spell — a *transaction*. The largest the world had ever seen. They would take the war entire — every battle, every name, every grievance, every grave's location and meaning — and move it out of every living mind, and seal it somewhere it could not leak back. Peace, purchased at the price of the truth. Every widow would forget she was a widow. Every orphan would forget whom to mourn. The dead would be dead and no one would know what for.

Someone had to hold the key. Someone had to be the vessel — the last witness, carrying the whole of it sealed inside, because a lock needs a shape to fit, and the shape of this lock was a human soul that had seen everything and agreed.

They asked for a volunteer. Everyone in that vault knew what it meant: to carry the war was to lose your own life around it, to be hollowed and refilled, to walk out with gaps where your self used to be.

I raised my hand.

I remember why. That's the worst of it, the part I'll never be able to unknow again: I remember *why*. Because I had read the casualty rolls until the numbers stopped being numbers. Because I had held a dying friend who begged me to make sure no one else ever learned what he'd done in Vael Corath, not out of shame but out of mercy, because knowing had made him do it again. Because I believed — with the clean, cold certainty of a twenty-three-year-old knight who had buried more friends than she could count — that some truths are wounds, and that a wound the whole world shares is a wound the whole world dies of.

I said yes. I lay down on an altar like this one. They pressed my fingers into the wolf's eyes — the wolf was mine, my sigil, the Wolf of the Northern Marches, they'd carved it because I was the witness and the witness gets a mark — and the war poured into me like a river into a cracked jar, and everything I was poured out to make room.

The last thing my old self thought, before the erasure took even the thinking: *Let them be happy. Let them be happy and never know the price.*

Then I was a woman named Astrid Vane with a scar she couldn't explain, waking up in a ditch outside Ashgate with a sword she didn't recognize and a hole in her head shaped like a war.

* * *

I came back to myself in the flooded chapel with my hand still on the altar and my heart going like a siege drum, and Oscar saying my name, and saying it again, and reaching for my arm.

I hit him. Not hard — harder than I meant to, softer than I wanted to. He went down into the shallows and came up spluttering, glasses askew, and had the sense not to come at me again.

"How long," I said.

"You were — a minute. Maybe two. You were talking, though. Not to me." He wiped his face. His hands weren't steady. Good. Mine weren't either. "You said, 'let them be happy.' Twice. Astrid, what did you see?"

What did I see. I saw a boy gathering burning wheat. I saw a hundred thousand names that lived nowhere now except inside my ribs. I saw why the guild wanted me found and the Crown wanted me buried and why the map had led here, to me, because the map wasn't a map to a place at all. It was a map to a person. It had always been a map to a person, and the person was kneeling in two inches of floodwater in a dead church, shaking.

Everyone wants me because I am the war. I am the last copy. I am the vault, walking around in stolen boots, and every power in the world has been hunting a frightened woman with gaps in her memory, and the gaps aren't damage. The gaps are the *seal*.

If they open me — if they take what's inside — every grievance comes back. Every debt. Every name on every grave, and the reason for every grave, and the hatred that grows from reasons like wheat from seed. Two hundred years of peace, and it ends the moment someone remembers why they're supposed to hate their neighbor.

Or worse. Worse is Corvane. Worse is selling it in pieces, war by war, atrocity by atrocity, to noble houses who'd pay a fortune to learn exactly whom their ancestors killed and how much is owed.

"No secrets," Oscar said quietly. He was still in the water. He hadn't gotten up. "That was the bargain. You don't have to tell me everything. But tell me it's bad, or tell me it's good, because I've spent three weeks watching you carry something and I can't — I can't do the sums anymore, Astrid. I need one number."

I looked at him. The clerk. The liar. The man who hid maps in false ledger pages and confessed to thieving memories the way other men confess to drinking, and who had, despite everything, walked into a drowned church because I told him to.

"It's bad," I said. "It's the worst thing there is. And I'm it."

Something went through his face — not surprise. Confirmation. He'd known. Some part of the bookkeeper had known since Ashgate, since the seal matched the fragment, since the map chose me, and he'd let me walk ahead anyway because he needed me to find out on my own terms.

That should have made me furious. It made me tired instead, tired all the way down to the wolf-shaped scar, and underneath the tiredness, colder than the water:

Terrified. Because I finally knew why everyone wanted me, and I knew what I was carrying, and I knew — with the certainty of the girl in the burning wheat, the certainty I had chosen once and was now being asked to choose again — that the moment anyone else learned what I knew, the peace died.

"Get up," I said. "We're leaving. And Oscar — when I tell you what's next, you're going to want to argue. Don't. We have to go deeper under the capital, and we have to go before Corvane finds out the key just remembered where the door is."

His mouth opened. Closed. "How do you know what's next?"

"Because," I said, wading past him toward the gap in the roof, toward the grey light, toward the rest of my terrible life, "I've done this before."
