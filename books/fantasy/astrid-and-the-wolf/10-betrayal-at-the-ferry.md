## 10. Betrayal at the Ferry

**Astrid**

The river was called the Sorrow, which struck me as honest naming for once. Most rivers get pretty names — Silvermere, the Laughing Water, whatever some bard made up after a good night. The Sorrow just sat there under a grey sky, wide and slow and brown as old tea, looking like it had heard every secret ever carried across it and kept none of them clean.

The ferryman was a one-eyed woman named Brack who charged double because she could smell desperation on travelers the way I could smell rain coming. She took our coin without counting it, which told me she'd already decided we were trouble.

"Crossing at dusk," she said. "Mist comes up off the water. Good cover."

"From what?" Oscar asked.

"Whatever's following you," she said, and spat over the side in a way that suggested this was not her first desperate cargo.

I looked at Oscar. He was doing his ledger-keeper thing — hands folded, face arranged into mild apology, the whole performance of a man who has never once been interesting. I'd spent three weeks on the road with him and I still couldn't decide whether he was the worst liar I'd ever met or the best.

"You knew we were followed," I said to him.

"I suspected," he said. "There's a difference. Legally speaking."

"We're not being sued, Ishikawa."

"No. We're being chased by people who buy memories and people who burn them. I find it helps to keep my categories precise. It's how I stay calm."

He wasn't calm. I'd learned his tells by now: when Oscar Ishikawa was frightened, he got tidy. He straightened his cuffs. He checked the false bottom of his satchel where the map lived, and then checked it again ten minutes later like a man patting a wound to make sure it hadn't reopened.

I watched him do it now. Third time since noon.

Here is what nobody tells you about having holes where your life used to be: you develop instincts to fill the gaps. I didn't remember learning to read a man's fear, but I could do it the way I could hold a sword — knowledge living in the body with no story attached. And every instinct I had said that Oscar was carrying more than one secret, and that one of them had my name on it.

Not Astrid Vane. The other name. The one the map's seal whispered at me in dreams — a word in no language I knew that I somehow understood anyway, the way you understand a bell means dinner even if you've never seen a kitchen.

*Vaelthorne.* The drowned chapel. The altar that had shown me a flash of snow and blood and a crown going into dark water before I'd snatched my hand back like a girl touching a stove.

I hadn't told him about that. First secret I'd ever kept from him, and I'd found it fit surprisingly well. Like a coat cut for me years ago.

"Mist's rising," Brack called. "On the boat, or swim."

We boarded. The ferry was flat-bottomed and foul-smelling, poles and rope and a lantern that gave up more smoke than light. Oscar sat near the middle with his satchel in his lap like a child holding a cat it wasn't allowed to keep. I stood at the prow, watching the far bank resolve out of the mist — a mud landing, a shrine to some river god whose statue had lost its face to weather or vandalism, a road climbing away toward the mountains.

Toward the vault.

Halfway across, the mist behind us coughed.

I don't know how else to describe it. Mist doesn't cough. But something moved through it wrong, the way water moves wrong around a submerged log, and Brack's single eye went wide and she said, very quietly, "That'll be your trouble, then," and began poling faster, which was the bravest and most useless thing available to her.

"Astrid." Oscar's voice had gone thin. "How many?"

I counted shapes in the fog. Four, maybe five, moving along the bank parallel to us, keeping pace easily. Guild enforcers didn't hurry. Hurrying implied doubt.

"Enough," I said, and drew my sword.

* * *

**Oscar**

Let me tell you what I know about drowning, since I got an education in it that evening.

You don't drown in water first. You drown in arithmetic. My mind, being a ledger-keeper's mind, did the sums while the enforcers closed in: four of them, one of her, one of me, and me worth approximately nothing in a fight except as a hostage, which was precisely my market value to everyone involved.

The first enforcer came aboard at the bow like fog given a job. Tall, grey-cloaked, face wrapped so only the eyes showed, and the eyes were the worst part — patient, uninterested, the eyes of a clerk repossessing goods. I recognized him. Maren. He'd inventoried my counting room twice a year for six years. He'd once complimented my penmanship.

"Ledger-keeper," Maren said. "Guildmaster wants the map. And the Wolf, if she's undamaged. Damaged is negotiable."

"She's not merchandise," I said, which was stupid, because everything is merchandise where I come from. That's the entire business model.

Maren drew a short hooked blade. Astrid met him in the space between the cargo crates, and I will spend the rest of my life failing to describe what happened there properly, because it lasted nine seconds and contained a lifetime. She fought the way water fights — no wasted motion, no anger, just inevitability finding its level. Maren went over the side with a broken wrist and a changed opinion of the evening. The second enforcer came at her from behind and learned why you don't do that. The third hung back, and the fourth —

The fourth went for me.

Of course he did. I was the soft target, the insurance policy, the man holding the actual object everyone wanted. He caught my collar and put the hook of his blade against my throat and said, "Drop it or I open him," and Astrid — mid-fight, bleeding from a cut above her eye — turned and looked at me across the deck with an expression I will be honest about now, because honesty is all I have left:

She looked tired.

Not afraid. Not furious. Tired, the way you're tired when a debt you always knew was coming finally arrives with interest.

"Give it to them," she said.

And here is the moment I should tell you about the second map.

Because there were two. There had always been two. The night the stranger died in my counting room, before the enforcers reached the guildhall door, I had done the most natural thing a bookkeeper can do: I made a copy. Not the original — the original stayed hidden in the false ledger page, and later traveled in my satchel. But a copy, pressed and sealed, tucked into the lining of my coat, existed from the very first night. Insurance. Leverage. A card to play if Corvane cornered me, a way to buy my life with someone else's secret.

I told myself it was prudence. Bookkeeping is not thieving; copying is not betraying. I had a whole ledger of sentences like that, each one balanced neatly against the truth.

The truth was that three weeks ago, in a smuggler's inn, I had watched Astrid recognize the seal on that map like a wound recognizing salt, and I had chosen — quietly, deliberately, in the part of myself where I kept the real accounts — not to mention the copy. Because a man who surrenders all his leverage is a man who trusts, and I had built six careful invisible years on never trusting anyone further than I could audit them.

She was standing eight feet away, sword wet with guild blood, telling me to give it up, and I understood with perfect clarity that she already knew. Women like Astrid don't say *give it to them* unless they've done the same arithmetic I have. She'd known there was a copy. Maybe since Hollowmere. Maybe since the inn. She'd walked beside me for weeks knowing it, waiting to see whether I'd confess.

I hadn't confessed. So now the enforcers would take it, and the confession would be written in their taking.

The fourth enforcer's knife bit into my shoulder as he searched me — not deep, just a butcher's punctuation — and his fingers found the oilcloth packet in my coat lining, and he pulled it free and held it up, and the lantern light caught the wax seal, and Astrid looked at it, and then at me, and something in her face closed like a ledger at end of day.

"The deal with Corvane," she said. Her voice was almost gentle, which was infinitely worse than shouting. "When was it made? Before the inn, or after?"

"After," I said. "It was never — I never sent it. It was just —"

"Insurance." She nodded slowly. "Yes. You'd have needed a word for it. Something with fewer teeth than betrayal."

The enforcers were withdrawing — Maren back at the rail, cradling his wrist, the packet secured, the job half-done and satisfied with it. The mist took them the way it takes everything on the Sorrow: completely, without comment.

Brack poled hard for the far bank. The current helped. The current, I think, wanted to be rid of us.

I knelt on the deck holding my shoulder, blood between my fingers, and watched Astrid stand at the prow with her back to me, and I understood that the crossing was over in every sense that mattered.

* * *

**Astrid**

The mud landing received us like a mouth. I stepped off first. Oscar followed, pale, dripping, pressing his coat against his shoulder, and he opened his mouth to speak and I held up a hand.

"Don't," I said. "Not yet. If you talk now I'll do something permanent."

He closed his mouth. Points for obedience. He'd have scored higher if he'd managed to look sorry instead of sorry-for-himself, but grief arrives however it arrives, and his had clearly arrived wearing his own face.

Brack pushed off without a word. Smart woman. The last ferryman who lingered over our business was probably still being invoiced for it.

I walked up the road toward the faceless shrine and sat down on its steps, because my legs had opinions, and let Oscar stand there bleeding in the dark for the length of ten breaths. Then I tore a strip off my cloak and threw it at him.

"Bind it. Properly. You're no use to anyone drained dry."

He bound it. His hands shook. Of course they did.

"How long," I said.

"What?"

"How long have you known you'd sell me? Was it the plan from the start — hand me to Corvane, collect your fee, retire somewhere with better ledgers? Or did you grow into it? Did it come on gradually, like rot?"

"It was never a plan." His voice cracked down the middle. "It was a door I kept unlocked. That's different. That's —"

"That's worse." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "A plan you could repent of. An unlocked door is just who you are."

He flinched like I'd hit him. Part of me wanted to hit him for real. A larger part, and this is the part I hated, remembered Hollowmere — the village with no mothers, the way he'd wept and told me the truth about the guild with nothing held back, the punch I'd thrown him and the walking we'd done together afterward. Three weeks of shared fire and shared watch and one conversation at 3 a.m. outside a ruined chapel where he'd asked me, quietly, what I'd do if I got my memories back, and listened to the answer like it mattered.

I had believed that man. That was the injury. Not the copy — the copy was just paper and wax. The injury was that I'd believed him, and belief, in my experience, was a currency that only lost value.

"You could have told me," I said. "Any night. Around any fire. One sentence: *there's a copy, I'm keeping it as leverage.* I'd have argued. I'd have been angry. But I'd have known you chose me over the exit. Instead you let me walk beside a locked door for three weeks and call it trust."

"If I'd told you, you'd have taken it from me."

"Yes," I said. "Probably. And then neither of us would be sitting here wondering if the other one is a liar."

The river sighed behind us. Somewhere across the water, the guild's men were carrying my map — my map, my sealed past, the thing that had surfaced in my hands at the chapel like a fish rising for air — back to a man who sold history by the pound.

And underneath my fury, quiet as sediment, another thought settled: they had the copy. Which meant Corvane had what he needed to reach the vault without me. Unless the vault needed more than paper. Unless it needed blood, and consent, and a woman who couldn't remember why she'd bled for it the first time.

Unless I was still the key, and they'd stolen the map to the lock while leaving the lock itself standing in the road, bleeding, furious, and free.

I stood up. My sword arm ached. My eye throbbed where the enforcer's blade had kissed it. None of it mattered.

"Where are you going?" Oscar asked.

"Away from you." I picked up my pack. "They have the copy. They don't need me tonight, which means tonight is the only night I can move without a leash. I'm going to reach the vault before Corvane does, and I'm going to do it alone, because alone is the only company I've never been betrayed by."

"And me?"

I looked at him. Bleeding, soaked, kneeling in the mud at the foot of a faceless god, holding a strip of my cloak against his shoulder like it was something precious. Half of me wanted to leave him there forever. The other half — the half that had punched him in Hollowmere instead of walking away — knew exactly what kind of cowardice mercy was.

"The guild knows your face, the Crown wants your head, and you're wounded on the wrong side of a river full of things that follow desperate people," I said. "You'll survive. You're very good at surviving. It's the only thing you've ever been good at honestly."

I turned up the mountain road.

Behind me, I heard him say my name — not *the Wolf*, not *Vane*, just my name, the way he'd said it at 3 a.m. outside the chapel — and I heard something else beneath it, further down the bank, where the mist was thick and moving wrong again.

Hooves. Many. Moving with the unhurried confidence of an institution.

I didn't look back. Looking back is how you learn whether the person you left bleeding got up or didn't, and I discovered, climbing into the dark, that I didn't want to know — because if he didn't get up, part of me would grieve, and if he did, part of me would hope, and both of those were more dangerous than anything the guild owned.

The hooves reached the landing behind me.

Then a voice I didn't know, smooth and reasonable as a legal document, said: "Master Ishikawa. The Crown has questions. Let's begin with the map."

I walked faster.
