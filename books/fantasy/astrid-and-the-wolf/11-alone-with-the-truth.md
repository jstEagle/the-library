## 11. Alone with the Truth

There is an art to being captured, and I had always assumed I'd never need to learn it. I was a ledger-keeper. My hands were stained with ink, not blood. In the taxonomy of dangerous men, I ranked somewhere below the guild's tea-steward and slightly above the boy who polished the memory-bottles until they gleamed like a saint's teeth.

And yet here I was, face-down on the floor of a river barge's hold, with a knee between my shoulder blades and the particular smell of government-issue boot leather filling my nose. The Crown's inquisitors, it turns out, do not announce themselves with trumpets. They announce themselves with a hand over your mouth and a whispered question: *"Where is the map, clerk?"*

I told them I didn't know what they meant. I told them I was a simple accountant, that my most serious crime to date was under-reporting the guild's candle expenditures to avoid a tariff. The man holding me down — a broad, patient sort with the eyes of a man who has heard every lie ever invented and filed them alphabetically — waited until I finished, and then he said, "Master Ishikawa. We have been watching you since the stranger died in your counting room. We know what you carry. The only variable in tonight's arithmetic is whether you arrive at your destination breathing."

I have been accused of many things, most of them accurate, but I have never been accused of being bad at arithmetic.

So I breathed.

* * *

They took me to a manor house in the hills above the river, the kind of place that looks from the road like a wealthy family's summer retreat and looks from the inside like exactly what it is: a place where questions get answered whether or not anyone in them wishes to answer. The cell they gave me was, I will admit, comfortable. A real bed. A washbasin. A window with an actual view of orchards going gold in the late light. I have stayed in worse inns, and paid for the privilege.

That was the first lesson of Inquisitor Hale, though I didn't understand it yet: he does not frighten you first. He makes you comfortable first. Fear makes men stupid and stubborn. Comfort makes them *reasonable*, and reasonableness is a door that swings both ways.

I was fed. I was allowed to sleep. In the morning, a servant brought me tea in an honest ceramic cup, and with it came Hale.

I had imagined the Crown's chief inquisitor as a slab of a man, all jaw and doctrine. What came through the door was a thin, grey gentleman in a plain coat, carrying a leather folder the way a tutor carries lesson plans. He looked tired in the way that honest men look tired — not from dissipation, but from carrying something heavy for a very long time. He set the folder on the table, sat across from me, and poured his own tea.

"Oscar Ishikawa," he said. "Ledger-keeper, third rank, Guild of Impossible Things. Eleven years of service. Commendation for accuracy. Reprimand for — let me see —" he consulted a page, though I suspect he knew it by heart — "unauthorized sentimentality regarding a client in Vell's Cross. You returned a memory of a wedding without recording the fee."

"The fee was extortionate," I said.

"It was the guild's standard rate."

"Which is what I said."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. A smile's distant cousin, the kind that shows up at funerals. "You're wondering why you're not in a dungeon."

"The thought had crossed my mind, along with several of its relatives."

"Because I don't want your fear, Master Ishikawa. Fear is cheap. I can buy fear from any thug on the Crown's payroll. What I want is your *understanding*." He turned the folder around and slid it across the table. "Before you answer any questions, I want to show you what you're carrying. What you've involved yourself in. And then I'm going to ask you to help me, and you're going to want to. That's not a threat. It's a warning."

I looked at the folder. It was old, older than the guild, older than the dynasty, if the wear on the leather meant anything. The seal on it had been broken so long ago that the wax had gone the color of old bone.

"Before I open this," I said, "I'd like to note for the record that I'm a clerk, not a hero, and that everything I've done so far has been the result of other people making decisions at me."

"Noted," said Hale. "For the record: I believe you."

And that, more than the knee between my shoulder blades, more than the comfortable cell, more than the tea — that unsettled me. Because I recognized the tone. It was the tone I used with clients who came to sell their grief, the tone that said *I am not your enemy, the world is, and I am merely its bookkeeper.*

I opened the folder.

* * *

The archive did not look like much. Paper, mostly. Hundreds of pages in a dozen hands, some careful, some frantic, some written by people who were clearly crying while they wrote. There were inventories. Rosters. Grain requisitions. Lists of the dead that went on for so many pages that the numbers stopped being numbers and became a kind of weather.

"Before the erasure," Hale said quietly, "there were eleven kingdoms where there are now four. The war lasted ninety-three years. It did not begin over territory or faith or honor, whatever the ballads of the surviving kingdoms might eventually have claimed. It began over a famine, and a border granary, and two governments that each believed the other had stolen bread from their children's mouths."

I turned a page. A city's name. I did not know it. I would never know it; that was the point, wasn't it? The page listed its population before the war and its population after, and the arithmetic between those two numbers was the kind that keeps clerks up at night, and I am a clerk, and I have not slept properly since.

"There were atrocities," Hale went on, in the tone of a man reading a shipping manifest, because I think he had learned that the only way to say these things aloud was to say them flatly. "On all sides. That is not a deflection; it is a finding. I have read the accounts from every kingdom. Every army burned something it should not have burned. Every government did something to its own people that it could not survive its own people knowing. And by the war's last decade, the grievances had become so layered — this kingdom's grandmother starved because that kingdom's grandfather burned a granary, and that kingdom's grandfather burned it because the other one had hanged his sons — that no peace could hold. Not a truce. Not a treaty. Not a victory. Any ending that left memory intact would only be an intermission."

"So they ended the war," I said. My voice came out strange. "By ending the *knowing* of it."

"There was a working. The greatest ever performed, before or since. Every mind alive, on every side, made to forget the war entire. The dead were given new causes. The ruined cities were given new explanations — fires, plagues, the ordinary cruelty of the world. And the few who could not be made to forget — because the working required anchors, vessels, witnesses to hold the truth so it could be sealed away rather than simply destroyed —" he paused, and I watched him choose his words with the care of a man defusing something — "those few gave up everything else instead. Their names. Their histories. Themselves."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere outside, an orchard bird was making an absolute idiot of itself, and I resented it deeply.

"Astrid Vane," I said.

"Was one of them," Hale agreed. "Perhaps the last of them. The vault does not open to her blood alone, Master Ishikawa. It opens to her *consent*. She agreed, once, already. She is the lock's own key, and the lock remembers her."

I sat with that. I sat with the folder open in front of me and the ghost-arithmetic of dead cities swimming in my eyes, and I thought about Astrid, who punched like a landslide and trusted like one too, who had gaps in her memory she'd once described to me as "rooms with the furniture missing." She hadn't been robbed. She had *paid*. She had walked into that vault and bought the world's peace with the only currency she had, which was herself, and the world had taken the payment and forgotten to say thank you, because saying thank you would have required remembering what the thanks was for.

"Why show me this?" I asked. "You could have just told me to hand over the map. I'd have handed it over. I'm not brave. It's one of my best qualities."

"Because the map is the lesser problem." Hale folded his hands on the table. "The map can be burned. Astrid Vane can be — managed. But the vault itself, and the archive within it, and the truth sealed there — that is a wound that has been stitched shut once, and every generation, someone picks at the thread. The guild. Old noble houses that half-remember their ancestors' grudges without knowing why. Ambitious fools who think a war is an asset. And now a ledger-keeper with a commendation for accuracy and a reprimand for sentimentality, holding a copy of the way in."

"I don't have a copy," I said, which was true at that moment, since Astrid had left me bleeding on a riverbank with my copy confiscated and my dignity in similar condition. But Hale's expression suggested that the Crown's intelligence on the ferry ambush was better than mine, and that he knew exactly what I had and what I'd lost, and that the losing had been noted and possibly even engineered. I revised my assessment of the tea. It had been a bribe all along. A very good one.

"The guildmaster, Corvane," Hale said, "intends to open the vault and sell the war in pieces. A grievance here, an atrocity there, priced for the noble houses that still keep their grandfathers' swords oiled. He was there, Master Ishikawa. At the sealing. He was one of the architects, before he decided that forgetting was a product rather than a mercy. He has spent two lifetimes waiting for the market to ripen." Hale's voice did not rise. It got *flatter*, which was worse. "If he opens that vault, the war comes back. Not the armies — those are ash. The *reasons*. And reasons, given enough time and enough ledgers, grow armies the way rot grows mushrooms."

"And the Crown's answer is to bury it deeper."

"The Crown's answer is to finish the job. Properly, this time, with witnesses who understand what they're burying and why. The first erasure was desperate, rushed, done by dying people in a burning world. It left threads. The map is a thread. Vane is a thread. The vault is a knot, and knots want picking." He leaned forward, and for the first time I saw what was under the tired grey gentleman — not cruelty, never cruelty, but a conviction so complete it had the same silhouette. "I am offering you a place in the finishing of it. Help me seal the vault forever. Help me retire Astrid Vane's part in this — gently, I give you my word, as gently as such things can be done. And when it's finished, you go back to your ledgers, and no one ever again has to choose between a truth that kills millions and a lie that keeps them alive."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you die with it," he said, without heat, the way one reads the terms of a loan. "I won't pretend otherwise. I don't enjoy that part. But I have buried a great many things I didn't enjoy burying, and the world is quieter and kinder for it, and I have made my peace with the interest on my soul."

I looked down at the folder. At the lists of the dead, whose names I would forget by tomorrow, because that was the design, because that was always the design. And here is the thing I am not proud of, the thing I will carry even after I've carried everything else away: for one long, warm moment, I *wanted* it. I wanted the comfortable lie the way a cold man wants a fire. I wanted to hand over the map and the girl and the whole impossible tangle, and go back to a counting room where the worst thing on my conscience was a candle tariff. I wanted someone competent to make the choice so I wouldn't have to. Peace, purchased wholesale, no further thought required. It was the guild's oldest product, I realized — not memories, not storms, not stolen years. *Certainty.* And Hale was offering it at a discount.

"Master Ishikawa," Hale said softly, watching my face the way I'd watched clients' faces for eleven years. "You've spent your life keeping accounts for thieves. Here is a chance to keep the last honest one. The account of a debt the world paid once, and should never be asked to pay again. All it costs is a map, a knight's consent, and your silence." He closed the folder gently, the way one closes a coffin. "Take the evening. Think about how heavy the truth is. And think about how much lighter you'll be without it."

They escorted me back to my comfortable cell. The bed was soft. The window showed orchards going gold. And I lay there in the dark doing the one thing I am genuinely, catastrophically good at: keeping accounts.

Because Hale had made one error, the same error the guild makes, the same error every buyer makes. He had shown me the ledger. And I had read it. And in ninety-three years of war and one miracle of forgetting, in all those pages of the dead, I had found no entry — not one — for what Astrid Vane had paid. No line item for her name, her history, her self. The world's greatest debt, and it had never been recorded, because the people who owed it had forgotten they owed anything at all.

I am a clerk. I do not forgive an unbalanced book.

Somewhere down the corridor, a guard's boots passed and faded. Somewhere beyond the orchards, Astrid was alone with a truth she hadn't told me, moving toward a vault that remembered her. And somewhere in my coat, in a seam I'd sewn with the same hands that kept the guild's false pages, there was a thread of the map I had never surrendered — not to Corvane, not to the enforcers, and not, as of tonight, to the comfortable lie.

I had until morning to decide how to use it.

Hale thought he had captured a clerk.

He had. That was rather the point.
