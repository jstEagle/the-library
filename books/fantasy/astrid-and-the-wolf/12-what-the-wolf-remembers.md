## 12. What the Wolf Remembers

The inn at Grayhollow had one bed, one chair, and a roof that leaked in three places with the confidence of long practice. I took the chair. Oscar was two days behind me on the road somewhere — bleeding, probably, and lying about it to some farmer's wife who'd patch him up because he had that face, that apologetic ledger-keeper's face that made people want to feed him. I'd fed him twice myself before I remembered I hated him.

I sat by the fire with my boots off and my sword across my knees, and I did what I'd been doing every night since the drowned chapel: I waited for my own head to betray me.

It always did. That was the strange mercy of it. You'd think a mind with holes in it would be quiet, but no. The holes leak. Memories come back the way water comes through a dam — not all at once, which would at least be honest, but in trickles, in seeps, in one wet finger finding the crack and working at it until the whole thing gives.

That night, the crack was the smell of woodsmoke and lilies.

* * *

I need to explain something about how memory comes back when it's been taken rather than lost. Lost memory is like a room with the furniture gone — you know the shape of it, you walk around the absences. Taken memory is different. Taken memory is a room someone bricked over while you were standing in it, and now they're knocking the bricks out one at a time from the other side, and each one comes through still warm.

Woodsmoke and lilies came through warm.

I was standing in a hall I knew — knew the way you know your own hands — a great vaulted thing built of white stone gone gray with incense, and there were banners hanging from the rafters that I could not read, and I understood, even inside the memory, that I could not read them because reading them would mean something, and the something was the whole point of the sealing wax on my thoughts.

There were nine of us. I counted us the way you count soldiers before a march, out of habit, out of dread. Nine people in a semicircle around an altar, and I was the tenth, standing apart, holding a knife that wasn't for anyone else.

A woman with silver in her braids was speaking. I couldn't hear her words — they'd been cut out of the memory like a tumor, cleanly, with margins — but I could see her mouth move, and I could see the others nodding, and I could see that three of them were crying and none of them were ashamed of it.

And then the woman turned to me, and her face —

Her face was my face. Older. Tired in a way I recognized from mirrors, the tiredness that lives under the eyes and doesn't wash off. She looked at me across whatever distance separated the woman giving the memory from the woman receiving it, and she said — and this part hadn't been cut, this part had been left in deliberately, like a note pinned to a door —

*"You'll hate us for this. Hate us anyway. Do it anyway."*

Then the knife turned in my hand, and I looked down at my own palm, and I made the cut.

* * *

I came back to myself sitting on the floor of the Grayhollow inn with the chair tipped over behind me and my heart going like a drum corps. The innkeeper's dog was regarding me from the doorway with the particular disappointment dogs reserve for humans who wake up screaming.

"Fine," I told the dog. "Everything's fine."

The dog did not believe me. Dogs never do. It's why I prefer them to people.

I righted the chair and sat back down and made myself breathe, in through the nose, four counts, hold, out — the way some drillmaster had taught me once, in a life I couldn't reach, and the fact that my body remembered discipline my mind had lost was either comforting or horrifying and I hadn't decided which.

Here is what I knew now, laid out on the floor of me like instruments after a battle:

I had been there. At the sealing. Not nearby, not adjacent, not a witness at the edge of things — I had been the knife. Nine people had given up their memories of the war, poured them into the vault like wine into a cask, and someone had to seal the cask, and that someone had been me. And then — this was the part that made my hands shake, this was the part I kept turning over like a stone looking for the soft underside — then I had done it to myself too. Last. Voluntarily. Because a vault sealed by nine memories and guarded by a tenth witness isn't sealed at all if the witness remembers.

I was the lock. I had always been the lock. The wolf-shaped scar on my ribs, the gaps in my past, the way I flinched at white stone and woke at night tasting lilies — I hadn't been robbed by the guild or the Crown or any thief with clever fingers. I had robbed myself, on purpose, and then hidden the key so well that even I couldn't find it.

Except the key was finding itself. Brick by brick. Warm from the other side.

* * *

You want to know the worst part? Not the cutting. Not the sacrifice — though I'll get to that, give me a moment, I'm building up to it the way you build up to pressing on a bruise.

The worst part was that I remembered choosing it. Not the reasons — those were cut away, sold or burned or buried, I don't know which — but the *choosing*. The weight of it. There's a specific feeling that comes when you decide something enormous and irreversible, and it doesn't feel like triumph or terror. It feels like setting down something very heavy and knowing you can never pick it up again. Relief and grief in the same breath, twins, indistinguishable.

I had felt that. In the white-stone hall, with my own blood welling in my palm, I had felt relief.

And now, sitting in a cheap inn with a bad roof, I felt its opposite: the horror of picking the weight back up without knowing why I'd ever set it down.

Because here's the arithmetic that kept me awake until the candle died:

If I chose forgetting once — knowing everything, weighing everything, with nine witnesses and a war's worth of dead stacked behind my decision — then the woman I had been was smarter than I am. She had more information. She had the whole picture, and she looked at it, and she said: bury it, bury it deep, and let the world start over clean.

And I was walking toward the vault to dig it all back up.

* * *

I didn't sleep. Around the third hour of the morning, when the fire had burned down to a red eye and the rain started doing its work on the roof, I got out the map.

Not mine. His. The copy — the second copy, the one he'd pressed into my hands at the ferry bank with blood on his teeth and honesty finally, finally coming out of him like pus from a wound. *Take it. I copied it as insurance. I was going to sell you, Astrid. I want you to have it instead.* He'd said it kneeling in the mud, and I'd hit him — open-handed, which for me is practically a caress — and taken it, and walked away, and hated myself for taking it, and here I was, two days later, holding it by firelight like it was scripture.

Wax-sealed vellum, guild-made, the kind of craftsmanship that costs more than a farm. The Vault of Unremembered Kings, drawn in a cartographer's hand, with annotations in at least three scripts, two of which I couldn't read and one of which I now realized — with a cold sliding feeling in my stomach — I could.

The old script. The one on the banners in the white-stone hall. The one I supposedly couldn't read.

I spread it flat on the floor and knelt over it and read.

Most of it was route-marking. Distances, hazards, the usual smuggler's litany — *flooded gallery, unstable stair, do not trust the third bridge* — the kind of notes that say someone had walked this road before and come back changed enough to warn the next fool. But down in the corner, beneath the drawing of the vault itself, there was a passage in the old script, small and cramped, written by a different hand than the rest. A hand I knew. I knew it the way you know your own handwriting, because it *was* my own handwriting, older, pressed hard into the vellum like the writer had been afraid the words wouldn't take.

I translated it slowly, my lips moving, feeling like a child sounding out letters — except the child was me, thirty years ago, or three hundred, time went strange when I tried to look at it directly.

It said:

*To whoever finds this, and most of all to me:*

*The vault opens to my blood and my consent. Nothing else. No army, no guild, no crown can force it. Remember this when you are tempted, because you will be tempted — they will offer you your past like a plate of sweets, and grief will make you hungry.*

*I gave up everything so that no one would ever again know how to hate the way they hated. If you undo that, you had better have a reason worth nine lives and a hundred thousand dead. You won't. You never do. Reasons are what we call our appetites when we want them respected.*

*Seal it again. Seal it better. And this time, leave yourself a way to remember that you chose.*

*— A.V.*

* * *

I sat back on my heels for a long time.

Rain on the roof. The dog had given up on me and gone to sleep by the door. Somewhere out on the dark road, a cart passed, wheels hissing in the wet, going somewhere that wasn't here, carrying someone whose worst problem was probably weather.

*A way to remember that you chose.*

She — I — the woman in the white-stone hall — she had known. Of course she'd known. Anyone clever enough to design a lock out of their own skull is clever enough to wonder what happens when the skull starts leaking. So she'd left herself a message, scratched into the only copy of the map that mattered, a letter from the woman who knew everything to the woman who would know nothing.

And then the guild had gotten hold of the original. Corvane's people, or their predecessors — harvesting, curating, filing the past away in ledgers like everything else. They'd copied the map's geography because geography sells, but the corner inscription, the warning, the *letter* — either they hadn't been able to read it, or they hadn't thought it mattered, or — and this possibility arrived last and stayed longest — they'd left it deliberately, because a lock that doesn't know it's a lock is easier to pick.

Either way, the original was gone. Burned or buried or locked in Corvane's archive with the rest of the world's stolen property.

Which meant the copy in my hands — Oscar's copy, the insurance policy of a man who betrayed me and then, in the same breath, unbetrayed me — was the only place in the world where my own decision still existed.

He didn't know what he was carrying. He'd copied it as leverage, as a bargaining chip, as the coward's hedge that ledger-keepers keep against a rainy day. He had no idea he was carrying the last testament of the person I used to be. When he pressed it into my hands in the mud, bleeding and sorry, he thought he was giving me a map.

He was giving me myself. Or the piece of myself that mattered most — not the war, not the grief, not the hundred thousand names, but the *choice*. Proof, in my own handwriting, that when I knew everything, I chose peace. That the hunger for my past — the ache I woke with every morning, the sense of being robbed, the wolf pacing in the cage of my own skull — that hunger was exactly what she'd predicted. *Grief will make you hungry. They will offer you your past like a plate of sweets.*

And I had been eating out of the guild's hand for weeks.

* * *

Here's the thing about resolve. People think it feels like steel. It doesn't. It feels like exhaustion that's decided to keep walking anyway.

I folded the map along its old creases and put it inside my shirt, against my skin, where the scar was. Then I sat by the dead fire and did the accounting — because apparently that disease is contagious, and Oscar had infected me with double-entry bookkeeping of the soul.

Assets: one map, one returning memory, one body that remembered how to fight even if I didn't remember learning. One knight's oath I couldn't fully recall but whose shape I could feel, the way a missing tooth's neighbors lean into the gap. One ledger-keeper, presumably alive, presumably contrite, presumably following me with the doomed persistence of a dog that's been kicked but hasn't learned anything.

Liabilities: a guild that wanted the vault opened and auctioned. An inquisitor who wanted it buried so deep it never existed. A war underneath the world, waiting to be remembered by everyone at once. And me — the lock, the key, the knife, the witness — walking toward all of it with a piece of vellum over my heart and no idea whether I was going to save the world or end it.

Because that was the question, wasn't it. That was the whole question, the one the woman with my face had answered once already and trusted me to answer the same way.

Did I complete the erasure? Did I stand in that vault with my blood on the altar and consent, again, willingly, knowingly — finish what we started, make the forgetting total and permanent, and lose whatever fragments were still seeping back? Lose the white-stone hall. Lose the silver-braided woman. Lose the sound of my own name spoken by people who loved me, if anyone ever had.

Or did I open it? Give the world back its war — every grievance, every atrocity, every name and grudge and inherited hatred, all of it flooding back into millions of minds at once like water into a cracked dam — and call it truth, and call it justice, and watch what grew in the field I'd plowed?

My hand found the scar through the cloth. Old, ridged, wolf-shaped, self-inflicted or otherwise — I still didn't know its story. Maybe I never would. Maybe that was the price, and maybe the price was fair.

"You'll hate us for this," the woman had said. "Hate us anyway. Do it anyway."

"I don't hate you," I said aloud, to the empty room, to the rain, to her. My voice came out rougher than I intended. "I just wish you'd left me a better letter."

The letter was the letter. *Reasons are what we call our appetites when we want them respected.* She'd known me. Knew that given a choice between my past and the world's future, I'd dress up wanting as needing, and she'd left me a mirror instead of a map.

Well. I had both now.

* * *

I slept two hours, dreamless, which I took as a gift from whatever managed the department of mercy. Then I dressed, strapped on the sword, paid the innkeeper triple for the broken chair and the dog's trouble, and stood in the doorway looking out at a gray dawn the color of dishwater.

East lay the ruined capital, and under it the vault, and under that the war, sleeping. Behind me, somewhere south, a bleeding clerk was probably limping toward the same destination, chasing a woman who'd left him in the mud, carrying nothing but guilt and penmanship.

I should leave him behind. Every instinct honed on twenty years of bounty work said so: loose ends get you killed, sentimental attachments get you killed slower, and a man who'd sell you once would sell you again the moment the price improved. Corvane would buy him back with pocket change. Hale would turn him with a kind word and a threat.

But.

But he'd copied the map, and the copy was the last of me. But he'd knelt in the mud and handed it over instead of running. But when I'd asked him — that first night in the smuggler's inn, blades drawn, both of us lying — he'd lied badly, the way honest people lie, and I'd known even then that he was the worst thief I'd ever met, and that someday that would matter.

And besides. Someone had to carry the ink. If I was going to seal a vault, I wanted a witness who kept accounts. Let it be written down somewhere, in a fair hand, in real numbers: *On this day, Astrid Vane chose. Here is what it cost. Here is what it bought.* Even if the only one who ever read it was me, afterward, not remembering why the words made me cry.

I turned east into the rain.

Behind me, faint on the wet road, I heard footsteps — uneven ones, favoring the left leg, accompanied by the apologetic jingle of a man trying to walk quietly and failing at it professionally.

I didn't draw. I didn't even turn around.

"You're following me," I said to the rain.

A pause. Then, from the gray morning behind me, wretched and sincere: "Technically, I was heading the same direction."

I almost smiled. Almost. Some things, I'm told, survive even forgetting.

"Hold this," I said, and threw him the map.

I heard him catch it — fumble, nearly drop, clutch it to his chest like the relic it was. And I kept walking east, toward the vault, toward the war, toward the choice I'd already made once and was about to make again — this time with witnesses, this time awake, this time as the wolf instead of the woman who became her.

Whatever happened under the ruined capital, it would happen on my terms.

I'd written them down myself.
