## 13. The Prisoner's Bargain

There is a particular quality to a cell in the Inquisition's tower that no guild ledger has ever managed to capture, and I say this as a man who once catalogued the interior of a bottled thunderstorm for inventory purposes. The cell is not cruel. That's the first thing you notice, and the thing that frightens you most. The cot is clean. The blanket is wool, decent wool. There is a pitcher of water on a shelf, and the shelf is sanded smooth so you won't catch splinters. Someone thought about your comfort here, and that someone wanted you to know it.

Cruelty is honest. Kindness with an agenda is a locked door painted like an open one.

I had been in the cell — I estimated — two days. Time moves strangely when you have nothing to count but your own pulse, and I am a man who counts things professionally. Two days, give or take a lie.

On the third morning, Inquisitor Hale came down himself.

I want to be clear about what I expected. I expected the rack, or whatever the Crown's modern equivalent is — I'd always assumed they'd moved on to something with more paperwork. I expected threats, maybe a demonstration on my fingers, the standard grammar of coercion. What I got instead was a tray of breakfast. Bread, butter, a boiled egg, tea that smelled like actual tea and not like the guildhall's usual floor-scrapings.

"Mr. Ishikawa," Hale said, setting the tray on the shelf beside the pitcher. He was a broad, grey-templed man with the face of a schoolmaster who had long ago stopped being disappointed and settled into something quieter and worse: patience. "I hope the accommodations are adequate."

"They're better than my rooms," I said. "Which says something damning about either the Crown or my landlord."

The corner of his mouth moved. Not quite a smile. A smile's rough draft. "You're a bookkeeper."

"I'm a ledger-keeper. There's a distinction. Bookkeepers record what happened. Ledger-keepers record what was traded. It's a subtler science."

"And which were you doing the night a bleeding man died in your counting room?"

Here we go, I thought, and reached for the lie I'd been rehearsing against the ceiling for two days. "He wasn't there for me. He was looking for the guild's memory-broker on the east row. Wrong door. People do that constantly. You wouldn't believe how often—"

"Oscar." My name, said gently, the way you'd steady a spooked horse. He sat down on the stool across from the bars — he'd brought his own stool, I noticed, which told me this visit had been planned down to its furniture — and folded his hands. "I'm going to tell you something true, because I find lies waste everyone's morning. We already have the map."

My stomach performed a maneuver I can only describe as professional envy — it dropped the way a ledger drops when the columns don't balance.

"You don't," I said.

"We found the false page in your counting room. Clever work, the hollow binding. Corvane's people use the same trick; did you know that? You learned forgery from thieves, Mr. Ishikawa, and thieves learn from each other." He unfolded his hands, refolded them. "What we do *not* have is the woman. Astrid Vane. And the map we recovered is incomplete — your stranger tore it, or you did, and the missing piece matters. So you see, I don't actually need you to confess anything. I need you to help me finish something."

"The war," I said. "You want to bury it again."

Something changed in his face. Not surprise — I think he'd expected me to know — but a kind of relief, the relief of a priest who no longer has to explain sin to a congregation that's already kneeling.

"Yes," he said. "And before you decide how to feel about that, I'm going to show you why."

* * *

They took me up, not down. I had assumed the Inquisition kept its horrors below ground, because everything I'd ever read suggested horrors are subterranean by tradition. Instead we climbed. Four flights, five, past offices where clerks looked up at me with the same incurious eyes I'd seen in the guildhall mirrors, until we reached a door of black iron and Hale spoke a word to it that I pretended not to memorize.

Inside was the archive.

I am going to describe it badly, because it cannot be described well. Imagine every ledger I ever kept, multiplied by a city. Shelves rising into shadow, and on them — not books. Vessels. Glass, ceramic, lead-sealed jars, each tagged in a hand so uniform it could only have been institutional. And threaded through the aisles, faint as heat-haze, a hum. Memory has a sound when enough of it is gathered in one place. Like a crowd heard through a wall. Like a country murmuring in its sleep.

"This is perhaps a tenth of it," Hale said quietly. "The rest is beneath the capital, behind the vault your stranger died trying to open. But this portion is mine to curate. Come. I want to show you a shelf."

He led me down an aisle to a section marked only with a date-range, and lifted a jar no bigger than a wine bottle. Inside, instead of liquid, there was a slow silver turning, the way fog turns when it can't decide whether to be weather or thought.

"A baker," Hale said. "From the city of Vel Marrow. Do you know it?"

"No."

"Neither does anyone. That's rather the point." He held the jar up to the lamplight. "This is his memory of the siege. Forty-one days. He remembered the smell of the bread ovens converted to fire-ovens. He remembered his daughter's name. He remembered the sound the eastern wall made when it came down. When the peace was signed, the Crown took all of it — from him, from every survivor on both sides, from every soldier, every widow, every child who watched from a hillside. We replaced it with a gentle nothing. A war that never happened, fought over a city that never burned, between nations whose grievances we filed away like tax records."

"Why?" I asked, though I knew. I'd known since Corvane bought that soldier's massacre over brandy. Knowing and feeling it are different animals, though. One lives in the head. The other lives somewhere lower and doesn't come when called.

"Because before the forgetting," Hale said, "that war had lasted three hundred years. Three hundred years, Mr. Ishikawa. Fathers handing their sons a hatred the way you'd hand down a trade. Atrocities kept fresh on purpose, pickled in song and story, fed to children as heritage. Both sides believed they were the wounded party, and both sides were right, and that is precisely why it could never end. Peace through victory was impossible — neither side could ever win enough. Peace through justice was impossible — the debts ran too deep in both directions. There was exactly one door left." He set the jar back on its shelf with a care that undid me more than any threat could have. "So we closed it. All of us. Together. The last witnesses agreed to forget, and the Crown enforced the agreement, and the killing stopped inside a generation. Millions live who would have died. Millions of grandchildren exist who would never have been born. That is what forgetting bought. I have read the accounts. I keep them myself." He turned to me, and his eyes were tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "Now tell me what the Guild of Impossible Things intends to sell, and to whom, and tell me again that truth is sacred."

I opened my mouth. Closed it. Because the honest answer was that Corvane intended to sell the war in installments — a grievance here, an atrocity there, priced for noble houses with old names and older grudges — and that I had balanced those ledgers without once asking what the entries weighed.

"I'm not defending the guild," I said finally.

"No. You're defending yourself. It's different, but related." He gestured down the aisle. "Walk with me."

We walked. And Hale talked, and I will grant him this: he didn't flatter, didn't seduce, didn't offer me anything except the view. He showed me the shelves the way a surgeon shows a student the body — plainly, without apology, trusting the material to make its own argument. Here, the memories of the eastern front. Here, the western. Here, a jar labeled simply *the order*, which contained, he said, the final command both armies received on the last day: *forget, and go home.* Here, a whole case of small things — a lullaby from a country that no longer remembered having a language, a recipe, a grave-digging technique, the name of a god.

"Every jar is a person's consent or a person's theft," he said. "Mostly consent. Mostly genuine. Some were taken from the dying, some from the defeated, and I will not pretend the accounting is clean. Nothing the Crown does is clean. But dirty water, Mr. Ishikawa, is still water, when the alternative is fire."

"And if someone wants it back?" I asked. "If a baker's granddaughter grows up and wants to know why her family flinches at the smell of bread?"

Hale stopped walking. For a moment the schoolmaster left his face entirely, and underneath was something rawer — a man standing in front of a door he had locked himself, hearing knocking.

"Then she flinches," he said, "and does not know why, and her children flinch less, and their children not at all. That is the bargain. That is what I believe. I have believed it for thirty years, and I have buried colleagues who died keeping it, and I will die keeping it myself, and I am telling you this not to convert you but because you deserve to know what you're fighting." He resumed walking. "Astrid Vane is the key to the vault. Whatever she carries, she carries unwillingly now — her choice was taken from her along with everything else. If the guild reaches her first, they will crack her open like a strongbox and auction the contents. If we reach her first—" He paused. "We will bury it again. Deeper. Permanently. And her with it, if need be."

"There it is," I said.

"There it is," he agreed. "I won't insult you by dressing it differently. So. Your options, laid out honestly: help me, and live comfortably under supervision for the rest of a quiet life. Refuse, and I regret to inform you that the Crown has protocols, and the protocols involve taking your memories of the last month and most of your childhood, and leaving you a pleasant, empty man who sells fish. Or—" and here he smiled the finished version of that smile, and it was worse than the draft, "—you can try to escape, and discover that the tower's wards respond to intent, and that I will be required to report your death as an accident, which I have done before, and which I did not enjoy."

"Has anyone ever chosen the third option?" I asked.

"Once. A guild courier, eleven years ago. Quick man. Faster than the wards." He said it without pride. "I signed the report myself."

* * *

They put me back in the cell, and I sat with the arithmetic.

That's what I do. It's the only prayer I know. I sat on the clean cot under the decent blanket and I added the columns, and the columns refused to balance, and I understood that this was the design. Hale hadn't threatened me into compliance. He'd shown me the truth — the real truth, the heavy kind — and let it do the threatening for him. Because the truth was that he might be right. Because the truth was that millions of living people stood on the far side of a universal amnesia, and every one of them was an argument. Because the truth was that I had spent my whole career telling myself bookkeeping was not thieving, and here was a man who had spent his telling himself burying was not murdering, and we were the same animal wearing different collars.

And underneath all of it, quiet as a false bottom in a drawer, another column I didn't want to read:

*Astrid.*

She didn't know what she carried. She was walking around with a war sealed in her skull like a letter she'd been forbidden to open, hunted by everyone, trusted by no one, and the only person who knew where the letter was addressed — the only person she'd punched instead of abandoned — was sitting in a comfortable cell drinking decent tea.

I thought about the drowned chapel. I thought about the look on her face when the altar touched her, the terror she thought she'd hidden, the way she'd told me nothing and I'd pretended not to notice, both of us lying by omission in the same direction, which is the closest thing to intimacy liars have.

Hale wanted to bury her. Corvane wanted to sell her. And I —

Well. I'm a thief. It was finally time to act like one.

* * *

He came back at dusk. I'll say this for Hale: he kept appointments. He brought the tray again, and the stool, and sat down across the bars with the weary patience of a man about to hear a confession he'd already absolved.

"Have you decided?"

"I have a counteroffer," I said.

His eyebrows rose a fraction. Encouraging. Inquisitors, I've decided, are just auditors with authority.

"Your wards," I said. "Respond to intent, you said. Escape intent. Which means they're keyed to reading a mind — mine, specifically, since I'm the asset in the cage. Yes?"

"Go on." Careful now. The schoolmaster scenting a trick in a student's sums.

"So here's my offer. I'll give you what you actually want. Not the map — you have most of it, and we both know the missing piece isn't paper, it's her. No. I'll give you the *guild*. Corvane's accounts. Every sale, every buyer, every noble house he's courted, every memory the guild harvested off battlefields and sold back to the losing side as comfort. Names, dates, prices. You could gut the entire trade in a season. Isn't that worth more than one clerk and half a map?"

It was, and I watched him know it was. His hands stilled in his lap. "Corvane's ledgers," he said slowly. "You memorized them."

"I memorize everything. It's my affliction." True, as far as it went. Also a stall, and he knew that too, damn him, because his eyes narrowed the precise width of a suspicion.

"And how would you deliver these accounts? From inside a ward that reads intent?"

"Dictation," I said. "You bring ink. I recite. No intent to escape required — just a man doing what he does best, which is putting numbers in order." I leaned forward, and I gave him my best honest face, the one I'd spent years perfecting in guildhall mirrors. "You said you don't enjoy the reports you sign, Inquisitor. Here's a chance to sign a different kind. Take the guild apart. Let the girl go. Bury your war twice as deep, with the people who'd dig it up rotting in your cells instead."

For a long moment he said nothing. The lamp between us hissed. Somewhere above us, the archive hummed its crowd-through-a-wall hum, ten thousand strangers dreaming in jars.

"It's a good offer," he said at last. "Which is why I don't trust it. You're a liar, Ishikawa. A competent one. I've watched competent liars for thirty years, and they all have a moment — a flicker, a shift, a breath taken slightly wrong — when the real plan surfaces under the offered one. So before I accept, I'm going to do something I rarely permit myself." He stood. "I'm going to check the sums."

He unlocked the cell.

I hadn't planned for that. I want to be honest, since honesty is the theme of the evening: I had planned for dictation, for ink, for the slow smuggling of truth out through a lawful channel, because that is what I am — a man who steals through paperwork. I had *not* planned for Hale opening the door and stepping inside and saying, quietly, "Hold still. This will feel like standing too close to a fire," and reaching toward my temple with two fingers and a focus that made the air around his hand bend like heat.

He was going to read me. Directly. Strip the ledgers out of my skull by force, verify the offer, and find — buried under the guild accounts, under the map, under everything — the one column I could not let him see. Astrid's face at the altar. The torn corner of the map sewn into my coat lining. The plan I'd built in two days of counting, the real plan, the one where I dictated nothing and escaped everything and got to her before either crown or guild could close their hands.

Panic, I have found, is just bad arithmetic done very fast. And in the space of one bad sum, I did the only thing left in my inventory.

I gave him a memory.

Not mine. Hers — no. Not hers. *Mine to carry.* The dying woman's last summer, the one I'd stolen back in chapter — forgive me, the one I'd taken months ago at her family's request, the harvest festival, the river, the grandson's laugh, the whole warm weight of it that I'd sworn to deliver somewhere safe someday. I had carried it in my head like contraband ever since, guilt pressing on the inside of my skull. And now I pressed it outward, hard, the way you'd shove a stranger out of the path of a carriage — straight through his reaching fingers, into the open door of the Inquisitor's mind.

Memory-transfer is supposed to require consent, apparatus, hours. It's supposed to be a trade. Nobody had ever told me what happens when you *throw* one.

Hale made a sound I will hear for the rest of my life. Not a cry. Something smaller and worse — the sound of a man inhaling a summer that isn't his. He staggered back against the cell wall, and his eyes went wide and gold-lit, and I knew exactly what he was seeing, because I'd seen it first: the festival lanterns. The river going orange. An old woman laughing with her whole ruined body while her grandson chased a dog through the shallows. A last summer, perfect and ordinary and utterly unguarded, pouring itself into a man who had spent thirty years believing that feeling was a debt the world couldn't afford.

He slid down the wall to the floor of the cell and stayed there, breathing.

I stepped over him. My hands were shaking so badly it took three tries to work the key ring off his belt, and I want it noted for the historical record that I apologized — out loud, to the man unconscious on the floor of my prison — because apparently even my crimes come with footnotes.

Then I stopped. In the doorway. With the corridor dark and the stairs going up and freedom four flights away, I stopped, because the Inquisitor was crying. Silently. Still under, deep in a dead woman's festival, tears running down the schoolmaster's face into his grey beard, and his lips were moving, shaping words I leaned close to catch.

"*Stay,*" he was whispering. "*Please. Stay, just — stay—*"

He wasn't begging the memory. He was begging the summer not to end. Thirty years of burying other people's warmth, and one stolen hour of it had found the exact size of the hole it left in him.

I could have taken the keys and gone. I nearly did. But I knelt, and I did the single strangest thing I have ever done in a life organized around strange transactions: I eased him fully under, gently, the way you'd settle a drunk friend, and I left the memory in him. All of it. The whole summer. Let him wake up owning a dead woman's joy, unasked, unrecoverable, a gift he'd never consent to and would never return. Let him sit with what the Crown takes from people. Let him audit *that*.

Then I locked the cell door behind me — symmetry matters, even in burglary — hung the keys on the outside hook where his clerks would find them, and walked up four flights of stairs past incurious eyes, out a service door, into a city that smelled like rain and coal smoke and being alive.

I didn't run until the second street. Professionalism.

* * *

By dawn I was three miles out, soaked, keyless, coinless, and grinning like an idiot into the rain, because I had committed treason against the two most powerful institutions in the known world and gotten away with a stolen summer as my only weapon. Somewhere behind me, an inquisitor was waking up with a festival in his chest and a decision to make: report the failure and hunt me, or sit alone with a dead woman's laughter and ask himself what he'd been burying all these years, and in whomse name.

I was betting — on no evidence whatsoever, on nothing but the look on his face — that Edmond Hale would sit with that question a long time. Long enough.

Ahead: mountains. Cold passes, thin roads, and one disgraced knight with a war in her skull, probably furious, possibly starving, certainly not expecting me. I had a torn map-corner sewn in my lining, a head full of other people's yesterdays, and absolutely no plan beyond the next ridge.

Which, for a ledger-keeper, felt suspiciously like freedom.

I walked north, toward the snow, toward the Wolf.
