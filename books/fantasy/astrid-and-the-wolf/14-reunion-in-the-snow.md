## 14. Reunion in the Snow

*Oscar*

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that no ledger can balance: the cost of a lie told to the one person who trusted you, paid out over weeks of frozen mountain passes while you try to catch up to her.

I had been doing that math since the ferry. It did not come out even. It never came out even.

Astrid Vane traveled like a wolf runs — not fast, but never stopping, always choosing the ground that would break a pursuer's ankles before it broke hers. I knew this because I had spent eleven days following the evidence of her passage through the Greymarch passes: a snare reset and left for whoever came behind (a courtesy, or a warning), a fire ring built against the wind the way soldiers build them, a boot print in the scree with the heel worn on the outside edge, the mark of a woman who walked like she expected the world to lunge at her.

And once, pressed into the snow beside a dead campfire, a single word scratched into the ice of a stream with what looked like a knife point:

**LIAR**

It had frozen overnight into something almost beautiful. I stood over it for a long time, my breath smoking, and thought: well. At least she knows.

Here is what I confessed to no one but myself, because there was no one else to confess to: I had copied the map. Not once but twice. The copy Astrid found at the ferry was insurance for Corvane — a down payment on my own skin, because I am, at bottom, a creature who keeps exits open the way other men keep knives. But there had been a second copy, hidden in the false ledger page where the stranger's map had lived for one terrible night, and when the guild enforcers took me off that riverbank, it had gone with me into Hale's keeping, and then out again, because Inquisitor Hale, whatever else he was, kept meticulous records, and meticulous records can be misfiled by a man who spent ten years learning exactly how ledgers lie.

So I had the map. All of it. Every ridge line, every drowned chapel, every instruction written in a dead king's cipher about blood and consent and doors that only open for the woman who closed them.

And I had a choice, which is the one thing I have never been able to hold without dropping it.

I could sell it. Corvane would pay enough to buy me a new name, a new city, a life where nobody said *Astrid* in my presence ever again. I could burn it, and let the vault stay shut, and let Astrid live out her life as a bounty hunter with holes in her head, safe and ignorant, which was the option I had been telling myself was noble ever since Hollowmere. Or I could do the thing that terrified me most, which was to walk up to a woman who had left me bleeding on a riverbank — deservedly, I want to be clear, deservedly — and hand her the truth with both hands and no conditions, and find out whether she cut my throat for it.

The guild teaches you that everything has a price. The Crown teaches you that everything has a purpose. Nobody teaches you what to do with a thing that has neither, that you simply *give*, and I suppose that's why it took me eleven days and most of my dignity to decide.

On the twelfth day, above the tree line, where the world went white and silent and the wind spoke only in complaints, I crested a shoulder of black rock and saw her camp below me in the lee of a fallen boulder. Small fire. One bedroll. A woman sitting up against the stone with her sword across her knees, awake, watching the pass behind her.

She had heard me coming, of course. She had probably heard me coming for two days and been deciding whether to bother killing me.

I raised both hands, empty, fingers spread, the universal language of *please don't*. Then I walked down into her firelight like a man walking into his own trial.

"Astrid," I said.

"Ledger-keeper." Her voice was flat as the snowfield. "You're limping."

"The enforcers were thorough."

"Good." She didn't move. The sword stayed across her knees, which meant it was not a threat yet, which from Astrid Vane was practically an embrace. "Sit. You look half-dead, and I want to know what you're carrying before I decide whether to finish the job."

I sat. The fire was small and mean and the best thing I had seen in eleven days.

"I'm going to tell you everything," I said. "Not because you'll forgive me. I've done the sums on forgiveness, and they don't come out even either. I'm going to tell you because you should have had it at the ferry, and because if I die on this mountain with it still in my head, it dies twice."

Her eyes narrowed. "Start with the map."

I reached inside my coat, past the false seam, past the page where a stranger's blood had once dried, and drew out the copy. Wax-sealed. Whole. The original seal, broken and re-sealed with the guild's own wax, which she would notice, because she noticed everything.

I held it out.

She didn't take it. For a long moment the wind filled the silence, and I knelt there with my arm extended like a man offering alms to a statue, and I said, "It's yours. No price. No conditions. No ledger entry. I stole your past, Astrid — not the memories themselves, I never had those, but the *door* to them, and I treated it like inventory. Like something I might trade for my own skin. That's what I am. That's what I've always been. And I'm done being it, at least with you."

"You copied it," she said. "At the ferry. Before we even crossed."

"Yes."

"You were going to give it to Corvane."

"I was going to *keep* it to give to Corvane. There's a difference. It's the difference between a knife and a knife you're holding behind your back." I turned my hand so the map caught the firelight. "I told myself it was insurance. I told myself a lot of things. I'm a very good storyteller when the audience is me."

"Why now?"

"Because Hale showed me the archive." My voice cracked on the second word, and I let it. Honesty, I had discovered, was mostly just letting yourself crack in front of witnesses. "Cities, Astrid. Names. Eleven years of war, and every person in it, catalogued and shelved. And then a peace treaty whose entire text was *forget*. Everyone forgot. Both armies, both kingdoms, the farmers and the kings and the children born after. They woke up in a world with a hole in it shaped like a war, and they called the hole peace." I swallowed. "And you sealed the door. You gave up everything you were to lock it. I know that now. I didn't know it at the ferry, and I should have, because you'd been telling me all along without words — the scar, the gaps, the way you flinched at the word *vow*. I was too busy keeping my exits open to listen."

The fire popped. Somewhere above us, snow slid off a rock with a long soft sigh.

Then Astrid Vane reached out and took the map from my hand.

She didn't thank me. She broke the seal with her thumb, read it by firelight with the same expression she wore reading contracts for bounties — cold, thorough, missing nothing — and then she folded it along its old creases and put it inside her breastplate, against her heart, where soldiers keep the things they'd die for.

"Corvane will come for it," she said.

"He's already coming. He was one of the architects. He doesn't want the war remembered, he wants it *itemized*."

"And Hale?"

"Hale let me go." I shook my head, still not entirely believing it myself. "I made him feel it. One memory, pushed into him raw — the last hour of the war, the one the archive keeps under triple seal. What forgetting actually cost. He sat down on the floor of his own office and didn't speak for an hour. Then he opened the door and told me if I ever came back he'd have to report it, so I shouldn't come back." I managed something that was almost a smile. "He believes forgetting saved millions. I think he needed to check his math."

Astrid studied me across the fire for a long time. Snow gathered on her shoulders and she didn't brush it off. The wolf-shaped scar at her collarbone caught the light, pale against her throat, and I thought about how many times I had looked at it and filed it away as *detail* instead of *wound*.

"You're still a liar," she said finally. "You'll lie again. It's in your bones, like the swordwork is in mine."

"Yes."

"But you came up a mountain in winter to hand a hunted woman the one thing everyone wants, with no price on it." She picked up a stick and fed it to the fire, and her voice, when it came again, was quieter. "That's not in your bones. That's something new. I don't trust new things."

"No," I agreed. "Neither do I."

She lay down with her back to me, sword within reach, and said, "Sleep, ledger-keeper. We move at dawn. And Oscar—" a pause, the first time she'd used my name since the ferry, and it landed on me like a warm coal, "—if you make another copy, I won't leave you bleeding next time. I'll leave you *precise*."

I slept anyway. It was the best sleep I'd had in weeks. Fear, I find, makes a surprisingly good blanket, provided you're tired enough.

* * *

*Astrid*

I did not forgive him. I want that written somewhere permanent, in case future-me gets soft: *he lied, and you did not forgive him.*

But here is the trouble with not forgiving a man while traveling with him through the Greymarch in deep winter: the mountains don't care about your grievances. The mountains require cooperation. When the cornice above the Sorrow Col groaned, someone had to grab someone's collar, and when the whiteout came down on the third day like a lid closing, two people had to rope together or become two search parties looking for each other's corpses. Trust, it turns out, isn't a feeling. It's a technique. You practice it the way you practice sword forms, badly at first, mechanically, until one day your body does it without asking your permission.

By the fifth day after the reunion, my body had started trusting Oscar Ishikawa without consulting me, and I resented it enormously.

He was better in the mountains than I expected. Ledger-keepers, I had assumed, were indoor creatures, but he'd grown up somewhere cold — he never said where, another gap in his story I chose not to audit — and he read weather the way he read accounts, spotting the lie in a clear sky, the debt coming due in a wind shift. He cooked. Badly, but he cooked. He talked constantly for the first two days, narrating our route, our supplies, our odds, a running commentary of figures designed, I realized, to fill the silence where my anger lived. Then he ran out of numbers, or courage, and went quiet, and the silence between us stopped being a wall and started being a room we both happened to occupy.

We didn't talk about the vault. We didn't talk about the ferry. On the sixth night, huddled in a hollow while the wind tried to peel the sky off, he asked instead:

"What was her name? The woman whose summer you carry?"

I looked at him. "How do you know about that?"

"You punched me in Hollowmere and then bought bread with money you didn't have the day before. Someone paid you. Guild rates for a harvest are fixed — I set half those rates myself." He shrugged, sheepish. "Bookkeeping. It gets everywhere."

"Maren," I said. "Maren Ostwick. Dying of the lung-rot. Her daughters couldn't bear to let the summer go with her, so they sold it to me to carry." I fed the fire a knot of pine. "It's a good summer. Fair in the village green, her granddaughters' feet in her lap, honey wine. I walk through it some nights when the mountains get too quiet. It's the only memory I own that I know is real."

Oscar was quiet a moment. "When this is over," he said, "I'll take you to them. The daughters. So you can give it back properly. It shouldn't live in a hired sword's head. It should go home."

"That's not how it works. Once it's carried, it's carried."

"I know." He stared into the flames. "I've returned stolen things before. There's always a moment where the owner takes them back and looks at you like you're a stranger who's been wearing their coat. But it's still right. Things should go home, even if home has changed."

I didn't say anything. But I thought about it later, lying awake, Maren's fair spinning softly behind my eyelids, and I thought: this man, who has spent his whole life moving other people's lives around like furniture, wants, more than anything, to put one thing back where it belongs. Maybe that's not redemption. Maybe it's just a clerk's idea of grace. But I had known knights — true-sworn, chapel-vowed, gold-and-scarlet knights — who never once wanted anything back where it belonged, least of all themselves.

On the eighth day we came down out of the high passes and saw the ruined capital for the first time.

It sat in its valley like a jawbone in the sand — the Old City, the one the histories pretend was abandoned for plague, its towers snapped, its walls shrugged off like a coat. Even from the ridge, even at three miles, I felt it. Not recognition exactly. Something older than recognition. A key turning in a lock I hadn't known was in my chest.

My scar burned. Not hurt — *burned*, like a brand remembering the iron.

Oscar saw me stop. He came up beside me, breath ragged, and followed my gaze down into the valley, and I watched him understand what he was looking at: not a ruin, but a door, and standing in front of it, the hinge.

"The map says beneath," he said quietly. "Beneath the palace district. There are stairs."

"There are stairs," I said, and then heard myself, and went cold. "I didn't know I knew that."

He didn't gloat. He didn't say *your memories are coming back, the vault is calling you, this proves my copy was worth the betrayal.* He just stood in the wind beside me, a liar trying very hard to be a wall to lean on, and said, "Do you want to rest first? We've got light for two more hours, but the vault's waited thirty years. It can wait one night."

Thirty years. The number moved through me like a fish through dark water. Thirty years since I sealed it — no, not sealed it, *chose* it, chose the erasure, chose to forget my own name's weight — and in all that time the door had held, and now it was opening from my side, memory by memory, fragment by fragment, like water finding cracks.

"No," I said. "We go now. While I still remember wanting to."

We descended as the light failed, down switchbacks glazed with ice, into the valley where the Old City waited with its broken teeth. Behind us, far back along the ridge we'd crossed that morning, I caught it once and made sure not to stare: a line of torches, moving with the unhurried discipline of people who owned the mountain.

Guild colors. Corvane's hand, closing.

"They're a day behind," Oscar murmured, not looking. "Maybe less. He'll have bought the passes' best trackers."

"Then we stop being careful," I said, and drew my sword, and led us down into the dark toward the stairs I already remembered, toward the door that only opened for my blood and my consent, toward the war beneath the world — with the guild a day behind us, and the Crown, I had no doubt, already waiting below.
