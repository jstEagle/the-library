## 16. Descent to the Vault

The ruined capital had a name once. I knew that the way you know a word is on the tip of your tongue — not the word itself, just the shape of its absence. The maps called it Vhalen's Rest now, which was a lie of a particularly bureaucratic kind, because nothing there was resting. Everything was waiting.

We came down into it at dusk, through streets where the stones were cut too large and fitted too well for any mason alive today. My boots remembered the way before I did. That sounds like poetry until you live with it — until your own feet start making decisions your head hasn't approved, and you have to decide whether to trust them or look like a madwoman walking into walls.

"Left here," I said.

Oscar looked at the street. It was a wall. A very convincing wall, actually, collapsed in a way that looked like it had been collapsed for three hundred years and would be collapsed for three hundred more.

"That's a wall," he said.

"It's a door."

"It's a wall, Astrid."

I put my palm against the stone, and something under my skin — some old muscle memory that wasn't muscle — turned over like a sleeper, and the wall sighed open along a seam no eye could find. Dust breathed out at us, warm as exhalation.

Oscar stared. "How long have you known you could do that?"

"Since the chapel." Since the drowned chapel, since the altar, since the flood of it — the war's last witness, sealed inside me like a letter in a lead box. I still hadn't told him everything. I'd told him enough. "Since the chapel," I said again, because repeating it made it smaller.

He didn't push. That was one thing about Oscar Ishikawa: for a professional liar, he'd gotten remarkably good at knowing when not to ask questions. He adjusted the strap of his satchel — the one with the copied map, the one I still hadn't forgiven him for, the one I'd taken from him in the snow and never given back — and followed me into the dark.

* * *

Underneath, the capital went down farther than cities should. We walked stairs cut for people taller than us, through corridors where the air tasted of cold iron and old candle smoke, as if someone had blown out the lights yesterday and simply never come back.

My memories came back in pieces, and none of them arrived politely. They came like weather. One moment I'd be counting steps, the next I'd be standing in this same corridor with soot on my face and a dead man's blood drying stiff on my gauntlets, and I'd have to stop and breathe until the present reasserted itself. Oscar learned to read the signs. He'd stop too, and wait, and say nothing, which was somehow worse and better than asking.

"You're doing the thing," he said, somewhere around the fourth hour underground.

"What thing."

"The thing where you go somewhere else and come back and pretend you didn't."

I kept walking. "There are things down here I haven't told you."

"I assumed. You have the facial expression of a woman carrying a secret the size of a cathedral."

That startled a laugh out of me, short and ugly. "You would know. You've carried a few yourself."

"Mine were all filing-sized," he said. "Yours have architecture."

We passed a chamber where the ceiling had fallen and in the rubble lay helmets — hundreds of them, fused together by heat into a single black mass, like a shoal of fish turned to slag. I didn't remember this place. But I understood it instantly, the way you understand a grave marker without reading the name. Both sides had fought here. Both sides had burned here. The helmets couldn't tell you whose they'd been, and that, I was beginning to grasp, had been rather the point.

"The vault remembers," I said quietly. "That's what the map meant. *Where kings are unremembered.* Not destroyed. Filed away. Everything that happened up there—" I gestured vaguely at the world above, the world that had forgotten an entire war, "—it's all down here. Every death. Every name. Kept safe from everyone, including the people it happened to."

Oscar was silent for a long moment. Then, in the voice of a man who has spent his life among ledgers and cannot help himself: "Someone had to keep the accounts."

"Yes," I said. "And look what keeping the accounts did to them."

* * *

We heard the pursuit before we saw it — or rather, we felt it, a wrongness in the deep quiet, like a held breath. Torches, far above and behind. Voices pitched low, disciplined. Two groups, moving separately, converging on the same throat of stair we'd used.

Guild enforcers moved like men who'd done this a hundred times. Inquisitors moved like men who believed God was watching. I'd learned to tell them apart in Ashgate, and the sound drifting down to us had both textures in it.

"They found the entrance," Oscar whispered.

"They always find the entrance. Entrances want finding. That's their whole personality." I pulled him off the main stair into a side passage my feet insisted on, though my eyes saw only darkness. "Hale will have the Crown's charts. Corvane will have bribed someone for Hale's charts. Everyone's working from the same paperwork except us."

"We have the original."

"We have a copy of a copy of a dying man's last thought. We're the least informed people in this city, Oscar. What we have is *me*, and I'm only half unpacked."

He glanced back toward the sound of pursuit, then at me, and something in his face was steadier than I expected. "Half of you got us past a wall that isn't a wall. I'll take my chances with the other half."

It was a stupid, flattering, clerk-shaped thing to say, and I hated how much it helped.

We went deeper.

* * *

The memories got louder as we descended, which told me something I didn't want to know: the vault wasn't just ahead of us. It was *around* us. The whole undercity was soaked in it, the preserved truth of the war bleeding upward through the stone like damp through plaster. By the time we reached the long gallery — a hall lined with empty alcoves, hundreds of them, each one exactly the size of a kneeling person — I was walking through a crowd of ghosts who didn't know they were ghosts.

I stopped in front of one particular alcove and my knees nearly went.

Because I remembered kneeling there.

Not imagined. Remembered. The stone cold through my trousers, my hands flat on my thighs, a voice — whose? a woman's, kind and terrible — saying, *Are you certain? Once it is done, even you will not know why you did it.*

And me answering. Me saying yes. Me meaning it.

"Astrid?" Oscar's hand hovered near my shoulder, not touching. He'd learned that too.

"There's an alcove with my shape worn into it," I said. My voice came out flat and strange. "I've knelt here before. This is where they took it out of me. Where I *asked* them to take it out of me." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Do you understand? Nobody stole my past, Oscar. I donated it. I'm not a victim of the vault. I'm a *contributor*."

He was quiet. Then, carefully: "Whatever you said yes to — you said it knowing everything. That's more than most people ever get to do."

"And now I'm walking back into it half-remembering, chased by a guildmaster and an inquisitor, and the only person I trust is a man who copied my map behind my back."

"Technically it was the guild's map—"

"*Oscar.*"

"Right. Yes. Terrible man. Continuing."

But he was smiling a little, and so, damn him, was I, and we kept walking, and the gallery of kneeling-shapes fell away behind us like a congregation dismissed.

* * *

The last door was not hidden. That was the first thing that unnerved me. After miles of concealed passages and walls-that-were-doors, the vault announced itself with a doorway twenty feet high, framed in black stone carved with figures whose faces had been deliberately smoothed blank — not eroded, *smoothed*, by hands, with care. Kings, I realized. The Unremembered Kings themselves, rendered faceless by their own decree. Above the lintel, in a script older than any nation still using letters, ran a line I could suddenly read, which frightened me more than anything else that day:

**ONLY WHAT IS GIVEN MAY BE KEPT. ONLY WHAT IS KEPT MAY BE GIVEN BACK.**

Behind us, faint but growing, torchlight bled down the corridor. Both pursuers. Closing.

"This is it," Oscar said. He sounded like a man reading his own obituary to check it for typos. "Blood and consent. That's what the stranger's note said. Your blood, your consent."

"My blood, my consent, and presumably my complete and enthusiastic understanding of the consequences," I said. "Consent given blind doesn't count. I remember that much. The altar at the chapel wouldn't take me until I understood what I was agreeing to." I drew my knife. Old habit — Vane steel, the one possession I'd carried through disgrace and bounty-hunting and every bad inn between. "So let's find out what I'm agreeing to."

I opened my palm along the edge, quick, the way you pull off a bandage, and pressed my bleeding hand to the center of the great door.

Nothing happened.

I stood there, hand on stone, blood running down carvings that had waited centuries, and nothing happened, and behind us the torchlight grew brighter, and I felt the first cold thread of real fear.

"Why won't it open?"

"Maybe it wants more blood," Oscar offered.

"Maybe it wants honesty," I said, and then heard myself say it, and stopped.

Because that was it. Of course that was it. Consent. True consent. And I was standing at the door of the thing that held my entire stolen life, about to ask it to give that life back to a world that had traded it for peace — and underneath all my fine talk of duty and witnesses and finishing what I started, there was a part of me that wanted my memories back for *myself*. That wanted to be Astrid Vane whole, with her history and her griefs and her name for every scar, including the wolf-shaped one I'd never earned that I could recall. I wanted it selfishly, hungrily, the way a starving woman wants bread, and the vault could taste that want through my hand like a dog smells fear.

"It knows I'm lying," I whispered. "Not to it. To myself."

The torchlight reached the mouth of the corridor. Shouts — two sets of voices arguing with each other even as they converged on us, guild and Crown united only in wanting what I carried. Oscar stepped in front of me, absurdly, a thin clerk in a good coat squaring up against armed men, and said, "Astrid. Whatever you're deciding — decide fast."

I closed my eyes.

And I gave it up. All of it. Not just the plan, not just the noble version — the selfish want too, the hunger, the little girl's fist clenched around her own past. I said to the door, silently, in whatever language doors hear: *Take it. Take all of it, if it comes to that. Take my memories, my vengeance, my name. If the price of peace is that Astrid Vane stays a stranger to herself forever, then I pay it. Again. Willingly. Witnessed by one liar with a ledger and however many ghosts are listening.*

I meant it. That was the terrible, freeing thing — I meant every word.

The door drank my blood off the stone like rain into summer ground.

And opened.

* * *

What came out of the opening was not light, exactly. It was *remembering* — the raw substance of it, pouring over us like warm water, and I heard Oscar make a sound I'd never heard from him, small and undone, because a man who traffics in bottled memories for a living was suddenly standing in the river they're all bottled from.

Beyond the threshold: stairs going down into a dark full of waiting, and far below, something vast turning over in its sleep — the war itself, archived, alive, patient. Every battle. Every name. Every ordinary person on both sides who had only wanted to go home.

And behind us, boots on stone, closing fast — Corvane's enforcers with their guild-sigils, and behind them the measured tread of Inquisitor Hale, who sincerely believed that forgetting had saved millions and would kill to keep believing it.

Oscar looked at me. I looked at Oscar.

"Well," he said. "No turning back now."

"No," I agreed, and stepped through the door of everything anyone had ever chosen to forget.
