## 17. The War Beneath the World

*Oscar*

I have kept accounts for the Guild of Impossible Things for eleven years, and in that time I have learned that every number is a person pretending to be a number. A bottled storm is a fisherman's drowned village. A stolen year is a daughter's wedding nobody attended. I knew this, the way you know the meat on your plate was once a thing with eyes. You know it. You eat anyway.

Descending into the Vault of Unremembered Kings, I finally understood the difference between knowing and understanding, and the difference was roughly the depth of the world.

The stairs went down longer than the world should allow. Astrid's hand found mine somewhere around the two-thousandth step — not romance, just physics; the dark was total and she had the only lantern — but she didn't let go after the physics stopped mattering, and I didn't mention it, because mentioning it would have made it a thing with edges, and some things survive better unexamined.

"You're doing the thing," she said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you narrate your own terror in a dry voice so it doesn't sound like terror."

"That's not terror. That's professional assessment. We are, at current estimate, four hundred feet beneath a capital city that was supposedly built on solid rock, following a dead man's map into a vault that only opens for your blood, and I left my good pen upstairs. Terror would be an overreaction."

Her laugh echoed strangely, as though the dark were tasting it. "Your good pen."

"It has a silver nib. Corvane gave it to me the year I balanced the Vellum Accords ledger. I've been meaning to lose it for a decade."

"Then I hope a monster eats it."

"I hope the monster chokes."

The stairs ended at a door that was not a door. It was a slab of black stone with a basin carved into its center, and when Astrid pressed her palm to it — she didn't even cut herself this time; the vault had apparently gotten the point — the stone drank the light from our lantern and gave it back as memory.

That's the only way I can describe what happened next. The light came back wrong. It came back as *then*.

* * *

*Astrid*

I have spent six years living with holes in my head. You get used to it the way you get used to a missing tooth — your tongue keeps finding the gap, keeps worrying it, keeps being surprised. I knew I had been a knight. I knew I had been called something before they called me the Wolf. I knew there was a scar on my ribs shaped like a running wolf that no one could explain and that I had stopped asking about, because every person I asked flinched.

The vault gave me back the flinches first.

The door opened and we stepped through, and the world became a country I had burned.

Not literally. That's the thing I need you to understand, whoever finds this account, if accounts survive what we're about to do. I did not burn it. Nobody burned it. That's the horror. It burned itself, slowly, the way a house burns when the family inside can't stop feeding the fire.

The memory took us the way deep water takes you — no violence, just pressure, and then you're somewhere else and your lungs have forgotten they were ever for air.

I stood in a wheat field outside a city called Vael Corith, and I was not Astrid Vane. I was a farmer's son named Tam, seventeen, and I was holding a spear because the King's levy had come through and the alternative to holding a spear was a rope, and the wheat was taller than I was, and somewhere across the field were men from the Ashlands who were also seventeen and also holding spears for exactly the same reasons, and neither of us knew why we were there. That was the part that undid me. Not the battle. The *not knowing why*. Tam's whole understanding of the war was that the Ashlanders were devils who ate children, and he believed it the way you believe the sun rises, because everyone he loved had said it since before he could talk, and the Ashlander boy across the field — I saw through his eyes too, the memory was generous that way, generous like a knife is generous — believed exactly the same thing about us.

We killed each other in the wheat. I felt both spears go in. I felt Tam's mother receive the news, and I felt the Ashlander boy's mother receive hers, on the same afternoon, in the same rain, and both of them said the same sentence: *he was such a gentle boy.*

The vault moved on. It did not let me rest. It was an honest archivist, and honesty is the cruelest thing there is.

* * *

*Oscar*

The war lasted ninety-three years. I know because the vault told me, in the patient voice of a ledger, and I — who have spent my life converting suffering into columns of figures — felt for the first time what my columns had been hiding.

Ninety-three years. Long enough that the reasons were lost twice over: once when the generation that started it died, and once when the generation that inherited the lie burned the archives to keep the lie warm. By the end, kingdoms were fighting over grievances so old that the grievance was the only inheritance left. Grandmothers taught hatred to grandchildren the way they taught bread recipes, with the same patience, the same insistence that the old way was the true way.

I walked through it as a witness with no name, because the vault had no body to lend me, only its memory, and memory doesn't care whose eyes you borrow.

I saw a siege where the defenders ate the leather from their shields, and the attackers, who could have waited them out, broke the siege to send in bread — and then resumed the war the following spring, because mercy and duty, it turned out, could live in the same chest without ever speaking to each other.

I saw a truce at a river ford, one midsummer, where soldiers from both banks swam together and traded buttons and songs, and an officer on each side rode down to break it up, and both officers, I saw it, both of them wept while they did it.

I saw the peace negotiations at the end. That was the part the histories would have made grand, if histories had been allowed. It was not grand. It was eleven exhausted people in a room that smelled of tallow, and one of them — a woman with grey in her braid and a scar I recognized, because I had kissed the newer version of it on a cold mountainside — stood up and said the sentence that ended the war.

"We cannot forgive each other. We have proven that for ninety-three years. So we will do the one thing left. We will forget, all of us, together, and we will make the forgetting the treaty. Not a clause. The whole treaty."

They argued. Of course they argued. A general from the Ashlands said forgetting was theft from the dead. A bishop said it was blasphemy. And the woman with the braid — Astrid, though the name meant nothing yet, though it was just a word in a warm room — said:

"The dead are dead. The living are still here, and they are still teaching their children to kill. I would rather rob the dead of their grief than the living of their grandchildren."

They signed it in mutual forgetting. Every mind alive, touched at once. Every archive sealed. And the cost of the working — the vault needed an anchor, a living mind to hold the seal from inside, someone who would surrender their own memories as the keystone — that cost was volunteered.

She volunteered. The woman with the braid. I stood in the vault's memory and watched Astrid Vane, Knight-Captain of the Last Accord, walk into the dark on purpose, and the last thing her face did before the erasure took it was *relieve*. Like a woman setting down a load she had carried so long she'd forgotten her spine was straight.

Then the vault closed, and the world woke up lighter, and lighter, and lighter.

* * *

*Astrid*

I came back to myself on my knees on the vault floor, and Oscar was on his knees too, and neither of us had the dignity to be embarrassed about it.

"Tam," I said. My voice came out wrong. "The boy in the wheat. His name was Tam."

"You remember him?"

"I remember *everything*." And I did — that was the terrible joke of it, the vault's last gift. It had held the war for a hundred years, and when it opened for me, it poured the whole of it into the one vessel designed to receive it. The seal was breaking; I could feel it, a hairline crack of returning light. My old self's memories, the ones I'd surrendered as the keystone, coming home.

And with them, the choice. I saw it whole now. I had stood in that tallow-smelling room. I had said the words about robbing the dead. I had walked into the dark because someone had to be the lock, and I had chosen it, and I had been *glad*, at the end, to put it down.

"Say something," Oscar said. "You've gone somewhere I can't follow."

I looked at him. He was grey-faced, shaking, a clerk who had just been made to read the original documents after a lifetime of the summaries. And I understood — this was the part that frightened me — I understood that I could not hate the people in the memory. I had been given both sides. I had been the Ashlander boy and the King's levy and the mother in the rain and the officer weeping while he ended the truce. There was no monster anywhere in ninety-three years of war. There were only people, frightened people, feeding a fire they inherited, each certain their hatred was a heirloom and not an infection.

If I carried this out of here — if the world carried it — every grandmother would remember her grievance as gospel. Every border would become a wound again. Tam's mother would get her grief back, and with it, the certainty that someone owed her blood for it.

My old self had known. That was the unbearable part. She hadn't been a coward or a tyrant. She had been *right*, in the way that only someone who has seen both spears go in can be right, and being right had cost her everything, and she had paid it *gladly*.

"Astrid." Oscar's hand on my shoulder. "The vault's opening. I can hear —"

I could hear it too. Boots, far above, coming down the long stairs. More than one set. And beneath the boots, a sound I knew from the guildhall: the particular creak of a strongbox being carried by men who expected to be paid.

"Corvane," Oscar said.

* * *

*Oscar*

She stood up out of her grief the way I had once seen her stand up out of a river — all at once, no wasted motion, as though the water were something that had happened to other people. But her eyes were still in the wheat field. I could see Tam's spear in them.

"Astrid, whatever you're deciding —"

"You already know what I'm deciding." She turned to face me, and the lantern light caught the wolf scar, and for the first time since I'd known her it looked less like a wound than a signature. "You read the accounts. You've read them for eleven years. Tell me what the guild would do with ninety-three years of grievance."

"Sell it," I said. "By the pound. Selectively. Corvane would sell each kingdom only its own innocence and its neighbor's crimes, and he'd sell it twice, and he'd call it reconciliation."

"And the Crown?"

"Hale would bury it so deep it would fester in the dark, and in a hundred years some archivist would find the crack in the seal, and we'd be standing here again. Except next time there'd be no knight to volunteer as the lock."

"Then there's only one honest ledger left." She said it evenly, and I felt the floor of my stomach go, because I knew — I had known since the drowned chapel, since the altar, since the first fragment came back to her — I knew what the honest ledger was.

"You're going to finish it," I said. "The erasure. For good."

"With witnesses, this time. That was her mistake — my mistake — doing it in secret. A secret peace is just a debt with the interest hidden." Her jaw set. "It gets done in the open or not at all."

"And you? You'll lose all of it again. Tam. The room with the tallow. Your name. Everything you just got back."

Something crossed her face then — not hesitation, exactly. Grief making room for itself.

"I'll lose the war," she said. "I'd like to keep the rest, if the rest can be kept. But if it can't—" She looked at me, and it was the look from the tallow room, the look of a woman setting down a load. "Oscar. The boots are getting closer, and I need to know, before they get here, whether you're standing with me or standing behind me. There's a difference, and it matters, and I don't have time to be gentle about it."

Above us, the vault's great door began, very slowly, to open — and in the widening crack of light I saw not enforcers, not yet, but something worse: Corvane's grey gloves, and behind him, the glint of noble signets, and the sound of a man clearing his throat the way he did before an auction.

"Ledger-keeper," Corvane's voice came down the stairs like oil. "I do hope you've been keeping the accounts current. We have *buyers*."
