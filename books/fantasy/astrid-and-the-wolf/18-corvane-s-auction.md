## 18. Corvane's Auction

I have attended many auctions in my life. I once catalogued the sale of a bottled thunderstorm that went for the price of a fishing fleet. I watched a widow buy back her wedding day from a man who had stolen it out of her head while she slept, and I recorded the transaction in a neat column with a neat sum at the bottom, as though grief had an exchange rate.

None of it prepared me for Corvane's auction in the Vault of Unremembered Kings.

He came down into the dark like a man arriving late to his own funeral and delighted about it. Twelve enforcers in guild grey, lanterns hooded to throw narrow blades of light. And behind them, descending the spiral stair with the careful tread of people who had paid a great deal of money not to slip: the buyers.

I knew some of their names. Everyone who keeps ledgers knows the names that never appear in them. Lord Aldous Vey of the Marches, whose family crest was a wolf before someone decided wolves were bad for business. Lady Sarelle Imre, whose grandmother had signed something in a burned city and whose face had been smoothed by three generations of forgetting until it looked like a portrait left out in the rain. A cardinal's legate. A general with no army, which is the most dangerous kind.

They moved through the antechamber of the vault the way tourists move through a cathedral — hushed, covetous, already composing the things they would tell no one.

And Astrid stood between them and the open door.

She had not moved from it since we came down. Her hand rested on the stone — her blood had opened it, her consent had unsealed what she herself had sealed a lifetime ago — and she stood there with the particular stillness of a wolf on a ridge, the kind that makes you check where the rest of the pack is before you take another step.

There is no rest of the pack, I wanted to tell the buyers. There is only her. That should be worse for you.

Corvane stopped six paces away and looked at her the way he looked at everything: appraising. I had spent eleven years watching that appraisal across a counting table. It had never once been pointed at me with this much interest.

"Astrid Vane," he said warmly. "The last witness. Do you know how long I have looked for you? Longer than you've been alive, in a sense. You were an investment before you were a person."

"That's very honest of you," Astrid said. "Most men lead with flattery."

"Flattery is for people I need to want something." He spread his hands. "You want nothing. I can see it. So let us be honest together, knight. The vault is open. The war is in there — all of it, every name, every grievance, every reason two nations spent thirty years teaching each other's children to hate. I am offering these good people portions of it. Verified provenance. Clean extraction. The truth, sliced thin enough to swallow."

"And thick enough to choke on," Astrid said.

"Lady Imre wants to know what her grandmother signed," Corvane continued, serene as a ledger line. "Lord Vey wants the name of the man who burned his ancestral seat. The legate wants proof that the old faiths were right to be feared. Each of them will pay a fortune for one piece of the past. And none of them will get the whole, because the whole—" he smiled, and it was the smile of a man balancing books, "—the whole is worthless. Who buys a war entire? No one. But a grievance? A grievance is liquid. A grievance appreciates."

I stood behind a pillar with my heart going like a hammer on a nail, and I did the thing I always do when I am afraid, which is count.

Twelve enforcers. Two at the stair, four flanking the buyers, six moving wide through the antechamber in a pattern I recognized because I had entered its cost allocations myself. One buyer's clerk taking notes. Hale nowhere, which frightened me more than if he'd been anywhere.

Astrid, alone, with a sword she'd taken off a dead enforcer at the ferry and a memory returning to her in pieces like a ship coming up from deep water.

And me. With a satchel full of paper.

* * *

Here is what nobody understands about ledger-keepers, including, most days, ourselves: we are not neutral. We like to think we are. We sit at the edge of other people's crimes with our pens poised, and we tell ourselves that ink is not blood, that a column of figures is not a knife, that recording a thing is not the same as permitting it.

Eleven years I told myself that.

But here is the other thing about ledger-keepers: we read everything. Every entry passes under our eyes twice — once when it comes in, once when it's balanced. And the Guild of Impossible Things kept immaculate accounts. Corvane saw to that. He was proud of our records. He used to say a guild that couldn't prove what it owned didn't own it.

So I knew what was in my satchel. Not copies — originals, pulled from the archive during the weeks after the ferry, when I was Hale's prisoner and then Hale's guest and then, briefly, Hale's problem. I had walked out of that interrogation carrying the only thing they never search a clerk for: work.

Ledger 114, folio 88: *Acquisition, mass lot. Village of Hollowmere, 340 heads, mixed ages. Seller: Crown liaison. Price per head: negligible. Note — children under seven yield poorly; recommend earlier harvest next season.*

Ledger 121, folio 12: *Sale, private. To Lord Vey (grandfather): memory of the burning of Caer Duloc, edited. Retained: the flames. Removed: the order giving them. Buyer wished to remember himself as rescuer.*

Ledger 133, folio 201: *Disposal. Forty-one memories deemed unsellable — too true. Burned with witnesses present.*

And so on. Eleven years of it. I could recite half of it in my sleep, and often did, which is why I drank.

Corvane was still talking. He had produced a velvet tray now, and on the tray lay small glass vials glowing faintly, like fireflies in prison. Extracts. He must have had an extractor among the enforcers, working the vault's outer shelves while Astrid guarded the door — skimming the surface of the preserved war, bottling appetizers.

"First lot," Corvane said. "The name of the traitor who opened the eastern gates. Verified against three sources."

Lady Imre raised a finger.

"No," said Astrid.

It wasn't loud. But the vault carried it, rolled it around the stone, gave it weight. The buyers' fingers lowered.

Corvane sighed with genuine regret. "Knight, you cannot guard this door forever."

"I don't intend to," Astrid said. "I intend to end the market."

"With what army?"

And that — that was my cue. I'd like to say I rose to it nobly. In fact my legs went numb first, and I had to grip the pillar, and the first words came out thinner than I'd rehearsed in my head for three sleepless nights. But they came out.

"With mine," I said. "Such as it is."

* * *

Every eye turned. I stepped out from behind the pillar into the blade of lamplight, and I held up my satchel, and I said, in the voice I use for reading totals aloud at year's end — flat, clear, impossible to argue with:

"My name is Oscar Ishikawa. For eleven years I was the Guild of Impossible Things' keeper of accounts. Everything Master Corvane proposes to sell you tonight has a record. He is proud of those records. He should be. They are thorough. I am going to read you some of them."

"Enforcers," Corvane said mildly, "kill the clerk."

"Kill me," I said, "and the satchel goes to the Crown's inquisitors, the guild's rivals, and three newspapers I trust, in that order. I've made arrangements. Arranging things is the one skill my profession gives you." This was perhaps forty percent true. Hale had the other sixty percent, wherever he was. But fear is a currency too, and I have spent my life watching people spend it badly.

The enforcers hesitated. Enforcers are not soldiers; they are security for transactions, and transactions have rules, and a dead clerk with a dead-man's arrangement is a bad position. I watched the arithmetic move behind their eyes. I had done that arithmetic for them, in a sense. It was the kindest thing I'd ever put in a ledger.

"Read, then," Corvane said, and his voice had gone cold and smooth as river ice. "Amuse us. Then we'll see what your paper weighs against steel."

So I opened Ledger 114.

"*Village of Hollowmere,*" I read. "*Three hundred and forty souls harvested of their memories of their own children. The operation was justified internally as a quality-control measure following a plague scare. The children were relocated. The parents were compensated with the belief that they had never had any.*"

I looked up. Lady Imre had gone white.

"*Cost per soul: four silver. Total: sixty-eight crowns. Profit margin, resold in fragments to collectors: nine hundred percent.*"

"Hollowmere," I said, and my voice cracked on the second syllable, and I let it. "Some of you passed through it on your way down the mountain. Did you wonder why the innkeeper couldn't tell you his own daughter's name? Why the schoolhouse has forty desks and no pupils anyone remembers? You are bidding on the machinery that emptied it."

"The clerk editorializes," Corvane said to the buyers, wearily. "Note that."

"I do more than editorialize." I turned pages. My hands were steady now — isn't it strange, how the body finally settles exactly when it should be shaking? "*Ledger 121. Sold to the grandfather of Lord Aldous Vey, present company's honored ancestor: the memory of the burning of Caer Duloc, edited. We removed the order that gave the flames. We sold him a version in which he was the rescuer. His house has paid the guild annually for forty years to maintain the edit.*"

Lord Vey stood up. "That's a lie."

"It's folio twelve," I said. "Come and read it yourself. Bring your own spectacles; mine are poor."

"*Ledger 133,*" I went on, louder, over him. "*Forty-one memories destroyed as unsellable. Among them: the confession of General Marren, who ordered the drowning of the refugee barges at Sorrel Ford. Nine hundred people. The guild judged his confession too true to sell and too dangerous to keep. So we burned it, and General Marren died comfortable, remembered as a peacemaker.*"

"*Ledger 140. Acquisition from the Crown, price: one favor, nature unspecified. The complete order of battle for the final year of the war. The guild has never sold it. The guild has been waiting — quote — 'for the market to ripen.' End quote. Master Corvane wrote that note himself. I balanced the entry. I initialed it. My initials are beside his.*"

I stopped. The vault was very quiet. Even the enforcers had stopped pretending to scan the shadows.

"You want pieces," I said to the buyers. "A name here, a flame there. He'll sell them to you clean, and you'll go home richer in grievance, and in twenty years your grandchildren will pay the guild again to edit whatever you do with it. That is the business. That has always been the business. Wars end; the guild keeps the receipts. You are not buyers tonight. You are inventory that hasn't been processed yet."

"Enough." Corvane's voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about it — it never rose. "Clerk. Do you imagine any of these people care? They came here to purchase leverage. You're showing them the invoice. Leverage doesn't mind being dirty."

And that was the moment I understood I had miscalculated — or rather, that I had calculated correctly and hated the answer. Because I watched their faces, the buyers', and Corvane was right. They weren't horrified. They were *interested*. Lady Imre was looking at the vials again. Lord Vey had sat back down and was doing sums behind his eyes. Horror, in that room, was just another form of due diligence.

I had testified, and the testimony had become a catalogue, and the catalogue had become a menu.

Astrid caught my eye across the chamber. She didn't look despairing. She looked — I want to say proud, but it was fiercer than pride. She looked like a person watching a fuse burn and knowing exactly how long it had left.

"Clerk," she said clearly, "read the last entry."

"There is no last entry," Corvane said. "His ledgers end at the archive."

"This one doesn't," Astrid said. "Tell them what you wrote the night you decided to come down here instead of running. Tell them what you keep in the back page."

My mouth went dry. I hadn't shown her that page. She'd seen me writing in it by firelight for a week and had the courtesy — or the cruelty — of never asking.

I took out the loose folio at the back of Ledger 140. My own hand, my own account, the only entry in eleven years I had ever made without a column for price.

I read it.

"*Entry, personal. Witness: O. Ishikawa. Tonight I understood that I have not been keeping records of theft. I have been the theft. Every neat figure was a person made small enough to file. I hereby enter into the record the following debt, which cannot be balanced, only acknowledged: to the three hundred and forty of Hollowmere. To the nine hundred of Sorrel Ford. To the forty-one burned. To Astrid Vane, whose life I helped make an entry in someone else's book. No price attached. Some things should never have had one.*"

Silence. Real silence this time, the kind that has weight.

Then Corvane laughed. Softly, almost kindly, which was worse than anything he might have shouted.

"Oh, Oscar. You sweet, useless man. Sentiment is not a weapon. Confession is not a court. There is no judge down here but appetite, and appetite is *hungry*." He lifted the velvet tray. "Buyers. Last call. The gates of the east, the fires of Caer Duloc, the traitor's name — who opens?"

Lady Imre's hand rose.

And the vault door, behind Astrid, began to glow.

* * *

I have thought since about what came through that light, and I still don't have the word. Not an army. Not a ghost. Something older and simpler: the sound of the vault remembering it was full.

Hale stepped out of it.

The chief inquisitor of the Crown, coat scorched at the hem, face gray as ash, walking out of the Vault of Unremembered Kings as though he'd been in there for years instead of hours. Behind him the light poured up the stairwell like water finding its level, and every vial on Corvane's tray flickered — the extracts shivering in their glass, homesick.

"Inquisitor," Corvane said, recovering beautifully. "You're late. And trespassing."

"I've been reading," Hale said. His voice was steady, but I knew that steadiness. I'd heard it in my own voice the night I wrote the back page. It's what steadiness sounds like when it's holding something down. "All night. Your guild's accounts are in there too, Corvane. The originals. Before your edits." He looked at the buyers, one by one, and there was no hatred in it, which made it unbearable. "I came here to bury this place. I believed forgetting saved millions. I still believe it saved millions." He drew a long breath. "But I no longer believe it was ours to take. And I find I cannot sell what I no longer believe I own."

"You're mad," said Lord Vey.

"Quite possibly," Hale agreed. "It runs in the profession." He turned to Astrid, standing at the door with her hand on the stone, and inclined his head — a knight's salute, from an inquisitor, in front of God and everyone. "Wolf. Whenever you're ready."

Astrid looked at me. Just for a heartbeat. And in that heartbeat I saw her decide — saw the choice arrive in her the way entries arrive in a ledger, inevitable, already written somewhere, merely waiting for the fair hand to copy it down.

She turned to face the door.

"Master Corvane," she said, "your auction is closed."

The light behind her rose like a tide, and the war beneath the world opened its eyes.
