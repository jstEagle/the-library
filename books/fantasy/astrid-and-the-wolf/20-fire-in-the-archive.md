## 20. Fire in the Archive

**OSCAR**

I have read a great many accounts of battles in my time — ledgers of wars bought and sold, inventories of sieges, a whole shelf in the guildhall devoted to *Conflicts, Wholesale*. None of them mentioned that a battle is mostly waiting. Standing in a vault older than any kingdom, holding a torch I did not want to light yet, listening to Corvane's buyers argue about shipping costs.

That was the part no chronicler ever thought worth recording: the haggling.

"Sixty percent," Corvane was saying, his voice carrying that particular warmth he reserved for transactions. "The griefs go to House Marrow, the grievances to Vell. The victories we split evenly — everyone wants a victory."

A fat man in ermine — Vell, presumably — sniffed. "And who certifies authenticity?"

"The Guild of Impossible Things has certified impossible things for four hundred years, my lord." Corvane smiled. "We have never once sold you something false. Merely things better left forgotten by others."

I stood at the edge of the firelight with Astrid's hand on my arm, keeping me still, and I looked at the shelves.

The Vault of Unremembered Kings was not what the map had promised. No throne of bones, no crown on a cushion of dust. It was an archive. Rows upon rows of crystal vessels, each one glowing faintly with the light memories give off when they are kept too long in the dark — a pale gold, like candlelight through honey. Ten thousand vessels. A hundred thousand. Each one a piece of a war that eleven million people had agreed, all at once, to forget.

The peace treaty of the Unremembered War had been signed not in ink but in absence. Every mind alive, wiped clean of the conflict in a single night — and the cost of the wiping gathered up, bottled, and sealed here so it could never be accidentally remembered. Astrid had carried the key in her skull for twenty years without knowing it. She had paid for the world's sleep with her own past.

And now Corvane wanted to sell it back in installments.

"You're frowning, Ishikawa," Corvane said pleasantly, without turning around. He always knew where I was. Fourteen years of balancing his books will do that. "You disapprove? You, of all people? You kept the ledgers. You know exactly what every memory in this room fetched at market."

"I do," I said. "That's rather the problem."

He turned then, and I saw he had brought enforcers — six of them, big as doors, standing along the walls with the patient blankness of men whose inconvenient feelings had been sold years ago. Probably to us. Probably I had entered the sale into a ledger myself, under *Miscellaneous, Human*.

"My lords," Corvane said to his buyers, "if you'll excuse me a moment. An employee requires correction."

"Corvane." I stepped forward, because Astrid's grip had loosened, which meant she was about to do something violent, and I preferred to be the one doing the talking when violence was still optional. "Before you correct anyone, you should know the vault doesn't open for coin. It opened for her blood and her consent, and she's given neither to you."

"Her blood is already spilled on the threshold," Corvane said mildly. "As for consent—" he gestured, almost gently, at Astrid, "—she consented once before. Twenty years ago. She sealed this place herself. All I require is that she stand aside while professionals do the work. Isn't that right, Knight-Captain Vane?"

I felt Astrid go rigid beside me. So did he. He smiled wider.

"Oh yes. I remember you. I helped design the seal, after all. I sat across the table from the woman who volunteered her own history as the lock." His voice softened into something almost tender, which from Corvane was more frightening than shouting. "You were very brave. It would have been a shame if anyone had let you remember it."

"That's enough," Astrid said.

"It is indeed." And Corvane snapped his fingers, and the enforcers came off the walls like furniture learning to walk.

* * *

**ASTRID**

Six of them. I'd fought worse odds, but never sober, and never with something worth losing standing behind me.

The first one came fast — they always send the fast one first, to test your reach. I gave him my elbow instead of my sword and felt his nose fold like wet paper. The second grabbed my sword arm; I broke his grip by breaking his fingers, one, two, three, like counting. Old habits. Somewhere under twenty years of fog there was a woman who had done this for a living, and she was waking up angry.

"Oscar!" I shouted. "The shelves!"

Because I'd understood it the moment Corvane said *she sealed this place herself*: these weren't trophies. They were the war. Every grief, every massacre, every mother's scream from both sides of a border nobody could name anymore — all of it stored here, intact, waiting. If the enforcers shattered the vessels, the memories wouldn't die. They'd *escape*. Into whoever stood nearest. A room full of broken war.

Oscar understood too. I saw him bolt for the far aisle, torch in hand, and I put my back to a pillar and got to work.

The third enforcer was smart. He didn't rush me; he circled, looking for the opening. I let him think he'd found it, then took his legs out and drove him down onto the stone hard enough to ring his skull like a bell. The fourth and fifth came together, which was rude of them, and for a moment it was all I could do to keep their blades from finding me — parry, turn, give ground, give ground, my heels nearly at the edge of the dais where the oldest vessels sat in their cases of black glass.

And Corvane just watched. That was the worst of it. He watched with his hands folded, the way a man watches a horse being broken, and when I caught his eye over the shoulder of the fourth enforcer he said, almost kindly:

"You can't win, Astrid. But you can be useful. Give me your consent freely and I'll see you compensated. There are markets for knightly valor. Nostalgia sells beautifully."

"I'm not for sale."

"Everyone is for sale. I've seen the receipts."

Something in me went very quiet and very cold, the way it used to before battles I couldn't remember fighting. I stopped giving ground.

I took the fourth enforcer's sword away from him — not the man, just the sword, a clean pull-and-twist that left him staring at his empty hands — and I threw it down the aisle where it rang off the flagstones, and then I did the same to the fifth, and then I walked through both of them the way weather walks through a field, and I came up the steps of the dais and put my blade against Corvane's throat before his smile had finished dying.

Behind me, the enforcers lay groaning among the shelves. Alive. All of them alive. Some old ghost in my hands had insisted on it, and for once I was glad of the ghost.

"Consent," I said. "Here's mine: burn it."

* * *

**OSCAR**

There is a particular sound a memory makes when it burns.

I learned it that night, crouched in the aisle of the Vault of Unremembered Kings with a torch in one hand and a ledger in the other, feeding four hundred years of the Guild of Impossible Things into the flame. The vessels cracked and the memories inside them went up not as smoke but as light — brief, gold, unbearable. For half a heartbeat each one showed me what it held. A farmhouse burning. A field of wounded men singing to keep their courage up, badly. Two soldiers on opposite sides of a wall sharing bread because neither could remember why they were supposed to hate each other, only that hunger was simpler.

They rose past me like sparks going home.

I had expected to feel triumph. What I felt instead was the ledger-keeper's oldest terror: that the numbers would stop adding up. Because here was the arithmetic I could not balance — every vessel I burned was a truth destroyed, and I had spent my whole life believing truths were sacred, even the ones I sold. But I had also spent that life watching what happened when truths were kept in the hands of men like Corvane. Truth in his vault was not sacred. It was inventory.

Some things, I decided, burning page after page of *Sales, Atrocity, Assorted*, are only holy once you've set them free or set them alight, and it hardly matters which.

"Ashikawa." Corvane's voice, from the dais, strained against Astrid's blade. "Do you understand what you're doing? That archive is worth more than the Crown. More than the Church. You are burning a fortune."

"Yes," I said.

"You were *my clerk*. I taught you double-entry."

"You taught me that every entry needs two sides." I held up a page so he could watch it catch. "Here's yours."

He made a sound I had never heard him make in fourteen years — small, involuntary, human — and I realized with a strange, distant pity that he was watching not a fortune burn but himself. Every vessel, every page, every careful record of everything the guild had ever taken from the world: it was all he was. Erase the books and there was no Corvane underneath. Just a man who had once, long ago, helped sign away a war and had spent the centuries since selling the receipt.

Then the doors banged open, and Inquisitor Hale walked in with thirty Crown soldiers, and I thought: ah. There's the second side of the entry.

* * *

**ASTRID**

Hale. Of course it was Hale. The Crown's chief inquisitor, grey coat, grey eyes, the most patient man I had ever wanted to kill. He had hunted us across three provinces. He had offered Oscar a comfortable lie and Oscar had — somehow, stupidly, wonderfully — refused it. Now here he was at the end of everything, soldiers fanning out behind him, and I tightened my grip on Corvane and braced for the order.

"Seal the exits," Hale said. "No one leaves."

The soldiers moved. Corvane laughed — actually laughed, blood-thin with relief. "Inquisitor. Excellent timing. These two are destroying Crown property—"

"They're destroying *your* property," Hale said. "Which the Crown has, until tonight, permitted you to hold. I am revising the permit."

I stared at him. So did Oscar, halfway down the aisle with ash in his hair and a look on his face like a man doing sums that refuse to come out.

"Hale," Oscar said carefully. "You came to stop us."

"No." Hale walked forward, unhurried, his boots clicking on stone, and stopped before the nearest shelf of vessels. He looked at them the way a man looks at a grave he dug himself. "Three weeks ago, your friend stole a memory into my head. Against my will. A violation of law, of dignity, of everything I have spent my life defending." He turned. "It was the last summer of a woman named Marta Ilves. She died at Ashgate. She died in the war none of us can name, and I have spent every night since inside her last summer, and I will spend every night for the rest of my life there, and I am *grateful*."

The vault was very quiet. Even the fire seemed to listen.

"I believed forgetting saved millions," Hale went on, and his voice did not waver, which somehow made it worse. "I still believe it. Read the archive before you burn it — I have. The alternative to forgetting was extinction; both nations dead to the last child. Forgetting was mercy. But mercy administered in secret becomes theft. Mercy warehoused and *sold*—" he glanced at Corvane with an expression of such cold contempt that the guildmaster actually flinched under my blade, "—becomes this. I came to bury the truth forever. I find the work has already begun." He drew his sword and laid it flat across his palms, offering it hilt-first to no one and everyone. "I am told the proper procedure requires witnesses. Consider the Crown witnessed."

For a moment nobody moved. Then Oscar — because it would be Oscar, because it was always Oscar who found the third door in a two-door room — said, hoarsely:

"Inquisitor. Would you care to help with the ledgers?"

* * *

**OSCAR**

So it became this: the Guild of Impossible Things and the Crown's Inquisition, sworn enemies for four hundred years, working side by side in the dark beneath the ruined capital, feeding an archive to a fire.

Hale's soldiers formed a chain, passing vessels down the shelves hand to hand. Some of them wept as the memories brushed their minds on the way past — you couldn't help catching fragments, a smell of woodsmoke, a child's name — and none of them stopped working. Hale worked hardest of all, sleeves rolled, grey face lit gold, feeding the flames with the solemnity of a priest who has finally found a god worth the incense.

Astrid held Corvane on the dais and made him watch. I thought that was crueler than killing him, and I thought she knew it, and I thought she had earned the right.

"This is madness," Corvane kept saying. "You're burning history."

"We're returning it," Astrid said. "To the only place it was ever safe. The dark."

It was near the end — the shelves nearly empty, the air thick with golden ash, my ledger reduced to a black-edged stub in my hands — that Corvane made his move. I should have expected it. A man with nothing left but himself will always bet himself.

He twisted out from under Astrid's blade with a skill I had never suspected in him — of course, of course, the architects of the erasure had been soldiers first — snatched a knife from his boot, and went not for Astrid, not for me, but for the last shelf. The oldest vessels. The founding stock. If he could shatter those, the raw war would pour out into every mind in the room, and grief does not need consent to spread.

Astrid hit him at full sprint.

I have seen knights fight in tournaments, all flourish and heraldry. This was not that. This was two people who had both helped build the vault, grappling on its steps like the last two survivors of something, and it lasted perhaps ten seconds. She caught his wrist. She turned the knife. She put him down on the stone with his arm bent to the exact edge of breaking and her knee on his chest and her face inches from his, and I heard her say, quietly enough that only he and I and maybe Hale heard:

"You don't get to break it. You lost that right when you priced it."

She could have snapped his arm. She could have driven the knife home and called it justice, and Hale's soldiers would have looked away, because that is what soldiers do. Instead she took the knife, closed his bleeding hand around nothing, and stood up.

"Bind him," she said. "Alive. Let him live in a world with nothing left to sell."

Corvane stared up at her, and for one unguarded moment he looked not like a guildmaster but like a very old man in a very large empty room. "You'll regret mercy," he whispered. "Everything regrets mercy, eventually."

"Maybe," Astrid said. "But I'll regret it as myself. That's more than you ever let anyone have."

* * *

**ASTRID**

By dawn the vault was a hearth.

We sat around it — Oscar, Hale, a handful of soldiers, me — warming our hands over the ashes of a war, and nobody spoke for a long time. The golden light was fading now, the memories finally finishing their long climb out of the world, and what remained was ordinary fire, orange and honest, eating the last of the guild's ledgers where Oscar fed them in page by page.

He saved one book for last. I recognized it — the false-bottomed ledger where he'd hidden the map, back in the beginning, back when we were strangers and liars. He weighed it in his hands for a moment.

"The account of everything we did," he said. "Every sale. Every harvest. Hollowmere. Ashgate. All of it."

"Burn it," Hale said. "The world doesn't need the evidence. It needs the absence."

"No." Oscar shook his head. "Half right. The world doesn't need the evidence. But *someone* should carry the account, or none of this meant anything. Someone has to remember that we chose this — that forgetting was a decision, made by people, paid for by a woman who gave up her whole life for it." He looked at me. "Someone has to keep the books honest."

"And that someone is you?" Hale asked.

"I'm a ledger-keeper," Oscar said. "It's the only prayer I know."

He tore out a single page — the final page, the summary, the totals — and folded it small, and put it inside his shirt, over his heart, which was exactly where I would have guessed he'd keep it. Then he dropped the rest of the book into the fire, and the Guild of Impossible Things ended not with a battle but with a smell, like a library after rain.

Hale stood, sheathed his sword, and looked down at the two of us for a long moment. "The Crown will declare this site collapsed. Seismic activity. There will be an inquiry that finds nothing, conducted by me, reported to no one." Almost — almost — he smiled. "I find I'm rather good at making things disappear. It seems I finally have a use for the talent."

"And Corvane?" I asked.

"Will stand trial for crimes the court cannot be told the nature of. He will be convicted of smuggling, tax evasion, and fraud. Small crimes. Humiliating ones." Hale's eyes were flint. "He built an empire on stolen memories. He will end his days remembered — if at all — as a common thief. I can imagine no punishment he would dread more."

He saluted then, actually saluted, fist to chest, the old style, the kind knights used before knighthood became a thing you could buy. To me. I didn't return it. I just nodded, and that seemed to be enough, and he turned and marched his soldiers out of the ashes and up toward a world that would never know how close it had come to being sold its own grief back in installments.

Which left the two of us, and the fire, and the silence.

Oscar sat down next to me, close enough that our shoulders touched, and pulled the folded page from his shirt, and looked at it without opening it.

"So," he said. "Totals."

"Totals," I agreed.

"Eleven million memories, returned to the dark. One war, unfought, forever. One guild, bankrupted. One knight-captain—" he glanced sideways at me, wry and tired and something warmer underneath, "—still missing about twenty years of herself. The vault's last key. That would be you."

"Is there a column for what happens next?"

"There's always a column for what happens next. It's just usually blank." He held the page over the fire, hesitated — and I saw in his face that he was thinking about my memories, the ones he carried, the ones he'd promised, the choice he'd been choking on since the drowned chapel. Giving them back would open the vault of me. Keeping them would keep me safe and small and his to protect.

I put my hand over his, and together, we let the page fall.

It caught. It curled. It was gone.

"Blank column," I said, as the last of it went up. "Let's go find out what belongs in it."

Above us, far above, through miles of stone and ruin, morning was coming to a capital that had forgotten why it was ruined — and somewhere in that waking city, in a cell beneath the inquisitor's hall, a man with nothing left to sell was already planning how to buy his way out.
