## 21. What We Keep

The fire took three days to die.

I know because I counted them the way I count everything — in ledgers, in columns, in the small tidy arithmetic of a man who spent twenty years believing that if you could write a thing down, you could control it. The vault burned for two of those days beneath the ruined capital, and the guild's archives burned for one more above it, and by the end there was nothing left to sell and nothing left to bury and nothing left but ash that smelled, strangely, like rain.

Astrid stood at the edge of it with her arms crossed and her face lit orange, and she did not smile, and neither did I. Some fires you celebrate. This one we attended. There is a difference, and we both felt it in our teeth.

Hale had gone by then — walked into the smoke without a word, carrying whatever he'd carried out of my stolen memory of the war, and I have wondered since whether he went back to the Crown to report failure or simply kept walking until the road ran out. I hope it was the second. I have no way of knowing. That is rather the point of everything we did.

But this chapter of my account — and yes, I am still keeping accounts; old habits are the last thing to burn — is not about the fire. It is about what came after. It is about the part nobody warns you about.

It is about giving things back.

* * *

You would think returning stolen property would be the easy part.

For twenty years I told myself that bookkeeping was not thieving. I balanced the Guild of Impossible Things' ledgers with clean hands and a clean conscience, or something shaped enough like one to pass inspection at arm's length. I recorded the trades. I catalogued the bottled storms and the stolen years and the soldier's memory of the massacre that Corvane bought over lunch while I watched and said nothing. I never took anything myself, I told myself. I only wrote down what other people took.

Then I took a dying woman's last summer at her family's request, and discovered that a memory, once it lives in your head, has weight. Not metaphorical weight. Actual weight, the way grief has weight, the way a name you can't place has weight at three in the morning. After that I stopped pretending the ledger was neutral. A ledger is just a theft with good handwriting.

So when the vault was ash and Corvane was marching off under guard to face whatever justice the world could improvise on short notice, I sat down in the ruins of the guildhall with every memory I had ever carried, and I made a list.

There were forty-one of them.

Forty-one lives, or pieces of lives, folded into mine like letters in a drawer. Some were legitimate — taken with consent, paid for fairly, held in trust. Most were not. Some I had taken as a thief before I ever became a clerk, back when I was young and hungry and the guild found me the way the guild finds everyone: by noticing what I could do and making it respectable. Some I had taken since, quietly, because the work required samples, because verification demanded I hold a thing to know it was real. Every one of them had an excuse attached. Excuses always do. That's how you fit forty-one people inside one man without bursting.

I sat in the guildhall with its scorched rafters and its empty shelves, and I opened my ledger one last time, and I began.

* * *

The mechanics of it are duller than you'd hope. Memory-work is not lightning and incantation. It is closer to surgery performed from the inside — you find the seam where the memory sits against your own mind, and you unpick it stitch by stitch, careful not to take your own thread with it. Done badly, you come out the other side not knowing which recollections are yours. Done well, you come out the other side lighter and slightly hollowed, like a house after the furniture goes.

The first returns were easy. A cooper's memory of his daughter's wedding, held in escrow against a debt he'd never settled — I sent word to his village and rode there myself, because some deliveries should not be trusted to couriers. He didn't remember me. He remembered the wedding, suddenly and completely, mid-sentence, standing in his own doorway with sawdust on his hands, and he sat down hard on the step and wept for an hour while his wife held him and asked him, gently, over and over, what was wrong. Nothing was wrong. Everything was back. That is a strange kind of wrongness, and I don't recommend explaining it to anyone.

The second was harder. A woman who had sold her childhood to pay her mother's debts — a fair trade, on paper, the kind the guild loved to point to when anyone accused it of monsters. But she had been eight years old when she signed, and fair is a word adults use about children's choices. I returned her winters. All of them. She was a grey, competent innkeeper with a reputation for never smiling, and I left her laughing at something her brother had done with a sled thirty years ago, and I got out of the common room before she could ask my name.

That was the pattern, week after week. I traveled the length of two provinces with a ledger full of debts that weren't debts, knocking on doors, standing in doorways, handing people back the missing rooms of their own houses. Some wept. Some raged — at me, at the guild, at the years they'd lived as strangers to themselves. One man hit me with a poker, which I deserved, and then fed me dinner, which I did not, and which was somehow worse.

And through all of it, Astrid rode beside me.

Not every mile. She was doing her own accounting — hunting down the enforcers who'd scattered after the fall of the guild, making sure Corvane's noble buyers understood that the merchandise no longer existed and that pursuing it would be unhealthy. She was very persuasive. She is a large part of why the word "unhealthy" did most of the work without any actual unhealthiness occurring.

But she was there at the cooper's doorway. She was there the night the man with the poker fed us stew and wouldn't meet my eye. And she noticed — because she notices everything, it is her single most annoying virtue — that each time I gave something back, I came out a little smaller.

"You're emptying yourself," she said, on the road south, watching me rub my temples for the third hour running. "Like pouring out a jug."

"It's not my wine," I said.

"Some of it's soaked into the wood by now." She said it without cruelty. That's the trick of Astrid Vane: she says true things the way weather happens to you. "What happens to the parts that are yours now? The cooper's daughter's laugh — you've heard it in your head for six years. Is that going too?"

"I'll manage."

"That's not an answer, Oscar."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

Because here is the thing I hadn't said aloud, even to myself, even in the ledger where I confess everything: I was afraid. Not of emptiness. Of the opposite. I was afraid of what I might find still in me when the drawer was finally bare. Forty-one memories, and somewhere among them, folded so deep I'd stopped feeling its edges, was the reason a dying stranger had pressed a map into my hands and whispered a name. Somewhere in me was the night it all started. And somewhere in me — I suspected, I dreaded, I half-hoped — was everything Astrid had ever entrusted to me, back when she was someone else entirely, back when the war was ending and a knight with a wolf-shaped scar had made a choice that cost her every yesterday she owned.

She had given her past to the vault. But vaults need keepers. And keepers, apparently, become clerks.

* * *

By the time we reached the coast, I was down to nine.

Nine memories, and each one heavier than the forty-one had been together, because these were the ones with names on them. A fisherman's last morning. A child's first snow. Small things, human things, the residue of a trade I'd spent two decades calling honest. I returned them one by one along the grey shore roads, and each departure left me lighter and stranger, until one evening in a tavern that smelled of tar and onions, Astrid looked across the table at me and said:

"There's one more. Isn't there."

It wasn't a question. Questions give you room to lie.

"The ones you entrusted to me," I said. "Yes. Those go back last. They're — they're the whole reason any of this worked. The vault needed your consent, and your consent needed your choice, and your choice needed—" I stopped. Wry narration has limits, even for me.

"Oscar." She set down her cup. Her eyes in the lamplight were the exact color they had been in every fragment of her I'd ever carried, which is a sentence that will make sense to almost no one, and I've decided I'm allowed one of those per chapter. "Give them back."

"They're yours. Of course they're yours. I was always going to—"

"Then why do you look like a man being marched to his own funeral?"

Because, I wanted to say, because once you have them back you will remember everything, including me, including what I was, including the ledger-keeper who copied maps behind your back and bargained with devils and needed a burning vault and a changed inquisitor to finally stand up straight. Because remembering is not always mercy. Because I have seen what comes back when the missing rooms of a house are refurnished, and sometimes the furniture includes things the current owner cannot bear.

But what I actually said was: "Tomorrow. Let me do it properly. There's a technique. If I rush it, you'll get the memories scrambled with mine, and you'll spend a year wondering which griefs are original."

She studied me for a long moment. She knew I was stalling. She let me stall anyway, which is either kindness or strategy, and with Astrid the two were never fully separate.

"One day," she said. "Then the ledger closes."

* * *

I did it at dawn, on the beach below the tavern, because she insisted on open sky and I insisted on privacy and the compromise satisfied neither of us.

The memories she had entrusted to me were not like the others. The others had been inventory — goods in transit, weighed and shelved. Hers had been a covenant. Twenty years ago — longer, now; the arithmetic of erased wars makes liars of calendars — a knight named Astrid Vane had stood before the Vault of Unremembered Kings and chosen to forget, and the forgetting required a witness, and the witness required a vessel, and the vessel had been a young thief with a gift for holding things, whom she had chosen not despite his flaws but because of them, because a flawed man keeps secrets better than a proud one.

I had been that vessel. I had carried her sacrifice inside me through two decades of ledgers and lies, and I had never known what it was, only that it hummed.

Unpicking it was the hardest work of my life. Harder than escaping Hale. Harder than reading the guild's atrocities aloud to Corvane's assembled buyers while enforcers reached for their blades. Every stitch fought me, not because the memory resisted leaving but because some traitorous part of me resisted letting it go — the part that had loved her before I knew I loved her, that had loved her in the abstract, as a duty, as a trust, long before a bleeding stranger collapsed into my counting room and made her a person with a name.

And then it was out. And she had it. And I stood on the cold sand with my hands shaking and watched Astrid Vane receive twenty lost years like a woman catching rain in cupped palms.

She didn't move for a long time. The tide came in around our boots. Gulls argued about something overhead, indifferent to the end of history.

When she finally spoke, her voice was steady, which frightened me more than screaming would have.

"I remember the vault," she said. "I remember choosing. I remember—" and here her voice did crack, just slightly, "—I remember you. Younger. Frightened. Swearing you'd keep it safe." She turned. "You were supposed to be temporary, Oscar Ishikawa. A lockbox. Twenty years was the outside estimate."

"I overdeliver."

Something that was almost a laugh moved through her face and didn't quite land. Then she stepped forward and took my hands, and hers were warm and calloused and trembling very slightly, and she said the thing I had been dreading and hoping for since the first stitch came loose:

"Why didn't you tell me? At Hollowmere. At the chapel. Any of the hundred times it would have cost you nothing and saved us both blood."

"Because telling you felt like spending you," I said. "Every piece of you I carried was leverage, whether I wanted it to be or not. The moment I mentioned it, I'd be trading on a trust instead of keeping it. I've watched people do that their whole careers. I couldn't be one more."

"And instead you copied the map behind my back."

"Yes."

"And nearly died on a riverbank."

"Yes."

"And it worked out."

"It appears to have."

She squeezed my hands once, hard, and let go, and looked out at the water, and I understood that forgiveness, for Astrid Vane, was not a door that opened but a tide that came in — slowly, over days, in increments you could measure only by looking away and looking back.

"You kept one thing," she said, after a while. "I can feel the shape of it. You returned everything else, but there's one memory in you that's yours, and it's about me, and you didn't offer it, and I'm not asking for it." She glanced at me sideways. "But I want you to know I know. And I want you to know it's safe. Whatever it is. Consider it — witnessed."

"Astrid—"

"Don't make it weird, Oscar."

"I would never."

She smiled. Actually smiled, small and crooked and real, and walked back up the beach toward breakfast, and I stayed a moment longer in the cold with the one thing I had kept.

* * *

Here is what it is. Since we are closing accounts, and a closed account should balance.

It is not her sacrifice. It is not the war. It is not anything the vault held or the guild wanted or the Crown buried. It is smaller than all of that, and worth more.

It is a night in a smuggler's inn called the Drowned Rat, early in our acquaintance, when she still intended to turn me in and I still intended to double-cross her. She had fallen asleep in a chair by the fire, sitting up, sword across her knees, because she was the sort of person who slept like a sentry even among allies. And the firelight caught the scar on her arm — the wolf-shaped one she couldn't explain — and she shifted in her sleep and murmured something, half a word, and frowned, chasing a dream she'd never be able to name.

And I remember thinking, with sudden terrible clarity: *she has been robbed.* Not by the guild, not by the Crown, not by the vault. Robbed the way a house is robbed — of small irreplaceable things, photographs, letters, the ordinary furniture of a self — and she didn't even know what was missing, only that the rooms echoed.

And I remember deciding — right there, in a chair I hadn't earned, in a life I hadn't examined — that I was going to help her find out who she was. Not for the map. Not for the guild. Not even, though I told myself it was, for the truth.

Just because the rooms shouldn't echo.

No one asked me to surrender that memory. No ledger required it. It was never inventory, never collateral, never leverage. It was mine — the first wholly honest thing I ever kept — and I am keeping it, and if that makes me a bad former memory-thief, then I have finally found a failing I'm proud of.

* * *

We wintered in a fishing town whose name I'll omit, partly for discretion and partly because Astrid threatened me about it. The tide of her forgiveness came in, as tides do. We learned each other again — not the old us, who belonged to a war and a vault and a version of events that no longer existed anywhere except in one clerk's private keeping, but new versions, built from scratch, out of shared silences and arguments about navigation and the discovery that she snores and I talk in my sleep and between us we make a complete nuisance.

In the spring, a letter found us. No seal. No signature. Just a line in a hand I recognized from a wax-sealed map that no longer exists:

*The vault was not the only one.*

I showed it to Astrid. She read it twice, and then she looked at me with the expression she gets when a fight is coming and she intends to enjoy it.

"Well," she said. "Pack light."

Some habits, it turns out, survive even forgetting.
