## 22. Astrid and the Wolf

The village was called Millbrook, which is a name so aggressively ordinary that I trusted it immediately. After everything — the drowned chapel, the vault beneath the ruined capital, the fire that ate an archive older than any kingdom standing — I had developed a deep suspicion of places with dramatic names. Hollowmere. Ashgate. The Vale of Sorrows, which turned out to be a valley that mostly grew turnips and complained about the weather.

Millbrook promised nothing. That was its charm.

I came down the north road in the last week of autumn, boots worn through at the heel, a sword on my back that I'd stopped drawing months ago and hadn't yet found the courage to sell. My hair had grown out past my shoulders. My hands still remembered how to hold a blade even if the rest of me had misplaced most of what came before, and there were mornings when I woke reaching for someone who wasn't there, with a word half-formed in my mouth that dissolved before I could catch it.

I'd learned to stop chasing those words. Mostly.

The school sat at the edge of the green, a long low building with a slate roof and a bell that some enterprising child had recently decorated with mud. Through its windows I could see rows of small heads bent over slates, and at the front of the room, a man with ink-stained fingers writing figures on a board.

He was thin. Thinner than he should have been, with the look of someone who had been eating whatever the village paid him in and calling it enough. His hair was going grey at the temples, which struck me as unfair, because I was fairly sure he wasn't old enough for that. He wrote a column of numbers, underlined it twice, and then caught a boy flicking a wet rag across the room with the unerring aim of a man who had once watched a great many things very closely for a living.

Oscar Ishikawa. Ledger-keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things. Keeper of accounts that no longer existed, because we had burned them, along with the vault, the map, and every record of a war that had ended by being forgotten.

He didn't know I was watching. Or — and this occurred to me with a small, complicated warmth — he did know, and he was pretending not to, because that was the kind of careful, invisible man he was. Had been. Was again, apparently, in a schoolhouse, teaching children their sums.

I stood in the cold a while longer than I needed to. Long enough to notice that he kept a second ledger open on his desk beside the teaching one, and that he checked it the way other men check a wound — often, gently, expecting it to hurt.

Then I pushed open the door, and the bell above it rang, and twenty children turned to stare at me like I was a story come to life, which, depending on whose version you believed, I suppose I was.

"Master Ishikawa," I said. "Your books are crooked."

He looked up. And here is the thing about forgetting someone: you forget them, but your body doesn't. His face did something complicated — surprise folding into recognition folding into something quieter underneath, like a river running under ice — and then he smiled, and it was the smile of a man greeting a stranger he was absolutely certain he knew.

"They're always crooked," he said. "That's why they pay me. Class, this is—" He paused. I watched him reach for a name and find the shelf empty where it should have been. "—a friend," he finished. "From the road."

"Vane," I said. "Astrid Vane." It felt important to give him that much. To let him put it in his book somewhere.

"Miss Vane," he repeated gravely, and the children chorused it back at me, and one small girl in the front row asked if I'd killed anyone, because apparently that's just what you ask a woman carrying a sword, and Oscar said, "Marta, we do not ask guests that before lunch," which told me he'd been here long enough to have rules, and rules meant roots, and roots meant he wasn't running anymore.

Neither, I realized, standing there dripping snowmelt on his clean floor, was I.

* * *

They gave me the loft above the tavern, because Millbrook was that kind of place — the kind where the innkeeper decided within an hour that I was staying whether I'd said so or not. I earned my keep hauling timber the first day, and fixing the miller's fence the second, and by the third day the children had decided I was theirs, and there is no force in this world, including the ones we burned, more binding than being claimed by children.

Oscar and I circled each other for a week. Polite. Careful. Two people sharing a table at the tavern, trading small talk like coins we weren't sure were still legal tender. He told me he'd been here since spring. I told him I'd been walking since summer. Neither of us mentioned the vault, or the fire, or the mountain pass where I had taken a copied map from his hands without forgiving him, or the way he had knelt in the ash afterward and returned, one by one, every memory he had ever stolen from anyone — except, he'd said, one. Except one he kept.

I didn't know which one. I had a suspicion. Suspicions were nearly all I had left, and I'd learned to live on them the way other people live on bread.

It was the second ledger that finally broke the silence. I saw it again a week after I arrived, when I brought him a delivery of paper from the stationer in the next town over — real paper, good stock, bought with coin I'd earned honestly, which felt like a strange new luxury. He thanked me, set it down, and then, thinking I'd gone, opened the ledger and began to write.

I read upside down. Old habit. One of the few things that survived the erasure intact, along with swordwork and a deep distrust of guildsmen.

He was recording debts. Small ones. *Widow Hale — two eggs, repaid in mending.* *The school — chalk, owed to Teller's shop, due at month's end.* Page after page of honest, boring, beautiful arithmetic. And at the back, in a hand tighter than the rest, a single line he'd written and crossed out and written again:

*Astrid Vane —*

Nothing after it. Just her name, and the dash, waiting.

"You could just ask me what you owe me," I said.

He didn't jump. I'll give him that. He closed the ledger slowly, the way you close a door on a room you've been crying in. "I don't know what I owe you," he said. "That's the trouble. I know there's a debt. I can feel it. Like a sum I can't make balance no matter how many times I carry the one."

"And you hate that."

"I hate that more than I can properly express." He looked up at me, and his eyes were tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "You were there. Weren't you. In the — wherever it was. Whatever we did." He rubbed his ink-stained fingers together. "I dream about fire. Not burning. Just light, and the smell of old paper, and someone standing next to me. I never see her face. But I wake up and I'm not afraid, which doesn't make sense, because you'd think a man dreaming of fire would be afraid."

"Maybe she was keeping the fire off you," I said.

"Maybe." He studied me. "Do you dream it too?"

"Every night," I said. "And I'm not afraid either."

We sat with that. Outside, the children were shouting something about a dog and a sled, and the light came through the schoolhouse window all gold and ordinary, and I thought: this is what we bought. This exact moment. We traded a truth for this, and everyone said we were mad, and maybe we were, but the truth had teeth, and this has none. This just sits here, warm and small, asking nothing.

"I keep almost remembering you," Oscar said quietly. "Isn't that strange? Not what happened. Just — you. The shape of you. When you walked in last week, my hands wanted to do something. Some gesture. Like a greeting I used to know." He demonstrated, helplessly, a small motion halfway between a bow and a reach. "Does that ever happen to you?"

"All the time," I said. "There's a word I say in my sleep. I asked the innkeeper's wife if I talk at night and she said only that one word, over and over, soft, like an apology." I hesitated. "It's 'stay.'"

His breath went out of him slowly, like air from a room whose door had finally opened.

"Well," he said, after a while, in the voice of a man doing sums he already knows the answer to. "Then perhaps the debt runs both directions."

"It usually does," I said. "In my experience. What little of it I have."

* * *

Winter came early that year, the way winters do when they want to make a point. Snow sealed the passes, which meant no travelers, which meant the village turned inward and got busy about each other, and I discovered that a disgraced knight with no past makes an excellent tutor in practical matters. I taught the older children how to hold a staff, how to fall, how to stand back up. Oscar taught them numbers. Between us we produced a generation of Millbrook youth who could take a hit and calculate exactly how much it cost them, which the innkeeper called "a fine education" and Oscar called "a lawsuit waiting to happen," a phrase I found so funny I laughed until I cried, though neither of us could have told you why.

Some nights we sat in the schoolhouse after hours, him grading slates, me oiling the practice staves, the stove ticking between us. We talked about everything except the thing we'd lost, and sometimes, carefully, obliquely, we talked about that too.

"Do you ever regret it?" he asked me once, near midwinter. "Choosing what we chose. If we chose. I assume we chose — I can't imagine either of us letting it be done *to* us."

"We chose," I said. I was certain of that the way you're certain of your own heartbeat. "I remember choosing. Not what, or why. Just — the weight of it. Standing in front of something enormous and saying yes anyway."

"Was it worth it?"

I thought about the village below us, smoke rising straight from forty chimneys into a windless sky. I thought about the fact that no one in Millbrook had ever heard of the war, and no one mourned it, and no one would ever march their sons off to avenge it, because there was nothing to avenge. A peace signed in mutual forgetting. We had completed the signature. Willingly. With witnesses.

"There are children down that hill," I said, "who will grow up never knowing there was anything to forgive. I used to think that was a tragedy." I turned the stave in my hands. "Now I think it might be the kindest thing anyone ever did for them. We just aren't allowed to enjoy the credit."

"That sounds like something I would have written in a ledger," he said. "'Peace: purchased. Price: everything. Receipt: destroyed.'"

"'Balance: acceptable,'" I added, and he laughed, and the sound of it did something to my chest that I couldn't account for and didn't try to.

Because here is what nobody tells you about starting over: you don't start from nothing. You start from the residue. The habits the forgetting missed. He still counted things when he was nervous — fence posts, floorboards, my footsteps on the stairs. I still slept facing the door with a knife under the pillow, though the knife had migrated, over the months, to a drawer, and then to a shelf, and lately I'd been considering the cellar. He still made tea too strong. I still drank it without complaint, and neither of us could explain why that felt like a ritual instead of a preference.

And there was the wolf. Of course there was the wolf.

The scar had faded but never vanished — that pale shape across my ribs, four claw-marks in the rough silhouette of a beast, the mark that had named me before I ever earned a name. Children asked about it. I told them I'd won it wrestling a very large dog, which was possibly true, and possibly the last living piece of a story the world had agreed to lose. Some nights I dreamed of running beside something huge and warm through snow, and I woke with my hand pressed flat against the scar, and I felt — guarded. Kept. As if some part of me had refused the erasure on principle and stayed behind to watch over what was left.

Maybe that's all memory ever is, in the end. Something that stays behind to watch.

* * *

On the first morning of spring, I came down to the schoolhouse early and found the door already open, and Oscar standing at his desk with the second ledger in both hands, looking like a man holding something alive.

"I finished it," he said.

"Finished what?"

"The account." He turned it toward me. There, on the last page, under all those weeks of small honest debts, was a final entry, written in his neatest hand:

*Astrid Vane — owed: one life, one truth withheld, one map copied in bad faith, one winter of silence. Paid: unknown. Outstanding: everything.*
*Recommendation: remain. Recalculate daily.*

Below it, freshly inked, the ink still shining:

*Recalculated this morning. Balance: acceptable.*

I read it three times. Then I looked up at him, and he was watching me with the expression of a man who has just handed over his whole ledger to an auditor and is braced for the corrections.

"It's crooked," I said.

"I know," he said. "They're always crooked. That's rather the point of me."

So I crossed the room — and my body knew this too, knew it the way it knew the sword, the way it knew the scar, the way it knew the word I said in my sleep — and I took his ink-stained hand in mine, and I said the word awake this time, deliberately, so it wouldn't have to spend another night apologizing in the dark.

"Stay."

"Stay," he agreed. "For as long as the sums hold."

Outside, the snow was coming off the roofs in long silver sheets, and the bell in the schoolyard rang wrong because a child was hitting it with a stick, and somewhere south of us the ashes of a vault we would never find again were feeding the grass of a country that no longer remembered what it had survived. Somewhere a guild was rebuilding itself around smaller, meaner ambitions. Somewhere an inquisitor was growing roses and telling no one why. Somewhere, in a drawer in a loft above a tavern, a sword waited for a day I hoped would never come.

But that was all out there, beyond the hill, beyond the ledger, beyond the reach of anything we owed.

In here, there was chalk dust and strong tea and a crooked book that balanced, barely, daily, because two people kept choosing to make it balance. You can burn an archive. You can erase a war. You cannot, it turns out, erase a choice — not really, not entirely. It leaves a residue. It leaves a shape. It leaves a wolf-shaped scar and a word said softly in the dark, and it leaves a clerk who keeps a page open under a name he can't place, waiting, patient as arithmetic, for the sum to come home.

My name is Astrid Vane. I don't remember who I was.

I remember, every morning, who I choose.

And every morning, I choose this.
