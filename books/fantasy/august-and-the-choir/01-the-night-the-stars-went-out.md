## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The mule was named Turnip, and she hated Tamsin Marchetti with the pure, uncomplicated hatred of an animal that had once watched him call lightning out of a clear sky.

He couldn't blame her. He hated that version of himself too, or at least he'd spent nine years burying it so deep beneath salt dust and smuggler's silence that some nights he could almost believe it had died. Almost. The trick to being nobody, he'd learned, was practice. You practiced walking like a man whose shoulders carried nothing heavier than flour. You practiced a face that gave nothing back. You practiced not flinching when thunder rolled over the Thornfells and every nerve in your body itched to answer.

Tonight there was no thunder. That was the first wrong thing.

Tamsin paused on the switchback above the Greywater cut, one hand on Turnip's lead rope, and listened. The pass at this hour should have been talking to itself—the creak of ice in the scree fields, wind worrying the flagstones of the old watchtower below, somewhere far off the low complaint of a mountain goat with opinions. Instead: nothing. A stillness so complete it felt less like quiet and more like held breath.

"Feel that, girl?" he murmured.

Turnip flicked one ear and declined to dignify him with more.

He checked the sky out of habit, because a man who moved contraband through high country after dark learned to read stars the way farmers read clouds. Except tonight the reading came back wrong. He knew the winter constellations the way he knew his own hands—had navigated by them for nine years, since the night he'd walked out of the storm-caller chapter house with nothing but his name and a burn scar across his palm. The Wagon. The Weeping Sisters, three stars in a crooked line that sailors swore cried salt down onto the sea. The Shepherd's Eye, bright enough to cast your shadow on moonless nights.

The Shepherd's Eye was gone.

Not clouded. Not dimmed. *Gone*, as if someone had reached up with a wet thumb and smudged it off the world. And where the Weeping Sisters should have hung—two stars now, then one, then—

Tamsin stood very still on the frozen switchback and watched a star go out.

It didn't fall. It didn't flare. It simply stopped, the way a voice stops mid-word when the singer forgets why they were singing. One moment the Wagon's rearmost wheel-star hung in its ancient place; the next there was a hole in the sky shaped exactly like it, blacker than the black around it, and Tamsin's skin crawled from wrist to collar because he had felt that kind of hole before. Once. In a binding circle, eleven years ago, when the elders had opened the old wards to renew them and shown the assembled order what they were actually keeping out.

*Something is eating them,* he thought, and the thought arrived whole and terrible, the way true thoughts do, already wearing its own funeral clothes.

He made himself move. That was the discipline, the whole of it, the thing the order had never understood about surviving: you made yourself move when every instinct screamed to stand and stare at the impossible. He clicked his tongue at Turnip, took up the lead rope, and started down toward the Greywater cut where the salt barrels waited under their canvas shroud. Sixty pounds of white gold apiece, scraped from the evaporation pans along the coast and hauled three hundred miles inland to sell at four times worth to mountain towns the king's tax men couldn't find on a map. Honest work, if you squinted. Invisible work. His work.

His hands were steady. He noted that with detached professional interest, the way he'd note good weather. Steady hands, even pulse, breath slow. Nine years of practice holding a calm he did not feel, and here it was, doing its job, the way a wall does its job while the flood climbs its face.

Because underneath the calm, something else was happening. Something he didn't have a name for yet but recognized anyway, the way you recognize a smell from childhood before you can place it.

His storms were going quiet.

Not dormant—he knew dormant, had lived dormant for nearly a decade, a closed door in the center of his chest that ached only when the weather turned violent. This was different. Dormant was a door. This was the door *dissolving*, the pressure behind it draining away like water into cracked stone. All his life there had been a hum under his skin, faint as a struck tuning fork heard through a wall, the ambient awareness of every charged cloud within fifty miles. Even exiled, even bound, even sworn to never touch it again—that hum had remained. It was the last piece of himself the order hadn't been able to take.

Tonight the hum was fading, note by note, like the stars.

"No," Tamsin said aloud, and his voice cracked the stillness and immediately regretted it. Sound fell dead in the pass, swallowed without echo, as if the mountains themselves had been told to be quiet.

Turnip stopped and would not start again.

He knelt and put his ear against her neck. Her heart was hammering, triple-time, and her breath came in short frightened puffs—but she wasn't spooked, not the way horses spook at wolves or rockslide. She was listening. Animals always listened better than people; it was why he'd trusted them more, most of his life.

Then he heard it too.

Faint. Far. Coming from everywhere and nowhere, from the east where the border lay thirty miles off, from the sky itself, from under the ground—a sound like singing heard from the bottom of a well. Wordless. Patient. Thousands of voices braided into one long sustained tone, and threaded through it, thin as wire, a melody that Tamsin's body knew before his mind caught up, because his body had been built around that melody's opposite. Every storm-caller learned the counter-pattern in infancy, the way infants learn their mother's heartbeat. Harmony against chaos. Song against song.

This was no harmony. This was the thing the songs were built to hold down, and it was awake, and it was *singing*.

Tamsin rose slowly. His knees wanted to lock; he made them bend instead. Practice. Movement. The flood climbing the wall.

Far to the east, past the shoulder of Mount Ceren and the black teeth of the Sister Stones, a light bloomed along the horizon. Not dawn—it was hours shy of dawn, and dawn didn't come green. The glow pulsed, twice, three times, in rhythm with the distant tone, and Tamsin understood what he was looking at two full seconds before it happened, the way you understand a falling tree has passed the point of argument.

The border wards of Vaelhar were coming down.

They'd stood for six hundred years. Raised by the first storm-callers, blood and lightning and names that predated the kingdom they protected, renewed each generation at cost—the order had never let the young ones forget the cost, though they'd dressed it in ceremony and called it duty. Those wards had a sound, if you'd ever been close enough to hear them working: a vast subsonic chord beneath the world, felt in the teeth rather than the ears, so constant that the whole kingdom lived inside it the way fish live inside water and never think of water.

The chord broke.

There was no other word for it. The green light along the horizon shattered—not faded, *shattered*—into a thousand falling threads, and the sound that rolled across the mountains a heartbeat later was the sound of every pane of glass in the world breaking at once. It came through the ground and through the air and through the bones of Tamsin's feet in his boots, a crystalline shriek that went on and on, avalanches starting in three valleys at least, snow sliding off slopes that had held their loads for centuries. Turnip screamed and bolted three steps before the lead rope snapped taut and Tamsin held on, both hands, boots skidding on ice, dragged a full yard before the mule's terror exhausted itself against his grip and his weight and the simple stubborn fact of him refusing to let go.

"It's all right," he lied, gasping. "It's all right. Stand. *Stand.*"

She stood. He stood. The glass-shriek faded by degrees into ringing silence, and in that silence the eastern sky glowed faintly green and began, slowly, to darken—not to night's honest black but to something emptier, a darkness with appetite in it.

And Tamsin Marchetti felt the last of the hum go out of his skin.

That was the moment the calm finally broke. Not at the wards falling, not at the dying stars, not even at the singing—but here, alone on a frozen switchback with a trembling mule and sixty pounds of illegal salt, feeling the door in his chest dissolve to nothing. Because he had built his life on a specific architecture of invisibility: no name that mattered, no magic that worked, no history anyone in these mountains could trace. The order had taken his standing. Exile had taken his home. He had taken everything else himself, deliberately, methodically, until what remained was a man who could walk through any room in Vaelhar unremarked, a ghost with a salt cart.

But the ghosts of the world had all just felt the same thing he had. Every ward-breaker, every hedge-witch, every smuggler with a charm stitched into his coat lining—every soul with so much as a drop of the old blood had felt the wards die, felt their small magics gutter like candles in a draft. Which meant that come morning, the whole border country would be awake and asking questions, and the answers would point east, and the roads east would fill with soldiers and refugees and worse—and somewhere in the machinery of panic, someone, someday soon, would say the words *storm-caller* out loud, and someone else would remember that there used to be such people, and someone third would start looking for them.

Invisibility had just become the rarest commodity in the kingdom, and Tamsin could feel his supply running out.

He got Turnip moving. Down through the Greywater cut, past the watchtower ruins where he'd cached rope and dried plums, along the frozen streambed where the ice had cracked in long radiating lines from the ward-fall, as if the river itself had flinched. He walked fast and thought faster, sorting the night into problems, because problems were manageable and awe was not.

Problem one: the salt was worthless now. Nobody would be buying luxuries in a kingdom whose sky was dying. Write it off.

Problem two: Lowmark, the haven town two days south, would be drowning in whatever poured through that broken border. Soldiers deserting. Families fleeing. Things that wore soldier shapes and weren't. He needed supplies, information, and a route west that didn't touch a main road—all of which meant Lowmark, and none of which meant safety.

Problem three, which wasn't a problem so much as a cold stone sitting in his stomach where his magic used to live: the singing hadn't stopped. It had settled into the background of the world, patient as erosion, and sometimes—when the wind died and Turnip's hooves found soft snow—Tamsin could almost make out words in it. Or near-words. A language of invitation. *Sleep,* it seemed to say, in the voice of everyone you'd ever lost. *You're so tired. Everyone is so tired. Let us carry it.*

He was tired. That was the horror of it, the part he kept folding away and it kept unfolding: the song was *beautiful*, and he was tired, and some traitorous chamber of his heart wanted nothing more than to lie down in the snow and let the melody do the remembering for him.

Instead he sang. Quietly, under his breath, half-hummed, the only defense he had left—the counter-pattern, the old harmony, the nursery-rhyme bones of a binding rite he'd sworn at nineteen never to perform again. His voice was poor and his memory of it imperfect, but the melody caught the singing's thread and pulled, just slightly, just enough, and the invitation receded to the edge of hearing.

Turnip's ears swiveled back toward him. She walked easier.

"Well," Tamsin said to her, when the pass finally opened onto the long southern descent and the first grey hint of false dawn touched peaks that had lost their stars. "Looks like we're done being nobody."

The mule said nothing, but she leaned her warm weight briefly against his shoulder, which from Turnip was practically a speech of devotion.

Behind them, the east went on glowing its hungry green. Ahead, down in the valley, a single light burned in the dark where Lowmark should be—and as Tamsin watched, a second light kindled beside it, then a third, then dozens, torches and lanterns blooming along the valley road like a fever rash. People moving in the night. People running from something.

People singing, faintly, sweetly, in their sleep as they walked.

Tamsin tightened the lead rope, turned his back on the broken border, and started down toward the lights, practicing his nobody's face one last time before the world stopped believing in it.

* * *

He smelled Lowmark before he saw it properly: woodsmoke and wet wool and, underneath, the sour tang of too many bodies in too little space. The haven town sat in a bowl of the southern foothills, three hundred souls on a good day, built of timber and secrets—a place where the king's writ ran thin and every second door opened onto a cellar where things could be stored or hidden or buried. Tamsin had been coming here six years. He knew which innkeeper watered the beer, which ferryman asked no questions, which alley stank of tanning hides and which of something the tanners preferred not to discuss.

The town had doubled overnight.

Refugees packed the square and spilled down every lane: families with cart-loads tied under oilcloth, soldiers without weapons or insignia, a whole village's worth of people from the border march who still wore the ash of their hearths in their hair. They moved strangely, Tamsin thought from the ridge above—slowly, gently, without the jostling desperation of real flight. Some of them were smiling.

Some of them were asleep on their feet, eyes open, walking in loose concentric patterns while their lips moved in unison around a melody Tamsin's spine recognized before his ears did.

He brought Turnip down the drover's path to the back of the tannery, where old Sef the ferryman kept a gate that opened for silver and closed for questions. Sef took the coin, looked at the eastern sky, looked at Tamsin, and said only: "Bad night to be carrying anything."

"Bad night to be carrying a name, you mean."

Sef almost smiled. "Even bad nights end." Then, quieter, as Tamsin led the mule through: "Ward-breakers came through at dawn. Asking after anyone with the old blood. Someone's paying for names, Marchetti—or whatever yours is this season."

Tamsin filed that away in the drawer of things that would kill him later, and stepped into the noise and the torchlight and the singing, and did not look back at the sky where the stars kept going out one by one, patiently, like candles after a wake.
