## 2. Salt and Ash

The last mile into Lowmark smelled wrong.

Tamsin had made this run eleven times in four years, and the haven always announced itself the same way: woodsmoke, tallow, wet stone, and under it all the mineral bite of salt—his cargo, everyone's cargo, the one trade that never died because people never stopped needing to preserve what they couldn't bear to lose. He'd built his whole life on that principle. Carry the salt. Keep things from rotting. Don't get attached to anything perishable, especially yourself.

But this morning the smell was ash. Not hearth-ash either. The fine grey drift of burned thatch and burned cloth and, beneath it, something sweeter and worse that he refused to name until he crested the ridge and saw the valley.

Lowmark sat in its bowl of granite like a dropped coin, and around it, spilling down every switchback of the pass road, came people. Hundreds. A river of them, flowing downhill in the wrong direction—toward the border, toward Vaelhar, which was madness, because nobody flowed toward Vaelhar unless Vaelhar itself had become the safer place to be. They moved with the terrible efficiency of the frightened: no carts abandoned, no crying children put down, just a long grey-brown ribbon of humanity winding through the pines with their belongings on their backs.

Tamsin stood at the treeline with forty pounds of salt on his shoulders and watched them, and felt the quiet where his storms used to be press against his ribs like a hand.

They're running *from* something, he thought. And whatever it is, it's behind me too.

He should have turned around. That was the arithmetic he'd lived by since exile: see trouble, subtract yourself, arrive at safety. Four years of clean equations. But his boots were already moving down the slope, and he hated himself a little for how easily they did it.

* * *

Lowmark's gate—a formality, really; the haven had walls but no army—was jammed open, and the customs man who usually shook down smugglers for two coppers a crate was standing on a barrel directing traffic with a voice worn down to gravel.

"—single file past the well, water first, questions after! If you've got skills say so to Mistress Hark at the longhouse, if you've got coin keep it quiet, and if you've got sickness stay downwind or I'll have you downwind myself!"

Tamsin slipped into the current. It was a skill, moving unseen through crowds; exile taught you that faster than it taught you anything else. Shoulders down, eyes level, face set to nothing. He'd been so good at it for so long that being good at it had become the whole shape of him.

Then he started hearing what the refugees were saying.

Not to him. To each other, in fragments, in the low urgent register of people trading fear like currency.

"—the whole garrison, singing, not a word of it any tongue I know—"

"—my brother's wife is from Duncarrow, she said the soldiers marched out at midnight, all of them, eyes open, and sang the whole way—"

"—it's the king," an old woman kept saying, over and over, like a prayer worn smooth. "It's coming from the king. The king is singing us to sleep."

"*Hush*, Mother, you'll start a panic—"

"The stars went out," someone else said, flat with shock, as though repeating it might make it comprehensible. "I saw it. One by one, west to east, like candles pinched by a hand."

Tamsin's load suddenly weighed eighty pounds. He set the salt crate down beside the well, carefully, the way you set down something when your hands aren't steady, and told himself it was to free them up.

The wards. Last night the border wards had come apart above his head with a sound like a cathedral window giving way, and he'd told himself it was mountain-sickness, echo, anything. He'd spent four years telling himself stories about sounds in the night. But the wards hadn't fallen. They'd been *broken*—and broken from which side?

The king. He turned the phrase over. Tamsin Marchetti had never seen King Aldric, had no love for kings in general, but even from the far side of the border even exiles knew the shape of the story: Aldric the Steady, thirty years on the throne, a kingdom that ran like clockwork because its king refused to sleep through anything. And now his own soldiers were marching out of their barracks at midnight with their eyes open, singing.

A child near the well was humming. Three notes, rising, falling, rising again—the same three notes, patient as dripping water. Tamsin's skin crawled before he understood why. It was the tune he'd half-heard on the pass last night, threading through the silence where the stars should be. The child's mother pulled her away mid-hum, and the melody cut off, and the silence it left behind felt less like absence than like holding a breath.

"Salt-man." A voice at his elbow. "You going to stand there all day or are you going to help?"

* * *

The longhouse was chaos organized by one woman: Mistress Hark, seventy if she was a day, moving between cots and cookfires with a ledger in one hand and a ladle in the other, triaging the flood of bodies into rows. Tamsin carried water. Then he carried a stretcher. Then he carried an old man whose feet had frozen black on the pass, and stood outside afterward in the cold breathing until his stomach agreed to behave.

It was full dark when the woman found him.

He was stacking firewood against the longhouse wall, working by feel, when he heard footsteps stop behind him and a voice say, conversationally:

"You stack like a storm-caller."

His hands didn't stop moving. That was the training. But everything below his hands went cold and still, the way air goes still before weather.

"Storm-callers don't exist," he said. "Ask anyone."

"Mm. And yet here's a man carrying salt through Thornfell passes alone, walking the smuggler roads like he was born to them, keeping his head down in exactly the way a man keeps it down when he's hiding from something specific." A pause. "You always did have a tell, Tam. You go quiet with your hands."

The wood block slipped from his fingers.

He turned slowly. She stood in the doorway with a lantern held low, and the light climbed her the way it climbs a stranger—until it reached her face. Older. Harder. A scar through her left eyebrow that hadn't been there at nineteen. But the mouth was the same, the crooked, skeptical mouth that had once informed him, aged seventeen, that destiny was a story grown-ups told to make chores sound important.

"Odessa," he said.

"Hello, Tam."

Four years. Four years of rehearsing this moment without knowing he was rehearsing it—in the bad nights, in the hollow ones, he'd imagined finding her again a hundred ways, and in none of those imaginings had he managed to say anything better than her name.

"You look—" he began.

"Don't." She set the lantern on the woodpile. "Don't do the thing where we pretend it's been four years of nothing. It hasn't. You vanished. I looked. Then I stopped looking, because looking was getting people asking questions I couldn't answer." Her jaw worked. "You could have written. One line. *Alive. Don't find me.* Even that."

"I know."

"Do you?" She stepped closer, and up close he could see exhaustion carved under her eyes, the kind that doesn't wash off. "Do you actually know what it's like, Tam? Because I've spent four years wondering if the order got to you. If they found you on some road and—"

"They didn't." His voice came out flatter than he intended. "Nobody found me. That was rather the point."

Something flickered across her face—hurt, quickly converted, the way she'd always converted hurt, into sharpness. "Right. The point. Exile as a career move."

"It wasn't a *career move*, Odessa—"

"I know what it was." And there it was, underneath the anger: she knew. Of course she knew. She'd been there, or near enough; the trial had been sealed but news traveled, and Odessa Vane had never in her life let a locked door stop her. "I know exactly what it was. I also know you didn't trust me with it. You trusted the mountains instead. You trusted *salt*."

"The mountains don't ask questions."

"No. They just kill you." She laughed, short and bitter, and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes for a moment, and when she lowered them the anger had burned down to something rawer. "Gods. Look at us. Four years and we're fighting like it's been four days."

"That's usually a sign," Tamsin said quietly, "that the fight never finished."

She looked at him then, really looked, and he watched her cataloguing the changes the way he was cataloguing hers—the scar, the new lines, the calluses on her hands that weren't a climber's calluses or a fighter's. They were a worker's. Specific ones. He'd seen hands like that once before, on the ward-smiths who maintained the order's boundary stones.

"You broke the wards," he said.

It wasn't a question, and he watched her not deny it. Watched her chin come up in the old defiant angle even as her eyes went somewhere else entirely.

"Last night," he said. "That sound. That was you."

"Yes."

"Why?"

For a long moment he thought she wouldn't answer. The refugee river murmured beyond the longhouse wall; somewhere inside, a baby cried and was hushed. When Odessa finally spoke, her voice had gone careful, the way ground goes careful over a mine.

"Because they have my sister," she said. "And because you're the only person alive who might be able to help me get her back—and I need you to hear the rest before you decide whether you hate me. Because you're going to want to hate me, Tam. I'd want to hate me."

She picked up the lantern. "Walk with me. There's a cellar you need to see, and a dead man who isn't, and I promise you—" the crooked mouth twisted, "—nothing about tonight is going to make sense ever again."

Tamsin looked back at the longhouse, at the light and the noise and the hundreds of sleeping refugees who had fled a singing king. He thought about turning around. He thought about the pass, and the salt, and the clean arithmetic of a small invisible life.

Then he heard it, faint, from somewhere among the tents: a child humming three notes, rising, falling, rising.

The past he'd fled hadn't found him. It had been waiting for him, patient as winter, and now it wanted a word.

He picked up the lantern and followed her into the dark.
