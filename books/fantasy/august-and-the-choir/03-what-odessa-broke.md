## 3. What Odessa Broke

Odessa Vane had broken a great many things in her life, and she had a professional's relationship with the guilt that followed. A ward-breaker who flinched at every shattered seal didn't eat. So when the border wards of Vaelhar came down under her hands—came down *because* of her hands, because she had knelt in the snow for six hours with her tools in the ice and unpicked a working older than the kingdom—she did what she always did. She catalogued the damage. She counted the cost. She put the guilt in its box and sat on the lid.

The box was not holding.

She watched Tamsin Marchetti across the common room of the Drowned Lantern, and the lid kept sliding.

He'd changed. Ten years would do that to anyone, but exile had done it thoroughly. The boy she'd known had been all sharp angles and louder opinions, a storm-caller's son who laughed like weather coming in. The man drinking bad ale in the corner wore grey like everyone else in Lowmark, kept his hood up, kept his back to the wall, kept his voice down even when he wasn't saying anything. Invisibility as a craft. She recognized the workmanship because she practiced the same trade.

But he'd looked at the sky last night. When the stars went out one by one over Thornfell, every smuggler in the Lantern had muttered and gone back to their cups, and Tamsin Marchetti had stood in the doorway with his face turned up like a man listening to something no one else could hear. And his hands—she'd seen his hands. They'd been half-curled, fingers spread, the way storm-callers held them when they were listening for lightning.

He still felt it. Whatever had gone quiet in the world, he still felt it.

That was useful. That was also terrifying, because Odessa Vane was about to ask this man to walk into the mouth of the thing that had eaten the stars, and she was going to have to tell him why, and the truth was a wound she'd been keeping clean by never looking at it.

"You're staring," said Ilen Corvane, beside her, not looking up from the map he was pretending to study. The old astronomer had a voice like dry paper and a talent for being ignored, which in Lowmark was a survival skill. "Staring is a confession. Confess quietly."

"Shut up, Ilen."

"Gladly. After you tell him."

She took a breath that hurt on the way in. "Wish me luck."

"I don't believe in luck. I believe in arithmetic." He folded his map. "And your arithmetic is bad. You're calculating whether he'll say yes. You should be calculating what happens if he says yes and then finds out you lied."

"I'm not going to lie."

"No," Ilen agreed mildly. "You're going to tell the truth in an order designed to sound like a lie. There's a difference, and men like Marchetti can smell it."

She left him with his map and crossed the room, weaving between tables of refugees and smugglers and the particular species of person who was both. The Lantern smelled of tallow smoke and wet wool and the river-mud stink of Lowmark itself, that drowned little town wedged into the mountain's armpit where the law forgot to look. Someone was crying two tables over. Someone else was singing—the *wrong* song, a lullaby, soft and tuneless, and Odessa felt her spine go cold every time she heard it now. Half the refugees hummed it in their sleep. The soldiers at the border had sung it too, they said, marching in perfect unison with their eyes closed.

She sat down across from Tamsin without asking.

He didn't startle. That was another change; the boy would have startled. The man just moved his cup an inch out of her reach—not hostile, just tidy—and looked at her with eyes the color of a sky deciding whether to rain.

"Odessa Vane," he said. "They told me you were dead."

"They tell everyone that about everyone. It keeps the funerals cheap." She set her own cup down. "You look terrible."

"You look successful."

It was almost banter. Almost. Ten years of silence sat between them like a third person at the table, and neither of them reached for it yet.

"I broke the wards," she said.

His expression didn't move. Practiced. But she saw his hand flatten against the table, the small tell, the body bracing before the mind agrees to. "I know. Everyone knows. The whole border came down like a dropped chandelier. They're calling it the Fall of the Veil in the lowlands. Poets are already working."

"Do they know who?"

"Some say the king's own mages turned traitor. Some say the wards simply died of old age." His eyes came up. Flat. Careful. "Some say a ward-breaker was seen on the pass three nights before, and that she walked away alive, which no one has ever done."

"And what do you say?"

"I say you're sitting here, so either you're the best liar in Vaelhar or the worst." He leaned back. "Which is it, Dess?"

*Don't call me that.* The name landed like a hand on an old bruise. Nobody had called her that since—since before. Since the order, since the trial she'd attended in secret, since the day Tamsin Marchetti refused to feed a child to a binding and got himself erased for it, and she had said nothing, done nothing, stayed silent in the back row with her apprentice robes and her cowardice, and let them take his name.

"I broke them," she said. "I want to tell you how. And then I want to offer you a bargain. And I need you to hear all of it before you decide anything, because if you interrupt me I will lose my nerve, and I've spent ten years building up quite a lot of it."

Something flickered behind his eyes. Not warmth. Recognition, maybe—the look of a man seeing a familiar animal caught in a familiar trap.

"Go on," he said.

"The wards weren't failing. Everyone wants to believe they were failing, that it's nobody's fault, that stone wears out. They weren't. I've read those workings—I helped maintain the northern anchors for four years before I went freelance. They were sound. They would have held another century." She turned her cup slowly on the table, watching the ring of moisture it left. "Someone opened the door from inside. Someone gave the Choir's people the anchor-sites, the key-words, the maintenance schedules. And someone knelt in the snow on the Widow's Pass and unpicked the master seal stitch by stitch while grey-cloaked men stood behind her humming."

Tamsin had gone very still. Stillness, she remembered, was what he did instead of shouting. The boy had shouted. The man went quiet as deep water.

"Why?" he asked. One word. No heat in it at all, which was worse than heat.

"Because they have my sister."

There it was. Out. The wound open to the air of a smoky tavern full of strangers, and the strange lightness that came with it, the vertigo of a thing long carried finally set down.

"Sera," Tamsin said quietly. He remembered. Of course he remembered; Sera Vane had followed her big sister everywhere, had once tried to braid lightning into her hair because Odessa could hold a charge in wire and Sera thought that was the same as magic. "Sera's in the capital."

"Sera was in the capital. Sera was a novitiate at the Court Observatory—she wanted to chart stars, can you imagine, our family producing one honest scholar. Then the king started sleeping. Then the observatory staff started singing. Then one morning there was a letter in my saddlebag written in her handwriting, except the handwriting was wrong, the loops were too even, like someone had taught themselves to forge her by watching her write." Odessa heard her own voice go flat and precise, the ward-breaker's voice, the voice she used for describing damage. "The letter said: come to the Widow's Pass on the first of Frostmoon, bring your tools, do exactly as instructed, or we will send you the rest of her."

"Did you go to the authorities?"

"To whom?" She laughed, and it came out ugly. "The border garrison? Half of them are already humming. The court? The court is the thing doing this. The storm-caller order?" She watched him flinch, just barely, at the name. "The order has eleven living members and a chapter house they haven't opened in years, and every one of them knew the binding was thin and said nothing. No, Tam. There were no authorities left. There was just me, and my tools, and a pass full of grey cloaks, and a letter written in my sister's stolen handwriting."

"So you broke them."

"So I broke them." She met his eyes. "Six hours on my knees in the ice. And I want you to understand something, because I know how it sounds. I didn't hesitate because I'm brave and I didn't do it because I'm a coward. I did the arithmetic. One sister, weighed against—" she gestured vaguely at the world, the fallen border, the refugees, the extinguished stars, "—all of that. And I couldn't make the numbers work in any direction, so I chose the number I could touch. That's the whole confession. That's everything. I am not sorry enough and I am sorrier than you can imagine, both at once, and I've decided to live with it."

The fire cracked. Somewhere behind them the crying had stopped and the humming had started, soft, in time, and Odessa made herself not turn around.

Tamsin was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was low. "You could have come to me."

"What?"

"After. Before. Anytime in ten years. You knew where I was—or you could have found out. Ward-breakers find things out." Something had shifted in his face, the flatness cracking along old lines. "You sat in that trial hall, Dess. I remember your face. I sang the refusal and I looked for one friendly face in that whole marble room and yours was the only one, and you looked away. And then ten years, and then you break the wards, and now you sit down at my table. Why now? Why me?"

"Because you're the only storm-caller left who isn't dead, corrupted, or complicit," she said. "And because I owe you a debt with interest, and I pay my debts." She leaned forward. "Here's the bargain. Hear all of it."

"I'm listening."

"The Choir completes its song at the winter solstice. Ilen—that's Master Ilen Corvane, court astronomer, officially deceased, currently hiding in a flooded cellar—he believes there's a way to stop it. A counter-working. Old magic, first-binding magic. It needs a storm-caller. A real one. There are perhaps five people in Vaelhar who could attempt it and four of them are wearing grey cloaks or a crown." She let that land. "I'm going to the capital. Ilen's coming. We leave within two days, on the smuggler roads, ahead of the solstice. I want you with us."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange, when this is done—if we live, if it works—I go before the order. What's left of it. Elder Maren, whoever breathes. And I give testimony. Full testimony, witnessed, sealed: that Tamsin Marchetti's refusal at the renewal was not treason but principle. That the binding rite as practiced demands the blood of a child, and that he was the only one in a hundred years to say no out loud." Her hands were not quite steady on the table, and she let him see that they weren't. "I'll clear your name, Tam. Or I'll die trying, which given our itinerary is a real possibility."

He looked at her for a long time. She let him. She owed him the looking.

"You should know," he said finally, "that I don't care about the name anymore. You spend ten years being no one, you get good at it. The name stopped mattering around year three."

"Liar," she said softly. "You keep your hood up in a town where everyone wears hoods. You sign nothing. You flinch when anyone says 'Marchetti.' Men who don't care about their names don't guard them like that."

His jaw tightened. Point scored, and they both knew it, and ten years ago he'd have argued for an hour out of sheer delight. Now he just looked tired.

"There's something else," he said. "Last night. When the stars went out. I felt—" He stopped. Started again. "My storms are quiet. Have been since the exile—they bound them, mostly, left me a trickle. But last night it wasn't quiet like silence. It was quiet like held breath. Like something enormous was listening back through the gap where my magic used to be." His eyes came up to hers. "If I come with you, Odessa, and whatever's wearing the king notices me... everyone near me is in reach of it. You understand that. I'm not a weapon. I'm a lit beacon walking into a dark room."

"Good," Odessa said. "Beacons are useful. Dark rooms are the problem." She pulled a folded scrap of paper from her coat—a route sketch, the smuggler roads, the passes that remembered travelers—and laid it on the table between them. "We leave tomorrow, an hour past dawn, from the salt cellar on Tanner's Row. Ilen will explain the Choir. He explains everything; it's insufferable. You don't have to decide tonight."

"Yes I do," Tamsin said. "We both know I do."

And he was right, and they both sat with it, and Odessa realized her heart was pounding like a girl's, like an apprentice's, like the girl who had once braided wire into her hair and called it lightning.

"Then decide," she said, "and know this much. I broke the wards with my own hands and I'd do it again, because she's my sister, and I won't apologize for loving someone more than the world. But I have spent every hour since doing the other arithmetic—what the world costs, what love costs, what a person is worth on each side of the scale. And I have concluded that the scale is garbage, Tam. The scale is the enemy. The Choir doesn't sing people to sleep with force. It sings them into agreeing. Into thinking surrender is harmony." She pushed the paper toward him. "I want to fight something that fights with agreement. I want to be disagreeable until my dying day. Come be disagreeable with me."

A slow breath went out of him. And then—so fast she almost missed it, so brief she'd wonder later if she'd imagined it—the corner of his mouth moved. Not a smile. The ruin of one. The foundation where a smile used to stand.

"Tanner's Row," he said. "An hour past dawn."

He picked up the paper. He didn't say yes. He didn't have to; he folded the route sketch with careful hands and tucked it inside his coat, next to his heart, the way men carry things they've already decided to die for.

Odessa stood, legs unsteady, and made herself walk away without looking back, because looking back was for people who hadn't just gambled the only coin they had left.

Behind her, Ilen Corvane fell into step, murmuring, "Well?"

"He took the map."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer ward-breakers get, Ilen. Nobody ever says yes out loud. They just show up." She exhaled, long and shaking, and found her hands were trembling after all, now that no one was watching. Trembling, and—gods help her—lighter than they'd been in ten years. "He'll show up."

"And if the Choir comes for your sister before we reach her? If everything you've offered him is built on a hostage you may already be too late to save?"

Odessa looked at the window, at the black square of sky where the stars used to be, and gave the honest answer.

"Then I'll have traded my sister for the world," she said, "and I'll spend whatever's left of my life making sure it was worth the price. Starting with getting that stubborn, exiled, self-buried man to the capital before solstice." She pulled her coat tight. "Come on. We've got a cellar to dig an astronomer out of, and I want to know what Ilen knows about bindings that demand voices—because the letter they forged in Sera's hand had a postscript, Ilen. Four words. And I've been turning them over for a week."

Ilen stopped walking. "What words?"

"*The binding wants a voice.*"

Somewhere above them, beyond the smoke and the slate roofs, the starless dark pressed down on Lowmark like a held note—and in the flooded cellar-library beneath the town, a lamp was already burning, waiting for them.
