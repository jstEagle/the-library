## 4. The Astronomer in the Cellar

The dead man's library smelled of ink and river.

Tamsin followed Odessa down a stair that had once belonged to a merchant's house and now belonged to the water. The Sere had shifted its course two winters back, flooding half of Lowmark's lower quarter, and the smugglers who ran the town had done what smugglers always did: they'd stopped calling it a disaster and started calling it an asset. A drowned cellar was a cellar no tax collector or king's man would bother searching.

The water came up to their shins, black and still as poured glass. Oil lamps hung from hooks along the walls, their light doubled in the flood below, so they walked through a world of hanging flames. Bookshelves rose out of the water on every side, ten feet tall, sagging under the weight of volumes that had swollen fat with damp. Somewhere ahead, in the dark past the lamplight, something dripped with the patience of a metronome.

"You're telling me," Tamsin said quietly, "that the court astronomer of Vaelhar is living in a flooded basement."

"Faked his death," Odessa said. "Four years ago. Officially he drowned in the Sere during a survey expedition. Unofficially—" she lifted her lamp higher, sending shadows wheeling across the shelves "—he drowned himself into this."

"And you know this how?"

"Because I broke his wards." She said it flatly, without apology. "Three years ago. Paid work. Someone wanted the old observatory records sealed and I was the best ward-breaker between here and the coast. When I got inside, there was no one there. Just a letter addressed to me by name, thanking me for my craftsmanship and asking me not to tell anyone what I'd found."

"What did you find?"

"Nothing I understood at the time." Her voice did something complicated on the last word. "A star chart with dates going forward fifty years. Every date after last month crossed out."

They waded on. Tamsin kept his hand near the knife at his belt out of habit more than fear. He had spent six years learning to be unremarkable—a salt-smuggler with a limp he exaggerated and a face people forgot—and everything about tonight was stripping that disguise away layer by layer. First the stars going out over Thornfell. Then the wards falling like glass. Then Odessa Vane looking at him across a crowded haven and saying his name like it had never stopped being true.

Now this.

The dripping sound resolved, as they rounded a shelf of drowned atlases, into a voice.

"—no, no, the *declination*, if you adjust for the drift the whole sequence aligns, it has to align, unless—" A pause. A splash. Then, closer: "Unless someone is standing in my library."

He was smaller than Tamsin expected. Court astronomers were supposed to be gaunt towers of men, all elbows and altitude, but Master Ilen Corvane stood barely to Tamsin's shoulder, a round-shouldered figure in a robe that might once have been white and now matched the color of the floodwater. His beard was grey and trimmed short, practical, a soldier's beard on a scholar's face. He held a lamp in one hand and a long brass instrument in the other, and he pointed the instrument at them with the weary competence of a man who had done this before.

"Sextant," he said, by way of explanation. "Heavy end first. State your business."

"We were told you could explain what happened to the sky," Odessa said.

Something moved behind the astronomer's eyes—the flicker of a man deciding whether hope was worth the cost of carrying it. He lowered the sextant.

"The sky," he repeated. "You mean the stars."

"I mean all of them," Tamsin said. "Gone. One by one, over three nights. And the border wards came down with them, and the soldiers on the wall started singing in their sleep, and every refugee coming through Lowmark says the same thing—that the king's men smile too much." He stepped forward, water swirling around his knees. "I'm a storm-caller. Or I was. My magic went quiet the same night the stars did. Like something put its hand over my mouth."

For a long moment Corvane only looked at him. Then he set the sextant down on a shelf above the waterline with the care of a priest laying an offering, and said:

"A storm-caller. Here. Now." He laughed, a small dry sound with no humor in it. "I spent four years preparing for a catastrophe and I never once imagined the answer would walk in through the front door. Come. Mind the third shelf, it's rotten. And don't touch anything with mold on the spine—it bites."

* * *

His study was the driest corner of the cellar, a platform of stacked crates and salvaged doors above the flood, hung with charts. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Star maps layered over star maps, pinned and strung with red thread, and at the center of the web, where all the threads converged, a single chart of the northern sky dated forty years in the past.

Corvane cleared a space with his elbow and set down three tin cups. The tea he poured into them was grey and tasted of iron, but it was hot, and Tamsin realized he hadn't been warm since the pass.

"Now," Corvane said, settling onto a crate like a king taking a throne. "Before I show you what I know, I need to know what you believe. Not what you've seen. What you *believe*. Because what I'm about to say will sound like madness, and the only vaccine against madness is conviction."

"I believe the stars didn't go out," Tamsin said slowly. "I believe they were taken."

Corvane closed his eyes briefly, the way a man does when a prayer is answered. "Good. Yes. Taken. Now watch."

He pulled the central chart from the web of thread and laid it flat. It showed the northern sky as every child learned it: the Wagon, the Shepherd's Staff, the Crown of Vaelhar blazing at the zenith. But around each star, Corvane had drawn rings in faded ink—concentric circles, like ripples.

"This is the sky of my apprenticeship. Forty years ago. Note the rings—they're not decoration. They're measurement. Each ring marks the boundary of a star's light, its reach, the sphere of night it governs." His finger traced one circle. "Stars are not lamps, Master storm-caller. They are voices. Each one holds a single sustained note of the great harmony, and the night sky is not darkness decorated with fire—it is a *song*, held in perfect balance, and everything that lives beneath it lives inside that song. The tides keep time to it. The seasons. Sleep itself. The wards your order built were tuned to it like instruments to an orchestra."

Tamsin felt cold that had nothing to do with the flooded cellar. "And something is eating the singers."

"One by one," Corvane agreed softly. "Silencing them. And when a voice goes silent, its note doesn't stop—it gets *absorbed*. Listen." He began, very quietly, to hum. Three notes, rising. Then he stopped the third note halfway and let the second swallow it, bending the melody so that what remained was a single note twice as large, twice as loud, wrong in a way Tamsin's bones understood before his mind did. The lamp flames leaned toward the sound. The water in the cellar stilled.

Odessa took a step back. "Stop. *Stop.*"

Corvane let the note die. The lamps righted themselves. "That," he said, "is the Choir. An entity older than the kingdom, older than the borders, possibly older than the stars themselves. A chorus with an appetite. It doesn't devour light—it devours *voices*, and it wears what it eats. It has eaten its way inward from the edge of the sky for centuries, and for centuries it was held. Bound. Caged behind a binding so old that the order that made it has forgotten it made anything at all."

"The first storm-callers," Tamsin said. The words came out of him without permission, from some place deeper than memory.

Corvane looked at him sharply. "You know that name?"

"It's a story we tell children. That the first callers sang the storms into shape when the world was raw. That's all. A story."

"It is not a story." Corvane was on his feet now, moving along the chart-wall, pulling pins, laying documents side by side with trembling precision. "It is the load-bearing wall of your entire civilization, and it is failing. Look. Look!" He spread the papers before them: fragments of text, copies of copies, glyphs Tamsin almost recognized from the order's oldest rites. "The binding was sung by the first callers at the world's beginning—or near enough. It requires maintenance. Renewal. A voice, given periodically, to re-tension the cage. And for generations, the renewal was performed by your order. The storm-callers weren't weather-workers, boy. Weather was the *byproduct*. You were wardens. Your storms were the bars of the cell."

The cellar seemed to tilt. Tamsin gripped the edge of the crate-table. Six years of exile, six years of salt and smuggling and deliberate smallness, and underneath all of it the truth had been waiting: that his magic wasn't a talent, wasn't a curse, wasn't the thing that got a child nearly killed and him cast out. It was a *duty* he hadn't known he was abandoning.

"The order knew," he said. His voice sounded far away. "They knew what we were for."

"The order knew, or the order's founders knew, and everyone since has been singing the words without reading them." Corvane's voice gentled, which was somehow worse. "But that is the smaller horror. Here is the larger one."

He took down a final chart—one Tamsin hadn't noticed among the others, because it wasn't a chart of the sky at all. It was a drawing of a man. Fine-lined, precise, the portrait of a king in middle age: broad brow, tired eyes, the heavy crown of Vaelhar rendered in loving detail. Around the crown, in Corvane's hand, were annotations in red ink, dense as frost.

"King Aldric," Odessa said. "What about him?"

"Four years ago," Corvane said, "the Choir finished eating the outer heavens and began to look for a way *in*. Down. Into the world. But it cannot enter where it is not invited, and it cannot sing where it cannot be heard. So it needed a throat. A voice at the center of things, with authority, with reach—with access to every ear in the kingdom." He tapped the crown in the drawing. "It found one."

Tamsin stared at the tired eyes in the portrait. "The king invited it?"

"The king *prayed* to it." Corvane's laugh was terrible. "Drought year, famine, unrest. Aldric is—was—a devout man. He prayed every night on the observatory balcony for guidance, for deliverance, for the kingdom's salvation. And something at the edge of the sky finally answered. It sang to him. Gently. For months. A lullaby with a hook in it. And one winter night he opened his mouth and let it in, thinking he was letting in a god."

The lamp flames had gone very still again. In the flooded dark beyond the study, the drip of water counted on, patient as ever.

"He's still alive?" Odessa asked. "Inside it?"

"Alive. Aware, in flashes. A prisoner wearing his own body as a cell, while the thing that ate him wears his body as a *crown*." Corvane sat back down heavily. "Every decree, every edict, every smiling soldier—it all flows from that throne room. The Choir doesn't want to destroy Vaelhar. Destruction is loud. It wants to *harmonize* us. To fold every voice in the kingdom into itself until there is nothing left that sings alone. The refugees singing in their sleep aren't enchanted, they're being *tuned*. And the wards fell because the binding that held the Choir at the edge of heaven has run out of voice to sustain it."

"How long?" Tamsin asked.

Corvane looked at him, and for the first time the little astronomer looked old—not aged, but *old*, worn down to the bedrock of himself.

"The solstice," he said. "Nineteen days. On the longest night, when the veil between the world and the sky is thinnest, the Choir completes its song. Every voice in Vaelhar joins the chorus. Permanently. There will be no conquest, no war, no ruins. There will be a kingdom of smiling, sleeping people, and a sky with no stars left to see it by." He folded his hands. "Unless the binding is renewed. Unless a storm-caller reaches the capital and sings the counter-song before the last note falls."

The silence stretched. Water dripped somewhere in the dark. Odessa was looking at Tamsin, and he could feel her gaze like a hand on his shoulder, and he hated her a little for it, and loved her a little too, and both feelings belonged to a life he had buried six years ago.

"I was exiled," he said finally. "Cast out. The order will never—"

"The order is ash," Corvane said simply. "Or soon will be. What remains of it doesn't matter. What matters is that you are the only storm-caller I know of who is free, alive, and outside the Choir's reach. Which means, Master Marchetti—" and the use of his name landed like a stone in still water "—that the fate of every voice in this kingdom currently rests on a smuggler with a bad knee standing in a flooded basement."

Tamsin laughed. He couldn't help it; it came out cracked and disbelieving.

"Nineteen days," he said. "To cross the mountains, reach the capital, get inside a palace full of things that wear people like coats, and sing a song I've never heard, written by ancestors I didn't know I had, to free a king I've never met."

"Now you're beginning to understand the scale of it," Corvane said, with the ghost of a smile. "Fortunately, I have spent four years preparing for exactly this. I have the counter-song. Or most of it. Or—" he paused, and the pause had a crack in it "—a fragment. Perhaps a third. The rest was lost with the order's archives."

"Where were the archives kept?"

"At the chapter house. In the capital."

Odessa snorted. "So we need to break into the one city in the kingdom where every person is being tuned into a monster's chorus, find a dead order's hidden vault, steal a song, and then perform it perfectly on the longest night of the year."

"Yes," said Corvane.

"And the plan for getting out?"

"There is no getting out," Corvane said pleasantly. "There is only getting it done. Now—more tea? No? Good. Then let us discuss roads. Because the mountain passes are watched, the low roads are watched, and the only route I trust is one that doesn't officially exist."

He turned to his charts, already talking, already planning, a round little man in a drowned basement laying siege to heaven. And Tamsin stood in the lamplight with his cup of grey tea going cold in his hands, feeling the silence where his storms used to live, and thought:

*Nineteen days.*

Somewhere far to the north, faint as a memory, he could have sworn he heard humming.
