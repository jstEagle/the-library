## 5. Roads That Remember

The smuggler's road out of Lowmark wasn't a road, exactly. It was more of a rumor with gravel on it.

"Old-timers say the mountain built it," Odessa said, walking point with her hood up and her ward-breaker's kit clinking softly at her belt. "Says it moves when it wants to. Says you don't find the road—the road decides whether it likes you."

"I hate roads that have opinions," Tamsin said.

"You'll love this one, then. It's got a whole personality."

Ilen Corvane brought up the rear, breathing hard, his scholar's robes swapped for a smuggler's oilskin that fit him like a tent. He'd insisted on bringing three books from the flooded cellar-library, wrapped in waxed canvas against his chest like infants. Every hundred paces he stopped to check them.

"They're dry," Odessa said without turning around.

"Astronomical records are never merely dry," Ilen said.

They climbed out of Lowmark's smoke-haze as the sun came up behind them, which meant they were heading east, toward Vaelhar, toward the capital where a king wore an entity like a crown and sang the sky dark. Three days since the stars had gone out. Three days since Tamsin had felt his storms go quiet as a held breath.

He kept touching the place inside himself where the storm used to live. The way you'd touch your tongue to a missing tooth. Nothing there. Nothing there. Nothing—

Something flickered.

He stopped mid-stride.

"What?" Odessa said.

"Nothing." He started walking again. But he'd felt it—a single pulse, deep down, like lightning deciding whether to bother existing. Like the storm hadn't died in him after all these years of exile. Like it had been waiting.

*Don't,* he told himself. *You gave it up. That was the whole point.*

The mountain agreed to let them pass around midmorning, if you believed Odessa's theory. The trail narrowed along a shelf of black stone above a valley full of cloud, and the switchbacks ahead—Tamsin could have sworn they hadn't been there a moment ago—arranged themselves into something almost gentle.

"See? It likes us," Odessa said.

"It's lulling us," Ilen said. "The Choir's agents use old ways. The mountain remembers older ones."

"The mountain," Odessa said, "is not on their side or ours. The mountain is on the mountain's side. Which today happens to be ours." She glanced back at Tamsin. "Probably."

* * *

They made camp at dusk in a hollow beneath an overhang, no fire because Odessa said fires were for people who wanted to be found. They ate hard cheese and darker bread, and Ilen unwrapped one of his books just long enough to show them a page: a star chart drawn four hundred years ago, every constellation inked in gold leaf.

"This is what we're getting back," he said quietly. "All of it. Every light."

Tamsin looked at the gold constellations and thought about the night sky going dark star by star, like lamps being snuffed down a long corridor by someone very patient.

"What does it want?" he asked. "The Choir. What's the actual want?"

Ilen closed the book. "To be heard. That's the horror of it—it isn't hungry the way wolves are hungry. It's lonely the way only something enormous can be lonely. It eats stars because stars are the loudest things there are, and even that isn't enough. So now it sings through a king, through soldiers, through anyone it can reach, trying to make the whole world hum along." He paused. "The first storm-callers bound it because a chorus needs a counter-melody. Something loud enough and wild enough to drown it out. Storms are the sky arguing with itself. The Choir can't harmonize with argument."

"So the order knew how to fight it," Tamsin said. "And instead they spent four centuries feeding it children."

"Yes," Ilen said simply. "Frightened people always pay in other people's coin."

Odessa lay back with her arms behind her head. "Then we're going to need a lot of thunder."

Tamsin said nothing. Inside his chest, the flicker pulsed again—twice now, closer together—and he pressed it down like a coal under a boot.

* * *

The hunters found them on the second day, at a place where the road crossed a scree field and the mountain's mood turned, all at once, mean.

Tamsin felt it before he saw anything: a wrongness in the air pressure, a flattening, like the world holding its breath. Then sound—no, not sound. The absence of wind. And underneath it, faint as a hairline crack in glass:

Humming.

Three figures in grey came over the ridge above the scree. Grey cloaks, grey hoods, faces in shadow that shadow shouldn't have been able to fill. They didn't walk so much as pour downhill, smooth and unhurried, and the humming grew as they came—not a tune Tamsin recognized, but his body recognized it anyway. His heartbeat tried to match it. His breath tried to match it. Somewhere behind his eyes, a door he didn't know he had began, very gently, to open.

"*Down*," Odessa snapped, and threw something—a shard of copper wire twisted into a knot—that burst against a boulder in a spray of green sparks. The nearest hunter staggered, its humming stuttering.

It recovered in half a breath.

"Tamsin." Odessa's voice had gone flat and fast, the voice of someone doing arithmetic at speed. "The wards I broke—I know their frequency. I can hold two of them off for maybe thirty seconds. The third one's yours."

"I don't—" His mouth was dry. "My storms are gone. That's the whole— I'm exiled. There's nothing left in me."

Even as he said it, the flicker pulsed again, and this time it wasn't a flicker. It was a rolling boil somewhere below his ribs, and the air around him tasted of ozone, and the hairs on his forearms stood up like they were greeting an old friend.

"No," he whispered. "No, no—"

The third hunter reached the bottom of the scree and raised its hooded face toward him, and though it had no visible eyes, Tamsin felt seen. Truly seen. The humming bent toward him, personal now, intimate, a lullaby with his name hidden inside it somewhere.

*Tamsin Marchetti. Storm-caller. Traitor to the order. Exile. Come home. Come hum. Come sleep.*

His hands shook. Not with fear—or not only fear. With recognition. The power rising in him wasn't coming back; it had never left. Eight years of exile, eight years of salt-smuggling and silence, and the storm had sat inside him the whole time, patient as bedrock, waiting for a reason.

That was the terrifying part. Not that he might fail. That he might not.

"TAMSIN!" Odessa had both hunters tangled in her wire-work now, sparks flying, her movements precise and desperate. "NOW!"

The hunter in front of him tilted its head, and the humming swelled, and Tamsin felt the last of his resistance start to dissolve like sugar in warm water—

—and he thought, absurdly, of the child. The one he'd refused to give them. Eight years ago, kneeling on cold stone while the elders chanted, a little girl with a hum already starting in her throat, and Tamsin saying *no*, saying *I won't*, saying it until they took his storms' blessing and cast him out.

He had said no once. He could say it again.

"No," he said aloud, and the word came out with weather in it.

The storm broke.

Not from the sky—from *him*. It went up and out through his skin, a column of grey-blue fury, and the scree field lit white, and the hunter in front of him was simply *gone*, unmade, scattered into a fine ash that rained down the mountainside like dirty snow. Thunder cracked so hard the overhang groaned. Wind screamed across the hollow in a circle around the three of them, and nothing else moved.

When Tamsin came back to himself, he was on his knees. His ears rang. His nose was bleeding freely. The other two hunters were also ash—Odessa stood amid drifting motes, wire still in her fists, staring at him with an expression he couldn't read.

"That," she said finally, "was thirty seconds' worth of my work and about one second of yours."

"I didn't mean—" His voice came out wrecked. He swallowed blood. "I haven't done that in eight years. I didn't know I still *could*."

"You didn't know you still could," Ilen repeated. The old astronomer had taken refuge behind a boulder and now emerged, pale, clutching his books. He looked at the ash field, then at Tamsin, and his eyes were wide with something that wasn't fear. Recognition. "Boy. Do you understand what you just did?"

"Killed three hunters?"

"You called a true storm. A *full* storm. Out of exile, out of practice, with no focus and no rite." Ilen crouched in front of him, gripping his shoulder hard enough to hurt. "The elders told the order that exiles go quiet. That the gift dies without sanction. They've been teaching that lie for generations, because the truth is worse for them: the storm doesn't answer the *order*. It answers the *need*. Which means every exile they've ever cast out has been a loaded weapon walking around loose in the world, and they knew it."

Tamsin looked at his own hands. They were still trembling, but not from weakness. From the memory of how good it had felt—one second of pure, unfiltered rightness, the storm and his heart beating as one, everything simple and enormous and *loud*. He could still taste the ozone. He wanted it again already.

That wanting scared him more than the hunters had.

"I felt it," he said quietly. "How much I liked it."

"Good," Odessa said. She crouched beside him, close enough that he could see the exhaustion under her bravado. "Feel it and be scared of it. That's the correct response. The ones who aren't scared are the ones who end up wearing crowns and singing."

She held out a hand and pulled him up. Her grip was steady. His wasn't, but he let it be.

"We move," she said. "That flash was visible from the valley. Maybe farther. Every Choir agent in these mountains just felt a storm-caller light up like a bonfire, and they're going to come looking. We've bought ourselves a head start and spent our anonymity buying it."

"The road will help," Ilen said, squinting up the trail. "If the mountain liked us before—"

The switchbacks above them had changed. Tamsin was certain of it. Where the trail had climbed gently toward a saddle, it now plunged into a narrow gorge, steep-walled and shadowed, invisible from the valley floor.

"It's hiding us," Odessa breathed. "Look at that. Grumpy old thing."

They climbed. Tamsin went last, and halfway up the gorge he stopped, just for a moment, and put his hand flat against the black stone.

Inside him, the storm settled—not gone, not silent anymore, but coiled, watchful, a banked fire that would never again pretend to be ashes. It frightened him. It steadied him. Both at once, the way a drawn blade frightens and steadies the hand that holds it.

"Thank you," he murmured—to the mountain, or the storm, or whatever was listening.

Far above, past the gorge's rim, the starless sky pressed down like a lid. But somewhere east, three days' ride away, a king sat on a throne singing the world to sleep, and for the first time in eight years, Tamsin Marchetti had a reason to be loud.

Behind them, faint but unmistakable, drifting down from the ridge they'd fled:

More humming. Many voices. Coming on fast.
