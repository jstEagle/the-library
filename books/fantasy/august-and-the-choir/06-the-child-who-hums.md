## 6. The Child Who Hums

The caravan found them at dusk, or maybe they found the caravan—on the smuggler's roads it was hard to say who discovered whom. Odessa heard it first as a sound she hadn't heard in weeks: wheels. Real wheels, iron-rimmed, groaning against stone, a whole litany of them coming down the switchbacks above the river gorge like a slow avalanche of the desperate.

"Refugees," Ilen said, from his seat on a flat rock where he'd been pretending to rest for an hour. He wasn't resting. He never rested. He was sketching star charts on slate with a stub of chalk that he kept licking between lines, and when he said *refugees* he didn't look up.

"I can see that."

"Then you can also see they're walking straight into the pass where the grey-cloaks were camped two nights ago."

Odessa's hand went to the ward-breaker's kit at her hip without her permission. It did that lately. Her body had opinions before her mind did, and most of them were about running.

Tamsin stood at the edge of the overlook, wind pulling at his coat, and said nothing. That was Tamsin's whole vocabulary some days: silence shaped like a man. But when she glanced over, he was already looking down the trail toward the caravan, and there was something in his face she recognized from years ago, from before, from the girl she'd been and the boy he'd been and the friendship they'd both buried under separate avalanches of guilt.

He was going to help them. He just hadn't admitted it yet.

"We don't have food to spare," Odessa said, because someone had to be the practical one and apparently that job had fallen to her along with everything else.

"We have half a wheel of cheese," Ilen said. "And a theoretical knowledge of foraging."

"That's not food, that's a joke with rind on it."

Tamsin turned from the overlook. "There's a hollow below the ridge line. Old shepherd shelter. Wind-sheltered, one entrance." He paused, and Odessa watched him do the thing he did—weigh every word like a smuggler weighing salt, because words got people killed. "If we light no fire, forty people could sleep there tonight."

"And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow they keep walking. Same as us."

It wasn't charity. She knew him well enough—or remembered him well enough—to know that. It was arithmetic. Forty refugees moving through a pass with hunters in it made noise, made smoke, made mistakes, and drew the Choir's attention like blood drew sharks. A quiet caravan was a dead caravan. A shepherded caravan might live long enough to stop being his problem.

But watching him start down the slope without waiting for agreement, Odessa thought: *that's not why you're going.* And she followed, because she'd spent eleven years telling herself she'd stopped following Tamsin Marchetti anywhere, and here she was doing it again, and the worst part was how easy it still felt.

* * *

The caravan was worse up close. Everything was worse up close; that was the trick of distance, the mercy of it.

Forty-three souls, by Odessa's count, though counting children was a coin flip between heartbreak and denial. They came from the river villages east of Vaelhar, the ones that had flooded last spring when the wards failed and nobody came to fix them. They'd walked three weeks. Their oxen had been eaten somewhere around the second week, if the grease-stained ropes still looped to empty yokes meant what she thought they meant. The eldest among them was a grandmother with a rifle older than the kingdom. The youngest was a baby who didn't cry, which frightened Odessa more than crying would have. Babies should cry. Silence in something that small wasn't peace. It was surrender.

"They won't speak," the grandmother told them, spitting to the side in the manner of a woman who had decided these strangers were acceptable but only barely. "Not since the singing started. You ask them their names, they look at you like you've asked them the price of the moon."

"The soldiers sing in their sleep," a man said behind her. He had a carpenter's hands and a carpenter's way of talking, flat and structural. "Whole garrisons. My brother was conscripted at Ford Ansel. He wrote me a letter in spring—complained about the boots, asked after mother, normal things. Then in autumn a different letter came. Same handwriting. It just said *the song is kind, the song is kind, the song is kind*, forty times, and then his name, spelled wrong."

Ilen had gone very still over his slate. "May I see the letter?"

"You may not. My brother's letters are my brother's letters." The carpenter's jaw worked. "But I'll tell you what it smelled like. It smelled like the inside of a church. Like wax and cold stone. And underneath that—" he hesitated, and Odessa saw that his hands, those steady builder's hands, were shaking, "—underneath that it smelled like the sea at night. Like deep water. Like something enormous breathing under the floor of the world."

Nobody spoke. Above them the sky did its nightly work of being wrong—stars missing in patches now, whole constellations erased like chalk smudged by a careless thumb—and Odessa caught herself mapping the absences out of habit, because a ward-breaker reads gaps the way a sailor reads water.

Tamsin crouched by the caravan's lead cart, speaking low with a woman holding a lantern, organizing. Of course he was organizing. Eleven years of exile and he still moved through groups of frightened people like a keel through water, everyone orienting around him without deciding to.

That was when Odessa noticed the girl.

She sat apart—not pushed away, but set aside, the way families set aside the thing they love most and can't fix. A wooden cart-tail for a bench, knees drawn up, a blanket around her shoulders that had been mended so many times it was more patch than wool. Maybe ten years old. Thin wrists. Hair cut short and unevenly, the kind of cut you give a child yourself when there's no one else to do it and lice are the enemy.

She was humming.

Odessa's whole body went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the mountain air. Because she knew that melody. Everyone in Vaelhar knew it now, the way you know a word stuck in your teeth—it drifted through the capital's streets at night, leaked from barracks windows, hummed by sleeping soldiers and sleepwalking bakers and once, horribly, by Odessa's own sister, standing in a doorway with her eyes open and her self gone elsewhere. The Choir's song. The melody that ate people. The tune that wore King Aldric like a crown, according to a mad old astronomer who was currently sitting twenty feet away licking chalk.

And this child was humming it into the dark like a lullaby, and nothing was happening to her.

No glaze over the eyes. No slackening of the mouth. Her fingers were moving too—little patterns against her knee, patient, deliberate, like a weaver keeping count. When she reached the end of the phrase she simply began again, unhurried, unafraid, the way other children hummed songs about turnips.

"Tamsin," Odessa said quietly.

He looked. She watched him hear it—watched the recognition land on him like weather. His storm-sense, whatever was left of it under all that exile and silence, must have registered something, because his head tilted the way a man tilts when he hears a note slightly off-key in an otherwise perfect chord.

"She's not taken," Odessa whispered. "Why isn't she taken?"

"Perhaps," Ilen said behind them, in the voice he used for discoveries, which was unfortunately also the voice he used for describing soup, "because she is not being sung *to*. She is singing *with*."

They approached slowly. Children who don't speak have usually learned that adults approach either to help or to take, and the difference is rarely announced. Odessa kept her hands visible and loose, an old habit from negotiating with paranoid smuggler-lords, and knelt in the dirt a few feet away, which put her below the girl's eye level. Below eye level. Non-threatening geometry. Her sister had taught her that, actually—Sera, who'd been afraid of dogs until Odessa showed her how to make herself small and safe. Sera, who was currently somewhere in Vaelhar dissolving into a chorus, content and hollow, while Odessa crossed mountains to reach her.

The girl looked at Odessa. Grey eyes, level, ancient in the way children's eyes go ancient when they've seen the thing that took the stars.

"What's your name?" Odessa asked.

The girl's mouth opened. Closed. She touched her own throat, then shook her head—a small, clean gesture, not tragic, almost administrative. *No. Not that.*

"Mute?" Ilen murmured.

"Not born," the grandmother said, materializing the way old women do, without footsteps. "Chatty as a magpie until midsummer. Then the singing came down the valley one night, and come morning every soul in her village was humming, and she—" the old woman's voice caught on something and steadied itself, "—she opened her mouth and nothing came out but that tune. So her father gave her to us. Said a voice that won't be stolen ought to travel with people who'll guard it."

"And does anyone guard *her*?" Odessa asked, sharper than intended.

The grandmother looked at her for a long moment. "You've lost someone to the song."

It wasn't a question, which was its own kind of rudeness, and Odessa hated how accurately it landed. "My sister."

"Mm." The old woman rocked back on her heels. "Then you understand what I'm about to say. That child is the only person in this caravan who sleeps well. The only one the nightmares skip. Whatever that tune is, it doesn't want her. Or—" and here the grandmother's eyes went narrow and shrewd, "—it already has what it wants, and left the rest of her standing."

* * *

They made the hollow by full dark, forty-three refugees folded into a shepherd's shelter built for twelve, packed together for warmth, no fire. Odessa walked the perimeter twice, checking sightlines, checking the one entrance, checking the places where a grey cloak could stand and listen. The hunters didn't need eyes anymore, Ilen had told her. They listened. The whole mountain had become an ear, and somewhere beneath it a choir was singing a kingdom toward sleep, and every step they took toward Vaelhar was a step deeper into the audience.

She found Tamsin at the entrance, on watch, naturally, because the man treated sleep like a debt he refused to owe.

And she found Ilen crouched near the girl, which was a problem.

Odessa had known Ilen Corvane for less than a month and had already catalogued his tells the way she catalogued lock mechanisms. The chalk-licking meant thinking. The slate-tapping meant excitement. And the particular hunched eagerness he was wearing right now—the scholar leaning toward a specimen—meant that somewhere in that ruined cathedral of a mind, a terrible idea was being born and dressed in respectable clothes.

He had his slate out. He was writing musical notation on it. He was showing it to a mute ten-year-old.

"No," Odessa said.

Ilen didn't even flinch. "You don't know what I'm going to ask."

"I know exactly what you're going to ask. You're going to ask her to do it again. On command. For science." She stepped between them, deliberately, breaking his line of sight to the child. "She's a refugee, Corvane. Not a research subject."

"A natural anchor," Ilen said, and his voice had gone soft with wonder, which somehow made it worse. "Do you understand what that means? The binding that holds the Choir—it wants a voice. A willing voice, sustained, held in place. Every account of the first binding says the same thing. And this child carries the melody *without being consumed*. Do you know how rare—"

"I know exactly how rare." Odessa's voice cracked on it, and she let it crack, because some things deserved the crack. "I have a sister who was rare too. Clever Sera, funny Sera, who could pick up any tune after hearing it once. And the Choir found her useful, and now she stands in a doorway in the capital smiling at nothing, and when I get there—if I get there—I will spend everything I have to pull her out, and it might break her, and I will do it anyway, because she is my *sister* and not a *tool*." She was shaking. She let herself shake. "So no. You will not sit here in the dark and calculate what my sister's fate looks like when it happens to be wearing a smaller face."

Silence. Around them the shelter breathed—forty-three sleepers, the baby finally crying softly, the grandmother's rifle propped within reach like a family member.

Ilen set down the slate. Slowly. "You're right," he said.

"Don't patronize me."

"I'm not." He rubbed his eyes with chalk-dusted fingers, and for a moment he looked every year of his faked death. "I've spent thirty years studying the sky from a tower, Villalobos. Stars are easy. They stay where you put them on the chart. People don't." He glanced past her, at the girl, who had drawn her blanket up and was watching them both with those grey, level eyes—watching, always watching, missing nothing. "But I will tell you what I told no one else, and then you can decide how angry to be. The counter-song, if it exists, if I can reconstruct it from the fragments in the flooded library—we cannot hold it alone. The binding requires an anchor. Someone who can carry the melody without drowning in it. If this child is what I think she is, she may be the only living soul in Vaelhar who can stand inside that song and survive it."

"And you'd ask that of a child."

"The order I served asked it of children for four hundred years," Ilen said quietly. "They called it sacrifice and dressed it in gold. I am asking whether she might *choose*—when she's old enough to choose, if she ever chooses—with people beside her who would die before letting her do it alone. There is a difference. It is the entire difference. But I hear you. I hear what it costs you to hear me." He picked up his slate, hesitated, and set it down again, leaving it—notation and all—on the ground between them like an offering or a dare. "So I won't ask her anything. You will decide what she's told, and when, and whether. Consider it your ward to break or keep."

He withdrew to his corner, chalk and misery and starlight, and Odessa stood there hating him a little and respecting him a lot and understanding, with sick clarity, that he was right about the mathematics even if she'd die before admitting it aloud.

The girl was still awake. Of course she was.

Odessa crouched by her again. Up close, in the dark, the child smelled of woodsmoke and road dust, ordinary as bread. "I'm sorry about him," Odessa said. "The old man. He forgets people aren't puzzles."

The girl shrugged—one shoulder, economical. Then she pointed at Ilen's abandoned slate, and raised her eyebrows.

"You want to see?"

A nod.

Odessa retrieved it and held it where the thin moonlight could find the notation. The girl studied it, frowned with the specific frown of a critic, and then—delicately, with one finger—moved Odessa's hand two inches to the left, and tapped the slate twice, and hummed three notes: the same phrase from before, but corrected. Altered. *Better.* As if the Choir's terrible song had a version that hurt less, and she'd been carrying the revision in her chest all this time, waiting for someone who could read.

Odessa's skin prickled. "You changed it."

The girl nodded. Then she pressed her fist flat against her own sternum—*here, it lives here*—and looked up at Odessa with an expression that needed no translation at all, because it was the same expression Odessa saw in her own mirror every morning:

*I have something the monsters want. Help me keep it.*

Across the shelter, Tamsin had turned on his watch. He was looking at them—at Odessa, at the girl, at the slate—and in the dark his face was unreadable, but his silence had changed shape. This silence wasn't a wall. It was a held breath.

Above the ridge, far off down the pass, something began to hum back.

Many voices. Grey and patient and wrong, threading up through the stone like cold water through a floor.

The girl's hand found Odessa's sleeve and gripped it, and her humming—her small, stubborn, corrected version of the song—did not stop.
