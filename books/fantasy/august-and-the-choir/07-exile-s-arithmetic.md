## 7. Exile's Arithmetic

The road out of Lowmark climbed through pine and shadow, and Tamsin counted his steps the way he always did when memory came hunting. One for the stone. Two for the root. Three for the place where the path bent north. Numbers kept the past in its cage.

It wasn't working today.

Behind him, Odessa picked her way over scree with a ward-breaker's economy of motion, every step placed like she was defusing it. Ilen Corvane lagged, wheezing, muttering star-charts to himself as though altitude were an argument he could win by repetition. And ahead, the pass waited — grey sky pressed down on grey stone, the whole world the color of ash that hadn't decided whether to fall.

Tamsin walked and counted, and the counting failed, and eleven years ago opened under his feet like a crevasse.

* * *

The trial chamber of the storm-caller order had been carved from the living mountain, back when the order still believed mountains were alive. Lightning-glass ran through the walls in veins, and when the elders spoke, the glass hummed with their voices, so that every judgment sounded like weather deciding.

Tamsin stood in the center of the floor, twenty-four years old, rain still drying in his hair from the climb up, and watched Elder Maren read the binding-rite aloud.

She read it the way you'd read a grocery list. That was the part that undid him, later, in exile, in the dark: not the cruelty itself but the *boredom* of it. The ritual was old. It had been old for nine hundred years. A voice, young and unbroken, given willingly into the binding-stone, to hold the Choir's melody steady through another generation. The first storm-callers had written it in desperation, and every generation since had copied it out in comfort.

"The candidate is prepared?" Maren asked.

"She is," said Elder Sorrel, and there was something in his voice Tamsin took a full minute to identify, because he'd never heard it directed at a binding before. Pride. Warm, grandfatherly pride.

They brought her in. A girl of maybe eight, barefoot on cold stone, wearing the white shift they gave to sacrifices because someone, centuries back, had decided white looked holy instead of looking like a shroud. Her name was Wren. He knew her name because he'd taught her to whistle three weeks earlier, down by the cisterns, and she'd been terrible at it, and they'd laughed until the novices told them to hush.

"Wren," Maren said gently, "do you give your voice freely?"

The girl looked up at Tamsin. Not at Maren. At him. Because he was the one who whistled, he was the one who made thunder-shapes with his hands, he was the one the children followed around the chapter house like a duckling that had imprinted on the wrong thing.

"Freely," she said, in the small clear voice they'd been rehearsing into her for a month.

And here was the arithmetic that had ruined his life: eight years old. Nine hundred years of tradition. One kingdom sleeping safe behind wards woven from a child's stolen song. Against all of it, one man's refusal, which would accomplish nothing, save no one, and cost him everything.

The math said sing along. The math had always said sing along.

"No," Tamsin said.

The lightning-glass went quiet. In nine hundred years, the chamber had probably never heard silence before, and it didn't know what to do with it.

Maren turned. She was not a cruel woman — that was the other thing he'd needed years to understand. She was a tired one, tired the way load-bearing walls are tired, holding up a weight she'd never chosen. "Tamsin Marchetti. The rite requires—"

"I know what the rite requires." His voice cracked on the second word and he let it. "I've known since I was six, when they showed me the binding-stone and told me I might be chosen someday, and I was *proud*. Do you know what's in that stone? Have any of you actually listened to it? It's not a song anymore. It's a scream that's forgotten how to stop."

"The binding holds," Sorrel said. "The Choir sleeps. Vaelhar stands."

"Vaelhar stands on a little girl's throat."

"Vaelhar stands," Maren repeated, "on nine hundred years of voices, each given as this one is being given. You did not invent mercy today, Tamsin. You are not the first to flinch. You are only the first to do it *here*, in front of everyone, where your flinching becomes doctrine."

"Then let it be doctrine." He crossed to Wren — two elders moved to stop him and Maren, with one lifted hand, let him pass, and he would spend eleven years trying to decide what that permission meant — and knelt, and took the girl's small cold hands in his. "Wren. You don't have to be brave. Being brave is just what grown-ups call it when we don't give you a choice. Come away from the stone."

She didn't move. That was the worst of it, the detail that visited him at 3 a.m. for a decade: she didn't move, because she'd been taught so well, because a month of gentle rehearsal had built a prison more secure than any ward, and she whispered, "But the kingdom needs me," in a voice that believed it.

He stood up then and faced the circle of elders and gave them the only speech of his life worth remembering.

"You want arithmetic? Fine. Here's mine. One child, weighed against a kingdom — that's the sum you've been doing for nine centuries, and you've never once checked your work. Because if the binding needs a child, then the binding is broken, and you've been feeding a wound and calling it medicine. Find another way. Spend your own voices. Spend mine. But if the only answer you have left is a barefoot girl in a white shift, then the order isn't protecting Vaelhar. It's just afraid, and it's dressed its fear up as duty, and I won't carry it anymore."

The silence afterward lasted long enough for him to hope. That was the cruelest part. It lasted long enough for hope.

Then Maren said, "Take his storms," and the arithmetic resumed, and he was exiled by nightfall.

* * *

"—and if you keep walking like the mountain insulted you, you'll walk us straight off it."

Odessa's voice dropped him back into his body mid-stride. He blinked. The pass had narrowed while he wasn't watching; the trail clung to the cliffside above a drop full of cloud, and Ilen was leaning against a boulder with the expression of a man renegotiating his relationship with gravity.

"I was thinking," Tamsin said.

"You were somewhere else. There's a difference, and the difference is that one of them watches the trail." Odessa came up beside him, close enough that he could see the frost in her eyebrows. Eleven years, and she still read him faster than anyone alive. "Where?"

"Nowhere."

"Liar. Where?"

He looked at the clouds below instead of at her. "A trial."

Something shifted in her face — not softness exactly; Odessa's face didn't do softness, it did recalibration. "Yours?"

"Mine."

She waited. That was another thing about her: she could wait like pressure, like deep water, until you told her things just to breathe. But Ilen saved him, or doomed him, by arriving at the boulder and saying, "If we're stopping, I'd like it noted that my knees have filed grievances," and the moment broke, and Tamsin found himself almost grateful.

"We camp early," Odessa said, scanning the sky with a ward-breaker's squint. "There's weather coming that isn't ours."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of a fallen pillar-stone, some ancient marker worn faceless, and ate salt-pork and hard bread while the light died. Tamsin took first watch because he always took first watch, because sleep was where the past kept its office hours.

Except tonight the past worked days too.

He sat with his back to the stone and watched the stars come out — the ones that remained. That was the thing he couldn't stop noticing now that he'd seen it: gaps. Constellations with missing teeth. The Shepherd had lost his crook. The Sisters were one short. Whatever the Choir was eating, it was eating methodically, course by course, and the sky over Vaelhar was going dark from the edges inward.

Ilen emerged from his bedroll without pretending to sleep, which Tamsin suspected was the astronomer's natural state. The old man settled against the pillar-stone with a groan and followed Tamsin's gaze upward.

"Counting the losses?" Ilen asked.

"Does it show?"

"Everything shows on you. It's very inconvenient for you, I imagine." He pulled his coat tighter. "In the court observatory we kept records going back four hundred years. Star registers. Every astronomer's first duty was the count — how many lights, how bright, in what positions. Children think astronomy is about wonder. It's mostly bookkeeping." He smiled without humor. "The registers started showing losses sixty years ago. Small ones. A dimming here, a vanishing there. We reported it. The court reported it to the king. And the king—" He stopped.

"The king already knew."

"The king was already *wearing* it." Ilen's voice dropped. "You understand, I faked my death rather than keep filing reports into a mouth that swallowed them. Sixty years, Tamsin. The Choir has been at its meal for sixty years, and the binding that should have held it has been failing the whole time, and nobody — not the crown, not the order —"

He stopped again. Tamsin felt the sentence land even unfinished, because he knew how it ended.

"Not the order," Tamsin said quietly.

"They must have known. The binding-stone answers to them; they'd feel it thinning. If they knew the binding was failing for decades and said nothing—" Ilen shook his head. "No. Surely they searched for another way. Surely they—"

"They didn't search for another way." Tamsin heard his own voice go flat and old. "Eleven years ago they renewed the binding the same way they always had. A child. Eight years old. They rehearsed her for a month so she'd say 'freely' with conviction."

Ilen stared at him. Above them, a star flickered — or perhaps it was only cloud; he couldn't tell anymore, and the not-telling was its own kind of grief.

"And you refused," Ilen said.

"I refused."

"So they exiled you. The order that exists to guard the binding cast out the one man who told them the truth about it."

"They cast out the man who embarrassed them in front of the lightning-glass." Tamsin turned a piece of hard bread over in his fingers without eating it. "Exile isn't punishment, Master Corvane. Not really. It's disposal. You don't punish a problem you admit you have. You hide it."

For a long moment the old astronomer said nothing. Then, in the tone of a man setting down a heavy instrument carefully: "Do you regret it? Refusing?"

Tamsin had expected the question for eleven years and had never once managed to answer it honestly, because the honest answer had two heads. Yes: yes, because Wren had gone into the stone anyway, with another caller standing in for him, and his refusal had changed nothing except the shape of his own life. No: no, because the alternative was becoming the kind of man who heard a child say *the kingdom needs me* and agreed.

"Yes," he said. "And no. Both, every day, in about equal measure. That's the arithmetic of it. Nobody tells you that doing the right thing doesn't balance the ledger. It just adds a column."

Ilen nodded slowly, the way scholars nod — receiving data. "Then here's a figure for your ledger." He pointed up, at the gapped constellations, the dark spreading inward from the sky's edges. "Your order fed the binding children for nine hundred years. And the binding failed anyway. Slowly, quietly, over decades — but it failed. Which means either the sacrifice was never enough, or—" his finger traced downward, from the sky to the south, toward Lowmark, toward the fallen border wards, toward the direction a disgraced ward-breaker had come from — "or the sacrifice was never the point at all. Someone corrupted the rite long ago. Gave frightened people a cruel ritual and called it the original. And your order, rather than question it, spent nine centuries perfecting it."

The wind came down the pass and carried the smell of snow.

"You're saying the Choir learned to eat," Tamsin said slowly, "because we taught it that voices were food."

"I'm saying," Ilen said, "that monsters are rarely original. They take what we give them. And we have been giving, Tamsin Marchetti, for a very long time."

* * *

Odessa appeared out of the dark without sound, the way she did everything, and sat down across the coals from them. She'd been listening; her face admitted it and dared them to object.

"The order knew," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"If they knew the binding was failing," Tamsin said, "they hid it. Buried it under ritual. Kept feeding it children so they'd never have to look at what they were feeding."

"Then your exile bought them eleven more years of not looking." Her eyes caught the firelight. "Comfortable arrangement. For them."

"For them."

"And for you?" She tilted her head. "Eleven years smuggling salt through passes like this one. Invisible. Nameless. Was it worth it? The choice?"

He thought about lying to her, and discarded it — she'd hear the lie the way she heard everything, instantly and without mercy. He thought about the true answer, which was a tangle, and tried to find one thread of it.

"A man came through Lowmark last winter," he said finally. "Refugee from the eastern valleys. His daughter had been taken by the Choir's agents — one of the first harvests, before the wards fell. He wasn't angry. That was the terrible thing. He just wanted to know *why*, and there was no one to ask, so he asked me, because I sold him salt and I listened. And I realized — sitting there in that freezing warehouse — that I'd spent eleven years asking myself the same question. Why her. Why a child. Why does the whole architecture of safety rest on the smallest throat available." He fed the bread to the fire, uneaten. "I don't know if refusing was worth it. I know that refusing was the only version of me I can live inside. Everything else is just a room I'd be hiding in."

Odessa studied him across the coals for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had lost its edge, which from Odessa was practically a confession.

"When they held my sister's name over me," she said, "and told me to break the border wards or watch her be unmade — I told myself it was arithmetic. One life against a kingdom's shield. Simple sums." Her jaw tightened. "I did the sums. And every day since, I've been checking my work, and every day the work comes back wrong, and I can't tell anymore whether I'm a traitor or just bad at math."

"You're neither," Tamsin said. "You're coerced. There's a difference, and the difference is that coercion removes the choosing. What happened to you wasn't a choice. What happened to me was. That's the whole difference between guilt and grief, and I've had eleven years to learn the taxonomy."

"That's a very smug thing to say to a woman who betrayed a kingdom."

"It's a very accurate thing to say to a woman who's about to help un-betray it."

Something that was almost a laugh escaped her — one breath, quickly recaptured. Ilen, who had been pretending to doze, opened one eye. "If the two of you have finished exchanging confessions, I'd note that the girl Brann hasn't slept. She's sitting at the caravan's edge humming again. Same melody. Always the same melody."

Tamsin went cold in a way the mountain wind couldn't account for. "The Choir's song."

"The Choir's song," Ilen agreed. "From a mute child who has never heard it sung. Who shouldn't know it. Who carries it the way some people carry a tune and others carry a wound." He rose, joints crackling, and looked down the trail toward the small shape of the girl silhouetted against the last grey light. "Nine hundred years of binding, and the rite demands a voice given freely. Your order heard 'voice' and reached for throats. But a voice, truly given — sustained by memory, by love, by *choice* —" He shook his head. "I need my books. I need the first texts, the pre-corruption rite. And I need to understand why a child who cannot speak is walking around with the counter-melody in her chest like a second heartbeat."

"Because she's an anchor," Odessa said quietly. "Like I told you. Some people are born holding the line."

"Or," Ilen said, "she's something older than the binding. Something the binding was *copied from*." He said it lightly, the way men say enormous things when they're too tired to be afraid of them. "Either way, gentlemen and ladies, I suggest we all get some sleep, because tomorrow we cross the high pass, and I have a professional suspicion that the Choir's hunters will not have missed our little procession."

Tamsin looked up at the wounded sky, at the Shepherd missing his crook, and felt — beneath the dread, beneath the old shame finally reframing itself into something he could carry — a faint, familiar pressure behind his breastbone. Like weather deciding. Like a storm that had been locked in a cellar for eleven years turning over in its sleep.

His magic had been quiet since the night the stars went out. Quiet as held breath.

As he banked the fire and settled onto his bedroll, the pressure pulsed once — sharp, unmistakable, answering something far off in the dark of the pass.

Something up there was humming back.

* * *

Dawn came grey and late to the high pass, and with it came the sound.

Not wind. Wind didn't harmonize.

Tamsin stood at the mouth of the defile with the others behind him and watched figures emerge from the frozen mist — grey-cloaked, unhurried, moving with the terrible patience of tide. A dozen of them. Two dozen. Their humming rolled down the rocks ahead of them, and where it touched the frost, the frost *sang along*.

Beside him, Odessa swore in three languages. Behind him, Brann's small hand found his — the first time she'd touched him voluntarily — and her grip said what her voice couldn't: *that's my song they're stealing.*

The pressure behind Tamsin's breastbone stopped being a flicker.

It became a horizon-wide front, and it wanted out.
