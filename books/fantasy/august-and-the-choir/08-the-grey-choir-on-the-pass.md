## 8. The Grey Choir on the Pass

The mountain turned against them on the third day.

Tamsin felt it before he saw it—a wrongness in the wind, a hush where there should have been the ordinary gossip of snow and stone. The smuggler's road had carried them up through the pines and into the bare grey country above the treeline, where the pass narrowed to a throat of ice between two walls of black rock. Good ground for an ambush. He'd known it the moment they entered, and he'd said nothing, because there was no other way through, and because Odessa would only have given him that look—the one that said *I know you know, so say it or shut it*—and he hadn't wanted the conversation.

Now the conversation was happening anyway, in the language of cold air and held breath.

"They're here," Ilen said. The old astronomer stood with his back against the rock wall, his breath pluming white, his eyes fixed on the sky. "I can't see them. But I can hear them."

Tamsin heard it too. A hum. Faint, at first, like a fly trapped behind glass—but it was coming from everywhere, from the ice under their boots and the stone overhead, as if the mountain itself had caught the melody and couldn't stop repeating it. The same tune the refugees in Lowmark had murmured in their sleep. The same tune Brann carried without knowing she carried it.

Odessa had gone very still. She stood three paces ahead of him, her ward-breaker's kit strapped across her back, her hands loose at her sides. Tamsin had watched her work wards for six days now, and he knew what stillness meant on her: it meant she was counting. Reading the shape of whatever trap lay ahead by the way it bent the world around it.

"How many?" she asked quietly.

Ilen shook his head. "The song doesn't count."

"Then it's more than we want it to be." She glanced back at Tamsin, and something in her face softened—not much, just enough. "Well. You always did pick the scenic routes."

He almost smiled. Almost. Then the hum changed key, and every hair on his arms rose beneath his sleeves.

They came out of the rock.

Not climbing over it—*out* of it, the way water comes out of a cracked jug. Grey-cloaked figures stepping through the cliff face as though the stone had been a curtain all along, unfolding onto the ice with a smoothness that made Tamsin's stomach drop. There were eight of them. Then twelve. Then he stopped counting, because counting meant knowing, and knowing meant fear, and fear was a luxury they'd stopped being able to afford somewhere below the treeline.

They didn't draw weapons. That was the worst part. They simply walked forward across the frozen pass, unhurried, humming, their faces shadowed beneath their hoods—and where their boots touched the ice, the ice went grey. Not white-grey. *Empty*-grey. Color draining out of the world in slow circles, frost turning to ash, ash turning to nothing.

"The wards," Ilen breathed. "They're carrying pieces of the border wards. Broken ones. They walk inside the holes Odessa made."

"I'm flattered," Odessa said, and her voice was steady, but Tamsin saw her knuckles whiten on the strap of her kit. "How many holes did I leave?"

"Enough," Tamsin said.

"Then we run."

They ran.

The pass narrowed ahead of them—or the world narrowed, which amounted to the same thing—and the hum swelled behind them, no longer faint, no longer patient. Tamsin's lungs burned with the thin air. His boots slipped on ice that kept trying to forget itself under his feet. Ahead, Ilen stumbled, and Odessa caught him by the collar and hauled him upright without breaking stride, and Tamsin thought, absurdly, *this is what family looks like when it's running*—

And then the ice ended.

A crevasse split the pass from wall to wall, twenty feet across, bottomless, breathing out a cold that wasn't weather-cold but something older. The road remembered a bridge once—Tamsin could feel its ghost, the old smuggler-magic of it, a rope-and-plank crossing that the mountain had eaten decades ago. But ghosts don't hold weight.

Behind them, the humming stopped.

That silence was worse than the song. In the silence he could hear their footsteps, precise as metronomes, closing the distance with the confidence of things that had never once needed to hurry.

Odessa skidded to a stop at the crevasse edge. Ilen beside her, chest heaving. Tamsin beside them both, and the grey cloaks fanning out across the ice behind, cutting off retreat, and nowhere left to go except down.

"Well," Odessa said. "Options."

"We could negotiate," Ilen offered.

"With those?"

"I've negotiated with worse. I worked at court."

Despite everything, despite the cold and the crevasse and the twelve silent shapes walking toward them, Tamsin laughed. It came out ragged and strange, torn out of him like something escaping a cage. And the laugh broke something loose in his chest—something that had been locked since Lowmark, since the night the stars went out, since years before that, since the trial, since the child, since the door of the chapter house closing behind him forever.

Because his storms weren't quiet anymore.

He'd felt them go silent the night the sky emptied—that eerie deadness he'd told himself was relief. But standing at the edge of the crevasse with death walking toward him at a metronome's pace, he realized the silence had never been absence. It had been pressure. Years of it. All the weather he'd swallowed since exile, every squall he'd refused, every thunderhead he'd walked away from, packed down inside him like a fist slowly closing.

The grey cloaks were thirty feet away. Twenty-five.

"Tamsin," Odessa said. She'd seen his face. She knew. Of course she knew—she'd known him when he was still allowed to be what he was. Her voice dropped low, urgent, not afraid of him but afraid *for* him. "You don't have to. We'll find another way."

"There is no other way."

"There might be—"

"*There isn't.*"

Twenty feet. The lead figure tilted its hooded head, and beneath the hood something listened. Something that had been listening for him since the night the stars went out. He understood that suddenly, completely, the way you understand a thing you've spent years refusing to understand: they hadn't come for Odessa's guilt or Ilen's knowledge. They'd come because the Choir had lost one of its instruments, and instruments that wander free are called other names.

*Tamsin,* the silence seemed to say. *Come back to the song.*

"No," he said aloud.

And opened his fist.

It had been seven years since he'd let a storm all the way out. Seven years of small magics, careful magics, the trickles and leaks an exile permits himself—a gust to fill a sail, a chill to keep salt fresh, nothing that would make the order look twice, nothing that would make the sky remember him. He'd told himself the smallness was freedom. Standing on the ice with the pressure of seven years finally cracking open, he understood it had been the opposite. It had been a dam. And dams don't protect anything. They just postpone the flood.

The flood came.

It started in his chest—a crack like the first thaw, then another, then a hundred—and rolled outward through him in a wave that stole his breath. The wind hit the pass before he finished raising his arms. Not mountain wind. *His* wind, the wind he'd been born carrying, full of salt and lightning and the green smell of the coast he'd been exiled from, and it tore up the crevasse and hit the grey cloaks like the fist of a god who'd been sleeping off a grudge.

Three of them went over the edge instantly. No screams. The crevasse took them the way it took everything: silently, hungrily, done.

The rest stopped walking.

For the first time, they made a sound besides humming. A kind of static, a hiss, a chorus hitting a note it hadn't rehearsed—and Tamsin felt it land on him like a searchlight. Every hollow face beneath every hood turned toward him at once, and he felt himself become *visible*, truly visible, for the first time in seven years, and gods help him, it felt magnificent.

The storm built. He fed it everything—the fear from the pass, the dread from Lowmark, the shame of the trial, seven years of practiced invisibility—and the sky above the black walls ripped open and poured out its answer. Lightning walked the ridge lines. Snow rose off the ice in spirals and hung there, defying gravity, glittering in the strobing light like the stars the Choir had eaten, and Tamsin stood in the middle of it with his arms spread and his heart hammering out a rhythm wilder than any song, and he thought: *oh. Oh, this is what I am.*

He'd forgotten. That was the unforgivable part. He'd spent so long being small that he'd forgotten the size of his own weather.

"Hold the melody!" Ilen shouted, somewhere behind him, tiny against the roar. "Whatever happens—hold the melody!"

Tamsin didn't ask what he meant. Later. Everything later. Right now there was only the storm and the grey shapes staggering in his wind, and one of them raised a hand—not a weapon, a *gesture*, palm out, and the empty-grey color leapt from its fingers and struck the storm itself.

The lightning dimmed. The wind stuttered. Where the grey touched his storm, the storm forgot how to be a storm—winds unraveling mid-gust, thunder collapsing into silence, whole spirals of snow going blank and drifting down like ash.

Anger answered it. Pure, blinding, seven-years-deep anger, and the storm roared back to life around him with a sound like the sky clearing its throat. He threw a bolt that split a boulder the size of a house. He drove a gust that peeled two more hunters off the cliff wall and sent them tumbling into the white. The pass became a war between his weather and their emptiness, and for one glorious, terrible minute, his weather was winning.

Then he felt it—the pull.

Subtle, at first. A tugging at the edges of the storm, as if something vast had opened a mouth at the far end of the wind and begun to drink. The grey cloaks had stopped attacking. They stood in a half-circle in the screaming snow, hoods up, unmoved by cold or lightning, and they were *humming again*, and the melody had found the frequency of his storm and was pulling it apart note by note, drinking it, siphoning it away into that patient, starved silence.

They weren't trying to kill him. They were trying to *collect* him.

The realization landed colder than the ice. His storm was mighty, but it was finite, and theirs was a hunger with a kingdom behind it. He could feel the drain now, a slow widening spiral, his power streaming out of him and going somewhere he couldn't follow, and somewhere in the roaring dark of his own thunder he heard a voice—not the Choir's, but memory's, Elder Maren's voice from seven years ago, dry and certain:

*A binding wants a voice, not a life. Remember that, boy, when your pride tells you otherwise.*

He didn't know why he remembered it now. He didn't know what it meant. But he knew the drain was winning, and he knew his knees had begun to shake, and he knew that if he kept pouring himself into the sky, there would be nothing left to pour.

"Tamsin!" Odessa's hand closed on his arm. When had she crossed to him? Through the storm, through the wind—he couldn't track her anymore; the world had narrowed to lightning and drain. Her face was pale, snow-whipped, fierce. "Let it go! You're feeding them!"

"If I stop—"

"Then we *run*, you stubborn bastard, that's what running is *for*—"

The crevasse. Twenty feet of nothing between them and the far side. And the storm—his storm, still raging, still magnificent—could carry them. Could carry *him*. He'd ridden winds like these before, in another life, in a body that belonged to someone with a name and an order and a future.

He grabbed Odessa's wrist. Grabbed Ilen's collar. And threw them—all three of them—into the gale.

The wind caught them like a father catching children thrown from a burning window. For one impossible moment they flew, the three of them, over the black mouth of the crevasse, over twenty feet of hungry nothing, the storm bearing them up with the last of its strength—and then the far side rushed up and they hit the snow, rolling, tumbling, slamming into rock and each other, and the world became pain and white and the fading scream of wind.

When Tamsin opened his eyes, the storm was gone.

Not defeated—*spent*. He'd emptied himself. The sky above the pass was already smoothing over, closing its wound, and the grey cloaks stood on the far side of the crevasse in a silent row, watching him with their hollow patience. Not following. Not needing to. One of them raised its hooded head, and though Tamsin couldn't see a face, he felt it smile.

*Found you,* the silence said.

Then they turned, all together, and walked back into the rock, and the mountain swallowed them, and the pass was empty except for snow and the three of them, gasping in the aftermath.

* * *

They didn't speak for a long time.

There was no shelter on the far side of the crevasse—just a shallow overhang where the cliff leaned out against the wind, and they crowded beneath it while the light died. Odessa built a fire from damp pine scrub she'd had the foresight to stuff in her pack below the treeline, muttering the whole time about smugglers who planned ambushes but not campsites. Ilen sat with his back to the rock, hands wrapped around his knees, staring into the flames with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like.

Tamsin sat apart, the way he'd sat apart for seven years, and tried to feel the size of what he'd done.

His storms were quiet again—but a different quiet than before. Emptied. Like a room after the furniture's been sold. He reached inward, tentatively, and found only echoes: the ghost of wind, the memory of thunder. Days, maybe, before he could call even a trickle. Weeks before anything true.

And they knew. That was the arithmetic underneath everything. Somewhere in Vaelhar, the Choir wearing the king's face now knew exactly where he was, exactly what he was, exactly how much he had left. The invisibility he'd built his whole exile on—the small magics, the false name, the careful grayness of his life among smugglers—was gone. Burned off in ninety seconds of glory on a frozen pass.

"You're sulking," Odessa said.

"I'm calculating."

"You're sulking with numbers." She dropped down beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched, and pressed a tin cup of something hot and terrible into his hands. "Drink. You look like a wrung-out rag."

He drank. It tasted like pine needles and regret. "They weren't trying to kill us," he said.

"No."

"They were trying to collect me."

"Yes." She stared into the fire. "Ilen says the Choir doesn't destroy what it can use. It files things away. Voices, mostly." A pause. "Storm-callers most of all, apparently. You're rare, Tamsin. Rare things get collected."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be. Comfort's not my department." She bumped her shoulder against his, briefly, an old gesture from an older life. "Surviving until morning is my department. You handle the lightning."

He almost smiled. Then the smile faded, because the firelight chose that moment to catch the far wall of the overhang, and he saw the marks there—thin lines scratched into the rock, ancient, deliberate, arranged in rows.

Ilen saw them too. The old man leaned forward, breath catching. "Those aren't graffiti," he said softly. "Those are notation. Storm-notation. First-order notation." He was on his feet before Tamsin could react, tracing the lines with trembling fingers. "This is a counter-song. Fragmentary, but—it's a counter-song. Someone stood in this exact spot, generations ago, and wrote down how to sing *against* the Choir."

The fire popped. Wind moved in the dark beyond the overhang, and somewhere far off—too far to be real, too near to ignore—Tamsin thought he heard humming.

"Why here?" he asked. "Why would anyone write that here?"

Ilen's finger stopped on a line of notation, on a symbol Tamsin recognized from childhood lessons he'd spent seven years trying to forget. The old man looked up, firelight deepening the hollows of his face.

"Because whoever wrote it was running too," he said. "And I think—" he swallowed, "—I think they were heading for the capital. Same as us. And I think we need to move tonight, because that hum you hear isn't the wind, and the things that made it now know your name."

Odessa was already packing. Tamsin looked at the notation one last time—at the counter-song, at the hope some dead runner had scratched into stone—and felt the exhilaration of the storm still singing in his emptied bones, shadowed now by the certainty of what it had cost.

Visible. Collected-in-waiting. Named.

He stood, and shouldered his pack, and followed the others into the dark.
