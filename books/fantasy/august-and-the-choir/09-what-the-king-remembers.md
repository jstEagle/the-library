## 9. What the King Remembers

The silence has a texture. That's the thing no one tells you. Aldric Vaelhar, third of his name, Shield of the Marches, Warden of the Winter Gate, had imagined silence his whole life as an absence—blank, restful, the space between notes. He had been wrong. The Choir's silence is a substance. It fills the ears like wax. It fills the skull like floodwater. It has weight, and the weight increases every day, and today—whatever today is—it is nearly more than he can lift.

He surfaces the way a drowning man surfaces: all at once, violently, with no warning and no guarantee of staying up.

*Now*, he thinks. *Move now. You have no idea how long you have.*

His eyes open. The throne room is dim. Candles burned down to puddles—someone lit them, or they lit themselves, or the candles are a memory he's having rather than a thing that happened. The great windows show a sky the color of a dead hearth. No stars. There haven't been stars for— he tries to count and the number slides away from him like fish through fingers.

He is standing at the window. He does not remember walking to the window. That's the first horror, and he has learned not to look at it directly, because looking at it directly is how the water comes back.

*Ninety seconds*, he tells himself. *Maybe less. Don't waste them on grief. Grief is a luxury of men with time.*

His hands are wrong. He knows this the way you know your own name—immediately, completely, and without being able to explain it. They are too still. They hang at his sides with the patience of things that have been set down. When he wills them to move they obey, but slowly, like servants who have grown used to another master.

*Write*, he thinks. *You always meant to write. How many times have you surfaced and thought* write *and then stood here admiring the sky like a man at his own funeral?*

He turns from the window. The throne room stretches away from him, vast and candlelit and empty, and the emptiness is not empty. He can feel them in the walls. In the stone. In the mortar between the stones. The Choir does not live in his head, whatever he told himself in the first weeks. It lives in the palace. It lives in the mountain under the palace. It lives in the bedrock of the world, and it is singing, and the song is not sound, it is *permission*—permission to rest, permission to stop, permission to let the long dark carry you the way a river carries a leaf.

Somewhere in the castle a servant is humming. He can hear it through six feet of granite, which should be impossible, and is.

*Write.*

There is ink on the council table. There is always ink on the council table; the council still meets, or something that looks like the council still meets, twelve men and women with his face's vacant calm who nod when he nods and smile when he smiles and sign what he signs. He has watched his own hands seal orders he does not remember dictating. He has watched himself open the border wards with a word in a language he does not know, and weep while doing it, and the weeping felt like weather—something happening *to* him, not *from* him.

The quill is where quills are. Small mercies. The world's furniture has not moved. Only the world has.

He dips the quill. His hand shakes. Good, he thinks, *good*, shaking means it's mine, shaking means the hand still belongs to Aldric Vaelhar and not to the thing wearing him like a crown of wet stone.

He writes on the back of a grain ledger, because paper is paper:

*I am still in here. I am still—*

The word he wants is *king* and he cannot make himself write it, because what sits on the throne is not a king, and the thing in his chest that used to answer to the word has gone quiet as a banked fire. He strikes through it. The ink drags. His hand is heavy, so heavy, as though the quill were a plough and the page a frozen field.

*I am still in here. If anyone reads this: the song is old. Older than the crown. Older than Vaelhar. It was bound once—bound by the storm-callers, the first of them, before the order became what it became. The binding is failing. That is not a metaphor. I can feel it failing the way you feel a rope fraying, strand by strand, and every strand that parts is a star.*

*The stars are not dying. They are being eaten. It eats them to sing. It needs voices to sing with and it has taken mine and I cannot—*

His hand stops.

Not because he wills it to stop. Because the water rises.

It comes the way it always comes: from below, from behind the eyes, a slow warm pressure that is almost gentle, and the gentleness is the worst of it. If it were cruel he could hate it. If it were cruel he could fight. But it is a lullaby. It is his mother's voice and his first nurse's voice and the voice of a girl he loved at nineteen whose name he can no longer— no. *No.* Her name was—

The candles gutter.

*Fight*, he tells himself. *Ninety seconds. You've had ninety seconds before. You had them and you spent them standing at the window like a fool. Spend this one better.*

He bends over the page. Sweat drops from his forehead onto the ledger, blooming in the grain-dust. His hand moves like a man swimming upstream, each letter a stroke against a current the weight of a sea.

*It wants a life. That is what the old order taught—that the binding must be renewed with a life, a child, a voice cut short before it learned its own song. That is what they did. That is what they did for a thousand years and called it duty. But I have been inside the song. I have been inside it for— I have been inside it, and I know its hunger, and its hunger is not for blood.*

He is crying now. He notes it distantly, the way you note rain. The tears fall on the page and he writes around them.

*The binding wants a voice, not a life.*

*Do you understand? It was never meant to be fed. It was meant to be answered. A lock is not fed. A lock is answered—by the right key, in the right hand, turned willingly. The first storm-callers did not give it a death. They gave it a song. A counter-song. A voice that said: we hear you, and we are not afraid, and we will sing with you until you sleep. It is a starving thing, not a hungry one. There is a difference. A hungry thing takes. A starving thing—*

The word *waits* will not come.

His hand hovers. The letters swim. Somewhere far below him, deeper than the cellars, deeper than the roots of the mountain, the Choir turns over in its sleep and begins, very softly, to hum.

*It waits*, he writes, forcing it, dragging each letter up from a depth where words have already begun to dissolve. *A starving thing waits. And what it waits for is a voice given freely. Not taken. Not cut. Not a child on a stone. Given. That is the whole of it. That is the secret the order buried under a thousand years of ritual and ash and small graves.*

*Whoever finds this: do not give it a death. It has had enough of those. Give it a voice. Give it—*

The water rises past his chin.

He feels it happen and he hates it and he loves it, and that is the trap, that has always been the trap. The Choir does not conquer. It *comforts*. The pressure behind his eyes is warm. The humming in the walls is rising toward him like a tide coming home, and part of him—part of him, God forgive him, part of him leans into it, because the song is rest, and he has been so tired for so long, tired since before the song, tired since the war, tired since the winter his daughter—

His daughter.

He has a daughter.

The thought comes up out of the dark like a body rising in a flooded cellar, and he grabs it with both hands, and for one blazing moment the water recedes an inch, because *he has a daughter*, her name is— her name is— she used to sit on the arm of this throne and demand he tell her about the stars, and he would tell her about the stars, and she would say *Papa, who names them*, and he would say *we do, little bird, we do*, and she would laugh, and the laugh sounded like—

The name is gone. He reaches for it and the water closes over the place where it was, smooth and warm and total, and he understands with a clarity that feels like a blade that this is how it will take everything: not all at once, but name by name, laugh by laugh, until Aldric Vaelhar is a room with the furniture removed.

*Not her*, he thinks. *Whatever else it takes. Not her laugh. I'll keep the laugh if I lose everything else. I'll keep the laugh.*

He does not know, even now, whether the laugh is hers or his own invention. That is what the song does. It doesn't steal your memories. It *edits* them, gently, lovingly, the way a mother edits a child's story before bed—smoothing the sharp parts, humming over the holes, until what remains is soft enough to sleep inside.

*Write*, he thinks. *You have seconds. Write.*

The quill has torn the page. He doesn't care. He writes over the tear, huge, ugly, the letters of a man carving rather than writing:

*GIVE IT A VOICE NOT A LIFE*

*NOT A LIFE*

*A VOICE*

The last word trails off into a scratch, because his hand has stopped being his.

He feels the moment of it the way you feel a door close in another room—a change in pressure, a settling of air. The warmth comes up through the soles of his boots. The humming in the walls resolves into melody, and the melody resolves into meaning, and the meaning is simple and sweet and lies: *rest now, it is finished, you have done enough, rest now.*

*No*, he thinks, from very far away, from the bottom of a warm dark sea, looking up at the light getting smaller. *Not finished. Someone is coming. I felt the wards break and I felt something else break with them, something that sounded like glass and something that sounded like— like a storm remembering itself. Someone is coming. I wrote it down. I wrote it down. Whoever finds the ledger—*

The thought does not finish. Thoughts no longer finish. They are taken from him mid-stride, gently, like a parent lifting a sleeping child from a cart, and carried away into the warm.

His body straightens. His face smooths. The tears on his cheeks dry in the candle-heat, or seem to; nothing in the throne room is entirely subject to physics anymore. He turns from the table with the ledger on it—face-down now, the words hidden, the grain-counts showing, the most important sentence in the history of Vaelhar sleeping under a list of barley—and he walks back to the window, and he stands at the window, and he looks out at the starless sky with an expression of mild and gracious contentment that the court painters would have called *majestic*.

Inside, at the very bottom of the drowned dark, in a space no larger than a coffin, in a space that is smaller every day, something that is still, against all evidence, the King of Vaelhar does the only thing left to him.

He counts.

He counts the things he can still name. The mountain: Thornfell, that's Thornfell, the passes, the salt-roads. The sea: the Vail, grey in autumn. The city below: Vaelhar, his, theirs, its bells used to ring off-key on festival days and no one ever fixed them and he loved that, he loved that the bells were wrong, because wrong bells meant the city was alive. His wife's face: still there. Fading at the edges like a photograph left in sun, but there. His daughter's laugh: there. He guards it. He stands over it in the dark of himself with his arms out like a man defending a doorway, and the water comes, and he holds, and he holds, and he does not know how much longer he can hold, and he holds.

Above him—far above, beyond the palace, beyond the walls, beyond the mountains where the sky has gone out—somewhere, a storm is remembering itself.

He cannot know this. The song does not let him know things. But at the very bottom of the drowned dark, the king smiles, a small and secret smile that the Choir does not notice, because the Choir does not watch for hope. Hope is not a threat to a thing that eats stars.

Hope is only weather.

And weather, the king thinks, in the last clear thought of the night, weather always finds a way through.

* * *

In the morning—or what the palace calls morning, the hour when the candles are replaced and the humming servants bring bread no one tastes—a chamberlain enters the throne room and finds His Majesty at the window, radiant and vacant, gazing at the dark.

Behind him, on the council table, a grain ledger lies face-down in a puddle of dried tears.

The chamberlain straightens it. He squares its corners. He does not open it; one does not open the king's papers; one has not opened the king's papers in two years, not since the night the stars began to go out and the king stopped being worth listening to.

He carries it to the records room and sets it on a shelf with four hundred identical ledgers, in a flooded cellar of a castle in a kingdom going quietly to sleep, and he closes the door, and the dark inside the records room is total, and patient, and full of voices.

And far away in the Thornfell mountains, in a smuggler's road that shifts with the mountain's mood, a man who used to call storms wakes from a dream of drowning, gasping, with a melody in his ears he does not recognize and cannot stop humming—

—and does not yet know that it is the counter-song, or that a dead king wrote him a letter in barley-counts, or that the girl asleep beside the fire has been humming it all night in her sleep, soft as starlight, patient as the dark.
