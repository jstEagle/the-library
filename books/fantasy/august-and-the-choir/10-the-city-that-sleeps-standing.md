## 10. The City That Sleeps Standing

Vaelhar announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke and bread and river-mud, the ordinary perfume of a living city. Tamsin stood on the last ridge of the Salt Road with the others and breathed it in, and for one stupid moment he was nine years old again, riding his father's cart toward the harvest market, the capital a smudge of gold on the plain.

Then he saw the lights.

Every window in Vaelhar burned. Every streetlamp, every lantern, every candle in every sill — lit at once, at dusk, burning with the steady, unwavering flame of lamps no hand had trimmed. The city glowed like a coal laid on dark velvet, and nothing about it flickered.

"That's wrong," Odessa said quietly.

"Everything about this is wrong," Ilen said. He had his brass astrolabe out despite the cold, taking readings Tamsin suspected were more ritual than science now. "But note it anyway. A city of forty thousand doesn't light itself."

They came down off the ridge as the stars came out overhead — the few that remained, scattered like coins dropped through a torn pocket. Below them the plain spread flat and white with frost, and the road filled gradually with other travelers: farmers with carts, peddlers, pilgrims, all moving toward the glow with the unhurried contentment of people going home to something good.

Tamsin watched them. He'd spent eleven years learning to read faces — smugglers' faces, customs men's faces, the faces of people deciding whether you were worth robbing. These faces told him nothing, because there was nothing to read. No fear, no greed, no fatigue. Just a mild, even pleasantness, like milk.

A woman walking beside their little group turned and smiled at him. She had flour on her hands and a basket of turnips on her hip.

"Evening," she said. "Cold one."

"Cold one," Tamsin agreed.

"Lovely night for it." And she walked on, humming.

Four notes. The same four notes Brann hummed when she didn't know she was doing it. Tamsin's blood went cold in a way the frost couldn't account for.

* * *

They entered through the River Gate just after full dark, mixed in with a wool convoy Odessa had bribed with the last of her silver. The gate guards didn't check papers. They barely checked faces. One of them glanced at each traveler in turn, smiled the same smile the turnip woman had smiled, and waved them through while humming under his breath.

Inside, Vaelhar was worse than the view from the ridge.

The streets were clean. Swept, ordered, free of the usual drift of city filth. Shopfronts stood open past any sane hour, lamplight spilling gold across cobbles, and everywhere people moved — bakers hauling flour, clerks with ledgers, children running errands in the dark — all of it smooth, all of it synchronized, like watching a play where every actor knew their mark perfectly because there was only ever one way to say the lines.

Nobody hurried. Nobody loitered. Nobody argued. At a corner near the chandlers' row, two men stood over a spilled cart of candles, and instead of cursing they simply knelt together and restacked them, smiling, in perfect rhythm, their hands moving like mirrored reflections.

"They're not asleep," Tamsin murmured. "That's the horror of it. They're awake."

"The song doesn't put them down," Ilen said. His voice had gone thin. "It tunes them. Like instruments left in a case too long — still whole, still beautiful, but no longer theirs." He pulled his hood lower. In the lamplight his face looked ten years older than it had in Lowmark's cellar. "I taught half these clerks. I dedicated a star chart to the chandler's daughter's wedding. They'd know me if they looked. So we don't let them look."

They split at the Fountain of Saint Verity, as planned. Odessa took the south quarter, toward the house of the grey-cloaks who held her sister — she carried a token they'd taken from a hunter on the pass, and a face arranged into something suitably hollow. Ilen went north to the astronomers' college, where old colleagues might shelter a dead man for the sake of an old debt. And Tamsin—

Tamsin went home.

He hadn't decided to. His feet decided, carrying him east along the canal path before his mind caught up, and by then it seemed pointless to argue. The Marchetti house had stood on Tanner's Lane for four generations. It would stand tonight. He wanted to see it the way you want to touch a bruise.

The lane was quiet. Lamplit. A cat sat on a wall and did not watch him pass, which was somehow worse than if it had. And there it was: the blue door, the iron boot-scraper shaped like a leaping fish, the window box where his mother had grown thyme every summer of his childhood. Dead now, the thyme. Brown stalks in frozen dirt. But someone had watered the box recently — the dirt was dark.

His chest did something complicated and painful.

He knocked.

It took a long time. Long enough that he almost ran. Then footsteps, even and unhurried, and the door opened, and his mother stood in the warm yellow light with a dish towel over her shoulder.

She was smaller than he remembered. Greyer. Her hands were cracked from years of work he hadn't been there to share. But her face —

Her face was the smile. The vacant, pleasant, tuned-to-perfection smile that the turnip woman wore, that the guards wore, that everyone in this glowing, singing city wore.

"Tamsin," she said.

Not a question. Not a cry. Not a mother's voice breaking on eleven lost years. Just his name, delivered mildly, the way you'd say *evening* or *cold one*, followed by that smile.

"You're home," she said. "How lovely. Come in, I've soup on."

And God help him, he stepped inside, because some part of him was still nine years old and starving, and the house smelled exactly like it used to — rosemary and onions and woodsmoke — and his mother moved through her kitchen humming four soft notes while she ladled soup into bowls, and everything was exactly, precisely, horribly fine.

"Mama," he said. "Mama, look at me. Look at my face. Do you know me?"

"I know you," she said pleasantly, setting the bowl before him. "You're my son. You're home. Eat your soup, love, before it goes cold."

"Do you remember—" His voice cracked. He steadied it. "Do you remember the day I left? The storm? What they did to me?"

The spoon paused, mid-stir. For half a heartbeat — less — something moved behind her eyes. Something vast and deep and drowning, like a face glimpsed far below the surface of dark water. Her hand trembled. The ladle clicked against the pot rim.

Then the surface closed over it.

"You left for work," she said, smiling. "You're home now. Eat your soup."

Tamsin ate his soup. He didn't taste it. He watched his mother hum and wipe the counter and hum, and he understood, with a clarity that felt like a knife going in, what the Choir actually was. Not a monster wearing a king. A monster wearing *everyone*. A lullaby so complete that the people inside it couldn't even feel themselves being sung to sleep, because sleep felt exactly like peace, and peace felt exactly like home.

Somewhere in this city, behind palace walls, the thing that had done this to his mother was rehearsing its final verse. Three days. Maybe fewer.

He set down his spoon.

"Mama," he said gently. "I have to go out again. Work."

"Of course, love." She kissed his forehead — her lips cool, her smile fixed — and at the door she said, "Come home soon," and closed the blue door behind him, and went back to her humming, and Tamsin Marchetti stood alone on Tanner's Lane with tears freezing on his cheeks and the taste of soup he couldn't remember eating in his mouth.

* * *

The rendezvous was a shuttered dye-house by the canal, a smuggler's bolt-hole Odessa had known in another life. Ilen arrived first, pale as paper.

"They know me," he whispered. "That's the trouble. The college let me in — old Pellam recognized me on sight and didn't blink, just handed me my old office key as though I'd been away a fortnight. Eleven years dead, and not one question asked. Tamsin, the archives are *dusted*. My charts are maintained. Someone has been keeping my life going in my absence, tidying it, keeping it ready." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound. "The Choir doesn't erase. It curates."

"And Odessa?" Tamsin asked.

Odessa came in twenty minutes later, soaked to the knees from the canal shallows, and she was shaking — not from cold. She sat down hard on a dye barrel and put her head in her hands.

"I found the house," she said. "The grey-cloaks' house. I got in with the token, no trouble, they barely looked at me — they're so *sure* of themselves here, why would they look? And I saw her." Her breath hitched. "Sera. Through a doorway. She was sitting by a fire with three of them, and she was laughing, and Tamsin — she was *happy*. Genuinely happy. Half-dissolved into that song and happier than I've ever seen her, and I stood in that hallway with a knife in my sleeve and realized I don't know which would be the cruelty. Pulling her out. Or leaving her there, singing."

Nobody answered. There was nothing to answer with.

"So that's the city," Ilen said at last, into the silence. "Three days to solstice. A king who is a mouth, a city that is a choir, and between us and the throne room, forty thousand people who will smile at us and mean us no harm and lift not one finger to be saved." He looked up, and beneath the exhaustion his eyes had gone bright and hard, the astronomer rekindled. "Unless we can find the counter-song in time. Which means tomorrow we move. All of us. Because I found something in my curated archives tonight — a reference, one line, in a marginal note in Aldric's grandfather's reign. A record of the first binding. And Tamsin—"

Ilen leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the fear in his face.

"It says the binding was renewed *annually*. And the record of the last renewal is dated the winter solstice, sixty-one years ago. Whatever the first storm-callers did at that binding — whatever it truly required — the answer is in the palace archive. Behind the king. Behind the Choir. We have to go into the throat of the thing itself."

Outside the dye-house, faint through the walls, Tamsin could hear it: the whole city humming in its sleep, forty thousand voices in perfect harmony, counting down.

Three days.
