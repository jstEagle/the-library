## 11. Sister, Sister

The house on Candlemaker's Row still had the blue door.

That was the first thing Odessa noticed, and it was the thing that nearly broke her before she'd even gone inside. Everything else in Vaelhar had been sanded smooth by the Choir's song—the cobbles scrubbed of grime, the faces scrubbed of worry, the whole city humming at a pitch just below hearing—but the door was chipped and faded and needed repainting, exactly as it had needed repainting for six years, because Sera had always said she'd get to it next spring. Next spring. Next spring. The most honest lie in the world.

Odessa stood across the street with her hood up and her heart going like a trapped bird, and she watched a woman come out of the blue door carrying a basket of washing, and the woman was singing under her breath, and the woman was Sera.

She looked good. That was the obscene part. Odessa had spent two years imagining her sister starved in a cell, chained, weeping, and instead Sera Vane stood in the morning light with color in her cheeks and an easy smile for a neighbor who smiled back with the same vacant warmth, and there was no one guarding her, because no one needed to guard her. You don't post sentries around a woman who has already been taken.

*They said they had her,* Odessa thought. *They said if I broke the wards they'd let her go.* And the wards were broken, and here was Sera, free as weather, walking to a line with washing on it, and Odessa understood with a cold sliding certainty that the promise had never been a lie exactly. They had let her go. Into this.

She crossed the street. Her legs did it without consulting her.

"Sera."

Her sister turned. For one heartbeat—one raw, unguarded heartbeat—something moved behind Sera's eyes. Something that knew her. Something that was drowning and knew it.

Then it smoothed over, like water closing above a stone, and Sera smiled the city's smile. "Good morning! Are you new to the Row? I don't think I've seen you before." Her voice was warm. It was warm the way a hearth is warm when the house around it has burned down.

"It's me," Odessa said. "It's Dess."

"Sera," the woman said pleasantly, and held out her hand to shake.

Odessa took it. Her sister's hand was soft. Sera had worked rope and rigging and ward-chalk her whole life; her hands had been hard as his. Whatever lived in her now didn't do work. It just sang.

"I came back," Odessa said. She heard how her voice cracked and hated it. "I came all the way back, Ser. Through Thornfell, through the passes, through—" She stopped. Sera was looking at her with polite, patient attention, the way you look at a stranger telling you about their dreams. "Do you remember Lowmark? Do you remember the winter the roof went, and we slept in the taproom, and you stole the landlord's slippers?"

Something flickered. Flickered and guttered.

"You should come inside," Sera said kindly. "You look tired. Everyone who comes from outside looks tired. Inside it's easier."

Inside it's easier. Odessa followed her sister through the blue door into a house that was clean in the wrong way—not tidy but *erased*, no dust anywhere, no clutter, no marks of living. The mantel held three candles burned down to stubs and nothing else. No photographs. No keepsakes. On the kitchen table sat two cups of tea, both full, both cold, and Odessa understood that Sera poured them every morning for guests who never came, or perhaps for versions of herself that no longer existed, and then forgot them.

"Sit," said Sera. "I'll make tea."

"You have tea."

Sera looked at the cups. A small crease appeared between her brows, the first true expression Odessa had seen on her face, and it was confusion, and it was almost human, and then it smoothed away again. "I'll make tea," she repeated, and did, humming.

Odessa sat with her hands flat on the table to keep them from shaking, and she watched her sister move around the kitchen, and she made herself look. Really look. Because Ilen had told her what to look for, and Tamsin had told her what he felt when the song got close, and neither of them had prepared her for this: that the Choir didn't devour people the way fire devours a house. It devoured them the way the sea devours a coastline. Slowly. Gently. Leaving the shape intact long after the substance is gone.

There was a place behind Sera's left ear—a faint silvery mark, like frost ferns on glass—that hadn't been there six years ago. There was the way her breathing kept time with something, a rhythm slightly slower than her heart. And there was the moment, pouring the tea, when Sera paused mid-motion, cup tilted, and simply stopped—for four seconds, five—and stood in the kitchen with her eyes fixed on the middle distance and her lips moving around words too quiet to hear, and Odessa knew she was listening. Receiving. Being sung to.

Then Sera blinked and set down the tea and smiled. "You never told me your name."

"Dess," Odessa said. "My name is Dess."

"That's a nice name," said Sera, and drank her tea, and hummed.

* * *

Odessa stayed three days.

She told herself it was reconnaissance. Ilen needed to know how deep the Choir ran in the capital; Tamsin needed to know where the order's chapter house lay; someone had to map the patrols of the grey-cloaked hunters who walked the streets in slow processions, humming, and made the citizens turn and smile as they passed. All true. None of it was why she stayed. She stayed because every night she lay on the cot in Sera's spare room—her old room, though the walls held nothing of hers anymore—and listened to her sister sing herself to sleep, and every night she thought: *I can get her out. There's a way. There's always a way. I'm a ward-breaker; breaking things open is the only thing I've ever been good at.*

And every morning Sera greeted her at breakfast like a guest. "You're still here! How lovely. Are you passing through?"

"Yes," Odessa said, each time, each knife-turn. "Passing through."

On the second day she tried the direct approach. She waited until the hunters' procession had passed the Row, until the street was empty, and she took Sera's hands across the kitchen table and said, "Ser. Listen to me. Two years ago men came and put you in a cellar and told me they'd hurt you unless I broke the border wards. So I broke them. I betrayed everything and everyone, and I've been dying of it ever since, and I came here to get you out. Do you understand? I did it for you. Say you understand."

Sera looked at their joined hands. Then at Odessa. And slowly, gently, with genuine compassion, she pried her fingers free.

"That sounds like it hurt," Sera said. "Why would you do something like that?"

"For you."

"But I'm fine," said Sera, wonderingly, as if Odessa were the one who couldn't see straight. "Everything's fine here. Nobody argues. Nobody leaves. Nobody dies alone in the dark wondering if anyone's coming." She smiled, and the smile was serene and bottomless and terrible. "You should stay, Dess. You'd stop hurting so much. Everyone stops hurting, eventually. That's what the song is *for*."

And there it was. Not a prison. An anesthetic. The Choir had taken the kingdom's grief—the wars, the plagues, the winters, the dead—and offered to carry it, and a starving kingdom had said yes without ever knowing it was saying yes. Odessa thought of the refugees in Lowmark, whispering about soldiers who sang in their sleep, and understood that some of them hadn't fled the fall of the wards at all. Some of them had fled *this*. The ones who could still feel the hook in the melody. The ones whose pain was too stubborn, too particular, too theirs, to be harmonized away.

"The binding wants a voice, not a life," Tamsin had said, repeating the king's scrawled warning, and Odessa had nodded along, but sitting in her sister's erased kitchen she finally understood what the king had meant. The Choir didn't want lives. Lives were fuel, sure, fuel for the song. But what it wanted, what it had always wanted, what it had eaten the stars to get, was consent. Willing voices. A kingdom that sang along.

Sera wasn't a prisoner. Sera was a volunteer who didn't remember volunteering.

On the third night, Odessa found the seam.

She'd gone through her sister's room while Sera was at evening song—the whole neighborhood gathered in the square, hundreds of upturned faces, singing in unison, and Odessa had watched from a window with her skin crawling—and beneath the floorboard under the bed she found a box. In the box: a child's drawing, faded, two stick figures holding hands under a scribbled sun. A broken hair ribbon, the kind they'd worn matching at nine years old. And a letter, unsent, addressed in Sera's handwriting to *Dess, somewhere*, that began: *If you ever come back, I want you to know I fought it. I want you to know that for as long as I could.*

The letter was dated fourteen months ago.

*I fought it. For as long as I could.*

Odessa sat on her sister's bedroom floor with the letter shaking in her hands and understood, finally, completely, that the woman downstairs humming over her cold tea was not Sera making the best of things. She was Sera's body with the doors locked from the inside. And somewhere far down under all that serenity, the real Sera was still there—fourteen months deep, maybe deeper, pressed against the glass, watching her sister walk around the kitchen and not be recognized, screaming without a mouth.

Or she wasn't. Or she was long gone, and the letter was the last true thing she'd ever written, and what remained was just the song wearing her face.

Either way, Odessa realized, the choice was the same. Either way, someone had to decide whether to pull.

* * *

She went to Tamsin that night. He was lodging in the tanner's loft near the river gate, keeping low, and when she laid it all out for him—the blue door, the cold tea, the letter—he listened the way he'd learned to listen since Lowmark: completely, without flinching, saying nothing until she finished.

"Can you get her out?" he asked.

"I can break anything," Odessa said. "That's my whole cursed talent. Wards, locks, bindings, oaths. If there's a seam, I can find it, and if I find it, I can split it." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "But that's the question, isn't it? What happens after I split it. Ilen says the ones who are pulled out early—there aren't many, but he's read accounts—they don't come back whole. The song fills in the gaps. You pull a person out of the Choir and what's left is..." She looked at her hands. "Holes. Holes shaped like everything the song ate. Fifteen years of holes, Tam. Birthdays. Our mother's funeral. The reason she learned to whistle. All of it gone, and she'll know they're gone, and she'll spend the rest of her life reaching for things that aren't there."

Tamsin was quiet for a long time. Down in the yard, the tanner's dogs stirred and settled.

"When I refused the binding," he said at last, "everyone told me the alternative was worse. Let one child suffer or let the whole border fail. Simple arithmetic." His jaw tightened. "It was a lie dressed as arithmetic. But here's the thing I learned in exile: sometimes the arithmetic is real anyway. Sometimes every option costs, and the only choice you actually get is *which cost is yours to carry*."

"So which is it?" Odessa's voice came out raw. "Leave her singing? Leave her happy? She's *happy*, Tam. She's content in a way I have never once been in my entire life. I could leave her there and she'd live out her days in that little house with the blue door, smiling at neighbors, pouring tea for ghosts, and she'd never hurt another day. Is that mercy? Is leaving her in there the loving thing to do?"

"And if it were you in there," Tamsin said quietly, "what would you want her to do?"

Odessa opened her mouth. Closed it.

Because she knew. She knew the way you know your own name. Sera had written it down fourteen months ago, in ink, in the dark, pressing hard enough to tear the page: *I fought it. I want you to know that for as long as I could.* You didn't fight for fourteen months because you wanted to be left comfortable. You fought because you wanted *out*. Even knowing what out might cost. Especially knowing.

"She'd want me to pull," Odessa whispered.

"Yeah," said Tamsin. "I think she would."

* * *

She did it at dawn, because dawn was when the song was thinnest—Ilen had worked that out from the old astronomical tables, the hour when the devoured stars were lowest below the horizon and the Choir's voice stretched furthest to reach its sleepers. Dawn was when the seams showed.

She sat Sera down at the kitchen table, in the chair she'd sat in as a girl, and she took both her sister's hands, and she said, "Sera. I'm going to do something that will hurt. Maybe forever. But you asked me to, fourteen months ago, in a letter you hid under the floorboards, and I am so sorry it took me this long to read it."

Sera smiled her gentle smile. "You're such a strange guest."

"I know," said Odessa. "Family usually is."

Then she closed her eyes and reached for the seam.

Every ward, every lock, every binding she'd ever broken had a seam—a place where the maker's will met the world's resistance, a hairline of tension you could feel with your talent the way a blind woman feels a draft. The Choir's hold on her sister was the largest working she had ever touched, continent-wide, star-fed, ancient beyond reckoning. Finding its seam in one small mind should have been like finding one thread in the sea.

But the Choir was a chorus. And a chorus, whatever else it was, was made of voices. And every voice, however willing, however dissolved, still had to *join*—had to cross over, had to pass through a doorway of its own consenting. Sera had consented once, starving and afraid in a cellar, and the doorway of her joining was still there, scarred into her like an old break in a bone. Odessa didn't have to find a thread in the sea. She only had to find her sister's door, and knock, and break the lock from the outside that her sister had never been given the strength to break from within.

She found it on the third breath.

It felt like frost. Like touching the silver mark behind Sera's ear from the inside. And it fought her—not with malice, nothing so personal, but with *sweetness*, with wave after wave of remembered peace rolling over her talent like warm water: *you could have this, you could stop hurting, everyone stops hurting, come inside, inside it's easier.* It offered her her mother's voice. It offered her the day she and Sera had swum in the quarry lake and neither of them had drowned. It offered her rest.

Odessa Vane had spent her whole life breaking things, and every other time she'd broken something, part of her had loved the breaking. This time she hated it with her entire soul, and she broke it anyway, and that was the difference, and that was the price.

The seam split.

Sera screamed.

Not the scream of the body—the neighbors didn't stir, the song smoothing it over before it could reach the street—but the scream of a woman surfacing from fifteen months underwater into air full of broken glass. Odessa held her sister's hands and poured her own name into the breach, over and over, the way you shout into a well to tell someone falling how far the bottom is: *Dess. It's Dess. I've got you. Dess. I've got you.* And Sera's eyes flew open and they were Sera's eyes, terrified and human and *present*, and for eleven seconds—Odessa counted them, she would count them for the rest of her life—her sister was back.

"Dess," Sera gasped. "Dess, it's so loud, it's so loud without it, how do you stand it, how do you *stand*—"

"I know. I know. Hold onto me."

"Where's—" Sera's face worked. Searching. Reaching. Odessa watched her reach for fifteen years and find nothing, watch the horror bloom as her hands closed on absence after absence after absence. "Where's Mother's funeral. Where's the winter we—Dess, where's *my whistling*, I used to whistle, why do I know that and nothing *else*—"

"I'm here," Odessa said, and her voice broke clean in half. "I'm here, Ser. I'll be your whistling. I'll be all of it. I'll tell you everything, every day, I swear it—"

But the song had noticed. Of course it had noticed. Eleven seconds of open sky in its seamless heaven, and the whole chorus turned toward the wound. Odessa felt it coming up the street like weather—the hunters' hum sharpening, converging, the melody bending toward Candlemaker's Row with the focused attention of something vast and patient—and she saw it hit her sister's face, saw the sweetness roll back in like a tide, saw Sera's eyes start to smooth over even as she clawed at Odessa's wrists and said, fast, desperate, spending her last human seconds like a dying woman spending coin:

"The catacombs—under the Chorister's Hall—there's a door the old choirboys used, Dess, the *old* song, the first binding, it's *written* there, I can hear it under everything, it's the one note they're afraid of—"

And then the tide closed, and her sister smiled at her, warm and vacant and gone.

"What a lovely visit," said Sera Vane. "You must come again soon."

* * *

Odessa made it three streets before she stopped walking and put her fist through a wall.

She stood in the alley with blood running down her knuckles and shook, and did not cry, because crying would have meant it was over, and it wasn't over, it would never be over, because her sister was alive twenty feet from a blue door and dead behind the eyes and *alive*, both at once, permanently, and there was no rescue coming. She had done the brave thing and the right thing and the loving thing, and the reward was that Sera now knew what she'd lost, and would lose the knowing again by nightfall, and might retain—might, if the seam healed ragged, if Odessa's breaking left scars the song couldn't smooth—fragments. Shards. Eleven seconds' worth of terror and a single sentence about catacombs.

Fragments were not rescue. Fragments were worse than nothing, almost. Almost.

Almost—except.

Except that Sera had spent her eleven seconds not on goodbye. Not on *why*. Not on her own agony. She'd spent them on intelligence. On a door under the Chorister's Hall. On the first binding, written, hidden, feared. On the one note the Choir was afraid of.

Even drowning, even shattered, even hollowed to a shell with a stranger's serenity poured in—her sister had spent her last free breath trying to save the world.

Odessa pressed her bleeding hand against her coat and started walking toward the tanner's loft, faster and faster, until she was running, because Tamsin needed to hear this tonight, all of it, and Ilen needed to hear this tonight, and somewhere under the Chorister's Hall there was a door, and three days remained until the solstice, and her sister had bought them a fighting chance with the only currency she had left.

Behind her, faint and sweet, rising from a hundred open windows across the waking city, the Choir began its morning song.

Odessa Vane did not sing along.
