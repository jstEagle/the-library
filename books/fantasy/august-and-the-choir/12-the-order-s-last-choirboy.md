## 12. The Order's Last Choirboy

The chapter house was supposed to be a rumor.

That was how the order liked it—every chapter house a rumor, every road to it unmarked, every initiate sworn to forget the way home. Tamsin had been taken there at nine years old, blindfolded on a mule, and had spent three days afterward memorizing the turns anyway because he was Tamsin Marchetti and being told not to know something had always made him want to know it more.

He'd expected the memory to have rotted. Twenty years of exile, twenty years of deliberately not thinking about the place where they'd tried him and cut his name out of the rolls. But the mountain remembered for him. His feet knew it. The switchback above the frozen waterfall, the cairn with the lightning-split stone, the hollow where the pines grew in a perfect ring like a congregation.

"It's real," Brann said beside him, and he startled, because she almost never spoke aloud anymore. She signed most of what she said, her hands quick and certain, but sometimes—when something mattered enough—the words came out rough and rusted. "You thought you dreamed it."

"I hoped I dreamed it," Tamsin said.

Ilen Corvane leaned on his walking stick and squinted up at the ring of pines. The old astronomer had barely slept since the catacombs of Lowmark; he'd spent the whole climb muttering calculations about the Choir's rate of consumption, and now his lips were still moving, working through some final figure before he let himself look at anything else. "There should be wards," he said. "A chapter house of the storm-caller order would be warded so heavily that even I would feel it from a mile out. My teeth would ache."

Tamsin felt for them. That was the thing about being born to storm-calling—you never stopped feeling for wards, the way a man who'd once broken his leg never stopped testing ice. He reached out with that quiet, listening part of himself, the part that had gone silent the night the stars went out.

Nothing. Not silence-the-way-wards-silence-when-they're-working. Silence the way a room goes silent when everyone in it is dead.

"They're not down," he said. "They're *gone*. Like someone pulled the thread all the way through."

Odessa Vane stood a little apart from them, arms crossed against the cold, looking at the pine ring the way she looked at locks she hadn't decided whether to pick or break. "Could be an ambush," she said. "Your order knew you were exiled, not dead. They hear you've come back with a ward-breaker and a court astronomer, they might want a conversation."

"The order doesn't ambush," Tamsin said. "The order *deliberates*. There are forms. There are committees." He heard the bitterness in his own voice and didn't try to sand it off. "I sat through six months of deliberation before they voted on me."

"And then threw you into the snow," Odessa said.

"And then threw me into the snow."

Brann touched his sleeve. When he looked down, her hands were moving: *Go first. We watch your back.*

So he went first.

* * *

The door was oak, iron-banded, older than the kingdom it predated, and it stood open by a hand's width.

Snow had drifted across the threshold in a smooth white tongue, undisturbed. Whatever had happened here had happened before the last snowfall—days ago, maybe a week. Tamsin pushed the door and it swung without a sound, hinges recently oiled, and that small domestic detail hit him harder than any carnage could have: someone had cared for this door. Someone had oiled these hinges every season for four hundred years, and then stopped mattering.

Inside, the entry hall rose two stories, its rafters hung with the old storm-bells—bronze, each one tuned to a different kind of weather, meant to be rung in sequence when the binding needed renewal. They hung motionless. Dust lay on them like grey fur.

The smell reached him next. Old blood has a particular sweetness, iron gone over to rot, and under it something else—the ozone ghost of spent lightning, soaked so deep into the stone that it would never wash out.

"Stay behind me," Tamsin said, and for once nobody argued.

He walked the hall the way he'd walked it as a boy, and his body kept betraying him with recognition. There was the bench where he'd waited for his first lesson. There was the seam in the flagstones where Elder Corrin had tripped during a demonstration and laughed—laughed, actually laughed—and Tamsin had understood for one shining moment that these grim grey-robed judges were people. He'd spent years chasing that moment, trying to find the people inside the institution, right up until the day they'd put a child in front of him and told him to open his veins alongside hers.

The refectory doors were open too.

He didn't go in. He looked, and that was enough. Nine bodies, maybe ten, arranged wrong—not fallen, *arranged*, laid out in rows as if someone had wanted the room tidy afterward. Grey robes. The order's colors, storm-grey shot with silver thread. Their hands were folded on their chests.

"They didn't fight," Ilen said quietly over his shoulder. "Look. No scorch marks, no broken furniture. The Choir's hunters don't arrange bodies."

"No," Tamsin said. His voice came out flat and strange. "But the Choir doesn't need to. If the hunters came humming, if the brothers and sisters just... set their work down and lay themselves out..." He thought of the refugees in Lowmark, the soldiers who sang in their sleep, the whole border country smiling its vacant smile. "The song doesn't kill you. It just makes you agreeable."

Brann's hand found his again. Her grip was fierce.

He counted the robes. Twelve. The Thornfell chapter had held thirty-one souls when he'd left it. Where were the other nineteen? Taken singing into the mountains? Walking toward Vaelhar with empty eyes and harmonies in their throats?

He found the answer at the end of the east corridor, in the scriptorium, where the order kept its true records—the ones even initiates weren't shown. The room had burned. Recently, thoroughly, professionally: someone had stacked the ledgers and fed them page by page, and the fire had been hot enough to crack the slate floor. In the corner, half-melted, lay the bronze frame of the great weather-glass that had hung there since the founding, the instrument that showed the health of the binding at a glance.

Tamsin stared at it and felt something in his chest turn over.

Because he'd seen that glass every day of his childhood. And it had always, *always*, read green.

* * *

She was in the chapel.

He nearly walked past her. She sat in the last pew with her back straight and her hands folded, an old woman in storm-grey, and for one disorienting second Tamsin saw her as the girl she must have been—small and upright in a hard wooden seat, learning to hold still the way the order taught you to hold still, stillness as devotion, stillness as armor.

Then she turned her head, and it was Maren. Elder Maren. The woman who had read the sentence.

"You took your time," she said. Her voice was dry as bellows-ash. "Twenty years, Marchetti. I half believed you'd learned sense at last."

Every plan Tamsin had made on the walk up—the questions, the accusations, the cold controlled fury he'd rehearsed like a counter-song—all of it went out of him at once, and what came out instead was raw: "Where is everyone?"

"Gone." She looked back at the altar, where the order's sigil was carved into the stone: a bolt enclosed in a circle, the storm bound within the world. "Some of them are walking to Vaelhar. Some of them are lying in the refectory. I put them there. It seemed—" For the first time, her voice caught. "It seemed wrong to leave them where they fell."

"You *arranged* them?"

"I closed their eyes. Folded their hands. Yes." She turned to face him fully, and he saw then what the dimness had hidden: her wrists, wrapped in linen stained brown. Self-inflicted or punishment or penance, he couldn't tell, and hated that he couldn't tell. "You can hate me for it. Everyone else did, at the end. The ones the song took, they smiled at me while they lay down. Do you understand? They *smiled*. Halvard was humming the third harmony and he looked at me like I was his mother, and he lay down on the cold stone because the song asked him to, and I could not—I was not going to leave him like a dropped tool."

Behind him, soft footsteps: Odessa, Ilen, Brann filing into the chapel doorway. Maren's eyes tracked them, cataloguing. Ward-breaker. Astronomer. Child.

"A ward-breaker," Maren said, and something moved through her face that might have been laughter, if laughter could die and haunt its own corpse. "We hunted your kind for two centuries, Vane. Burned the guild rolls. And now one walks into our house carrying our last exile like a lantern."

"People change," Odessa said. Her voice was ice with a current under it. "Institutions don't. Isn't that the lesson here?"

Maren looked at her for a long moment. Then, slowly, painfully, she rose from the pew and stood, and Tamsin braced for the old authority, the voice that had sentenced him, the full weight of the order deployed like weather.

What she said was: "Ask me."

"What?" Tamsin said.

"Ask me what we knew. Ask me when we knew it. You've carried the question for twenty years—I can see it in you, boy, you've been carrying it since the trial, the question underneath all your other questions: *did they know? Did they know what they were asking of me?*" Her hands trembled at her sides. "So ask. I have been sitting in this chapel for six days waiting for someone to ask, and God help me, no one has come, and I am starting to think I will have to say it unsanctioned, unblessed, out loud, like a woman and not an office."

The chapel was very quiet. Somewhere far below, water dripped in the flooded cellars, keeping time.

Tamsin asked. "Did you know the binding was failing?"

"Yes," Maren said.

"How long?"

"Forty-one years."

The number went through the room like a blade through wet canvas. Ilen made a sound low in his throat. Odessa swore, softly, fluently, in three languages.

"Forty-one years," Tamsin repeated. "The binding started failing when I was *learning my letters*."

"The weather-glass began showing amber in the spring of the year the comet passed. You wouldn't remember; you were five. The Grand Chapter convened in secret. We agreed the finding was preliminary. Then it stayed preliminary. Year after year. Amber, then deep amber, then—" Maren's mouth twisted. "There is no word for what it showed in the last decade. The scale only went to red. We commissioned a new scale. We spent eleven years commissioning a new *scale*, Marchetti. Subcommittees. Correspondence between chapters. A formal debate on whether the glass itself might be miscalibrated, as though the glass were the problem."

"Why?" The word tore out of him. "Why hide it? Renewal is the whole—the entire *purpose*—"

"Renewal requires a life." Maren said it simply, the way you'd state the load-bearing wall of a building. "A young life, given willingly, bound into the chorus. That was the rite. That was always the rite. And forty-one years ago, the order looked at that price and flinched. So we hid the bill." She met his eyes, and her own were terrible—clear, unflinching, lit from behind by everything she'd refused to see. "And then the years passed, and the flinch hardened into doctrine, and the doctrine hardened into silence, and by the time you came before us with your refusal, the order had spent two decades telling itself that the binding was fine, that the glass was wrong, that renewal rites were archaic superstition best left dormant. Your trial wasn't a trial, boy. It was a *relapse*. One elder—Corrin, bless her—moved to reinstate the rite using you and the Vane-border girl as candidates. The rest of us voted her down and voted you out, because voting you out let us pretend the question was about your insubordination and not about our cowardice."

"The girl," Tamsin said. His mouth had gone numb. "Sera. What happened to Sera?"

"She lived," Maren said. "Grew up, married a ferryman, has three children in Lowmark, if Lowmark still stands. She was never told how close she came. None of them ever are." A pause. "You were the only candidate in the order's history who refused. Did you know that? Two hundred years of renewals. Every one of them willing. And then you."

"Don't," Tamsin said.

"You stood in that hall and you said *find another way*, and we cast you into the snow for it, and every year since, the binding has gotten worse, exactly as you said it would—"

"*Don't.*" His voice cracked the word in half. Brann pressed against his side, a small solid weight, and he put a hand on her shoulder and gripped until it hurt him. "Don't you dare make me the vindicated one. I didn't refuse because I saw the truth. I refused because I was angry and frightened and seventeen and I couldn't do it, that's all it was, I couldn't *do it*—and you're going to stand there and rewrite it into prophecy because that's what institutions do, isn't it? They take a person's worst night and mint it into doctrine!"

"Yes," Maren said. "That is exactly what institutions do. And then the institution rots around the doctrine, and the doctrine keeps its shape after everything living inside it has died, and one day the hunters come up the mountain humming, and the doctrine cannot save anyone, because a doctrine is not a voice." She looked down at her bandaged wrists. "The Choir didn't defeat us, Marchetti. It didn't even have to sing, not really. We'd already silenced ourselves. Forty-one years of it. The song just finished the job."

* * *

They sat her by the chapel's small fire while Ilen examined the melted weather-glass and Odessa walked the corridors alone, checking what Odessa always checked—which ways out, which ways in, who else might be coming.

Brann sat next to Maren. Tamsin watched, unable to stop it, as the old woman looked at the mute girl for a long time and then said, quietly, "Do you hum?"

Brann nodded.

"Sing it for me," Maren said. "The Choir's melody. I want to hear it from someone it hasn't taken."

And Brann—Brann, who trusted no one, who had needed three weeks to trust *them*—hummed four bars of the Choir's song into the ruined chapel, sweet and clear and unafraid, and Elder Maren closed her eyes and wept without sound, the way the order had taught her, stillness as devotion, stillness as armor, and none of it helped her at all.

When she opened her eyes, she looked at Tamsin.

"There's something you need," she said. "Something the fire didn't get. Corrin hid it the year before the hunters came—she'd finally read the first-binding texts properly, the originals, not the sanitized copies. She said the rite we'd been performing for two centuries was wrong. Corrupted. She said the original binding never wanted a life at all." Maren's voice dropped, and the words came out like a confession extracted under torture. "She said it wants a *voice*. Willingly given. Held by memory. And she hid the proof somewhere on this mountain, and she died before she could tell me where, and I have spent six days searching this house and I cannot find it, because I am old and slow and I was never the one she trusted."

Tamsin felt the ground tilt under the world. *A voice, not a life.* The sentence rang in him like a struck bell, familiar in a way he couldn't name yet, the way a melody is familiar before you remember where you learned it.

"Where would Corrin hide it?" he asked.

Maren almost smiled. It was the saddest thing he'd ever seen. "You knew her better than you think, boy. Think. Where did she take you, when you were small and the lessons were too heavy? Where did she go, when the deliberations ran long and the elders wore her down?"

And Tamsin closed his eyes, and his feet answered before his mind did—his feet, which had never forgotten a single turn of this mountain—carrying him down the east corridor, past the burned scriptorium, to the low door beneath the stairs, the one that led out onto the ledge above the frozen waterfall, where a woman had once sat with a tired boy and taught him that storms were not weapons but *voices*, and that the sky had been singing long before anyone thought to sing back.

The loose stone was exactly where she'd shown him, twenty-five years ago, when he'd thought she was only teaching him to hide.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, was a book. And pinned to the book's cover, a note in a hand he recognized, addressed to no one and everyone:

*To whoever refuses next —*
