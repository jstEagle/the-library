## 13. A Voice, Not a Life

Ilen Corvane had spent forty years learning how the sky lied.

Stars dimmed and brightened on schedules no honest thing would keep. Comets returned wearing different faces. The whole heavens were a performance, and his life's work had been catching them in the act. He had been good at it. Good enough that the court had given him a tower, a title, and eventually a reason to fake his own death and crawl into a flooded cellar beneath Lowmark with the only books that mattered.

So when he finally understood what the first binding actually said — not what the order claimed it said, not what three centuries of frightened scholarship had layered over it like sediment — he did not shout. He did not weep. He sat very still in the ankle-deep water of the cellar-library and laughed once, a short ugly sound, because the truth was so much simpler than the lie that he felt personally insulted by it.

*It wants a voice. Not a life.*

He read the passage again. The old Vaelharic was dense, pre-imperial, the script of people who wrote as if parchment were rationed by the word. But the grammar held. *A voice freely yielded, held in memory, kept in love.* Three clauses. Three conditions. No blood anywhere in the sentence.

The corruption came later. Of course it came later. Fear always arrived after courage and moved into its house.

Ilen lifted his head from the codex and looked across the cellar at Tamsin Marchetti, who was standing watch at the flooded stairwell with the particular stillness of a man who expected the dark to have opinions. Odessa Vane sat sharpening a knife she had already sharpened twice. And Brann — small, silent Brann, who had hummed the Choir's melody without being hollowed by it — sat cross-legged on a dry crate, watching him with those grave dark eyes, waiting for him to finish reading as if she already knew how it ended.

"Master Corvane," Odessa said, without looking up from her blade. "You've made that face before. In the mountains, when you worked out the wards were failing from the inside. It's the face of a man about to say something we won't like."

"It's the face of a man about to say something you won't *believe*," Ilen corrected. "Which is worse, but more survivable."

He stood. His knees cracked. Forty years of tower stairs and one decade of cellar damp had exacted their toll, and he resented every joint that announced itself. He waded to the center of the chamber, where a guttering lamp threw their shadows tall against the drowned shelves, and held up the codex like a priest raising a relic.

"The binding," he said. "The original binding. The one the first storm-callers laid on the Choir before Vaelhar had kings, before it had this capital, possibly before it had a name." He turned the pages toward them. "Every account we have — every chronicle, every order doctrine, every fireside version — agrees on one thing: the binding must be renewed with a sacrifice. A life, given into the song. That's why the order took children. That's why they took *you*, Marchetti, or wanted to."

Tamsin's jaw tightened. He said nothing. Ilen had learned that about him: the man treated silence like currency and never spent it cheaply.

"That's wrong," Ilen said. "Not morally wrong. *Textually* wrong. A mistranslation compounded by cowardice, preserved for three hundred years because admitting the error would have meant admitting the order had been murdering children for nothing."

The knife stopped moving. Odessa looked up.

"Read it," Tamsin said quietly.

Ilen read it. His voice, trained for lecture halls and observatory domes, filled the flooded cellar with words older than the kingdom:

"*A voice freely yielded, held in memory, kept in love. So long as the given voice is remembered, the Choir is bound. So long as it is loved, the Choir sleeps.*"

Silence. Water dripped somewhere in the dark, patient as a metronome.

"A voice," Odessa repeated. "Not a life."

"Not a life." Ilen let the codex lower. "Look at the conditions. Freely yielded — the voice cannot be taken, coerced, or bargained for. Held in memory — someone must remember the one who gave it, or the binding frays. Kept in love—" he paused, because the next part was the part that explained everything, "—and here is where the order went astray. They read *kept in love* as a cost. As something owed. But it isn't a cost. It's the *mechanism*. Love is what holds the door shut. Memory is what keeps the lock oiled. The binding doesn't run on death. It runs on being *remembered*."

"Then why children?" Tamsin asked. His voice was flat, controlled, the voice of a man holding something down. "Why did they—"

"Because a child's voice is pure," Ilen said, "and a child is easy to take, and taking is easier than asking. Somewhere in the second century, a generation of storm-callers faced a renewal and were afraid — genuinely afraid, the way institutions are afraid, which is to say completely and without conscience. They couldn't find a voice *freely yielded*, so they took an unwilling one and dressed it in liturgy. And it *worked*, Marchetti. That's the horror of it. An unwilling voice binds too — badly, leakily, requiring constant renewal, like a dam patched with bodies. The order built a machine that ran on murder and called the murders holy necessity. And when the machine began to fail — as all such machines fail — they hid the failure rather than question the machine."

He watched Tamsin absorb it. Watched the exile do the arithmetic he'd been doing since the trial: *they knew. They always knew. My exile was the price of their lie.*

"You refused," Ilen said. Not a question.

"I refused."

"And they exiled you for refusing to feed a corrupted binding a child." Ilen allowed himself the smallest, driest smile. "Congratulations. You were right. It will bring you no comfort whatsoever, and you'll receive no apology, because the institution that owes you one is currently a massacre site. But you were right."

Odessa set the knife down. "Then the solstice. The Choir completing its song. If the true binding needs a willing voice—"

"Then the counter-binding needs one too," Ilen finished. "Yes. That's the shape of it. The Choir is starved, Vane. Starved for exactly the thing it was denied for three centuries — a voice given *freely*. Every stolen voice has been ash in its mouth. It wears Aldric because Aldric's surrender was the closest thing to willingness it's tasted in generations, and even that was despair dressed up as consent. It is a creature dying of a diet of theft." He tapped the codex. "And the cure is written right here, in grammar older than the crime."

"So someone gives their voice," Tamsin said slowly. "Willingly. And everyone else remembers them. And loves them. And that holds it."

"Yes. With one complication." Ilen glanced at Brann. "Two complications. First: the giving must be sung, not spoken. A counter-song against the Choir's own melody, note for note, inversion for inversion. Whoever sings it stands inside the Choir's attention while they do it. The Choir unmakes things, Marchetti. Memories first. It will eat the singer's past while the singer stands there offering their future. That's the price now. Not death. *Erasure.*"

"And the second complication?"

Ilen looked at the girl on the crate. She looked back, unblinking, and hummed — soft, four notes, the Choir's own melody rendered small and harmless, a lullaby instead of a leviathan.

"She can hold it," Ilen said. "That's the miracle nobody ordered. The anchor-melody. The binding, original or counter, requires two parts — the voice that yields, and the voice that *holds the line* while the yielding happens. A drone beneath the song. Without it, the counter-song is just noise; the Choir swallows it. With it—" he spread his hands, "—with it, the yielding voice has ground to stand on. Brann carries the Choir's melody without being taken. Which means she can carry ours. She is, functionally speaking, the only tuning fork in the world pitched against the end of everything."

"No." Odessa was on her feet before the word finished leaving her. "No. Absolutely not. We are not putting a child in front of that thing. I've seen what it leaves of people. I've *pulled* what it leaves of people out of—"

"Vane." Ilen raised both hands. "Listen to me carefully, because I am about to defend your position using my own argument. Nobody puts her anywhere. She *chooses*. That is the entire mechanism — free yield. If she is coerced, frightened, used, the binding fails and likely takes us all with it. Your protectiveness isn't just decent. It's *structurally required*. The moment anyone treats that girl as a tool, the whole architecture collapses." He softened his tone, which for him meant lowering the volume by perhaps ten percent. "But ask her. Ask her properly. Explain it to her as you'd explain it to an adult, because she is one, whatever her size, and then let her decide."

Odessa looked at Brann. Brann looked back. Then the girl did something Ilen had not seen her do before: she nodded, slow and deliberate, and patted the crate beside her — an invitation, unmistakable, for Odessa to sit and be talked to like an adult.

Odessa sat. Ilen turned away to give them privacy and found Tamsin beside him at the shelf, eyes on the codex.

"How long do I have to learn it?" Tamsin asked.

Ilen blinked. "Learn—"

"The counter-song. You said it's sung. Note for note. Inversion for inversion." Tamsin's voice stayed level, but Ilen had spent decades reading the sky, and skies and men lie in the same ways. "You're looking at me, Corvane. You've been looking at me since you said the word *voice*."

"Because you're the only storm-caller left," Ilen said simply. "The binding was always renewed by storm-callers. The counter-song rides the same current — it needs a throat that can carry weather. Yours is dormant, not dead. I've watched it flicker. And because—" he hesitated, then decided honesty was cheaper than tact, "—because you've already given everything else. Your name, your home, your order. You are the most practiced volunteer for loss I have ever met. God forgive me for noticing."

Tamsin huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Three days."

"Solstice is in three days. Yes."

"Can I learn a song in three days that took the first storm-callers a generation?"

"They had a generation and no deadline," Ilen said. "You have a deadline and no alternative. Deadlines are excellent teachers." He put the codex into Tamsin's hands — the old man's arms were tired, and the book was heavy with more than paper. "We start tonight. Brann holds the drone. You learn the inversions. And Marchetti—" Ilen caught his arm before he turned away. "One more thing. The Choir eats memories. While you sing, it will come for yours. So spend these three days *giving them away*. Tell Odessa your mother's face. Tell Brann the smell of salt. Fill other people with yourself. Because if the binding holds — if love and memory hold the lock — then everything you give away comes back to you through the ones who kept it."

Tamsin looked down at the codex. Then at Odessa and Brann, heads bent together over the crate like conspirators planning something gentle.

"And if it doesn't hold?"

"Then we'll all be singing harmony in the dark," Ilen said, "and I will die profoundly annoyed at having been right twice in one week."

Above them, muffled by stone and floodwater, the city of Lowmark went on humming. Three days to solstice. Somewhere north, the king slept standing in his crown of silence, and the stars waited, patient as debts, to see whether anyone still remembered how to give a gift.

Ilen blew out the lamp, opened the codex to the first page of the counter-song, and began, in the dark, to teach.
