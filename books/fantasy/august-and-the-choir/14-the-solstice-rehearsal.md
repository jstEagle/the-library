## 14. The Solstice Rehearsal

Three days to solstice, and the catacombs beneath Vaelhar smelled of candlewax and old stone and, faintly, of the sea — a smell that had no business being two hundred miles from any coast. Ilen said the founders of the city had built the vaults over an ancient cistern, and that water remembered what it carried. Tamsin thought it was more likely that someone down here was smuggling salt, same as he'd once smuggled salt, because some trades never died no matter what empires rose or fell above them.

He was thinking about salt because thinking about salt was easier than thinking about singing.

They'd cleared a chamber off the third gallery — a round room with a domed ceiling painted with constellations so old that half of them no longer existed, their stars eaten, their shapes forgotten except here in paint. Ilen had wept a little when he first saw it, quietly, in the way he did everything: as if embarrassed by his own body's insistence on feeling things. Now the astronomer stood at the room's center with chalk in hand, drawing lines on the flagstones that matched the faded sky overhead.

"The binding was laid out like this," Ilen said, tapping a circle of seven points. "Seven voices. The first storm-callers sang in harmony with the Choir itself — not against it, *with* it. That's what everyone forgot. You don't silence a thing like that by fighting it. You give it something to harmonize with, and it forgets to be hungry."

"And when the harmony fails?" Odessa asked. She sat on a sarcophagus lid with her boots up, whittling a piece of kindling into nothing. She'd been doing that for three days. There was a small drift of wood shavings at her feet now, like snow.

"Then it eats," Ilen said simply. "It has been eating for thirty years. Slowly. A star at a time. A king at a time." He looked at Tamsin. "A voice at a time."

Tamsin didn't answer. He was looking at Brann.

The girl sat cross-legged on the floor near the one brazier, humming. She hummed almost constantly now — softly, under her breath, a melody with no beginning and no end that made the candle flames lean toward her like flowers toward sun. It should have been terrifying. It was the Choir's own song coming out of a nine-year-old's mouth. But there was something in the way she held it, the way you might hold a bird — gently, without gripping — that made it feel less like a haunting and more like a lullaby.

She caught him looking and stopped humming long enough to smile at him. Then she started again.

"Right," Odessa said, dropping her boots down and standing. "We've got three days, a dead woman's notation, a mute child, and a storm-caller who hasn't sung since before I broke my first ward. What could possibly go wrong?"

"You broke your first ward at nineteen," Tamsin said. "You told me it was an accident."

"It was an accident." She grinned. "I'm counting on you to be one too."

* * *

The problem, Ilen explained, was the high harmony.

The counter-song had seven parts, but six of them were anchors — holding tones, steady as bedrock, the kind of singing that required patience rather than skill. Brann could hold the anchor line alone; she'd been born holding it, apparently, the way other children were born with brown eyes. But the seventh part, the crown of the song, was the counter-melody itself — the voice that rose above the others and turned the Choir's hunger back on itself. That part needed a storm-caller. That part needed him.

"That part needs training," Ilen said, for the fourth time that morning. "Storm-calling is not singing. The order taught you to throw your voice like a weapon — to crack stone, to call lightning. This is the opposite. This is asking the sky to hold still."

"I know how to be quiet," Tamsin said.

"No," Ilen said, not unkindly. "You know how to hide. Those are different things."

Tamsin wanted to argue. He didn't, because the old man was right, and they both knew it, and there wasn't time for pride. Three days. Somewhere above them, the city slept standing up, smiling its vacant smile, and somewhere above that, the stars hung eaten and dark, and somewhere above even that — if stars could be said to have an above — the Choir wore a king like a crown and waited for the longest night to finish its work.

"From the top," Ilen said. "Brann, anchor first."

Brann hummed. The note filled the dome, round and warm, and the painted constellations seemed to brighten, which Tamsin knew was impossible and refused to question anyway. Ilen raised two fingers, counted them down, and nodded at Tamsin.

Tamsin breathed in, found the pitch Ilen had drilled into him for two days, and began to climb.

The counter-melody rose in thirds against Brann's anchor, then fifths, then — at the crest, where the old notation showed a mark like a bird in flight — it leapt. An octave and a fourth. A note written for a voice trained from childhood, for lungs that had never breathed smoke, for throats that had never swallowed exile.

His voice cracked.

Not badly — not the humiliating adolescent squawk of a boy's changing years — but enough. The note splintered, wavered, fell apart between one heartbeat and the next, and Brann's anchor wobbled with it, sympathetic as a struck string, and Ilen closed his eyes with the expression of a man watching a bridge fail under load.

Tamsin stopped. The silence rang.

"Again," he said.

"Tamsin—"

"*Again.*"

"You've done it eleven times," Odessa said from her sarcophagus. "You'll do it twelve, and thirteen, and you'll still crack on the fourteenth, because you're singing it like a man climbing a wall instead of a man walking through a door." She stood, crossed the room, and put one wood-shaving-dusted hand flat on his sternum. "Breathe here. Not up here." She tapped his throat. "You're strangling the note before it's born. Let it come out already alive."

"You don't sing."

"I break things for a living. Same anatomy." She stepped back. "Again. And stop trying to be the boy the order wanted. He's dead. Sing like the man who replaced him."

That shouldn't have helped. It helped.

He breathed low, the way she'd shown him, and Ilen counted them in, and Brann's anchor rose warm and patient beneath him, and this time when the melody leapt, Tamsin didn't reach for the note. He let it arrive. It came out thin and clear and slightly rough at the edges, like wind through a doorway, and it held — two beats, three, five — before it frayed.

But it held.

Brann looked up at him with an expression of grave professional assessment, the way a master mason looks at an apprentice's first arch. Then she shook her head, reached out, and pressed two fingers lightly against his throat, adjusting the angle of his chin by a fraction of an inch.

She hummed the passage again, slower, demonstrating — not mocking, never mocking, just showing, the way water shows a stone what shape to become. Then she let go of his chin, folded her hands in her lap, and waited.

"Patient as a teacher," Ilen murmured. "Where did a child learn patience like that?"

Tamsin thought about the refugee caravan on the pass. About the weeks of walking, of silence, of watching adults panic around her while she sat calm as deep water. About whatever she'd seen that took her voice and left her this instead.

"She learned it the hard way," he said. "Same as the rest of us."

* * *

They rehearsed until the candles burned down to puddles.

By the second day, Tamsin could make the leap eight times out of ten. By the evening of the second day, something stranger had begun to happen: the six of them — himself, Odessa, Ilen, Brann, and the two Lowmark smugglers who'd volunteered as extra anchors, Ferro and his wife Sela, neither of whom could carry a tune in a bucket but both of whom could hold a single note like a grudge — had begun to sound like a *group*. Not seven voices performing one song. One voice with seven mouths.

Odessa's anchor was the surprise. She claimed she couldn't sing, and technically she was right — her pitch wandered, her tone was rough — but she held her line with the stubbornness of a woman holding a door against a mob, and nothing moved her. Nothing. Once, mid-rehearsal, a rat ran across her boot and she didn't so much as flicker. Ferro swore afterward that her note hadn't bent by a hair.

"That's the whole secret," Ilen said, delighted, pacing the chalk circle like a professor on the best day of his career. "Do you understand? The original binding didn't fail because the singers were weak. It failed because the order made it cruel. They kept trying to replace love with discipline — discipline holds a note, yes, but only for a lifetime. Love holds it longer than that." He gestured at all of them, at the shabby circle of them, the deserter and the criminal and the fraud and the mute child and two salt-smugglers. "This is why it will work. Not because we're good. Because we mean it."

Sela laughed. "Speak for yourself. I'm here because Ferro volunteered me."

"You're here because you'd have killed me if I'd left you above ground," Ferro said.

"Also true."

It was, Tamsin realized, the first time he'd heard anyone laugh in Vaelhar. Real laughter, not the vacant choir-hum of the sleeping streets. It echoed strangely off the dome, up among the dead constellations, and for a moment the catacombs felt less like a tomb and more like a kitchen — some vast, improbable kitchen where a strange family had gathered to argue about bread.

* * *

On the last night — solstice eve, though none of them said the word aloud — Ilen called an early end to rehearsal.

"Rest," he said. "Tomorrow the voice is all you'll have. Sleep, eat, don't talk about the plan, don't look at the ceiling like it's going to tell you anything new." He paused at the gallery door, chalk dust on his sleeves, and added, quieter: "And thank you. All of you. Whatever happens tomorrow — I spent forty years believing knowledge was the strongest thing in the world. I was wrong. It's this. Sitting in a hole in the ground with people who refuse to leave each other."

He left. Ferro and Sela followed, arguing amiably about whose turn it was to fetch water.

Which left three of them in the constellation room: Tamsin, Odessa, and Brann, who had fallen asleep sitting up against the sarcophagus, mid-hum, her anchor note dissolving gently into breathing.

Odessa draped her cloak over the girl and sat back down. For a while neither of them spoke. The candles guttered. Above, faintly, through fathoms of stone, the city hummed its empty harmony, and beyond that, the eaten sky waited.

"Do you remember," Odessa said eventually, "the night we met?"

"You mean the night you robbed me."

"The night I *divested you of excess inventory*, yes." Her mouth twitched. "You were seventeen. You had a knife you clearly didn't know how to use and a purse you clearly didn't deserve. And when I pinned you against the wall of the tannery, you looked at me — not scared, not angry — *relieved*. Like you'd been waiting years for someone to catch you."

Tamsin stared at the floor. "I don't remember saying that."

"You didn't say it. You didn't have to." She picked up another piece of kindling. "I've thought about that a lot, these past weeks. About how we find people. I spent twenty years telling myself I worked alone. Ward-breakers work alone. Then I broke the wrong ward for the right reasons and lost everything, and the only person in the world who came looking for me was a smuggler I'd robbed once." She shrugged. "Found family isn't found, Tamsin. It's *kept*. Every day. Like a fire."

Brann stirred in her sleep. Without waking, without opening her eyes, she resumed humming — soft, automatic, the anchor note rising out of her like breath. Even asleep, she was holding the line.

Odessa watched her for a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — lower, careful, the voice she used for things that mattered.

"If tomorrow goes wrong—"

"It won't."

"If it goes wrong," she repeated, "I want you to know I'd do it again. The wards. All of it. To get her back." She nodded at Brann. "And I want you to know that whatever the Choir takes from you tomorrow — and Ilen says it takes, Tamsin, he read the accounts, it *takes* — whatever it takes, we'll be standing right here when it's done. However long that takes. However many nights it takes to say your story back to you." She looked at him straight on. "You gave up everything once already, walking out of that order. Tomorrow you give it up again. Nobody should have to pay that price twice alone."

Tamsin's throat was tight. He managed: "You've gotten sentimental in your old age."

"I've always been sentimental. I just used to rob people about it."

He laughed — really laughed, for the first time since Thornfell — and the sound went up into the painted dark among the dead stars, and Brann hummed in her sleep, and for one impossible moment the catacombs felt warm all the way through, like a house, like a home, like the thing he'd spent fifteen years insisting he didn't need.

Then the candles went out.

All of them. At once. No draft, no guttering — one instant flame, the next darkness total, and Brann's humming stopped mid-note, which had never happened, not once, not even in sleep.

In the black, very far away — or very close, it was impossible to tell — something began to sing back.

Odessa's hand found his arm in the dark. "Tamsin," she whispered. "Tell me that's not what I think it is."

Above them, through the stone, through the sleeping city, the hum of Vaelhar changed key.

The solstice had come early.
