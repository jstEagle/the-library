## 15. Odessa's Price

Odessa had broken a hundred wards in her life, and every one of them had taught her the same lesson: everything that holds can be made to let go. Stone, iron, oath, blood. You found the seam. You pressed. Things came apart.

She'd never learned how to put anything back together.

Three days before solstice, she sat in the catacombs beneath Vaelhar with her back against a wall that hadn't been built by human hands—older than the city above it, older than the kingdom—and watched Brann hum.

The girl sat cross-legged on the cold floor with Ilen's transcription spread out before her, notes scratched in his cramped astronomer's hand, and she didn't read them so much as taste them. Her lips moved without sound. The melody came through her anyway, low and steady, a thread of sound you felt in your teeth more than heard. Tamsin stood across from her, eyes closed, learning the shape of it. Every time he sang the counter-line back, his voice cracked on the high harmony, and every time Brann paused, patient as stone, and gave it to him again.

*Again,* she'd say—or rather, she wouldn't say it at all, because Brann didn't speak, but her hands would make the small circular gesture that meant *again*, and Tamsin would sigh and do it again.

They were going to be ready. That was the terrible thing. They were actually going to be ready.

Which meant Odessa had exactly one job left, and it was the one she'd been avoiding since they'd come down into the dark: deciding what to do about Sera.

* * *

Her sister was three streets away and half a world gone.

The Choir had taken Sera eleven months ago—not violently, nothing so crude. Odessa had come home from a contract in the borderlands and found her sister setting the table for two, smiling that new soft smile, humming under her breath. The food had been perfect. The table had been perfect. Sera had looked at Odessa with love in her eyes and none of Sera behind it, and said, "You're just in time. Sit. It's better if you sit."

Odessa had run. She wasn't proud of it. She'd broken wards on temples and treasuries and one notoriously vindictive duke's panic room, and when the thing wearing her sister's face asked her to sit down to dinner, she had climbed out the window like a thief.

Then she'd spent eleven months telling herself Sera was dead. It was easier. Dead was clean. Dead meant grief, and grief had rules—you buried it, you carried it, you got up in the morning. What she couldn't carry was the alternative: that Sera was alive in there, folded up small somewhere behind the song, aware, waiting, while something else used her mouth.

Ilen said the taken weren't destroyed. He said the Choir kept what it consumed the way a singer keeps a breath—held, not spent. "There's a difference," he'd told her, adjusting his cracked spectacles, "between a dead man and a held note. One of them can still resolve."

So tonight, while Tamsin drilled harmonies and Brann anchored them, Odessa slipped up through the maintenance ways into the sleeping city, and went home.

* * *

The door wasn't locked. Of course it wasn't locked. Nothing in Vaelhar locked anymore; locks were a kind of fear, and fear had been sung out of the city like damp sung out of a cellar.

Inside, everything smelled the same. Rosemary oil on the wood. The lye soap Sera bought from the chandler on Corver Street because it was cheap and she liked the way it stung. Someone had kept the house. Kept it *well*—dusted, ordered, the clock on the mantel ticking in perfect time, which was its own kind of horror, because Sera had never once in her life owned a clock that ran true.

"Sessa." The voice came from the kitchen doorway, warm as banked coals. "I knew you'd come back before the singing."

Sera stood there in an apron, flour on her hands, and she looked—gods help her—she looked *good*. Better than good. Rested. The hollows under her eyes were filled, the sharpness smoothed away. Twenty-nine years old and serene as a saint in a shrine painting.

"You're not my sister," Odessa said.

"No," Sera agreed pleasantly. "But I remember being her. Would you like tea? I remember how she makes it. Too strong. She always boiled it angry."

"Stop it."

"I'm not mocking her." Something flickered across the smooth face—a crack, a hairline fracture, there and gone. "That's the thing you don't understand, Sessa. We don't erase. We *keep*. Everything she was is still in here. I can show you her ninth birthday. I can show you the name of the boy she kissed behind the tannery. I have all of her, and she has all of me, and we are very, very happy—"

"The binding breaks in three days."

The serenity didn't waver, but the teapot in Sera's hands did. A fine tremor, porcelain clicking against porcelain. Odessa saw it and understood, with a lurch, that she was looking at a hostage negotiation conducted entirely inside one body.

"Yes," Sera said softly. "We know."

"And when it breaks, what happens to the people you're holding?"

"They'll be released." A pause. A too-long pause. "Mostly."

"*Mostly.*"

"Some of them have been held a long time." The crack widened; Odessa could see through it now, could see something small and terrified pressing against the inside of her sister's face. "Some of them have forgotten how to be people. Some of them—" The voice dropped to a whisper that was pure Sera, eleven months buried and still fighting. "*Sessa. Listen to me. It knows where you are. It's always known. I've been holding it back from the catacombs but I'm not—I was never the strong one, you were always the strong one, and I can't hold it much longer, and if I give it the catacombs it will leave me alone, it promised, it will let what's left of me stay, please, please, I'm so tired—*"

And then the smoothness closed over again like water, and Sera smiled, and set down the teapot, and said in the Choir's warm chorus-voice: "She's lying, of course. Or she isn't. It's so difficult to tell, in here. Shall I tell you what I told her? That if the doors of the catacombs stand open on solstice eve, we will let her keep a corner of herself. A small room. Warm. With a window."

"That's not mercy. That's a kennel."

"It's more than most get." The Choir tilted Sera's head, studying Odessa with her sister's grey eyes. "You should understand something, ward-breaker. We have worn kings and priests and generals, and every one of them fought us the same way your sister fights us, and every one of them lost the same way. But you—we've never had a ward-breaker. The one who unmade the border. Do you know what you could be, inside us? Not held. *Kept*. Honored. Your seams, your craft, given a kingdom to work on—"

"I'd rather be dead."

"Yes," the Choir said, with something almost like respect. "She says that too."

* * *

Odessa walked back to the catacombs through streets full of smiling sleepwalkers, and she did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since Lowmark.

If the Choir already knew where they were—and Sera's terror said it did—then the rehearsal was a trap no matter what they did. Move, and they'd be hunted through a city of eyes. Stay, and the hunters would come down the tunnels on solstice eve with the counter-song half-learned. There was no version of this where everyone walked away, because the Choir didn't need to win a battle. It only needed to interrupt.

But there was one version where the interruption happened early, loudly, and in the wrong place.

One person could hold a tunnel. Ward-breaking wasn't fighting—it was making things hold, or making them let go, and Odessa Vane had spent twenty years learning how to make stone refuse passage. Give her a choke-point and an hour's preparation and she could build a door that would take a dozen grey-cloaks the better part of a night to open. Long enough. Long enough for Tamsin and Brann and Ilen to finish, or die trying, or whatever happened at the end of songs.

The cost was written on the other side of the ledger, and she made herself read it. Whoever held the door would be behind it when it finally failed. And the Choir wanted her specifically—the first ward-breaker, the honored seam. If she was standing in the tunnel when it broke, they would not kill her. They would *keep* her.

She thought about Sera's window. A small room. Warm.

"No," she said aloud, to the empty street, in the old Vane family tone that had ended arguments since before either of them was born. "No windows."

* * *

She told them at dawn, flat and fast, the way you pull a tooth.

Tamsin took it worst, which surprised her until she thought about it. Of course he did. This was a man who had wrecked his whole life rather than let an order feed a child to a binding. Sacrifice-for-the-cause was the exact shape of the thing he'd spent ten years running from.

"No," he said. "Find another way."

"There isn't one. I've looked. I'm better at finding seams than anyone alive, and there isn't one."

"Then we move the rehearsal—"

"—and they follow us, and we fight them in the open with a half-trained storm-caller and a mute girl, yes, brilliant, I can see why the order exiled you for stubbornness and not talent." She softened it with a hand on his arm. "Tamsin. Listen. I'm not doing what the order did. Nobody's feeding me to anything. I'm choosing a door, and I'm choosing to stand in it, and those are different things. You taught me that, actually. You and your stupid beautiful refusal."

"You could fail," he said. "The door could break early."

"Everything breaks eventually. I'm a ward-breaker. Breaking things is the family trade." She grinned at him, and it cost her more than the whole speech had. "Hold the high harmony. Don't crack on it. That's your price to me."

Ilen said nothing at all, which from Ilen was a eulogy. He just took off his ruined spectacles, polished them on his sleeve, put them back on, and looked at her over the rims the way masters look at students who have finally understood the lesson. Then he bowed. Actually bowed. Old court manners, dusted off for her.

Brann crossed the floor and took Odessa's hand and pressed it flat against her own sternum, over the humming, and held it there. *Feel. Remember. Carry.*

"Yeah," Odessa said, and her voice came out rough. "Yeah, little anchor. I'll carry it."

* * *

Solstice eve came down grey and windless, the sky over Vaelhar the color of dirty wool, not one star left anywhere in it.

They chose the tunnel themselves: the flooded gallery off the old cistern, where the passage narrowed to shoulder-width and the ceiling came down low. Odessa worked through the afternoon, laying her craft into the stone the way her mother had taught her—seam by seam, pressure balanced against pressure, a lock with no key except patience. By dusk it was beautiful. She stood back and looked at it the way other women looked at finished embroidery.

Tamsin stood beside her. Neither of them spoke for a while.

"When this is done," he said finally, "if it works—if we're both somehow still—Lowmark's going to need its wards rebuilt. Proper ones. I know a disgraced ward-breaker who owes me a drink."

"She doesn't owe you anything. You drank hers for a month in '09."

"Then she owes me a rematch."

Odessa laughed. It came out real, which annoyed her. "Go sing your song, storm-caller. And Tamsin—" She caught his sleeve. "When they come, you'll hear me. However it goes, however it ends. You'll hear me. That's not a promise, exactly. It's more like a map."

He frowned at her, not understanding, and she didn't explain, because explaining would have meant admitting her hands were shaking.

* * *

They came at moonrise, and they came humming.

She heard them long before she saw them—the melody bleeding down the flooded gallery, dozens of voices braided into one, wrong in the way a reflection is wrong. The water at her feet shivered with it. Her wards shivered with it. Grey shapes filed into the far end of the tunnel, torchlight sliding off their cloaks, and at their center walked a figure wearing her sister's walk.

"Sessa," the Choir called, gentle, carrying. "Open the door. She's so tired. Let her rest."

"You let *her* rest," Odessa said, "and I'll consider it."

"We could offer you the window again."

"I told her. No windows."

She pressed her palms to the stone and let go of everything she'd built, all at once, deliberately—not breaking the ward but *spending* it, every seam igniting at once, and the tunnel roared. Stone ground against stone. The gallery sealed itself into a throat of rock, and the first rank of hunters slammed into a wall that had not existed an hour ago, and Odessa Vane stood alone in the dark with maybe forty minutes of craft between the Choir and the end of the world.

It bought her an hour. She'd underestimated herself, which was, she decided, the nicest thing she'd ever done for anyone.

They didn't rush her. That was the horror of it—they simply began to *unpick*, patient as tide, the Choir's own counter-craft flowing into her seams and loosening them thread by thread, and Odessa sat down on the wet stone with her back to the sealed door and did the only thing left that was hers to do.

She started to sing.

Not well. She was a ward-breaker, not a choirgirl; her voice was smoke-rough and habitually sarcastic. But she knew the melody now—three days of Brann's humming had planted it in her deeper than craft—and she sang it loud, and she sang it *her* way, bent and defiant, a signature scrawled across the Choir's careful script. And she knew—Ilen had explained it, and she'd pretended not to listen—that sound carried in the tunnels, that the flooded gallery acted like a throat, that everything she sang down here would roll up through the bones of the mountain into the catacombs where a storm-caller was learning a counter-song.

*You'll hear me,* she'd told him. *That's not a promise. It's a map.*

Let him track her by it. Let him know exactly which tunnel, exactly which hour, exactly what it cost. Let the last thing the Choir consumed of Odessa Vane be a song it hadn't authorized.

The wall behind her groaned. Cracked. Light spilled through the fissure, grey and choral, and the humming swelled to fill the world.

Odessa stood up. Brushed off her knees. Sang louder.

The last thing she thought, before the grey took her—not the last, she corrected herself, the *held*, the note that could still resolve—was of Sera at nine years old, missing a front tooth, teaching her the words to a sailor's song their mother hated.

She sang that one too.
