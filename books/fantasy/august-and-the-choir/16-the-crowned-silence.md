## 16. The Crowned Silence

The palace of Vaelhar had no guards.

That was the first wrong thing. Tamsin had spent three days learning its rhythms from a rooftop across the square—watching servants come and go with their vacant smiles, watching the king's banners hang motionless in air that should have been full of winter wind—and in all that time he had not seen a single spear, a single patrol, a single locked gate. The Choir did not need soldiers. It had made the whole city into a garrison that didn't know it was one.

He went over the wall at dusk on solstice eve, when the sky was the color of an old bruise and not one star showed through it. The stone was cold under his palms, familiar in a way that made his chest ache—he'd climbed worse walls for less reason, back when he was nobody, back when being nobody was the whole point. He hooked his fingers into a seam between blocks and pulled himself up hand over hand, breath fogging, boots finding purchase by feel.

Below him, Vaelhar glowed. Not torchlight—the city burned almost nothing now—but a pale, even luminescence rising off the streets like heat shimmer inverted, like light falling upward. The citizens walked through it without casting shadows. They smiled at each other. They hummed.

Tamsin knew the melody now. He hated that he knew it. Three weeks on the road, two nights in the catacombs rehearsing Ilen's counter-song until Brann's patient humming corrected him note by note, and the Choir's tune had lodged itself in his skull like a splinter. He caught himself humming it while filling the water skins. He caught himself humming it in his sleep. Odessa had shaken him awake twice already, her face grey, because he'd been singing harmony in a voice that wasn't quite his own.

*It gets in,* she'd said. *That's how it works. That's how it got my sister.*

He reached the top of the wall and lay flat against the cold stone, letting his breathing slow, and looked out at the palace proper: a sprawl of white towers and black slate roofs, the great hall crowned with a dome of green copper gone silver with frost. At its peak, where a weather-vane should have been, something hung in the air that cast no shadow at all—a darkness shaped like a crown, or like a hole in the shape of one.

The king was in there. What was left of the king.

*The binding wants a voice, not a life.* Aldric's ninety seconds of sanity, scrawled in a shaking hand and smuggled out by a laundress who didn't remember doing it. Ilen had wept over the paper. Odessa had read it four times and said nothing, which for Odessa meant it had gone in deep.

Tonight they would find out what a voice could do.

But first Tamsin had to cross the throne room floor without forgetting why he'd come.

* * *

Ilen's maps said there was a servant's corridor behind the kitchens, running under the great hall, used in the old days to move wine and scandal without either passing through public rooms. The door to it was hidden behind a rack of copper pots, and the pots were still there, polished bright by hands that moved in perfect unison, and when Tamsin slipped past them into the dark he felt the song change.

Not louder. Closer. Like wading from a river's edge into the current.

The corridor smelled of dust and old wine and, underneath both, something sweet—the smell he'd first noticed in Lowmark, the smell the refugees carried on their clothes. Honey and cold iron. The smell of being sung to.

He counted his steps. Ilen had told him to count his steps. "The song takes sequence first," the astronomer had said, hunched over his charts in the catacomb lamplight, ink-stained fingers trembling. "Memory is a chain, you understand—it doesn't erase, it *unlinks*. You'll lose what comes before before you lose what comes after. So anchor yourself. Numbers don't sing. Count."

Forty steps. Fifty. The sweetness thickened. Somewhere above him, muffled through stone, a thousand throats breathed in together, and the sound of it was so gentle that Tamsin's feet wanted to stop moving, wanted to turn around, wanted to climb up toward it—

Sixty-one. Sixty-two. He dug his thumbnail into the pad of his forefinger, hard, and kept walking.

Odessa had wanted to come with him. Of course she had. He'd argued her down in the catacombs with logic that felt thin as ice: someone had to hold the tunnel, someone had to keep Brann safe, someone had to be outside if everything inside went wrong. But the truth underneath the logic was simpler and uglier. If the Choir peeled him as he approached—if he forgot Odessa Vane somewhere on that throne room floor—he did not want her to see it. Some prices should be paid alone.

Seventy steps. A stairway rose ahead, spiraling up toward the great hall's undercroft, and the air coming down it was warm and sweet and full of voices.

Tamsin stood at the bottom of the stairs and did what he'd promised himself he would do. He took inventory.

"Odessa," he said aloud, barely a breath. *Ward-breaker. Broke the border wards with her hands tied by her sister's life. Laughs like a door slamming. Forgave me before I asked.*

"Ilen Corvane." *Court astronomer who faked his death and then couldn't stop correcting everyone's grammar. Maps the sky like other men map battlefields.*

"Brann." *Small. Mute except when she hums, and then she holds the whole melody like it weighs nothing. Holds me, too, some nights, when the splinter in my skull starts to sing.*

"My name is Tamsin Marchetti." He paused on that one, because it was the one the Choir would want most. An exile's name is a loose thread; pull it and the whole garment comes apart. "I was exiled for refusing to feed a child to a binding. I am going to give that binding something better."

He put his foot on the first stair.

The song took the numbers first.

* * *

He noticed it as a small absence, the way you notice a clock stopping in a quiet house. He had been counting—one hundred and twelve, one hundred and thirteen—and then the numbers were simply gone, like coins slipping through a torn pocket, and he was standing on a landing with no idea how many stairs remained, and the panic that rose in him was pure animal, wordless, a creature scrabbling at the walls of his chest.

*Anchor,* said a voice in his head that might have been Ilen's or might have been his own. *You anchored. Say it again.*

"Odessa." His lips moved. The name came. Relief hit him so hard his knees nearly buckled. "Odessa Vane. She broke the wards because they held her sister. She laughs like—" 

Like what?

He stood on the landing in the warm sweet dark and reached for the laugh and found a shape where the laugh should have been. He knew there was a laugh. He knew it mattered. It was like holding a book in a language he suddenly couldn't read.

"No," he whispered. "No, no—"

*Lowmark,* said the voice. *Start smaller. Start closer.*

"Lowmark." The haven in the mountains, salt-smugglers' rest, the flooded cellar-library where they'd found Ilen alive among drowned books. He could see it—could see the water-stained shelves, the lamplight on wet stone, Ilen's gaunt face emerging from behind a collapsed shelf like a ghost deciding to be flesh. Good. Lowmark held. For now.

But something else was peeling away beneath it, something older, and he felt it go with a physical lurch, like a tooth leaving the jaw: the pass where he'd fought the hunters. The storm he'd called. The terrible bright joy of it, lightning answering his rage after seven years of silence—that was dimming, smoothing over, becoming a story about someone else.

They were taking the storm from him before he'd even given it. Taking the road, the fight, the fear. Unlinking the chain link by link, and each link that fell away made the next one easier to lift, because memory holds memory the way stones hold stones in an arch.

Tamsin climbed. It was all he could do. Climb and say the names.

"Brann," he breathed. "Brann hums. Brann is the anchor. Brann is waiting in the dark with Odessa and Ilen and when I open the way she'll start the melody and I have to be ready to sing the counter-song and I can't sing it if I forget—" 

Forget what? The high harmony cracked him every rehearsal. Brann would touch his wrist, patient as tide, and hum the phrase back until his throat remembered. That was real. That was—

His mother's face.

It went quietly, which was the worst part. No tearing, no grief-cry. One moment he was carrying it the way he always had, folded small against the exile years, the wind-chapped woman with the storm-grey eyes who'd taught him that thunder was just the sky clearing its throat—and the next moment he was reaching for it and finding only the fact of it. *I had a mother. Her face was a thing I knew.*

He stopped on the stairs. He put his forehead against the cold stone wall and shook, silently, the way you learn to shake when you've been invisible your whole life.

*I had a mother. I don't remember her face. I will never remember her face, because I am spending it right now, spending it climbing these stairs, and this is the price, this is the actual price, and Ilen never said—Ilen said the song takes sequence, but he never said it takes the ones you'd die before giving—*

Except he had said it. In the catacombs, on the second night, quietly, while Brann slept against Odessa's shoulder: *"Whatever you carry dearest, spend it last. Spend it slow. And know that you may reach the throne with holes in you that never close."*

Tamsin lifted his head. Above him, through the floor, the Choir sang, and the sweetness of it was unbearable now, honey poured into every crack, and he understood suddenly why the citizens smiled. It wasn't horror that made them smile. It was relief. The song felt like being forgiven for things you hadn't done yet.

That was the trap. That was the whole architecture of it.

He spat on the floor—an exile's gesture, a smuggler's gesture, a human gesture—and kept climbing, and began, deliberately, to lie to himself.

*My mother's face was round,* he told the dark. *She laughed at bad jokes. She named me for a season I never learned.* None of it true, maybe none of it true, but the lie had weight, and weight was resistance, and he would fill the holes the song tore in him with anything he could carry, invented or otherwise, because a man made entirely of the Choir's silence was no use to anyone.

* * *

The undercroft opened onto the great hall through a curtain of grey velvet gone greyer with dust, and beyond the curtain, the throne room waited like the inside of a bell.

Tamsin had expected grandeur. Vaelhar's kings had ruled for nine generations and built accordingly—pillars of veined marble, windows of colored glass forty feet tall, a floor of black-and-white stone laid in a pattern meant to make every visitor feel small. All of it was still there. All of it was wrong. The colored glass showed no colors anymore, just a milky glow, as if the sky outside had been replaced with parchment. The pillars were furred with something pale and fine, like frost, like feathers, like the down inside a bird's hollow bone. And the song, unmediated now, unshielded, filled the hall the way water fills a hull.

He pressed his back to the cold pillar beside the curtain and took inventory again, fast, before the current could take more.

"Odessa." The name came. The shape of the laugh came with it this time—almost. A door slamming. Yes. A door slamming in a good way. He held onto that.

"Lowmark. Ilen. Brann. My name is Tamsin Marchetti and I was exiled for saying no."

Something answered him.

It came from the far end of the hall, from the dais, and it was not words. It was a single note, low and warm, held longer than any throat could hold it, and it moved through the marble floor and up through the soles of his boots and settled in his sternum like a hand. Come, the note said. Come and rest. You've been walking so long. Everyone walks so long. Put it down.

And Tamsin, standing in the shadow of the pillar, felt his body lean toward the dais the way a plant leans toward light, and understood with cold clarity that this was how Aldric had been taken. Not dragged. Invited. A tired man in a big room, and the room offering to carry him.

He stepped out from behind the pillar.

The distance from the curtain to the dais was perhaps eighty paces. He would pay for every one of them. He knew that now the way you know a price posted on a door. Eighty paces, and the song would take whatever it could reach, and he had maybe—maybe—twenty paces of himself left to spend.

He started walking, and he spent as he went.

At ten paces he lost the taste of salt, the first flavor of the smuggling years, the taste that had meant freedom once and then meant survival and then, strangely, meant home. Gone between one step and the next, like a candle pinched out.

At twenty paces he lost Thornfell's passes—not the fact of them, but the feel of them, the particular ache of a climb at altitude, wind trying to peel you off the mountain. He grieved that one audibly, a small broken sound, and kept walking.

At thirty paces he lost the trial. Elder Maren's voice pronouncing his exile, the circle of grey-robed storm-callers, the child he'd refused to surrender standing at the door with wide eyes—all of it smoothed away into a general knowledge that he had once been sentenced to something, by someone, for reasons that had seemed important. The injustice stayed. The details bled out. He clutched at the injustice like a rope.

At forty paces, halfway, the doors at the far end of the hall boomed open.

Grey cloaks. A dozen of them, spilling into the hall in perfect silence, humming as they came—and behind them, held under the arm of the largest like a rolled carpet, a girl.

Brann.

Her mouth was moving. No sound came out—but her eyes found Tamsin across the eighty paces of stolen air, and they were furious, and they were terrified, and they were *his*, and the melody she was shaping with her silent lips was the anchor-song, the first phrase, the one that goes *up* where the Choir's tune goes down.

They'd been taken. The tunnel had fallen. Odessa—

The thought arrived whole and then the song took it, snapped it off clean, and Tamsin stood at the forty-pace mark with a scream building in him and no name to attach it to. Someone had been taken. Someone with a slamming-door laugh. Someone whose name was—

Was—

"*Odessa*," he said, and the name came, ragged but whole, because some things go deeper than sequence, deeper than chains, down where the song's long fingers couldn't reach without an invitation. "Odessa Vane. I'm sorry. I'm sorry I left you in the dark. Hold on. *Hold on.*"

On the dais, the thing wearing King Aldric turned its head.

It had the king's face—the face from the coinage, noble and tired, fifty years of rule carved into it—but the eyes were not eyes anymore. They were apertures. They were the places stars used to be. And when it saw Tamsin standing in the middle of its hall, defiant and half-unmade, its mouth curved into a smile of such vast, patient tenderness that Tamsin's knees finally gave.

It spoke, and the hall spoke with it, and every citizen of Vaelhar spoke with the hall, ten thousand sleeping throats in perfect unison:

*"There you are. We saved you a place in the song."*

Behind the grey cloaks, silent-lipped, tears cutting tracks through the dust on her face, Brann hummed the first phrase of the counter-song—and Tamsin, kneeling on the black-and-white stones with less than twenty paces of himself remaining, opened his mouth to answer her.
