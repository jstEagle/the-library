## 17. Counter-Song

The throne room had no doors anymore. The Choir had eaten them, along with the windows and the banners and the names of every guard who once stood watch, and what remained was a single vast chamber of black stone that hummed like the inside of a struck bell.

Tamsin walked in through the space where the doors used to be, and the song came for him before his second step.

It didn't hit him. That was the terrible thing about it—it *invited* him. It poured into the hollow places he'd spent nine years filling with salt-smell and smuggler's roads and Odessa's profanity, and it began, gently, to empty them.

He kept walking.

Brann was behind him in the doorway, small hands folded over her sternum, her lips parted around the first note of the anchor melody. Ilen knelt beside her with the old binding-score spread on the floor, weighted at the corners with four stones from Thornfell, because even now—*especially* now—the mountain wanted to be part of this. The girl's voice rose thin and clear, a single thread of sound, and where it touched the Choir's song the black stone shivered.

"Good," Tamsin said, not turning around. "Hold it."

She couldn't answer. She never could. But she hummed harder, and the thread thickened.

At the far end of the room, on a dais that might once have held a throne, King Aldric sat slumped in a shape that was only half a man. The other half was light—or what passed for light in a kingdom whose stars had been swallowed—a slow churn of grey radiance that moved like a throat swallowing. The Choir wore him the way a crown sits on a head: heavily, and without love.

*Tamsin Marchetti,* the song said, and it used his own voice to say it, warm as a hearth. *Storm-caller. Exile. You came so far to be quiet.*

"I came to sing," Tamsin said.

His voice cracked on the first note.

Not from fear—from memory leaving. He felt it go the way you feel a tooth come loose: a wrongness, then an absence, then the tongue worrying at the gap. The smell of salt off the Lowmark docks thinned, faded, and was gone, and he knew only that something had been there and that its name had been *salt*, and soon it wouldn't even be that.

The counter-song was in his chest. Ilen had drilled it into him for three days in the catacombs until Brann could correct him by touch alone, tapping his ribs when he went flat. It was built from the same root as the anchor melody, the first binding's true shape: a voice, willingly given, sustained by memory and love. Not a life. Never again a life.

But the Choir would take everything else while he sang it. That was the arithmetic. That was the price written in the margins of Ilen's score in a dead astronomer's cramped hand: *it will unmake you as you unmake it. Sing anyway.*

Brann's melody steadied beneath him like a floor.

Tamsin planted his feet on the black stone and sang.

* * *

The first thing to go was his mother's face.

He felt it leave mid-verse—a warmth behind his eyes, a softness in his jaw where a smile used to live—and then it was simply not there anymore. He knew a woman had loved him once. He knew she'd had flour on her hands most mornings. He could not, for the life of him, find her face in the dark behind his eyelids, and the grief of that loss was so clean and total that it went straight into the song and made the note stronger.

That was the trick of it. That was the filthy, beautiful trick. Every memory the Choir peeled away became fuel for the counter-song, because the song ran on exactly what the Choir was starving for: a voice given freely, paying freely, loving what it lost even as it lost it.

The Choir understood this about halfway through the second verse.

The grey light on the dais convulsed. Aldric's body arched, and the churn of radiance pulled inward, dense and furious, and the whole throne room screamed—not with sound but with *absence*, with a thousand stolen voices trying to harmonize at once.

*You are feeding us,* the Choir said, using his father's voice now, though Tamsin could no longer remember if his father had been kind or cruel. *Every note you lose, we keep. You cannot starve us with your own flesh.*

"I'm not feeding you," Tamsin sang, and the words were the words, the actual words of the counter-song, shaped by the first storm-callers a thousand years ago. "I'm showing you what eating costs."

Because here was the thing Ilen had found in the oldest score, the thing the frightened generations had buried under child-sacrifice and ritual and fear: the Choir had not always been a devourer. It had begun as a hunger, yes—but a hunger that had once been *answered*. Answered by voices willing to give. The corruption came later, when the order decided that willingness was too expensive and children were cheaper. The Choir had been fed coercion for a thousand years, and coercion is a poison, and poison makes monsters.

Tamsin gave it willingness.

He gave it the salt smell, gone now, offered up like bread. He gave it Lowmark's flooded cellar-library and the lantern-light on Ilen's ruined face. He gave it the frozen pass and the storm he'd called down, the intoxicating, terrifying roar of it, and he felt that memory leave him and did not flinch, because a storm is just weather, and weather belongs to everyone or no one.

The Choir drank and drank and grew no quieter.

*More,* it said, in Odessa's voice, and that one hurt worse than all the others, because—

Because.

He reached for her name and found the shelf where it lived and the shelf was bare.

"No," Tamsin said, and stopped singing.

The silence lasted one heartbeat. In that heartbeat the Choir surged forward down the length of the room, grey light flooding across the black stone like oil, and Brann's melody bent and strained, and somewhere behind him Ilen shouted a warning that Tamsin would not remember tomorrow.

Then Tamsin laughed.

It was a broken sound, wet and ragged, the laugh of a man standing in a burned house. Because the shelf wasn't bare. He'd checked the wrong shelf. The Choir had taken the *name*—Odessa Vane, ward-breaker, traitor, friend—but it hadn't taken what the name pointed at, because that wasn't stored anywhere the Choir could reach. It was in his hands, which remembered the weight of her shoulder when she'd slept against them on the shifting road. It was in his ribs, which remembered the tap-tap-tap of Brann correcting his pitch. It was in the stubborn, stupid, unbought thing in his chest that had refused to sacrifice a child nine years ago and refused, now, to stop singing.

"You can take my memories," Tamsin said. "You can't take my loves. They're not mine. They're *ours*."

And he understood, all at once, standing in the dark with his past dissolving around him, what the offering had to be. Not a life—Ilen's score had never asked for a life. Not even the memories, though the Choir would strip those regardless. What the first binding wanted, what it had *always* wanted, was the thing underneath the memories. The self they were carved from.

A voice. A name. A place in the world.

Willingly given.

"Take my name," Tamsin said, and began to sing again.

* * *

The counter-song changed key.

Ilen would have called it modulation; Brann heard it and adjusted her anchor note without hesitation, sliding down a third, patient as tide. Tamsin barely heard himself. He was too busy being unwritten.

The name went first, properly this time—*Tamsin Marchetti* peeling off him like bark off a birch, and beneath it the exile, the trial, the order, the nine years of careful invisibility, all of it offered up in open hands. Then the storms. Oh, the storms—he felt them leave like blood leaving a limb, the pressure that had lived in his bones since childhood draining out through the soles of his feet into the black stone, and the Choir took them and took them and *shook* with taking, because it had never been given anything before. A thousand years of theft, and now, for the first time since the first binding, someone stood in front of it and *offered*.

Hunger does not know what to do with a gift.

The grey light stuttered. The churn slowed. Somewhere inside it, King Aldric made a sound—a human sound, cracked and small—and the Choir's song wavered, because the king's ninety seconds were surfacing, and even drowned, some part of Aldric was watching a stranger give away everything the Choir had ever tried to steal.

*Why,* the Choir said, and for the first time it had no voice to use but its own, and its own voice was very old and very tired and sounded, underneath everything, like a child humming alone in the dark.

"Because you were never meant to eat," Tamsin sang. "You were meant to be sung *to*. And everyone who should have known better taught you to take instead."

He was crying. He noticed it distantly, the way you notice rain on someone else's window. His cheeks were wet and his voice did not break and the counter-song rolled out of him verse after verse, each one bought with a piece of himself he could feel going: the mountain roads, the taste of cheap wine, the color of Odessa's eyes—gone, gone, gone—and still the loves stayed, anchorless and indestructible, floating free of their stories like stars refusing to fall.

Brann's melody wrapped around his. The two threads braided. The black stone began, very softly, to glow.

*We accept,* said the Choir.

It said it the way a drowning man says *help*. It said it the way a starving animal says *enough*. The grey light gathered itself off the dais and out of Aldric's body and hung in the air above the throne room, vast and diminished at once, a chorus reduced to a single trembling chord.

*A voice willingly given,* it sang, in a thousand stolen voices and one new one. *Sustained by memory and love. Bound.*

"Not bound," Tamsin corrected, and his voice was almost gone now, worn down to a thread to match Brann's. "*Anchored.* There's a difference. Ask me in August."

He didn't know why he said August. He wouldn't remember saying it. But the word went into the binding like a keystone into an arch, and the Choir—starved, gifted, transformed—sang the last note of the counter-song *with* him, and the sound that filled the throne room was not a battle and not a death.

It was a lullaby. The first honest one in a thousand years.

The grey light came apart gently, into motes, into embers, into points of pale fire that drifted upward through the missing ceiling toward the missing sky—and outside, above the city that slept standing, the first star in nine years blinked back into existence.

Then a second.

Then a hundred.

Tamsin Marchetti sat down on the steps of the dais, next to a king who was waking up, and looked at the sky coming home, and tried to remember his own name.

Nothing came.

Behind him, Brann's melody faded to silence, and her small hand found his shoulder—tap, tap, tap—and he turned to look at this child he did not know, who was smiling at him with tears running down her face, and he smiled back at her the way you smile at a kind stranger.

"Hello," he said. "I'm sorry. Have we met?"

* * *

In the doorway, Ilen Corvane lowered the binding-score with shaking hands and did not go to him. Not yet. Some thresholds a man has to sit inside alone for a moment, even empty.

Outside, over Vaelhar, the stars kept returning—one by one, like a congregation filing back into a church—and down in the dark of the palace cells, in a corridor the hunters had abandoned in the chaos, a woman pressed her ear to a wall and heard, faint and impossible, a lullaby she'd last heard sung on a shifting road in the mountains.

Odessa Vane started to run.
