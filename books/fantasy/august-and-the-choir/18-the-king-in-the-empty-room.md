## 18. The King in the Empty Room

The first thing Aldric Vaelhar knew was cold.

Not the cold of the palace—though the palace had been cold for years now, cold as a held breath—but a true cold, the cold of stone steps under his palms and winter air moving through an open window. He was sitting on the floor. He did not remember sitting down.

He opened his eyes.

The throne room stretched away from him, vast and dim, lit by a single guttering brazier near the doors. The great windows at the far end were dark, and beyond them—

Beyond them there was a star.

One star, small and stubborn, hanging above the eastern ridge like the first word of a sentence someone had finally remembered how to speak.

Aldric stared at it. His mouth tasted of iron and honey. His hands, when he lifted them into his sight, were his own hands—knuckled, scarred at the left thumb where he'd cut himself at fourteen learning to hold a sword properly—but they shook, and they felt borrowed, like gloves worn by someone else for a very long time.

*Sing,* said something far beneath his thoughts, faint as a bell rung underwater.

He pressed his palms flat against the stone. "No," he said aloud, and his voice cracked on it, rusted from disuse. "No. It's done."

The thing beneath his thoughts did not answer. Or rather—it answered with silence, which was worse, because for three years silence had been its voice, its body, its whole weather. But this silence felt different. This silence had edges to it. This silence was *empty*, the way a room is empty after the guests have gone.

He got up. It took three tries. His legs had forgotten their own architecture, and he stood swaying in the middle of the throne room of Vaelhar like a man newly landed after a long sea voyage, waiting for the floor to stop tilting.

The throne was empty.

That was wrong. That was deeply, terribly wrong, and it took him four heartbeats to understand why: because he should have been in it. Because for three years he *had* been in it—or his body had—and the Choir had sat behind his eyes like a crown fitted too tight, singing through his throat while he drowned somewhere underneath, screaming up through fathoms of song that no one could hear.

But the throne was empty, and he was here, on the floor by the steps, and the star was coming back.

As he watched, a second star kindled above the ridge. Then a third. They came slowly, one by one, each one arriving like a lamp relit in a house everyone had believed burned down. The night sky over Vaelhar had been blank for three years—a smooth black nothing that the astronomers called an eclipse and the priests called a test and Aldric, in the last clear moments before the drowning, had known was neither.

It was an appetite. And it had been eating him alive.

"Aldric."

The voice came from the doorway, and he turned so fast he nearly fell. A woman stood there—grey-haired, sharp-faced, wearing a scholar's coat gone ragged at the cuffs, holding a lantern that trembled in her grip. Behind her, half-hidden in the shadow of the doorframe, a small girl clutched her sleeve. The girl was humming. Softly, steadily, a melody Aldric knew down to the marrow of him—the Choir's melody, the one that had been the walls of his prison—and yet hearing it now did not hurt. Hearing it now was like hearing a lock turn open.

"Ilen?" he said. The name came out broken. "Ilen Corvane? You're dead. I signed the—" He stopped. His memory was full of holes, great wet holes where things used to be. "I signed your death warrant. Didn't I?"

"You did." She came toward him without fear, which was either courage or madness, and set the lantern on the bottom step. Up close she looked older than her years by a decade, and her eyes were red-rimmed, and she looked at him the way one looks at a wrecked ship washed ashore—not with hatred, but with grief. "And I let you. It seemed cleaner. You weren't entirely you when you signed it, Your Majesty. Though you were more you than any of us understood at the time."

"The Choir." His voice was steadying now, finding its old channels. "Where is it? What happened?"

Ilen glanced back at the girl in the doorway. The girl hummed on, unbothered, watching the stars come back through the window with an expression of quiet professional interest, as if she'd personally arranged them.

"It's unbound," Ilen said. "Truly unbound, this time, not patched. The old binding wanted a voice given willingly—it always did. Three hundred years ago the first storm-callers understood that, and then their heirs got frightened, and frightened people make cruel arithmetic, and somewhere along the way *a voice* became *a life*, and the binding started rotting from the inside." She paused. "Tonight a man gave it what it actually asked for. Not his life. Everything else."

"What do you mean, everything else?"

Ilen didn't answer. Instead she stepped aside from the doorway, and gestured gently, and said, "Come and see. He won't know you. I want you to understand that before you speak to him. Whatever you say—he will not know you. He may not even know himself."

The man was sitting on the palace steps outside, in the colonnade where petitioners used to wait, back when petitioners still came to the palace instead of being sung into smiling compliance. He sat with his elbows on his knees and his hands loose between them, and he watched the sky with the fixed attention of someone watching snow fall on a fire.

He was young. Younger than Aldric expected—younger than the stories, though the stories had been thin, smugglers' rumors mostly, a storm-caller exiled from his order who'd come out of the Thornfell mountains with a ward-breaker and a dead astronomer and a mute child. In the lantern light his face was ordinary and handsome and utterly vacant, the way a house is vacant when the family has moved out but left the furniture.

Aldric stopped six feet away. The man didn't look up.

"Hello," Aldric said.

Nothing. The man's breathing was slow and even. Above them, another star arrived, and its light touched the colonnade, and still the man watched the sky as if he were waiting for it to tell him something important.

"Can you hear me?"

"I can hear you." The voice was quiet, level, pleasant. It was the voice of a man answering a question about the weather. "I don't know who you are."

"My name is Aldric. I'm—I was—the king."

"Was?"

"There was a war inside me," Aldric said. "You ended it. I'm told."

The man considered this. His brow furrowed slightly, the way a man's brow furrows when he is trying to recall a dream from three nights ago. "I don't remember doing anything," he said. "I don't remember much of anything. There was a woman." He said it carefully, testing the words. "There was a woman, and she laughed, and I can't—" His hands closed, slowly, into fists, and then opened again, deliberately, as if he'd practiced this. As if someone had taught him, recently, how to be angry without breaking things. "There are shapes. Where names should be. Do you know what I mean?"

"Yes," Aldric said, and meant it with everything he had. "I know exactly what you mean."

They sat in silence for a while. The stars kept arriving. By ones and twos now, faster, the sky remembering itself in patches—the Shepherd's Arc first, then the pale scatter of the Fisher's Net, then, low over the southern wall, the bright knot of the Anchor, which sailors swore by and astronomers charted and which Aldric's father had once pointed out to him from a balcony not unlike this one, saying, *that one never moves, boy. Remember that. When everything else drifts, find the Anchor.*

His father had been dead eleven years. The Choir had eaten the memory of that funeral, along with most of the rest of him, and now the memory was coming back in pieces, and each piece hurt like a stitch pulled through raw skin, and Aldric found, to his surprise, that he was grateful for every one.

"They're coming back," the man said, nodding at the sky. "The stars. Someone told me they would. I think—I think that was the point of me." He frowned. "Was that the point of me?"

"I don't know what the point of you was," Aldric said honestly. "But I know what you bought. All of this." He gestured—at the sky, at the city below the hill, where lights were beginning to show in windows, real lights, uneven and human, not the smooth synchronized glow of the sleeping harmony. Somewhere down in the Warrens a dog was barking, gloriously off-rhythm. "Every star. Every waking person. Every off-key thing in this kingdom that isn't singing the same note anymore."

The man looked at him then, directly, and his eyes were the worst part. They were clear eyes, intelligent eyes, and behind them was nothing where a life should be. It was like looking into a well and seeing no water.

"I have a name," he said. "I'm almost sure I have a name. It's right there." He touched his own chest, lightly, over the sternum. "It's right there and I can't get it out. It's like—it's like a word on the tip of the tongue except the tongue is gone."

Aldric's throat closed. He was king of Vaelhar; he had addressed parliaments and armies and once, memorably, a dragon that had gotten lost in the northern passes; he had never in his life been this afraid of a conversation.

"Your friends know it," he managed. "Your name. The astronomer says they've been telling you. Every day."

"Every night," the man corrected softly. "She comes at night. The grey-haired one comes with maps and questions, and the little one hums, and the woman—" Something moved across the vacant face, brief as a fish under ice. "The woman tells me stories. She tells me the same story every time, like she's afraid I'll catch her skipping a part. About salt smuggling in the high passes. About a trial. About a child she wouldn't give up." He paused. "Is that me? Is that my story?"

"That's your story," Aldric said. "I'd stake the crown on it. What's left of it."

The man almost smiled. Almost. It reached his mouth and stalled somewhere short of his eyes. "The woman cries when she tells it," he said. "She thinks I don't notice. She tells it anyway. Every night. She says—" and here his voice changed, took on an accent, a cadence not his own, a woman's cadence, roughened by mountain wind and held grief, and he recited it perfectly, having heard it surely a hundred times: *"You gave up everything so nobody else would have to give up anything. So we're going to sit here and give it all back to you, one piece at a time, and you don't get a vote."*

Above them, the last patch of black sky filled in. The Milky Way poured across the darkness like spilled milk, like a river finding its old bed, and the colonnade filled with starlight, and the man tipped his head back and looked at all of it with an expression Aldric couldn't read—wonder, maybe, or hunger, or the particular loneliness of a man standing in a room full of gifts addressed to someone whose name he couldn't remember.

"Do you know my name?" the man asked. "You're the king. Kings know things."

"No," Aldric said. "I was asleep for three years. I know less than anyone."

"Oh." The man nodded slowly. Then, after a moment: "Will you stay a while? Until she comes? I don't like the gaps. When she's talking, there aren't any gaps."

And Aldric—who had a kingdom to reassure, a council to reconvene, a sister-in-law who'd ruled as regent in all but name and would need handling, a decade of damage to inventory—Aldric sat down on the cold stone steps beside him, and said, "Yes. I'll stay."

They watched the stars together for a long time. At some point the little girl came out and sat on the man's other side and leaned against his arm and hummed, and the man's shoulders came down from around his ears, and Aldric understood that this was medicine, this humming, that the child was a living splint holding something broken together.

Near midnight, footsteps on the gravel path. Quick, uneven, running-then-walking-then-running footsteps, and a woman came around the corner of the colonnade with a lantern swinging wild in her hand and blood on her sleeve—dried blood, old blood, days old—and she saw the man on the steps and stopped as if she'd hit a wall.

"You're out," she said. Her voice was wrecked. "You're out of the catacombs. Ilen said—Ilen said you walked out and came here and—" She saw Aldric and stiffened, hand going to a knife at her belt, and then some royal instinct or simple exhaustion made her recognize him, and she curtsied, badly, without taking her eyes off the man. "Majesty."

"Madam," Aldric said, and rose, because there were things kings did even hollow, and giving two people room was one of them.

The woman knelt in front of the man. She took his hands. She looked at him the way people look at harbors.

"You're late," the man said. "You're always late. You tell me the same story every night and tonight you're late."

"Got held up," the woman said. "Escaping. Long story. I'll tell you the long version tomorrow." Her thumb moved over his knuckles, back and forth, back and forth. "Did you watch the stars?"

"All of them. I think all of them. There was one missing and then it wasn't." He tilted his head. "Odessa."

The woman went absolutely still.

"Odessa," the man said again, slower, tasting it. His face was doing something complicated—hope and terror fighting for the same ground. "Is that—is that yours, or mine?"

The woman made a sound that was half laugh and half sob, and pressed her forehead against their joined hands, and said, "Mine. Yours too. God, yours too. You remembered."

"I remembered one thing." The man sounded frightened now, genuinely frightened, a man standing on ice listening to it crack. "Is that all there is? One name? Odessa, and then nothing?"

"Then I'll tell you the rest," the woman said fiercely, lifting her head, and her eyes were blazing and wet. "Every night. However many nights it takes. One piece at a time, and you don't get a vote—"

"—and I don't get a vote," he finished, and this time the smile reached further, and Aldric, standing quietly in the starlight with his empty crown and his returned sky, turned away to give them privacy, and looked out over his capital, where ten thousand windows were going dark one by one as people fell asleep—truly slept, unwatched, unsung—for the first time in three years.

Somewhere south, he thought, the Choir was diminished and loose, wounded, hungry, patient. Somewhere in his own chest, ninety seconds of freedom had already cost him the certainty that it would last. And somewhere on these cold steps, a man who had saved a kingdom was waiting to be introduced to himself.

Aldric Vaelhar, king of a waking country, began—to the stars, to the night, to no one—to plan how a kingdom repays a debt like that.

Behind him, the woman started the story from the beginning. *"It starts with salt,"* she said. *"It always starts with salt."*
