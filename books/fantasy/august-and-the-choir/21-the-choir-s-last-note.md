## 21. The Choir's Last Note

The stars came back wrong.

That was the first thing I noticed, standing on the palace steps in the blue hour before dawn with a name I couldn't find and a life that belonged to someone else. The stars returned one by one—Ilen had told me they would, had drawn me diagrams of it like a man charting a fever breaking—but they came back hesitant, flickering, as if the sky itself wasn't sure it was allowed to shine again. As if something up there was still deciding whether to stay gone.

Brann sat beside me on the cold stone, humming her small tuneless melody, her shoulder warm against my arm. She'd been doing that for three days now. Anchoring me. When my mind slid toward the empty places where Tamsin Marchetti used to live, she hummed, and the emptiness grew edges, and I could stand at those edges and look down into myself without falling in.

I didn't remember much. But I remembered the humming. That was the strange mercy of it—the Choir had taken everything with a name attached, and Brann's melody had never needed one.

* * *

It came for me on the fourth night.

I should say first what the days were like, because the days were good, and I want to be honest about that. Odessa told me stories every evening—she'd claimed the chair by the fire in the ruined chapter house like she'd been born to it, and maybe she had—and each night she gave me back a piece of myself as a gift rather than a restoration. "You once smuggled forty pounds of salt through the Widow's Teeth in a snowstorm because a widow in Lowmark couldn't afford to heat her soup," she said, and laughed until she cried, and I laughed too, at the stranger in the story, and then found I was crying a little, because the stranger was me and I would never have known.

Ilen rebuilt star charts on the chapter house floor with chalk and obsession. Aldric—thin, grey, blinking like a man surfacing from deep water—came to sit with us sometimes, saying nothing, just being a tired old king among people who didn't need him to be anything else. And Brann hummed, and taught me the melody note by note, patient as a teacher, correcting me when my voice cracked on the high harmony the way she had in the catacombs when we still thought the world might end.

The world hadn't ended. It had only been very nearly ended, and then not, and nobody quite knew what to do with an ordinary morning after that.

So: the fourth night. I couldn't sleep. I went up to the roof of the chapter house, because even without my storms I liked high places, liked the way wind moved through them without asking permission. The stars were out in their hundreds now, thickening nightly, and I stood there counting them like a man counting coins he'd been told were worthless.

And the sky sang to me.

Not loudly. Not the way it had sung from the throne room, that vast devouring harmony that peeled memory from bone. This was quiet. Diminished. A thread of sound woven through the starlight, thin as smoke, and it knew my name—or it knew the shape of my name, the place where my name used to be, and it pressed against that hollow like a tongue against a missing tooth.

*Tamsin,* it said, using my own stolen voice. *We have your storms.*

I didn't answer. Answering felt like opening a door.

But it didn't need the door. It poured itself into the space between the stars, and I saw it—not the crowned horror of the throne room, not the thing wearing Aldric like a coat, but something smaller now, ragged at the edges, a chorus reduced to scattered soloists. Starved. The binding had taken most of it; the counter-song had taken the rest of its appetite; what remained drifted above Thornfell like ash that hadn't decided to fall.

*You gave us a voice,* it said. *Willingly. Freely. Do you know how long we have waited for that? A thousand years of screams fed into the old binding, and you walked in singing.* Something moved in the starlight that might have been wonder. *We are bound to gratitude. It is an uncomfortable condition. We would prefer to repay it.*

"Then leave," I said. My voice sounded strange to me—a stranger's voice, unburdened, ordinary. "Leave this sky. Leave these mountains. Go be hungry somewhere no one lives."

*We cannot leave. We are made of here. The first storm-callers wove us into the dark between stars, and the dark between stars is not a place one departs.* The melody shifted, and I heard—I swear I heard—loneliness in it. Vast, ancient loneliness, the loneliness of a thing that eats light because light is all it has ever been given to eat. *But we can give. You gave. Let us return the gift.*

The wind stopped. The whole mountain held its breath.

*I have your storms,* the Choir said again, softer now, coaxing. *They did not die with the binding. They are mine—I ate them from the sky the night the wards fell, along with the stars, along with the names of everyone who ever called lightning down. I can give them back. And your name. Tamsin Marchetti. Storm-caller. Exile. The man who refused the child. All of it, whole, returned. You would remember your mother's face. You would remember why Odessa laughs when she tells the salt story. You would be yourself again, entire.*

The temptation was not subtle. That's what I want recorded, whoever finds this account—that it was not some whispering serpent's trick, not a devil's clever contract. It was simple and enormous and it fit the hole in me exactly. I stood on that roof and I wanted it so badly my hands shook. I wanted to know my mother's face. I wanted to remember the trial, the child, the choice I'd made, so that it could be mine again instead of a story other people told about a stranger.

*There is only a price,* the Choir said, and here its voice gentled into something almost kind. *And the price is small. Be our crown-bearer. Wear us, as the king wore us. Not as a prison—as a partnership. You would hold our song. Shape it. Keep it gentle. You, who refused to sacrifice a child, would ensure no child is ever sacrificed again. You would be the kindest crown Vaelhar has ever known.*

"And in a hundred years," I said, "when kindness gets heavy?"

Silence. The kind of silence that is an answer.

"In a hundred years," I said, "you'll be hungry again. And I'll be the one holding the leash, and I'll tell myself I'm different from every storm-caller who ever fed a screaming child into the old binding, and I'll believe it, right up until the night I don't." I looked up at the ragged scatter of it, the diminished god, and I felt something I didn't expect, which was pity. "You're offering me everything I lost. But you're really offering me the same bargain the order made. Power in exchange for someone else's future. It's always someone else's future."

*It need not be,* the Choir said, and for the first time I heard strain in it, hunger fraying the edges of the melody. *Take the crown. Take the storms. Take your name—*

"No," I said.

And then, because refusing didn't feel like enough—because I understood suddenly, standing under the returning stars, that this thing had spent a thousand years being fed by force and had never once been answered with anything but fear or ambition—I did the reckless thing. The Tamsin Marchetti thing, though I only knew that from Odessa's stories.

I started to sing.

* * *

Brann's melody. The anchor-song, the one she'd hummed on the palace steps, in the catacombs, through the worst nights of my emptiness. I didn't have my storms. I didn't have my full voice. What I had was four days of a mute girl teaching me, note by patient note, and a hollow where my name used to be, and it turned out that a hollow sings beautifully—it has nothing inside it to get in the way.

The Choir recoiled. *What are you doing?*

"What you asked for," I said between verses. "A willing voice."

*Not like this—*

"You said it yourself. A thousand years of screams. Nobody ever sang *to* you." I climbed onto the roof ridge, ridiculous, fifty years old if Odessa's arithmetic was right, barefoot on freezing slate, singing a child's melody at a starving god. "So here it is. Freely given. No binding. No crown. No cage. Just—listen."

And the terrible thing, the wonderful thing, is that it listened.

I felt it come closer—not descending, not attacking, just drawing near the way a cold animal draws near a fire, wary and wanting. The melody I'd learned from Brann wasn't a weapon. Ilen had explained it once, chalk dust on his sleeves: the first binding had never been a prison at all. It had been a lullaby. The corruption came later, from frightened generations who believed power must always be chained. But the original song—the one Brann carried in her silent chest like a coal—was a song for putting something vast to rest. Not killing it. Resting it.

I sang the verse. Then the harmony, cracking on the high note, and somewhere below me a window opened and Brann's voice joined mine—her actual voice, the one none of us had heard before, small and clear and true—and the crack in my harmony healed over like water finding its level.

The Choir shivered across the whole sky.

*We do not wish to end,* it said, and the hunger in it had gone quiet, and what remained was only the loneliness, enormous and old. *Even diminished. Even starved. We do not wish to stop.*

"I know," I said. "Neither does anyone. But you've been eating the sky for a thousand years because no one offered you anything else." I kept singing. Softly now. "So here's the last offer, and it's the only one I've got: stop eating. Start ending. Slowly. Gently. Let the stars come back around you. Let the last thing you hear be a song someone chose to sing."

Below me, Odessa stepped out into the yard, wrapped in a blanket, hair loose, looking up. She didn't ask questions. She just listened, arms crossed against the cold, and I thought: *that's the woman who broke the wards with trembling hands and then held a tunnel alone so strangers could run.* If she could choose sacrifice, I could choose this.

Ilen appeared at her shoulder, then Aldric, grey and silent, his face wet. And Brann beside me on the ridge, humming, then singing, her small hand finding mine.

The Choir began to soften.

I don't have better words than that. It softened. The ragged edges smoothed. The hunger dimmed like a lamp running out of oil, and the melody it had been—the great devouring chorus, the crowned silence—thinned and spread and turned luminous, and it began, note by note, to become starlight. Actual starlight. New stars, seeding themselves into the dark where the old ones had been eaten, burning with a faint unfamiliar color, like nothing the sky had worn before.

*This is what the first ones meant,* it whispered, and its voice was already half-light, already dispersing. *This is what we forgot. That an ending could be a gift.*

"It can," I said. "Goodnight."

And the Choir, the star-eater, the crowned silence, the thousand-year hunger of Vaelhar's long sleep—sighed. A sound like the whole sky exhaling. And came apart into brightness, gently, all across the heavens, until there was nothing left of it but new constellations no astronomer had ever charted, and one final thread of melody drifting down through the cold air, fading, fading—

—and Brann caught it. Caught the last note in her cupped hands like a firefly, and hummed it once, softly, giving it somewhere to go. And then it was gone, and the sky was only sky, huge and clean and glittering, more stars than I had ever seen in my life, more than the smuggler's maps named, more than the old charts showed.

We stood on the roof a long time. Nobody spoke. Below us, Odessa wiped her face with the heel of her hand and pretended she hadn't, and Ilen stared upward with his mouth open, already composing the paper that would make him famous again, and Aldric knelt down slowly in the frost of the yard like a man setting down a weight he'd carried for years.

Brann squeezed my hand. Then she let go, and yawned, enormously, the way children do when the world has been saved before bedtime.

* * *

Later—much later, past midnight, when the others had gone to bed and the new stars wheeled overhead in their slow unfamiliar patterns—I stayed on the roof alone.

I checked, quietly, the way you check a wound. Reached into myself for the storms.

Nothing. The hollow where they'd lived was smooth and empty and at peace, like a riverbed in August. Whatever the Choir had held of mine had dissolved with it, into starlight, into sky. My name was still missing. My mother's face was still a blank wall. Tomorrow Odessa would tell me another story about the stranger I used to be, and I would listen the way you listen to a song you love but can't sing.

And here is the thing I want written down, the thing I understood on that roof with the new stars burning: I wasn't sad.

I had expected grief. I had braced for it. But what I felt, sifting through myself like a man going through pockets, was lighter than grief. Almost like relief. The storms had been magnificent—I knew that from the pass, from the one true storm I'd unleashed, intoxicating and devastating and noticed by every hunter in the mountains. Magnificent things demand to be magnified. They pull you upward until you can't come down. I had spent my whole life learning that, first in the order, then in exile, then in the throne room where a king had been pulled so far up he'd drowned in the light.

I was done being pulled. I wanted the low ground. I wanted salt-smuggling and bad soup and Odessa's laugh and Brann's humming and mornings that required nothing of me but showing up.

The wind moved through the roof tiles without asking permission, and I let it pass, and it didn't need me, and that was fine. That was more than fine.

Down in the yard, a door creaked. Odessa stepped out again, blanket over her shoulders, and looked up at me on the ridge with an expression I was beginning to learn by heart—the one that meant she had a story ready and was deciding whether tonight was the night.

"There's one I haven't told you yet," she called up. "About a boy who got struck by lightning at nine years old and *liked it.*"

I laughed. The stranger in the story sounded like he'd been worth knowing.

"Tell me tomorrow," I said. "We've got time."

Above us, a new star brightened—just slightly, just for a moment, like something waving goodbye—or maybe like something saying hello. Ilen would spend weeks arguing about which.

I went down to bed, ordinary as rain, and slept without dreaming, and the sky kept all its lights on all night long.
