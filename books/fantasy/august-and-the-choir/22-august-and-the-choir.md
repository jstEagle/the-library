## 22. August and the Choir

The stars came back in August.

Not all at once — that was the strange part. Ilen had predicted a flood, a sky cracking open like an egg, but instead they returned the way light returns to a room when someone opens shutters one by one: a star here, a constellation there, each one arriving with the small, unremarkable confidence of something that had never actually left. By midsummer the night over Thornfell was thick again, and on clear evenings the whole mountain range seemed to lean back and look up at itself.

Tamsin watched them from the porch of the waystation every night. Not because he remembered them — he didn't, not really — but because Odessa had told him he used to love them, and loving them secondhand felt like a kind of prayer.

The waystation sat in the crook of the old salt road where it bent down toward Lowmark, a long low building of grey stone that had once been a smuggler's storehouse and before that, if you believed the foundation stones, a shrine. They'd spent the spring rebuilding it. Odessa had haggled the timber down to half price from a Lowmark merchant who owed her more favors than she could count; Ilen had drawn floor plans in charcoal on the walls until Brann took the charcoal away and drew better ones; Tamsin had carried things. He was good at carrying things. It required no memory, only shoulders, and his were broad enough.

They opened the doors on the first of August, though there was nothing to open, really — the door had been open since June, because refugees did not wait for ribbon-cuttings. But Odessa insisted on a ceremony anyway. She stood on the porch step with a cup of plum wine raised to the road and said, "To everyone who's still walking," and Brann hummed three notes — her own melody now, not the Choir's, though it had grown out of it like a branch out of a trunk — and Ilen cried, quietly, into his sleeve, the way he did at everything: weddings, sunsets, well-made soup.

That was how the summer went.

* * *

In the mornings Tamsin worked the kitchen, because Odessa said his hands remembered bread even if the rest of him didn't, and she was right. His hands knew the weight of dough, the turn of the knead, the exact moment the crust went from gold to done. Sometimes he'd stand over a rising loaf and feel something move behind his eyes like a fish under ice — not a memory exactly, more the shape where a memory used to be — and then it would sink again, and he'd go on shaping loaves for people who arrived at the waystation hollow-eyed from the passes.

They came all through August. Families out of Vaelhar who had spent half a year smiling at nothing, waking slowly from the Choir's long song like divers coming up too fast, some of them weeping without knowing why. Shepherds from the high valleys whose flocks had wandered during the silence and come home thin. A woman who had been a warden on the eastern border and could not stop checking a gate that no longer existed. They came, mostly, the way water comes: downhill, in the dark, following whatever channel would have them.

Odessa met them all. She had a gift for it — or maybe not a gift, maybe just the particular hardness-softness of a woman who had broken wards under threat and pulled her sister out of a chorus and refused, afterward, to ever again let a person be treated as a thing. She took their names. That was the first thing she did, always: asked their names, wrote them in the big ledger behind the counter, in her sharp slanted hand. "Names matter," she told Tamsin once, not looking at him, stirring the stew. "You lose enough of them, you learn."

He hadn't understood what she meant then. He thought he might be starting to.

Ilen ran the school, such as it was — a corner of the common room, a slate, and any child who wanted to know why the stars had gone and how they'd come back. He taught them the old astronomy, the real one, the one that had gotten him exiled from court twice and nearly killed once. He taught them that the sky was not a ceiling but a sea, and that the Choir had drunk from it for a hundred years while the kingdom slept standing up, and that a man with no name and a girl with no words had sung it quiet. The children loved him. He pretended this embarrassed him. It did not embarrass him at all.

And Brann — Brann was everywhere. Twelve years old now, or thirteen; nobody knew, including Brann, who had shrugged when Odessa asked and held up seven fingers and then four more, which was as close to an answer as anyone got. She still didn't speak. She had never spoken, not before the Choir and not after, and the healers in Lowmark said her voice had simply chosen another door. But she hummed. She hummed constantly, tunelessly, wonderfully — fragments of the anchor-melody braided with songs she picked up from travelers, so that a Vaelharri lullaby might wander into the binding-song and out again into something no one had ever heard before. At night, when the new arrivals lay awake staring at ceilings, listening for a chorus that wasn't there anymore, Brann would walk the dormitory rows humming, soft as weather, and one by one they'd sleep.

"She's not doing magic," Ilen had said, early on, watching her work. "I want to be very clear about that. There's no binding in it, no power. It's just a girl making sure people aren't alone in the dark." He had wiped his eyes. "Which is, historically, most of what magic was supposed to be."

* * *

On an evening late in August, with the first cold breath of autumn already moving through the pass, a traveler came up the salt road alone.

Tamsin saw him from the kitchen window — a small figure, dust-colored, walking with the careful economy of someone who had learned to spend his strength like coins. Refugees usually came in twos and threes now, in families, in little caravans with mules and hand-carts. This man came the old way. Alone, on foot, the way people had come during the worst of it.

By the time the man reached the porch, Odessa was already there, ledger open, wine poured. She had a sixth sense for arrivals that Tamsin suspected was just ten years of watching roads and refusing to be surprised by anything.

"Evening," she said. "You'll want soup. Everyone wants soup, they just don't know it yet."

The traveler smiled — a tired, cracked thing, but real. He was perhaps fifty, grey at the temples, with the sunburnt neck of a man who'd walked a long way looking at the ground. "Soup," he agreed. His voice was rough with disuse. "And — is it true? What they say in the valleys. That the stars are back."

"Come see for yourself," Odessa said, and stepped aside so he could look up.

The man looked up. Tamsin, carrying the soup out, watched his face do what every face did now, the first time: the slow unbuckling. Wonder without fear. For six months the sky had been a wound; now it was just a sky, enormous and indifferent and full, and people kept forgetting they were allowed to look at it.

"The Choir's gone," the man said. "Truly gone?"

"Gone," Odessa said. "Unbound. Sung apart." She glanced back toward the kitchen, and Tamsin pretended not to notice. "It cost somebody."

"They say it cost a storm-caller." The man set down his pack. "They say he gave everything. His name, his storms. They say he doesn't remember any of it."

"People say a lot of things," Odessa said evenly.

"I walked through Thornfell in the bad years," the man went on, quieter. "I saw the wards fall. I heard the hunters humming on the passes. I hid in a culvert for two days while they sang my neighbor's name until he walked out to them smiling." His hands had started to shake around the cup of wine. "I owe whoever ended it more than soup can pay. I just want — I'd like to thank him. If he's here."

The common room had gone quiet. Ilen had stopped pretending to read. Brann stood in the doorway with her slate against her chest, watching.

"He's here," Odessa said.

She looked at Tamsin. And Tamsin — who had spent eight months being a story he couldn't remember, a name in other people's mouths, a hero in a tale that fit him like a borrowed coat — set down the soup and crossed the room and stood in front of the traveler, because Odessa had taught him that much, at least: you don't hide from people who need to say thank you. Hiding was the old life. The old life was over.

"Thank you," the traveler said. His voice broke on it. "Whatever it cost you. Thank you."

Tamsin nodded. He put out his hand, and the man shook it, gripping hard, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the fire popped, and outside the August stars burned in their thousands, patient as ever.

"What's your name?" the traveler asked, wiping his face, laughing a little at himself. "I should know the name of the man who saved the sky."

And here was the thing — the thing Tamsin had been circling all summer like a moth at glass. He didn't remember his name. Not the way you remember your own name, the way it lives in your mouth without asking. What he had was secondhand: Odessa saying it across the ledger, Ilen murmuring it over old charts, Brann tracing it in spilled flour on the kitchen table, patient as a teacher, the letters getting straighter every week. He had a name the way you have a scar: given to you by events, carried without pain, proof of something survived.

But scars are yours. That was the discovery of the summer, the one he'd been growing into like a season. The memories weren't his yet. Maybe they'd never fully be. But the name — the name Odessa gave back to him every morning, the name Brann wrote in flour, the name Ilen had whispered like a spell over a blank-eyed man on the palace steps — that name had been handed to him by people who refused to let him vanish, and a gift like that, accepted long enough, becomes yours. Not restored. Rebuilt. Better than restored, maybe, because a rebuilt thing knows it was built, and knows who built it, and loves them for it.

"Tamsin," he said.

His own voice surprised him — steady, warm, sure of itself.

"My name is Tamsin."

The traveler smiled. "Tamsin," he repeated, tasting it. "I'll carry that down the mountain. People should hear it."

"They will," Odessa said, and there was something in her voice that made Tamsin look at her — the fierce wet brightness she got when she was determined not to cry in front of strangers. "Everyone will."

* * *

Later, after the traveler slept and the fire burned low, Tamsin went out to the porch, because that was his hour — the last one, the stars-and-nothing hour. Brann came out and sat beside him without asking, which was her way, and after a while Odessa came too, and Ilen, wrapped in a blanket against the coming cold, and none of them said anything for a long time, because they had learned that the best parts of the day were the ones that needed no words.

The sky was full. Somewhere up there, past the stars that had returned, past the ones still coming home, the last dissolved thread of the Choir drifted as starlight, singing nothing, wanting nothing. Brann hummed three notes at it, softly, the way you'd speak to an old dog. Not a binding. Just company.

"You know," Ilen said eventually, "the order used to say a storm-caller's worth was measured in what he could call down." He adjusted his blanket. "Ridiculous people. It was always measured in what he was willing to give up."

"I don't miss the storms," Tamsin said. It was true. He'd checked, all summer, the way you check a tooth that isn't hurting anymore. There was a space where they'd been, clean-edged, healing. "I thought I would. I don't."

"No," Odessa said. "You wouldn't." She bumped her shoulder against his. "You were always loudest when you were quiet."

Brann leaned her head against his arm. Above them the stars turned, slow and certain, keeping their old appointments. Down in the valley a lamp moved along the road — another traveler, another name not yet written in the ledger, another person walking out of the dark toward a lit door.

Tomorrow there would be bread to bake, and names to write down, and a girl's letters to practice in flour, and a woman's laugh to learn again, and an old man's charts to hold steady while he pointed at the sky. Tomorrow, like every tomorrow since the solstice, would be ordinary. He had given up everything to buy the world its ordinariness, and the world, in return, had given him back a life one small piece at a time — not the old life, not the lost one, but a new one, built by hand, by the people who had refused, with trembling hands and stubborn hearts, to let him go.

The lamp on the road came closer. Tamsin watched it, and listened to Brann humming, and felt the name sit easy in his mouth, ready for whoever asked next.

"My name is Tamsin," he said again, to the stars, to the road, to the family beside him. "And I'm home."
