## 1. The Weight of Ink

There are three hundred and eleven ways to be invisible in the Gilded Threshold, and I have made a study of most of them.

The first and finest is ink. Ink stains your cuffs and your reputation in equal measure, and nobody flirts with either. By my fourth year in the counting room, my hands had gone permanently grey at the fingertips, and by my ninth, so had my prospects. Merchants who could buy a harbor with a sneeze would look straight through me when I brought them their contracts to sign. I once stood at the shoulder of Lady Vess Coriander for the better part of an hour while she negotiated the purchase of a lighthouse that had, according to the paperwork, never existed, and when the deal was struck she turned to me and asked if I was new.

I said, "Nine years, my lady."

She said, "Time flies," and signed.

That is the trade I have made, and I will tell you plainly: it was a good one. The guild deals in impossibilities — bottled storms, borrowed years, grudges sold by the pound — and the men and women who broker such things burn bright and brief, like paper lanterns. They are remembered, which in the Threshold is another word for hunted. But a ledger-keeper? A ledger-keeper is furniture. Furniture is fed, sheltered, and left alone until it falls apart, and I had every intention of falling apart quietly, at a respectable age, with a full stomach and an unremarkable grave.

So when I tell you that I loved my life, understand that I loved it the way a mole loves its tunnel: not because it is grand, but because it is exactly the size of me.

* * *

That night began the way all my nights began, with a lamp, a stool, and the evening's intake.

The Gilded Threshold guildhall is built like a fist around its counting room — a great round chamber at the heart of the building, ringed with clerks' desks, and above us all, a skylight of leaded glass through which you could see, on clear nights, the lanterns of Cinderport's harbor drifting like drowned stars. The brokers worked the floors above and the streets beyond, and whatever they bought or sold or swindled came down to us at day's end in crates, cages, jars, and paper, to be catalogued, valued, and entered into the ledgers before the morning bell.

I had the impossible trades. I should explain that. In a guild of four hundred souls, every clerk has a specialty, and mine had been assigned to me in my first year by old Master Quillon, who looked at my neat handwriting, my unremarkable face, and my total absence of ambition, and said, "Yarrow. You'll take the strange ones." It was not a compliment. It was also, I have since decided, the truest thing anyone ever said about me. The strange ones require a clerk who does not covet them, because a clerk who covets a bottled storm will eventually find a way to uncork one.

The evening's intake sat before me in a row of containers that would have gotten a lesser guild burned as witches: a storm in a bottle the size of my thumb, its cloud still turning slow and grey; a jar of last winter, packed in salt; a wedding vow that had outlived both spouses and was, per the manifest, "still active, handle with gloves"; and a small locked box labeled, in a broker's hurried hand, *one year, used gently, seller's grandmother's*.

I catalogued the storm first. That is the proper order — weather before sentiment — because weather is honest. The bottle had been corked with wax stamped by a storm-caller out of the Salt Road, and when I held it to the lamp I could see rain moving inside it, fine as needlework, and something that might have been a tiny shipwreck turning in the swell. I entered it as *Item 4,417: tempest, minor, bottled; provenance: Salt Road; condition: restless*. Then I weighed it, because the guild weighs everything, and the scale — a brass thing older than the guildhall itself, older than the guild, possibly older than the city, though no one likes to say so aloud — the scale read the storm at four ounces, which is heavier than any storm has a right to be, and heavier than it had read the last time we'd weighed one. I made a note of that. I made notes of everything. That was the whole of my religion: *write it down, and it cannot lie to you later.*

The jar of last winter I entered as *Item 4,418: season, winter, one (1), jarred; contents faintly melancholy; do not store near the vows*. The vow went in at 4,419, and it hummed against the glass when I wrote its entry, the way active contracts do, and I put on the gloves, because I am not a fool, only invisible.

The year was the trouble.

Borrowed years are the guild's most profitable line and its most cursed. You cannot weigh a year on brass. The scale reads them as nothing — the pan doesn't so much as tremble — and so they must be assessed by feel, which means a clerk must open the box, which means a clerk must hold a year of someone's life in his hands and decide what it was worth to the person who lived it. Most brokers won't touch the trade. Most clerks won't either. But I had discovered, over nine years, that I was good at it, in the way that a man who cannot swim is sometimes good at finding the bottom of a river with his feet.

I unlocked the box. Inside, folded in tissue, was a year the color of late afternoon: a year of somebody's grandmother, per the manifest, sold by a granddaughter who needed passage money and had no use for sentiment. I held it, and it held me back, the way years do, and for a moment I was standing in a kitchen that wasn't mine, smelling bread that had never been baked for me, hearing someone hum a tune I almost knew.

I set it down. I entered it: *Item 4,420: year, borrowed, one (1); contents: kitchen, bread, a song; valuation: more than the seller knew; note: buyer should be vetted for sentiment.*

Then I sat for a while with my pen in the air, because that was the part of the job no one warns you about. The ledgers of the Gilded Threshold are honest, which is the guild's one redeeming virtue and its most closely guarded secret. Every impossibility that passes through our hands is written down exactly as it is, and there is a strange, cold comfort in that. The world above us is full of brokers and inquisitors and kings who lie for a living. Down here, in the counting room, a storm weighs four ounces and a grandmother's year smells of bread, and the ink says so, and the ink does not care who it inconveniences.

I have thought, since, about how much of my life was spent taking comfort from that. *The ink cannot lie.* It never occurred to me to ask what happens when the ink tells you something true that you were never meant to read.

* * *

The morning bell was still two hours off when Master Quillon came down the stairs, which was the first wrong thing.

Quillon does not come down. Quillon is the counting room the way the counting room is the guildhall — permanently, structurally, without variation. He is seventy if he is a day, built like a filing cabinet, and he has not climbed those stairs in the nine years I have worked under him because, as he puts it, "everything that matters is already down here, boy, and everything that doesn't should stay up there." So when his shoes appeared on the bottom step, I put down my pen, because in the Gilded Threshold, an anomaly in the routine is worth more attention than an anomaly in the inventory.

"Yarrow." He said my name the way he said all names, which is to say like an item number. "You're wanted upstairs."

"Am I being promoted?" I asked, because I joke the way other men pray — quietly, and mostly for my own benefit.

"You're being *summoned*." He let that sit. "Guildmaster Corvane wants the intake manifests from tonight. All of them. In his own hand, before the bell."

That was the second wrong thing. Corvane does not read manifests. Corvane reads *balances* — the great summations, the quarterly truths, the numbers by which the guild knows itself. A guildmaster reading a single night's intake is a merchant reading a single grain of rice. I said as much, or a polite version of it, and Quillon looked at me for a long moment with an expression I had never seen on him before and have never stopped seeing since.

"Since when do you care what the guildmaster wants with a night's intake?" he said. "You're the strangest clerk we have. Act like it. Catalogue, don't wonder."

He went back up the stairs. I sat there with my pen in my hand and the storm in its bottle turning its small grey weather beside me, and I did what I have always done with things that frighten me: I wrote them down. *Note: Quillon descended. Note: Corvane requests manifests, same night. Note: no precedent.* Three lines in my private margin, in my own shorthand, which no one else in the guild can read. It is a small rebellion, keeping a private margin. Every clerk keeps one. It is how we remember what the ledgers are not permitted to say.

I copied the manifests fair, in my best hand, and carried them up.

* * *

I want to be honest with you about what the guildhall looks like at night, because I loved it, and you should know what a man loves before you watch him lose it.

By lamplight, the upper floors of the Gilded Threshold are a forest of brass and paper. Corridors lined with contract cabinets that go back further than the guild admits. Meeting rooms where the air is thick with old bargains — you can taste them, a sweetness like overripe fruit, if you walk through uninvited. The brokers' common room, loud at this hour with the night trade: a man selling luck by the spoonful, a woman auctioning the right to be forgotten, someone's apprentice asleep in a corner with his head on a cage that was, per its tag, *occupied, do not open, do not name what's inside*.

I walked through all of it invisible, manifest in hand, and I was — I want this on the record — *happy*. Nine years I had walked these corridors unseen, and every step of it had been a life I had built on purpose, plank by plank, out of unremarkableness. Low-born, low-ambition, low-profile. The guild had raised me from nothing, which sounds like generosity until you learn that the guild raises everything from nothing; it is simply what the guild does, the way a tide does it. I had never once asked whether the tide had plans for what it raised. I had never once needed to.

Guildmaster Corvane's office is at the top of the hall, behind a door of black oak with a brass plate that reads, in letters worn nearly smooth: *ALL ACCOUNTS SETTLED HERE*. I had been inside it exactly twice in nine years — once when I was hired, once when I was nearly fired for entering a miracle under "miscellaneous" instead of giving it its own line. Corvane had laughed, that time. I remembered the laugh. It had sounded like a ledger closing.

I knocked. The door said, "Enter," in Corvane's voice, before I touched it. That was the third wrong thing, and I noted it, and I went in anyway. You will find, as my story goes on, that this is the pattern of my life: I note the wrong things, and I go in anyway. A clerk's courage. It has never once saved me.

Corvane sat behind a desk the size of a coffin lid, and he was not alone. There was a woman by the window — tall, grey-cloaked, standing the way soldiers stand when they are trying to look like furniture. She did not look at me. That, more than anything, told me what she was. Everyone in Cinderport looks at a man who walks in carrying paper; it is the one law of the city. She didn't. She looked *through* me, with the practiced boredom of a professional, and I thought, with the part of my mind that catalogues impossibilities: *inquisitor. Crown. Here. In our hall. On a night when the guildmaster wants a single evening's manifests in his own hand.*

"Yarrow." Corvane didn't look up. "The storm. Item forty-four-seventeen. What did it weigh?"

"Four ounces, guildmaster."

He did look up then. "And the last storm of that grade, from the Salt Road, three years ago?"

"Two and a half, guildmaster." I had it by heart. I have everything by heart; it is cheaper than paper.

"Storms are getting heavier." He said it to the window, to the woman, to no one. "Interesting. Leave the manifests."

I set them on the desk. And because I am the strangest clerk the Gilded Threshold ever employed, and because the ink cannot lie and I have built a whole religion on that, I said: "Guildmaster — with respect — storms of that provenance have been gaining weight for years. It's in the ledgers. Half an ounce a decade, give or take. No one's ever asked me about it before."

The silence that followed had texture. The woman by the window turned her head, just slightly, and for the first time she *saw* me — really saw me, the way you see a word you've read a thousand times and never once looked at — and something in her face shifted, some small recalculation, and I remember thinking, with the last of my comfortable, invisible, unremarkable life: *ah. There it is. The moment I stop being furniture.*

"Half an ounce a decade," the woman repeated. Her voice was dry and even, like paper tearing slowly. "You keep records of that."

"The guild keeps records of everything, my lady."

"The guild," she said, "keeps records of everything," and she said it the way a man says a prayer he no longer believes, and then she looked at Corvane, and Corvane looked at me, and whatever passed between them was a transaction, and I have spent my whole life reading transactions, and this one I could read perfectly: *he is selling something, and I am the buyer, and the goods are standing in front of you holding a manifest.*

"Thank you, Yarrow," Corvane said warmly. "That will be all."

I went back down the stairs. The counting room was emptying toward the bell; the other clerks were capping their ink, and the storm in its bottle had gone still, as storms do before they decide what they'll be.

I sat at my desk. I opened my private margin. And I wrote, in my own shorthand, the entry I would give anything now to have been wrong about:

*Note: the inquisitor saw me.*

*Note: she was not surprised.*

*Note: storms are getting heavier, and nobody asks why, and tonight somebody did, and it was me.*

The morning bell rang. Above the skylight, the harbor lanterns were going out one by one, drowned stars sinking, and somewhere in the hall above me a door I could not hear opened onto a stair I did not know, and the day began — the date, though I did not yet know it, that had been written down a hundred years before I was born, in a book I was scheduled, by the guild's own intake manifest, to catalogue before the bell tomorrow night.

The ink cannot lie. I have never stopped believing that. It was the ink, in the end, that ruined me — but I am ahead of myself, and the ledgers, as the guildmaster says, will keep.
