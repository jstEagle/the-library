## 2. A Date That Hasn't Happened

There is a particular smell to a seized estate. Most people would say it's dust, or grief, or the sour note of panic that soaks into wallpaper when the family knows the wagons are coming. I've catalogued eleven seizures in my six years with the Gilded Threshold, and I can tell you it's none of those things alone. It's all three, layered like lacquer, and underneath them something else — the smell of a house that has stopped being anyone's.

This one was the Vessing estate, up on Lantern Hill, and it had stopped being anyone's about four days ago, when guild assessors arrived with writs and the Vessings arrived at the realization that bottled storms are not, in fact, legal tender anywhere south of the Salt Reaches.

I want to be clear about my role in all this. I am not an assessor. Assessors get to wear the grey-and-gold sash and make pronouncements. I am a ledger-keeper, third class, which means I follow the assessors around and write down what they find, in what order they found it, and what it was appraised at before the appraisal was inevitably revised downward once the assessor remembered the guild takes its cut first. It is work for a person who does not exist, performed by a person who has made a career of almost existing, and I was — am — very good at it.

My name is Xanthe Yarrow. You will have to take my word for that, because nobody else in the Gilded Threshold could pick me out of a lineup of clerks, which is a lineup of identical men in identical ink-stained cuffs, and I count that as the single greatest achievement of my life.

* * *

The trouble started, as trouble usually does for me, with an inventory discrepancy.

"Yarrow." Assessor Brannick stood in the doorway of the estate's library, holding a manifest like it had personally insulted his mother. "The manifest says four hundred and twelve volumes. I count four hundred and thirteen."

"Then someone miscounted," I said, without looking up from my column of figures. Miscounting was the most common sin in the guild, and the second most forgivable, after drinking.

"The manifest is in your hand."

That got my attention. I checked. He was right. Four hundred and twelve, in my neat, narrow script, written three days ago when we'd done the preliminary walk-through. And yet here was a library containing four hundred and thirteen books, one of which was not on any list I'd ever written.

I should explain something about seized estates: they lie. Not maliciously — houses don't have malice — but they shift. Attics settle and reveal. Floorboards warp and gape. A book that wasn't there on Tuesday will be there on Friday, wedged behind a shelf or slid beneath a loose stair, because the people who lived here hid things, and hiding things well means even you forget where they are. The guild's official position is that this is fraud. My private position, after eleven estates, is that it's just what homes do when they're dying. They cough things up.

So I did what I always do. I found the extra volume, logged it, and prepared to move on with my small, orderly life.

The book was behind the shelf on the north wall, in the gap between the shelving and the stone, which is exactly the kind of place a house keeps its secrets. It was small — smaller than a hymnal, bigger than a prayer card — bound in leather gone the color of old tea, with no title on the spine and no guild seal, which meant no assessor had touched it, which meant it was mine to catalogue by whatever judgment I saw fit. This is one of perhaps three powers granted to ledger-keepers third class, and I hoard it like a dragon.

I sat down on the floor — undignified, but the library was empty and dignity is a currency I don't trade in — and opened it.

The first thing I noticed was the handwriting. It was beautiful. That's not a word I use lightly; I spend my life among handwriting, and most of it is either chicken-scratch or the elaborate calligraphy of people paid by the flourish. This was neither. It was precise and flowing at once, every letter formed as if the writer had thought about it individually and then decided, out of love, to connect it to the next one anyway. Guild-trained eyes might call it clerk's hand. But clerks don't write books like this.

Because it was a book of dates.

Not a diary. Diaries have weather in them, and grievances. This had only dates — columns of them, marching down each page in that lovely hand, some with short annotations beside them, most without. I flipped forward, reading idly, the way you read anything while deciding how to classify it.

*3rd of Thawmonth.* Beside it: *the ferryman drowns; the ferry survives.*

*19th of Highsummer.* *Two brothers quarrel over a mill. Only one walks home.*

*9th of Leaf-fall.* Nothing at all. Just the date, sitting there, patient as a stone.

I frowned. Prophecy, then? The free cities had a taste for prophecy the way Cinderport had a taste for fried eel — cheap, everywhere, and best not examined too closely. Street-corner seers sold paper fortunes for a copper. Noble houses kept pet astrologers. And every few years some self-declared oracle would publish a book of predictions, sell two hundred copies to the credulous, and vanish before anyone could check the math.

But those books were always vague. That's the trick of prophecy-as-commerce: *a dark-haired stranger brings change*, and then every dark-haired stranger who changes anything gets claimed as proof. This book was different. This book had specific days. Specific outcomes. *The ferryman drowns; the ferry survives* is not a fortune. It's a record.

Except it couldn't be a record, because the dates were wrong. They were all wrong. The ink was a century old — I know old ink the way a fishmonger knows fish, and this had the browned, iron-tang look of a hundred years' oxidation — but the dates ran past the year of the book's binding, past last year, past this year, into years that hadn't happened yet. Dates sitting in century-old ink, waiting.

I told myself it was a hoax. I want that on the record, since apparently the universe keeps ledgers too. I told myself it was a hoax, a clever one, a century-old con still running its long game, and I turned pages looking for the flaw, the seam, the place where the trick showed its stitching.

I found today's date instead.

You have to understand what that felt like. Imagine opening a stranger's letter and finding your own name at the bottom in their handwriting. Imagine hearing your own voice come out of a locked room. There is a category of wrongness that doesn't frighten you at first — it just stops you, the way a miscounted column stops you, and says *look again*. So I looked again.

*14th of Rainsong.* Today. The fourteenth of Rainsong, in the current year, written in ink older than my grandfather's grandfather.

Beside it, in that beautiful, patient hand:

*Xanthe Yarrow. The gate opens.*

* * *

I sat on the floor of a dead man's library for a long time.

Let me reconstruct my reasoning, because I'm proud of it, and because pride was all I had left by the end of that afternoon. Reasoning one: coincidence of names. Yarrow is not a rare name in the Marchlands; there were three Yarrows listed in the Cinderport tax rolls alone, and Xanthe, while unusual, is the kind of name low-born parents give when they want their child to sound like more than they'll ever be. My mother chose it off a ship's manifest, she told me once, because she liked how it looked written down. Somewhere in a century of Yarrows, one of them was probably named Xanthe. Probability favored it.

Reasoning two: forgery. The book was old, yes, but old books get written in. Marginalia, additions, a later owner adding their own "prophecies" in imitation of the original hand. Except — and here my professional eye betrayed me, damn it — the entry was not marginal. It was in the main column, in the main flow of ink, with the same pen pressure, the same slant, the same everything. If it was added later, the forger was better than anyone alive, including me, and I can spot a forged ledger entry at forty paces. It's the one party trick I have.

Reasoning three: it doesn't matter. This was my strongest argument, and I clung to it like a man clinging to a door in a flood. Because here is the truth about prophecies, real or fake: they are not contracts. Nothing in a book makes anything happen. If some dead scribe wrote my name next to today's date, then either he was wrong, or he was guessing, or he was playing a joke across a hundred years, and in all three cases the correct response was to log the volume, close the cover, collect my wage, and go home to my rented room with its view of the tannery wall and its one window that sticks.

That was the plan. That was absolutely the plan.

I logged the volume. *One (1) book, untitled, leather-bound, contents: calendrical annotations, provenance unknown, value: negligible.* I wrote *negligible* with a steady hand. I am fairly sure it was steady. Then I closed the cover, and my thumb — traitor, saboteur, the one part of me that never learned to be invisible — brushed the page open again, and I looked one more time.

*Xanthe Yarrow. The gate opens.*

And beneath it, in smaller letters, squeezed against the margin as if the writer had hesitated, had almost not committed it to the page:

*He will not believe it. No keeper ever does.*

* * *

I finished the day's work. I want that noted too, because it matters to who I am. The Vessing estate yielded sixty-one crates of goods, nine items of contested provenance, and one scandal involving the late Lady Vessing's second cousin that I recorded under "miscellaneous textiles" because it wasn't my business and I've survived thirty years by knowing what isn't my business. Brannick signed off. The wagons rolled. I walked down Lantern Hill through the early evening drizzle with a book in my satchel that I had absolutely no intention of stealing, except that it was in my satchel, and we both knew it.

Here is the thing about a life built to be overlooked: it works. It works beautifully, right up until the moment something looks back.

For thirty years I have been the man nobody remembers. The guild took me in at seven — foundling, workhouse stock, the kind of child the Gilded Threshold acquires the way it acquires everything, cheaply and with an eye to resale value — and I understood the bargain immediately, even at seven, maybe especially at seven. Be accurate. Be quiet. Be useful in the way furniture is useful. In exchange: meals, a cot, a trade, and safety, because the guild protects its assets, and a ledger-keeper who never draws attention is a very durable asset indeed. I have watched flashier clerks rise and fall. I watched a boy named Tam Ossel get promoted twice in one year for charm and then dismissed for embezzlement, and I attended neither celebration nor disgrace, and I slept fine both times. My world was a small room with excellent lighting and every figure balanced, and I loved it. I love it. Present tense. I'm choosing present tense.

A book with my name in it does not fit in that room.

By the time I reached the guildhall — its gilded threshold literally gilded, worn to bare brass in the middle where ten thousand feet had polished the guild's vanity into something almost honest — I had constructed a full and reasonable theory. The book was a curiosity. Curiosities belonged in the archive, sub-level two, shelf forty-one, between a taxidermied griffin chick and a jar of preserved tears (provenance disputed). Tomorrow I would deposit it, log the deposit, and by month's end it would be one of four hundred and fourteen volumes in a dead man's collection, and I would be a man who had once had a strange afternoon. Everyone has strange afternoons. That's what makes them survivable — they end.

I climbed the outer stairs. The hall was loud with evening — traders settling accounts, assessors drinking their per diems, the great brass scales in the atrium swinging with the weight of other people's fortunes. Nobody looked at me. Three people greeted me by title rather than name. One asked me to confirm a figure from memory, which I did, correctly, and was thanked by having already turned away.

Ordinary. All of it ordinary. The safest feeling in the world, and I stood in the middle of it breathing it in like a man outside a bakery.

Then, crossing the atrium toward the archive stairs, I passed the notice board where the guild posts its internal bulletins — duty rosters, price amendments, the endless administrative weather of a large organization. And there, pinned at eye level, fresh ink, official seal:

*NOTICE OF SEIZURE REVIEW. Estate of Aldous Vessing, deceased. Special inventory audit ordered by direction of Guildmaster Corvane. All volumes and documents from said estate to be held for personal inspection. No item to be archived, released, or copied pending review.*

Dated today. Dated this afternoon — after Brannick and I had finished, after the wagons, after I'd put the book in my satchel.

Corvane. The guildmaster himself, who had spoken to me exactly twice in six years, once to tell me my figures were acceptable and once to tell me to stop humming, had personally ordered a hold on the Vessing papers. On the same day a century-old book coughed up my name.

Coincidence, I told myself. Estates draw attention. Rich dead men draw attention. Corvane audits everything worth auditing; it's why the guild fears him and why the crown tolerates us.

Coincidence, I told myself, climbing down to my cot in the clerks' wing, past the tallow lamps, past the other invisible men already snoring.

Coincidence, I told myself, lying in the dark with the book on my chest, rising and falling with my breath like something sleeping.

But here is what thirty years of ledgers teaches you, the one lesson that outranks accuracy: numbers don't lie, but they also don't reassure. When a column balances, you don't feel relief — you feel *verified*. And nothing in my safe, small, balanced life verified anymore. The date was right. The name was right. The guildmaster's order was timed like a trap snapping shut around an afternoon I couldn't un-have.

I lay awake listening to the rain on the roof tiles, and somewhere past midnight, in the honest dark where I do my best accounting, I admitted the thing I'd been refusing to enter in any column all day:

The book hadn't said *Xanthe Yarrow dies* or *Xanthe Yarrow triumphs* or anything with an ending I could file and forget. It said *the gate opens*. And I don't know why — I have no reason, no memory, no justification I can point to — but when I read those words, some part of me that I keep in a drawer, under lock, beneath the neat and invisible clerk, had recognized them. The way you recognize a hymn you've never been taught. The way a house recognizes its owner's step.

*No keeper ever does,* the margin had said.

I didn't sleep. Around the fourth bell, I got up, lit my lamp, and opened the book to the page with today's date, telling myself I was only checking the ink again, only verifying, only doing my job.

Below the line about keepers, new in the margin — or old, and I'd missed it, though I would swear on every balanced ledger of my life that it hadn't been there — a final line waited in that beautiful, patient hand:

*The guild knows what you are. Ask them whose name signs the third ledger.*

Outside, far off across the sleeping city, a bell began to ring. Not the hour bell. Not any bell I knew.

It sounded, if you asked me — and nobody was asking, nobody ever asks — like a gate, opening.
