## 4. What the Inquisitor Burns

The problem with burning a man in a harbor city is the wind.

I had learned this in Saltmere, where the smoke of my first pyre blew sideways into a fish market and set three stalls alight, and I had learned it again in Dunmark, where the wind carried the ash into the governor's garden and he complained about it for a week. Cinderport's wind came off the water in long gray gusts that smelled of brine and coal, and it wanted very much to push my fire into the tannery district, which would have been inconvenient for everyone except, perhaps, the tanners' competitors.

So we built the pyre in the customs yard instead, behind the seawall, where the wind broke and died, and where the only things that could catch were the man himself and the scaffold beneath him.

I do not enjoy this work. I want to say that clearly, at least once, to whoever reads these reports after me — though no one reads them after me; I read them before me, the ones written by my predecessors, in the same careful hand, with the same careful phrasing, *subject was uncooperative*, *subject wept*, *subject recanted falsely*, *cleansing achieved*. The reports are all the same because the work is all the same, and the work is all the same because heresy is all the same: a person who knows something they should not, and will not surrender it.

Tobias Merrow knew something he should not.

He was fifty-one years old, a cooper by trade, which is to say he made barrels, which is to say his hands were strong and patient and entirely unsuited to what was about to happen to him. He had gatekeeper blood — thin, third-generation, diluted the way blood always dilutes when people run from their own names — but he had made the mistake of keeping his grandmother's hymnal. Not even a dangerous hymnal. A child's primer, with the gate-hymns written out in notation so simple a six-year-old could plunk them on a dulcimer. He kept it in a false bottom under his good boots, which is where everyone keeps the thing that damns them, because people believe hiding a thing is the same as being safe from it.

It never has been. Hiding is just confession with better posture.

"Master Merrow," I said, standing before the pyre with my ledger open on its stand, "do you wish to make final statement?"

He looked at me. His face was already smoke-colored from the night in the cells, and his lips were cracked, and he said, "You have the wrong man."

"I don't," I said. "But I'll note it."

That is the part people never understand about the inquisition, the part that makes them call us monsters when monsters are, at least, honest about enjoying themselves. We are not cruel. Cruelty requires appetite. What we have is procedure, and procedure is worse than cruelty, because cruelty ends when the appetite is sated and procedure does not end until the paperwork is complete.

I opened to the correct page. Form Seven, Cleansing of a Confirmed Line, subsection C, Diluted Blood, Non-Flight Case. There is a subsection for everything. There is a subsection for gatekeepers who flee (Form Nine), gatekeepers who fight (Form Ten, rarely used, they die quickly), gatekeepers who turn crown-loyal and inform on their kin (Form Eleven, and yes, we burn those too, eventually, because a knife that can be bought once can be bought twice). Tobias Merrow was a Form Seven-C, which meant the rite took eleven minutes and required two verses of the Crown Litany and one verse of the Severance Hymn, sung by me alone, since my choir-acolyte, Brother Fenwick, was below decks being seasick, which he had been for the entire voyage from the capital and would be, I suspected, for the rest of his short career.

"The Crown remembers," I began.

The crowd was small. That is the way of harbor cities; they are busy places, and a burning is an appointment you keep only if your curiosity outweighs your trade. Perhaps forty people stood along the seawall, fishermen mostly, and a few dockhands, and one woman in a green shawl who did not look away even once, and whom I marked mentally for follow-up, because the ones who do not look away are either very brave or very guilty, and both categories interest me.

"The Crown remembers what was taken," I continued. "The Crown remembers what was hidden. The Crown remembers the gates, and the keys, and the hands that turned them."

Tobias Merrow began to cry. Quietly. The way men cry when they have decided dignity is still available to them. I have watched hundreds of people die and I will tell you the truth that no ballad will: almost none of them curse us. They cry for their mothers, or their children, or their barrels — this one, I think, genuinely mourned his barrels, half-finished in his shop, staves split and soaking, a season's work going to rot — and some few of them pray, and none of it changes anything, and I write it all down.

Because that is the other thing about procedure. Procedure insists that every death be witnessed by ink. If a cleansing happens and no ledger records it, then it did not happen, and if it did not happen, then the Crown's memory has a hole in it, and holes are how heresy grows back. So I write. I write the name, the trade, the bloodline fraction, the offense, the resistance offered, the last words. I have written four hundred and six entries in eleven years of service. I could recite perhaps forty of them. The others live in the ledgers, sleeping, the way everything sleeps in ledgers, waiting for someone with a reason to open the book.

Someone always has a reason, eventually. That is the whole history of the world, if you ask me: someone opening a book.

* * *

The fire caught properly on the second attempt, the wind having died at precisely the right moment, which I noted in the margin as providence, though privately I noted it as weather. Brother Fenwick arrived midway through the Litany, pale and damp, and sang his part a half-beat behind me, which is technically a liturgical error and practically a mercy, because it meant the hymn lasted longer, and longer hymns mean calmer crowds. Crowds are like dogs. They can smell hesitation, and they forgive it if you dress it in ritual.

When it was done — and it takes less time than people think, that is another truth the ballads get wrong, the whole affair is over almost before the screaming starts, the body gives up its argument early — I closed my ledger and dismissed the crowd with the traditional words, *the Crown thanks you for your witness*, and the crowd dispersed the way crowds do, relieved, hungry, already talking about supper.

Except the woman in the green shawl. She stayed.

She was perhaps sixty, dockside-worn, with the forearms of someone who had hauled rope for forty years, and she walked up to me while Brother Fenwick was dousing the embers, and she stopped three paces away, which is exactly the distance at which one can be heard without being touched, and she said:

"She wasn't a witch."

"No," I agreed. "Witchcraft is not among our charges."

"She was a good woman."

"That is also not among our charges." I closed the ledger strap. "Goodness is not evidence of anything, madam. I have burned good people and pardoned wicked ones, and the ledgers weigh neither."

"You'll burn for it," she said. "Someday. Someone'll build a pyre for you, and there'll be a woman standing there with a book, writing down your last words, and you'll say something small and stupid like everyone does, and she won't care."

I considered her. This happens perhaps twice a year, this moment, when a stranger looks at me and sees not the office but the woman inside it, and tells me my future as though it were news. Most inquisitors, I am told, have a prepared answer. Mine was: "Probably. But I will have the better handwriting."

She spat at my feet, which is a crime, technically, contempt toward the Crown's officer, Form Twelve, though I let it go, because Form Twelve citations against grieving dockwomen generate paperwork that benefits no one, and I have always believed paperwork should earn its keep.

"What was her name?" I asked.

That surprised her. Good. Surprise is useful; it opens people faster than kindness does.

"Myra," she said, grudgingly. "Myra Merrow. His wife. She ran the hymn school, back when there was one. Taught the little ones the songs their grandmothers sang. She wasn't hurting anyone."

"And where is Myra Merrow now?"

"Gone. Three days before you landed. Someone tipped her off." The woman's chin came up, defiant, daring me to arrest her for knowing it. "Vanished clean. You're too late for the ones that matter, inquisitor. You got the old man because he was too stubborn to leave his shop."

I wrote that down. I write everything down; I told you. *Subject's spouse fled prior to arrival. Source of warning unknown.* And underneath, in the private shorthand I use for things that will matter later: *who?*

Because here is what the woman in the green shawl did not know, and what Tobias Merrow had not known, and what nearly no one in Cinderport knew: Myra Merrow was not important because she taught children's hymns. She was important because of who her students' grandchildren might become. Bloodlines thin, madam, but they do not break, not entirely, not ever — a drop survives in the barrel-stave, in the ship's timber, in the child who hums a tune she cannot name and whose mother says, *where did you learn that*, and the child says, *nowhere, it's just in my head*. The Crown has spent a hundred years trying to drain a hundred casks, and every year we find one more drop, and every year the finding gets harder, and lately —

Lately the findings have gotten strange.

* * *

That evening I sat in the harbormaster's borrowed office — the inquisition travels light and borrows heavily; we occupy rooms the way water occupies a hull, without asking — and I reviewed the dispatches that had come up from the capital aboard the same ship as me.

There were three. The first was routine: confirmation of my commission, the list of Cinderport households flagged for survey, nineteen names, most of them merchants with gatekeeper ancestry three generations back, none of them urgent. The second was a requisition correction regarding Brother Fenwick's seasickness remedies, which I approved with the weary generosity of a commander who has learned that acolytes vomit more productively when not resentful.

The third was not routine.

It was from the Office of Records, which is the inquisition's memory, and it concerned a seizure. A guild estate, here in Cinderport — the Gilded Threshold, that fat and respectable trading company whose ledgers are famous in four cities for their immaculacy — had surrendered certain assets under the Crown's tithe, and among those assets had been found books. Old books. And one of the old books, per the Records Office's cataloguing clerk, had been flagged by the automatic procedures — date-matching, they call it, a newfangled arithmetic that cross-references dates in recovered texts against the civil calendar — and the flag had come back positive.

A century-old prophecy text, the clerk wrote, containing a date that falls within the current week, associated with a personal name.

They had not included the name. Of course they hadn't. Names in prophecy texts are classified above my clearance, which is itself above the clearance of everyone I have ever met, which means the name is known to perhaps nine people in the world, and I am not one of them, and the fact that I am not one of them irritated me more than I can politely express.

What the dispatch did include was an instruction: *Inquisitor Vail to prioritize survey of Gilded Threshold personnel records. Cross-reference guild roster against flagged parameters. Report directly to the Office of Records. Do not delegate.*

Do not delegate. In eleven years, I had received that instruction twice. Once, it had led me to a smuggler with royal blood in his heels. Once, it had led me to a well.

I sat with the dispatch for a long time, listening to the harbor sounds through the shuttered window — the gulls, the winches, the endless complaint of rope — and I thought about the woman in the green shawl, and how she had said *you're too late for the ones that matter*, and how she had been wrong, because the ones that matter are never the ones running. The ones that matter are the ones who don't know yet. The cooper's wife, humming in some safe house. Or a clerk somewhere, a bookkeeper, a person of no consequence whatsoever, walking around this city tonight with a name in his head that matches a name in a book, entirely unaware that the most efficient killing machine in the northern kingdoms has just been pointed at him by a piece of arithmetic.

I dipped my pen. I wrote my report to the Records Office: *Dispatch received. Survey commencing. Gilded Threshold roster requested from guildmaster under standard tithe pretext. Will report findings within the week.*

And then, because I am thorough, and thoroughness is the only prayer I know, I added a line of my own, unprompted, unofficial, the kind of line that a stricter inquisitor would have struck:

*Query: in the flagged text, does the associated date precede or follow the associated name's discovery? I ask because in my experience, prophecy is rarely prediction. It is usually memory, arriving early.*

I sealed it. I did not send it that night. I held it a day, which is against procedure, and then sent it anyway, which is also against procedure, and I recorded neither delay nor deviation in my log, which is the third thing against procedure, and I mention all this only to demonstrate that I am capable of recognizing heresy when I commit it myself.

The wind off the water smelled of ash for two more days. By the third day it smelled only of brine and coal again, and Cinderport went back to its barrels and its barges, and somewhere in that city a guild clerk was sharpening a stylus over a column of numbers, unaware, innocent, alive.

I intended to find him before the week was out.

Procedure demanded nothing less.
