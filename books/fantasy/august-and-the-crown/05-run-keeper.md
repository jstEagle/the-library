## 5. Run, Keeper

The knock came at the hour when even the lamps in the Gilded Threshold grow honest—too tired to lie about how late it is.

I want it noted that I almost didn't answer. I had a ledger open on my desk, a cup of tea gone cold enough to qualify as a pond, and a life that fit inside four walls like a coin fits in a palm: snug, worn smooth, worth exactly what everyone agreed it was worth. Answering doors after midnight is not a habit of unremarkable men. Unremarkable men sleep. I had built my whole existence on the principle of sleeping through things.

But the knock came again, three taps and then one, which is the guild's old signal for *urgent but deniable*, and some habits are carved deeper than prudence.

I opened the door on Merrick Sallow, who kept the guild's receiving room and its secrets in roughly equal disrepair. He was wearing his coat inside out, which among Threshold men is either a fashion statement or a warning, and given that Merrick considered matching buttons a form of vanity, I assumed the latter.

"You're marked," he said.

"Good evening to you too."

"Xanthe." He said my name the way you'd say *fire* in a crowded theater—not loudly, just with the appropriate sense of consequences. "Corvane's put watchers on you. Two at the fish gate, one on your stair. They're calling it an audit."

"An audit of me?"

"An audit of everything you've touched this month. Which is a polite way of saying they're deciding whether you're evidence or an asset." He glanced past my shoulder into my small, tidy room—the bed made with corners sharp enough to draw blood, the shelf of ledgers in perfect order—and something in his face went soft and sorry. "You should sit down."

"I'd rather stand. Standing people can run."

"That's the spirit," he said grimly. "Because running is next on the agenda."

* * *

Here is what I knew by the time we reached the receiving room, moving through the guildhall's back corridors like two men smuggling their own shadows:

One. The prophecy book from the Vell estate seizure was gone from my cataloguing table. Not reshelved—gone. And catalogued items do not simply walk; I would know, I've spent nine years making sure of it.

Two. Corvane had asked for me twice today, and both times been told I was at supper, because I had been at supper, because I am a man who keeps schedules, and apparently my punctuality had been noted with more interest than usual.

Three. The third ledger—the one I'd found behind the false back of the archive cabinet, the one listing a century of payments to keep a gate *sealed and starving*, signed in my family's name like a brand on livestock—that ledger was currently wrapped in oilcloth against my chest, because I had made the singular mistake of being thorough, and thoroughness, it turns out, is a capital offense.

"They'll want the book," Merrick said. We were in the receiving room now, and he was pulling things from drawers with the speed of a man who has rehearsed this. A purse. A knife with more rust than edge. A pass-stamp for the harbor gates, its seal a generation out of date. "And the ledger. And probably you, if the crown's inquisitors get a say. Corvane's had a bird from the palace since yesterday. Whatever's in that prophecy, someone upstairs wants it handled quietly."

"It's a book of nonsense," I said. "Riddles and dates. Half of it's already wrong."

"Is it?" He looked at me over the drawer. "Then why did you copy three pages of it into your own hand before you reported it?"

I hadn't told anyone about that.

"Merrick—"

"Don't," he said. "Don't explain. Explaining takes time, and time is the one thing the watchers won't give you." He pushed the purse across the table. "There's forty crowns there. It's not a fortune. It's a fortune-shaped object that will buy you a week."

"A week of what?"

"Of being alive somewhere else." He said it without drama, which somehow made it worse. Merrick Sallow had taught me my letters, back when the guild took in gutter-children as cheap labor and called it charity. He had shown me how to hold a stylus so the ink didn't blot, how to spot a forged seal, how to be invisible in a building full of ambitious men. He was, in every way that mattered and none that appeared on any ledger, the closest thing I had to a father.

And he was packing me for exile at midnight.

"Why are you helping me?" I asked. "If I'm marked, helping me marks you."

He smiled, and it was the saddest thing I'd seen all year, and I had spent the year reading a ledger full of children sold for bottled storms. "Because thirty years ago somebody helped me off a dock in Vell with nothing but a pass-stamp and a lie about my name. Because the guild raised us on debts, boy, and I pay mine." He shoved the coat into my arms. "Now go out the fish gate."

"The fish gate has a watcher."

"Not anymore. I sent him to check a shipment that doesn't exist. You have perhaps ten minutes before he discovers the shipment and I discover a very creative explanation." He gripped my shoulder, hard, once. "Xanthe. Don't come back. Don't send word. Don't be clever. Be elsewhere."

"And if I'm innocent? If I'm just a clerk who read a strange book?"

"Then you're an innocent clerk in a city where the crown burns people for their grandfathers' names," he said, "and you should still run. Innocence is a fine quality. It travels badly."

* * *

I want to tell you I left with dignity. That I walked out of the Gilded Threshold with my chin up and my grief folded neatly, like a man closing a ledger at the end of a fiscal year.

What actually happened is that I stood in the middle of my own room for four minutes—I counted, because counting is what I do instead of feeling—and tried to decide what to take.

Four minutes. In a building where watchers were multiplying like arithmetic errors.

The problem was that my entire life fit into categories, and categories are comforting right up until they betray you. There were the ledgers I'd kept, nine years of them, every trade and toll and tithe recorded in my careful hand. Evidence of competence. Evidence of a life. But ledgers are heavy, and besides—if the guild wanted to erase me, my ledgers would only help them do it thoroughly.

There were the gifts. A scarf Wren-of-no-relation had left me three winters ago, though she'd deny it. A bottle of storm-wine Corvane gave me the year I caught the Ashford forgery, still sealed, because I'd been saving it for an occasion and had never quite believed I deserved one. Small objects that proved I had been, once, someone's colleague. Someone's almost-friend.

I took none of them. I took the things that weren't mine to leave behind.

The third ledger went inside my shirt, bound tight with the oilcloth. My grandfather's iron stylus went in my boot, because I am a clerk to the marrow and a stylus in the boot felt like the correct amount of paranoid. And then, at the last, I stood in front of my own bed and did the thing I will regret until I die, which is nothing—I simply looked at it. Four posts, one blanket, corners square. The shape of a safe life, abandoned mid-sentence.

Somewhere below, a door closed that shouldn't have.

I went out the window.

* * *

Cinderport at night is a city pretending to be asleep, which is to say it's awake and lying. Fish-gut lanterns burn green along the harbor road. Drunks argue theology with gulls. Somewhere a tide-bell counts hours nobody asked it to keep.

I moved through it the way I'd learned to move through the guildhall: quickly, apologetically, and along walls. The fish gate stood open ahead of me, torchlit, and for one merciful moment the watcher's post was empty—

"Ledger-keeper Yarrow."

I stopped. Of course I stopped. Nine years of obedience doesn't wash out in one bad night; it pools in the boots.

Corvane stepped out from between two drying racks, and I understood, with the particular clarity of the doomed, that Merrick's ten minutes had been spent buying me a head start, and that the head start ended here.

Guildmaster Corvane was not a large man. He didn't need to be. He had the kind of presence that filled rooms the way water fills a hull—quietly, and from below. He wore his gray coat and his gray beard and his expression of mild, perpetual disappointment in the universe's bookkeeping, and standing in front of him I was nineteen years old again, presenting my first flawless inventory, waiting to be told I'd done well.

He never told me I'd done well. He told me I'd done *correctly*, which is different, and which I mistook for love for nearly a decade.

"Xanthe." He said my name gently. That was the worst part. He always said it gently, right up until the sentence turned. "Where are you going at this hour?"

"Couldn't sleep, sir. Thought I'd take air."

"At the harbor gate. With a packed bag." His eyes went to the satchel on my shoulder, and I watched him perform the arithmetic—he was, after all, the finest accountant in the free cities, and I was a sum that wasn't adding up. "Merrick Sallow emptied the contingency drawer tonight. Did you know that? Forty crowns, a harbor stamp, a knife. All signed out under a requisition he'll swear was mine."

"He wouldn't—"

"He would, and he has, and it breaks my heart, because I'll have to report him." Corvane sighed, and for a moment—just a moment—the gentleness in his face was real. I'm sure of it. I choose to be sure of it. "You found something in the Vell estate, boy. Something old. And instead of bringing it to me, you hid it, and copied it, and now the crown is asking me questions I cannot answer without giving them either you or myself. Do you understand the position you've put me in?"

"Yes, sir," I said, because I did, perfectly, and because understanding has never once saved anybody.

"I can fix this." He took a step closer. Behind him, at the gate, a watcher had returned and was watching with the professional blankness of a man being paid by the hour. "Give me the book. Give me whatever else you took. Come inside, and tomorrow this is an administrative error, and in a year you're junior archivist, and none of it ever happened. That's the offer, Xanthe. It's a good offer. It's the best offer you will ever receive, because no one else in this world is going to offer you anything at all."

And here is the thing I learned about myself on the fish-slippery cobbles of Cinderport, at half past midnight, with my heart going like a debt collector's knuckles:

I believed him.

Every word. I believed he could fix it, believed in the junior archivistship, believed in the warm administrative future where I filed things forever and was never remarkable and never in danger. Some traitor part of me had already taken three steps toward the guildhall and started composing the apology.

But my hand had gone, without consulting me, to the place under my shirt where the third ledger sat against my ribs. And through the oilcloth I could feel the edges of the pages, and on those pages, in a hundred years of careful entries, was my family's name beside the words *sealed and starving*.

They had paid the guild to find us. Generation after generation. Every payment logged, every keeper located, every one of them delivered like goods received. And Corvane had known—or half-known, or chosen not to know, which in accounting is the same crime with better penmanship.

My whole life, I had thought I was overlooked. I hadn't been overlooked. I had been *inventoried*.

"No," I said.

Corvane's face didn't change. That frightened me more than anger would have. "Think carefully."

"I've thought about nothing else since I opened that cabinet, sir." My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "You taught me that every entry tells the truth eventually. You said the books always confess. Well—" I backed toward the gate, toward the watcher, toward the open dark beyond it, "—yours just did."

For a heartbeat, the finest accountant in the free cities looked at me the way a man looks at a figure that refuses to balance. Then he lifted two fingers, and the watcher moved, and I ran.

* * *

I ran the way clerks run, which is to say badly, with a satchel bouncing and my lungs filing formal complaints. Down the harbor road, past the green fish-lanterns, past a drunk who offered to sell me a miracle for a copper. Behind me, boots—two sets, maybe three, keeping pace with the patience of professionals who know the city better than I do and the night better than anyone.

I cut through the tannery rows because only a desperate man cuts through the tannery rows, and desperation, it turns out, is an excellent navigator. The stink hit like a wall and kept going through me, and I heard my pursuers slow at the edge of it, weighing their salaries against their nostrils, and I gained twenty steps I would spend the rest of the night repaying with interest.

The harbor stamp got me past the night-wharf gate—the seal was a generation stale, but the guard was younger than the seal and twice as bored. I went down the ladder to the low wharves, where the boats are smaller and the questions fewer, and I stood there gasping in the dark with the black water slapping the pilings, and I realized I had no plan whatsoever.

Forty crowns. A dead man's stylus. A stolen ledger. A city full of people paid to find me.

That was the inventory. That was everything. Nine years of careful, invisible living, reduced to a line item: *assets removed from premises, value negligible.*

A bell rang somewhere above—harbor watch, or worse, the guild's own signal, the one that meant *close the doors*. I thought of the guildhall sealing itself behind me, bolt by bolt, window by window, the great brass scales over the entrance gleaming in the torchlight while the men inside decided what to write in the space where my name used to be. *Departed. Circumstances unclear. No further entries.*

Grief is a strange thing in the middle of a chase. It arrives without scheduling itself around your convenience. I stood on the low wharf with hunters closing in and the sea smelling of salt and rot, and what undid me was not fear—it was the memory of my desk. My lamp. My cold tea. The small warm cell of a life I had built so carefully, brick by brick of routine, believing that if I was only useful enough and quiet enough, the world would let me keep it.

It had never been mine. That was the truth underneath the truth. The guild hadn't raised me; it had *held* me, the way you hold collateral. Every kindness had a margin note. Every corner of my snug little world had been drawn by someone else's hand, in someone else's ink, for someone else's purposes. I had loved a cage and called it a home, and now the cage had locked its doors from the outside, and all I felt was homesickness for a thing that had been hunting me all along.

I wiped my face with my sleeve, because even breaking down, I am economical.

Then I saw the boat.

It was a smuggler's skiff, riding low and riding quiet, tied up in the shadow of the customs house where honest ships don't bother. At its tiller sat a figure I recognized—the woman who'd left me a scarf three winters ago and denied it ever since, coiling rope with the unhurried hands of someone who has been waiting exactly long enough to enjoy being right.

"Wren," I said.

"Ledger-keeper." She looked me over—the satchel, the oilcloth outline against my chest, the tannery stink, the general condition of a man being hunted. Her mouth curved, not kindly. "Let me guess. You've recently become interesting."

"I've recently become evidence."

"Even better. Those appreciate in value." She tossed a mooring line down to me, casual as dealing cards. "Well? Are you getting in, or are you planning to stand on that wharf composing your memoirs until the inquisitors arrive to edit them?"

I looked back once, at the city where I had been born and shelved. Torches were moving along the harbor road in the orderly patterns of a search conducted by professionals. Somewhere in the dark behind them, the Gilded Threshold sat sealed and golden and utterly indifferent, and Corvane was presumably already drafting the entry that would make me a rounding error.

I climbed down into the boat.

"Where to?" Wren asked, pushing off from the pilings, oars biting the black water.

I had no destination. I had a ledger full of payments and a prophecy with my name in it and a grandfather's stylus pressing cold against my ankle, and every one of those pointed the same direction—south, away, toward whatever the words *the gate opens* were waiting to mean.

"South," I said. "Anywhere south. And Wren—" my voice cracked on her name, and I hated it, and kept talking anyway, "—thank you. For the boat. For the scarf. For—"

"Don't." She rowed, eyes forward, voice flat as a calm sea, which is how she says everything true. "Gratitude goes in the ledger at the end of the trip. Everything does." A pause, and then, quieter, almost too soft for the water: "You can cry if you need to. Just do it quietly. Sound carries."

So I did. Quietly, in the bottom of a smuggler's skiff, while Cinderport shrank behind us into a smear of green lanterns and lying lights—grieving, properly at last, for the small safe life that had never actually existed, and for the boy who had lived in it so faithfully, keeping his perfect books, never once noticing that every page had been written for him.

Behind us, a bell tolled the closing of the harbor gates.

Ahead, the sea ran black and open, and somewhere south of it, a date with my name on it was coming due.
