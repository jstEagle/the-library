## 6. The Salt Road South

I had never been on a road before. That sounds like a small confession, the kind of thing a man admits over ale with a self-deprecating laugh, but I mean it literally. In twenty-six years of ledger-keeping for the Gilded Threshold, the longest journey I had ever taken was from my cot in the clerks' dormitory to the counting room, and back, and occasionally to the privy, which in winter felt like an expedition. Roads were things that happened to other people. Roads were entries in a column: *freight, southbound, salt, 40 cwt.* I had written thousands of roads without ever standing on one.

Now I was standing on one, and it was trying to kill me.

The Salt Road south of Cinderport was not made of salt, as I had assumed from the name. It was made of mud, and gravel, and the particular variety of sharp stone that seems designed by someone with a grudge against boots. My boots—guild-issue, good for ten thousand hours of sitting—had developed opinions about this within the first hour. By the second hour they were filing formal complaints. By nightfall I had stopped listening to them and started listening to my feet instead, which had moved past words to a kind of low, continuous screaming.

I walked anyway. Behind me, Cinderport's chimneys smoked against a bruised evening sky, and somewhere back there was a guildhall with its doors locked to me, and a guildmaster who had signed my name into a book of payments, and inquisitors who burned people whose only crime was being born to the wrong bloodline. Ahead of me was nothing, which struck me as a considerable improvement.

I had the third ledger wrapped in oilcloth against my chest, strapped there like a second heart. I had my grandfather's iron stylus in my pocket, where it pressed against my hip with every step like a small, insistent finger reminding me: *you are carrying something. You are carrying something. You are carrying something.*

And I had exactly no plan whatsoever, which for a man who had spent his life planning other people's accounts was its own special circle of discomfort.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about fleeing for your life: it is mostly walking. The stories make it out to be all narrow escapes and dramatic leaps from windows, and certainly I'd had one window-leap already, thank you very much, but the truth is that terror is exhausting and the body eventually insists on boring logistics. You walk. You drink water. You eat whatever you thought to steal from the guild kitchen, which in my case was two apples and half a wheel of cheese that I now understood had been intended for someone else's lunch, and I felt briefly guilty about that, and then remembered the guild had sold my family out for a century, and ate the rest of the cheese.

By the third day I had learned three things about roads.

One: everyone on a road is pretending. The carter who nods pleasantly at you is wondering if you're worth robbing. The pilgrim with the downcast eyes is counting your coin purse. The soldier asleep under a hedge has one hand on his knife even in dreams. A road is just a ledger where everyone's entry is false.

Two: mud does not care about your problems. Neither does rain, sun, blisters, or the particular ache in your shoulders that comes from sleeping sitting up against a milestone because you don't trust inns anymore—inns have registers, registers have names, names get reported.

Three: a man walking alone, badly, looking over his shoulder every hundred paces, might as well be wearing a sign. Which is how, on the morning of the fourth day, she found me.

* * *

I heard the cart before I saw it—the creak of bad axles and worse singing—and when it crested the rise behind me I stepped off the road into the gorse, because four days of fugitive living had taught me at least that much. The cart was drawn by a mule with the expression of a tax collector, and driven by a woman who sat sideways on the bench with her boots up on the rail, reins looped around one wrist, eating an apple with the concentration of someone defusing a bomb.

She saw me immediately, of course. Hiding in gorse is not the sophisticated art I believed it to be.

"You're doing it wrong," she called, without slowing. "If you're going to hide, don't stand up in it."

"I wasn't hiding."

"You were absolutely hiding." She reined in—not for me, I realized, but because the mule had decided independently that this was a fine place to stop, and arguing with the mule was apparently not among her ambitions. She looked me over with the frank appraisal of a horse trader. Mud to the knees. Guild boots ruined. Two days of beard on a face that had never needed more than a weekly razor. "You've been on this road four days."

"How could you possibly know that?"

"Because you walk like a man reading a map he hates." She took another bite of apple. "Also you've got the look. Fled-something look. Everyone who comes down the Salt Road southbound in autumn has either fled something or is chasing something, and chasers walk faster and buy their meals."

"I'm not buying anything."

"No," she agreed. "You're clearly not." She swung her boots down off the rail and studied me properly then, and I noticed her eyes were an odd color, a gray-green like the sea after weather, and that she had a knife at her belt with a handle worn smooth by a great deal of use. "Ledger-keeper," she said suddenly.

"What?"

"Your hands. Ink in the knuckle creases, callus on the middle finger, right hand. You write fast and hold the pen too hard. Guild-trained, probably Threshold—they press too hard, all of them, like they're engraving their own tombstones." She said it lightly, but I felt the word *Threshold* go through me like a fishhook. "Relax. I'm not one of Vail's hounds. If I were, you'd already be ash and I'd be complaining about the smell."

"You know who Seraphine Vail is."

"Everyone knows who Seraphine Vail is. She's the reason the Salt Road south of Grayfen doesn't have any gatekeeper families left on it." Something flickered across her face when she said it—there and gone, quick as a fish turning—but I was too tired and too frightened to read it properly. "Name's Wren. I run cargo. Small cargo. Specialized cargo."

"What kind of specialized cargo?"

Her smile was quick and crooked. "Small impossibilities."

* * *

I want to record, for the sake of honesty, that everything in me said *no*. Everything in me that had survived twenty-six years in the Gilded Threshold by being unremarkable, by keeping my head down and my columns straight and my existence so quiet that even the guildmaster sometimes forgot I existed—that entire architecture of invisibility screamed at me to nod politely, let the cart pass, and keep walking alone into the hills where no one would ever find me.

But here is the arithmetic, and I am a ledger-keeper, so I did the arithmetic. Alone, I would die on this road. Not dramatically—nothing so flattering. I would simply run out of food, or sleep in the wrong ditch, or meet the wrong traveler, or fail to talk my way past the toll at Grayfen, because I had never in my life talked my way past anything. Every skill I owned was a skill for rooms with ledgers in them. Out here I was an entry in someone else's column, and the column was headed *losses.*

Whereas this woman—this stranger with the knife and the sea-colored eyes and the mule with the soul of a bureaucrat—knew the road. Knew Vail's reach. Had looked at my hands and read my life in thirty seconds, which meant she could also read the danger on me, which meant traveling with her was dangerous, yes, but traveling alone was certain.

Certainty, I had recently learned, was just risk wearing better clothes.

"I need to go south," I said. "Past Grayfen. Toward the Marchlands."

"Everyone needs to go somewhere. Passage costs money."

"I don't have money." I hesitated. This was the part where a sensible man lied. "I have information."

"Information." She said it the way Corvane used to say *charity.*

"The most wanted document in the free cities right now," I said, "is a ledger. Third ledger of the Gilded Threshold. A century of payments. Names, dates, amounts—who paid whom to do what, and to keep what sealed and starving." I watched her face carefully. "There are people who would burn cities to read it. There are people who are burning people to suppress it."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. The mule, sensing drama and disapproving of it, began to walk, dragging the cart a few feet, and she let it.

"A page," she said finally.

"What?"

"You'll give me one page of it. My choice of page. And in exchange I take you south past Grayfen, feed you, and lose you somewhere sensible." She held up a finger before I could object. "Not the whole book. One page. Because if you give me the whole book, you're a fool, and if I take the whole book from a fool, I'm a thief, and I'm not having either of those on my cart."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then enjoy Grayfen. The toll-warden reports travelers' descriptions to the way-stations, the way-stations report to the inquisition, and Seraphine Vail reads reports the way other women read love letters." She shrugged. "You'll last maybe six days. Seven if you're lucky and ugly."

"I'm told I'm unremarkable."

"You *were* unremarkable. Then somebody wrote your name in a prophecy book and now you're the most remarkable thing on two legs between here and the sea." She said it flatly, watching me, and I went cold all over, because I had never told her that. Never mentioned the prophecy, the date, the words *the gate opens.* "Oh, don't look like that. A clerk flees the Threshold with a hidden ledger the same week the inquisitors start asking questions in every port from here to Cinderhaven? Word travels. Word always travels. I deal in impossibilities; I hear about them first."

The wind came up the valley, smelling of salt and coming winter, and the mule sighed the sigh of a creature who has seen every bargain ever struck and been unimpressed by all of them.

"One page," I said. "My choice of page."

"Your choice of—" She laughed, short and genuine. "Fine. Your choice. But choose knowing I'll know if it's worthless, and worthless pages void the contract."

"It won't be worthless."

"Then get up on the cart, keeper, before I come to my senses." She kicked a bundle of sacking off the bench beside her. "Rules of the road. You don't ask what's under the tarp. You don't touch the mule—he bites, and he holds grudges, and frankly I respect him for it. And when we pass checkpoints, you're my cousin Tam, who's simple, and you'll play him convincingly because you have the face for it."

"I do not have the face for it."

"You absolutely have the face for it. Congratulations. First honest work you'll have done in years."

I climbed up onto the bench beside her, and the cart lurched forward, and that was how I came to leave the Salt Road behind on wheels instead of feet, sitting next to a smuggler who traded in small impossibilities, with a stolen ledger against my chest and a page of it promised away.

It should have felt like another loss. Another piece of the safe small world pried loose and handed over. But as the miles unwound and the hills folded green and gray around us, I found myself—cautiously, suspiciously, the way you test ice with one foot—something adjacent to comfortable. She didn't ask questions she didn't need answered. She didn't fill silence with noise. When I dozed against the cart's rail, she woke me gently at the checkpoints rather than letting the warden's shadow fall across my face first.

That night, camped in a hollow with a small fire and worse stew, I took out the third ledger and turned its pages by firelight, looking for the page I would give her. Most of it was horror dressed in neat columns—payments to find families, payments to watch them, one terrible recurring line item marked only *maintenance* that I refused to think about yet. But near the end, in fresher ink, there were pages recording recent transactions. Payments to a network of river-smugglers. Routes. Rates. A name repeated in the margin in a clerk's careful hand: *the wren runs the fen route, reliable, discreet.*

I looked up at her across the fire. She was sharpening her knife and pretending not to watch me.

"Wren," I said. "As in the fen route?"

"As in the fen route," she said, not pausing the blade. "Guild's paid me twice to move things they didn't want seen. Never asked what. Now I'm starting to wonder."

"Then I have good news and bad news about the page you're owed."

"Bad news first. Always."

"The page I can give you is a page about you."

The knife stopped. For a moment the fire cracked and popped and the mule chewed with tremendous indifference, and Wren looked at me with those sea-gray eyes and I watched her do her own arithmetic—cargo, routes, a guild that pays for discretion and burns the discreet.

"Well," she said at last, quietly. "Ain't that just like a ledger. You go looking for one thing and find out you were in it all along." She pointed the knife at the book in my hands, not at me, though it was close enough to feel. "Show me."

I turned the pages around and passed the ledger across the fire, and she read her own name in a century-old conspiracy by firelight, and I understood—as clearly as I had ever understood anything—that I had just done the most dangerous thing available to me.

I had told the truth to someone who could still walk away.

She read for a long time. Then she closed the book, handed it back, and looked at the fire instead of at me.

"Grayfen tomorrow," she said. "There's a family at the tollhouse there you'll want to ask about. Gatekeeper stock, old line, kept their heads down same as yours." She fed a stick to the flames. "We'll want to reach them before Vail's people do. And keeper—if we're both in this book, then this isn't passage anymore. This is a convoy."

The fire settled. Somewhere out in the dark, a nightjar called, long and low, like a hymn sung by something that had forgotten the words.

I slept that night better than I had since the guildhall locked its doors, and hated myself a little for it, and slept anyway.
