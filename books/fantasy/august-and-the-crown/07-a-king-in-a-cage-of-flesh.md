## 7. A King in a Cage of Flesh

I have been dead for ninety-one years, and no one has had the courtesy to tell me.

That is not quite true. The crown knows. The crown tells me every morning, in its way, which is to say it reaches down through my skull with fingers made of other people's memories and turns me over like a page, checking that I am still legible. I am. Barely. The margins have rotted. But the important words are still there, and so the usurper keeps me, the way a miser keeps a coin too old to spend and too heavy to throw away.

My name was Aldric Vane. I say *was* because names, like everything else down here, belong to the past tense. I was crowned at nineteen in the Basilica of the Threshold, before the gate was sealed, before the gatekeepers were scattered like ash on wind, before a man I called brother put a circlet of black iron on his head and let it eat him from the ears inward. I am told there are still coins with my face on them, worn smooth by thumbs in market stalls. I hope they use my face to scrape wax. It would be nice to be useful.

Here is what I do now: I hang.

Not dramatically. There is no drama left in me; the crown skimmed that off years ago along with my anger, my appetite, most of my memory of my mother's face. I hang from chains in a cell beneath the palace of a man who wears my throne like a coat he stole from a corpse — which, in fairness, is exactly what it is. The chains are silver-cored, because silver remembers oaths, and mine were sworn into it. Every link is a small, patient accusation. We have gotten to know each other well, the chains and I. They do not gloat. That is more than can be said for the visitors.

* * *

The visitors come on a schedule, because cruelty in an organized state is always bureaucratic. Once a month — sometimes twice, when the crown is hungry — they bring me up two hundred steps to a room with a brazier and a chair, and the Hollow King sits across from me and lets the thing on his head drink.

I want to be clear about what that means, because the histories will get it wrong if they get written at all. He does not torture me. Torture would imply he wants something from me. What happens instead is worse and quieter: the crown opens, the way a flower opens if the flower were an account book, and it reads me. It takes inventory. And because I am the anchor — the last living oath tied to the gate it cannot open — whatever it skims off me goes somewhere. Into the seal. Into the lock. My memories are the grease in the hinges of my own prison.

So I ration myself. This is the discipline of my life now, such as it is: I hoard. Small things. The smell of rain on the palace flags the summer I turned twelve. The sound my sister made laughing with a mouthful of honey-bread. The exact weight of a gatekeeper's hand on my shoulder the day I was crowned — old Maren Yarrow, her palm rough as bark, saying *remember, majesty, the door swings both ways.* I keep these in a room inside myself with the door barred, and when the crown comes prying I feed it gravel instead: tax ledgers, harvest yields, the tedious etiquette of embassies long dissolved. Let it eat paperwork. Let it choke on the twelfth-century grain tariffs of a kingdom that no longer exists.

It has noticed. I think it has noticed. Sometimes, mid-reading, the Hollow King's eyes focus — his actual eyes, brown, human, terrified behind all that iron — and for half a breath I see the man I knighted at twenty-two, Corvane's predecessor's predecessor's liege, the boy who once wept at his own coronation because he understood what crowns cost. Then the iron closes over him like water over a stone, and the reading continues, and I go back to my cell with a little less of myself and a great deal more patience.

Patience is the one weapon they never thought to confiscate. You cannot chain a man's waiting. I have waited through nine decades in a body that should have crumbled, kept alive by the same oath that binds the gate: *while the king endures, the door stays shut.* They need me breathing the way a lock needs its key intact. So I breathe. Slowly. Spitefully. With tremendous attention to detail.

* * *

Today the schedule broke.

No visitor. Instead, the crown came to me — or rather, its attention did, pouring down through the stone like cold water finding a crack. I felt it pause above my cell, the way a wolf pauses at a fence line when something on the far side smells wrong.

And then I dreamed. Not my usual dreams — my usual dreams are inventory, the crown filing me away — but a real dream, one of mine, uninvited and unedited, which after ninety-one years is roughly as shocking as a sunrise in a cellar.

In the dream I stood in the gate chamber beneath the free cities, the place where my life ended without the decency of stopping. I knew it instantly, the way you know your own handwriting. Iron doors tall as cathedrals. Seven recesses around the arch, six of them dark, one still faintly warm, like a hearth gone out that morning. And standing before the doors, a figure with his back to me, writing on the air itself with a rod of grey iron.

A stylus. A ledger-keeper's tool. I knew that too, because Maren Yarrow carried one exactly like it, and she had tapped it against my crown the day I swore my oaths and said, *kings make promises, keeper, but ink makes them stick.*

"Turn around," I said, or tried to. In dreams your voice belongs to someone else.

He didn't turn. He was young — younger than me, young the way green wood is young — and he wore the plain, ink-stained clothes of a counting-house clerk, and he wrote with the quick, apologetic precision of a man who has spent his life being overlooked and has made a fortress of it. I recognized the posture. I had seen it a century ago on the shoulders of the last gatekeepers as they sang the door shut with the Hollow King's army howling on the far side of it.

Except this boy was writing on *this* side of the door. Our side. Now.

And in his margin — because even in dreams I could see it was a ledger, the whole world rendered as columns and entries — he had entered today's date. My date. The date the crown circles like a carrion bird, the date the iron above me has been counting toward with the eagerness of a debt collector.

Beside the date, in a small, careful hand: *the gate opens.*

I woke with my heart slamming, which is a miracle in itself; I had begun to suspect it had been repossessed. The chains clinked their dry commentary. Above me, through two hundred feet of stone and politics, the crown was screaming — not aloud, nothing so honest, but I felt it the way you feel weather change in an old wound. Something had slipped its ledger. Somewhere in the world, a name had appeared where no name should be, and the iron was very, very interested.

* * *

Let me tell you about the binding, since I am apparently the only one left who saw it whole, and since telling it is the only act of kingship available to me.

A hundred years ago — ninety-one, if we are being precise, and I am always precise now; precision is the last furniture in the house — the man who calls himself the Hollow King found the crown. I will not use his name. Not out of spite, though there is plenty. Out of arithmetic: the crown eats names, and I refuse to feed it his. Call him the Usurper, call him the Wearer, call him what the gatekeepers called him in their final records — *the Mouth*. He found the crown in the deep vaults under the old capital, where my grandfather's grandfather had sealed it after the first age learned what it was. It was not treasure. It was a mouth. An appetite wearing the shape of authority, forged — the gatekeepers believed — by something older than kingdoms, something that had looked at the way men obey shiny metal on heads and decided to make a career of it.

He put it on knowing. That is the part the songs will soften, if any singer is fool enough to sing them. He knew, and he put it on anyway, because he wanted the throne more than he wanted his own face, and the crown promised him the throne the way a loan-shark promises a house.

For three years he ruled well. That is the horror nobody survives hearing: the first years were *good*. Harvests up, borders quiet, justice swift and strangely fair. The crown is patient. It waters the garden before it eats the gardener. By year four he was forgetting meals. By year six he was forgetting his daughter's face, and then forgetting he'd had a daughter, and then — this I witnessed, from my own throne room, before they took me — he paused in the middle of sentencing a man to death and asked the court, gently, sincerely, *why are we all here?* And no one could answer him, and the crown answered for him, and the man died, and the question went on eating.

The gatekeepers rose against him in the seventh year. Maren Yarrow led them — the Yarrows were always the ledger-keepers of the order, the ones who tracked what the magic cost, and it was Maren who discovered the crown's accounting: that it paid for its power out of the wearer, and when the wearer ran empty, out of anyone standing near, and when *they* ran empty, out of the world's own seams. She brought me the figures herself. I remember the numbers better than I remember my mother's voice. I signed the order to seal the gate that same night, and I have had ninety-one years to decide whether it was the bravest thing I ever did or the coward's version of the same.

We sealed it. Six keys — six bloodlines, because the gate does not recognize objects, it recognizes *people*, it recognizes inheritance, it recognizes the slow stubborn persistence of families who agree to be doors — and the seventh key was the crown itself, locked outside. The rite took four days. On the fourth day the gatekeepers' hymn cracked, because the crown reached through the closing gap and began devouring the singers, and Maren Yarrow stepped into the gap herself and finished the verse alone, and the door shut with her on the wrong side of alive.

And the price — there is always a price; I run a kingdom of accountants, or I did — the price was an anchor. A living oath on our side of the door, to hold it shut from within. The gate chose me. Or Maren chose me, dying, with her last entry, and the gate agreed, and my own council chained me in this cellar before the usurper's troops finished taking the palace, because a dead anchor breaks the seal and a live one holds it, and so my survival became the kingdom's last wall.

They think they captured me that night. They did not. They *stored* me. There is a difference, and the difference is that stored things can be retrieved.

* * *

The dream came again on the third night. Same chamber, same boy, same iron stylus scratching its careful entries into the air. But this time he spoke, without turning, in the tone of a man reading aloud from a column he doesn't fully believe:

"There's a payment outstanding," he said. "Against this account. A survival, purchased. Do you know anything about purchased survivals, majesty?"

"I know all about them," I said. "I am one."

"Mm," said the boy, and wrote something down, and the scratch of the stylus was the loudest sound I had heard in ninety-one years, and I loved him for it, absurdly, completely, the way you love a key you've been carrying in a locked room.

Then the dream tilted, the way dreams do when something larger leans over them, and I saw — briefly, at the edge, the way you see a figure at the end of a corridor — a woman walking a salt road south with a smuggler's stride, and a child asleep against her side humming a hymn that made the dream's edges glow like heated iron, and an old man in a monastery lighting a candle with hands that shook and did not stop him. Keys. Walking. In the world. After a century of ash.

The gate is waking. Someone is gathering what the crown spent ninety years trying to burn.

I woke, and laughed — actually laughed, a rusted, appalling sound that brought the jailer running with his lamp, convinced the mad king below had finally broken. Let him think so. Let them all think so. Hope, in this place, is safest disguised as damage.

Because here is what I know that the crown does not, what it cannot know, though it devoured Maren Yarrow's dying mind and sifted it for a century: she hid a flaw in the seal. Deliberately. A single unsung note in the fourth verse, a hinge left oiled, a door that opens *from the inside* if — and only if — the keys come home willing. She told me once, tapping her iron stylus against her palm: *a lock with no way out isn't a lock, majesty. It's a grave. And graves make ghosts, and ghosts make tyrants.*

She built us a way out. She just needed someone to find the ledger.

Above me, the crown has stopped circling. It has turned south, all its hunger narrowing toward the free cities, toward Cinderport and its aqueducts and its guildhalls full of quiet, overlooked men with ink on their fingers. It thinks it is hunting.

It has no idea it is being *collected.*

I settle back against the stone. The chains murmur their old accusations, and I hush them like children. Ninety-one years I have hung here, patient as a promise, and tonight, for the first time, the waiting has a direction.

Somewhere above me, a boy with an iron stylus is writing today's date beside his own name.

Hold the pen straight, keeper. The door swings both ways — and I intend to walk through it under my own power, or not at all.
