## 8. The Hollow Court

There is a particular indignity in reviewing your own army when you cannot remember ordering it to march.

I stood on the balcony of the Obsidian Hall — my hall, I am assured, though the assurance came from a chamberlain whose eyes never once rose above my collarbone — and watched forty thousand soldiers move through the Marchlands below like ink poured across wet paper. Banners of black and bone-white. Siege engines tall as chapels. The whole magnificent apparatus of conquest, assembled at my word.

Except it had not been my word. It had been *the* word, spoken through my mouth while I sat in the back row of my own skull like a tenant who has fallen behind on rent, listening to the landlord give a speech in his voice.

"Your Radiance is pleased?" asked Chamberlain Ossric, who had served me for eleven years and had not looked me in the eye since the ninth.

"I am," said my mouth, before I could stop it. "The cities will kneel by midsummer."

Ossric bowed with the deep satisfaction of a man who has heard exactly what he was instructed to hear. And I thought, as I have thought every day for some years now: *midsummer*. I used to know what midsummer meant. Hay-cutting. The smell of cut grass and horse sweat. My sister's wedding had been at midsummer; she'd worn a crown of marigolds, which she complained about the entire day because marigolds, she said, were a flower for funerals, and she would be buried in them someday just to spite everyone.

She was right, as it happened. But that is another ledger entry.

* * *

Let me explain how one arrives at this arrangement, because I imagine you are wondering why a grown man permits a hat to conduct his foreign policy.

The answer is: gradually, and with excellent reasons at every step.

I was not always the Hollow King. I was Aldren Voss, third son of a Marchland baron, which meant I inherited a sword, a temper, and nothing else. When the old king died without an heir and the realm began eating itself — three claimants, two civil wars, one plague that arrived with impeccable comic timing — I did what ambitious men of no inheritance do. I found something powerful that nobody else wanted.

They said the crown in the vault beneath the ruined palace was cursed. Of course they did. Everything worth taking is cursed; that is how you know it's worth taking. They said the last man who wore it had been found sitting perfectly still in his throne room for nine days until he starved, smiling. I remember thinking, at twenty-six, that a man who dies smiling at his own coronation must have found something in those nine days worth the price.

I was correct. That is the horror of it. I was *correct*, and I would do it again, and some cold patient thing behind my eyes knows it and uses that knowledge against me every single morning.

Because here is what nobody tells you about a crown that eats its wearers: it does not devour you all at once, like a wolf. It devours you the way a guild ledger devours a life — line by line, transaction by transaction, each one reasonable, each one small, until you look up from the page and cannot find yourself anywhere in the arithmetic.

It took my vanity first. That was painless; vanity goes easily when something more flattering offers itself. Then my grief — it drank my mother's funeral straight out of me, and I woke one morning unable to remember whether I had wept or merely been informed of the custom. Then my appetites, one by one, until hunger became a rumor and sleep became an administrative formality. What remains is very efficient. What remains wins wars. What remains is not, strictly speaking, *me*.

And yet I persist. Some stubborn sediment of Aldren Voss sits in the cellar of this body, watching, remembering, keeping accounts. The crown cannot seem to dig me out entirely. Perhaps it keeps me the way a miser keeps a coin too worn to spend. Perhaps it enjoys the audience.

I have decided to believe it is the second thing. A man needs his vanities, even after they've been repossessed.

* * *

The review continued. Below the balcony, the vanguard crossed the river at Grayfen ford, and I felt — *it* felt — a pulse of satisfaction so vast and impersonal that it passed through me like weather.

"The free cities have recalled their envoys," Ossric reported. "Cinderport's council debates surrender. Their walls are old, Your Radiance. Old walls break."

"Old walls," agreed my voice, "and older debts."

That phrase was not mine. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only here, in the private ledger of a man being slowly written out of his own story. *Older debts.* The crown speaks in ledgers. It thinks in balances owed. Sometimes I catch its meaning a half-second after my mouth has moved, the way you understand a joke in a language you almost speak, and I have learned to read its intentions the way a sailor reads weather — not by understanding, but by the ache in old scars.

Its intention, today, was south. Toward Cinderport. Toward the aqueducts.

Toward the gate.

Ah yes. You'll want to know about the gate. Everyone does, eventually, usually while kneeling.

A century ago — ninety-eight years, if the crown's memory serves, and the crown's memory is a library that eats its librarians — there were keepers who bound something beneath the free cities. Not a monster, whatever the songs say. A *door*, and what doors are for. The keepers sealed it, and the sealing required keys, and the keys were not iron but bloodlines, families threaded into the lock like stitches into a wound. I know this because the crown knows it. The crown ate the keepers' memories along with everything else it has ever consumed, and sometimes, in the long administrative nights, it turns those pages. I have seen a woman's face — gray-eyed, ink-stained fingers, laughing at something I could not hear — and known her name was Yarrow, and known that the crown hated her name with a hatred so old and patient it had outlived the language it was coined in.

The Yarrows are dead. The crown made sure of that, a generation ago, through intermediaries who were paid through intermediaries who were paid through a guild whose name I am not permitted to think clearly about. Permitted. Yes. There are subjects my mind slides off of, the way rain slides off oiled leather. The guild. The payment. The child who survived. Each time I approach these thoughts, something smooths them flat, and I find myself thinking instead about supply lines, or the grain tariff, or how satisfying it would be to watch Cinderport burn.

But lately — and this is new, this is *interesting* — the smoothing has begun to fail.

* * *

That evening there was a court function. There is always a court function. The Hollow Court is less a government than a ritual performed by frightened people for an audience of one, and the one is increasingly unsure who is performing for whom.

They held it in the Long Gallery, beneath windows of black glass, and the lords and generals and guild factors of my empire filed past the throne making their obeisances, and I sat with my hands on the armrests like a man holding onto a boat in rough water.

"Your Radiance." A factor from the Gilded Threshold, plump and precise, bowing at the exact angle that converts flattery into currency. "The guild's caravans stand ready to provision the southern campaign. We ask only—"

"You ask," said my mouth, "what you have always asked. To remain useful, and therefore untouched."

The factor's smile flickered. Just for a heartbeat. And in that heartbeat I saw him recalculate everything — the war, the crown, the wisdom of standing in this gallery at all — and arrive, as they all do, at the same conclusion: better the devil whose invoices you can read.

"We live to serve," he said.

"Yes," said my mouth. "You do."

And then — I swear this on whatever is left of my name — the crown turned its attention on the factor the way a lender turns over a debtor's ledger, and I felt it *weigh* him. His years. His health. The small impossibilities his guild traffics in, bottled storms and borrowed time, all of it inventory, all of it collateral. And beneath the weighing, faint as a hymn through a wall, I felt it reach for something else. Something south. Something young.

Something with ink-stained hands.

I gripped the armrests. For one vertiginous moment I was not in the gallery at all but inside a memory that was not mine — a garret room, a boy bent over a ledger, ordinary, unremarkable, *built to be overlooked*, and the crown looking at him the way a collector looks at a painting everyone else has walked past. Recognition. Appetite. The particular tenderness of a creditor toward the one account still open.

Then the moment closed, and I was on my throne, and my face was doing whatever faces do when they belong to something else.

"Your Radiance is unwell?" Ossric murmured.

"Your Radiance," I said — *I* said, mine, wrested up from the cellar like a drowning man surfacing — "is contemplating mercy."

The gallery went quiet in the way rooms go quiet around dangerous animals. Ossric's face performed concern while his eyes performed arithmetic. Mercy. The word dropped into the Hollow Court like a coin into a well, and everyone waited to hear how far it fell.

"A passing fancy," said my mouth, reclaiming the tongue smoothly, smiling my smile. "The campaign proceeds. South, by the salt road. All roads lead where they must."

All roads lead where they must. Which is to say: toward the gate, toward the last key, toward a boy who does not yet know he is an heir, and toward a settling of accounts one hundred years overdue.

* * *

I do not sleep anymore, but I dream. This is the crown's cruelest economy: it took rest and left the theater. At night, in the dark of the sealed bedchamber, the memories it has eaten play against the inside of my skull like light through a cracked lantern, and I lie in the wreckage of other people's lives and try to salvage what I can.

Tonight it was the gray-eyed woman again. The Yarrow matriarch. She was younger in this memory, laughing in a kitchen, flour on her hands, telling someone — a husband? a daughter? — that prophecy was just bookkeeping done honestly. *"The future's a ledger like any other,"* she said. *"You write it down plain, and you pay what it says you owe."*

I wanted to warn her. She has been dead for decades and I wanted to warn her, and the wanting was so fierce and so useless that it cracked something, and through the crack I saw—

A stair. Beneath a city. Iron, ancient, singing.

And a face above me — no. Below me. Looking down through a century at the thing in the dark wearing a king. Young. Ordinary. Ink-stained hands. Holding a stylus of black iron like a man holds a key he hasn't yet admitted to finding.

The crown convulsed. I felt it slam that door shut, felt it flood me with the grain tariff, the siege schedules, the pleasing image of Cinderport's towers under black banners — but the crack stayed. Small. Hairline. Mine.

The boy is real. The boy is alive. The crown has known for days and has told no one, not even its own mouth, because the crown trusts nothing — not its armies, not its guild of fattened factors, certainly not the hollow man it wears. It is going south itself, quietly, ahead of the trumpets, to collect.

And I — the sediment, the tenant, the worn coin in the miser's pocket — I find that I do not want it to collect.

Understand what this costs me to admit. The boy is nothing to me. He is a stranger with my enemy's face. If he reaches the gate, the gate opens, and what the gate opens onto is the one debt the crown fears, and if the crown falls, I fall with it, because there is no longer any daylight between us. My survival argues for Cinderport's burning. Every sane ledger says: let the hat win.

But I lay in the dark tonight, in the body that used to be Aldren Voss, and I thought about a woman with flour on her hands saying *you pay what it says you owe*, and I thought: she kept honest books. And the crown has kept none. A hundred years of theft, entered nowhere, owed to everyone.

Someone should audit it.

Someone should stand in front of the thing wearing me and read out the arrears, line by line, and watch it discover what it feels like to be the debtor.

I don't know if the boy can do it. I don't know if anyone can. But tomorrow the columns march south, and I will stand on my balcony and watch them go, and behind my eyes the crown will purr its appetite, and underneath the purring, quiet as a hairline crack, I will keep my own ledger now.

First entry: one crown, value incalculable, condition — *hollow*.

Second entry: one keeper, last of his line, currently unaware he is owed anything at all.

Third entry, pending settlement: everything.

Let the collection begin.
