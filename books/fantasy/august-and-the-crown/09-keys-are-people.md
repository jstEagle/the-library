## 9. Keys Are People

I've smuggled a lot of things in my life. Contraband wine in coffins, a fugitive countess in a pickle barrel, once — and I want this on the record that I was younger and stupider then — a live wyvern hatchling disguised as a shipment of hats. But I had never smuggled hope before, and let me tell you: hope is the worst cargo there is. It's heavy. It leaks. It looks at you with big brown eyes and asks questions about bloodlines over breakfast.

That was Xanthe Yarrow for you, three days out of Cinderport on the Salt Road South, sitting across from me at our sad little cookfire with my ledger page — his ledger page, technically, the one he'd traded me like a fool trades a kingdom for a horse — spread between us like an accusation.

"Read it again," he said.

"I read it last night."

"Read it again."

There are two kinds of people who ask you to do something twice. The kind who didn't hear you, and the kind who heard you perfectly and is praying the words rearrange themselves into something survivable. Xanthe was the second kind. He had the look of a man rereading his own death warrant hoping to find a typo.

So I read it again. Out loud, because apparently I'd been promoted from smuggler to schoolmistress somewhere around the second river crossing.

"'Item: one page of the third ledger of the Gilded Threshold, being payments received for services rendered to an unnamed crown client, term of contract one hundred years, subject matter: the gate beneath the free cities, condition: sealed and starving.'" I looked up. "You know what's funny? In my trade we call this a bill of lading. This is just a bill of lading for the end of the world."

"It's not funny," Xanthe said.

"No," I agreed. "It isn't. That's why I said it. When things stop being funny, that's when I start saying they're funny. It's a whole system. Keeps me upright." I tapped the page. "You want the lore or not?"

He wanted the lore. Of course he wanted the lore. He'd been wanting the lore since Grayfen, since the tollhouse, since the child Odile had hummed a hymn in her sleep that made the horses refuse to drink. He'd been wanting it so badly he hadn't asked, the way you don't ask the surgeon how deep the cut goes while the knife's still in you.

* * *

Here's what I knew, and where I knew it from, and I'll thank you not to interrupt, because this part took me eleven years and two nearly fatal mistakes to piece together.

My mother ran contraband up and down the Marchlands before I could walk. Not wine, not silk — she called her trade "small impossibilities." A locket that stayed warm near liars. A compass that pointed at whatever you loved most instead of north, which sounds romantic until you meet a man whose needle spins itself ragged trying to point in six directions at once. She taught me early that the world has rules, and that most people only ever see the cheap ones.

And one night, camped under the aqueduct outside Cinderport — the same aqueduct, though I didn't know it yet, that would later try very hard to kill all of us — she told me about the gate.

Not the gate as the guild tells it. The guild version, if you can get a Threshold clerk drunk enough to talk, is tidy: there is a door beneath the cities, the first kings locked something terrible behind it, the keys were lost, end of lesson, have another pint. Tidy stories sell pints.

My mother's version had no pint attached.

"The gate isn't locked with keys," she told me, turning a spit over the fire while I pretended to sleep, which was how I learned everything worth knowing. "The gate is locked with *people*. Bloodlines. Seven families, once, each one carrying a piece of the lock inside them — in their blood, their bones, their breath. The gate doesn't open for a key you hold. It opens for a key you *are*."

I remember asking — I was maybe nine — whether that meant you could lose your key down a well.

She laughed, but not the good laugh. "You can't lose it, Wrennie. You can only be lost. Every key that dies without passing the blood on is a tooth broken off the lock. And someone has spent a hundred years breaking teeth."

* * *

Xanthe listened the way ledger-keepers listen, which is to say completely and terrifyingly. He didn't fidget. He didn't interrupt. He sat with his hands still and his eyes doing arithmetic, and when I finished the story of my mother's fire-side lessons, he said:

"Seven families. How many are left?"

And there it was. The question I'd been circling for three days like a gull after a fishing boat.

"That depends," I said, "on how much bad news you can hold."

"I keep ledgers," he said. "Bad news is my native language."

"All right." I fed the fire a stick, mostly so I'd have something to do with my hands that wasn't reaching across and squeezing his shoulder, which he would have hated, and which I would have done anyway. "The seven lines. The Yarrows — gatekeepers proper, wardens of the threshold, iron styluses, all that. The Vess family, who kept the eastern approaches. The Tollkeepers of Grayfen — Odile's lot. The Anselm line, though by now they're probably so far underground even they don't remember what they are. The Marrow-singers, who held the hymn. The Duskwardens. And the Sevens, which is either a real family name or the worst joke in history, and knowing gatekeepers, both."

"And?"

"And here's the arithmetic." I scratched it in the dirt, because some lessons need to be written down to land properly. "The Vesses died at Saltmere, forty years back. Officially it was fever. Unofficially, fever doesn't leave every body in a house face-down pointing toward the front door. The Marrow-singers went next — the crown called them heretics, burned their choir-houses, and burned the hymn with them. Except." I nodded toward the back of our little camp, where Odile slept curled against our pack-mule like the mule was her grandmother. "Except the hymn doesn't live in choirs. It lives in blood. And sometimes blood keeps humming when nobody's teaching it."

"The Duskwardens?" Xanthe asked quietly.

"Went into the mountains two generations ago and stopped answering letters. Either they're hiding so well they've hidden from themselves, or they're gone. Nobody knows. The Sevens were never more than a rumor with a name attached." I looked at him. "The Tollkeepers you saw yourself. You stood in that tollhouse. You counted what was left."

He flinched. Good. I didn't want him numb; numb men make sloppy allies.

"So that's four lines dead or vanished, one line slaughtered down to a child who hums in her sleep, one monk-shaped question mark in the mountains, and—" I turned my hand over, palm up, presenting him to himself like a merchant presenting damaged goods. "One Yarrow. Sitting at my fire. Eating my terrible stew."

* * *

He didn't say anything for a long time. The fire popped. Somewhere down the valley a nightjar made its idiot sound, over and over, like it was asking a question no one would answer.

"You're wrong," he finally said. "About the numbers."

"Oh?"

"There were other Yarrows. My parents. My grandfather." His voice did something complicated on the word *grandfather*, the way voices do when they're carrying more than one thing at once. "They died when I was six. Fever, the guild said. Or bandits. The story changed depending on who told it and how much they'd had to drink."

"Mm," I said.

"*Mm?*" He leaned forward. "What does *mm* mean, Wren? You don't get to go quiet and cryptic, that's supposed to be my— that's a terrible habit, actually, Corvane used to—"

"Corvane," I said, "used to do a great many things, and we'll be having words about all of them eventually. What *mm* means is: think about it. Think about it the way you think about a ledger, because that's the only way you know how to think about anything, and it's going to save your life someday, so use it now."

He closed his eyes. I watched him do it — watch him lay the facts out in columns, debit and credit, and I will say this for the man: he is honest in his columns. Most people, when they audit their own lives, fudge the entries. Xanthe doesn't. I've seen him write down things about himself that would make a saint wince.

"Fever," he said slowly. "Or bandits. Two different stories for the same event. Guilds don't lose track of stories — stories are inventory. Unless the story was changed deliberately." His eyes opened. "A child survives. Both parents die. The guild raises the child itself, inside its own walls, gives him work that keeps him invisible, keeps him *counting*—"

"Keeps him findable," I said, because somebody had to say it and it might as well be me. "Xanthe. Your guild didn't raise you out of charity. Charity doesn't come with a century-long contract in a third ledger. They raised you the way you raise livestock you've been paid to deliver. You weren't an orphan they took pity on. You were an asset in storage."

The nightjar asked its question again. Xanthe stared into the fire, and I watched the color go out of his face in stages, like a tide going out.

"They signed it," he whispered. "In the third ledger. Payments to keep the gate sealed and starving. Signed Yarrow. My family name. I thought—" He laughed, one dry note with nothing behind it. "I thought it meant my ancestors had been paid off. Traitors, taking crown money. I almost preferred that. At least then they'd chosen something."

"And now?"

"Now I think the signature means they were the *goods*." He rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand. "Every payment in that ledger, a hundred years of them, and the item being purchased never changes. Findable. Keep the last ones findable. The guild wasn't guarding the gatekeepers. It was keeping them shelved. Like—" His voice caught. "Like me. Like a bottle on a high shelf waiting for a buyer."

I let him sit in it. There's a skill to that, letting someone sit in the truth without rushing to mop it up, and I learned it the hard way, from watching my mother hold silence for people twice her size until they told her things they'd sworn to take to their graves.

When he spoke again, his voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man reading figures aloud.

"If the keys are bloodlines. If four lines are gone, one is a child, one is a rumor, and one is—" he gestured at himself, that small bitter gesture again, "—me. Then the prophecy book wasn't wrong. 'The gate opens.' It opens when enough keys stand at it. And I'm not a man who happens to be standing near a prophecy. I'm a *component*. I'm inventory. I've been inventory my entire life, and I never once saw it, because I was too busy being proud of how good I was at being overlooked."

"Yes," I said.

"That's it? *Yes?*"

"What do you want me to say, Xanthe? That it's not fair? It's not fair. That you deserved better? You deserved better. That some kindly power put you exactly where you needed to be out of love for you specifically?" I shook my head. "No. Here's the ugliest truth I own, and I give it away free: nobody chose you. The blood chose itself. It survived because survival is the only thing blood knows how to do. Everything else — the duty, the debt, the dying — that gets handed to you like a package you didn't order, and the only choice you ever get is whether to sign for it."

* * *

He was quiet a long time after that. Long enough that I started to worry I'd broken something load-bearing.

Then Odile stirred against the mule, half-waking, and began to hum.

Three notes. Four. The same fragment she always found in her sleep, the little loop of melody that made the air feel thicker and the fire burn steadier and the horses calm. She hummed it the way children hum — without knowing, without meaning, the way hearts beat. Then she rolled over and was gone again, and the ordinary dark came back.

But I saw what it did to Xanthe. I saw him turn toward the sound like a plant turning toward light, helpless about it, hungry about it. And I understood — really understood, for the first time since I'd taken his ledger page and his company — that I hadn't picked up a passenger. I'd picked up a keystone. And keystones don't get to ask whether they feel like holding up an arch.

"My grandfather carried an iron stylus," Xanthe said at last. "It was the only thing of theirs I kept. I used to think I kept it because it wrote nicely. It never blotted. Never wore down." He pulled it from his coat — he carried it everywhere, I'd noticed, the way some men carry knives and fools carry regrets — and turned it in the firelight. Old iron, plain as a nail, except that the firelight did something strange in it, bent slightly, the way light bends near deep water. "It's not a pen, is it."

"No," I said. "I'd wager my ship it's not a pen."

"What is it?"

"A tool for working on things that aren't paper." I shrugged. "That's as far as my mother's lessons got before the crown's tax collectors became suspiciously interested in our cargo manifests and we had to become considerably less interesting people. Ask your monk, when we find him. If he's alive. If he's what I think he is."

"Brother Anselm," Xanthe murmured. "The monastery in the Verreault passes." He looked at the stylus, then at the sleeping child, then — briefly, unwillingly, like a man checking a wound — at nothing at all, at the middle distance where futures live. "Four lines dead. One child. One old man who's been hiding in plain prayer. And me."

"That's the arithmetic."

"It's terrible arithmetic."

"It's the only arithmetic there is." I poked the fire down to embers, because dawn was a few hours off and embers hold heat longer than flames, which is a lesson for fires and for people both. "Get some sleep, keeper. Tomorrow we climb."

He lay down by the coals, stylus in his fist like a child with a talisman, and I sat up the rest of the watch, because someone had to, and because I wanted to look at him a while longer and decide something.

Here's what I decided, and I'll deny it if you quote me: I decided I was in. All the way in. Not because of prophecies or gates or crowns — I've never trusted any object with that many consonants in its purpose. But because when the truth landed on him tonight, heavy as a collapsed roof, he didn't shove it off onto anyone else. He signed for the package. I watched him do it.

And because of the other thing. The thing I haven't told him yet, that I found scrawled in the margin of his precious third ledger page, in a hand older than the ink around it, a single line that my mother's ghost has been shouting at me since the moment I read it:

*The seventh key was never a family.*

I turned the page over in my fingers, watching the embers through it, and wondered what in the hells a gate does when it runs out of teeth — and what, exactly, it would settle for instead.

Dawn came up gray over the pass. We climbed.
