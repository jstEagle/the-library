## 10. The Tollhouse at Grayfen

There is a particular kind of silence that follows violence. I had read about it, of course—everyone who keeps ledgers reads about everything eventually, because reading is the one vice the Gilded Threshold never taxed. But reading about a silence and standing inside one are two very different trades.

The silence at Grayfen was the kind that has weight. It sat on my chest like a wet wool blanket, and under it I could hear the river moving, indifferent as ever, and somewhere a crow making its opinions known to no one in particular.

Wren stopped at the edge of the road and put out an arm across my chest. She did this a lot. I'd begun to think of it as her way of saying *hello*.

"Smoke's old," she said. "Day, maybe two."

"Then we're late."

"We were always going to be late. That's what being a step behind an inquisitor means." She squinted at the tollhouse where it sat at the river crossing, a squat stone building with a covered porch and, I now noticed, a door hanging off one hinge like a broken wing. "You still want to go in?"

"I came four hundred miles to go in."

"You came four hundred miles because a book told you to. There's a difference."

She had a point. Wren had a lot of points, the way a knife has edges—useful, and uncomfortable to hold for long. I ducked under her arm anyway and walked toward the tollhouse, because the book had told me to, and because the book had been right about everything so far, and because I had run out of other explanations for my life.

Let me back up.

The third ledger—the one I'd stolen out of the guild's hidden vault along with my grandfather's iron stylus and whatever remained of my illusions—listed payments. That was all a ledger ever did, but the *subjects* of these payments were what mattered. For a hundred years, the Gilded Threshold had paid stipends to certain families across the Marchlands. The entries were coded, of course. Guilds code everything; it's half of what we're for. But codes are just arithmetic wearing a mask, and I am very good at arithmetic.

*Grayfen crossing. Keeper line, second key. Stipend continued. Note: family does not know the source of funds.*

That was the phrase that had turned my stomach for three days on the salt road. *Does not know the source of funds.* The guild had been paying these people for generations—paying them to exist, essentially, the way you might pay rent on a house you never visited but couldn't afford to lose. And they'd never told them why. Somewhere in Grayfen there was a family who thought their modest tollhouse income came from a distant merchant uncle, and all the while they were a key to a gate under the world, and the crown was paying my guild to make sure they never learned it.

I had been one of them once. That was the part that kept me walking. Somewhere in that ledger, pages back, there had been a payment with my family name on it, and I had grown up thinking my orphan's pension was charity.

* * *

The tollhouse had a toll board, still hanging by the road: *CART, 2 COPPERS. FOOT TRAVELER, FREE. GOD'S PEOPLE, FREE.* Someone had loved this place. Someone had painted the board fresh within the year; the letters were bright, the wood unweathered. There was a child's chalk drawing on the lower corner—a lopsided sun, and a stick figure waving.

I stood in front of that drawing for longer than was useful.

"Xanthe." Wren, behind me, quiet. Wren was only quiet when it mattered, which was how I knew the difference between her silences and the tollhouse's.

"I'm coming."

"You're standing."

"I'm doing both. It's a talent."

Inside, the silence got worse.

I have catalogued seized estates. I have inventoried the possessions of the dead for the guild—debtors, mostly, and the occasional merchant who died mid-fraud. I know what a room looks like when death has passed through it in the ordinary way, the way that comes for everyone eventually. There's a dignity to it. Things are where they were left. A cup sits by a chair. A ledger lies open to the last written page.

This was not that. This was death that had been in a hurry, or had wanted to make a point, or both. Inquisitors, I was learning, were not subtle. Subtlety was not in their budget.

The main room of the tollhouse had been a home. I could see it under the wreckage: a table scrubbed pale with use, benches, a hearth with a kettle still hanging on its hook, a shelf of chipped crockery arranged by size because someone in this family had my kind of mind. Now the table was split down the middle—not broken, *split*, as if something had come down on it with intent. The crockery was in pieces against the far wall. There were marks on the floorboards I made myself stop looking at, because I had a job to do and a man who spends his time cataloguing blood spatter is a man who has stopped being useful.

"Three," Wren said from the doorway of the back room. Her voice had gone flat, which was her version of gentle. "Two in here. One out back by the woodpile. All cold."

"Any sign of—"

"Nothing. Whoever did this took nothing. That's how you know it wasn't robbery." She came back into the main room and stood beside me, arms folded. "Inquisitors don't loot. Looting implies the goods have value. To them, these people *were* the goods."

I had come to Grayfen to find the second key. I had rehearsed the conversation on the road. *Good morning. I'm Xanthe Yarrow, formerly of the Gilded Threshold, and I have reason to believe your family is one of three bloodlines that—* No. It had never sounded right in any rehearsal. I'd settled on just showing them the ledger and the stylus and letting the iron do the talking, because iron, at least, doesn't stumble over its words.

Now the conversation was a chalk sun on a toll board, and I was talking to a dead man's kettle.

"Say it," Wren said.

"Say what?"

"Whatever you're chewing on. You've got the face. Ledger face. The one where you're balancing something and the numbers won't come out."

"The numbers won't come out," I said, "because the guild paid these people for sixty years. It's in the ledger. Sixty years of stipends, and Vail's inquisitors walked in and killed them anyway. The crown is paying my guild to *find* these families, and then it kills them the moment they're found. We weren't keeping them safe. We were keeping them *labeled*."

Wren was quiet a moment. "That's a bad ledger."

"It's a very good ledger. That's the problem. Everything's in it. Nothing balances."

* * *

I did what I could. It wasn't much and it wasn't enough and I knew both things while I did it. I carried the man from the back—the father, I decided, though I had no way of knowing; he had a toll-collector's leather satchel slung across him even in death—into the main room, and laid him out with what dignity the split table allowed. Wren helped without being asked, which from Wren was practically a eulogy.

The mother was in the back room with a girl of maybe fifteen. They had died close together, and I chose, deliberately, with the full force of my professional discipline, not to examine how. I laid them straight and covered them with the quilt from the bed, a quilt that had been mended in at least four places by at least four different hands, because that's what quilts are: a family's arithmetic, stitched.

I found the household ledger on a shelf, because of course they had one. Everyone keeps ledgers; they just don't all know it. This one was kept in a careful, uneducated hand—numbers laborious and proud, tolls counted daily, small charities noted. *Widow Marsh, 2 coppers. Pilgrims, free. Odile's boots, 9 silvers, God help us.*

Odile.

The name went through me like a splinter. I flipped back through the pages. *Odile lost a tooth. Odile can whistle through her fingers now. Odile's boots.* A child. A child of perhaps six or seven, judging by the boots and the chalk sun and the tone of the entries, which had the particular exhausted wonder of parents documenting a small miracle on a daily basis.

There was no small body in the tollhouse.

"Hey." I said it too loudly, and the silence flinched. "Wren. There's no child here."

"Kids hide," she said. "It's the one thing they're better at than we are."

"Two days."

"Then either she's dead in a ditch, or she's the best hider in the Marchlands, or someone took her." Wren crouched by the hearth, examining the cold ash with the air of a woman reading a very short, very grim letter. "Inquisitors don't take. If Vail wanted the girl, there'd be a report and a receipt."

"Then she's in a ditch."

"Or she's hiding. Two days is a long time for a six-year-old, but not an impossible one. There's a cellar?"

We found the cellar door behind a hanging rug, and the rug had been hung *recently*—the nails were bright. Under it, the cellar: turnips, a barrel of salted fish, a coil of rope, and a nest of blankets in the darkest corner with a clay cup beside it and the remains of a loaf gone to green mold.

And a smell. Faint, under the turnips and the mold. The particular smell of a small person who has been very frightened for a very long time.

I got down on my knees, which in a cellar is not far to go, and I said, in the voice I imagine I'd use to soothe a ledger into balancing, "My name is Xanthe. I'm not with the soldiers. I knew your family kept this tollhouse. I came to talk to them. I'm going to leave a coin on the top step, and then I'm going to sit down here where you can see me, and I'm not going to come any closer. That's a trade. I'm told you people respect trades."

Silence. The river-silence upstairs, the crow outside, my own heart doing unprofessional things.

Then, from behind the barrel of fish, a sound.

It was not a word. It was not a sob, though it was close to one. It was *humming*.

Four notes, then three, then four again. A pattern. My skin did something I didn't authorize. Because I knew it—I knew it the way you know a song you heard once as a child and never consciously learned. The gate-hymn. The one from the prophecy book's margins, the one my grandfather's stylus seemed to warm toward when I held it near certain pages. The one the third ledger called, in one dry marginal note in my grandfather's hand, *the binding measure*.

A six-year-old, in a cellar, humming the song that held shut a door under the world.

"Odile," I said. "That's a beautiful song. Who taught it to you?"

The humming stopped. A long pause. Then, from the dark behind the fish barrel, a voice like a dry leaf: "Nobody. It's mine. It comes when the bad men come."

* * *

She wouldn't come out for the coin. She came out for the bread.

I should record that honestly, because I have a feeling that in whatever gets written about this—and something will get written, the gate seems to keep its own books—the truth will matter. I offered her silver and truth and the name of her family's distant merchant uncle, and none of it moved her. What moved her was Wren tearing a loaf into pieces, buttering it with the last of our stores, and saying, "Eat first. Talking's better on a full stomach. That's not a trick. It's just true."

Odile came out the way small animals come out: in stages, in a low crouch, eyes doing all the work her legs wouldn't. She was small even for six, with her mother's dark hair cut raggedly—by her own hand, I guessed, or by someone hurrying—and her father's stubborn chin. She looked at Wren first, decided Wren was weather, something to be endured, and then looked at me.

She looked at me the way the stylus looks at iron. I don't know a better way to put it. Something in her went *there you are*, and something in me went *oh no*.

"You're the one from the song," she said.

"I don't think I'm in any songs."

"You are." She took the bread. "It got louder when you came up the road. It always does that. Mam says—" and here her face did something terrible, the thing a small face does when it remembers mid-sentence that the sentence can't finish—"Mam said the song knows its own."

Wren and I looked at each other over the child's head. *The song knows its own.* I thought of the prophecy book with my name in it. I thought of the third ledger with my family name on sixty years of payments. I thought of the gate beneath Cinderport, waiting, patient as a creditor.

We buried her family that afternoon. It took hours, because the ground by the river was clay and we had one shovel between us and a child who insisted on helping and whom we could not, for reasons I couldn't have explained then, refuse. She placed river stones on each grave. She hummed while she did it, that same four-and-three pattern, and I noticed the graves' silence felt different afterward. Not lighter. *Kept.* As if the song had agreed to sit with them so the rest of us could go.

I burned the tollhouse. It was the only mercy left in my inventory. The chalk sun on the toll board went last, and I stood there watching a child's drawing curl into ash and felt something in me finish hardening.

I want to be precise about this, because rage is a ledger entry like any other and it deserves accurate bookkeeping. I had been afraid since Cinderport. Fear had been my co-author for weeks—fear of Vail, fear of Corvane, fear of the prophecy, fear of the growing suspicion that my entire life had been a line item in someone else's accounts. But fear is a soft metal. It bends. It makes you run, and running had kept me alive, and I had told myself that running *was* the plan.

Standing in front of four mended quilts and a chalk sun, the fear finished its work and handed the ledger over to something else.

They had been *labeled*. That was the crime. Not just killed—killing is old and common and the world's oldest debt—but catalogued, marked, paid stipends by clerks like me so that when the crown's hand came down, it would fall in the right place. My guild hadn't raised me despite what I was. It had raised me *because* of what I was, the way you raise a calf, and when the calf's family was no longer useful, the inquisitors had come with their receipts and their procedure, and the only reason this child was breathing was that she was small enough to fit behind a fish barrel.

Odile tugged my sleeve. I looked down.

"Where are we going?" she asked. Not scared. Negotiating. Her father's chin again.

"Somewhere the bad men can't follow."

"The song says they can always follow."

"The song," I said, "is going to have to learn to be wrong."

She considered this with the gravity of a guildmaster reviewing a contract, then nodded once, and took my hand, and that was the whole treaty.

* * *

We walked south that evening, because south was where the third ledger said the second key's line had branched, and because Wren said the Grayfen roads would be crawling with inquisitors by morning, and because walking was the only thing my body still knew how to do that didn't hurt.

Odile rode on Wren's shoulders, asleep inside ten minutes, which is either the resilience of children or their curse, and I have not yet decided which. And as she slept, the humming started again. Soft. Four notes, three, four. Her fingers twitched against Wren's collarbone in time with it.

Wren glanced back at me. "She does that in her sleep?"

"Apparently."

"You know what it is."

"It's the binding measure. The hymn the gatekeepers used to hold the seal. There are three keys—three bloodlines—and each one carries a verse. Hers is the second verse. It's in the ledger's margins, in my grandfather's hand. I've read it a hundred times and never once heard it sung." I watched the child's small back rise and fall. "She doesn't know what she's carrying. Nobody ever told her family. The guild made sure of that."

"So the crown kills the singers," Wren said, "and the guild keeps the songbooks."

"And I," I said, "am the last one holding both."

The road bent south along the river. Behind us, Grayfen's smoke was a gray smudge against the early stars, and the crow had finally shut up, and the child hummed the second verse of a song meant to hold shut a door under the world, and I walked with my grandfather's iron stylus in my pocket, growing warmer against my leg with every note.

Two verses, I thought. Two keys. One still unaccounted for, and one of them asleep on a smuggler's shoulders.

The song knows its own.

I only prayed it knew what it was doing, because I was increasingly certain that I didn't—and that somewhere behind us, riding a road we'd left too late, Inquisitor Seraphine Vail had already read the next entry in her own ledger, and her next line item was a child who hummed in her sleep.
