## 11. Bargains in Borrowed Years

The river at Grayfen did not look like the kind of place where you could buy anything, let alone years of your own life. It looked like a tired brown ribbon that had given up on ambition somewhere upstream and was now just going through the motions toward the sea. Reeds slumped along its banks. A heron stood in the shallows with the posture of a clerk waiting out his last hour before retirement.

But Odile had stopped humming.

That was how I knew. For three days since the tollhouse — three days of carrying her, feeding her, listening to that thin gate-hymn thread through her sleep like wire through wax — she had not once gone quiet. Now, standing at the water's edge with Wren's hand heavy on her shoulder, the child had simply stopped, the way a compass needle stops when it finds north.

"She hears something," Wren said.

"I know."

"You don't like it."

"I don't like anything," I said. "It's a policy. Keeps expectations low."

Wren gave me the look she reserved for my worst deflections — the one that said *I have smuggled live thunderstorms in pickle barrels, boy, do not lie to me about your feelings* — and let it go, which was its own kind of mercy.

Here is what I had not told her: I had recognized the place before we ever reached it. Not by sight. By paperwork.

The third ledger — the guild's hidden ledger, the one listing a century of payments to keep a gate *sealed and starving*, signed in my family's hand — had an entry dated ninety-one years back. *To the Ferryman of Grayfen: one year of life, tendered annually, in exchange for passage unmolested across the water.* There were forty entries in that column. Forty years bought from some ancestor of mine, spent keeping the second key's bloodline safe at the tollhouse.

And we all knew how well that had ended. The tollhouse was ash. The family was dead. The payments had apparently stopped mattering the moment the crown decided they should.

Still. The ledger said the Ferryman traded in years. And the ledger had never once lied to me. People lied constantly — Corvane had lied to me my entire adult life, the guild had lied to me, the whole gilded architecture of my existence had been one long lie with excellent bookkeeping — but ledgers didn't. Ledgers just recorded what was true, even when the truth was monstrous.

I trusted the ledger more than I trusted any living thing. This is either a character flaw or the entire reason I was still alive. Possibly both.

* * *

We made camp upriver from the crossing, in the lee of a drowned mill whose wheel had rusted mid-turn like a clock that had died of embarrassment. Wren built a fire small enough to hide in a cupped hand. Odile ate bread and honey and then fell asleep against Wren's side, mid-hum, the hymn dissolving into breath.

"Tell me the plan again," Wren said, "because the plan as I understand it involves you walking down to that river alone at midnight, and I have opinions about plans shaped like that."

"It's a transaction," I said. "A negotiation. I've done hundreds of them."

"For salt futures. For bottled storms. For a shipment of borrowed years that one time, which, incidentally, should have been a warning to me about tonight." She poked the fire. "You've never negotiated with a thing that eats time, Xanthe."

"The guild trades in borrowed years regularly. Crated, sealed, taxed at four percent. I've catalogued eleven crates of them." I pulled the ledger onto my knees. "The difference between a guild contract and whatever this is, is paperwork. Strip away the paper and every bargain is the same shape. Something offered. Something asked. Terms agreed. The trick is knowing what you're actually selling, because the other party always does."

"And what are you actually selling?"

I turned the ledger so she could read the entry. Her eyes moved down the column of years, and I watched her arrive at the arithmetic — forty entries, one per year, paid by people who shared my blood — and watch her face change.

"They paid with their lives," she said quietly. "Little bits of them. Every year. To keep that family safe."

"To keep *me* safe, maybe. If the prophecy's right and I'm what's left of the line." I closed the book. "Which means this river has been doing business with my family for a century. That's not nothing. Debts create familiarity. Familiarity creates leverage. It's the only leverage I have, so I'm going to use it."

"That's not leverage, that's a *tab*."

"Wren." I kept my voice level, because if I let it waver she'd hear what was underneath it. "Inquisitors burned a family alive two days ago to get to a child who hums in her sleep. They will do it again. They're behind us, and the road ahead goes through crown territory no matter which way we bend it. Odile cannot walk that road as she is — she's six, she's exhausted, and she glows like a lighthouse every time she dreams. I need the river's help moving her. And the river deals in exactly one currency."

"Years."

"Years."

She was quiet for a while. The fire popped. Somewhere downriver, the heron cried out once, a sound like a hinge that needed oiling.

"How many?" she finally asked.

"One decade," I said. "Ten years off the end. I'm twenty-six. I'll be sixty instead of seventy. Nobody gets the years they think they're owed anyway; I've read enough death registers to know that. Most men in Cinderport don't see sixty. I'll be beating the average."

"You've actually calculated the average."

"I'm a ledger-keeper. Grief and statistics live in the same room in my head and they've agreed to share the desk."

Wren looked at me for a long moment, and whatever she saw there made her stop arguing, which frightened me more than any argument would have. She reached over and squeezed my wrist, hard, once, the way sailors do — *I acknowledge you, I release you* — and said, "If the terms turn bad, you walk away. You hear me? No bargain is better than a bad bargain. That's the first rule of smuggling and the first rule of staying alive."

"And the second rule?"

"Don't tell the client everything." She nodded at the sleeping child. "Goodnight, keeper."

* * *

Midnight came down over Grayfen like a lid.

I left the fire and walked to the bank alone, the third ledger under my arm and my grandfather's iron stylus in my pocket, where it had taken to sitting like a splinter I'd decided to keep. Mist lay on the water in flat white sheets. The reeds had gone silent. Even the insects had signed off for the night, which struck me as professionally prudent.

"Ferryman of Grayfen," I said, because the ledger taught me that things like this respond to their proper titles the way dogs respond to their names. "I've come to open an account."

The river moved.

Not flowed — *moved*, the way a sleeper shifts under blankets. The mist drew back from a stretch of black water near the far bank, and something rose there without disturbing the surface, the way a thought rises without disturbing a face. It took a shape because I suppose it could tell I'd be uncomfortable otherwise: a tall figure of woven reeds and river-clay, wearing the suggestion of a ferryman's hat, holding the suggestion of a pole. Its face was smooth clay with two darker hollows where eyes might go, and I got the distinct impression that the face was a courtesy, and that what was actually looking at me had never needed eyes.

*Xanthe Yarrow,* it said, and its voice was the sound water makes under ice — heard through the thing itself rather than the air. *The last entry in the old book.*

"You know the ledger."

*I keep accounts older than yours.* The clay face tilted. *Your grandfather stood on this bank. His mother before him. They paid punctually. Punctuality is rarer than honesty among your kind.*

"My family's dead," I said. "Killed at a tollhouse upriver, nine days ago, by the crown's inquisitors. All except one." I jerked my chin back toward camp. "A girl. Six years old. She carries the same blood, or close enough that the crown wants it stopped. I need to move her south, fast and unseen, and every road and bridge between here and the free cities has eyes on it. But rivers—" I tapped the ledger, "—rivers run under roads. Rivers go where they like. Your ledger entries say you've moved protected cargo before."

The Ferryman was silent long enough that I started composing my own eulogy. Then:

*What do you offer?*

"Ten years," I said. "Of my life. Tendered now, in full, no installments."

The hollows of its eyes deepened. I had the vertiginous sense of being appraised by something that could see straight through my skin to the wet machinery underneath, counting beats.

*Ten years,* it repeated. *You offer ten years for the girl's passage. That is not the trade I expected from a Yarrow.*

"What did you expect?"

*Your grandfather offered one year at a time. Parsimony. Prudence. The long game.* The reed-shoulders shifted. *You offer a decade in a single stroke, for a child not of your hearth. Either you are a fool, or you have read further into the world's ledger than your ancestors did.*

"I'm a ledger-keeper," I said. "Foolishness and thoroughness look identical from the outside."

Something happened to the clay face that I chose to interpret as amusement, mostly because the alternative interpretations were worse.

*Then hear the terms,* it said, *and mark them well, because I will not repeat them and I do not renegotiate. Ten years, cut from the end, freely given. In exchange: the girl passes south by water, veiled from crown-sight, from here to the delta quays below Vellmarsh. My current does not sleep, does not eat, does not burn. What hunts her on land will find only cold trail.*

"And the cost to her?"

*None. The debt is yours alone. But—* and here the voice dropped until I felt it in my teeth rather than heard it, *—mark this, keeper. Years are not coin. Coin sits in a purse and waits. Years are *woven*. Pull ten threads from the cloth and the whole garment shifts. You will not feel them leave. You will simply have less cloth than you believed, sooner than you planned, in places you did not think to check. Do you accept the weave?*

Every sensible part of me — the part that had survived twenty-six years in the Gilded Threshold by being unremarkable, uninvolved, and unbilled — screamed the sensible thing. Walk away. Find another route. Lose a week, lose a month, lose anything except pieces of yourself.

But sensible parts of me had also locked the guildhall doors against a child fleeing a fire. Sensible had a price too, and I'd seen it collected at the tollhouse.

"I accept the weave," I said.

*Say it as a contract.*

I took a breath. Twenty-six years of guild training straightened my spine. "Xanthe Yarrow, last of the gatekeeper line, offers ten years of his natural span to the Ferryman of Grayfen, freely given, in exchange for the safe passage of the child Odile of the tollhouse Grayfen, from this water to the delta quays below Vellmarsh, veiled from the crown and its servants. Witnessed by the old ledger and the iron stylus." I drew the stylus from my pocket and pressed its point briefly against my own palm — a guild custom, sealing a verbal contract in blood when no ink was at hand. Old habits. Apparently they ran deeper than I'd known.

The river went very still.

Then the Ferryman extended one reed-woven hand, and I felt the years leave.

I want to describe it precisely, because precision was all I had to hold onto. It didn't hurt. It was like the moment when you set down a pack you've carried so long you forgot you were wearing it — a lightness, a sudden strange buoyancy, ten years of future weight lifting off shoulders that would never ache under it now. Somewhere ahead of me, a version of me at fifty-eight quietly ceased to exist, along with fifty-nine, and sixty, and all the ordinary mornings those versions would have had: the bad tea, the slow sunrises, the grandchild I'd never grumble at fondly. They didn't die. They simply were never going to happen, and the space where they would have been folded shut like a ledger page turned past.

My knees buckled. The mud caught me. Above the river, the stars swam and steadied.

*Paid in full,* said the Ferryman, and there was — I swear this — something almost gentle in the ice-water voice. *Your family always paid punctually, Xanthe Yarrow. But you are the first to pay standing up, for someone else's child. The account of your line ends better than it began.*

"Is that supposed to comfort me?"

*No. Comfort is not legal tender.* The figure began to sink back into the black water. *One more item, free of charge, because you paid more than asked. The crown's hounds crossed the north ford at dusk. They carry a woman's faith ahead of them like a lantern. She reads ledgers, this one. She reads them the way you do.*

The mist swallowed it. The river resumed being a tired brown ribbon, innocent as Sunday.

I knelt in the mud with an empty decade where my future used to be, and laughed — actually laughed, a cracked, unhinged sound that I quickly strangled, because Wren was asleep thirty feet away and Odile was curled against her, humming softly, safely, veiled now by a river's promise.

Vail reads ledgers the way I do.

That should have terrified me. It did terrify me. But underneath the terror, some stubborn clerk-part of my brain had already begun turning pages: because a hunter who reads ledgers can be *misled* by them, and a hunter who reads them *well* — thoroughly, faithfully, trusting every entry — is a hunter who can be fed a false column and will follow it off the edge of the page.

I got up. I wiped the stylus clean, tucked it away, and walked back toward the fire where my strange little crew slept, carrying ten fewer years and, for the first time since the prophecy cracked my life open, an actual plan.

At dawn, we'd put Odile in the water.

And somewhere behind us, following our trail with a faithful heart and a careful eye, Inquisitor Seraphine Vail was about to start reading the wrong ledger.
