## 12. Seraphine's Doubt

The clerk's name was Pellam Ostrow, and he wept the way he must have done everything else in life—thoroughly, apologetically, and with an eye toward the paperwork.

"Please," he said, for the eleventh time since they'd brought him in at dusk. "Please, I'm only a clerk."

"Everyone is only something," I said. "I am only an inquisitor. The difference is that I've made peace with it."

He was a soft man, Pellam Ostrow. Soft hands stained with ledger-ink, a soft belly straining the buttons of his guild-grey coat, a soft face already slick with sweat in the cold of the interrogation room. The Gilded Threshold had sent him to Grayfen to audit toll receipts—a punishment posting, by the look of his luggage—and my riders had taken him off the salt road like gulls taking a fish from a child.

I did not feel guilty about it. I want to be clear about that, because later—much later—I would need to remember exactly when the guilt started, and it was not this night. This night I felt nothing but the pleasant friction of procedure. Procedure is a comfort. Procedure is a prayer you can perform with your hands.

"The Gilded Threshold has been under review for some months," I told him, laying out my instruments on the table between us with the care of a surgeon and the patience of a tax assessor. "Irregular payments. Unregistered transfers. Your guildmaster is either corrupt or careless, and either way, the crown requires documentation."

"I don't know anything about—" He stopped. Swallowed. Looked at the instruments, which were arranged not menacingly but *tidily*, which I have found frightens people far more. "That is. I know about the ledgers. Obviously. I keep them. But the third one—the third one isn't mine. I've never even seen it. I only know it exists because Master Corvane mentioned it once, when he thought no one was listening, and then he looked at me like he wished I'd never been born, and I have thought about that look every day since, Inquisitor, every single day—"

"Pellam." I let his name sit in the air until he stopped vibrating. "Breathe. You are going to tell me about the third ledger, but first you are going to tell me about the payments. The regular ones. Century-old ones. Start with what you know, proceed to what you suspect, and conclude with what you fear. It's a good structure. I use it myself."

He blinked at me. People always do, the first time. They expect the burning and the screaming; they get bookkeeping instead. Terror rendered as bureaucracy is still terror, but it is terror with margins and footnotes, and men like Pellam Ostrow respond to margins and footnotes the way other men respond to mercy.

"There are annuity payments," he said. His voice had gone quiet and strange, the voice of a man reciting something he'd memorized without understanding. "Have been for as long as the guild has kept records. Out of the Grayfen chapter, out of Cinderport, out of six others. Quarterly. They go to... recipients aren't named. Just sigils. A closed gate. An open eye. A crown."

"A crown," I repeated.

"A crown," he said. "Paid *by* the guild, Inquisitor. To the crown. Or—to someone. The entries just say 'per contract, sealed and starving.' That phrase. Over and over, for a hundred years. 'Sealed and starving.' My predecessor thought it was a mine. Some shaft the guild bought rights to and never worked. Sealed and starving—like a seam left unmined." He laughed, a small horrible laugh. "I used to think that too."

"And now?"

"Now I think about the gatekeepers." His eyes came up to mine for the first time, and there was something in them I hadn't expected—not defiance, not calculation. Pity. Pity, aimed at me, from a fat clerk tied to a chair. "You burn them. All of them. Every bloodline, every descendant, every village that sheltered them. I've seen the requisition orders. Lamp oil for a family of four. Lamp oil for a family of nine. It's always lamp oil. Someone in procurement is very efficient."

"What about the gatekeepers?"

"You're paying to keep something sealed and starving," Pellam said, "and you're killing everyone who knows how it was sealed. Doesn't that strike you as—" He hesitated on the word, weighing it, deciding whether a clerk was permitted to say it to an inquisitor. He decided he was. We were both about to regret it. "*Counterproductive?*"

* * *

I had him untied after that. Not because I believed him—I didn't, not yet—but because a weeping man talks in circles and a terrified man talks in spirals, ever inward, and I needed him at the center where the truth lives. Also, my hands were tired. Interrogation looks dramatic from the outside, but mostly it is sitting still while another human being comes apart, and coming apart takes hours.

He talked for three more hours.

He talked about the guild's founding contracts, which predated the Hollow King's reign and were written in a hand so old the ink had gone brown as dried blood. He talked about Corvane's predecessor, and that man's predecessor, and the strange fact that every guildmaster for a century had died comfortably in bed despite handling sums that would tempt a saint to arson. He talked about the quarterly payments, and how the amounts had never once changed—not with inflation, not with war, not with famine—as though the contract had been struck at a fixed price by parties who did not experience time the way clerks do.

And then, near midnight, he said the thing.

"It's in the annexes," he whispered. "The old annexes, the ones nobody audits because nobody can read the notation anymore. If you want proof of who ordered what—if you want to know whether the massacre was the crown protecting itself or the guild protecting its investment—you'd need the annexes. But Inquisitor, if I show you, and you don't like what's in them, they'll burn me for showing you. And if you *do* like what's in them, they'll burn me anyway, because now I'm a witness. Either way I'm lamp oil."

"Yes," I agreed. "That is generally how knowledge works in the crown's service. Would you prefer ignorance? Because I can arrange that too, though it will cost you the same."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he laughed again—that same small horrible laugh—and said, "You know, you're not what the stories say."

"No one ever is. That's rather the point of stories. Fetch your keys, clerk. We're going to the archives."

* * *

The Gilded Threshold's Grayfen chapterhouse had been built on the bones of three older buildings, and the oldest of those bones was the archive annex: a cellar so deep that the candles burned blue at their tips, with shelves of ledgers bound in leather gone stiff as bark. The air tasted of dust and tallow and time. Somewhere above us, water moved through pipes, and I found myself listening to it longer than I meant to. Water in walls. Water going somewhere on purpose.

Pellam found the annex volumes by touch, the way a man finds his own house in the dark. He pulled down a ledger thick as a paving stone and blew off a century of dust, and then he stood there holding it, trembling slightly, like a man who has opened a door he cannot close.

"Page forty-one," he said. "The requisition orders. For the... for the cull."

I took the volume from him. My hands were steady. I remember being proud of that, afterward. My hands were perfectly steady.

The notation was archaic but not foreign—I had been trained in paleography as an acolyte, back when I believed that reading dead languages was a form of devotion. The entries were terse, administrative, damning in the way that all honest paperwork is damning. Dates. Locations. Numbers of confirmed lineages extinguished. Disbursements for oil, for rope, for mounted escorts, for the disposal of remains.

And at the head of each page, the authorization seal.

I knew that seal. Every inquisitor knows that seal. It was pressed into our commissioning writs, stamped on our warrants, branded—literally branded, a small neat scar on the inside of the wrist—into every servant of the crown who took the oaths. The open eye above the closed gate. The mark of the Office of Inquiry itself.

Except these orders predated the Office of Inquiry by thirty years.

I stood in that blue-lit cellar and did the arithmetic, because arithmetic is the last thing to abandon you. The seal was authentic—or a forgery so perfect it had fooled a century of auditors, which amounted to the same thing. The orders bore it. Therefore the crown had ordered the massacre of the gatekeepers.

Therefore the crown had ordered the destruction of the only people capable of maintaining whatever it was paying the guild to keep sealed and starving.

Therefore the crown had spent a hundred years buying a lock and systematically murdering every locksmith.

"There's more," Pellam said softly. He had watched me read. Clerks watch everything; it is their whole profession. "Turn to the marginalia. The auditor's notes. There's one from sixty years back—an auditor named Vessick. He flagged the contradiction. Look at what he wrote."

I turned the pages. Vessick's note was cramped and furious, the handwriting of a man writing quickly before his courage failed:

*Query submitted to the Office re: strategic inconsistency of ongoing cull. Response received within the day, which is itself irregular. Response reads: "The lock is not meant to hold forever. The locksmiths are meant to die before it fails. Cease queries."*

Below that, in different ink, added later, smaller:

*I have decided to stop querying.*

And below *that*, in a third hand, almost gentle:

*Vessick retired to his family estate the following spring. Died peacefully. The estate burned down two years later with all heirs inside. Lamp oil requisitioned.*

I closed the book.

For a moment the cellar was very quiet. The candles burned blue. The water moved in the walls, going somewhere on purpose, and I thought: *a lock is not meant to hold forever, and the locksmiths are meant to die before it fails.* Which meant the crown was not defending something. The crown was *managing* something. Keeping it starved, keeping it sealed, keeping a countdown no one was permitted to see—and killing, with lamp oil and requisition forms, every soul who might understand what was being counted down to.

"Inquisitor?" Pellam whispered. "What does it mean?"

I should have said *nothing*. I should have said *heresy*, and reported the annexes, and had them burned, and had Pellam burned, and gone back to my quarters and slept the dreamless sleep of the faithful. That was the procedure. Procedure is a prayer you can perform with your hands, and my hands had performed it flawlessly for nineteen years.

Instead I heard myself say: "It means someone in the Office, a hundred years ago, wrote a contract in your guild's books using a seal that didn't exist yet. It means the massacre wasn't policy. It means it was *maintenance*." I set the ledger down with exaggerated care, the way you set down a thing that might go off. "It means, clerk, that I have been burning people for the crime of knowing how a lock works, on behalf of a crown that wants the lock to fail."

"That's treason," Pellam said. Not accusingly. Almost hopefully. As if treason were a door and he wanted to know whether it locked from the inside.

"Yes," I said. "Isn't it refreshing?"

* * *

I did not sleep that night. I sat in my quarters in the Grayfen garrison with the commissioning scar itching on my wrist—it does that sometimes, in cold weather, or so I had always told myself—and I reviewed my life the way I would review a suspect's accounts: line by line, looking for the entry that doesn't balance.

Nineteen years. Two hundred and six executions, each one documented, each one justified, each one a gatekeeper or a sympathizer or a scholar who had read too deeply into the wrong subject. I had been diligent. I had been *exemplary*. I had risen from acolyte to full inquisitor faster than anyone in the history of the Office, and I had told myself it was because I was precise, and devout, and incorruptible.

But precision cuts both ways. That was the itch. That was the small hot scratch behind the faith, the splinter working its way toward the skin's surface: if I was precise enough to find heresy in a fishmonger's ledgers, I was precise enough to find it in my own. And I had just found it, hadn't I? Written in brown ink, in a cellar, in a dead auditor's furious hand. *The lock is not meant to hold forever.*

I thought about the gatekeepers I had burned. Not as categories—as people. The old woman in Marsh Hollow who had sung while they lit the pyre, a song with a structure to it, repetitions, a hymn-shape, and I had noted in my report that she died unrepentant and possibly deranged. The young man two winters ago who had begged me not for mercy but for time—*just let me finish teaching her, she's only seven, she needs to learn the rest of it*—and I had denied him, because procedure, because the song was proscribed, because a seven-year-old learning a forbidden hymn was a threat vector requiring immediate resolution.

She had hummed it in the flames. I remembered that now. I had written *deranged* in the margin.

It had not been derangement. It had been inheritance. She had been a key, and I had melted her, and somewhere a lock was counting down the years until it failed, and the crown had known all along, and the crown had sent *me*.

There is a particular vertigo that comes when the scaffolding goes but the building stands. I had built myself out of service, out of certainty, out of the clean architecture of right and wrong as defined by the open eye above the closed gate. Remove the eye's authority and I was not an instrument of holy order. I was a woman with a brand on her wrist and two hundred and six names she could recite from memory—which, God help me, I could, because I had always been thorough.

Near dawn, a runner came with dispatches. I read them by candlelight with hands that were, I noted distantly, still perfectly steady.

The first concerned the free cities: mobilization, ultimatum, the machinery of a war beginning to turn over like a great cold engine. The second concerned a fugitive—a ledger-keeper of the Gilded Threshold, low-born, marked by the Office for questioning regarding the third ledger and certain prophetic materials seized from the Yarrow estate. Last seen fleeing south along the salt road. All inquisitors in the Marchlands directed to treat the fugitive as priority-one.

Xanthe Yarrow. I turned the name over. A clerk. A nothing. A boy with ink-stained fingers who had opened a book he shouldn't have and run.

Under the old dispensation—the one I had inhabited until roughly midnight—I would have saddled up at dawn and hunted him with the clean joy of the righteous. Priority-one. A key, probably. The last of the locksmiths, running along a salt road with a stolen ledger, and every instinct I had trained for nineteen years said *burn him before the lock fails*.

But the lock was *meant* to fail. That was the whole point. That was what the brown ink said. The crown wanted it to fail, wanted it starved and sealed and counting down, and the only people who could stop the failure were the ones I had been melting with lamp oil and requisition forms since before I could grow a beard.

So the question was no longer whether Xanthe Yarrow was a threat to the crown.

The question was whether the crown was a threat to everything else.

I took out a fresh sheet of paper, and I did something I had never done in nineteen years of flawless service.

I falsified a report. Small, careful, procedural: *Fugitive Yarrow tracked to river country, trail lost at Grayfen crossing, recommend reallocation of assets to coastal approaches.* A lie with proper margins and footnotes. Terror rendered as bureaucracy, pointed the other way for the first time.

Then I packed my saddlebags, woke my horse, and rode south along the salt road after a clerk I had been ordered to kill—for reasons I could not yet have explained to anyone, least of all myself, except perhaps this: the water in the walls of that cellar had been going somewhere on purpose, and so, it seemed, was I.

Behind me, Grayfen burned its morning lamps. Ahead, the road ran down through the Marchlands toward Cinderport, toward a boy with an iron stylus, toward whatever was sealed and starving beneath the world.

My wrist itched the whole way. I did not scratch it.
