## 13. The Free Cities Arm

### Wren

I've sat in a lot of rooms where men decided whether I lived or died. Usually they were small rooms, and usually there was money involved, and usually the decision went against me because someone in the room had more of it than I did. The Council Hall of Cinderport was not a small room. It had been a temple once, back before the free cities decided gods were bad for trade, and its ceiling still arched up into gloom like a throat deciding whether to swallow. There were forty seats on the council floor and every one of them was occupied by somebody who owned something — docks, granaries, banks, a surprising number of people who just owned other people's debts. Money involved. Decision of life and death. Some things you can count on.

They'd let me in because I'd asked loudly enough and because half the council owed me favors they'd been hoping would expire quietly. Favors don't expire. That's the first thing they teach you in my line of work, right after *never carry anything you can't drop running*.

The old general was on his feet when I arrived, which meant he'd been on his feet for a while, because General Hesk did not stand for anything he could sit through. He was seventy if he was a day, built like a door that had been kicked too many times, with a voice that had been used to shout across battlefields and never quite learned indoor manners.

"—and I say again, walls have held this city for three hundred years! The Marchlands garrisons will slow them. The river will slow them. And while they bleed themselves against our stone, the League sends terms, the League sends envoys, and we negotiate from strength instead of kneeling!"

He said *kneeling* the way some men say a curse word. Half the chamber nodded along. The other half looked at their ledgers, which is what merchants do instead of nodding when nodding might cost them money.

I found Xanthe near the back, standing where the shadows pooled under the old gallery, holding Odile's hand like she was made of glass and he was made of nothing at all. He'd shaved, or tried to. There was a cut on his jaw from doing it badly. He looked like a man who had been told he was going to speak in front of forty owners of the world and had spent the morning trying to be sick about it privately.

"You're pale," I said.

"I'm always pale."

"Paler."

He didn't argue. That's how I knew it was bad. Xanthe Yarrow argues with weather. I'd watched him lose an argument with a mule outside Grayfen and come out of it convinced he'd won on points.

Down on the floor, Hesk was winding down toward his peroration, which meant volume. "We are not gatekeepers!" he thundered. "We are not kings! We are free cities, and our freedom has one foundation, and it is *stone*, and I will not spend the lives of Cinderport's sons chasing a children's story about doors beneath the earth!"

"Then you'll spend them on top of the earth instead," I said, not loudly, but the hall was one of those treacherous temples where everything carried, and forty heads turned toward the gallery like a field of ugly sunflowers.

Hesk squinted up at me. "The smuggler. You have the floor only by sufferance, woman."

"Everything in this room is by sufferance," I said. "That's rather my point." I came down the steps, because you can't argue with a man from above him; he'll just feel the architecture agreeing with him. "General. How long do your walls hold?"

"Against the usurper's host? A year. Perhaps two."

"And how many people does Cinderport hold?"

"Four hundred thousand, in the worst of times."

"And how much food in your granaries?"

Someone down on the floor shifted. A grain factor named Ossel, whose face had gone the color of his own unsold wheat.

"A year," Hesk said slowly, "if rationed."

"So the walls hold for two years and the bread lasts for one." I let that sit. Temples are good for letting things sit; they were built for it. "You're not proposing a defense, General. You're proposing a very long funeral with excellent acoustics."

There was laughter. Not much, and not kind, but there was laughter, and in a council chamber laughter is a weapon that changes hands fast.

"The gate is real," I said, before the laughter could be taken back. "I know because I've touched the threshold of it. So has the boy standing behind me, and the child asleep on a bench in the antechamber, who sings a song older than every contract in this room. The crown's whole army runs on one thing — a king who cannot die because he's anchored to a gate that keeps him alive. Break the anchor, break the army's faith, and half of it goes home. Faith is cheaper to kill than soldiers. I should know. I've smuggled both."

Ossel stood up, all jowls and indignation. "And if the girl is wrong? If the ledger-keeper is wrong? We commit the city's defense to a *prophecy*?"

"We commit it to arithmetic," I said. "Which is apparently the only scripture you people read."

That's when the chamberlain leaned over and whispered to the First Speaker, and the First Speaker's eyes came up and found the gallery, and found Xanthe, and I felt him go stiff beside me like a man hearing his own name called in a town where he owes money everywhere.

"The keeper of the third ledger," the First Speaker said, "will address the council."

And Xanthe Yarrow, who had spent twenty-six years perfecting the art of being overlooked, walked down into the light where nobody could overlook him, and I watched the terror hit him like cold water, and I watched him walk anyway.

That, more than anything I said, is why I'm telling this story.

### Hesk

I have been a soldier for fifty-two years, and in that time I have learned exactly three things worth teaching.

First: amateurs talk about courage. Professionals talk about supply lines.

Second: every plan works until it meets the enemy, and then the best you can hope for is that yours fails second.

Third: when a young man stands up in front of a crowd and looks as though he'd rather be flayed, believe him. That's not cowardice. Cowardice sits down. That's a man who understands precisely how much his words weigh, which is rarer than valor and worth more.

The ledger-keeper understood it. I watched him take the floor of the Council Hall the way a man walks into deep water — knowing the depth, going in regardless — and I confess I softened toward him before he opened his mouth, which was inconvenient, because I had prepared myself to despise him.

"My name is Xanthe Yarrow," he said. His voice cracked on the second word and he stopped, and breathed, and started over. "My name is Xanthe Yarrow. Until a month ago I kept accounts for the Gilded Threshold guild. Bottled storms. Borrowed years. Ordinary impossibilities. I was good at it because I was unremarkable, and I want to be honest with you: I liked being unremarkable. It was the one skill I ever cultivated on purpose."

A ripple went through the benches. People don't expect honesty in chambers. They expect performance, and when they get honesty they don't know where to put it.

"I'm not here to tell you a prophecy is true," he went on. "Prophecies are just records written by people who paid attention. What I can tell you is what's in the ledgers. I've read them. The guild has been paying, for a hundred years, to keep a gate sealed and starving. Someone wanted that gate shut badly enough to fund it across four generations of guildmasters. Ask yourselves what kind of expense survives a century of accountants. Only the important ones."

Ossel called out, "Ledgers can be forged!"

"Yes," Xanthe said. "I forge them professionally. These aren't forged. I know the difference the way you know the difference between real wheat and chaff, Master Ossel — though I notice your granaries are mostly the latter this season."

I laughed out loud, alone, like a fool, and half the chamber laughed with me because a soldier laughing gives the rest permission. The boy had done in ten seconds what I couldn't do in two hours: he'd made the grain factor ridiculous. There's hope for him.

"But here is my true argument," he said, "and it isn't prophecy, and it isn't ledgers. It's this. The Hollow King does not negotiate. I've seen what his inquisitors leave behind. I stood in a tollhouse at Grayfen where a family was put to death for the crime of being descended from the wrong bloodline, and the youngest survivor was six years old, and she survived because she hid in a flour barrel for two days." His voice didn't crack this time. It went flat and quiet, which is worse. "Your walls held against sieges, General. This isn't a siege. Sieges want the city intact. He wants it emptied. Terms are what he offers cities he intends to leave standing. Has anyone here actually seen terms?"

Silence. The bad kind. I'd heard it before, in war councils where everyone realizes at the same moment that the map is wrong.

"So the choice isn't walls or gate," he finished. "It's gate or grave. I'm sorry. I know it's not the answer anyone wants. It wasn't the answer I wanted either."

He stepped back, and the chamber erupted into argument, and I sat with my hands folded and thought about supply lines, and about the Marchlands garrisons, and about a report that had come in that morning that I had not yet shared with this chamber because sharing it would end the debate before it began.

The vanguard had crossed the Sere. Three days ahead of every estimate. Whatever else happened in this room today, arithmetic had already voted.

### Xanthe

Here is what nobody tells you about mattering: it feels exactly like drowning, except everyone is watching you drown and taking notes.

I stood on the council floor with my heart going like a debtor's fist on a door, and I waited for the shouting to resolve into something, and what it resolved into was worse than shouting. It resolved into procedure. The free cities had been born in rebellion against a crown, and they had inherited the crown's worst habit: when frightened, form a committee.

They formed three.

I retreated to the wall next to Wren, who handed me a flask without looking at me. It was not water. I drank it anyway.

"You did well," she said.

"I insulted a grain factor."

"You insulted a grain factor *accurately*. In politics that's called charisma." She took the flask back. "They'll vote. They always vote. The question is which way, and that depends on the general, and the general hasn't spoken since you did."

I looked down at Hesk. The old man sat motionless amid the storm of his colleagues, hands folded on the table, staring at a point somewhere in the middle distance where I suspected a map hung in his mind. When the First Speaker finally called for positions, he rose with the unhurried weight of a drawbridge coming down.

"I withdraw my motion," he said.

The chamber went silent so fast you could hear the candles burning.

"For fifty years I have argued that stone is the answer to every question a crown asks," Hesk continued. "I am not ashamed of it. Stone fed me, stone buried my friends, stone held Vhalen's Gate when eleven thousand men died on its slopes. But I have a report, dated this morning, that the usurper's vanguard crossed the Sere River yesterday. Not next month. Yesterday. They moved through the Marchlands at double march, which means they are not foraging, which means they brought their own supply, which means this was planned before any of us knew it was possible." He turned, slowly, and looked at me. "The ledger-keeper says the choice is gate or grave. I have spent the day trying to find a third option, because I am a stubborn old man and I hate being right for the wrong side. I have not found one. So I say this: arm the free cities. Every wall, every militia, every ship. And send the gatekeeper whatever he needs, because if he fails, none of the rest of it matters, and if he succeeds, perhaps the rest of it won't be needed."

He sat down. Forty owners of the world looked at each other, and did the thing that owners of the world do when a soldier makes the cost of disagreement visible: they voted yes.

I should have felt triumphant. Instead I felt the full weight of it land, all at once, the way a debt lands when the payment date arrives. Four hundred thousand people in Cinderport alone. More in the sister cities. All of them now, in some dim procedural sense, *mine* — or at least entrusted to a plan that ran through me like a road through a swamp.

Wren squeezed my shoulder. "Breathe, ledger-keeper."

"I'm counting," I said.

"Counting what?"

"How many ways this goes wrong. I'm at forty. It's a familiar number."

She laughed, and it helped, the way her laugh always did — not because it fixed anything but because it insisted things could be laughed at, which is a different service entirely.

It was Odile who ended the day. She had been sleeping in the antechamber through all of it, worn out from the road, guarded by one of Wren's crew who was probably stealing the candlesticks. And as the council emptied and the great doors boomed open and the ordinary noise of Cinderport flooded in — carts, gulls, a crier announcing the vote to a street that hadn't asked — Odile came to the doorway rubbing her eyes, and she began to hum.

The same hymn. The one from the tollhouse, the one she sang in her sleep, the gate-hymn that no living choir had sung whole in a hundred years.

But this time she wasn't humming it the way a child hums a lullaby. This time she was walking as she hummed, past us, past the guards, out into the evening light, turning left with the absolute certainty of a compass needle, and the hymn changed under her breath — gained a phrase neither Wren nor I had heard from her before, low and patient, like something answering.

"She knows the way," Wren said quietly.

"Knows the way to where?"

Odile stopped at the top of the council steps and pointed, small finger steady, down the avenue toward the old aqueduct, where the last light of day lay along the stone like a blade laid on a table.

"There," she said. "It's awake now. It wants its keys."

And beneath my ribs, faint as a pulse through a wall, I felt the iron stylus in my pocket grow warm for the first time since Grayfen.
