## 14. The Hymn Beneath the City

There is a particular kind of silence that lives under cities, and I had spent thirty years not thinking about it. Cinderport's aqueduct carries water down from the Grayfen hills in a long stone spine, and beneath that spine, in the dark where the foundation pilings go down into the wet rock, there is a silence so complete it feels less like an absence of sound and more like a held breath.

I was standing in it at two hours past midnight, ankle-deep in cold runoff, holding an iron stylus like a candle, listening to a seven-year-old hum.

Let me back up, because the ledger-keeper in me insists on proper sequence.

* * *

It started, as most of my problems now did, with Odile's sleep.

We'd been three days out of Grayfen, holed up in the kind of rooming house that charges by the hour and doesn't ask why your traveling party includes a child who won't stop humming. Wren had paid for the week with a coin she swore was legal tender in four cities and stolen in a fifth. Odile slept on the pallet between us—Wren insisted on the arrangement, and I'd stopped arguing about anything Wren insisted on, mostly because she was usually right and always louder about it than I could afford to be.

And in her sleep, Odile hummed. Not nursery tunes. Not the little songs smugglers' children pick up in taverns. It was a rising-falling pattern, five notes up, three notes down, then a pause like a held breath, then again. It had a structure to it. It had, if I'm honest, the structure of a ledger entry: balanced, deliberate, every note accounted for.

The first night I told myself it was nothing. The second night I found myself writing the pattern down in the margin of the third ledger, because apparently I was the sort of person now who transcribed children's sleep-songs into century-old books of forbidden accounts. The third night, Wren sat up across the room, looked at me in the dark, and said, "You hear it too."

"It's a hymn," I said. "A gate-hymn. The tollhouse family—the ones Vail didn't kill—they used to sing it at the changing of the watch. Odile's grandmother taught it to her, or she absorbed it before—" I stopped. Before the slaughter. That was the phrase I kept not saying.

"And?" Wren said.

"And gate-hymns aren't decorative. Anselm told me—they're not songs *about* the gate. They're songs the gate *taught*. They're how keepers found thresholds when the maps burned." I turned the ledger toward her, my transcription of the pattern. "I know what this is. I just don't know why I know."

"You're the last of them," Wren said simply. "Maybe it's in the blood. Maybe it's in the debt." She said the word *debt* the way she always did lately—lightly, but watching me while she said it, the way you test ice with one foot.

Because yes. We knew about the final page by then. We knew my parents had bought my life from something, and that something considered the account open. Wren had stopped asking me how I felt about it around day two, which I appreciated, because the honest answer was *like a shipment someone else marked freight-due*, and I wasn't ready to be that honest even with myself.

"Where does it point?" Wren asked.

"That's the problem. Hymns are directions, but they're directions for people who can hear the answering part. I can't hear anything. I'm tone-deaf to my own inheritance." I closed the ledger. "But she isn't. And she hums it loudest in her sleep, and last night she rolled off the pallet and crawled toward the window before I caught her. Toward the harbor side. Toward the old city."

"The aqueduct," Wren said.

"The aqueduct," I agreed.

She pulled her boots on. "Well. Nothing suspicious about following a sleepwalking child into the dark under a municipal waterworks at night. Let's go."

That is how, at two hours past midnight, I came to be standing in the held-breath silence beneath Cinderport, ankle-deep in runoff, with a smuggler, a child, and an iron stylus, waiting for a door to notice me.

* * *

Getting under the aqueduct had been insultingly easy, which should have been my first clue. The maintenance hatches along the lower channel were supposed to be locked; the locks were rusted open, as though someone had oiled them recently and then thought better of leaving evidence of the oiling. The inspection stair down into the piling gallery was supposed to be guarded by the Water Wardens; we passed one, asleep at his post with his lamp still burning, snoring with the deep contentment of a man who has been made to sleep. Wren crouched over him, sniffed, and grimaced. "Poppy-haze. Expensive stuff. Somebody wanted this route quiet tonight."

"Somebody," I said, "or something."

"Don't say *something*," Wren muttered. "I hate *something*. *Something* means the price list has no numbers in it."

Odile walked between us, awake now but strange with it—not frightened, exactly. Focused. The way a compass needle is focused. She hadn't spoken since we'd entered the hatch; she just walked, small bare feet finding dry stones in the wet dark without being shown them, humming her five-up-three-down under her breath, and the sound carried strangely down here, bouncing off stone in ways that made no acoustic sense, arriving at my ears from directions she wasn't standing in.

The piling gallery opened around us like the ribs of some drowned cathedral. Forty-foot columns of granite rose out of black water, holding up the weight of the aqueduct and half the city above, and between them the runoff channels braided and merged, and everything was furred with a century of pale mineral bloom. Our lantern showed us maybe twenty feet in any direction. Beyond that, the dark had texture. Weight.

And the silence. I want to be precise about the silence, because it mattered. Cities are never quiet—even underground, even at night, you hear the city through the stone: cart-wheels, drains, arguments, bells. Under the aqueduct there was none of that. Not muffled. Absent. As though we had stepped out of the world's hearing range rather than merely beneath its floor. When Odile hummed, the sound didn't echo so much as *land*, each phrase settling somewhere ahead of us like a coin dropped into a well we couldn't see the bottom of.

"She's paying a toll," I said quietly. "Listen. Every time she finishes the pattern, there's the pause. That's not rest. That's a question. Five up, three down, hold—and something holds it back."

"And answers?"

"Not yet." I lifted the stylus. In the lantern light the iron looked duller than usual, older, its worn tip catching no shine at all. My grandfather's stylus. The one tool of my inheritance I'd carried out of Cinderport the first time I fled it, back when fleeing was still something I believed I'd survive cleanly. "I think it's waiting for me to answer. And I don't know the words."

"Then hum," Wren said.

"I told you, I can't hear the—"

"Xanthe." She took my sleeve. In the lantern light her face was stripped of all its smuggler's varnish, and what was underneath was scared, and trying very hard to be only practical. "You're a ledger-keeper. You've spent your whole life balancing entries you never made. So balance this one. She asks. You answer. You've got the pen. Use it."

Which was, I will note for the record, terrible musical advice and excellent Xanthe advice.

I closed my eyes. I thought about ledgers—about the way a good clerk doesn't invent the missing figure but *listens* for it, lets the shape of the whole column tell him what the gap must weigh. Five up. Three down. Hold. I hummed it back, badly, flat, a clerk's rendition, and hated every note, and kept going anyway.

The silence changed.

I don't have a better word. The silence *changed*—tilted, the way a room tilts when a door opens at the far end of the house and you feel the draft before you hear anything. Odile stopped walking. Turned. Her eyes in the lantern light were wide and entirely awake and entirely unchildlike, and she raised one small hand and pointed past the third piling, into the dark.

"There," she said. First word she'd spoken in an hour. "He woke up."

"*He?*" Wren's hand went to the knife at her hip.

But I was already looking, because the stylus in my fist had gone warm. Not hot—warm, the specific warmth of a hand closing around yours, and I knew that warmth, I realized, I had known it my whole life without naming it, every time I'd balanced a column and felt that small click of rightness, every time ink had seemed to flow a half-second before I dipped the pen. It had never been talent. It had been company.

Beyond the third piling, where the lantern light gave up, the dark was no longer empty. It was *architectural*. As my eyes adjusted—or as something permitted them to adjust—I saw the shape of it: a stair. A grand one, cut down into the living rock behind and below the pilings, descending into a depth the aqueduct's foundations had no business containing. Its top steps were sealed—not blocked, *sealed*—a slab of grey stone fitted across them with a precision that made guild masonry look like a child's mud pies, and across the slab ran lines of lettering in no alphabet I knew, except that my eyes kept sliding off them and landing, every time, on the one character I did know: a mark like a stylus lying horizontal, repeated along the border like a signature line awaiting a name.

The gate's threshold. Under my city. Under the guildhall I'd given thirty years to, close enough that I might have walked over it ten thousand times, carrying other people's accounts across the sleeping body of the one account that mattered.

"Oh," I said. Very quietly. Very stupidly. "Oh, it's been *here*."

Odile tugged my coat. "It knows you," she said. "It's shy."

"Gates aren't shy."

"This one is. It's been shut a hundred years and everyone it loved is dead." She said it without drama, the way children report weather, and it went through me like a blade through wet paper. "Sing the rest."

"I don't know the rest."

"Yes you do." She pointed at my chest. "It's in there. Grandmother said the song isn't learned, it's *owed*. You just have to owe it out loud."

So I stood in the freezing runoff, in the breath-held dark, under the city that had raised me and sold me, and I sang to a sealed door. Badly. Flatly. A clerk's rendition of a hymn a murdered family had died keeping alive, delivered by the last heir of the blood that wrote it, accompanied by a seven-year-old whose voice did the actual work while mine stumbled along underneath like a man carrying furniture up stairs he's never climbed. Wren stood watch at the gallery mouth with her knife drawn, muttering statistics about our survival odds, which were, per her, "not great, don't ask."

And the seal listened.

I felt it listening the way you feel a creditor reading your letter—every line weighed, every figure checked against a master record you'll never be allowed to see. The warm presence behind the stylus leaned closer. Somewhere in the stone, deep below the slab, something vast shifted its attention from Odile's voice to mine, and the attention had *texture*: old, patient, wounded, and hungry in the way that only very old faithful things get hungry—for completion, for the rest of a sentence interrupted a century ago.

The last phrase left my mouth. The pause came. The held breath.

Then the letters on the slab moved.

They flowed like ink in water, rearranging themselves, and I read them—understood them—without any process I can defend, the meaning simply arriving in me the way figures arrive in a finished column:

**THE STYLUS IS KNOWN. THE HAND THAT HOLDS IT IS KNOWN. THE BLOOD IS ENTERED.**

Warmth flooded up my arm, and for one vertiginous moment I felt *recognized*—truly, wholly seen by something older than the guild, older than the crown, older than the lie of my small safe life, and I want to tell you it felt like homecoming. Part of it did. Part of it felt like a hand around my throat checking my pulse against a contract.

Then the letters moved again.

**THE GATE OPENS TO THREE KEYS TURNED AS ONE. ONE STANDS HERE. THE SECOND SLEEPS IN PRAYER. THE THIRD IS CHAINED IN IRON AND GRIEF.**

I read it twice. Three times. Because I am a careful man, and careful men check their arithmetic before they panic.

"One stands here," Wren repeated slowly. "That's you. Or the girl. Second sleeps in prayer—that's a monastery, that's Anselm's direction, that's findable. Third is chained in iron and grief." She looked at me. I looked at her. Neither of us said it, because saying it would make it load-bearing.

Chained. Iron. Grief. There was exactly one person in the world fitting that description that either of us knew of, and he was a king kept alive beneath a usurper's palace as an anchor, a man who appeared in dreams holding an iron stylus, a man whose bloodline was the third key, and he was a hundred leagues away inside the strongest fortress on the continent, in the personal keeping of the thing wearing the crown.

"We need a king," I said finally. "To open a door. Under a fish market."

"Aqueduct," said Odile helpfully.

"The gate demands a key we don't have," I said, "and the key is a *person*, and the person is the crown's favorite prisoner, and I am—" and here my voice did something humiliating, cracked slightly, the clerk's composure slipping its binding—"I am a ledger-keeper with a rusty pen and a dead family and a debt I can't read, standing in ankle-deep water, and the door thinks I'm *inadequate*, and the door is *right*."

The letters pulsed once. Gently. Almost kindly. And new characters surfaced at the bottom of the slab, smaller, like a postscript, like a note added in a different hand:

**THE DOOR DOES NOT THINK YOU ARE INADEQUATE, KEEPER. THE DOOR THINKS YOU ARE EARLY.**

Something in my chest broke a little, in the good way, the way ice breaks in spring. I put my palm flat against the slab. It was cold and then it was warm and then, faintly, from far below—from whatever chamber lay at the bottom of those drowned stairs—came a sound. Not a voice. A resonance. One low sustained note, like the memory of a chord, like a bell that had been struck a hundred years ago and was only now finishing its ring.

The gate, acknowledging receipt. Holding my entry open. Waiting.

Then the warmth withdrew, the letters stilled into dead stone, the ordinary dark poured back in, and the city's noise returned all at once—a cart overhead, a drain gurgling, the distant bell of the Harbor Watch counting three—and we were just three trespassers in a wet cellar, and the greatest door in the world was a slab again, and I was shaking so hard Wren had to take the lantern out of my hand before I dropped it.

* * *

We didn't speak until we were back above ground, sitting on the aqueduct's maintenance walk with our legs dangling over the channel, watching the sky go grey at the edges. Odile had fallen asleep against Wren's shoulder mid-sentence, which struck me as the single most sensible act of the night.

"So," Wren said. "Monastery in the mountains. Old monk. Then the impenetrable fortress and the imprisoned king." She ticked them off on her fingers. "One of those is hard. The other is impossible. And behind us, presumably, Vail is doing what Vail does, which is arrive."

"Behind us," I said, "is also Corvane."

She went still. "You think he's come himself?"

"He sent watchers after me within a day of the prophecy. He's had weeks. He's had my habits memorized for thirty years—he *made* my habits. If I were hunting me, I'd have guessed the aqueduct eventually. Everything in my life comes back to ledgers, and the oldest ledger in the world is under this city." I rubbed my face. "He'll come. And he won't bring soldiers. He'll bring an offer, because Corvane never spends force when a price will do. He taught me that. He taught me everything."

"Then we leave tonight. Now. Before dawn."

"In a moment." I looked east, where the grey was warming toward gold, where Cinderport was waking up above the sleeping god in its cellar—fishwives and clerks and dockhands, ten thousand people balancing their small daily accounts directly on top of the largest unpaid balance in history. Thirty years I'd been one of them. Thirty years I'd thought being overlooked was safety.

The stylus sat in my lap, cooling, ordinary, iron. But I could still feel the ghost of the warmth, and the postscript, and the patience of a door that believed I was early rather than insufficient.

Early. I could work with early. Early was a deadline, and deadlines were the one language I'd ever been fluent in.

"Wren," I said. "How fast can you get us to a monastery?"

She smiled, tired and sharp. "Define fast."

And that was when we heard the footsteps on the gravel path below the aqueduct walk—unhurried, measured, familiar as my own heartbeat—and a voice I had known since I was nine years old, calling up through the morning mist with terrible gentleness.

"Xanthe, boy. Your grandfather's stylus is showing. Come down, and let's talk terms."
