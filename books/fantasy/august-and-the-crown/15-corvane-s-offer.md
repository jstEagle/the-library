## 15. Corvane's Offer

The monastery road out of Grayfen was the kind of road that made you believe in nothing. It went up through pine and fog and old rock, switchbacking like a man trying to escape his own shadow, and for three days it gave us no one to meet but goats. I'd started to think we might actually make the high pass before anyone found us.

That was my first mistake: thinking in terms of *before* instead of *if*.

Wren spotted them first, because Wren always spotted things first — it was her whole trade. She stopped mid-stride on the trail, tilted her head the way she did when listening to a market she hadn't entered yet, and said, "We've got company. One man. Walking like he owns the mountain."

"Bandit?"

"Bandits don't walk like they're *owed* the mountain." She squinted back down the switchbacks. "He's alone. Grey coat. Guild cut."

My stomach dropped through the trail and kept going, all the way down to whatever lies beneath mountains. I knew only one man who wore a grey coat with a guild cut and walked like the world owed him interest.

"Corvane," I said.

"You know him."

"He raised me." The words came out flat, which was the only way I could say them without them breaking something. "Practically. He signed my apprenticeship papers. He taught me double-entry bookkeeping. He taught me that a ledger never lies, which is the closest thing to religion I ever had."

"And now?"

"Now he's climbing a mountain to find me, and guildmasters don't climb mountains for lost property." I looked at Odile, asleep against Wren's pack, her small mouth still moving around the shape of a hymn even in her dreams. "They climb mountains for liabilities."

* * *

There was a shrine at the next bend — one of those roadside things the mountain folk built to whatever god handled weather, a stone alcove with a rusted bell and an offering bowl full of rainwater and pine needles. Wren took one look at it and started planning exits, because that was Wren: she could look at any space and see four ways out of it, the way I could look at any ledger and see where the numbers had been made to lie.

I sat down on the shrine's low wall and waited, because running from Corvane on a narrow trail with a child and a wounded smuggler seemed like a good way to die tired.

He came up the last stretch of path unhurried, grey coat immaculate despite three days of mountain, his walking stick clicking against stone in the rhythm I'd heard outside his office door for fifteen years. Click. Click. Click. He looked exactly as he always had: silver at the temples, ink-stained fingers, eyes like two well-kept accounts — everything recorded, nothing revealed.

"Xanthe," he said. "You look terrible."

"I've been told."

"Running doesn't suit you. You were never built for it. You were built for desks." He set his stick against the shrine wall and lowered himself onto the stone beside me with the small grunt of a man whose knees had opinions. For a moment we just sat there, master and apprentice, looking out at the fog rolling through the valley below like a slow white tide.

It would have been almost pleasant, if I hadn't known what he was.

"The Threshold has searched for you since the night you ran," he said. "Do you know how much that costs? Trackers don't come cheap. Neither does silence."

"I imagine the crown pays well for both."

Something moved behind his eyes — not guilt, exactly. Guilt would have been new information. It was more like the flicker of a clerk finding an error in a column and deciding whether to correct it or leave it be.

"The crown pays *everyone*," Corvane said. "That's not corruption, boy. That's arithmetic. Every institution in this world is paid by someone to do something, and the Gilded Threshold has simply been honest about it longer than most." He reached into his coat and produced a flask, poured a measure of something amber into the cap, and offered it to me. I didn't take it. He drank it himself. "Your grandfather drank. Did you know that? A nervous man. Kept his ledgers beautifully, though. Best hand in the house until the end."

"You knew him."

"I trained him. As I trained his son. As I trained you." He capped the flask. "Three generations of Yarrows through my ledger hall. Do you understand what that means? Do you have any idea how rare it is, in my line of work, to keep a family *employed* for three generations? Most of our people burn bright and vanish. Yours endured. I was proud of that. I want you to know that, before anything else. I was proud of you."

"Before what else?"

He sighed, and it was such a familiar sigh — the sigh of a guildmaster about to explain why the bonus was smaller than promised — that for one horrible moment I was twelve years old again, standing on the worn flagstones of the counting floor, wanting nothing more than to make this man nod at me the way he nodded at the good clerks.

"Here is the situation," Corvane said. "The crown wants the third ledger. The crown wants the child. In exchange, the Threshold offers you protection — real protection, not the kind we sell. A place in the guildhouse at Vail's Crossing, under a new name, a stipend, a life. You could keep ledgers again, Xanthe. Honest ones. No prophecies, no gates, no kings. Just columns that add up. Isn't that what you always wanted?"

And the worst part — the part I'll be ashamed of until I die — is that for about four seconds, I wanted it. Four seconds of imagining the counting floor, the smell of ink and lamp oil, the comfortable anonymity of being nobody's heir and everybody's clerk. Four seconds of *home*.

Then I looked at Odile, humming in her sleep, and the four seconds ended.

"What happens to her?" I asked. "The child. Under your protection arrangement."

"That's not your concern—"

"It's entirely my concern. She hums a hymn in her sleep that makes inquisitors stand very still. What does the crown do with children who do that, Corvane? Does your protection cover that? Is there a column for it?"

He didn't answer, and his not-answering was louder than any confession.

* * *

"So let me see if I have the entries right," I said, when I could trust my voice. "Because I learned from the best, and the best taught me to reconcile every account."

"Xanthe—"

"The Threshold has been taking payments from the crown for a hundred years. Payments to maintain a registry. To keep records of gatekeeper bloodlines. To note births. To note deaths. To note *locations*." My hands were shaking, so I folded them in my lap like a proper clerk. "Every year, some clerk — maybe you, maybe whoever held your chair before you — writes down where the descendants are living. And every few years, the crown's people come and collect one. And the guild collects its fee. And the ledgers balance."

"Yes," Corvane said quietly.

"My grandfather. My father. They knew. They worked in your hall, they kept your books, and they must have known what those books were *for*. And they stayed anyway, because—" My voice cracked and I hated it. "Because staying was safe. Because the fee came with protection. Because as long as the registry was accurate, the Yarrows were worth more alive than dead. We weren't a family. We were an *asset class*."

"You were a family that survived," Corvane said. "Which is more than can be said for the others. Look down the list, Xanthe. The Tollhouses. The Merrowgates. The Vances. Extinct, extinct, extinct. And the Yarrows endure. Because we played it carefully. Because we understood that the alternative to complicity isn't purity — it's a shallow grave off the Salt Road."

"The Tollhouse family is dead anyway. All but one. Your careful registry led Vail's inquisitors straight to their door."

Something did cross his face then. I watched for it. Something old and tired, there and gone, like a fish surfacing in a deep pond.

"The registry was meant to protect, not to hunt," he said. "When I inherited the contract, that was the understanding. Findable, yes. Accounted for. But the crown's end of the bargain changed, and by the time I understood how much it had changed, the Threshold was too entangled to pull free. You don't untangle a century of payments in a season. You just... keep the books. And tell yourself the numbers mean something kinder than they do."

"That's not a defense. That's a confession with better posture."

"Perhaps." He turned and looked at me fully then, and I saw something I'd never seen in his face before: age. Not the performed dignity of a guildmaster, but actual age, actual weight, a man carrying a hundred years of other people's entries. "I'm offering you the only mercy I have left to give, boy. Take the girl out of the equation. Give me the ledger. Live."

"No."

"Xanthe—"

"No." I stood up, and my legs were steadier than I expected them to be. "You taught me that a ledger never lies. Fine. Here's the entry: you sold my family to the thing that ate them, for a hundred years, and called it stewardship. And now you're here to close the account, and you dressed it up as an offer because that's what we do — we dress things up. Bottled storms. Borrowed years. Protection. It's all the same trade, Corvane. Everything's got a price, and somebody else always pays it."

He rose too, slowly, taking up his stick. And I braced myself — for guards coming up the trail, for a knife, for the click-click-click of a man who had decided I was a bad debt to be written off.

Instead he said, "Where is the third ledger?"

"Burned."

"Liar. You fled Cinderport with it under your coat; the watchers saw. You've kept it closer than the child." He tapped the stick once against the stone. "I won't take it from you. Understand that. If I'd wanted it taken, you'd already be bleeding. I'm asking. One last time, as the man who taught you your letters: give it to me, and I will spend the rest of my life making the crown regret ever dealing with the Threshold. There are ways. There are *entries*, Xanthe. A contract a century old has clauses, and clauses have weaknesses, and I am very, very good at weaknesses."

For a heartbeat — half a heartbeat — I believed him. Or wanted to. That was the cruelest trick he'd ever pulled on me: making me hope.

"And if I say no?"

He looked past me, down the trail, at Wren standing with her hand resting easy near her belt-knife, at Odile stirring awake against her pack. Then back at me.

"Then I report that I found you, and that you refused, and the crown sends people who don't ask twice," he said. "I bought you three days by reporting false sightings in the Marchlands. Three days, perhaps five. After that, the inquisitors stop believing my reports and start checking them." He picked up his stick. "You were the best clerk I ever trained. It grieves me that you're about to become the worst debt I ever carried."

"Corvane." He paused, halfway into turning away. "Why come yourself? Why climb the mountain? You could have sent anyone."

He was quiet long enough that the wind filled the silence with pine-sound.

"Because someone owed you the truth face to face," he said finally. "And because I wanted to see, with my own eyes, whether you'd become what they say gatekeepers become." His eyes went briefly, involuntarily, toward Odile. "Whether there was any point in the last hundred years at all."

"And? Did you see?"

He looked at me for a long moment — measuring, weighing, entering something into whatever ledger he kept behind those grey eyes. Then he turned and started back down the trail, and his voice drifted up through the fog, dry as ever:

"I saw a man about to cost me everything. Keep the girl singing, Xanthe. It's the only currency the gate respects."

Click. Click. Click. Down through the pines, until the fog took him.

Wren came and stood beside me. "Well," she said. "That was either a rescue or a warning."

"With Corvane, it's usually both." I watched the empty trail a while longer. "He gave us three days. Maybe five."

"Is that a gift or a leash?"

I thought about the flask he'd drunk alone. The fish that surfaced once in the deep pond of his face. *Someone owed you the truth face to face.*

"I don't know yet," I said. "But I know we can't be here when the answer arrives. We need to move tonight, and we need to move fast — because if Corvane found us, so can the people who don't bother asking."

Behind us, half-asleep, Odile began to hum.

And far below us in the fog-choked valley, faint as a memory, something answered — a second voice in the hymn, rising from the direction of the aqueduct stair, from the dark beneath Cinderport, where a sealed door had begun, very softly, to listen.
