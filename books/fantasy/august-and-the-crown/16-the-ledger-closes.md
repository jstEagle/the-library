## 16. The Ledger Closes

*Xanthe*

Corvane's hand was still extended between us, palm up, patient as a collection notice.

"The ledger," he said again. "And the girl. That's all I've ever asked of you, Xanthe. Everything else—the roads, the food, the coin in your pocket—I've given freely."

I looked at his open hand and thought about how many times I'd seen that exact gesture across a counting table. It was the guild's oldest trick, older than Corvane: make the theft look like an exchange. Make the victim feel they were the one being generous.

"You've given me nothing," I said. "You *sold* me. Or rented me, at least. A century of payments to keep my family findable, and I'm the last item left on the shelf."

Something moved behind his eyes—not guilt; I'd have bet the whole third ledger he didn't have any—but something like regret that the merchandise had learned to read.

"Xanthe." He said my name the way he'd said it when I was nine years old and had miscounted a column of storm-bottles: gently, as if the failure were mine to carry. "The crown does not negotiate. The inquisitor is three streets away with thirty men. Give them what they want and live. Refuse, and I will watch you die, and it will grieve me, and I will still sleep soundly, because I will have done the arithmetic correctly."

"That's the difference between us," I said. "I never learned to sleep after arithmetic."

I heard Wren shift behind me—she'd been edging toward the stairwell since Corvane's hunters had filled the doorway of the tollhouse ruin, six of them, crossbows loose at their hips. Odile was pressed into the corner where the hearth had been, her small hands over her ears, and she was humming.

I hadn't noticed it at first because I'd been so busy being terrified. But now I did. The hymn. The one she sang in her sleep, the one the gate-hymn fragments in Anselm's letters matched note for note. It filled the ruined tollhouse like water filling a cup, quiet and rising.

One of the hunters nearest the door took a step back. Just one. His face did something complicated.

"Hold," Corvane said, without turning around. He knew his men. He'd trained them against things like this, or thought he had.

"Guildmaster," I said, and I picked up the third ledger from the table between us. My grandfather's iron stylus was already through my belt, warm from my hip. "You want this. You've wanted it your whole career, I'd guess—it's the only reason you let me live past chapter one of whatever contract has my name in it. So here's my counteroffer."

His eyebrows rose a fraction. He liked counteroffers. He respected them. That was going to be his mistake.

"I keep the ledger," I said. "I keep the girl. And you tell your hunters to stand down long enough for us to walk out of here, because if they shoot, I drop this book into the brazier, and then the only thing the crown gets for its century of payments is ash."

It was a bluff. Mostly. I would burn it if I had to, but every page of that book was a key to something larger than either of us, and Corvane knew it better than I did.

He studied me for a long moment. Outside, rain ticked against the broken roof tiles. Odile's hymn climbed another note.

"You've changed," he said finally, and there was something almost like pride in it, which was the cruelest thing he could have done. "The boy who catalogued bottled storms would never have bargained."

"The boy who catalogued bottled storms trusted you."

"Yes," Corvane said softly. "That was the arrangement."

And he snapped his fingers.

I'll give the hunters this—they were fast. Two bolts came out of the dark of the doorway before my brain finished processing the snap. I was already moving, though, because some animal part of me had understood, three seconds earlier than my dignity did, that there was never going to be a deal.

I threw myself sideways off the stool. The first bolt took the ledger's corner—a splinter of paper and binding spun past my ear—and the second buried itself in the table where my chest had been negotiating.

The brazier went over. Coals scattered across the floorboards, and the tollhouse, which had survived a hundred years of war by being beneath everyone's interest, began at last to burn.

"No!" Corvane lunged for the ledger where it had skidded under the table. I got there first—or rather, I got there second, but I got there with a boot, and I kicked the book deeper into the dark while his fingers closed on smoke and old wood.

Then Wren's shoulder hit me like a friendly landslide and we were both moving, through the smoke, toward the gap in the eastern wall where the roof had fallen in some earlier decade's catastrophe. Behind us someone shouted about the fire, and someone else shouted about the girl, and Odile—

Odile was running beside us now. I don't know when she'd started. She wasn't screaming. She was still humming, and the humming had gotten louder, or perhaps the world had gotten quieter around it.

A bolt cracked past my head and buried itself in the doorframe ahead of us. Another caught Wren's sleeve and tore it open along the seam.

"Aqueduct," she gasped. "South stair. Go."

* * *

*Wren*

Here's what nobody tells you about loyalty: it's mostly logistics.

People write songs about the dramatic bits—the standing in front of arrows, the dying beautifully—but the actual work of caring about somebody is unglamorous. It's knowing which streets flood at high tide. It's memorizing which guard drinks on Thursdays. It's carrying a child who isn't yours on your hip through a burning building because her legs are too short for the pace you need, and doing it without dropping her, because dropping her would be bad logistics.

So when the bolt came out of the smoke at the aqueduct stair—low, fast, aimed at the space between Xanthe's shoulder blades where a man carries everything he can't afford to lose—I didn't think. Thinking is for people with time.

I turned. That's all. The oldest maneuver in smuggling: put your body between the cargo and the loss.

The bolt hit me under the left collarbone, and I will be honest with you, it hurt considerably more than the songs suggest. Not the white flash of story-pain. A deep, hot, insulted pain, like being punched by someone you'd just paid.

I went down on one knee on the wet stone of the stair, and Xanthe made a sound I'd never heard a man make before—something between a word and a wound—and caught me before I folded.

"Wren. *Wren.*"

"It's fine," I said, which was a lie, and we both knew it, and I kept talking anyway because talking is also logistics; it keeps the panicking parts busy. "Through and through, probably. Probably. Get her down the stairs."

Above us, at the mouth of the stairwell, the hunters had stopped.

I saw why, and even bleeding into my own boot I felt the hair rise on my arms. They'd formed up in the archway—six of them, crossbows leveled, Corvane behind them with rain streaming off his gray face—and none of them fired. None of them could take the step.

Odile stood two stairs above me, small and soaked and lit from below by the burning tollhouse, and she was singing. Not humming now. Singing, full-voiced, a melody that seemed to come up through the stone itself, through the aqueduct's bones, through a hundred years of sealed doors. The air in the stairwell had weight. The air had *opinion.*

One hunter lowered his crossbow. Then another. Their faces weren't afraid exactly. They were *elsewhere*—the way men look when a song drags them somewhere they buried.

"Impossible," Corvane said. He said it quietly, to himself, the way a man reads a number that breaks his ledger. "The threshold was sealed. It was *paid* to stay sealed."

"Well," I managed, from my knee, blood in my teeth, "apparently somebody renegotiated."

Xanthe hauled my arm over his shoulders. He's not a big man—all those years of ledgers had built him out of ink and stubbornness—but he lifted me like I weighed nothing, or like weighing nothing was simply the price he'd decided to pay, and we went down the stairs together, the three of us, into the dark under Cinderport where the old water used to run.

Behind us, at the top of the stair, the hunters stood in the archway and did not follow. I heard one of them begin, very softly, to hum.

Corvane's voice came last, echoing down the stone, and it wasn't angry. That was the worst part. It sounded tired, and old, and almost kind:

"The crown will send worse than me, boy. Worse than Vail. Whatever you're walking toward—it's already been paid for."

"Then I'll dispute the charge," Xanthe called back, and his voice shook, and it carried anyway.

* * *

*Xanthe*

We went down until the firelight died and the hymn faded to a thread, and then I set Wren down on a dry ledge beside the dead channel and did the thing I was actually good at, the only thing I'd ever been good at: I assessed the damage and tallied it honestly.

Bolt entry below the left collarbone. No exit I could find. Bleeding steady but not arterial—Wren told me how to tell, months ago, on the salt road, in one of her lessons disguised as complaints. Her lips were gray. Her jokes, worryingly, had not slowed.

"If you say 'it's fine' one more time," I said, tearing my shirt into strips, "I'm leaving you here for the river-spirit. He gave fair rates."

"Now *that's* the guild training showing," she whispered. "Where's the girl?"

I looked up. Odile had knelt beside Wren without being asked, and she'd put both her small hands flat on the wound—not pressing, just resting, the way you'd steady a bird—and she was humming again, low, the same rising phrase. And I swear on every ledger I've ever balanced: the bleeding slowed. Not miracles. Not light from heaven. Just blood deciding, quietly, to wait.

Wren stared at her. Then at me. "Keeper," she said, "what exactly did we adopt?"

"An heirloom," I said, and bound the bandage as tight as she'd let me.

She caught my wrist with her good arm. Her grip was still strong. That was the best news of the night.

"You turned around," she said. "On the stair. You know that, right? Bolt was meant for you."

"I know."

"So don't you dare make it mean something tragic. I'm a smuggler. Getting shot is practically a certification." Her eyes were closing. "Just... get her to the gate. And Xanthe? When this is over, you owe me a page of that ledger. Page one. I want to know what my mother traded."

"You'll collect yourself," I said. "That's a promise, and unlike Corvane, I don't make promises I intend to sell later."

She smiled, half-asleep, blood-loss brave. "Terrible negotiator," she murmured. "Good man. Don't tell anyone I said so."

Odile curled against her uninjured side like she'd been assigned there, still humming, a lullaby now instead of a ward. And I sat in the dark under the city with the third ledger in my lap and my grandfather's stylus cold in my hand, and I listened to the two people who had no reason left in the world to stand between me and anything—and I understood, finally, what the prophecy book had been trying to tell me a hundred pages ago.

*The gate opens.* Of course it did. Gates don't open with keys of iron. They never had.

They open with people who turn around.

Somewhere far above, muffled by stone and rain, the free cities' bells began to ring—not alarm, not yet, but the restless pattern that meant the watchtowers had seen fires on the Marchland road. The Hollow King was coming. Vail would regroup. Corvane's warning sat in my chest like swallowed lead: *worse than me.*

I opened the ledger to its final page, the one Wren hadn't found yet, the one with my own name written in my father's hand and a debt recorded in a currency I was only beginning to understand. The gate was waiting below us. I could feel it now, faint as a pulse through the floor—an attention, a patience, a bill.

"Stay with her," I whispered to Odile.

She nodded without opening her eyes, humming.

And I went down the last flight alone, stylus in hand, to meet whatever had been paid for.
