## 17. What the Crown Remembers

I have not slept in eleven years.

This is not a boast. I want to be clear about that, because the histories—should anyone survive to write them—will likely record that the Hollow King did not sleep because he was too mighty, too terrible, too consumed with conquest to waste hours in unconsciousness. The truth is smaller and worse. The crown does not permit sleep. Sleep is a door, and doors are how things get out.

So instead I sit in the high tower of my palace—which was once someone else's palace, though I no longer remember whose, which is itself an answer—and I do not sleep, and the crown whispers to me in the dark like a creditor reading aloud from a very long ledger.

Tonight it is generous. Tonight it gives me memories.

You should understand how this works. The crown eats. That is its nature, as fire's nature is burning and water's nature is finding the lowest place. It ate the gatekeepers when we broke them a century ago—not their bodies only, but their *selves*, their years, their names, the small warm rooms of their lives. It keeps all of that. It digests slowly. And sometimes, when it wants something from me—or when it is bored, if a thing like that can be bored—it lets a little of what it has eaten seep back up into my skull, the way a flooded cellar seeps through floorboards.

It thinks this breaks me. It may be right.

* * *

The first memory arrives as a smell: woodsmoke and rosemary, bread baking somewhere out of sight. A kitchen. I am standing in a kitchen that is not mine, in a body that is not mine, and a woman is scolding me for tracking mud across her clean floor, and I am laughing, because in this memory I am eight years old and the mud was worth it, there was a frog involved, the frog is still cupped in my hands, damp and indignant.

The woman is my mother. No—the memory's mother. The keeper's mother. It doesn't matter. For the length of the memory she is *my* mother, and her hands are flour-white, and she calls me by a name I will never say aloud again because the crown listens even here, especially here.

Then the memory ends, the way they always end, mid-gesture, mid-breath, and I am back in the tower with my hands empty of frogs and full of nothing, and the crown hums a note that might be satisfaction.

That is the cruelty of it. Not the horror-memories—I barely feel those anymore; you cannot horrify a man who has done worse—but these. The small ones. The bread and the mud and the frog. The crown keeps the small ones freshest, the way a miser keeps his best coin polished.

* * *

There are nights—it is always night; I have made the night permanent over my Marchland camps partly for strategy and mostly so I never have to see the sun rise on what I am—when the flood comes all at once, and I drown.

Tonight is such a night.

It begins with rain. A memory of rain on a slate roof, and a man beneath it mending a net, humming. Then the man is teaching a child to write, guiding a small hand around the letters with an iron stylus, iron because the keepers wrote in iron or not at all, iron because iron remembers. Then the man is older, standing at a gate that I broke, singing a hymn with the last of his voice while my soldiers—

I will not describe what my soldiers did. I was there. I ordered it. The memory shows me both sides of it at once, which is the crown's favorite trick: it makes me the keeper and the killer in the same instant, so that I grieve and am grief, so that there is nowhere in my own skull to stand.

And then, in the middle of the drowning, a face.

Not a stranger's face. I know every keeper's face now; the crown made sure of that, filed them into me like knives into a rack. But this one surfaces and stays, and the crown goes quiet around it, almost careful, the way water goes still around a drowned thing.

An old man. Iron stylus behind his ear like a carpenter's pencil. Laugh-lines gone gray at the edges. He is looking at someone just out of frame, and his whole face is doing the thing faces do when they look at a grandchild—softened, ruined, proud past all sense.

Yarrow. The name comes up with him like a bubble from deep water. *Yarrow*, and under it, fainter, a child's voice saying the word wrong on purpose to make him laugh—*yarrow, marrow, sparrow*—and the old man laughing, really laughing, head back, and outside the window of that memory it is snowing and the world is still whole.

He was the last one who fought me with anything left. The others broke or fled or bargained. Yarrow stood at the threshold with his iron stylus raised like a sword, an old man against an army, and he sang the unbinding hymn alone, every verse, until the blood—

Until.

And the crown took him last of all, and took him slowest, and I remember—I have *his* memory of it, that is the filthiest part, I remember it from inside his skull—looking up at the man wearing the crown and seeing, for one moment before the dark came down, not a king. Just a man. Tired. Trapped. Almost sorry.

He pitied me. In the last second of his life, the gatekeeper Yarrow looked at the monster who had murdered everyone he loved, and his face did something complicated, and I have spent a hundred years telling myself it was hatred, because hatred would be easier to carry than pity.

It was pity.

* * *

Here is what no one understands about wearing the Hollow King's crown: I did not put it on because I wanted to rule. I put it on because I wanted to win—one war, one border, one long-ago insult avenged—and the crown said *yes, and more, and more, and more*, and each *more* cost a piece of me, and I kept paying because stopping felt impossible, the way stopping feels impossible to a man falling.

A century later there is very little of me left to pay with. I know this. Some mornings I catch myself giving orders in a voice that isn't quite mine, with cadences that belong to the crown, and I stand there afterward like a man who has found a stranger's hat in his house.

But tonight, with Yarrow's face floating in the dark behind my eyes, I find something the crown did not plan for.

I find that I am weeping.

I did not decide to. It simply happens, the way a wall happens to crack. One moment I am sitting in the dark being a monster, and the next my face is wet and my shoulders are shaking and the sound coming out of me is not kingly at all—it is small, and human, and ancient, the sound of a man grieving a hundred years of grief he was never permitted to spend.

I weep for the kitchen mother. I weep for the frog and the mud. I weep for the net-mender and the child learning letters, for every keeper whose name the crown ate and whose bread-smell it hoards like treasure. I weep for Yarrow most of all—for the snow outside his window, for the child who said *sparrow*, for the pity in his dying eyes that I have misread for a century.

And underneath all of it, quiet as a debt accruing, I weep for myself—for the man I was before the crown, whoever he was, whatever his name was. I cannot remember his face. The crown took that first, I think. It takes your own face before anything else, so that you will not recognize what you're becoming.

The crown stirs against my skull. It does not like this. Grief, I have learned, is the one thing it cannot digest properly—it sits in the crown like a stone, it slows the hunger, it makes the whispering go uncertain. A weeping king is a dangerous king. A weeping king might, in some unguarded moment, remember that crowns can be taken off.

Or broken.

The thought arrives whole, unbidden, terrifying, mine: *the hymn had verses I never let finish.*

I wipe my face with both hands, and when I lower them, the lucidity is already draining away, the crown pouring its cold certainty back into the spaces grief opened. By morning I will be the Hollow King again, seamless, hungry, giving orders in that borrowed voice. The weeping man will be folded away somewhere below the hunger, waiting, patient as a ledger.

But not yet. For one moment more I sit in the dark of my tower, an old man's stolen tears drying on a monster's face, and I hold the thought like a coal in cupped hands:

Somewhere, the keepers' blood is not all spilled. The crown knows it too—I felt it flinch tonight, when Yarrow's memory surfaced, felt it reach hungrily southward, toward the free cities, toward Cinderport, toward something young and unblooded that carries an iron stylus in its pocket and does not yet know what it is.

The crown wants the last Yarrow found before the hymn can be sung whole.

For the first time in a hundred years, I find that I want the opposite.

I do not know how much longer I can want anything. The crown is eating faster now; I can feel myself thinning at the edges, a man becoming a mouthpiece, a mouth becoming a door. But tonight—tonight I was lucid, and I wept, and I remembered pity.

If any part of me survives to see the gate open, I know what I will do.

I will stand aside.

* * *

Dawn is not permitted, so the torches are dimmed instead, and the crown resumes its whispering, and the Hollow King rises to review his armies marching south.

But somewhere far below the hunger, in the flooded cellar where the crown keeps what it has eaten, an old man with an iron stylus behind his ear turns his face toward the light of a memory, and waits.
