## 19. The Second Key

The monastery of Saint Vellum-and-Cold-Toes — I never learned its real name; the sign at the switchback read only *PRAY AND WIPE YOUR BOOTS* — clung to the mountain the way a barnacle clings to a hull: stubbornly, gracelessly, and with every indication of having been there first.

We had climbed for two days. Odile rode on my shoulders for the last mile because her legs were short and my guilt was long. Wren walked ahead with the easy stride of someone who considered altitude a personal insult she intended to win.

"You know what I like about monks," she said, not turning around. "They don't ask questions at the door."

"You know what I like about smugglers," I said. "Neither do they."

"That's because we're the questions, ledger-man. Everyone else is the answer."

Odile hummed against the top of my head. She'd been humming more since Grayfen — the gate-hymn, threading through her sleep and her waking hours both, sometimes swelling when we turned in directions it apparently approved of. It had led us up this mountain as surely as any map. Somewhere below us, behind us, in the direction of everything I'd lost, an army was gathering, and here we were following a seven-year-old's lullaby to a door full of men who had taken vows of silence, which struck me as the single most sensible religious commitment I had ever heard of.

The gate itself was unremarkable, which is to say it was exactly the kind of door you'd walk past a thousand times without seeing: oak gone silver with weather, iron hinges painted over so many times they'd become abstract art, a bell-pull worn smooth by centuries of hands that presumably all had better things to do. Carved into the lintel, almost erased by lichen, was a symbol I now knew too well — the same interlocking gate-mark that ran down the spine of the third ledger, that was branded into my grandfather's stylus, that had been carved into the flesh of every gatekeeper the crown had ever burned.

Wren looked at it. Looked at me. "Well," she said. "Either this is the place, or somebody in the twelfth century really liked geometry."

I pulled the bell.

* * *

The monk who answered was old in the way mountains are old — not decrepit, just patient, weathered into a shape that suggested he would outlast whatever you brought against him. He was small, bald as an egg, with hands like knotted rope and eyes the pale gray of slate. He looked at me for a long moment. Then at Odile. Then at the stylus in my belt, where I'd stopped bothering to hide it, because hiding things from people who live on mountains seemed rude.

"Brother Anselm," he said. His voice was dry and warm, like paper left near a fire. "You'll want the guest hall. Supper is barley. The barley is penance. We serve it at sundown."

"We're not here for the barley," Wren said.

"No one ever is." He stepped aside to let us in. "But you'll want to say your piece after you've eaten. Everything looks smaller on a full stomach. Even the end of the world."

I should have known then that he already understood why we'd come. Monks, I have since learned, are the guildmasters of the soul: they keep ledgers too, just of less interesting debts.

* * *

The refectory was stone and candle-smoke and forty men eating in silence, which should have been oppressive and instead felt like the quietest room I had ever stood in. After Cinderport's guildhall — after the whisper-networks and the watchers and Corvane's warm voice saying *you're family, boy* while he sold me by weight — silence among strangers felt like a luxury good.

Anselm sat with us afterward in a cell barely bigger than his bed, which was a plank, which was generous compared to some beds I've slept on. A single window framed a view of the valley floor, four thousand feet of nothing, and he positioned himself facing it as though the conversation were a formality between him and the drop.

"Say it, then," he said.

I said it. I told him about the third ledger, about the keys that were bloodlines and not objects, about Odile humming a hymn older than the free cities, about the gate beneath the aqueduct that had recognized my stylus and demanded more than I had. I told him about the Hollow King and the crown that ate its wearers, about the inquisitors and the tollhouse at Grayfen and the family slaughtered around one surviving child. I gave him the whole ugly inventory, because I am a ledger-keeper before anything else, and I have never once been able to resist a complete accounting.

He listened without moving. When I finished, he was quiet for the length of ten breaths. Then:

"The hymn," he said. "Sing me a line of it."

"I can't. I've never heard it. It's hers."

"Child," he said to Odile, gently, "will you sing for an old man?"

She climbed off my knee, stood in the middle of the cell with the absolute seriousness of a child who has been asked to perform, and sang. Four bars. That was all it took. Four bars of a melody I couldn't follow and didn't need to, because Brother Anselm's face did something I will remember until I die: it came apart. Not with grief. With relief. Like a man who has held a door shut for sixty years finally hearing footsteps on the other side.

He joined her. Quietly, under her voice, harmonizing — and the hymn doubled, filled the little stone cell, pressed against the walls the way water presses against a dam. The candle flames leaned toward them. My stylus went hot against my hip. And Odile turned to look at him with an expression of pure, furious delight, the way you look at someone who turns out to speak your secret language.

"There are forty-one men in this monastery," Anselm said, when the last note faded. "Forty of them think I'm the most boring man God ever made. I have spent fifty-three years being deliberately unremarkable. I wash feet. I copy out psalms in a hand I make uglier than it needs to be. I once spent an entire decade cultivating a stammer, because a man who stammers is never asked to lead anything." He smiled, and it was a young man's smile wearing an old man's face. "Do you know how difficult it is to sing badly on purpose? For half a century? Every festival, every funeral?"

"Why hide?" Wren asked. "If you knew what you were—"

"Because knowing what you are is how they find you." He said it without heat, the way you'd state the price of bread. "I was born Anselm of the Tollhouse line. My mother put me on a mule when I was six years old and sent me up this mountain with a note pinned to my coat that said *he likes cold weather*. That was her entire farewell. She was right to make it brief. The inquisitors reached our village eleven days later." He folded his rope-knuckled hands. "I have been the second key for sixty years, Master Yarrow, and I have kept that key the only way a key can be kept: by never opening anything. Including my mouth."

"And now?"

"Now a child sings my grandmother's hymn in my cell, and a man sits in front of me wearing a gatekeeper's stylus like it's a kitchen knife, and you tell me the crown is marching on the free cities." He shrugged, a small economical motion. "Now the hiding is finished. Hiding was only ever useful while there was something left to hide *for*."

* * *

I want to be honest about what I expected, because honesty is the one discipline I never abandoned, even when it would have been more comfortable to.

I expected reluctance. I expected bargaining — I'm a guild man; bargaining is my native tongue, and I had come prepared with offers, contingencies, three separate speeches about duty and legacy that I'd rehearsed on the switchbacks. I expected to have to talk an old man out of his life.

Instead he asked practical questions. How many inquisitors. Which passes were watched. Whether Odile could ride. Whether the gate-stair could hold against fire, since fire was Vail's preferred instrument. He asked them in the flat tone of a man planning a harvest, and when I answered, he nodded, and then he said:

"I'll need tonight to put my affairs in order. There's a psalter I've been copying for thirty years that no one else can finish — my hand is distinctive, worse luck. And there's Brother Cadel, who thinks I owe him money, and I'd rather he thought that forever. Grief makes men curious. Curiosity makes them follow." He looked at me. "You understand that I'm coming to die."

The words landed in the silent cell like coins dropped down a well.

"Not necessarily—" I started.

"Master Yarrow." He said my name the way Corvane used to, warmly, familiarly — and for one horrible second I flinched, and he saw it, and his expression softened with something that wasn't pity, exactly. Pity would have been easier. "Every key the crown has taken, it has taken by killing them or caging them. You know this. The girl survived Grayfen by accident and silence. I have neither accident nor silence left in me. I am seventy-eight years old. My heart is a bad tenant. If we walk out of these mountains, the odds are excellent that I do not walk back into them." He smiled again. "I have made my peace with God, with my brothers, and with the specific quality of the barley here. What I have *not* made peace with is dying in this bed while the world outside ends. Do not take that from me to make yourself comfortable."

I didn't have an answer. Wren did, because Wren always does.

"Then we leave at first light," she said, "and I'll want to know every smuggling route through these peaks, because if anyone's spent sixty years mapping ways in and out of a mountain without being seen, it's the man pretending to stammer."

Anselm laughed — a real laugh, rusty from disuse, startling a bird off the windowsill. "Sister, I have been sneaking down to the village for plum brandy since before your mother was born. Yes. I know the routes."

* * *

He let us sleep in the guest cells, and I lay on a straw pallet listening to wind move through the mountain like breath through a flute, and I did not sleep, because my mind had decided this was the night to run its accounts.

That's what grief is, I've come to believe. Not sadness. Accounting. You lie in the dark and the ledger opens itself and the entries scroll past: *one guildhall, lost. One name, forfeit. One life, exchanged for another without my signature.* And now: *one old man, spending himself like the last coin in a purse, and calling it fair trade.*

I got up. I don't know why. Monasteries at night are made entirely of corridors, and I followed one of them on instinct and found Anselm in the chapel, kneeling, not praying so much as sitting with God the way two old friends sit together when they've run out of things to say.

"You should be sleeping," he said, without turning. "So should I. Neither of us is going to."

"Can I ask you something? As one keeper to another."

"You may ask. Whether I answer depends on the question."

"When the gate takes its payment — when this is done — what happens to the ones who opened it? The keys. Does anyone know?"

He was quiet a moment. "My grandmother's letters survive. In code, in the infirmary library, filed under veterinary remedies, because no one has needed a horse remedy in a century." He rose, joints cracking like green wood, and faced me. "She wrote that the gate keeps what it keeps and gives back nothing. That the rite spends the singer. That her own mother sang the binding and was never heard from again except in the hymn itself." He touched my shoulder with a hand light as ash. "You carry a debt already, boy. I can see it in how you count things. So hear me: when the bill comes, pay it standing up. That's all any of us can do. That's all any of us ever could."

* * *

We left at first light, as promised. Forty monks stood in the courtyard to see us off, and thirty-nine of them believed they were watching Brother Anselm escort pilgrims to the valley shrine, a chore he'd performed a hundred times. The fortieth was the abbot, who shook Anselm's hand and held it a beat too long and said, "Bring back the brandy money," in a voice that cracked on *money*, and Anselm said, "Of course, Father," in a voice that lied beautifully, and I understood that everyone in this story had been keeping secrets for everyone else, all along, the way stones keep a wall standing: invisibly, and in common.

Odile took Anselm's hand as we passed through the gate. She looked up at him and hummed the first bar of the hymn, an offering, a greeting, a child's way of saying *I know what you are.*

He hummed the second bar back.

And far to the south, past the valley, past the Marchlands, past everything between us and the sea — I couldn't see it, but I could feel it the way you feel weather in an old wound — the crown was moving. Somewhere on those roads, an army marched behind a king who was mostly hunger now, and somewhere beneath a palace a true king dreamed of an iron stylus, and the hymn we carried down the mountain in three uneven voices was the only thing in the world that hadn't yet been bought, burned, or broken.

Four voices, I corrected myself, as Odile's thread wove into ours.

We had the second key. We walked toward the third.

And behind us, on the highest tower of the monastery, a bell began to ring — though no one, as far as I could see, had pulled the rope.
