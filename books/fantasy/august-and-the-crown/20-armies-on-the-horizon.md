## 20. Armies on the Horizon

They moved me on a Tuesday.

I know this because the man who unlocked my chains told me so, cheerfully, the way you'd tell a prisoner the weather. "Tuesday, Your Majesty. Big day for you." He had flour on his sleeves, which struck me as an odd detail for a gaoler in the usurper's palace, until I understood that everyone in that place was somebody else once—a baker, a clerk, a father—and the crown ate them all the same.

A century in a cell teaches you to count things. Days, mostly, though I gave that up around year forty when I realized I'd miscounted somewhere and could never find my place again. So I counted other things: the cracks in the ceiling (two hundred and eleven, if you count the hairline one behind the drainpipe, and I do, because it's mine). The words I still remembered of the coronation hymn (all of them; they don't let you forget the important ones). The number of times I had decided to die and then decided against it (I stopped counting that one too, out of shame).

But Tuesday. That I knew, because Tuesdays were when they fed me first, before the dogs, a small mercy from some kitchen hand who either pitied me or believed I was still a king. After a hundred years, pity and belief look identical from inside a chain.

"Where are we going?" I asked. My voice came out like something dragged over gravel. I hadn't used it in weeks. There was no one worth using it on.

The gaoler with the flour on his sleeves grinned. "You're going home, sire."

Home. As if a cell were ever home. As if the palace above me—the bright, hollow thing with its banners and its throne that wasn't mine—were anything but the largest cage in the world with the door painted on.

* * *

Here is what they don't tell you about being kept alive as an anchor: you become very good at listening through stone.

For a hundred years I have lain beneath the usurper's palace and listened. I heard the crown arrive—I heard it *scream*, actually, the night they brought it down into the treasury vaults, though no one else seemed to notice. Crowns scream. That's the first thing they never put in the songs. A crown is a hungry thing wearing the shape of jewelry, and hunger makes noise.

I heard the gatekeepers come, a century ago, all iron styluses and low voices, singing the hymn in parts like masons laying stones. I heard them fail. Not fail-fail—they bound him, they shut the Hollow King's power behind the gate—but they failed to finish, because finishing meant shattering the crown, and shattering the crown meant someone had to hold it while it broke, and holding it while it broke means dying, or worse: becoming it.

So they left it half-done. They left him wearing it. They left me chained to it, my bloodline a leash on a wolf, and they went home to their ledgers and their ordinary lives, and I lay in the dark and listened to a man be eaten slowly by his own ambition for a hundred years.

You develop opinions, in that situation. Most of them are not forgiving.

* * *

They dressed me in the morning. Actual dressers, four of them, hands shaking. Nobody had touched me gently in decades and I flinched at the comb like a horse at a whip, and the youngest dresser started crying, quietly, into my collar, and I found myself comforting *him*. "There now," I said. "It's only a shirt." My voice cracked on *only*. Nothing had been only anything for a century.

They had kept the clothes. Of course they had. The coronation regalia of King Aldric the Third, preserved in cedar chests by order of the usurper himself, because a prop must be maintained. The white tunic. The mantle worked with the nine towers of the old kingdom. They did not bring me a crown, which I noted with something adjacent to gratitude. I have heard what crowns do. I have listened to one eat a man for a hundred years through six feet of granite. If they had set a crown on my head that morning, I would have broken my own neck against the wall rather than wear it, and I want that recorded somewhere, by someone, in case the histories get creative.

"You look very well, sire," said the eldest dresser, who was lying.

"I look like a corpse they've stood up," I said, and he had the grace to nod.

That's the thing about a century in the dark: your body doesn't age properly, because the crown needs its anchor alive, but it doesn't *live* properly either. I am ninety-one years old and I have the face of a drowned man fished out at forty. My eyes are the worst part. Everyone says so. They've gone pale as river ice, from disuse, from looking at nothing. Children will cry when they see me. I know this in advance. I am going to be displayed to children.

* * *

They marched me up out of the dark on a litter, because my legs had forgotten their profession, and the light hit me like a slap. I wept. I'm not ashamed of it—or I am, but I've been ashamed of so many things for so long that one more barely registers. The sun. Do you know what the sun does after a hundred years? It doesn't warm you. It *accuses* you. All that light, all those days, spent without you, and here it is anyway, indifferent and enormous, and you realize the world never paused for your absence even once.

Then the banners came into focus, and the sun became the least of my problems.

They had me on a platform at the head of the vanguard. Ten thousand men in the usurper's colors, and above them, hoisted on poles so the whole column could see, a standard bearing my face—or a painter's idea of my face, younger, nobler, with both eyes the same color. Beneath it, stitched in letters tall enough to read from a mile off:

**ALDRIC LIVES. THE TRUE KING MARCHES TO RECLAIM HIS THRONE.**

"Well," I said to the captain standing nearest my litter. "That's a lie with excellent stitching."

He didn't smile. They're not permitted to smile at me, I think. Or perhaps the crown has eaten that out of them too, the way it eats everything, gradually, starting with humor and ending with names.

* * *

Let me explain the arithmetic of what they've done to me, because I have had a great deal of time to think about arithmetic, and this particular sum is obscene.

The Hollow King cannot march openly. His power is bound—leashed to my blood, anchored in my chained body, and the binding holds as long as I live and stay put. But a war needs legitimacy. Armies are made of farmers, and farmers need to believe they are not murderers. So the usurper has solved two problems with one procession: he marches me at the front, alive and visible, "rescued" from my captivity—which was *his captivity*, which he built, which I have occupied since before his grandfather learned to shave—and every farm boy who sees the true king riding at the head of the host believes he is marching toward justice instead of away from it.

Helplessness, weaponized. That's what I am. Not a king, not a hostage even—hostages can be ransomed, negotiated over, mourned. I am a *flag*. And flags do not get to say no.

Or do they?

I have been thinking about that. A flag has one power, and it is the power of what it stands for. They march me under the banner of the true restoration. Every soldier in that column believes, down in the marrow where belief keeps its house, that following me is the right thing. The usurper has spent a hundred years building an army on the promise of my return.

He has made ten thousand people loyal to a man he keeps in a hole.

There is a version of this where that becomes a weapon pointed back at him. I don't know how yet. I am ninety-one years old and I have the legs of a newborn foal and the political capital of a museum exhibit. But I lie on my litter at night, under open sky for the first time in a century, and I watch the stars wheel, and I think: *he gave me an army's love and forgot to ask me whether I'd spend it.*

Tuesday, they said. Big day. It was a big day. Just not the one they planned.

* * *

*Interlude: the Council of Cinderport*

"They're through the Marchlands," said General Hesk, and dropped a map on the council table hard enough to make the candle flames duck. "Vanguard crossed the Sorrel yesterday. Ten thousand, maybe twelve. Main host three weeks behind."

Cinderport's council chamber smelled of salt, tallow, and panic—that last one you can't see but everyone can smell. Eleven seats, eleven faces, and I'll tell you what I saw in them, because I was there, standing at the back where the servants stand, because Wren Yarrow-smuggler-extraordinaire does not get a chair:

Fear, mostly. Fear wearing six different costumes.

"We surrender," said Magistrate Corvel, whose family had surrendered to three separate invasions and prospered under each. "We open the gates, we kneel, we pay. Cities survive. Kings pass."

"The last city that knelt to him," said Hesk, "is Grayfen. You've seen the tollhouse reports. You've seen what his inquisitors leave behind."

"That was resistance," Corvel said. "Grayfen resisted."

"Grayfen *asked questions*."

And then, because apparently I have lost whatever sense kept me alive in a cell for a hundred years, I spoke. From the back. Loudly.

"You're arguing about walls versus gates, and you're both wrong."

Eleven heads turned. One of them belonged to Xanthe Yarrow, ledger-keeper turned fugitive turned—whatever he was that week—with a child asleep against his shoulder and a monk beside him and the expression of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like. The child, Odile, hummed in her sleep. I felt it in my teeth. More on that later, maybe. Maybe much later. Maybe never, if the world is kind, which it isn't.

"The walls won't hold," I said. "I've listened to that crown for a hundred years. It doesn't break sieges. It breaks the *people inside* the siege. It eats fear. Your fear is its supply line. Wall yourselves up and you're packing its lunch."

"And the gate?" said Corvel, dripping contempt. "A fairy story and a fisherman's child?"

"The gate is real," said the monk, Anselm, mildly, in the voice of a man who has prayed past the point where shouting seems useful. "I am one-third of it."

Silence. The good kind, for once. The kind where people stop talking because they've run out of certainty.

Wren leaned over to me. "Nice speech," she whispered. "Very inspiring. 'Your fear is its lunch.' Really rallying stuff."

"I was in a cell for a century," I whispered back. "Rhetoric is rusty."

* * *

*Interlude: the road south of the Marchlands*

The refugee columns move at the speed of the slowest grandmother, which is to say: slower than hope, faster than despair, and exactly the speed of love, because nobody leaves anybody behind, which is the whole problem and the whole point.

I walked among them for a mile once, hood up, counting again—it's a habit; you count things or the things count you. Carts. Goats. A woman carrying a loom, of all things, forty miles of walking with a *loom* on her back, because she said, when I asked, that a person can rebuild a house but not her weaving, and I understood her completely. We are all carrying our looms. Mine is a hymn I remember and a name they stole and a hundred years of listening through stone.

A boy marched beside me for a while pretending to guard me—he'd appointed himself, the way boys do—and he asked if I'd seen the true king, the one on the banners, and whether he was really coming to save everyone.

"He's closer than you think," I said.

"Do you think he's a good king?"

I thought about it honestly, because he deserved honesty. "I think," I said, "that whoever he is, he's been having a very bad century, and he'd like to go home."

The boy nodded gravely, satisfied, and ran off to guard someone else, and I stood in the middle of the road with dust in my throat and thought: *they're marching me toward these people.* Toward the boy and the grandmother and the woman with her loom. My face on the banner, my name on the lie, my chained blood pulling the leashed wolf straight at everything soft in the world.

Helplessness weaponized. Yes. But here's what the weapon-makers always forget about the helpless: we have nothing left to lose, and that is not weakness. That is *ammunition*.

* * *

On the ninth night of the march, the crown noticed me noticing.

I don't know how else to describe it. We were camped on the Sorrel flats, and I lay on my litter looking at stars I'd missed like a man misses water, and suddenly the night went cold in a way that had nothing to do with weather. Something vast turned its attention toward me from miles ahead—the way you feel a stare on the back of your neck, except the neck was my whole bloodline.

*You are awake,* it said. Not in words. In the way a debt is said. In the way a bill is presented.

*I have always been awake,* I answered, in the only language I had left, which was defiance with bad grammar. *That was rather the point of me.*

It laughed. Crowns should not laugh. This one did, and underneath the laughter I heard something else, faint, far away, impossible: a hymn. Sung in parts. Coming from the south. From Cinderport, or beneath it. A child's voice holding the high line, thin and clear as wire.

The gate was waking up. And the crown was afraid.

I smiled into the dark, a drowned man's smile, ninety-one years wide, and began, silently, in the privacy of my own ruined skull, to sing along.

They think they are marching me to legitimize a war.

Let them march. Every step brings the flag closer to the fire.
