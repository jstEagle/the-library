## 21. The Price Already Paid

Wren was a terrible patient, which I respected, and an even worse liar about it, which I didn't.

"You look like a woman who lost an argument with a crossbow," I told her on the third morning after the tollhouse road, when I found her sitting up in the safehouse cot with her arm strapped to her chest and a stolen apple in her good hand.

"I won," she said. "The bolt's out of me, isn't it? That makes me the winner."

"The bolt is out of you because Odile pulled it out while you were unconscious."

"Then Odile won, and I'm merely undefeated." She took a bite of the apple, which required her to hold it against her knee like a bear working a salmon, and gestured at me with her chin. "You've got your ledger face on."

"I don't have a ledger face."

"You have four faces, Yarrow. Ledger face, frightened ledger face, ledger face pretending not to be frightened, and whatever happened to you when Anselm started talking about the rite. This is the third one." She patted the edge of the cot. "Sit down. If you're going to brood, do it where I can watch. It's better than cards."

I sat. The safehouse was one of Wren's—a cellar beneath a chandler's shop in Grayfen that smelled of tallow and river damp, with two cots, a stove that smoked when it sulked, and a door hidden behind a rack of candles nobody had ever sold. Odile was asleep in the loft above, or pretending to be; sometimes I heard her humming through the floorboards, that thin thread of gate-hymn that followed her into sleep like a cat following a familiar lap.

Anselm was at prayer, or asleep, or both—the old monk could do them simultaneously, which he claimed was the whole point of forty years of practice.

And the third ledger lay open on the crate between us, because Wren had been reading it.

That should have been my first warning. Wren did not read. Wren *skimmed*, the way a smuggler skims a coastline—looking for the rocks, the shallows, the places where cargo could be moved quietly from one world into another. She read ledgers the way I'd once read them: for numbers, for names, for leverage.

She'd been at it for three days, whenever the pain let her sit up.

"You're on the last pages," I said.

"Mm." She turned the leaf with her bandaged hand, awkwardly, using the heel of her palm. "Your handwriting has improved since you were nine."

"That's not my handwriting."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't." And there was something in her voice then—not gentle, exactly. Wren wasn't gentle. But careful, the way you're careful walking a deck in weather. "Xanthe. How much of this book have you actually read?"

"All of it. Twice." I'd carried it out of the guildhall the night I fled Cinderport, tucked under my coat like a stolen ham. Every entry. Every payment. A century of sums paid by the Gilded Threshold to keep a gate *sealed and starving*, signed in a hand I recognized as my grandfather's, then my father's, then—after a gap of nineteen years—no one's at all. "Every line. Why?"

"Because you read it like a ledger-keeper." She held the book out to me, open to the final page. "Read it like a son."

* * *

Here is what I knew, before that night. Let me lay it out honestly, because honesty is the only currency I have left that doesn't clink.

I knew my parents died when I was six. Fever, the guild said. Winter fever, quick and unkind, and the Gilded Threshold took in their orphan boy because the Threshold took care of its own—or so Corvane told me, every year, on the anniversary, with a hand on my shoulder that weighed exactly as much as a debt.

I knew my family name appeared in the third ledger more than any other name. Yarrow, Yarrow, Yarrow, generation after generation, receiving payments marked *for services rendered to the Threshold*—services never specified, because the third ledger's services were never specified, because the third ledger existed to record what the first two were built to hide.

I knew my grandfather's iron stylus had been left to me in a box with no note, and that I'd used it for twenty years to enter figures in ordinary books without ever wondering why a poor guild clerk's family owned something that old, that heavy, that *wrong*—iron, when every other instrument in the guildhall was silver-gilt, because silver couldn't touch the third ledger's pages without burning them.

I knew all of that. I had known it the way you know the shape of your own ceiling: completely, and without ever once looking at it.

Wren put the open book in my hands and said, "Last page. Bottom entry. Read the date first."

The date was thirty years old. Two years before I was born.

I read the entry.

*Received of T. & M. Yarrow, in consideration of services yet rendered: one (1) favor, redeemable by the Gate or its appointed agents, terms unspecified, collection deferred until such time as the Gate requires. Witnessed under seal. Non-negotiable.*

Below it, in my grandfather's cramped, iron-stylus hand, a single line of commentary—the only commentary in a hundred pages of commentary-free accounting:

*They are buying the child's life. God forgive us, they are buying the child's life.*

* * *

The stove smoked. Somewhere above us, the chandler's dog turned over in its sleep. Odile's humming drifted down through the boards, three notes of a hymn older than the free cities, and I sat very still with a hundred-year-old ledger in my lap and felt the floor of my life tilt underneath me like a deck in weather.

"Favor," I said. My voice came out steady, which surprised me. Ledger-keepers learn to keep the voice level even when the columns don't balance. "Terms unspecified. Collection deferred."

"Yes."

"'Until such time as the Gate requires.'"

"Yes."

"Wren, this is a promissory note. This is—" I laughed, and hated the sound of it. "This is the worst contract I've ever seen. No amount. No date. No collateral clause. No exit. Any clerk who wrote this would be flogged out of the guildhall."

"It's not a contract," Wren said. "Contracts are between equals. This is a *toll*. Your parents stood at a gate with a dying child and the gate named its price, and they paid it, and the price was—" she waved her bandaged hand in a small circle that encompassed me, the cot, the last thirty years, "—all of this. On loan."

"They bought my survival."

"They bought your survival *from the gate*. Which means—" and here she did the thing Wren does, the thing that made her the best smuggler on the salt roads, which is say the terrible thing plainly, without anesthesia, because anesthesia costs extra and she's cheap, "—the gate owns the note on your life. And the crown wants the gate. So tell me, ledger-keeper. When the crown collects the gate... whose name is on the debt?"

I looked down at the page. My parents' initials. T. & M. Yarrow. Tomas and Mara. I had spent thirty years unable to remember my mother's face clearly and here were her initials in faded ink, attached to the purchase of my breathing.

"There's more," Wren said, softer now. "Look at the entries just before it. Go back a month."

I went back a month. The entries were small, domestic, almost heartbreaking in their ordinariness. Payments to a midwife. Payment to a carter. Payment—my throat tightened—to a woman in Cinderport's low quarter listed only as *for the keeping*, a sum large enough to buy a house, paid monthly, for six months, and then stopping.

"A wet nurse?" I said.

"A keeper. Someone paid to hold a child that wasn't hers." Wren shifted against her pillows, wincing. "You didn't grow up in the guildhall from birth, Xanthe. You were *placed* there. Someone chose the Gilded Threshold for you. Someone chose Corvane."

"My parents."

"Or the gate did. Or your grandfather did, following instructions neither of us wants to imagine." She let that sit. "Either way, you weren't orphaned into the guild. You were *delivered*. Like cargo. Like a small impossibility." Her mouth twisted. "My kind of shipment."

"And the fee," I said, because if I didn't keep making it arithmetic I was going to come apart right there on the crate, "was deferred. Deferred payment. The oldest trick in the book. You let the debtor walk away feeling saved, and you collect later, when they've built a life on top of the loan."

"Now you're thinking like a smuggler."

"I'm thinking like a mark." I closed the ledger. My hands were not entirely steady. "Thirty years. Thirty years I thought I was invisible. Unremarkable. Safe because I was small. And the whole time I was—what, *escrow*? A deposit held against future collection?"

"Probably why you survived the fever season that killed them," Wren said. "Paid-for goods don't spoil."

That was the sentence that broke something. Not loudly. Quietly, the way a keel breaks—somewhere below the waterline, where no one can see it, where the ship just starts taking water and listing by degrees until one morning the crew notices the horizon is at the wrong angle and has been for some time.

I had built my entire self on being overlooked. It was the one thing I'd been proud of. In a city of guilds and crowns and inquisitors, I had made myself a man no one bothered to burn—and I had called that safety, I had called it wisdom, I had lain awake in my narrow guild bed night after night *grateful* for my own insignificance.

And it had never been mine. It was purchased. My invisibility was a feature of the transaction, not a talent. The gate had needed its heir kept alive and unnoticed until collection day, and so the universe had arranged for me to be exactly what I was: a small, careful, forgettable man with ink on his fingers and a dead family and a stylus he never questioned.

Even my pride was a line item.

"Say something," Wren said, "before you do that thing where you go somewhere behind your eyes and start doing sums."

"I'm always doing sums."

"I know. It's how you pray." She waited. I didn't answer fast enough, so she answered for me, which was also how Wren prayed. "You're thinking: if my life was bought, then everything I've done with it belongs to someone else. Every year. Every choice. Even running—even us." She tapped the ledger with one finger. "And you're wrong, so stop it."

"How can I be wrong? It's written down. In my family's hand. Witnessed under seal. I'm the most documented debt in the Marchlands."

"Because a debt describes what you owe, not what you are." She said it flatly, but her eyes stayed on mine, and Wren's eyes never stayed anywhere they didn't mean to. "I've run cargoes my whole life. Do you know what a bill of lading says? It says what's in the box. It doesn't say what the box is *for*. Somebody could ship a sword in a crate marked 'spoons.' The paper doesn't get a vote." She leaned back. "Your parents signed a piece of paper. Terrible paper. Worst contract in the Marchlands, like you said. But they signed it to keep a six-year-old breathing, and everything that six-year-old did afterward—every figure you entered, every friend you made, every time you chose to be decent when decency cost you—that was yours. Nobody bought that. Nobody could."

I wanted to believe her. I want to be honest about that too: I wanted it so badly my teeth ached. Because the alternative was that I had never once acted freely in my life, that my flight from Cinderport, my bargain with Wren, my growing terror-tenderness toward a humming child—all of it was just a purchased parcel traveling toward its delivery address.

But wanting isn't evidence. I'm a ledger-keeper. Wanting has never once balanced a column.

"There's a worse question," I said.

"Of course there is. There's always a worse question. Ask it."

"If the gate holds the note on my life—and the crown is marching to take the gate—" I turned the ledger over in my hands, feeling its weight, its century of patient ink. "Then the crown doesn't just want the gate sealed. It wants to *collect*. Vail isn't hunting me because I'm a loose end. She's hunting me because I'm an asset. The last key, the last heir, and the last outstanding debt, all wearing one coat."

"And when the Hollow King takes the gate," Wren said, "he doesn't break the seal. He assumes the debt. He becomes the collector." She smiled, and it was not a nice smile; it was the smile she wore boarding other people's ships. "Which means, Yarrow, that every step you take toward that gate, you're walking toward the counter where your bill comes due. They don't want you dead. Dead men can't pay."

"Odile," I said suddenly. "If the crown learns what she is—if they think a second key—"

"Then we make sure they don't learn." Wren said it simply, the way she said *we sail on the morning tide*. "Old monk upstairs, wounded smuggler, humming child, and one very documented debtor. Against an empire." She considered. "I've run worse odds. Barely. Once. And I was paid better."

Above us, the floorboards creaked. Anselm's footsteps, slow and deliberate, crossing toward the stair—and then stopping, and then his voice, quiet and terribly calm, drifting down through the boards:

"Xanthe. Come up. There are riders on the Grayfen road, and they are not smugglers. They fly the crown's gray-and-gold."

Wren was already moving, bad arm and all, swinging her legs off the cot with a hiss of pain and reaching for her boots.

I put the ledger inside my coat, over my heart, where a debt belongs.

So much for grief. Grief, it turns out, is a luxury collected only from people who aren't currently being collected from.

I blew out the stove, and we ran.
