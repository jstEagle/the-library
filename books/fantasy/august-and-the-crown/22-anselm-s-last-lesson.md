## 22. Anselm's Last Lesson

Brother Anselm taught me to read the gate-hymn the way my old guildmaster taught me to read a ledger: slowly, twice, and with the assumption that everything important was hidden in the margins.

We sat in the monastery scriptorium on the third morning, in a room that smelled of tallow and cold stone and a hundred years of careful silence. Snow ticked against the shutters. Somewhere below us, Odile was singing to the kitchen brothers, who had already decided she was a blessing sent by some saint or other and were feeding her like one. Wren was in the stables doing something to a mule that I chose not to witness. And I was holding a page of music that was older than every guild charter I'd ever filed.

"It's not a song," Anselm said. "That's the first mistake everyone makes. The gate doesn't have ears."

"Then what does it have?"

"Debts." He smiled, and there was something in the smile that made me think of Corvane at his most tired — the look of a man who has counted too many accounts for too long. "It is a ledger, Xanthe. The oldest one. Everything that passes through it is written down, and everything written down must be paid for. The hymn isn't music. It's an entry. You are writing yourself into the record when you sing it, and the gate writes back."

I looked down at the notation. It wasn't any system I knew — not the round notes of chapel choirs, not the merchant cipher-marks we used for auction calls. The symbols were angular, deliberate, each one built out of strokes that reminded me uncomfortably of my grandfather's iron stylus.

"These are ledger marks," I said.

"Now you see why your family kept it." He turned the page with hands that trembled slightly, though whether from age or something else I couldn't tell. "The gatekeepers didn't guard the door with swords. They guarded it with bookkeeping. Every verse binds a promise. Every refrain records a payment. When your ancestors sang the Hollow King behind the gate a century ago, they weren't casting a spell. They were entering him into a contract he could never default on."

"And the unbinding?"

Anselm was quiet for a moment. Outside, the wind moved through the pines with a sound like pages turning.

"The unbinding is a cancellation," he said finally. "You must enter into the record that the contract is void. But nothing is ever simply voided, boy. Every cancellation carries a fee."

* * *

He made me copy the hymn out three times before he would let me sing a note of it. "Your hand learns what your mouth forgets," he said, which sounded like monk-wisdom until I realized it was exactly what Corvane used to say about figures — *your hand remembers what your head rounds off.* I wondered, not for the first time, how much of my life had been training for this without my knowledge. How many nights hunched over trade ledgers had been, in some sense, rehearsal.

The copying took all morning. My wrist ached. The third copy came out cleaner than the first two, and Anselm studied it for a long time before nodding once, which I gathered was the monastic equivalent of a standing ovation.

"Now," he said. "Sing it. Quietly. Just the first verse."

I sang. My voice cracked on the second phrase because the hymn sits in an ugly part of the register, all low gravel and no glory, and because the moment the words left my mouth the room changed.

I don't know how else to describe it. The candle flames leaned toward me. The snow against the shutters stopped mid-tick, as if listening. And beneath my feet — far beneath, deeper than the mountain's roots should allow — I felt something turn over in its sleep. A vast attention, patient and enormous and utterly indifferent to me as a person, the way a bank is indifferent to the people whose money it holds.

I stopped singing. My heart was going like a smuggler's oars.

"There it is," Anselm said softly. "It heard you."

"What *is* it?"

"The gate. Or the thing the gate keeps. Or the thing the gate is — even Brother Aldric's order never settled that question, and they had a century to argue about it." He poured me a cup of watered wine with steady hands, which was more than mine were doing. "What matters is this: the gate is a lock, a ledger, and a law, all in one. And you just introduced yourself to it. It will remember your voice now. That cannot be undone."

"Is that bad?"

"That depends entirely on what you do next." He sat back. "Which brings us to the lesson I have been avoiding for two days, and which I will now avoid no longer, because I am old and cowardice is undignified at my age."

* * *

He told it plainly, the way he seemed to do everything — no drama, no flourishes, just the facts laid out like entries in a column.

"The crown can be shattered," he said. "Not broken by force — armies have tried, hammers have tried, treachery has tried. Iron does not touch it. Fire does not touch it. Time only feeds it. There is exactly one thing in creation that can unmake it, and that is the gate itself, acting through its keepers. Inside the threshold, where the gate's authority is absolute, the crown is not a relic. It is a debtor. And a debtor can be called to account."

"So we get the Hollow King inside the gate—"

"If only it were so tidy." Anselm shook his head. "The crown will come to the gate on its own, boy. It has been crawling toward it for a hundred years. It *needs* the gate — the binding is the only thing keeping its wearer alive past his natural span, and the only thing keeping the crown from consuming him outright. When the gate opens, the crown comes. Like water finding a crack. You will not need to lure it. You will need to survive long enough to sing the cancellation while it fights you."

"Fights me how?"

"With everything it has eaten." His eyes held mine, and I saw that he was not going to spare me, and I loved him a little for it and hated him a little too. "A century of gatekeepers' memories, boy. Your ancestors. Their faces, their voices, their dying moments. It will offer them back to you. It will put your mother's voice in its mouth if it thinks that will make you stop singing. Do you understand? The rite does not fail because singers grow tired. It fails because singers grow *grieving*, and grief makes them listen."

I thought of my parents, whom I did not remember. Whose faces I had reconstructed over the years from other people's half-sentences, the way you rebuild a burned house from a neighbor's description. I thought about hearing my mother's voice coming out of a thing wearing a crown.

"I'll keep singing," I said.

"You believe that now." Anselm said it without cruelty. "Belief is cheap at this distance. But yes. We proceed on the assumption."

"And the fee?" I asked, because I was a ledger-keeper, and I had learned that the fee always comes, and better to hear it named than to find it on the invoice later. "You said every cancellation carries one."

Anselm rose. He crossed to the shuttered window and stood looking at the snow for a while, and when he spoke again his voice had changed — quieter, and heavier, the voice of a man reading aloud a figure he'd known was coming for years.

"When the gate opened last — truly opened, a century ago — your ancestors stood in the threshold and bound the Hollow King. And the gate protected them for it. That is the covenant, Xanthe. The keepers serve the gate, and the gate keeps them. It shields their bloodline. It hides them. It buys them time, health, luck — small impossibilities, if you like. Why do you think a low-born orphan survived a plague year that emptied half of Cinderport's dockside? Why do you think inquisitors walked past you for twenty-six years without seeing what you are?" He turned. "The gate has been paying your way your whole life. You've been living on credit you never signed for."

The room felt colder. "And if I shatter the crown—"

"You will do it from inside the threshold, as a keeper, using the gate's own authority. And the moment the crown breaks, the covenant ends. Not gradually. Not gently. All at once." He came back to the table and sat, and took both my hands in his dry old ones, and said the rest looking straight at me. "The gate's protection withdraws from you forever. Whatever it has been holding back — the sicknesses, the hungers, the attention of every sharp-eyed servant of the crown — all of it arrives. And more than that: you will be deaf to it. The hymn will leave you. You will stand in the gate chamber and hear nothing but ordinary air, for the rest of your ordinary life. No keeper hears the gate after he breaks the crown. That is the price. That is the whole price. The gate takes no interest and makes no exceptions. I have read the contracts, boy. I married into this bloodline's secrets fifty years ago, and I can tell you the gate's terms are the fairest and cruelest terms in creation: exact."

I sat with it. Outside, the snow went on ticking against the shutters, and somewhere below Odile laughed at something, bright and unguarded, and I found my eyes stinging and couldn't have said precisely which grief it was for.

Ordinary. Mortal. Deaf to the hymn.

And here is the thing I could not explain to Anselm, and barely understood myself: some traitorous, exhausted corner of me heard those terms and felt *relief*. Twenty-six years of being hidden, marked, watched, hunted, priced. A whole life spent as an entry in someone else's ledger. The idea of a life that belonged to no contract at all — that was nobody's key, nobody's heir, nobody's investment —

But then I thought of Odile humming in her sleep, and Wren sharpening her knives downstairs, and a chained king who hadn't seen sky in a century, and I understood that relief was not the same as choice, and that I would make the choice anyway, and pay whatever the difference cost.

"All right," I said. "Teach me the rest."

* * *

We worked through the afternoon. The second verse, which must be sung by two voices in harmony — "the gate trusts no single throat; it wants witnesses" — so Anselm sang with me, his voice cracked and thin and true underneath the cracks, and when our two lines braided together the candles leaned again and the floor hummed like a struck bell and I felt the vast attention below us shift, restless, almost eager.

The third verse was Odile's. Anselm wouldn't teach it to me at all. "She carries it already," he said. "She has carried it since before she could talk. You don't learn that verse. You only make sure she lives long enough to sing it." Which meant, though neither of us said it, that the child's safety was not sentiment. It was structure. Lose Odile and the rite collapses like a stool missing a leg.

The fourth verse required a king's blood freely given — which was why Aldric had to walk to the gate under his own power, why no rescue could substitute for his consent. Anselm explained this with a look toward the south, toward the war roads, and I thought of Wren's plan taking shape in her head and said nothing, because some ledgers you don't open until you're ready to read them.

By dusk my throat was raw and my head ached and I had filled eleven pages with corrections. Anselm lit fresh candles and did not dismiss me.

"One more thing," he said. "The last thing. Then you'll eat, and sleep, and tomorrow we begin again, because repetition is the whole of mastery and I intend to bore this hymn so deep into you that even grief cannot dig it out."

He sat down across from me and folded his hands.

"When the moment comes — and it will come faster than you want, and uglier — you will be standing in the holiest and most terrible place in the world, with the crown screaming in voices you love, and the gate waiting for your signature. And you will have a choice in front of you that I am obligated to describe, because your ancestors described it to *their* students, and the gate requires that the terms be spoken aloud." He paused. "The gate will offer you a trade. It always offers. Take up the crown's power as it breaks — absorb it, become the new anchor, the new hollow thing — and the gate will consider the debt paid in full. You keep everything. Your gift. Your protection. Your life, extended past all natural limit. You need only agree to wear what the usurper wears."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you pay the other way. Gift forfeit. Protection forfeit. Ordinary mortality, from that hour forward." He smiled, and it was a real smile, tired and warm. "I have prayed about this for fifty years, Xanthe, and I will tell you what I concluded, and then I will never mention it again. The crown is not a temptation because it offers power. Power tempts no one honest. The crown is a temptation because it offers *continuity*. It says: you need not end. You need not lose anything. You need not grieve. And every person who has ever taken it said yes for exactly that reason." He reached across the table and tapped the eleventh page of corrections with one crooked finger. "But look at what you've written here. Look at your own hand. Cancellations, fees, balances struck. You are a ledger-keeper, boy. You above all people know that nothing continues. Everything closes. The only question worth asking is whether the account balances honestly."

I looked down at the page. My handwriting. My grandfather's stylus, sitting beside the inkpot, iron gone dark with a century of use.

"And yours?" I asked. "Does your account balance, Brother Anselm?"

He laughed — a short, surprised sound, as if I'd caught him at something. "Oh, I'm overdrawn decades ago. I hid in plain prayer while the crown burned my kin, and I told myself hiding was patience, and I told myself patience was duty, and I got very good at telling myself things." He blew out one of the candles between us, and the shadows moved in closer, companionable. "Coming with you is not redemption. Coming with you is arithmetic. A very old man balancing a very old account. Don't make it prettier than that. I'll die at that threshold — the gate will take a key's life to open wide enough for the rite, and mine is the one it will bill, and I have known it since the day you walked into my refectory smelling of ink and fear. And I want you to know that I mind very little, and that the minding I do feel is not for me."

"Then who is it for?"

"Odile," he said simply. "She's going to lose another family before this is done. See she doesn't lose the next one."

* * *

I didn't sleep well, which surprised me, because I was bone-tired down to the marrow. I lay in the narrow guest cell listening to the mountain creak and rehearsed verses in my head until they blurred, and somewhere past midnight I gave up and went down to the scriptorium to look at the copies again by candlelight.

Wren was there.

She was sitting on the floor with her back against the lectern, a knife whetstone in one hand, and she didn't startle when I came in, which meant she'd heard me on the stairs and chosen not to move. There was a letter open on her knee. Her face in the candlelight was doing something complicated.

"You're up late," I said.

"So are you." She folded the letter away quickly, but not quickly enough. I'd seen the seal. Black wax, stamped with a wheel — the mark of the free cities' council. "Message from Cinderport," she said. "Rode up with the supply cart tonight."

"And?"

"And the council voted." She turned the whetstone over in her fingers, not looking at me. "They're not surrendering. Old General Marrec got his walls after all — they're stripping the countryside for grain and pulling every militia company inside the ring. Which sounds like good news until you notice what it means."

"It means the cities are going to be under siege," I said slowly, "at exactly the moment we need the roads open."

"It means worse than that." She looked up at me then, and I saw the thing under the complication in her face, and my stomach dropped the way it does when you catch an error on the final page and realize it's been propagating since the first. "The courier brought a second message. From the Marchlands. The usurper's vanguard crossed the river two days ago. They're not marching on the cities, Xanthe."

"They're marching here."

"They're marching *up*," Wren said. "Straight at the mountain. At the monastery. At the gate." She stood, sliding the knife home in its sheath. "Scouts say ten days. Maybe less, if the weather holds for them. So whatever lesson you're learning—" she nodded at the candlelit pages spread across the desk, at my grandfather's stylus lying among them like a sleeping animal— "I'd ask the old man to teach it faster."

Above us, faint through the floorboards, I heard Brother Anselm begin to sing the first verse alone in his cell — quietly, patiently, the way a man rehearses for a farewell.

Ten days.

I picked up the stylus, and started copying the hymn a fourth time.
