## 23. Vail Defects

I had burned forty-one people before I understood what I was burning.

That number is not a boast. It is a ledger entry, and I am, whatever else has become of me, a creature of ledgers. Inquisitors keep accounts the way guildsmen do—names, dates, offenses, dispositions. Ash, ash, ash. My own hand recorded every one in a small black book with a clasp of white iron, and for eleven years that book was the truest thing in my life.

Then came the archive beneath the Bastion of Saint Verre, and the orders signed in the crown's own cipher, and the truth sitting there in its neat columns like a corpse at a dinner party: *the gatekeepers were not heretics. The gatekeepers were inventory.*

You cannot unburn forty-one people. But you can decide what to do with the forty-second order, which arrived three days ago and bore my own name in the disposition column.

*Subject: Seraphine Vail. Offense: unauthorized retention of crown documents. Disposition: pending.*

Pending. Even my death warrant was filed with bureaucratic caution. There is something almost tender in that.

* * *

The free cities did not want me. I want to be clear about that, because history—if anyone writes it honestly, which no one will—should record that I stood in the mud outside Cinderport's land gate for six hours before anyone agreed to let me through, and that the man who finally opened it spat at my feet first.

"I know," I said. "It's an old habit of mine too."

The council chamber smelled of tallow smoke and fear, which is apparently the official perfume of governments under siege. They sat me in a chair with a broken leg propped on a brick, which I took as a fair assessment of my standing. Around the long table: the council of five, General Hask in his faded campaign red, a clerk with ink-stained fingers who kept looking at me the way you look at a snake you've been told is venomous but might, just possibly, be useful.

And at the far end, arms crossed, watching me with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like: Xanthe Yarrow.

He looked worse than the last time I'd seen him—which had been across a burning tollhouse, his face lit orange with rage while I read him the charges. Thinner. A healing cut above his eyebrow. He stood next to a smuggler woman with a scarred jaw and a crossbow she held the way other people hold opinions, and behind them both, half-hidden by the smuggler's cloak, a small girl who stared at me without blinking.

Children always stare at me. They can smell the ash, I think. Or they used to be able to.

"Seraphine Vail," said the chairman, reading from a paper as if the name might bite him. "First Inquisitor of the Crown's Commission. Architect of the Grayfen action." He looked up. "Why are you here?"

"Because I'm going to die," I said, "and I'd prefer not to die stupid."

Someone snorted. Hask didn't. Hask was studying me the way soldiers study terrain—looking for the slope, the ambush, the place where the ground gives way.

"You'll forgive us," the chairman said dryly, "if we don't take the word of the crown's chief executioner at face value."

"I don't ask for face value. I ask for verification. It's what I would demand." I set my satchel on the table and began unpacking it with the care of a surgeon laying out instruments. "In here you will find the crown's full campaign plans for the Marchlands assault: troop rotations, supply schedules, the siege-engine dispositions for the western wall of this city specifically. You will find the cipher keys to the Commission's dispatch network, current as of nine days ago. And you will find—" I paused, because this part mattered, and I wanted them to see that it mattered to me "—the original commission orders from ninety-eight years ago, authorizing the destruction of the gatekeeper bloodlines. Signed by the crown. Countersigned by the Gilded Threshold."

The room went very quiet. The smuggler woman's crossbow dipped an inch, then rose again, as if she'd caught herself.

"The guild," Xanthe said. His voice was flat. "Countersigned by the guild."

"Yes."

He closed his eyes briefly, and I watched a man absorb one more betrayal into a collection that already seemed, from the outside, quite complete. When he opened them again, they were dry. I respected that. I had never managed it myself; I'd simply stopped having occasion to weep, which is a different and lesser discipline.

"Why," Hask said slowly, "would the crown's own inquisitor bring us this?"

"Because I read it." I pulled the oldest document from the satchel—a vellum sheet gone amber with age—and laid it flat where all could see. "Eleven years I served the Commission. Eleven years I believed we were pruning heresy before it rotted the tree. Then I found the archive under Saint Verre, and I learned that the 'heresy' was bookkeeping. The gatekeepers weren't worshippers of some forbidden god. They were *maintenance*. The gate beneath your cities holds something caged, and the gatekeepers were the lock's living parts, and someone a century ago decided the lock was more useful broken—or rather, decided that whatever wears the crown now preferred the cage rusted shut and everyone who understood it dead." I tapped the countersignature. "This is not faith anymore, General. This is procurement. The crown bought the murder of every person alive who knew how the gate worked, and the guild invoiced it annually. Line item. 'Sealed and starving.'"

"Starving," the clerk repeated faintly.

"That's the guild's own word. From their third ledger. Which Master Corvane kept hidden even from his own clerks—including, until recently, the young man standing over there."

Xanthe's jaw tightened. He hadn't known about the annual invoices either, I gathered. Every revelation I brought seemed to cost him something personally, and still he kept asking for more. That is either courage or a compulsion, and in my experience the two are difficult to distinguish at close range.

* * *

They verified, of course. Three hours of it, while I sat in the broken chair and answered questions in my flattest procedural voice. Hask sent riders to check the supply depots I named. The clerk worked the cipher against intercepted dispatches and came back pale. The chairman sent for a scholar who confirmed the age of the vellum and then asked to sit down himself.

Through all of it, Xanthe Yarrow did not leave the chamber. He circled me, occasionally, the way one circles a fire that has gone out but might not be entirely dead. Finally, during a lull, he planted himself in front of my chair.

"The tollhouse at Grayfen," he said.

"Yes."

"Odile's family. Your people did that."

"My people did that. I signed the operational order." I met his eyes. "I have the signature memorized. Would you like to see it?"

Something flickered across his face—not forgiveness, nothing so generous. Recognition, maybe. The look of a ledger-keeper meeting another ledger-keeper across the terrible arithmetic of their respective books.

"No," he said. "I believe you. That's the problem."

"Most people find belief easier when it costs them nothing."

"What do you want, Vail? Truly. Not the speech. The want."

I considered lying to him. It would have been effortless—I have lied to better interrogators than a guild-trained clerk with a stylus in his pocket. But there was something in the question that deserved better than craft, some quality in his tiredness that made lying feel like a waste of both our remaining time.

"I want the forty-one names back," I said. "And since that transaction does not exist in any market I know, I want the next best thing. I want the thing that ordered those deaths to end, and I want to be holding the paperwork when it does." I leaned forward. "I spent eleven years believing I was the instrument of divine order. I have recently learned I was the instrument of a crown that eats people. I am not asking for your trust, Keeper. I am asking you to check my work. It's what I'd do."

He studied me for a long moment. Behind him, the little girl—Odile—was humming under her breath, barely audible, a thread of melody that made the hairs on my arms rise. I knew that hymn. I had heard fragments of it in confiscated papers, in the last words of the dying. Hearing it sung by a child in a council chamber felt like hearing a bell ring inside a tomb.

"You could be playing us," Xanthe said. "Every document could be bait. The cipher could be poisoned. You could be leading the whole Commission to our door."

"All true."

"And I'd have no way to know until it was too late."

"Also true."

"So why should I gamble on you?"

"Because," I said, "you already gambled on a smuggler, a monk, and a child, and the odds on me are no worse." I glanced at Odile. "And because she hums that song in her sleep, and the crown wants her dead for exactly that reason, and I have spent my entire career on the side that wants children dead for singing. I find I would like to defect to the other side of that particular line. Permanently. With prejudice."

The corner of his mouth moved. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment, perhaps, that I had learned to speak his language: the language of transactions, of debts, of things owed and paid.

"Hask gets the military documents," he said at last. "The cipher goes to Wren—she knows people who can use it. The century-old orders stay with me." He held out his hand, palm up, and waited.

I put the documents in it, one by one, and it felt less like surrender than like closing an account. Transferring assets. Whatever it was, my hands did not shake, and I counted that a victory.

"If this is a trap," he added quietly, "my friend with the crossbow has already picked out which bolt is yours."

"Efficient," I said. "I'd expect nothing less from a Threshold man."

"I was never really a Threshold man."

"No," I agreed. "I don't suppose any of us were ever really what we thought."

* * *

They gave me a cell, naturally. A comfortable one, by cell standards—there was even a window—but a cell, because trust is extended in installments and I had only paid the down payment. I sat on the cot and listened to the city around me: hammering on the walls, the low continuous murmur of ten thousand people deciding whether to be afraid today or tomorrow.

Near midnight, footsteps in the corridor. Not the guard's—heavy, deliberate, familiar rhythm. The door opened without a knock, and Xanthe stood there with a lamp, looking like a man who had argued with himself all evening and lost.

"I checked your work," he said.

"And?"

"The cipher's clean. Wren cracked three dispatches already—the crown's moving a reserve column toward the aqueduct road. They know about the stair." He set the lamp on the floor and sat down against the opposite wall, as far from me as the cell allowed, which I noted he chose deliberately. A man sits where he can watch the door and the danger at once. "The old orders are genuine. The scholar says the countersignature is Corvane's predecessor's. Same hand, same wax, year after year after year."

"A century of invoices," I said.

"A century." He rubbed his face. "Vail. Why tell me about Grayfen? You could have led with the campaign plans. The useful stuff. Instead you gave me the thing that makes you monstrous first."

"Because you'd have found it anyway. People always dig for the worst thing about a stranger—it's how we protect ourselves. Better it come from my mouth, dated and signed, than from rumor." I drew my knees up. "Also, because I wanted to see what you'd do with it."

"What did I do?"

"You got angry, and then you got to work. In that order." I tilted my head. "Most men I've shown that order to went straight to work and saved the anger for later, when it curdles. You're a strange one, Keeper."

"I'm a ledger-keeper. Anger's just another line item. You post it and move on or it unbalances everything." He was quiet a moment. Then: "The crown's orders. The new ones. With your name in the disposition column."

"Pending," I said.

"That doesn't frighten you?"

"It frightens me enormously. But I've discovered that fear and purpose can share a body, provided neither one gets greedy." I looked at him across the lamplight. "You should sleep, Keeper. Tomorrow you'll need to plan a defense of an aqueduct against an army, and I intend to help, and you'll hate how good I am at it."

He almost smiled. Almost. He rose, took up the lamp, and paused at the door.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the forty-one names. If we win—when we win—someone should carve them somewhere. All of them. Including the ones your hand wrote down."

I had prepared for suspicion, for bargaining, for hatred. I had not prepared for that. For a moment I could not speak at all, and the silence between us filled with the distant sound of hammers on the walls, building, building, while somewhere south an army came to tear it all down.

"Yes," I managed finally. "Someone should."

He nodded once and left. I lay back on the cot and listened to the city hold itself together, and for the first time in eleven years, the black book in my mind turned its page to tomorrow, and the column beneath it was empty, and I found I could not wait to fill it.

Toward morning, the guard's boots hurried past my door, and there was shouting from the wall, and a bell began to ring—the deep alarm bell, the one that means the horizon has changed.

I stood and pressed my ear to the cold stone.

The crown had arrived early.
