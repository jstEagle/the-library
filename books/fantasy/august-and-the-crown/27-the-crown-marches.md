## 27. The Crown Marches

I have learned that a man can get used to anything, provided the anything is gradual.

The crown does not do gradual. The crown does *sudden*. It does the lurch of a floorboard giving way beneath you at three in the morning, the certainty of the drop before your body has agreed to fall. For most of my reign—*reign*, what a generous word for it—I have been able to pretend there was still a me underneath the wearing, some small stubborn kernel of the man who once had a name I refuse to remember because remembering it hurts in places the crown has already eaten.

But tonight, on the road to Cinderport, with the free cities' walls rising black against the last light like the teeth of something patient, the crown stopped pretending with me.

It *sang*.

Not aloud. Aloud would have been a mercy; my generals might have thought their king was moved by piety and written flattering accounts of it. No—it sang inside the architecture of my skull, in the hollows where my own thoughts used to live before they were converted into storage. It sang a hymn. A gate-hymn. And beneath the hymn, so faint I might have invented it, a sound I had not heard in a hundred years:

Iron, waking up.

"You feel it too," I said to no one. My voice came out wrong. It has been coming out wrong for years—too resonant, too certain, the voice of a man who has never once doubted himself, which is how you know he isn't really a man anymore. "Good. Then we understand each other."

The crown did not answer. The crown never answers. It only *wants*, and lately its wanting has developed a direction, which is worse. For a century it was content to sit on my head and digest me at its leisure, a slow meal, a glutton with excellent manners. Now it pulled at me like a hand pulling a glove tighter, and somewhere far behind us—south, beneath an aqueduct, under the feet of people who had no idea what they were standing on—the gate had turned over in its sleep.

And the crown was *afraid*.

I want to be clear about this, because history will not be: crowns are not supposed to be afraid. Crowns are supposed to be the fear. But I felt it leaking down through the metal into my scalp like cold water through a cracked roof, and I understood, with the particular clarity of a drowning man, that whatever the gate was doing down there, the crown needed it stopped. Not wanted stopped. *Needed.* The way lungs need air. The way I used to need sleep, back when sleep was something I could still lose.

Which meant that for the first time in a hundred years, the crown and I were afraid of the same thing.

I found I hated it less than I should have.

* * *

The army crossed the Marchlands the way a spill crosses a tablecloth.

I reviewed them from the ridge at dusk, because kings review armies, and because from up here I could almost believe the thing below me was still mine. Two hundred thousand, if you counted the levies, and everyone counts the levies when they want the number to frighten. Banners the color of dried blood. Siege engines in their hundreds, great wooden ruminants chewing slowly along the roads. And everywhere, everywhere, the smell—not of men and horses, though that was there too, but of the crown's particular perfume, the scent of things being *used up*. My soldiers marched with the steady rhythm of people who have forgotten why they're walking and have decided not to ask. That was my gift to them. That was the crown's gift through me. Certainty, poured into ten thousand skulls like water into cracked jars.

"Your Radiance." General Maren, at the base of the ridge, helmet under her arm. She was one of the few who still looked at my face instead of the crown above it, which either meant courage or that she hadn't yet learned what to be afraid of. "Scouts report the cities are arming. Militia on the walls. They've recalled every ship to harbor."

"Good," I said.

Maren blinked. "Radiance?"

"Frightened men behind walls fight better than confident men in fields. Let them be frightened. Fear keeps a wall honest." I smiled, and felt my face make the smile rather than the reverse, which is a sensation I recommend to no one. "How long to Cinderport?"

"Four days. Five if the aqueduct country slows the engines."

Five days. Somewhere in my chest—a chest that was mostly furniture now, mostly display space for the crown's collection of devoured lives—something did arithmetic it wasn't asked to do. Five days until the walls. Five days until the crown could stop needing me to hurry. And after the walls?

After the walls, I suspected, the crown would no longer require a wearer at all. There are meals you finish, and then there are meals where you look down and realize you have been the plate.

"Five days," I said. "We will not need six."

* * *

That night the memories came, as they always come, and for the first time I tried to hold onto one instead of letting it pass through me.

This is the crown's cruelest economy: it eats people whole, and it stores them, and sometimes—in the dark, in the hour when even usurpers lie awake—it plays them back. Not to torment me. Torment implies malice, and the crown has no more malice than a fire has. It plays them back the way a full belly gurgles. Indigestion of the soul. A hundred years of gatekeepers, priests, rivals, children, all of them filed away in the vaults behind my eyes, and on bad nights the vault doors swing open and I am everyone I have ever consumed, all at once, none of them me.

Tonight the memory wore a grey coat and ink-stained fingers.

A ledger-keeper. One of the Threshold's, by the guild-mark on his collar. He was writing in a book by candlelight, and he was humming—of course he was humming, they all hummed, the hymn worked into them like grain into bread—and he paused, mid-entry, and looked up as if he could see across a century straight into the skull the crown was renting out.

*"You were kind once,"* the dead man said, or the memory said, or I said, in his voice, in his candlelight. *"Before. I have it in the accounts. Kindness, entered twice, never balanced."*

"I don't know you," I told him.

*"No,"* he agreed, sadly, dipping his pen. *"That's rather the point of you."*

He went back to his ledger. I stood in his little room and watched him work and felt something move in me that I had assumed the crown had stripped out years ago for scrap. It took me a moment to name it, because it has been so long since I felt anything that wasn't hunger or obedience.

Grief. Not for him. For the man who had been kind enough to be remembered kindly by a stranger's ghost.

"Get out," I whispered, and the memory folded itself away like a letter, and I sat alone in the command tent with two hundred thousand soldiers outside who loved me the way a lock loves a key, and I pressed my hands against my face and felt, beneath the palms, the faint vibration of the crown humming the gate-hymn back at itself.

Learning it. Memorizing it. Preparing, in its patient way, to sing it *wrong*.

* * *

On the third day we reached the walls.

Cinderport at dawn, seen from the crest of the last hill: white stone gone grey with salt wind, the aqueduct striding across the lowlands on its stone legs like a god wading out to sea, and the city huddled beneath it all, small and stubborn and lit already with ten thousand lamps. They knew we were coming. Of course they knew. You cannot hide an army any more than you can hide a funeral.

And beneath the city—beneath the aqueduct, beneath the streets, beneath the cellars and the bones of older cities stacked under this one like pages under a paperweight—the iron was awake. I could feel it now without trying. A warmth in the crown's band, a pulse, a *turning*. The gate was stirring the way a sleeper stirs when someone says their name in another room.

The crown surged.

I felt it happen and I could do nothing about it, which is the whole of my existence in a single sentence. Power flooded down through the metal into me and out through me into the army, and along the whole front line, two hundred thousand throats opened and roared—not orders, nothing so human as orders, just volume, just the sound of the crown flexing through every mouth it owned. My generals stared at me. My horse screamed and tried to bolt and I held it, barely, my hands shaking around reins slick with its sweat.

"Radiance—" Maren began.

"It's nothing," I said. "Battle-fever."

It was not battle-fever. It was the crown discovering urgency, and urgency, in the crown, looks exactly like this: a king dissolving. Because that was the other thing I felt, the thing I had been circling for days like a tongue around a broken tooth. The crown was pulling harder now. Tighter. Where before it had worn me the way a man wears a coat, now it was wearing me the way fire wears a wick, and I could feel the edges of myself going soft and bright and *gone*. My hands, when I looked at them, seemed slightly translucent at the fingertips, or perhaps that was the dawn light, or perhaps it was neither.

There was a version of me, once, who would have fought this. I searched for him and found only an impression, a shadow left on a wall by a man who had walked past long ago.

"Sound the advance," I said.

* * *

They say—I say, rather, in the rare moments when the narration of my own life is left to me—that a besieger feels no fear, because fear requires something left to lose. This is written by people who have never been a container.

The first assault went up the walls at noon and came off the walls by midafternoon, and the second, and between them I sat my horse before the gates and let the crown pour through me and did the arithmetic again, the forbidden arithmetic, the one I kept doing the way a sore tooth gets touched. The gate was waking faster. Each hour the pulse in the iron strengthened, and each hour the crown pulled harder on what remained of me, and I charted the two curves in my head and saw where they crossed, and the place where they crossed was very soon, and at the crossing point there would be no *me* left to notice the difference between victory and digestion.

The crown would win. That was never in doubt. The question—the only question left, the last coin in my pocket—was whether anything would survive of the winner to enjoy it.

"Pull back the third wave," I heard myself order, and then, with the small rebellious strength of a man kicking at his own chains from the inside, "and send riders ahead. Quiet ones. I want to know everything moving beneath that aqueduct. Every clerk, every smuggler, every child. If the gate has found itself a keeper, I want his name before nightfall."

Maren looked at me strangely. "A keeper, Radiance?"

"The old stories," I said smoothly, and the crown smoothed it smoother, pouring plausibility through my voice like oil. "Walls are stone. But these cities were founded on something older, and if our enemies have gone digging in it, I want to know."

She saluted and went, and I sat alone in the noise of the siege, and beneath the noise, beneath the drums and the screaming and the groan of engines, I listened to the iron under the city turn over again in its sleep.

And this time, unmistakable, threaded through the crown's hungry hymn like a hair in bread, I heard it answer.

Three voices. Small, and cracked, and human, and singing *true*.

One of them was a child.

I closed my eyes. Behind them, in the vaults, a hundred dead gatekeepers lifted their heads.

"Well," I said, to the crown, to the gate, to the wick that I was, "it seems we're all awake now."

The crown sang louder. And I began, quietly, in the last room of myself, to count the hours.
