## 28. The Debt Collector

I have kept ledgers for eleven years, and I have never once enjoyed being on the receiving end of one.

The gate chamber was not what I expected, which is to say I hadn't expected anything, because a man who expects things from gates has already lost the argument with himself about how the world works. It was round, and old, and lit by nothing — the light came from everywhere and nowhere in particular, the way light behaves in dreams and in churches when the collection plate comes around. The iron of the gate itself rose out of the floor like a tree that had given up on branches: a single slab of black metal veined with channels that might have been script or might have been scars. Odile stood beside me with her hand in mine, humming under her breath, and the gate hummed back.

Anselm's hymn still rang in my ears, or maybe it wasn't in my ears anymore. Maybe it had moved in permanently, taken up residence the way a tenant does who pays no rent and rearranges your furniture. Anselm was dead. He'd died holding the threshold with his voice, buying us the time to come down here, and I would grieve him properly later, when later existed. Right now there was only this room, and the gate, and the growing suspicion — the ledger-keeper's instinct, the itch at the base of the skull — that something was about to be itemized.

"You feel that too," Wren said. She'd come down behind me against every sensible instinct she owned, crossbow bolt wound freshly bound at her shoulder, and she was looking at the gate the way she looked at cargo manifests she suspected were lying to her.

"Feel what?"

"That we're being *waited for*."

"I was going to say 'audited,' but yes."

The channels in the iron began to glow. Not all at once — they filled the way ink fills a pen, from the bottom up, and then the words moved. They crawled along the veins of the gate like ants following a trail of sugar, gathering, sorting, arranging themselves into lines. And then, because the universe has a sense of humor and it is a cruel one, the lines arranged themselves into columns.

A ledger. The gate opened as a ledger.

"Oh, you have got to be joking," I said.

*You keep accounts,* said a voice that came from everywhere and nowhere in particular, and it sounded — and this is the part I will be embarrassed about until I die — it sounded like my grandfather. The man who'd left me an iron stylus and a name I didn't understand. Warm, dry, patient, the voice of someone who has balanced books through three recessions and a plague and found all of them funny in the end. *It seemed the courteous form.*

"The courteous form," I repeated. "Right. Yes. Very courteous. My grandfather's voice coming out of an eldritch door. Nothing unsettling about that."

*He is in here,* the gate said simply. *They are all in here. That is rather the point of what follows.*

Odile's grip tightened on my hand. "Xanthe," she whispered, "it's talking like Da."

"It's talking like everyone's da," I said. "That's its whole trick. Don't listen with your heart, listen with your head. What do the numbers say?"

She blinked at me — twelve years old, orphaned twice over in one week, and still sharp enough to catch what I meant. *What do the numbers say.* It was the first thing any Threshold clerk learned: feelings lie, figures don't. A merchant will weep about his losses while pocketing your coin; the ledger never weeps.

The gate, obligingly, turned to figures.

Light gathered in the center of the chamber, condensing into a page that hung in the air like frost on glass, and writing bloomed across it in a hand I recognized because it was my own — or close enough. Old Yarrow script. The family hand. And at the top, in letters that glowed a soft, terrible gold:

**ACCOUNT OF XANTHE YARROW, LAST KEEPER. OPENED: THE NIGHT OF THE FIRE.**

Below it, entries. Decades before I was born, entries. My father's name. My mother's. And then, dated the night of the fire I had always been told took our house and my parents and nearly me —

*One life, withdrawn from death. Paid by: Maren Yarrow and Tomas Yarrow. Security: one debt, undischarged, payable to the Gate upon presentation. Interest: compounding.*

I read it four times. My eyes went over it the way they go over any ledger, hunting for the error, the transposed digit, the entry in the wrong column, because there is always an error, there is always a way a thing isn't what it says it is. There was no error. There was no transposition. There was just the plain arithmetic of my own existence, written in my family's hand, glowing in the belly of the gate beneath Cinderport.

"They bought me back," I said. My voice came out very flat, which is what my voice does instead of breaking. "When I was a baby. When Vail's predecessors burned the keepers. They didn't get me out. They *bought* me out. From the gate."

*From death,* the gate corrected gently, in my grandfather's voice, which was worse. *The distinction matters. Death keeps honest accounts. So do I.*

"And the debt?"

*Is presented now.*

The page turned itself.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about temptation: it doesn't arrive wearing horns. It arrives wearing your mother's face.

The second page of the account showed me two futures side by side, the way a moneylender shows you two loan structures, and I want it noted for the record that I understood exactly what was happening to me even as it happened. I am not a fool. I have watched guildmasters sweeten terms for twenty years. I know a rigged offer when I'm standing inside one.

But knowing is not the same as resisting. Any clerk who tells you otherwise has never been offered anything he wanted.

In the left-hand future, I saw myself standing where I stood, but changed. Taller in the bearing, if not the body. The stylus in my hand burning white instead of rusting gray. The gate open behind me, its power running through me like a river through a channel, and beyond the gate — the crown. The Hollow King's crown, torn loose, held in both hands, offered. *Take it,* said the left-hand future, in my mother's voice now, warm as bread. *Take it and pay the debt whole. Take it and no one you love dies today. Take it and the walls hold, the armies break, Seraphine Vail lives, Wren lives, the child lives, everyone lives, and all it costs is what you were going to spend anyway.*

"All it costs," I said.

*Your mortality. Your smallness. Your ordinary days. You would trade them regardless — you were always going to die. This way the dying buys something.*

"That's not a price," I said. "That's a sales pitch."

*It is both,* said the gate, with what I could have sworn was my grandfather's shrug. *Most prices are.*

And the horrible thing — the thing I'll carry whether or not I walk out of this chamber — is that for one full breath, I wanted it. Not the power. Power has never tempted me; I spent my life perfecting the art of being overlooked, and there are comforts in invisibility that the powerful never learn. No. What tempted me was the arithmetic. Because the offer was *fair*, that's the trap of it, the gate had built the trap out of fairness itself: take the crown's power, pour it into the debt, and the ledger closes clean. Everyone lives. The battle above ends. Odile grows up. Wren grows old, insolent and beloved. And I become the thing the gate needs me to be, forever, and forever is a long time but it's a *price*, and prices can be paid.

My mother's voice said, *You were such a small thing in the fire. So loud. We couldn't save you ourselves. Forgive us the manner of it.*

"Don't," I whispered. "Don't do that."

*Do what?*

"Use them. They're dead. They paid already. Whatever's left of them in you, you don't get to spend it on me."

The light flickered — I want to believe it flickered — and then the right-hand future assembled itself beside the first, and this one was not warm at all.

* * *

In the right-hand future, I saw the rite. Anselm's hymn sung whole, three bloodlines at the threshold, the crown seized and broken against the gate's iron. And below it, in the dispassionate gold script, the costs, itemized:

*The gift of keeping: revoked.*
*The hearth-sense, the hearing of the hymn, the opening of ways: revoked, permanent.*
*Extended years purchased by the debt: revoked. Standard mortal allotment applies, retroactive.*
*Protection of the Gate from its bearer: revoked. You will be as any man. You may be killed by any man.*

And at the bottom, in smaller script, the way guild contracts put the real terms in small script:

*Note: the bearer should understand that the debt was never a punishment. It was a loan. Someone believed you worth lending to. The Gate merely asks what the loan was for.*

I stood there a long time.

Above us — muffled by thirty feet of stone and aqueduct — the battle went on. I could feel it more than hear it now, the gift that the gate was offering to take away working one last time: the tremor of militia boots on the streets, the wrongness of inquisitor fire somewhere in the upper dark, the vast slow pressure of the usurper's army against walls that were never meant to hold against it. Somewhere up there Anselm was dead. Somewhere up there Vail was commanding defenses she'd have burned a month ago. Somewhere out there, past the walls, in a camp full of enemies, a broken king sat chained in a tent being displayed like a war trophy, and Wren was already looking at him with the expression she got when she was planning a route through impossible country.

Everyone lives. That was the left-hand offer. Everyone lives, and I stop being human in every way that had ever mattered to me, and I wear the crown's power like the usurper wore the crown itself, and in a hundred years some other low-born clerk finds a prophecy book with their name in it and comes down these stairs to find *me* waiting, hungry, hollowed, a mouthpiece with my mother's voice.

Because that was the thing the left-hand future didn't say outright but wrote between every line, the fine print I could read because I had spent my life reading fine print: the crown eats its bearers. The gate knew it. The gate had watched it happen. The offer was fair the way a loan shark is fair — accurate to the last digit, and fatal.

"Question," I said.

*Ask.*

"When my parents made this loan. Did they know what the payment options would be?"

Silence. Real silence, the kind that means the answerer is choosing its words, and even a gate has to choose its words sometimes.

*They asked that the debt be made survivable,* it said at last. *They did not ask that it be made painless. There is no such term. There never has been.*

"Yeah," I said. "That sounds like them. I don't remember them, you understand. Eleven years of guild raised me, and the guild taught me that everything has a figure attached. But Wren told me once — she trades in small impossibilities, she'd know — she told me the best bargains are the ones where the other party wants you to win. My parents wanted me to win. So." I looked up at the hanging pages, at my mother's warmth and my family's handwriting and the two futures laid out like cuts of meat. "Show me the third option."

*There is no third option.*

"There's always a third option. It's usually worse, and it's usually the right one. What happens if I refuse to choose? If I just stand here?"

*The debt defaults. The gate closes. The crown takes the cities. Everything the left-hand future promised to save, dies — except you, who were bought and paid for, and who will live a long ordinary life in a city of ash.*

"There it is." I laughed, and it came out cracked. "Default, ruin, or surrender. You really were built by clerks."

*We were built by keepers,* the gate said, and for just a moment the voice was not my grandfather's, or my mother's, or anyone's — it was older than voices, the sound the hymn makes underneath the hymn. *Keepers and clerks are the same profession, Xanthe Yarrow. One keeps accounts of coin. One keeps accounts of the world. Both exist so that someone, someday, can look at the record and say: this was true, this was owed, this was paid. Now. Choose.*

Odile tugged my sleeve. She'd been quiet through all of it, humming her low note, holding the gate steady the way a child holds a ladder. "Xanthe," she said. "The numbers."

I looked at her.

"What do the numbers say?" she repeated, and pointed at the right-hand page. At the bottom line. At the small print I'd read and dismissed.

*You will be as any man.*

And beneath it, the line I'd skipped, because grief makes a sloppy reader of us all:

*Note: the debt was never a punishment. It was a loan. Someone believed you worth lending to.*

Eleven years I had thought I was nothing. Overlooked, unremarkable, a life built to be missed. And the whole time, the truth was the opposite: I was the most expensive object in the world. Two people had gone into debt against death itself to keep me. Every ordinary day I'd ever lived — every dull shift at the ledger desk, every bad meal, every night I'd fallen asleep grateful for my own insignificance — had been *financed*. Bought on credit. Spent freely by a boy who didn't know he was spending.

The gate wasn't asking me to pay back my life.

It was asking me whether I'd ever understood what my life had cost, and what it was *for*.

"Close the left-hand page," I said.

*Xanthe—*

"Close it. I've seen the offer. I've declined the offer. Write it down — you're the ledger, write it down: *bearer declines the crown's power, in full knowledge of consequences, under duress of affection, which is the strongest duress there is.*" My voice shook and I let it. "Now show me the rest of the right-hand page. All of it. Every fee, every penalty, every line of small print. I'm not signing anything I haven't read, and I am *not* paying this bill blind."

The gate hummed. Odile hummed with it. And somewhere far above, faint through the stone, I heard a sound that did not belong to the battle — a smuggler's whistle, three notes, the signal Wren used when a plan stopped being a plan and became a schedule.

The new page unfurled. The gold script began to write.

And the first line it wrote was not about me at all.

*Interim entry, pending settlement:* said the gate, in a voice gone suddenly urgent, suddenly young. *The anchor stirs. The true king walks. And the woman with the whistle has just stolen him out from under an army.*

I grabbed Odile's hand and ran for the stairs.
