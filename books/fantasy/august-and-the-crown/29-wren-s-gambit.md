## 29. Wren's Gambit

There are three rules to moving contraband through a war camp, and I learned all of them the hard way before I was nineteen.

One: never smuggle anything you love.

Two: if you must break rule one, don't let it know it's loved. Love makes cargo heavy. Cargo that knows it's wanted sits wrong in a cart, breathes too loud, looks at guards with eyes full of meaning. A barrel of pickled eels has never once gotten anyone caught because it believed in its own importance.

Three: always have a second route, because the first one is already sold to someone by the time you need it.

I stood at the edge of the usurper's camp with a stolen quartermaster's ledger under my coat, a dead man's seal ring on my thumb, and a plan that violated every rule I owned, and I thought: *Wren, you absolute sentimental disaster.*

The camp of the Hollow King spread across the Marchlands like a spill of ink that had decided to become a city. Ten thousand tents. Forty thousand soldiers, give or take the ones the crown had already eaten from the inside. And somewhere in the middle of it, in a pavilion with gold thread on the seams, a man who had once been a king was being kept alive as furniture — displayed to crowds as a "rescued monarch," paraded out when the propaganda needed a face, chained in the dark when it didn't.

Aldric. The true king. The anchor. The reason the gate would open or wouldn't.

Xanthe needed him at the threshold. Three keys, he'd said, his voice doing that thing it did when he was terrified and pretending to be calm, which was most of the time now. Bloodlines, not objects. Odile hummed the hymn. Anselm carried the second key in his old monk bones. And the third —

The third was locked in a cage of flesh beneath a golden tent, guarded by men whose faith made them fearless and whose fear made them thorough.

So naturally, I volunteered. Because apparently I'd caught whatever Xanthe had, this disease where you start believing your life belongs to something bigger than staying alive.

* * *

My crew found out the same way everyone finds out things in my crew: I told Tam, Tam told nobody, and everybody knew by morning.

We met in the hold of the *Salty Promise*, beached and half-careened in a Cinderport dry dock, surrounded by the smell of tar and the particular silence of six people deciding whether to mutiny.

"You want to walk into the Hollow King's camp," said Tam, who is large, loyal, and slow to anger the way glaciers are slow to anger, "and walk out with a king."

"Not a king," I said. "Cargo."

"That's worse."

"It's better. Kings get rescued. Rescues are famous. Famous gets people hanged. Cargo just gets moved." I tapped the quartermaster's ledger. "And I have paperwork."

Brack, my rigger, a woman who could splice rope in the dark and read people faster than she read charts, leaned over the table. "That's Vessic's seal."

"Vessic is dead," I said. "Vessic died three days ago of a sudden and tragic case of being strangled by me, after which I took his ledger, his ring, and his standing order for forty barrels of preserved fish, requisitioned by the Third Cohort, delivery to the camp of the Hollow King, payment on receipt."

Silence. The good kind. The kind where people are doing arithmetic instead of arguing.

"Forty barrels," Brack said slowly.

"Thirty-eight barrels of salt fish. Two barrels of king."

"And the guards at the gate just wave us through?"

"Guards don't read ledgers. Guards read seals, signatures, and the expression on the face of the person holding the paper. I've been forging that expression since I was twelve." I looked around the hold. "I need a driver who doesn't sweat. I need two people inside the camp with clean faces. And I need everyone else ready at the river crossing with the *Promise* refloated, because the second route is water, and water doesn't ask questions."

Tam crossed his arms. "Rule one," he said quietly.

"I know what rule one is."

"You're smuggling something you love."

The hold got very small around those words. I hadn't said I loved Aldric. I'd met him for the space of an hour, once, through a grate in a prison barge, when I was running guns upriver and he was being moved between cages, and he'd asked me — a stranger, a criminal, a girl with soot on her hands — whether the river still smelled of juniper in the spring. I'd said yes. He'd smiled like a man tasting a memory, and thanked me as though I'd given him something worth more than the guns.

Nobody loves a man they spoke to for an hour. That's not how love works.

Except sometimes you spend years building a life out of small impossibilities — bottled storms, borrowed hours, luck sold by the pinch — and you tell yourself it's because you're greedy, and then one day a man behind bars asks about juniper blossoms and you realize every impossible thing you ever moved was practice. Training. All those years of learning how to get precious, doomed, unlikely cargo past people with power.

He wasn't cargo I loved. He was the shipment my whole rotten career had been rehearsal for.

"Rule one applies to Xanthe too," Tam said, watching my face. "And the girl. And Anselm. You've gone and broken rule one about eight times, captain."

"Then it's a good thing," I said, "that we're professionals."

* * *

The camp gates of the Hollow King were less a checkpoint than a throat. Everything went in; the question was what came back out.

We came in as fish.

It's amazing, honestly, how much of war is stomachs. Armies march on rhetoric and arrive hungry. The Third Cohort's quartermasters had requisitioned everything within fifty miles, and preserved fish from the Cinderport salteries was on the list, because the list didn't care that Cinderport was technically a free city the army intended to eat next. Paperwork is its own country. I've lived there for years. It has excellent borders.

Vessic's seal opened the gate. My face did the rest — bored, competent, faintly resentful of the delay. A guard prodded a barrel with his spear, got a whiff of brine, and waved us into the belly of the thing.

Inside, the camp was worse than I'd imagined and more ordinary. That's the horror no one warns you about: evil at scale looks like logistics. Cook fires. Latrine trenches. Soldiers mending socks. And everywhere, threaded through it like wire through meat, the black livery of the inquisition, and the banners — grey silk, blank, because the Hollow King's heralds had determined that any symbol the crown didn't devour first.

Sela walked beside me in a quartermaster's tabern two sizes too big, carrying the tally book, her face arranged into the sublime nothingness that made her the best card player on my crew. Fen had come in the day before as a laborer conscript and marked the pavilion: south side, double guard, and — his words, scratched into a slate — *"they bring him out at dusk. crowd. banners. he walks. barely."*

They paraded him. Of course they paraded him. A rescued king is worth ten thousand recruits, and the crown spent him nightly like coin.

Which meant our window was dusk, when the cage was empty of audience but the guards were at their most bored, and boredom, in my professional experience, is the most bribable emotion ever devised.

* * *

Here is what they don't put in songs about rescues: the waiting.

We waited out the afternoon in the shadow of the fish wagons, Sela and I, passing a tally book back and forth and conducting an entire conversation in margins. *Guard rotation sloppy on west face.* *Boredom confirmed.* *Dusk bell in two hours.* Somewhere beyond the canvas walls, drums started the rhythm they used to summon the crowds, and I felt the camp shift, the way a body shifts when it decides to stand.

Fen slipped in among the barrels at the appointed hour looking like a man who'd aged a season in a day. "They've taken him out," he breathed. "Crowd's huge. But captain — there's a new detail on the pavilion. Inquisitors. Grey robes. They're going through his chains after every parade. Checking them against a writ."

"A writ."

"Sealed orders. One of them read it aloud while I was hauling water. 'The anchor is not to be moved, loosened, fed unrationed, or spoken to.'" He swallowed. "Spoken to, captain. There's a law in there about talking to him."

Of course there was. Words are how anchors come loose. The crown knew that better than anyone — it had eaten enough words in its century of wearing mouths.

"So we don't talk to him," I said.

Sela gave me a look. "You're going to talk to him."

"I am going to *transport* him," I said with dignity, "and transport requires minimal verbal coordination."

"Captain."

"Sela."

"He asked you about juniper blossoms once and you've been dying of it for five years."

I checked the seals on the empty barrels instead of answering, which is a form of answering, and she let it go, because Sela is kind in the specific merciless way of people who love you.

* * *

Dusk, and the parade came back.

We heard it before we saw it — the drums winding down, the crowd noise thinning, and then the strange hush that follows a spectacle, that emptied-out quiet where ten thousand people stop cheering all at once and remember they're tired. Through a gap in the tenting I watched them bring him back down the camp's central avenue.

He walked. Barely, like Fen said. Between two wardens with iron staves, wrapped in a grey cloak they'd clearly chosen to make him look less like a king and more like evidence. But even bent, even hollowed, he walked the way my grandfather used to describe honest men walking — like the ground owed him nothing and he owed the ground everything.

They took him into the pavilion. The wardens changed. The inquisitors withdrew to their writ-checking. And the camp settled into the great digesting drowse of an army after spectacle, forty thousand people simultaneously deciding that nothing else interesting would happen tonight.

Nothing else interesting happened tonight. Except us.

The fish wagons were parked thirty yards from the pavilion's supply entrance, because quartermasters park where it's convenient and convenience is the mother of every heist I've ever run. Fen had spent the afternoon becoming invisible among the laborers, and at the change of watch he did the thing he does best, which is start a small, plausible, deeply annoying problem — a wagon axle wedged in mud, requiring four guards' opinions and none of their help — while Sela and I came through the supply entrance carrying a barrel between us and wearing the expressions of people whose lives are fish.

The interior guard was one man, young, chewing something, leaning on his spear the way young men lean when the war has been long and the enemy so far hasn't been them.

"Delivery," I said. "Barrel count for the pavilion stores. Quartermaster's orders." I showed him the ledger without showing him the ledger, which is a skill: flash the seal, hide the sums.

"Pavilion doesn't take deliveries after dusk."

"New standing order." I let one eyebrow rise a fraction. "You can wake the cohort commander to confirm, or you can initial here, and in the morning there'll be fish."

War has been long. The enemy hadn't been him. He initialed.

Getting Aldric into a barrel is not something I'm proud of, and I won't dress it up. We did it fast and we did it gently and both of those things were hard. Sela held him upright while I talked — quietly, constantly, because a man kept in the dark for a century deserves a voice in the dark, rules be damned — telling him nothing useful and everything true. *Wagon. Fish smell, sorry about that. River by midnight. There are people who need you at a door underground, and one of them is a child who sings, and one of them keeps accounts like prayers.*

His eyes found mine in the dark of the barrel's mouth. Old eyes. Anchor eyes. And he whispered — breaking the crown's own law, spending his first stolen words in a century on this:

"The river. Does it still—"

"Juniper in the spring," I said. "I checked last month. For you, I checked."

Something moved through his ruined face that I will carry to my grave, and then we folded him down into the brine-smelling dark, packed straw around the barrel's rim, sealed the lid with a fish-salter's stamp, and rolled him out into the night on a handcart, past the initialing guard, past Fen's magnificent mud disaster, past the tents of forty thousand sleeping conquerors.

* * *

The first route died exactly on schedule, because rule three exists.

We were half a mile from the camp when the flare went up — a red star bursting over the east road, the road we'd planned to take, the road somebody had sold. Torches bloomed along it like a rash. Somewhere behind us, a horn found its voice.

"Second route," I said, and my crew, bless their mercenary hearts, didn't waste one breath on *are you sure*, they just turned the wagons south into the dark toward the river, wheels screaming softly on frozen rut, and the pursuit horns began their ugly arithmetic behind us.

They caught us at the ford. Not all of them — a rider party, six, maybe eight, outriders who'd cut the angle while the main force blundered down the sold road. We heard them before we saw them, and then we saw them, and then it was simply the oldest transaction in the world, the one with blood on both sides of the ledger.

I won't itemize it. Some entries you don't enter.

But I will write down the names, because a captain's real ledger is names. Rollo, who drove the second wagon and turned it sideways across the ford to buy us ninety seconds, and did not come back across. Merrin, who'd joined us eleven months ago with a stolen fiddle and a smile, and who put herself between a crossbow and the barrel-wagon because she understood, somehow, in her last minute, exactly what we were carrying and what it was worth. Ninety seconds and one bolt. That was the fare. That's what the king cost, and he'd have torn himself apart knowing it, which is why we'd never tell him until he was safe, and maybe not even then.

The *Promise* came off the bank like a gray ghost, Tam's big hands on the sweep, Brack hauling, the river taking us the way rivers do — without questions, without mercy, without memory. Behind us the ford burned torch-orange. Ahead lay black water and the long bend toward Cinderport, toward the aqueduct, toward a stair under the earth where a boy with an iron stylus was waiting for a key.

I sat on the deck beside the barrel with the wet straw and the breathing inside it, and I put my palm flat on the lid, the way you'd steady a crate, the way you'd touch a shoulder.

"We lost Rollo," I said quietly. "And Merrin. I want you to know that. I want you to carry it, because you're the only one who can carry it out of that camp alive, and grief needs somewhere to live."

For a long moment, nothing. Then, through the wood, low and cracked and unbearably human: "Then they did not die for nothing. I will make certain of it. I swear it on the gate itself."

"Gate's shut," I said.

"It will not stay shut," said the king in the barrel. "Not with children singing at it."

I laughed — a wet, wrecked, ridiculous sound — because what else do you do when a hundred-year hostage swears oaths through a fish barrel, and the terrible thing is you believe him?

Cinderport's lights came up around the river bend an hour later, and on the quay, small and sharp-eyed and unmistakable, stood a boy holding an iron stylus like a man holds a pen he's about to sign something with.

Behind him, on the dark water, the pursuit lamps were already rounding the bend.

"Get him off my boat," I called to Xanthe, "before I start charging interest."
