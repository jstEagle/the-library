## 30. The King Unchained

The first thing I learned about freedom is that it smells like fish.

Not metaphorically. Not in the way poets mean it—liberty as bracing sea air, the open horizon, all that. I mean it literally. Wren's crew had smuggled me out of the usurper's camp rolled in oiled canvas inside a salt-fish barrel, and for six hours of my first unchained existence I was nose-to-tail with a hundredweight of herring that had seen better centuries.

I have been a king, a prisoner, an anchor, and a relic. I can report with some authority that none of those conditions prepared me for the barrel.

"Sorry about that," said the smuggler woman when they cracked the lid and hauled me out into a sea cave that stank of tar and low tide. She was small, sharp-eyed, and had the manner of someone apologizing to furniture she'd bumped. "It was that or a grain shipment, and the grain had weevils."

"I've slept in worse," I said.

This was true. For one hundred and four years I had slept on stone in a cell beneath the Hollow King's palace, chained by wrists and ankles to iron set deep in the bedrock, because the crown that sat on the usurper's head needed something to anchor its hunger to, and I was what it had chosen. Or rather—I was what my own bloodline had made possible. The gatekeepers bound the Hollow King a century ago and failed to finish the work, and the binding needed a counterweight, and the counterweight needed to be royal blood, and so they took their own true king and made him into a mooring post for a monster.

I do not say this with bitterness. Bitterness requires energy, and I spent most of mine staying alive. But I will note, for the record, that when the histories are written—and I intend to write them myself, since apparently I'm going to live long enough to do it—the chapter on the gatekeepers should include a line about how they saved the world by sacrificing their king without asking him first.

Though I suppose, in fairness, they were rather busy.

* * *

My legs did not work at first.

That surprised me more than anything else. A century in chains, and you'd think I'd have expected it. But there is a difference between knowing a thing and discovering it, the way there is a difference between knowing you will die someday and waking up on the particular morning you do.

They carried me out of the cave on a smuggler's stretcher, and I lay there looking up at actual sky—gray, overcast, drizzling, objectively miserable—and felt such a violent rush of gratitude toward that ugly little piece of weather that I nearly wept, which would have been embarrassing, so instead I asked questions.

"How far to Cinderport?"

"Two days' walk," Wren said. "Three, carrying you."

"And the usurper's army?"

"Ahead of us. Behind us. Around us, if the scouts are right." She said it the way she'd said everything so far: flatly, efficiently, a woman reading out a manifest. "The walls will hold maybe four days once the siege starts. Maybe five."

"Then we should hurry."

"We *are* hurrying." She paused, and looked down at me with an expression I recognized from mirrors in another life—the look of a person deciding how much truth a stranger can carry. "You understand you're not exactly built for hurrying anymore."

I understood. I understood better than she knew.

Here is what a century of being an anchor does to a man: it does not merely waste him. Wasting is simple. Hunger does wasting; illness does wasting; grief does wasting, given time. What the anchoring did was stranger. The gate's power had run through me for a hundred years like water through a channel, wearing me into the shape of its passage. My bones had gone dense and dark, shot through with iron-colored veins. My skin had taken on a faint metallic sheen in certain lights, which the smugglers pretended not to notice and I pretended not to care about. When I closed my eyes I could still feel the far end of the chain—not the physical chain, which was somewhere behind us in a collapsed cell, but the other kind, the tether that ran from whatever remained of the crown's hunger to whatever remained of me.

I was half man, half mooring-stone. And the question that had begun forming in the barrel, between the herring and the dark, was which half intended to walk to Cinderport.

* * *

On the second day I stood up.

We had stopped in a hollow beneath a stand of wind-bent pines, the crew taking what rest they could while Wren argued quietly with her quartermaster about routes. I had been lying on a bedroll watching the clouds do their slow, indifferent work, and I thought: *enough.*

Getting upright took the better part of ten minutes and cost me more sweat than any battle of my earlier reign, which had been mercifully short on battles. My legs shook. My ankles, where the irons had sat for a century, bore rings of scar tissue smooth and pale as river stone. But I stood. The pine needles were wet under my bare feet—they'd lost my boots somewhere in the camp, along with everything else I owned, which was nothing—and the cold of them went up through me like a bell being struck.

Wren stopped arguing mid-sentence. The whole crew went quiet, the way people go quiet around a fire that has just done something unexpected.

"Well," I said. My voice came out rusty. Most of my voice was rusty; a century of speaking only to yourself gives you a very strange interior narrator and very poor conversational tone. "One foot in front of the other. I remember the principle."

"You don't have to walk," Wren said. "We've got the stretcher. We've got the mule."

"The mule has suffered enough." I took a step. Then another. The ground did not betray me, which felt, after a century of stone floors, like a personal courtesy. "And I have spent one hundred and four years being carried. By chains. By the gate. By other people's decisions." Another step. My knees complained in a language older than the free cities. "I'll walk to the gate or I'll fall down trying, but either way it will be under my own weight. That matters. I don't expect anyone else to understand why it matters."

Wren studied me for a long moment. She had a way of looking at people as though appraising cargo—weight, value, likelihood of spoiling in transit—and I could see the appraisal running now. Whatever she concluded, she kept it off her face.

"It's two days," she said finally. "You'll be slower than the army."

"Then we'd best not stop to argue."

* * *

I want to record, honestly, what walking felt like. Because I suspect no one who hasn't been a mooring post for a hungry god-crown will believe it, and because if I don't write it down now, while my hand still remembers how, I may never manage it.

Walking hurt. That part was ordinary. What was not ordinary was the *pulling*. Every step away from the palace, away from the cell, away from the buried geometry of the binding, put tension on the tether I still felt running through my chest. It wasn't pain exactly. It was more like hearing your name called from a great distance by a voice that knows it shouldn't use your name. The crown knew where I was. The crown had always known where I was. For a hundred years that knowledge had been a comfort of sorts—at least I mattered to something—and now it was a hook, and every mile I walked drove the barb a little deeper.

By the evening of the first day I was leaving bloody footprints.

"Don't," I said, when the young one—Tam, I think his name was, a boy with quick hands and frightened eyes—knelt beside me with bandages. "It's not the feet. The feet are fine. It's the pulling. You can't bandage the pulling."

"What's pulling you?" Tam asked, because the young have not yet learned which questions are rude.

"The same thing that pulled at everyone in that camp," I said. "Only I'm attached to the other end."

He didn't understand. Good. Understanding that particular arrangement was the worst thing that ever happened to me, and I wouldn't wish the curriculum on anyone.

But here is the thing I discovered, somewhere in the second day's walking, and it is the thing that made the whole enterprise bearable: the pull ran both ways.

Every step I took under my own power, the tether strained—and something in the strain *fed back*. Not strength, exactly. Not the gate's cold power, which I had carried so long it had become indistinguishable from my own pulse. Something smaller and warmer than that. It felt like—forgive the comparison, I was a king before I was a fixture, and kings think in terms of ceremony—it felt like a coronation running in reverse. Instead of a crown being lowered onto me, something was being lifted off, one ounce at a time, and each ounce lifted left behind a little more of the man I had been before.

I remembered things. Small things, stupid things, the things captivity takes first. The taste of honeyed pears. The name of my horse. The way my mother hummed when she sewed—off-key, always off-key, and defiant about it. Each mile gave me back a piece, and I hoarded them like a miser, and I walked faster.

"You're smiling," Wren observed, falling into step beside me. "You haven't smiled once since the barrel. I checked."

"I'm remembering my mother's singing."

"That bad?"

"Worse. She'd have been insulted if anyone told her otherwise."

Something almost like warmth crossed Wren's face, quickly suppressed, a smuggler's reflex against sentiment. But she slowed her pace to match mine, and she stayed there, and I understood that this was her version of ceremony too. Everyone has one. The gatekeepers had theirs—binding and blood and sacrifice. The usurper had his—hunger wearing a crown. Wren Yarrow's liturgy was logistics: she expressed devotion by making sure people arrived alive.

I decided, walking beside her, that hers might be the only honest religion left in the Marchlands.

* * *

We came within sight of Cinderport's walls on the third morning, and the sight of them stopped me in a way the pain never had.

Smoke rose beyond the city—a thin haze of cookfires and fear, thousands of refugees packed inside fortifications built for tens of thousands. On the plain below the walls, spread out in the gray light like a stain spreading through cloth, was the vanguard of the usurper's army. Tents. Siege engines half-assembled, their timbers pale as bone. Picket fires. And above it all, at the center of the camp, a standard I remembered from a century ago: black field, white gate, inverted.

Beneath that standard, in a pavilion I could not see but could *feel*, sat the thing that wore my kingdom's crown and my kingdom's king-shaped absence. I felt it the way a missing tooth feels the tongue: constantly, tenderly, against all sense.

"There he is," Tam whispered, beside me. "The Hollow King."

"No," I said.

They all looked at me. Wren, Tam, the quartermaster, the two others whose names I hadn't yet been given the chance to forget. I stood on the ridge with my ruined feet and my iron-shot bones and my century of scars, and I heard myself speak with a voice I hadn't used since before the binding—not the anchor's voice, not the prisoner's voice, but the other one, the one that had once opened sessions of court and named ships and read eulogies.

"He is not a king. Kings are answerable to something—to law, to people, to gods, to memory. He gave up every answer a long time ago. What's left down there is a mouth the crown speaks through, and a stomach the crown feeds through, and whatever poor remnant of the man it ate last is still conscious enough to watch." I looked at the standard a moment longer. "I knew him, you know. Before. He was not always that. He was ambitious and vain and clever, and he loved a woman named Isolde who died young, and I think the crown found the hole her dying left and grew into it like rot into timber. He was my cousin. I mourned him once already. I find I'm having to do it twice, and the second time is harder, because there's no body to bury. Just the shape of one, marching."

Nobody spoke. Somewhere downslope, a siege engine groaned as it was tested, a sound like a giant clearing its throat.

"So," I said, and turned from the plain, and faced the road down to the aqueduct and the sealed stair beneath it and the gate I had been chained to keep shut for a hundred years. "Which way to the threshold? I assume my escort ends at the door."

Wren blinked. "Your—what?"

"My part in this. I'm the third key, or near enough. Royal blood, gate-bound, unchained. Whatever rite the gatekeeper boy is assembling down there, it needs me, and I am done being needed at a distance." I started down the slope. My ankles screamed. I let them. Pain, I had decided somewhere in the fish barrel, was simply the body's ledger balancing, and I had spent a century in credit. "Walk with me if you like. But understand: I'm not cargo anymore. I'm not the rescued king paraded under a standard. I'm going to that gate on my own feet, and whatever happens when I get there—whatever the rite costs, whatever the crown tries to take back—I'll pay it standing up."

Behind me, after a moment, came the sound of boots following. All of them. Even the mule, in spirit.

* * *

The aqueduct stair swallowed us in late afternoon, and I will confess something I have confessed to no one: when the old stone closed overhead and cut off the sky, my first feeling was terror.

A century underground will do that. There are rooms in me that will always be that cell, no matter what gets built on top of them. I felt the walls lean in. I felt the iron in my bones sing a low, answering note to the iron in the stair, and I felt the tether snap taut as a bowstring, because we were close now, close to the gate, close to the crown's oldest wound, and the thing wearing my cousin knew it.

But then, from somewhere below—far below, down past the threshold, up through the stone itself—came a sound.

Singing. Thin, child-sized, slightly off-key.

Odile, I learned later her name was. The tollhouse girl. Singing the gate-hymn the way my mother used to sing while sewing: imperfectly, defiantly, as though the song belonged to her and she'd thank you to remember it.

And I stood on that stair, halfway between the world and the gate, between the man I'd been and the anchor I'd been made, and I laughed. Actually laughed—a cracked, rusted, ridiculous sound that echoed down the shaft and made Tam jump.

"What?" Wren demanded.

"Nothing. Everything." I wiped my eyes with the back of a hand that shook, and steadied it, and started down. "It's just—I've waited a hundred and four years to hear that song sung whole. And it turns out the ones singing it are a fish-smuggler, a ledger-keeper, a child, and a king in bare feet." I took the next step, and the next, toward the light rising from below. "History has a sense of humor after all. It only shows it to the prisoners."

Below us, the hymn swelled. Below us, the gate began to wake.

And far above, beyond the walls, beyond the plain, I felt the crown feel it too—and begin, at last, to move.
