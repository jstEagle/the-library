## 31. Siege Lines

*Seraphine Vail*

I once burned a man in Cinderport for the crime of running.

I remember the arithmetic of it. One heretic, forty minutes of procedure, a pyre built to specification so the smoke would not drift into the residential quarter. I signed the requisition for the oil myself. I was proud of my penmanship.

Now I stood on the same walls where that smoke had risen, holding a command staff I had no right to, and the arithmetic had changed shape entirely. Below us, the Hollow King's vanguard filled the Marchlands like ink filling a spilled cup—pikemen in ranks so deep they stopped being men and became a weather system. Siege engines crawled behind them on wheels the size of millstones. And above it all, faint as a scar against the morning, hung the banner of the crown: a circle of iron, open at the front, waiting for a head.

"Count them again," said General Marrec beside me. He was seventy years old and built like something the sea had given up on drowning. "I want the number wrong twice before I believe it."

"Forty thousand," said the signalman, miserable. "Give or take."

"Give or take," Marrec repeated, as if tasting something spoiled. "And we have?"

"Nine thousand militia. Four hundred guild deserters who claim they're sorry. Two hundred of your former inquisitors, Inquisitor-General, who claim nothing because they're afraid you'll have them flogged."

"They're right to be afraid," I said. "Flogging builds discipline."

Marrec snorted. "You've changed, Vail."

"I've read things," I said. "It's a disease."

The truth was worse than a disease. The truth was that three weeks ago I had knelt in a heretical archive and read, in the crown's own hand, orders describing the extermination of gatekeeper bloodlines as a *maintenance expense*. Line items. My whole faith—the pyres, the procedure, the beautiful clean arithmetic of it—had been someone else's bookkeeping all along. I had not been an instrument of divine order. I had been a clerk.

So now I commanded the defense of Cinderport, and I did it the only way I knew how: like a bureaucrat with a revelation.

"Wall sections one through four," I said, walking the line of officers. "Ballista crews fire on my mark, not before. The aqueduct district is evacuated?" A nod. "The lower stair is sealed and guarded by people who will die before they open it?" Another nod, slower. "Good. If anyone wearing guild colors approaches that stair, they are to be treated as enemy combatants. If anyone wearing *my* former colors approaches it, the same." I looked at each face in turn. "We are not defending the city. Understand me. We are buying time for three people to walk down a staircase. Everything else is expenditure."

A young officer—Millet, I think, the one who still flinched when I raised my voice—shifted his weight. "And if the three people don't reach the gate?"

"Then the walls were always going to fall," I said, "and we might as well fall loudly."

Below us, the first horns sounded. Not the war-horns of an army—that would come later—but the thin, sweet note of the crown's heralds, offering surrender. They offered it every dawn. Terms: open the gates, deliver the heretics, kneel, be fed.

Every dawn, Marrec answered the same way. He leaned over the parapet and shouted, in a voice that carried across half a mile of dead grass:

"THE FREE CITIES KNEEL TO NO ONE WHO WEARS A CROWN THAT EATS HIM!"

It was not diplomacy. It was, however, very good for morale, and morale was the only resource we had in surplus.

The heralds withdrew. The horns changed key. And the ink below us began to move.

*Xanthe*

Here is what nobody tells you about the end of the world: there is a great deal of waiting in it.

Wren and I crouched in the flooded cellar of a dyer's shop three streets from the aqueduct, and we waited, and Odile slept against my shoulder with her thumb in her mouth, humming. Even asleep she hummed. The hymn came out of her in fragments, half-swallowed, like a song remembered from before she could remember anything. Anselm had said—Anselm, who was two days dead and whose death I was carrying around inside me like a stone I kept forgetting I'd swallowed and then remembering, every time, fresh—"the child doesn't know the hymn, Keeper. The hymn knows the child. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

Above us, the walls of Cinderport shook. Dust sifted down through the floorboards in gray threads. Somewhere north, a trebuchet stone found its range, and the whole street jumped, and Odile woke, and did not cry, which frightened me more than crying would have.

"Is it time?" she asked.

"Not yet."

"You said that last time."

"I'm a ledger-keeper," I said. "Repetition is kind of my whole thing."

She considered this with the grave skepticism of a seven-year-old who has watched too many adults lie badly. Then she put her head back down and went to sleep, and started humming again within a minute, and I sat there in the dark with a dead man's hymn in my ears and thought about arithmetic.

That was the day's lesson, if you wanted one. Courage in the shadow of arithmetic. Vail had used the phrase—I'd heard her say it on the wall, before she sent us down here, in that flat inquisitor's voice that made even inspiration sound like a tax assessment. Nine thousand against forty thousand. Hours bought with lives, priced per life, spent without receipt. I had spent ten years learning to make numbers behave. Nothing had prepared me for numbers like these.

"Wren," I whispered.

"Mm." She was cleaning her crossbow for the fourth time. It didn't need it. It was a ritual, like prayer, except useful.

"When we get to the stair. If it comes to it. You don't have to—"

"If you finish that sentence," she said pleasantly, "I'll shoot you myself and save the inquisitors the trouble."

"—I was going to say *thank you*, actually."

She paused. In the dark I couldn't see her face, but I felt her look at me anyway; Wren's looks had weight, like a hand on the shoulder.

"You're welcome," she said. "Now shut up and count stones."

I counted stones. It was what I knew how to do. Each impact on the wall above, I marked—a scratch in the dust with my grandfather's stylus, one stroke per five—and somewhere past the two-hundredth stroke the rhythm changed, and Wren's head came up at the same moment mine did, because we had both heard it: not the boom of stone on stone but a long, tearing groan, wood and mortar and something older underneath, the sound a wall makes when it decides, privately, that it has done enough.

"The east curtain," Wren breathed. "They've cracked the east curtain."

The hymn in Odile's sleep stuttered. Changed key. Went on.

I got up. My legs were shaking and I let them, because shaking legs still work, that was another thing Anselm taught me—fear isn't the opposite of courage, fear is the raw material of it, you just have to spend it rather than hoard it.

"Odile," I said softly. "Time."

*Aldric*

They moved me in a gilded litter, which was the cruelest thing they had ever done, and they had done many cruel things.

For a century I had known the dimensions of a cell: nine paces by six, one wall warm from some furnace on the far side, one drain that sang at night. I had measured my world in paces the way other men measure it in years. Now the world was sky, and banners, and the smell of horses and cut grass and the sea beyond the Marchlands, and it was all a stage set erected around a prop.

Me. The rescued king. Displayed at the head of the usurper's column so that any free city looking out from its walls would see the true King of the Realm riding in chains beneath the crown's banner, and despair, and open their gates.

It nearly worked. Looking down from the litter at the walls of Cinderport—walls I had walked as a young man, when they were new and I was new to my own name—I felt the despair rise in me like floodwater. *Look what they did to him,* the sight said. *Look what resistance buys.*

But then the wind turned, and off the walls came singing.

Thin, ragged, thousands of voices not quite together—the militia, the fishwives, the dockhands, singing the gate-hymn. Badly. Off-key. The way you sing a thing you learned from your grandmother who learned it from hers, a thing worn smooth by generations of mouths that didn't understand it and loved it anyway.

The crown in the usurper's tent behind me went silent. I felt it go silent, the way you feel a held breath. It knew that song. It had eaten the people who wrote it, and it remembered the taste, and it was afraid.

I lay back among my gilt cushions, in my chains, under the eyes of a hundred guards, and I smiled.

Let them display me. Let them parade their anchor. Every soldier in that column who glanced at the old man in the litter and saw not a symbol but a person—hollowed, ruined, *still there*—was a soldier doing my work for me. Hope doesn't need to be loud. Hope just needs to be witnessed.

"Hold," I whispered, to the walls, to the singers, to whoever was left to hear. "Hold until the boy reaches the door."

Because I had dreamed him again, the night before. The boy with the iron stylus, standing in a dark place full of water-sound, singing my name. A century in a cage teaches you patience the way fire teaches wood. I could wait. Waiting was the one art they had never managed to burn out of me.

The litter swayed. The army marched. Above the walls, the hymn held.

*Xanthe*

The east curtain fell at midday, and the sound of it followed us through the streets like a debt collector.

Stone coming down is a specific sound. Not thunder—thunder ends. This went on and on, a rolling avalanche roar punctuated by the smaller, worse sounds of things that had been houses, and after it came the silence, and after the silence came the horns, and after the horns came the screaming, and we ran through all of it with Odile between us, Wren's hand locked on the girl's wrist, mine on Wren's belt, a chain of three moving through streets that were coming apart at the seams.

Vail's plan had accounted for this. Of course it had; the woman planned like a siege engine herself. The breach at the east curtain was expected, and the response was already in motion: militia companies folding back along the canals, giving ground on purpose, trading streets for minutes, funneling the crown's infantry into killing lanes where the ballistae from the inner wall could reach them. It was beautiful, in the way ledgers are beautiful—all balance, no mercy. Every minute we gained was written down somewhere in blood.

"Left!" Wren barked, hauling Odile into a tanner's alley as a squad of inquisitor outriders clattered past the mouth of it. She pressed us both flat against the reeking wall and watched the riders pass with an expression I can only describe as professional appreciation. "Good formation," she muttered. "Hate them."

"How far?" I gasped.

"Two streets. Maybe less, if the aqueduct bridge is still standing."

It was not still standing. We came around the corner onto the canal and the bridge was a row of broken teeth, and the aqueduct's great arch—the one that hid the sealed stair, the one Anselm had died to keep unguarded—was framed against smoke like a doorway in a burning house.

Between us and it: perhaps sixty yards of open embankment, and across that embankment, moving fast, a wedge of guild hunters in Corvane's colors, driving toward the same archway.

Corvane's men. The guild that raised me. The guild that had sold my family's names for a century, entry by entry, and called it bookkeeping.

Wren saw them at the same instant I did. I felt her start to calculate—she was good at it, better than me, the grim smuggler's math of odds and exits—and I felt her arrive at the same answer I did, which was *no path*, and then I felt her begin, with terrifying calm, to construct one anyway.

"Odile," she said, crouching to the girl's level, quick and gentle, a thief handling the one treasure she never stole. "Do you remember the game? The one from the salt road? Where you hold my hand and run exactly as fast as you can and never look back?"

"Yes."

"It's that game. Right now. Ready?"

Odile nodded. And then, small and clear, over the roar of a dying city, she began to hum—not fragments now, not sleep-mumbled pieces, but the hymn, whole and rising, a thread of melody pulled tight across the chaos.

The effect was not dramatic. That's what I would tell people afterward, and none of them believed me. It wasn't dramatic. It was *directional*. The nearest hunters faltered, turned their heads, lost a half-second of purpose, as though hearing their mothers call from a house that had burned down years ago. A half-second. Wren spent it like a coin.

"Now."

We ran.

Sixty yards of open ground, with arrows in the air and the hymn pouring out of a seven-year-old girl and the wall of my whole former life crumbling behind me. I remember it in flashes: Wren's braid whipping. A bolt striking the embankment a hand's width from my foot. Odile's small fingers crushing mine. The archway growing, growing—

A hunter stepped out of the arch itself. Big, guild-trained, crossbow leveled at Odile.

Wren didn't slow down. She did the arithmetic faster than he did—one body, one shot, three targets, choose—and she chose, and she twisted, and the bolt meant for the child took her high in the chest instead, and she made a sound I will hear for the rest of my ordinary mortal life, and she did not stop running, because Wren does not stop running, that is the whole of her creed.

I caught her. We went through the arch together, all three of us, tangled and bleeding and singing—no, not me, I couldn't carry the tune, it was all Odile, it had always been all Odile—and behind us the hunters reached the threshold of the arch and stopped as if they'd struck glass, because the hymn was holding there, holding, holding, the way Anselm had held it, the way keepers hold things.

Inside: darkness, water-sound, stairs going down.

"Wren." I lowered her against the wall. Blood between my fingers, too much of it. "Stay with me. Stay—"

Her eyes found mine, and even white-faced and breathless, she managed the ghost of her usual crooked smile. "Told you," she whispered. "Logistics."

Below us, in the deep of the earth, something ancient heard the hymn sung whole for the first time in a hundred years.

And began, very slowly, to wake.
