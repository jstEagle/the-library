## 34. The Unbinding

The gate opened the way a wound opens: slowly, and then all at once.

I had expected light. Everyone expects light. What came instead was a sound—low, iron-deep, like a bell struck underwater—and then the stair beneath us stopped being a stair. The stone went soft as loam under my boots, and the walls peeled back into darkness that was not empty but *attentive*. Odile's hand found mine without either of us deciding it should. Her hymn had not faltered once in three days of singing, and now, at the threshold, it rose to meet the bell-sound the way a stream rises to meet a river.

The chamber beyond was round, and old, and wrong in the way that only very honest things are wrong. No torches. No windows. The dark itself gave off a faint grey luminescence, like moonlight remembered rather than seen. And at the center stood the gate.

I want to describe it properly, because everything I'd been told about it—the ledgers, the prophecies, Anselm's dying lessons—had all described it wrong. It was not a door. Doors imply an elsewhere. This was more like a scar in the world, a seam where something vast had been stitched shut, and the stitching was iron: great bands of black metal crossing each other in a lattice, each band engraved with names. Thousands of names. Tens of thousands. I knew before I touched them whose they were.

Gatekeepers. Every one who had ever held the watch. My family's whole history, written in a metal that did not rust because it had no interest in time.

"Xanthe." Wren's voice behind me, low. She had her crossbow up, though what she planned to shoot in here I couldn't guess. "Tell me you feel that."

"The floor humming?"

"The floor is *listening*."

She wasn't wrong. The whole chamber had the quality of a held breath. Somewhere above us, muffled by forty feet of stone and aqueduct, the Battle of the Aqueduct was deciding whether Cinderport would stand or fall. I could hear it faintly—a rhythm of impacts, screams ground down to texture. Vail was holding the streets. Anselm had died holding the threshold hymn so we could get this far. Corvane had died holding nothing but a burning contract. There was a great deal of death stacked behind me like cordwood, and every piece of it said the same thing: *finish it.*

I set my grandfather's iron stylus against the nearest band of the lattice.

It sank in like a pen into wet vellum.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about performing a century-overdue unbinding rite: it is mostly bookkeeping.

Anselm had warned me. "You'll expect thunder," he'd said, on his last night, when his cough had gone from bad to architectural. "You'll expect the sky to split. But binding is a ledger-work, Xanthe. A gate is an account, kept balanced between one side and the other. Unbinding is just... closing the account. You'll need every name. You'll need to read them all aloud, and mean them, and not stop when it hurts." He'd smiled with most of his face. "It will hurt."

So I sang. Or rather—I recited, because I cannot carry a tune in a bucket, and the gate, mercifully, did not care. The rite was words, and the words were names, and the names were written along the iron bands in a script I could suddenly read, the way you can suddenly read your own handwriting in a dream. I put my stylus to the first name and spoke it, and the iron drank the syllable, and somewhere far below us something enormous shifted its weight.

Odile harmonized. She didn't know she was doing it; her eyes were closed and her small voice threaded through mine like wire through cloth. Wren stood at the chamber mouth with her crossbow and her jaw set, watching the dark behind us for whatever the crown would send next.

For perhaps a hundred names, it worked.

Then the crown noticed us.

I felt it arrive the way you feel a change in weather—not a sound but a pressure, a dropping in the ears. The grey luminescence dimmed. The humming in the floor curdled. And out of the dark between the iron bands, the crown began to speak.

Not with the Hollow King's voice. With others'.

"Xanthe?"

My stylus stopped.

I knew that voice. Of course I knew that voice—or thought I did, the way you know a face from a portrait painted after the sitter died. It was my mother's voice, or the memory of it, worn smooth by eleven years of forgetting. I was four years old when they died. Four. Whatever I remembered of her was mostly invention: a smell of soap, a laugh I might have borrowed from someone else's mother entirely.

But the voice coming out of the dark was not an invention. It was precise. It was *hers*, restored, polished, presented to me like a gift.

"You've grown," it said, warm and wondering. "Look how tall. Your father said you would be. He's here too—he's right beside me—we've been waiting such a long time for you to find this place."

"Don't," Wren said quietly. She hadn't turned around. "Don't answer it."

"It knows things," I said. My voice came out flat, which was the only way I could keep it level. "It ate a century of gatekeepers. It has their voices. All of them."

"Then it has a hundred dead people's mouths," Wren said, "and not one living heart among them. Keep singing."

I looked at the next name on the band. *Yarrow, Maren.* My grandmother. I put the stylus to it and spoke it, and the iron drank it, and the dark said:

"That's my mother's name," the voice said gently. "Your grandmother Maren. She's here too, sweetheart. They're all here. That's what no one told you—that's what the gate never says. When a keeper binds a name into the iron, the name doesn't vanish. It goes *down*. Down to where the crown can reach. We're not gone, Xanthe. We're held. And if you finish this rite—if you close the account—" the warmth cracked, just slightly, like ice underfoot, "—you'll close us with it. You'll let go of the rope and everyone holding it falls. Is that what your books taught you? Is that what the old monk died teaching you? To drop your own family into the dark?"

And there it was. Not thunder. Not armies. Just arithmetic—the kind I was raised on, the kind I was good at. Debits and credits. The crown had finally found the currency I understood, and it was spending it freely.

Because here is the thing, the shameful thing, the thing I will admit now because the dark already heard it: for three full seconds, I believed it. Three seconds standing in that chamber with my mother's voice pouring honey into my ear, and I thought, *of course, of course it's true, why wouldn't it be true, why would anything I love ever come free?*

Three seconds. Then Odile squeezed my hand.

She hadn't stopped singing. Not once. Her hymn ran under the crown's sweetness like a keel under water, steady, unimpressed. And I realized she had heard every word the dark was saying, and she was four years older than I had been when my parents died, and she was still singing.

"Odile," I said. "Do you hear it? The voices?"

Her eyes opened. She looked at the dark with the flat, unfooled gaze of a child who had already survived the worst day of her life in a tollhouse full of her family's blood. "It's lying," she said simply. "Grown-ups lie like that when they want something. It wants you to stop."

*It wants you to stop.*

Out of the mouths of babes, etcetera. I laughed—one short, ugly bark of a laugh—and put the stylus back to the iron.

*Yarrow, Maren,* I said again, and meant it, and moved to the next name.

* * *

The crown escalated. Of course it did. It was, after all, a collector—it had devoured the memories of every keeper who had ever opposed it, and it began spending them like a drunkard spends his last coin.

My grandfather's voice. My grandmother's. A woman I didn't recognize who called me *little ledger-man* in a tone of such specific affection that I had to grip the stylus until my knuckles went white. Then strangers. Then worse than strangers: scenes. The dark stopped talking and started *showing*.

I saw my parents' house. Not as I'd imagined it—as it was. The crooked shelf. The chipped blue bowl on the table. My father laughing at something, head thrown back, and my mother rolling her eyes with love so plain it hurt to look at, and a small boy—me, four years old, fat-cheeked and solemn—stacking ledger pages into a tower while they weren't looking. I saw the night they signed the debt. I saw my mother's hands shaking as she wrote, and my father's arm around her, and the gate's presence over them like weather, and I heard my mother say, in that same voice the crown was wearing, *"Whatever it costs. Whatever it costs him. He lives."*

"They sold their future for yours," the dark murmured, gentle as a moneylender. "And now you'd spend it. Their one purchase. Their whole estate, and you'd burn the deed. Sing, little keeper. Sing, and orphan yourself a second time."

I kept singing.

I am not going to pretend that was brave. Bravery implies a choice made cleanly, and I made no clean choices in that chamber. I sang because Odile was singing. I sang because Wren was standing guard over a boy she'd known for a season, for reasons she'd never once explained, and if she could do that without being asked, I could do this. I sang because Anselm had told me the truth about the rite and I had believed him: *it will hurt, and you must not stop when it hurts.* Endurance, he'd said, is the highest form of love. Anyone can love in the easy hours. Love is what keeps your mouth moving when your throat is full of glass.

Two hundred names. Three hundred. The iron drank them faster now, eager, the lattice beginning to glow along its seams like a banked fire waking. The bell-sound underneath everything deepened. Above us, the battle's rhythm changed—harder, closer, the stone dust sifting down between the iron bands. Someone was trying to break through to us. Vail's militia or the crown's inquisitors; I couldn't tell, and I didn't have the luxury of caring.

Four hundred names. The crown stopped pretending.

The voices came all at once—a hundred mouths, a thousand, every keeper it had ever eaten, screaming, pleading, cursing, blessing, a flood tide of the dead all speaking through one hungry mouth. *Stop. Stop. Stop. You are killing us. You are unmaking us. We are in the iron and the iron is going down and you are the one pulling the rope—*

And underneath it, threaded through it, my mother's voice one final time, and this time it wasn't sweet:

*"Xanthe Yarrow. Look at me."*

I looked. Because I was always going to look; I was my mother's son, and she knew it, and the crown knew it, and knowing that is exactly how traps work.

The dark showed me her. Standing in the grey light, whole, real, reaching out one hand. Behind her my father. Behind them a crowd of faces going back and back, generations of Yarrows, the whole drowned ledger of my blood, all of them reaching, all of them saying the same word in a chorus that broke my heart into pieces small enough to swallow:

*"Wait."*

And Odile's voice cut through it—clear, furious, four feet of absolute certainty:

*"IT'S NOT THEM! IT'S NEVER BEEN THEM! THEY'RE NOT DOWN THERE, THEY'RE IN YOU, THAT'S WHY IT'S AFRAID OF YOU!"*

Silence. Total, ringing silence, except for the hymn.

And in that silence I understood what she'd said, really understood it, the way you understand a figure only when you total the column. The crown eats memories. That was its nature, its hunger, its whole terrible economy. But memories eaten are not memories destroyed—they're *held*. Possessed. And possession cuts both ways, doesn't it? Everything the crown had devoured, it carried. Every keeper it had killed lived inside it, indigestible, a century of swallowed grief sitting in its stomach like stones.

It wasn't showing me my parents. It was showing me *its own hoard*—and it was afraid, because every name I sang pulled another stone out of its belly and set it free.

"You're not holding them," I told the dark. My voice sounded strange to me—calm, almost amused, the voice I used for balancing impossible accounts. "They're holding *you*. They've been holding you for a hundred years. And I'm here to close the account."

I put the stylus to the five-hundredth name.

* * *

The rest happened fast, the way endings do.

Names six hundred through nine hundred took minutes. The lattice blazed now, every seam lit, the whole gate glowing like a forge door, and the bell-sound had become a chord, and Odile's hymn had become the melody riding on top of it, and I was no longer reading the names so much as *releasing* them—each syllable a lock turning, each lock a keeper stepping out of the iron and into whatever comes after. I could feel them go. Like weights leaving a scale. Like a ship shedding ballast and rising.

The crown screamed. Not with voices anymore—with its own voice, the Hollow King's voice, and beneath that another voice, older, and beneath *that* something that was barely a voice at all, just hunger given syntax. The chamber shook. Dust fell in sheets. From the stair behind us came shouting—boots, steel, Wren's crossbow snapping twice, three times, her voice snarling *"NOT TODAY"* at whoever was coming down—and I did not turn around. Anselm's last lesson rang in my skull: *the one who sings the final name cannot stop to fight. If you turn, you start over. If you start over, everyone dies tired.*

So I sang.

Nine hundred and ninety-nine names. One left.

And there, at the end of the last band, smaller than all the others, fresher, the engraving still sharp-edged after eleven years because grief keeps its own records well:

*Yarrow, Edran. Yarrow, Sefa. Debt assumed: one life, held in trust.*

My parents. Their signature on my survival. The bill the gate had been keeping for me since I was four years old.

I put the stylus to it, and my hand shook, and I let it shake, because some things should cost.

"I know what this says," I told the gate, and the dark, and whatever was listening above and below. "I know what they paid. I'm not paying it back. I'm paying it *forward*. There's a difference. Ask any ledger-keeper."

I spoke their names.

The gate opened.

Not the scar—the *truth* of it. The iron lattice folded back like hands opening, and beyond it was not darkness but depth, a going-down that was also a going-in, and at the bottom of it, faint as a star reflected in a well, something stirred that had been bound and starving for a hundred years. The true king's anchor. The seal's heart. The thing the whole rotten century had been built to imprison.

And rising up the stair behind me, heavy as a landslide, slow as a dying man—which was exactly what he was—came footsteps. Aldric. Walking to his own gate under his own power, as he'd promised, as Wren had bought with her crew's blood, as the whole broken century had been waiting for.

The crown felt him coming. I felt the crown feel it—a convulsion in the dark, rage and terror braided together, and then the sound of the chamber door above bursting open, and firelight spilling down the stair, and a shape descending that was less a man than a *wearing*—a hollow thing in a gold circlet, dragging its hunger behind it like a bridal train.

Wren's voice, ragged: "Xanthe—!"

Odile's hymn, unwavering, holding the threshold.

Aldric's footsteps, patient, ruined, unstoppable.

And the gate, open at last, waiting to see what I would do with the only choice that had ever really been mine.

The crown came down the stairs toward me, and I stepped forward to meet it.
