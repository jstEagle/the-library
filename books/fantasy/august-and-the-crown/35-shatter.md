## 35. Shatter

The crown was singing.

That's the thing no one tells you about power — it doesn't roar. It *sings*. A thin, sweet thread of a sound, threading through the gate-hymn like rot through fruit, and I could hear it getting into everything: into Odile's voice, into Anselm's memory of the rite, into my own heartbeat, which had begun keeping time with something that wasn't mine.

I kept singing.

My voice had gone to gravel somewhere around the third verse. The hymn is not short. The gatekeepers who wrote it clearly never intended for one man to sing all the parts, and they were right, and here I was anyway, doing exactly what they'd feared, because the alternative was letting the thing wearing the Hollow King's face finish its walk across the chamber.

It moved slowly. That was the horror of it. Not a charge, not a lunge — a *procession*. The way you'd walk toward something you'd already decided belonged to you. Its boots made no sound on the iron floor, and where it stepped, the old gate-script dimmed, letters guttering like candles in a draft.

"The ledger balances," it said, and the voice was three voices braided together — the usurper's, and beneath that older ones, devoured ones, a choir of everyone the crown had ever eaten. "You are owed, little keeper. Take what is yours."

"I'm familiar with the offer," I sang, because you can't stop mid-verse without starting over, and I did not have time to start over. "The answer hasn't changed."

"It will." Almost gentle. Almost kind. And underneath the kindness, so faint I might have imagined it, something else — a flicker, a stutter in the braid of voices, like a man drowning inside his own mouth. "We have your mother's voice. Would you like to hear how she sang this verse? She learned it from her mother, who learned it from hers. We keep them all. Nothing given to us is ever lost."

The hymn wavered in my throat.

*Don't*, said the sensible part of me, the part raised on ledgers, the part that knew every bargain has fine print. *Nothing offered free at the peak of a negotiation is free.*

But oh, I wanted it. You cannot understand unless you've spent twenty years being nobody — unless you've catalogued bottled storms and borrowed years while the world walked past you like weather — to suddenly be offered the one thing money can't buy. My mother's voice. My father's laugh. A single minute of conversation with people I'd built out of grief and guesswork because the guild never told me anything except that they were dead.

"Later," the crown said, reading me the way Corvane used to read a debtor's face. "After. Take us, and we will give you back every year the gate took. Every name. We are very good at keeping things, Xanthe Yarrow. It is the only thing we have ever been good at."

And there it was. The truth under the sales pitch, delivered by accident or by some last drowned fragment of the man inside: *keeping*. The crown didn't give. It collected. Every gift it promised was already in its vault, taken from someone else, and the interest was always paid in the same coin.

I thought about the third ledger. A century of payments, signed in my family's blood-ink, keeping a gate *sealed and starving*. I thought about Odile humming in her sleep. About Wren bleeding on the stair behind me because she'd decided, with no arithmetic whatsoever to justify it, that I was worth a crossbow bolt. About Anselm, who had known he would die for this and come anyway, folding his hands like a man settling in for evening prayer.

Everyone in my life had paid something for me. My parents with their lives. Wren with her blood. Anselm with everything. The guild with whatever passed, in Corvane's heart, for love.

I was done being an account that other people settled.

So I did the one thing the crown could not model, because nothing in its century of devoured memories contained it.

I stopped singing.

Not faltered. *Stopped.* Mid-verse, hands still moving through the rite's gestures, I let the hymn drop out of my throat entirely — and reached instead for the one skill I had that no prophecy, no crown, no gate had ever thought to value.

I read the terms.

The gate-chamber blazed around me, script crawling up the walls like ivy made of light, and I looked at it the way I'd looked at ten thousand contracts in the Gilded Threshold hall. What is being exchanged. Who bears the risk. Where the penalty clauses hide. And there — woven through the rite like a second signature — I saw it. The clause the gatekeepers had built in a century ago and never used, because using it required a keeper willing to stand inside the exchange itself.

*The crown may be unmade by the hand that refuses it.*

Refuses. Not resists. Not fights. Refuses — declines the transaction entirely, walks away from the counter with both parties' goods still on the table, and breaks the instrument of exchange against the anvil of the gate. The gatekeepers hadn't failed to finish their work a hundred years ago because they lacked strength. They'd failed because they couldn't stop wanting. Wanting the power. Wanting the protection. Wanting to be the ones who saved the world, and be thanked for it.

The crown had been waiting a century for someone who wanted nothing.

"You're lying," the Hollow King said, and for the first time the braid of voices frayed, and I heard just one voice underneath, ragged and human and *terrified*. "You need it. Everyone needs it. We watched a hundred years of them come down these stairs and every one of them needed—"

"That's the difference," I said. "They came down needing. I came down owing."

And I walked out of the circle.

The hymn screamed. Actually screamed — the gate itself protesting, the script flaring white, Odile crying out behind me as the rite bucked like a living thing. Every instinct I owned told me to step back into the pattern, to finish the song, to do the safe completed thing. Walking out of a rite mid-verse felt like walking out of a contract mid-signature, and every guild-trained nerve in my body revolted.

I kept walking.

The Hollow King raised a hand — and hesitated. Because to strike me was to damage the vessel it wanted; to wait was to let me reach the gate's central pillar, the great iron spine where the first key had been set a century ago and where, according to every devoured memory it possessed, every keeper before me had stopped and turned and *taken*.

"Xanthe." One voice now. Just the usurper's, worn thin as old paper. "What are you doing?"

"My job," I said. "For once, exactly my job."

I put my hand on the pillar.

Iron, cold as a debt unpaid, and beneath my palm the whole gate woke — I felt it looking at me, felt it weigh me the way I'd weighed ten thousand trades, and I spoke to it in the only language I trusted.

"I decline," I said. "Both offers. Yours and its. No power. No protection. No crown, no gift, no place in any prophecy. I want my mother's voice more than I have ever wanted anything, and I am not going to buy it with someone else's soul. Close the account. All of them. Every debt this thing has opened in a hundred years — I am refusing collection on behalf of every keeper who came before me, and if that costs me everything, then bill me, because I have been poor my whole life and I know how to survive it."

The silence lasted three heartbeats.

Then the gate *laughed* — a vast, grinding, iron joy, the sound of a mechanism engaging after a century of rust — and the light went through me like winter water, and I understood, in the half-second I had left, why the gatekeepers had written the clause and buried it. Because it only worked once. Because it required a keeper who meant it. And because meaning it cost precisely everything.

The crown was screaming now, all its voices at once, a hundred devoured souls howling as the gate's light found the seam the rite had spent itself opening. I tore my hand off the pillar, crossed the chamber in four strides, and seized the thing wearing the Hollow King.

It fought me. Of course it fought me. It showed me my parents' faces, my grandfather's stylus, the guildhall kitchen where a cook named Marta had slipped me extra bread for twenty years without ever once saying she cared. It showed me the whole inventory of my small life and offered to sell it back to me forever.

"I know," I told it, gripping the crown with both hands, iron biting into my palms. "I know exactly what I'm refusing. I've read the whole ledger."

And I broke it against the gate.

The sound was not loud. That's what I'll remember — that the death of a century of tyranny sounded like a bell cracking, one clean note that bent wrong and stopped. The crown came apart in my hands like ice, like ash, like a contract burned in a brazier, and everything it had eaten came out of it at once —

Light. Faces. Voices. A flood of the devoured pouring upward through the chamber, and among them, for one impossible moment, a woman turning to look at me with my own eyes, and a man beside her with my stubborn chin, and neither of them said anything, because the dead don't get dialogue, but the woman *smiled*, and that was enough, that was more than enough, that was a lifetime's worth of enough compressed into a single second, and then they were gone, upward, outward, home.

The Hollow King folded.

There was no monster left when the light finished passing through him. There was a man — old beyond his years, hollowed out, a century of captivity and consumption leaving him like water leaving a cracked jug. He went down to his knees on the iron floor, and I caught him, because someone should, and he weighed almost nothing.

"Who—" His voice was a ruin. His eyes, though. His eyes were clear for the first time in a hundred years, and they were ordinary eyes, tired and wet and human. "Who are you?"

"Nobody," I said. "Ledger-keeper. Formerly."

He laughed, and it turned into coughing, and I held him while he shook. Behind us the gate-script was fading, the great mechanism winding down, its work done, its purpose discharged. Somewhere above, muffled through a hundred feet of stone, the battle at the aqueduct had gone strangely quiet.

"They told me," the dying man whispered, "at the end, when I could still hear myself... they told me if I was ever freed, I should say thank you. To whoever did it. That it would matter. That saying it would..." He trailed off, gathered himself. "Thank you."

"You're welcome," I said, because what else is there?

"I wanted it, you know. At the start. That's the worst part. Nobody made me." His hand found my wrist, grip faint as a moth landing. "Don't let them make you into a story where you didn't want it either. You wanted your mother's voice. I saw. It nearly worked."

"I know."

"But you counted the cost." Something like wonder crossed the ruin of his face. "A hundred years, and you're the first one who counted."

His grip loosened. I held on anyway. Around us the chamber dimmed to embers, and Odile's voice started up somewhere behind me — small and clear and unafraid, singing the hymn's final verse alone, not as a rite now but as a lullaby, the way it had probably been sung before anyone thought to build gates or crowns or centuries of hunger.

The Hollow King died listening to it.

I stayed there a long time. Long enough that footsteps came down the stair — Wren, grey-faced, arm bound, leaning on the wall, taking in the scattered ash of the crown, the body in my arms, the darkened gate.

"Well," she said. "That's one impossibility off the books."

"Wren—"

"Don't." She came and sat down next to me on the cold iron, shoulder against mine, and looked at the dead king with something like respect. "He gets a proper burial. Above ground. Sunlight. I'll arrange it."

"He asked me to tell you—" I stopped. Started again. "He said thank you. Before the end. He said it mattered."

Wren was quiet for a moment. "Then it mattered," she said finally.

I should have felt triumphant. I kept waiting for it — the swell, the vindication, the chapter-ending chord. Instead what I felt was lighter and stranger and worse: I felt *ordinary*. The gate's hum, which had been in my bones since the night I opened that prophecy book, was gone. The script on the walls was just scratches now. When Odile finished her verse, I realized I could no longer hear the note underneath it, the deep harmonic that had always been there for a keeper's ears.

The door of the gift had closed. And standing outside it, I discovered I didn't regret the price — I grieved it, the way you grieve a country you can never return to, but I didn't regret it. Some accounts you settle gladly.

"Can you stand?" Wren asked.

I tried. Mostly succeeded. Together we got the old king lifted between us, and began the long climb up out of the earth, toward the noise of a city still fighting, toward daylight, toward whatever came after the end of the world's longest-running contract.

Halfway up the stair, Odile tugged my sleeve.

"Xanthe," she said. "The gate. Before it went quiet. It said something. Only to me."

I looked down at her. "What did it say?"

She frowned, working to remember exactly, the way she remembered hymns.

"It said: *the last payment clears at dawn.*"

* * *

Above us, through the aqueduct's broken arches, Cinderport was burning in places and holding in others, and dawn was still hours away, and I had a terrible feeling I knew exactly which account the gate had been talking about.
