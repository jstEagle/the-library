## 36. The Bill Comes Due

I have kept ledgers long enough to know that the worst entries are never the large ones.

Anyone can survive a number with a lot of zeros in it. You flinch, you swear, you pour a drink, and then you start carving it up into payments you can pretend to manage. No — the entries that ruin you are the small ones. The ones you signed without reading. The ones in your own handwriting that you don't remember writing. The ones where the column marked *paid* was filled in by someone else, a century before you were born, and you only find out when the collector comes knocking.

The gate came knocking.

I want to tell this part properly, because I suspect — and here my professional instincts assert themselves even now, even after everything — that no one else will ever record it accurately. Wren saw the aftermath. Odile saw the light and spent three days refusing to describe it, which from Odile is its own kind of testimony. Aldric saw nothing; he was on his knees by then, a man unburdened of a century and too busy weeping to audit anything. So the account falls to me, and I am, whatever else has been taken from me, still an accountant.

Here is what happened, as best as I can render it in figures.

* * *

The crown broke against the gate's iron with a sound I can only describe as *wrong* — not loud so much as final, the way a ledger closes. Not slammed. Closed. Every devoured voice inside it went out at once, and the silence that followed had texture, like felt laid over a bell.

The Hollow King came apart. Not dramatically — there was no pillar of fire, no scream that cracked stone. He simply became less. The crown's hunger let go of him the way a fist lets go of a bird, all at once, and what remained was a thin old man in ruined robes who folded down onto the threshold stones like laundry off a line. His lips moved. I leaned close, because some part of me — the part that had sung the rite until my throat bled — needed to hear whether it was a curse.

It was *thank you*. Barely a breath. Then he was gone, and I mean gone entirely, not dead but *unwritten*, the way a debt forgiven leaves no trace in the book at all. One moment a body. The next, clean stone.

Aldric made a sound I will not describe, because it belongs to him.

And then the gate turned to me.

I had known it would. Anselm told me — *the rite does not end, Xanthe, it settles* — and I had nodded like a man agreeing to weather he'd already decided to walk through. But knowing a bill is coming and reading it are different trades entirely.

It did not speak. Gates don't. What it did was open — not the way it had opened for our three bloodlines singing whole, but inward, into itself, and I understood that I was being shown the ledger. My ledger. The third ledger's great-grandmother, the original, the one my family had been keeping payments into for four generations without ever once seeing the other side of the account.

And there it was. All of it.

My survival, age six, bought when the crown's purge reached my parents' door. Paid for — I could see the entry, could read it the way I read guild script, fluently, helplessly — paid for with a lien against their son's gift. The gatekeeping sense, the hearing-of-the-hymn, the thing I hadn't even known I had until a prophecy book cracked my life open: held in trust. Collateral. And interest accruing, because gates are patient the way rivers are patient, and patience compounds.

*Paid in full,* said the gate, in the way that stone says things, which is to say by simply being true where I could feel it. *Or assumed.*

That was the choice. That was always the choice, and I understand now that it had never once been a choice between good and evil, between taking the crown's power or refusing it. That was the shape the temptation wore so I would recognize it as temptation. The real terms were underneath, and they were these:

Assume the debt, and become what the crown had been — a keeper of the gate sustained by it, fed by it, hollowed by it, a mouthpiece with a ledger instead of a crown. Or settle the account, and pay with the collateral.

Pay with the gift. Pay with the years the gift had quietly been lending me — because yes, that too was in the fine print, and I felt a cold professional horror as I read it, because I had wondered, idly, why my hair refused to gray while Corvane's went white at thirty-five. I had thought myself merely fortunate. I had *priced myself* as merely fortunate. There is a special shame in discovering you've been auditing your own life and missing the largest line item.

And pay with my place in the prophecy. The name beside the date. *The gate opens.* If the gate settled my account, the book would go quiet about me forever. Whatever I was meant to be, I would stop being it. I would become — and here the gate showed me, kindly, mercilessly, the way a good clerk shows a client the worst-case column — ordinary. Mortal. Deaf.

Deaf to the hymn.

You have to understand what that meant, standing inside the threshold with Odile's song still ringing in the stones and Aldric's bloodline humming through the iron like a struck bell. For one year — one wretched, hunted, miraculous year — I had heard the world's true music under everything. Under the rain. Under the crowd-noise of Cinderport. Under Wren's swearing and Odile's breathing in her sleep. It had made every terrible day bearable, the way knowing a ship has a keel makes the storm bearable. And the gate was offering to take it back.

Take it back, and give me — what?

A fish-market. A rented room. Fifty more years, maybe sixty, each one belonging to me alone, unborrowed, unliened, unwatched by anything older than kings. A rusting stylus in a drawer. Sunday dinners with people who loved me not because prophecy appointed them but because they chose to.

I stood there for what felt like a full fiscal quarter and was probably eleven seconds.

Wren says it was eleven seconds. She counted. Of course she counted; she was already moving toward me, already shouting something — my name, I think, or possibly *don't you dare*, which with Wren amounts to the same sentence — and I raised a hand to stop her, and in doing so I signed.

Not literally. There was no stylus. There was no ink. But every keeper in my line had signed that gate's book with something, and mine, it turns out, was a gesture I learned over ten years of pushing ledgers across guild counters: the little open-palmed turn that means *yours*, that means *received*, that means *no further dispute*.

I consented. The gate collected.

* * *

How do I describe having a thing removed that you didn't know you had until recently and cannot imagine living without?

Imagine the moment a tooth comes out. The sudden, shocking *space* where pressure used to be. Your tongue keeps going to the gap. Now make the tooth the background music of existence itself, and make the ache last — well. I'll get to how long it lasts. Ask me again in forty years.

The hymn didn't fade. It *stopped*, between one heartbeat and the next, the way a clerk snatches a page out from under your hand. And the silence underneath it wasn't silence — it was just the world, plain and unaccompanied. Rain on stone. Wren's boots skidding toward me. Odile crying my name in a voice pitched high with fear. Aldric breathing. All of it suddenly, terribly *only itself*.

I sat down. I'm told it was more of a collapse, but I prefer to book it as sitting down, briskly.

The last thing the gate gave me — or maybe the first thing mortality gave me, I genuinely cannot say which — was pain. Real pain, the honest kind, my knees hitting iron-edged stone and my palms scraping and my throat raw from a night of singing. Pain that belonged to me and would heal at the speed bodies heal, no faster, no gate-gift smoothing it away. I remember thinking, with my cheek against the threshold and my ears full of ordinary noise: *so this is what things cost now.* And I remember that the thought arrived with grief and relief in equal measure, braided together so tightly I couldn't have told you which strand was which if you'd put a knife to it.

Because here is the thing nobody tells you about being saved from a monstrous inheritance: you mourn it anyway.

Not the crown. Never the crown — I would shatter that thing again in every life I get, and I intend to get several, mortal ones, thank you. But the *gift*. The hearing. The sense that I mattered to something vast, that the universe had at least one line item with my name on it. For a year I had been the last heir of the gatekeepers, the fulcrum of a century-old design, and even hunted and betrayed and bleeding, there had been a horrible grandeur to it. Significance is a drug. Prophecy is a pension plan. And I had just voluntarily resigned from both.

Odile reached me first. She dropped to her knees in front of me and grabbed my face in both hands the way she did when she'd decided I was being stupid, and she looked into my eyes, and I watched her realize.

"You can't hear it," she whispered. "The song. Xanthe, you can't—"

"No," I said. My voice came out like gravel in an empty bucket. "No, I can't."

She started to cry, which set me off, which — I am assured by witnesses — set everyone off, including a hundred-year-old king who had forgotten his own mother's face and had just gotten it back along with everything else the crown stole, so perhaps I shouldn't be embarrassed. I am slightly embarrassed. Ledger-keepers do not cry in front of clients. But there were no clients anymore. There was just a girl with a slaughtered family behind her and a hymn in her chest, holding the face of a man who had traded his music for her safety and would do it again before she finished asking.

"It's still in me," she said fiercely, through the tears. "I'll sing it *at* you. Every day. Until you're sick of it."

"Deal," I croaked. "But I'm invoicing you for the noise."

She laughed wetly, and somewhere above us the aqueduct groaned, and the world went on being only itself, and I found — to my genuine surprise — that I could bear it.

* * *

They tell me I slept for two days.

I don't remember sleeping. I remember fragments: Wren's shoulder under my arm on the long climb up the stair, her cursing at every step with a fluency that should be studied by scholars. The grey shock of daylight at the aqueduct's mouth. Someone — Vail, I think, though I couldn't swear to it — putting a cloak around my shoulders with the crisp efficiency of a woman filing a document. The smell of stew. A bed that was not mine, in a house I didn't know, in Cinderport, which had held.

And dreams. This is the part I keep turning over, the way you worry a loose thread on a coat you can't afford to replace.

I dreamed of my parents. Not the crown's counterfeit version — the rite had shown me those, voices stitched together from devoured memories, and I had kept singing through them, and I will carry the weight of that refusal longer than any scar. These were different. These were just impressions, the ordinary kind, the kind a six-year-old keeps: my mother's hands smudged with ink, my father laughing at something I'd said, the specific warmth of a kitchen I have no waking memory of. No message. No prophecy. No *avenger, arise*. Just love, unaccompanied, the way a room is warm without anyone explaining why.

I woke up crying, quietly, in the dark, and lay there cataloguing the feeling, because that's what I do, and I determined that it was both of the things at once. Grief, because they were gone and had paid for my life with theirs and I had repaid the debt by breaking the only thing they'd left me. Relief, because the voices in my head were finally, finally only mine.

Both. Entangled. On the books forever, and I've stopped trying to separate them.

* * *

On the third morning I did what any ruined accountant does with an unfixable situation.

I took inventory.

Sitting on the edge of a stranger's bed in a safehouse above a chandler's shop, I ran the numbers, and I present them here for the record:

**Assets.** One body, functional, mortal, healing at standard rates. Approximately fifty years of lifespan, unencumbered, title clear — the first property I have ever owned outright. One iron stylus, grandfather's, its purpose fulfilled, henceforth decorative. One third ledger, water-stained, blood-spotted, closed. One found family consisting of a smuggler, a child, a king, a defected inquisitor, and — allegedly — a crew of reformed criminals who had lost two of their own getting Aldric out of the usurper's camp and who looked at me across the breakfast table with something I eventually identified, with difficulty, as acceptance.

**Liabilities.** Deafness to the hymn, permanent. Absence of any destiny whatsoever, permanent. A reputation, in certain quarters, as the man who killed a god-king, which I expect to be inconvenient, inaccurate, and impossible to correct on the record, since the accurate version — *a girl sang, an old monk died, a broken king walked, and I broke a hat* — will never survive retelling.

**Net position.** Solvent. For the first time in my life, actually solvent. Everything I have, I have. Nothing is borrowed. Nothing is liened. Nothing is watching me from between the lines of an old book, waiting for a date to come due.

I put the stylus on the table while the others ate, and I looked at it for a long time. It had bound my family to the gate for four generations. It had opened doors and sealed them. It had been the single most important object in the free cities for one incandescent year.

It was, this morning, a piece of iron with ink stains on it.

"Keep it," Wren said, following my gaze. She was buttering bread with the concentration of a woman defusing something. "Might be worth something someday. Antiques."

"It's worth exactly what it weighs," I said. "Scrap price."

"Then scrap price it is." She slid the butter knife under it, picked it up, and dropped it in my shirt pocket, right over my heart, with the brisk tenderness she uses instead of the words she doesn't say. "There. Filed."

Odile was watching me from across the table, chin on her fists, and I knew what she was going to ask before she asked it, because children always ask the question the adults are circling.

"Do you miss it?" she said. "Hearing it?"

And here is where I should report, honestly, that the answer changed between the second day and the third. On the second day, half-mad with the silence, I would have said *yes, God, yes, every hour*. On the third morning, with bread and butter and sunlight and these particular people making noise around me — noise that was only noise, needing no translation, carrying no burden —

"Sometimes," I said. "Less than I expected. Ask me again in a year."

"I will," said Odile, who keeps her own accounts.

Aldric cleared his throat. He had been quiet all morning, a big man learning to occupy a small life, and when he spoke his voice was rough as an old rope. "The council wants you," he said. "All of you. There's a ceremony. They want to give you things."

"What kind of things?"

"Titles. Land. A statue, I believe." His mouth twitched — the first almost-smile I'd seen on him. "I told them you'd refuse the statue."

"Told them correctly." I looked around the table — Wren, Odile, the restored king in a borrowed shirt — and felt the strangest sensation, which took me a moment to identify because I had never once felt it in the guildhall, in all my invisible, careful, overlooked years.

I felt *included*. Not appointed. Not prophesied. Not owed, or owing. Just — asked.

"Well," I said, and stood, and my knees ached, and it was wonderful. "Let's go see what the free cities pay out for saving the world. And before anyone gets sentimental — I want it noted, in writing, that I am charging a consulting fee."

Wren laughed. Odile grabbed my hand. And outside the window, over Cinderport's smoking, battered, stubborn skyline, the sun came up on a world with no music under it at all —

— and it was enough. It was, Gods help me, gloriously, ordinarily, *mine*, enough.

What I didn't know yet — what none of us knew, as we stepped out into the cheering streets — was that the council had received a visitor that morning. A clerk, mud-spattered, half-dead from the Marchlands road, carrying a sealed case of documents recovered from the purged inquisition archives. Documents that named names. Documents that included, on the first page, in a dead man's careful hand, one line that would change everything I thought I'd settled:

*A fourth key survived the purge. The records disagree on whose.*
