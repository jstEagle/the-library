## 37. After the Walls Hold

### Seraphine

The archives of the Inquisition smelled of ash and tallow and, underneath it all, of fear that had been shelved so long it had gone to wax.

I had walked these corridors for nineteen years and never once noticed the smell. That is what faith does — it anesthetizes the nose. You can burn a man on Tuesday and sleep soundly by Tuesday night if you believe, truly believe, that the fire is a kind of arithmetic. I believed. I had built my whole life out of that belief the way a mason builds with brick, one certainty laid against another, mortar of doctrine between.

Now the bricks were loose, and every time I touched one, the wall shifted.

The purge order had come at dawn, three days after the walls held. *Destroy all records pertaining to gate-lore, bloodline registries, and heretical prophecy.* Signed with the seal of the Crown Regent's office — which was to say, signed by a dead man's office, since the Hollow King had collapsed into a dying man on the aqueduct steps and the crown lay in pieces beneath the free cities like a broken promise.

I stood in the archive with a torch in my hand and four junior inquisitors behind me, waiting for me to do what I had always done.

"Begin with the eastern stacks," said Inquisitor Dorn. He was young. He still had the shine on him, the polish of a man who has never once doubted his own torch.

I looked at the eastern stacks. The bloodline registries. Names going back two hundred years — Yarrow, Tollhouse, Anselm's line before he ever took his vows, dozens more, each name annotated with dates of surveillance, dates of elimination. My own handwriting in some margins. Neat as ledger-work. I had been very good at my job.

"The registries are priority," Dorn prompted.

"Yes," I said. "They are."

I walked to them. I put the torch to the bottom shelf, where the oldest registry sat, its spine cracked, its pages soft as moth wing. Fire took it greedily. Paper burns fast when it wants to; I have often thought that about paper, and less often about people, though both observations came from the same trade.

Dorn nodded, satisfied. The juniors moved down the rows with their own torches, and I supervised, and the archive began to die around us in a warm orange hush.

What none of them saw — because I made sure of it, positioning myself just so, angling my body the way I'd once angled it to hide a smuggled letter from a superior — was the second registry. The true one. The master copy, kept in the warden's locked cabinet, the one that recorded not who the gatekeepers *were* but what the crown had *paid* to have them found. Contracts. Signatures. A century of receipts proving that the Inquisition's holy work had been, from the beginning, a hired errand.

That book went into my coat, against my ribs, where it rode warm as a second heartbeat.

When we finished, the archive was a field of black snow. Dorn asked if there would be anything else, Reverend Mother, and I told him no, the crown's will was done, go and rest. He saluted. He believed in me completely. It occurred to me, watching him walk away through the drifting ash, that this was how the whole rotten edifice had worked for a hundred years — not through monsters, but through people trusting other people to have done the checking.

I went back that night, alone, with a lantern and a sledgehammer, and broke open the sub-cellar the archives didn't officially have. There were more books down there. There were always more books down there. I worked until my hands bled, carrying armfuls up into the gray morning light, and I did not burn a single page.

Let the free cities read everything. Let them read it aloud in the fish-markets. Let them choke on it.

Faith, I had learned, is not cured by fire. It is cured by reading.

### Wren

Here is something nobody tells you about surviving the end of the world: afterward, there is so much soup.

The walls held. That was the miracle nobody could explain — the militia holding the harbor road while the usurper's vanguard dissolved into panic, the moment the crown shattered and half the enemy army simply sat down in the mud like children whose game had ended. We won. And now Cinderport smelled of woodsmoke and low tide and boiled barley, because the free cities' answer to victory was to feed everyone, endlessly, whether they wanted feeding or not.

I stood in the guildhall-turned-field-kitchen with a ladle in one hand and Odile's hand in the other, and I thought: this is the strangest smuggling run of my life, and I never even left the city.

"You're doing it wrong," Odile said.

"I've been ladling soup since before you were born, sprout."

"You're giving the tall ones more."

"They're taller. They need more. It's physics."

"It's favoritism." But she was smiling, that crooked gap-toothed smile she'd grown into over the winter, and her hand squeezed mine, and I felt the thing I'd been refusing to feel since Grayfen finally land on me like a gull after a dropped fish: *she's alive, we're alive, it's over.*

A crew takes many shapes. I'd run with thirty sailors and two goats; I'd run alone for a bad year; and now my crew was a nine-year-old girl who hummed hymns that opened gates, a monk-shaped hole where Brother Anselm used to be, and a ledger-keeper who was currently across the hall arguing with a fishmonger about the price of herring as though the fate of kingdoms hadn't passed through his hands six days ago.

"He's undercharging," Xanthe had announced this morning, apropos of nothing, gesturing at the market stalls through the window. "Post-siege demand curve, supply disruption, and he's charging pre-war rates. Someone has to keep these people honest or they'll bankrupt themselves out of sheer gratitude."

"You survived a god's war," I'd said, "and your first instinct is to audit a fishmonger."

"Somebody has to. The world doesn't balance itself."

Gods help me, I was fond of him. Fond in the way that had stopped being deniable somewhere around the third time I'd taken a bolt for him, which is a ridiculous way to arrive at a feeling but is, in my experience, the standard route.

Odile tugged my sleeve. "The lady with the burned coat is here again."

I followed her chin. Seraphine Vail stood at the kitchen door, out of uniform, wearing a plain gray coat scorched at one cuff, looking like a woman who had been dismantled and reassembled slightly wrong. Half the hall went quiet. She'd commanded the men who burned the tollhouse at Grayfen. She'd hunted Xanthe across half the Marchlands. She'd also, by every account including Xanthe's grudging one, delivered the campaign plans that let the cities hold the walls, and then turned her inquisitors' swords around to hold a barricade herself.

She crossed the room like a woman walking into weather. She stopped at our table. She set a book down — old, leather-bound, its spine scarred.

"The master registry," she said. "Every contract the crown ever signed. Every payment. Every name they bought." Her voice was flat, careful, the voice of someone reciting a confession she'd rehearsed. "It should belong to people who'll know what to do with it. I'm told you're good with ledgers."

"I know someone," I said.

Her eyes went to Odile. Something moved behind them — guilt, probably, the kind that doesn't wash out. "The child at Grayfen," Vail said quietly. "My men did that. I gave the orders that sent them. I won't ask forgiveness. I only want—" She stopped. Started again, worse, humaner. "Is there anything she needs?"

Odile looked up at the woman who had, however indirectly, orphaned her, and considered the question with the terrifying directness of children.

"Do you know any songs?" Odile said. "Ones that aren't sad?"

Vail blinked. "I— knew lullabies. Once."

"Learn a new one, then." Odile shrugged, magnanimous as an empress. "You can sing it at the remembering, when we say everyone's names. Anselm says — said — that's what songs are for. Carrying names so they don't get lost."

Vail stood there a moment, and I watched nineteen years of doctrine fail to find anything useful to say against a nine-year-old, which is, when you think about it, exactly the right weapon for the job.

"There's a hymn," Vail said at last, hoarse. "From the old rite. I burned the orders to destroy it. I could... write down what I remember."

"That's a start," said Odile, and went back to her soup, and that was that. Another soul added to the crew. We collect strays. It's a flaw in the ship.

### Xanthe

The dead took four days to count.

I know because I counted them — not the official rolls, those belonged to the council's clerks, but my own list, kept in the back pages of the third ledger, because somebody had to, and because I had learned the hard way that numbers are how I pray. Four thousand one hundred and twelve names from the free cities. Nobody had a full count from the Marchlands yet. The usurper's army had carried its dead away with it, dissolving back into the countryside like a fever breaking, leaving only the equipment and the questions.

Wren found me on the fifth evening sitting on the aqueduct wall above the sealed stair, legs dangling over the drop, doing nothing at all, which for me is a full-time occupation these days.

"You're brooding," she said, settling beside me. "You have a brooding face. Very tragic. Very 'last of his line.'"

"I'm not brooding. I'm resting my eyes."

"On a wall. Over a drop. At sunset."

"It's atmospheric."

She bumped her shoulder against mine. Below us, the aqueduct stair descended into darkness behind its new iron door — fitted by the council within a week of the battle, because the free cities love nothing so much as a lock on a problem. I couldn't hear the gate anymore. That was the arrangement. That was the price. Some nights I pressed my palm against the stone and felt only stone, cold and dumb and honest, and I couldn't decide if it was grief or relief, so I'd decided it was both, filed under both, cross-referenced.

"Vail brought the registry today," Wren said.

"I heard. I started reading it at supper."

"And?"

"And there's an entry from ninety-one years ago. A payment authorized to a Threshold factor named Yarrow. My great-grandfather." I turned the stylus over in my fingers — grandfather's iron stylus, rusted now along the nib, retired. "He took the crown's coin to report a gatekeeper family's address. Then, two years later, another entry. Same man. A payment *to* the gate. Everything he'd earned and more, paid back through a river-shrine, marked 'for the child.'" I laughed, and it came out wrong. "He sold a family to buy his grandson's life. And the crown spent a century hunting that same grandson. The ledger balances, Wren. It always balances. That's the joke."

"That's a terrible joke."

"I'm a ledger-keeper. Our jokes are terrible. It's tradition."

She was quiet a moment. Out in the harbor, the fishing boats were coming home with lamps lit, little constellations dragging through the dark water. Somewhere below, faint through the iron door, Odile was singing to the gate — not the opening hymn, just a made-up song about soup, because Odile sang to everything now, doors and cats and healing bones, and the gate, whatever it was now that its keys were scattered and its keeper was ordinary, seemed to like the soup song fine.

"What happens to you," Wren said, "now that there's no prophecy with your name in it?"

I thought about it honestly, which is more than I usually manage.

"I don't know," I said. "Nobody's written it. Isn't that wonderful?"

And she laughed, and I laughed, and the lamps came on along the seawall one by one, and for the first time in a year the future was a blank page instead of a filled-in date, and I found — this is the strange part, the part I'd never have believed back when I was safe and small and cataloguing bottled storms — that I wasn't afraid of it.

### Aldric

They brought me the crown's pieces in a velvet-lined case, as if velvet could apologize for what it carried.

I had asked to see them. That surprised the council — the rescued king, the living relic, asking to sit alone with the thing that had eaten a century of his life. But I needed to look at it. One last accounting, before the petitioners arrived with their thrones and their speeches.

It was smaller than I remembered. Of course it was; I'd been twenty-three when they bound it to my head, and everything had been larger then. Now it was five jagged arcs of black metal in a box, cold even through the velvet, and I sat with it for a long time in the council chamber while the candles guttered, and I felt — nothing. No pull. No whisper. Whatever Xanthe Yarrow broke at the gate, he broke it thoroughly. The metal was just metal. Dead as a nail.

A hundred years I had been its anchor. A hundred years of hearing it think through the walls of my cell, feeling it wear my memories like stolen clothes, watching through its eyes as it wore my face to legitimize its wars. And now it was scrap, and I was free, and freedom turned out to be a thin old man with shaking hands who cried at soup kitchens.

There were worse fates. I had seen most of them.

I closed the case. Tomorrow the council would come, and the ambassadors, and the ambitious cousins crawling out of exile, all of them certain they knew what a restored king should want. They would offer me the throne with both hands and expect me to seize it — what else does a king do?

I looked at the case, and at my reflection warped in its polished lid: a gaunt, gray, ordinary man.

"No," I said to the reflection, and to the council, and to the hundred years. "No, I don't think I will."

But that conversation could wait for morning. Tonight I wanted soup, and the company of the living, and perhaps — if the girl was singing — a song about soup, sung at the top of a stair to a shut door, in a city that had held.

The walls had held. Now, God willing, so would we.
