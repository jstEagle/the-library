## 39. The Ledger of Ordinary Days

There is a particular smell to a fish-market at dawn that no amount of honest work will ever wash out of a man's memory. Brine and ice and the sweet-rot edge of yesterday's catch, all of it cut through with the smoke from the chandlers' stalls. I used to think the guildhall smelled like money. It doesn't, really. It smelled like ink and floor wax and other people's secrets, which is to say it smelled like nothing at all. The Saltmarket smells like what it is. I have come to prefer that.

My stall — not mine, I rent it, which is a distinction I have learned to treasure — sits at the end of the row nearest the harbor wall, under an awning patched in three colors because Wren bought the canvas off a wrecked smuggler's sloop for less than the price of a good dinner. From here I can see the water, the cranes, the long gray line of the aqueduct marching inland toward the hills, and if I stand up and lean out far enough, the sealed stair beneath its arches where everything ended.

I don't lean out very often.

"Xanthe." Marta Ossory, who owns half the market and rents me the other half's paperwork, came by before the gulls had finished their argument with the fishwives. She had a crate of herring under one arm and her ledgers under the other, and she set both down on my table with the authority of a woman who has never once doubted that the world arranges itself around her convenience. "The Coriander's run. Captain says he'll pay his mooring fees when the season turns."

"The captain said that last season."

"And you wrote it down then, too." She tapped the ledger with one blunt finger. "That's why I keep you. Not because you're clever. Because you write things down."

I opened the book and wrote it down. *Coriander's run — mooring deferred — third quarter.* Somewhere in another life, a version of me catalogued bottled storms and borrowed years in a guildhall that locked its doors against him, and that version would have found this entry beneath contempt. This version of me finds it restful. A debt deferred is still a debt recorded. The world stays legible. Nobody's soul depends on it.

Most days, anyway.

* * *

Wren arrived mid-morning, which meant she'd been awake since before dawn doing something she'd deny later. She dropped onto the stool beside my table, stole an apple from the fruit-seller two stalls down without breaking stride — he saw her do it and pretended otherwise; that's the local economy of affection — and bit into it while she read over my shoulder.

"You've got the Coriander down twice," she said.

"I have the Coriander down once, and your elbow smudged the—"

She was already reaching past me to fix it, which is how we argue now: hands in each other's paperwork, voices low, nobody storming out. Her sleeve smelled of tar and rope. There's a new scar along her forearm from the siege, from the night she held the harbor gate with six crew and a borrowed ballista, and she catches me looking at it sometimes and flexes her fingers like it's nothing, like a body is just a thing that keeps score quietly.

"Crew wants to know if we're running the Grayfen route this winter," she said. "Tolls are light since the tollhouse reopened. Odile could stay with the Ossory woman."

"She hates staying with the Ossory woman."

"She hates staying with anyone who isn't us. That's different from hating it." Wren finished the apple and set the core neatly on the corner of my ledger, which is a crime against stationery I have stopped prosecuting. "We don't have to go. I told them that. I told them the famous Xanthe Yarrow doesn't run cargo anymore, he counts it."

"I count it," I agreed. "It's better than it sounds."

"It's worse," she said fondly. "But it's yours."

That's the thing about Wren. She never once asked me to be more than I am now. When the walls held and the songs started — and there were songs, gods help us, balladeers who couldn't scan and couldn't keep a straight face while singing about the man who broke the crown — she listened to every one of them with an expression I can only describe as commercial interest, and then she came home and said, "They've got the part wrong where you cry. You definitely cried."

"I did not cry."

"You wept, Xanthe. In front of a dying monk and a child and a smuggler. I'm keeping the memory for trade purposes."

* * *

Odile came home from her lessons at the fourth bell, as she always does, dropping her slate bag on my table the way she drops everything: with total confidence that the world will catch it. She's grown half a head since the spring. She hums when she does her sums now — not the hymn, never the hymn, that's gone quiet in her the way it's gone quiet in me, and neither of us talks about whether that's grief or healing — but some tuneless thing she made up herself, which I consider the finest music in Cinderport.

"Marta says you're writing down the Coriander again," she said.

"Somebody has to."

"When I'm grown I'm going to keep accounts for a whole fleet."

"Ambitious."

"No," she said seriously, "ambitious is Wren. I just want the numbers to come out even." She leaned over the ledger, hair falling in her face, and checked my arithmetic with the fierce concentration of a customs inspector. Then, softer, in the voice she only uses when she thinks she's being casual: "You're coming to supper? Both of you? Because Tammen's crew is back and they brought oranges, real ones, and Wren said we'd have them tonight."

"We'll be there."

She kissed the top of my head — she's tall enough to do that now, which she finds hilarious and I find mortifying — and ran off toward the harbor wall to watch the boats, and I sat there for a moment with my pen still wet and let the feeling arrive, the one that has no ledger line. Family, she calls us. Not asked, not announced. Just assumed, the way she assumes the tide, the way she assumes I'll be at the table. Once I built a whole life around being overlooked, and I thought that was safety. It turns out the actual safety is being seen so thoroughly by three or four people that the whole world's opinion becomes a rounding error.

* * *

The stylus lives in the second drawer of my desk, under the spare nibs and a coil of string, wrapped in oilcloth that I stopped refreshing sometime around midsummer. I took it out tonight after the market closed, because today is the anniversary — not the anniversary the balladeers sing about, the other one, the private one — and anniversaries make a man sentimental about iron.

It's rusting. Properly rusting, pitting along the shaft, the old gatekeeper markings going soft and brown like a scar fading. I held it up to the lamplight and turned it, and felt — nothing. No hum. No recognition. No cold thread pulling through my chest toward the aqueduct stair. Just iron, cold as any iron, heavy as any tool.

Once that weight was the whole architecture of my life. Now it's just a weight.

I wrapped it back up. I didn't put it away right away, though. I sat with it a while, the way you sit with a letter from someone dead, and I thought about my grandfather, whose name I carry in ink rather than blood-memory, and my parents, whose faces I know only from the crown's stolen memories and from a debt receipt. I thought about Anselm, who taught me the rite with the patience of a man folding his own shroud. About Vail, who sends letters now, dry and precise, cataloguing the dismantling of the inquisition archive page by page — she writes like a ledger-keeper, which I suspect is the highest compliment either of us knows how to give.

And I thought about the gate. Sealed. Silent. The hymn gone from my ears like a language forgotten in adulthood — I know I knew it, I can almost feel the shape of it behind my teeth, but the meaning slides away. Some mornings I wake thinking I hear it under the sound of the harbor, and it's only the water. Only the water. That's what ordinary sounds like, and I am learning, slowly, imperfectly, to love it.

* * *

Supper was oranges and smoked mackerel and Tammen telling the story of the siege harbor-gate for the eleventh time, improved with each telling until he personally held off forty inquisitors with a boat-hook. Odile corrected him on points of fact. Wren backed him up out of pure mischief. The lamp burned low and the wind came off the water smelling of salt and tomorrow, and nobody mentioned crowns or gates or debts, and when Odile fell asleep against Wren's shoulder mid-correction, we carried her to bed the way you carry something you've been entrusted with.

Later, Wren found me at the desk with the ledger open, closing out the day's columns.

"Balanced?" she asked.

"Balanced."

She looked over my shoulder at the neat figures, the deferred moorings, the honest small sums of an honest small life. Then she reached past me — she always reaches past me — and turned to a fresh page, and wrote at the top of it, in her terrible smuggler's hand:

*Tomorrow.*

"What's that for?"

"New column," she said. "You're always saying the world stays legible. So make it legible. Write down what you want in it."

I looked at the word a long time. Under the lamp, with the stylus rusting in its drawer and the gate silent behind its stone, with a child asleep in the next room calling us family without ever having asked permission, with a woman beside me who has seen exactly what I cost and stayed anyway —

I picked up the pen.

And that was when we heard it: faint, from the direction of the aqueduct, threading under the harbor sounds like a note remembered rather than heard. A single line of the hymn, rising where no hymn should rise, from a gate that has been shut and silent for a year.

Odile slept on. Wren went very still beside me.

Neither of us moved. Neither of us breathed.

The note hung in the air, and then — softly, unmistakably — it was joined by a second.
