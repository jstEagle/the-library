## 1. The Charm Seller's Hours

There are three rules to selling charms illegally in Ashfall City, and I break all of them before dinner.

Rule one: never work the same corner twice. I've had my stall at the mouth of Tallow Lane for four years now, wedged between a candlemaker who's going blind and a pie shop that smells like forgiveness. The Wardens could find me any day they cared to look. They don't care to look. Ashfall has eleven thousand problems and one woman selling luck-knots doesn't crack the list.

Rule two: never let them see it work. This one I keep, mostly, because watching a customer's face when a charm actually *does* something is the fastest way to get us both hanged. So my charms are subtle by design — a knot that untangles arguments over the course of a week, a pressed marigold that keeps nightmares at the edge of the blanket rather than banishing them entirely. Small mercies, priced for working people. If a charm works too well, I tell the buyer they imagined it. People are desperate to imagine things.

Rule three: never hope. Hope is the thing with teeth. Hope is what got the Sundering started, if you believe the schoolbooks, which I don't, but I believe in its conclusion well enough: magic is outlawed, the gate beneath my cellar floor is sealed, and every soul in this city sleeps easier knowing the other side of the world can't reach in and take what it wants.

I break rule three every evening at dusk, when the light goes amber on the cobbles and the lamplighter comes down Tallow Lane with his long brass pole, and the whole street turns briefly into something out of a story. That's when I catch myself thinking: *maybe today.* Maybe someone walks past my stall who sees me — not the charms, not the convenient little shop of plausible deniability, but me, Lena Pemberton, twenty-six years old, keeper of a sealed door and a great many secrets, and lonely as a lighthouse keeper who's forgotten ships exist.

Tonight the amber light came early, because autumn is leaning on the city like a drunk on a fence, and I was breaking rule three right on schedule when the bell above my stall jangled.

Not my bell. My stall doesn't have a bell; bells are memorable. It was the pie shop next door, and out of it came a man carrying two paper bags and the particular expression of someone who has just been informed, gently, that he has ordered far too much pie.

He stopped in front of my stall. He looked at the charms laid out on faded velvet — knots and sprigs and river-stones and one small brass compass that pointed, always, toward whatever the holder had lost most recently — and then he looked at me.

"Which one of these," he said, "makes people stop being sad?"

I'd heard a lot of opening lines at that stall. *What does this do? How much? Is this legal?* (No comment.) Never that.

"That depends," I said, "on whether you mean your sadness or everyone's."

His eyebrows went up. He had the kind of face that did things honestly — surprise arrived all the way through him, no reservation. Dark hair going gray early at the temples, like he'd worried some of the color clean out. A scar through one eyebrow, old and thin. And underneath all of it, something I couldn't name, a familiarity like a word on the tip of the tongue.

"Just mine," he said. "Everyone else's seems to be doing fine."

I reached under the counter without deciding to. There's a drawer there I don't open often, because what's in it isn't stock — it's the things I've made for myself, or failed to make for myself, or made on nights when the loneliness got loud enough that craft seemed like an answer. From that drawer I took a charm I'd braided from three colors of thread and a sliver of ember-glass, back in January, during the cold snap, during the bad week.

I don't sell those. I want to be clear about that. Rule four, the unwritten one: never sell a piece of yourself.

I sold it to him.

"Forget-me-not blue, ash-gray, and gold," I said, as he turned it over in his big careful hands. "The blue is for grief — it doesn't remove it, nothing removes it, it just holds the edges so it doesn't cut. The gray is for the city, because the city gets into everything. And the gold—" I hesitated. "The gold is ember-glass. Don't ask where I got it."

"I wasn't going to." He smiled. It rearranged his whole face. "How much?"

"Two coppers."

"Lena," he said, reading the little hand-painted sign on my counter, and my name in his mouth sounded like it belonged there, which was absurd, which was impossible, which made my chest do something complicated and unauthorized. "That's worth more than two coppers."

"It's worth exactly two coppers," I said, "because that's what I'm charging, and price is a kind of truth."

He paid me four and told me to keep the change, and I told him I didn't make change for flatterers, and he laughed, and somewhere in the middle of all that the amber light caught the ember-glass in his palm and it glowed — just barely, just a breath of orange, like a coal remembering fire — and he didn't see it, because he was looking at me instead of the charm, and I covered it with my hand fast enough that only the two of us and possibly the blind candlemaker would ever know.

*It's nothing,* I told myself. *Ember-glass catches light. It's reflective. It's fine.*

It was not reflective. Ember-glass doesn't reflect anything. Ember-glass remembers.

"What's your name?" I asked, because rule five, also unwritten: never ask their names. Names are threads, and threads pull.

"August," he said.

And the ground did not open up, and the sky did not fall, and the gate beneath my cellar floor did not so much as sigh. But every hair on my arms stood up in perfect silence, like an orchestra waiting for a downbeat, and August — the stranger with the familiar face and the bag of excess pie — said, "You look like you've seen a ghost."

"No," I said. "Ghosts I'm used to. You look like—"

Like the ledger. Like the ink. Like the page I keep locked in the cellar, in the dark, beside the door that must never open, where a hundred years ago somebody drew a man's face in iron-gall ink and wrote beneath it words I have spent five years pretending not to remember.

But I hadn't looked at the ledger in months. I couldn't have known his face from it. I'd made myself forget it, the way you make yourself forget a dream, and yet here it was walking around in daylight buying pie.

"You look like trouble," I finished.

"Good," said August. "I've been told I'm very good at it. Do you sell anything for that?"

"Trouble?" I said. "No. Trouble's the one thing in Ashfall City nobody needs a charm to find."

* * *

Let me back up, because I'm telling this wrong, the way you tell a story after you know how it ends — everything sounds like an omen. That evening it was just an evening. Let me give it to you properly, hour by hour, the way I lived it, back when I still believed my life was small enough to hold in two hands.

I open the stall at four. Always four, because four is when the shift-change crowds come through Tallow Lane — foundry men going home smelling of smoke, laundresses with red hands, children running errands for mothers who can't spare them the time to run themselves. Working people. People whose luck runs thin by Thursday and doesn't refill till payday. My people, though none of them know it.

The stall itself looks like nothing. That's the art of it. A folding table, a striped awning gone pale from sun, a hand-painted sign that says PEMBERTON'S KEEPSAKES in letters cheerful enough to be forgettable. Keepsakes. Not charms. Charms are illegal under the Ember Accords, Section One, Paragraph One, the very first law the new Council ever passed: *no mortal shall work sorcery, nor buy it, nor bear it.* Keepsakes, though? Keepsakes are sentimental junk, and sentimental junk is the birthright of every grieving fool in a city that lost half itself a century ago.

I sell keepsakes. What the keepsakes *do* is between them and their buyers.

By six I'd moved maybe thirty coppers' worth of stock. A river-stone to a dockhand whose brother was lost off the Graywater — "for the pocket nearest your heart," I told him, and didn't say *it will feel warm when you talk to him,* because he already knew, the way they always already know, and saying it aloud would ruin the deniability we were both depending on. A knotted cord to a girl of maybe nine, sent by her grandmother with exact instructions: green for growth, brown for staying rooted, and the grandmother paid me with a look that said she knew perfectly well what I was and approved anyway. Old women always know. They remember before the Accords, or they remember their mothers remembering, and they come to my stall with shopping lists written in a language the Council thinks is dead.

This is the part of my life that works. This is the part I'm good at. Five years since I inherited the shop and the cellar and the door from my mother, who inherited it from hers, back to a line of Pembertons longer than the city has had walls, and in five years I have kept the secret, kept the stall, kept the ledger dusted and the wards fed and the gate — the gate — quiet. I am, by any reasonable measure, a success.

Here is the part that doesn't work, the part I only admit at dusk, alone, counting coppers into a tin that used to rattle and now mostly clinks:

Nobody knows me.

Oh, they know the stall. They know Pemberton's Keepsakes, reliable, fair-priced, open rain or shine. Mrs. Hobb the candlemaker waves at me through the wall every morning. The pie shop sends over burnt crusts on Fridays because I once charmed — *keepsaked* — her oven into behaving. I am liked. I am a fixture. I am furniture.

And furniture doesn't get held. Furniture doesn't get asked how it's doing and listened to past the first *fine.* Five years of being useful and invisible, and I could count on one hand the number of people who knew my mother's name, and on no hands at all the number who knew that I talk to the door in my cellar at night, quietly, the way you'd talk to a sleeping animal, because the alternative is admitting that I'm talking to myself.

My mother warned me about this. Her last real lesson, delivered hoarse and fast with the end coming up the stairs like weather: *"The keeping will eat you alive if you let it be all you are, Lena. Keep the gate, yes. But keep a self. Promise me."*

I promised. I opened a stall. I told myself that counted.

Some nights I even believe it.

* * *

So: seven o'clock, dusk coming down amber, me breaking rule three, and August standing at my counter with his bags of pie and his impossible face.

He didn't leave, is the thing. Customers buy and go; that's the rhythm of the stall, four minutes per soul, five if they're grieving. August stood there turning the ember-glass charm over in his fingers like it was a question he was working up to asking, and finally he said, "Do you close soon?"

"At eight."

"Can I stand here till then? I'll buy something. I'll buy several things. I'll be the best customer you've ever had."

"Why would you want to do that?"

He considered the question with more seriousness than it deserved, head tipped, scar catching the lamplight. "Because I walked past this stall yesterday," he said, "and today, and the day before, and every time I thought, *I should talk to her,* and every time I kept walking, and I'm tired of being a man who keeps walking. So. Here I am. Standing still." He set a copper on the counter with ceremony. "That's for the privilege. Charge whatever you like for the conversation."

Now, a sensible smuggler of illegal sorcery — which is what I am, whatever gentler words I dress it in — hears a speech like that and starts counting exits. A stranger who's watched you for days is either a Warden's informant or a problem, and both wear the same friendly face. I did count the exits. There are always three: the lane, the alley behind the candlemaker's, and the cellar, though the cellar's less an exit than a last resort with a very heavy door.

But I also heard something under his words. Underneath the charm of him there was a current, the way there's a current under ice — purpose, moving fast and silent. He hadn't wandered to my stall. He'd *arrived* at it. And the ember-glass in his hand had glowed like a struck match, and my arms had risen in gooseflesh like a congregation, and rule six, the newest one, written that very moment: *when the impossible introduces itself politely, do not invite it home.*

"Tell you what," I said. "You can help me pack up. Company while I work. Then I'm closing, and you're going home to eat your weight in pie."

"Deal," he said instantly, and handed me one of the paper bags. "Apple. Bribe."

I shouldn't have taken it. I took it. It was still warm, and I was hungry in a way that had nothing to do with food, and I have never claimed to be a sensible woman, only a careful one, and careful women are allowed one apple per catastrophe.

We packed the stall together in the easy way of people who've done it a hundred times, which we hadn't, which should have been harder than it was. He folded the velvet without being told. He knew the awning pegs stuck. When I dropped the coin tin he caught it against his knee without looking, and we both froze for half a second, because that's not luck, that's practice, that's a body that has caught things before — and then he laughed it off and I let him, because the alternative was questions, and questions were a door I wasn't ready to open with a stranger holding a charm that remembered fire.

At half past seven, packed and shuttered, I stood on the cobbles with my apple and my tin and my three exits, and August stood across from me with his pie bags, and the lamplighter's flames came down the lane one by one behind him like a procession.

"Same time tomorrow?" he said.

"It's a stall," I said. "It's always the same time."

"Not what I asked."

I looked at him. The amber light was dying into blue and he stood in the seam between them, half warm and half dark, and the ache in my chest — the five-year ache, the furniture-ache, the *nobody knows me* ache — leaned toward him like a plant toward a window.

Guarded hope. That's what my mother would have called it. Hope with the exits counted.

"Tomorrow," I said. "Four o'clock. Buy something or don't, but don't watch me from across the street again. It's unsettling."

"Yes, ma'am," he said, grinning, and walked backward three steps before turning, like he couldn't bear to stop looking at the stall — or at me — and went off down Tallow Lane trailing the smell of apples and woodsmoke.

I stood there until the lamplight swallowed him. Then I went inside, down the stairs, past the shop's dead hearth, to the cellar door that I feed and ward and whisper to.

Because I had to check. You'd have checked too.

The gate was quiet, as it's been for five years, as it's been for a hundred. Sealed, sleeping, humming its one low note below hearing. But as I put my palm flat against the cold iron-banded wood, the hum changed. Lifted. Brightened, the way a room brightens when someone you love walks in.

And through the door, faint as a memory of a voice, in a language dead a century, something on the other side said a single word.

I don't speak the old tongue. My mother did; I only ever learned to recognize it, the way you recognize thunder.

But I know my own name in any language there is.

It said *Lena.*

I sat down on the cellar stairs in the dark with my apple going cold in my hand, and I did not sleep, and I did not scream, and I did not open the door, because I am a careful woman and careful women count their exits.

But all night, in the shop above, on the velvet where he'd left it behind to be repaired — he'd said the braid was fraying, he'd be back tomorrow, he'd *be back* — August's ember-glass charm glowed softly in the dark, steady as a heartbeat, like a coal that had been waiting a hundred years for someone to breathe on it.
