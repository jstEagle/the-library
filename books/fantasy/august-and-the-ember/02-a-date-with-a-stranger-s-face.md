## 2. A Date with a Stranger's Face

There are three rules to selling charms in a city that has outlawed magic.

One: never sell anything that actually works to anyone who might notice it working. Two: never let them see your hands shake. Three: never, under any circumstances, fall for a customer.

I had kept the first two rules for five years. I want it noted, for the record, that I broke the third one before the man even sat down.

* * *

He came into my stall at the tail end of dusk, when the lanterns along Copperlane were just deciding whether to be romantic or merely adequate. The Ember Market was doing its nightly impression of innocence—dried lavender, river stones, "lucky" horseshoes that had never seen a horse—and I was doing mine: the harmless charm-seller, all smiles and superstition, nothing under the counter but thread and good intentions.

Except for the things under the counter that were not thread and did not have good intentions so much as *opinions*. But that's between me and the cellar floor.

"You're the charm woman," he said. Not a question. He said it the way people say *you're the lighthouse*, like he'd been looking for me across a great deal of water.

"I'm a vendor of decorative folkcraft," I said, with the flat professionalism of someone who has rehearsed her innocence in a mirror. "Legally distinct from a charm woman."

His laugh surprised both of us. It came out low and warm, like something banked rather than lit. "Legally distinct. Right." He looked at my stall—the bundles of sage I'd blessed at actual moonrise, the knot-charms tied with knots that untied themselves when lies got too close—and his eyes did something careful. Something that said *I see the stage set, and I'm choosing not to mention the ropes.*

"August," he said, and put out his hand.

And here is where I should tell you about the strangeness, because it started there, at the handshake, before a single word of conversation. His hand closed around mine and my whole arm went quiet. Not numb—quiet. The way a room goes quiet when someone walks in who used to live there. Every ward I keep stitched into my skin, every little alarm-thread I've woven into my own pulse since the Sundering made caution a religion—all of it went still, like dogs lying down by a fire.

My magic knew him. That was the only word for it. My magic looked at this stranger and *sagged with relief*.

"Lena," I said, because apparently my mouth had also defected. "Lena Pemberton."

"Pemberton," he repeated, tasting it. His eyes—grey-green, the color of the canal right before rain—did a slow blink, like the name had struck a bell somewhere behind his face. "That's—" He stopped. Shook his head. Smiled. "Nothing. Sorry. You look like someone I was told about once. Someone from a story."

"People say that," I lied. Nobody had ever said that. People said I looked tired, or suspicious, or like a woman who owned an alarming number of scissors.

"Do they?" He didn't believe me. I could tell because he let it go immediately, which is what kind people do with other people's locked doors. "So. Is this the part where I buy a lucky horseshoe and you pretend we're strangers?"

"This is exactly that part."

"Good." He picked up a horseshoe, examined it with theatrical gravity. "This horseshoe is humming."

"It is not."

"It's humming a little tune. Very off-key. It sounds like it learned music from a cat."

"That horseshoe has never seen a horse and certainly cannot carry a melody," I said, and took it out of his hands before it could embarrass us both. Some of my stock got ideas. Especially around him, apparently, because as soon as his fingers left the iron, the humming stopped—as if it had been singing *for* him, which was impossible, and which I would be having words with it about later.

"So what do you recommend," August said, "for a man who's new in town and has the persistent feeling that he's forgotten something important?"

I should have sold him a dried lavender bundle and sent him on his way. That was rule one, rule two, and half of rule three, all wrapped up in twine. Instead I heard myself say:

"Forgetting? Or being forgotten?"

His smile faded into something more honest. "Is there a difference?"

"There's always a difference," I said. "Forgetting is a hole. Being forgotten is a room with you still standing in it."

The lantern light caught his face then, and I had the second strangeness of the evening, the one I'd spend the next week refusing to name: I knew the way his brow folded when he was thinking. Not recognized it—*knew* it, the way you know the chorus of a song you've never heard sung aloud. Somewhere in me a door I'd bricked over rattled on its hinges, and I put my shoulder against it and smiled like a shopkeeper.

"The knot-charms," I said briskly. "For remembering. They're decorative folkcraft."

"Naturally."

"Naturally. Tie one around your wrist, think of what you don't want to lose, and it holds the thought. Metaphorically."

"Metaphorically," he agreed solemnly, and held out his wrist.

* * *

We ended up walking, because the market closed and neither of us suggested parting. That's the thing about Ashfall after dark—it makes conspirators of everyone. Curfew bells ring at nine and the Wardens sweep Copperlane with their severance-lanterns, those ugly brass things that make your teeth ache if you're carrying so much as a spark. So the city empties, and whoever remains in the lamplit gaps becomes, briefly, family. Two people walking nowhere in particular past shuttered stalls are just two people. The night keeps our secrets out of professional courtesy.

August walked like a man reading a map he'd memorized as a child. Twice he turned a corner before I did and then paused, confused at himself. Once he said, "There used to be a fountain here," gesturing at a cobbler's, and I felt the ground tilt under my feet, because there *had* been a fountain there—I'd splashed in it as a girl—before the Wardens filled it in during the Purges, twenty years before he could possibly have been born.

"Good guess," I said.

"Yeah," he said. "Good guess." He didn't look convinced either.

We talked anyway, because the alternative was admitting the evening had teeth. He told me he'd come to Ashfall three weeks ago from somewhere north—he waved a hand vaguely in the direction of everywhere—and that he worked odd jobs, that he was between things, that he'd always been between things. He asked questions like a man collecting answers: what the market was like before the Accords (I gave him the sanitized version), whether I'd always lived here (yes), whether I believed in luck (I said no; the horseshoe in my satchel hummed indignantly).

"And you?" he said. "What's between things for you?"

"Between things," I said, "is where I keep everything."

He laughed again, that banked-fire laugh, and bumped my shoulder with his, casual as breathing, and my whole body reacted like he'd recited a password. I'm telling you, it was humiliating. Five years of careful invisibility, of being the woman nobody looks at twice, undone by a stranger's elbow.

At the corner of Belltower Row he stopped. The belltower rose above us against the stars, black and patient, and beneath it the Warden vaults slept behind three feet of stone, though of course I didn't know yet how much that would come to matter. August tilted his head back and looked at the tower for a long moment.

"My grandmother told me about this place," he said quietly. "Before she died. She said—if I ever found myself in Ashfall, I should find the charm seller on Copperlane."

The cold went through me like a Warden's lantern. "Lots of charm sellers on Copperlane."

"She said you'd know what she meant." He turned and looked at me, and his eyes in the lamplight were the exact grey-green of the gate in my cellar, the one I'd sworn on my life never to think about while standing above it. "Do you?"

"No," I said, with the conviction of a woman whose entire livelihood depended on the word. "I really don't."

He held my gaze for three heartbeats. Then he smiled, rueful, and let it go—letting it go, always letting it go, that terrible kindness. "Okay. Another time, maybe."

"Another time," I echoed, and hated how much I wanted there to be one.

* * *

Here's what happened at the end of the night, and I want to be precise about it, because precision is all I have left of that hour.

We stood outside my stall, which doubles as my home's front door, because a woman hiding a gate to another realm does not rent separate premises. The curfew bell had rung its second warning. He should have gone. I should have gone. Neither of us moved.

"I had a good time," he said. "Which is strange."

"Why strange?"

"Because I've been waiting for something my whole life. I mean that literally—I've had this feeling since I was a kid, like I left something running somewhere and I need to get back to it. And tonight it got quieter. Tonight it felt almost—" He searched for the word. "Almost paid off. Like a debt settled. Being here. Talking to you." He rubbed the back of his neck, embarrassed. "That's a lot to say to a woman who sells horseshoes."

"Decorative folkcraft," I corrected automatically.

"Right. My apologies to your legal team."

And because I am weak, and because the night was soft, and because some traitor part of me already knew I'd regret it forever either way, I reached into the stall and took down the last thing on the hook: a small pendant on a leather cord, a sliver of ember-glass set in copper. I'd made it years ago, back when I still made things honestly, before I learned to sand the magic off everything I touched. It was the one piece of real work I'd never managed to sell, partly because it hummed, and partly because every time I looked at it I felt like it was looking back.

"Forget-me-knot glass," I said. "It's—"

"Humming," August said softly.

It was. Louder than the horseshoe. A thin, sweet note, like a wet finger on a crystal rim, and as he took it from my palm the note climbed—climbed until the lantern flames bent toward it, until the hair on my arms lifted, until I saw, just for a heartbeat, just at the edge of seeing, a faint gold light moving *under his skin*. Under the glass. Around it. Like the pendant had spent years waiting to be near him and had finally, finally started to sing.

Prophecy-light. I'd read about it in books I shouldn't own. Old texts called it *the shine of the foretold*—the glow that clings to a person when a story older than they are has gotten its hooks in. It wasn't supposed to exist anymore. The Sundering was supposed to have burned every prophecy out of the world like a field fire.

The light guttered and went out. August blinked, unaware, turning the pendant over in his fingers. "It's beautiful. How much?"

"Free," I said, before my survival instincts could file their objection. "Opening-night discount."

"Lena." He said my name carefully, like setting down something fragile. "Thank you." And then, softer, with that same careful-not-to-mention-the-ropes gentleness: "Whatever your grandmother story is—whatever it is you're not telling me—you should know I'm not asking. Not tonight. But I'll be here."

"Where's here?"

"Wherever you are, apparently," he said, and grinned, and the grin cracked something open in my chest that I'd sealed with the same mortar as everything else.

Then he walked off down Copperlane, into the dark between lamps, and the pendant's light moved with him—a tiny gold star bobbing away through a city built on burying stars—and I stood in my doorway with my hand pressed flat over my sternum, feeling my heart do something it had no business doing.

Because here is the dread, the part I couldn't shake, the reason I didn't sleep: charms don't lie. I've built my whole crooked life on that fact. Thread doesn't sing for ordinary men. Iron doesn't hum for strangers. And ember-glass cut from a fire that predates the Sundering does not glow like a struck match for anyone unless the old stories have already written his name down somewhere.

Somewhere, something had been waiting for August. And it had just watched him walk away wearing a piece of my work, glowing like a beacon, in a city that executes people for glowing.

I bolted the door. I checked the cellar wards twice, then a third time, which I hadn't done since the night I found the gate. From below the floorboards, faint as breath, the ledger in the dark gave a single soft rustle—like a book turning its own page.

Like something settling in to wait for morning.

I told myself it was rats.

I have never in my life been good at lying to myself, but that night I gave it everything I had.
