## 3. The Book That Shouldn't Exist

I didn't sleep.

That's not dramatic flourish; it's an inventory of the night. I lay in my narrow bed above the shop and listened to the city settle around me like an old dog circling before lying down—carts on cobblestones, a drunk singing something mournful about a fishwife, the distant clank of the Warden patrol changing shifts at the Belltower. And underneath all of it, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, the gate humming in my cellar.

It had never hummed that loudly before.

For five years I'd lived above it the way you live above a sleeping bear: quietly, gratefully, with a running mental list of everything you'd do differently if it woke up. The ledger was supposed to be part of the arrangement. *Guardian's ledger,* the old woman who'd handed me the keys had called it, back when I was nineteen and desperate enough to take a job guarding a door to another world for rent money and a crash course in concealment charms. *It keeps its own records. Don't read them. Don't touch it. Feed it a drop of blood once a season and it'll stay quiet.*

She hadn't mentioned what to do if it started keeping records about me.

At half past three in the morning I gave up on sleep, lit a candle stub, and went down to the cellar in my nightgown with a poker held the way I hold pokers when I'm pretending they're useful: badly, and mostly for comfort.

* * *

The cellar smells the way all cellars smell—damp stone, old wood, the ghost of pickled things—but under that there's the gate's own scent, which is harder to name. Ozone, maybe. Or the air after lightning, or the moment just before snow. People pay good money for candles that smell like that moment. I get it free, every day, and mostly I try not to think about what it means.

The gate itself sits at the back wall behind a false shelf of preserves. It doesn't look like much—a seam in the stone, hairline-thin, running floor to ceiling in a shape that's almost a door if you squint and don't ask questions. The charm-work layered over it has taken me five years to build and maintain: nine interlocking veils, each one keyed to a different sense, so that anyone who wandered down here would see preserves, smell preserves, and—if they were magically inclined—feel a very convincing nothing.

The ledger rests on a lectern beside it. It's a heavy thing, bound in leather gone dark as dried blood, with a clasp shaped like two hands clasping. In five years it has opened exactly twice on its own: once when I first took the post (it showed me a page of instructions and then snapped shut like a disapproving schoolmistress), and once when a rat got into the cellar and it displayed, in elegant copperplate, the word *PEST* until I dealt with the situation.

So when I came down the stairs and saw it lying open, glowing faintly, I did what any sensible person would do.

I went back up two steps.

Then I felt ridiculous, because it's my cellar, my gate, my impossible book, and if I couldn't face my own inventory, I had no business calling myself a guardian. I came back down, candle raised, poker lowered to a more dignified angle.

The page glowed the way coals glow—not bright, but warm, as though the ink itself remembered fire. And the ink was moving. Not swimming or crawling, nothing vulgar like that. It was simply rearranging itself with the unhurried confidence of something that has been waiting a long time and knows exactly where everything goes.

I set the candle down. My hand shook. I noted this with the detached interest of a woman watching someone else's hand shake.

"Right," I said aloud, because talking to haunted furniture is only madness if no one answers. "What are you?"

The ink finished settling.

And there he was.

August. My August—the stranger from the market stall, the man who'd bought a luck-charm and looked at me like I was a question he'd been asking his whole life without knowing it. His face, rendered in fine engraved lines a century old, stared up at me from the page with the particular solemnity of old portraits: the strong jaw, the slightly crooked nose, the eyes that even in ink seemed to be holding back some private joke.

Beneath the portrait, in letters that had been inked before my grandmother learned to write:

*THE EMBER RETURNS.*

* * *

Here is the thing about vertigo: it isn't fear exactly. Fear has direction—it points away from the thing scaring you. Vertigo points everywhere at once. It's the floor forgetting which way is down. It's your whole life tilting on its axis while the teacups stay perfectly still on their shelf, because apparently the universe can reorder reality but wouldn't dream of disturbing the crockery.

I sat down on the preserve shelf's step stool and looked at the face of a man I'd met eleven hours ago, drawn by an artist who had been dead longer than Ashfall City had been called Ashfall City.

"That's not possible," I told the ledger.

The ledger, being a ledger, did not argue. It simply turned the page.

I should mention that the ledger turning its own pages is deeply unsettling in a way I cannot adequately describe. It makes a sound like a sigh. It moves with intention. Books are not supposed to have intentions. Books are supposed to be the most honest things we have—they sit where you leave them, they say what was written, they don't develop opinions overnight.

This one turned to a new page and presented me with a column of names.

I recognized the format immediately, because I'd seen it once, five years ago, on the instruction page: the covenant roll. Names of those bound to the gate. The old woman's predecessor. Her predecessor's predecessor. A list going back and back, each name in a different hand, each hand a different century, until the ink went strange and archaic and finally became marks that weren't letters at all but sigils, older than writing, older probably than the concept of trying.

And near the bottom of the column, in fresh ink that gleamed wet as I watched, a new entry was writing itself:

*August — the ember returns.*

No surname. No date. Just his name, appearing letter by letter in that same copperplate, as though the book had known how to spell him for a hundred years and had merely been waiting for permission.

"Who wrote you?" I whispered.

The ink paused. Then, beneath the entry, smaller, almost apologetic:

*You did not ask the right question yet.*

"Oh, wonderful," I said. "A cryptic book. Every guardian's dream."

But my heart was hammering, and not from the fright. From the other thing. The thing I'd been carefully not feeling since last night, since he'd smiled at me across the charm stall and the luck-charm between us had hummed like a struck bell. Because here was the truth I'd been carrying around all day like a stone in my shoe:

When I saw his face in the market, I hadn't thought *he's handsome*, though he was. I'd thought *there you are.* The way you think it about a house you dreamed once. The way you think it about a song you didn't know you knew.

And now a century-old book was telling me I wasn't mad. Which was somehow worse.

* * *

I did what I always do when the world misbehaves: I made tea.

Not in the cellar—I'm eccentric, not feral. I went up, put the kettle on, and stood in my kitchen watching it heat while I conducted a rational inventory of explanations.

One: hallucination. Unlikely. My charms include a clarity-ward against enchantment, and I've never once hallucinated anything with such excellent penmanship.

Two: forgery. Someone had planted the book, or altered it, to—to what? To lure me? To lure *him*? Who? How would anyone have known I'd meet August at the market yesterday? Unless they'd arranged the meeting, which opened a whole corridor of doors I did not want to walk through, because at the end of that corridor was the thought: *he sought me out*, and I liked him too much already to let that thought have legs.

Three—and this was the one I kept circling back to, the one that made the tea taste like tin—three: it was true. The prophecy was real, it was old, and it had been sitting in my cellar the entire time, patient as geology, waiting for me to bring home the wrong man from the right market.

The kettle screamed. I poured. I drank standing up, which my mother would have called barbaric, and then I went back down to the cellar, because I am my mother's daughter in stubbornness if nothing else.

The ledger waited where I'd left it, open, patient. The candle had burned down to a thumb's width. The gate hummed its low note, and I could have sworn—though I'd swear differently under questioning—that the hum had shifted pitch since I arrived. Warmer. Almost pleased.

"Don't you start," I told it. "You've been quiet for five years. Whatever this is, it's new, and I don't trust new."

The ledger rustled. Turned a page.

More text now, unspooling in that confident copperplate, and I read it standing three feet away because I refused to get closer, a boundary I maintained for nearly four minutes before curiosity won, as it always does, as it is my defining flaw and possibly my defining feature:

*The ember sleeps in mortal flesh. When it wakes, the door will know its keeper. What was sundered shall be offered: seal or shatter, bind or burn. The choice belongs to the ember. The cost belongs to the keeper.*

"The cost," I repeated. "What cost? Whose cost?"

The ink did its maddening pause-and-settle routine, and then wrote, in letters that seemed almost gentle:

*Ask him what his grandmother said.*

My tea went cold in my hand.

Because here's the thing—I hadn't told the ledger about August's grandmother. I hadn't told the ledger anything. He'd mentioned her only once, briefly, mid-laugh, the way people drop important things into casual conversation: *my gran used to say I'd know my way somewhere before I ever walked it. She was a bit odd, our family.* I'd laughed. I'd thought nothing of it. I'd thought *charming, odd, mine, maybe*—the little greedy thoughts of a lonely woman letting herself hope.

And the book knew. The book had been listening, or the book had always known, or the book and the gate and the prophecy and the man were all one long story that began a century before I was born and had simply been waiting, all this time, for me to catch up.

I set the tea down on the step stool. I pressed both palms flat against my thighs to stop them shaking. And I looked at his face again—that engraved, patient, century-old face—and felt the vertigo come back, but changed now. Sharper. Because vertigo on a staircase is frightening, but vertigo on a staircase you realize you were always going to fall down—

That's something else entirely.

"He's just a man," I said to the empty cellar. "He bought a luck-charm. He laughs at his own jokes. He has ink stains on his fingers like he writes letters nobody sends." My voice cracked on the last one, betraying me. "He's just a man."

The ledger turned one final page. At the bottom, beneath the portrait, beneath the prophecy, in small neat script, it added a single line, and I have read it perhaps four hundred times since, and it has not gotten easier:

*The past does not wait, keeper. It prepares.*

* * *

Dawn came grey through the cellar's high window, the way dawn comes in Ashfall—apologetic, smudged with forge-smoke. I had been down there most of the night. My tea was cold sludge. The poker had migrated from my hand to the floor at some point, defeated.

Above me, the shop bell jangled.

My heart did something complicated and embarrassing, because I knew—knew with the certainty of the ledger's copperplate—who it was. Nobody else came to Pemberton Charms at opening hour except Mrs. Halloway wanting wart salve, and Mrs. Halloway knocked, she didn't ring, she considered bells presumptuous.

I climbed the stairs. I checked my reflection in the hall mirror—grey-faced, wild-haired, a woman who had spent the night arguing with furniture—and did what I could with cold water and a comb, which was not much, and went down to open the door.

August stood on my step in the early light, holding his luck-charm in his palm like a coin he wasn't sure was real currency. He looked rumpled too. He looked like he hadn't slept either. He looked, most unforgivably of all, exactly like his portrait.

"Lena," he said. "I need to tell you something, and you're going to want to sit down."

Behind me, faint but unmistakable, rising up through the floorboards of my shop like warmth through a winter window, the gate hummed.

And I thought: *the past does not wait. It prepares. And apparently it rings the shop bell.*

"I know," I said, stepping aside to let him in. "Start with your grandmother."
