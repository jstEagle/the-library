## 4. Ashfall's Long Law

There are three things you should know about lying to a woman who sells charms for a living.

One: she can tell. Two: she will not say so, because saying so would mean admitting the charm trade exists at all, and in Ashfall City that admission comes with a cell and a severance-brand. Three: when she finally does call you on it, it will be over tea, in a cellar that shouldn't exist, with a book that shouldn't exist open between you like a wound neither of you wants to look at directly.

I know all three now. I learned them in order.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Let me start where a coward starts — with the walk across town, before any confessions, when I still believed I might get out of this evening with my conscience intact.

* * *

The Wardens were out in force that night, which should have been my first warning. Ashfall doesn't do anything in force without a reason; the city is too tired for enthusiasm. It's a place built of brick and habit, all chimneys and gaslight and rain that never quite commits to falling, and its law fits the same temperament. The Ember Accords don't scream at you from every wall. They just sit there, quietly, the way a debt sits there. NO SORCERY. NO GATEWORKS. NO COVENANT ARTS. The signs are printed in grey ink on grey paper, because whoever designed them understood that fear works best when it's boring.

I'd been in the city nine days. Long enough to learn the tram lines, short enough that the smell of the canal district still surprised me. And every one of those nine days, I had walked past a certain narrow shopfront on Vellum Street with a hand-painted sign reading PEMBERTON CHARMS & CURIOSITIES — LUCK, LOVE, LONG LIFE (SMALL FEES) — and told myself tomorrow. Tomorrow I'd go in. Tomorrow I'd stop being a man loitering outside a woman's shop like a stray dog hoping for scraps.

The Wardens passed me twice on that final walk. First pair near the fountain square: tall boots, slate-grey coats, the long iron rods they carry slung at their hips like swords but which are not swords. Severance-rods. I'd read about them on the crossing over — how they don't cut flesh, they cut *connection*, how a mage branded by one stops being able to hear their own gift, the way a deafened man stops hearing his own voice. Second pair on Vellum Street itself, standing under the lamp outside the baker's, talking in the low unhurried way of men being paid by the hour.

One of them looked at me. Just looked. The way you look at a door you're deciding whether to knock on.

I smiled the smile of a man with nothing in his pockets but receipts, tipped an imaginary hat, and walked into Pemberton Charms & Curiosities like I owned stock in it.

* * *

Here is what I should have said, that first night, when Lena Pemberton sold me a luck-charm shaped like a knucklebone and our fingers touched over the counter and something in my chest went off like a struck match:

*I came here for you. Not for the charm. My grandmother died four months ago with her hand around my wrist and a name in her mouth, and the name was yours.*

Here is what I actually said:

"Nice weather."

It hadn't been nice weather. It had been the kind of drizzle that gets inside your collar and sets up housekeeping. But she laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, the laugh of someone who hasn't been laughed *with* in a while — and I thought, catastrophically, *oh no*, and the plan I'd built over four months of grief and train tickets and rehearsed sentences went up like paper.

That was six days ago. Since then: one dinner, two walks along the canal, three conversations that ran until the lamplighters came through, and one growing sickness in my stomach that got worse every time she did something unguarded — tucking chalk behind her ear, humming under her breath while she priced dried lavender — because every unguarded thing made the lie heavier, and I kept carrying it anyway, like an idiot, like a man adding stones to a pack he already can't lift.

Tonight she'd invited me to dinner. Actual dinner, upstairs above the shop, in rooms so small and warm and fiercely tidy that I understood something about her before she said another word: this was a person who had built a life the way you build a wall, one careful brick at a time, knowing exactly what it was keeping out.

And after dinner — after the stew, after the bread, after I'd laughed twice at jokes I couldn't remember five minutes later because my blood was full of wasps — she set down her cup, folded her hands, and said:

"You've been lying to me since the night we met, August. So. Let's go downstairs."

* * *

The cellar smelled of dust and old paper and, underneath both, something mineral and cold, like the air off a deep lake. She lit lamps as we descended — real flame, no gaslight down here — and the light did strange things on the walls, throwing shadows that seemed to arrive a half-second late, as if the dark down here needed time to make up its mind.

In the center of the room stood a table. On the table lay a book.

I want to be precise about the book, because everything else that followed depends on it. It was large — lectern-sized, chained at one corner to an iron ring bolted through the tabletop, though the chain hung slack, more ceremony than restraint. Its cover was grey leather gone soft with age, and across it, embossed in metal that had once been bright, was a sigil: a circle broken by a vertical line, mended with what looked like a stitch of fire.

"The guardian ledger," Lena said. Her voice had changed. Upstairs she was quick and dry and a little guarded, a woman used to being underestimated. Down here she sounded like someone reciting a duty. "Every gatekeeper keeps one. Mine opened itself the night after I met you." She looked at me then, and her eyes were steady in a way that cost her something; I could see the cost. "You want to tell me why?"

So I told her the truth. All of it, in order, because I'd learned somewhere in those six days of carrying the lie that if you're going to detonate your own life, you may as well do it cleanly.

"My grandmother's name was Maren Cole. Before she married, Maren Thorne. She grew up here — Ashfall, or what Ashfall was before the Sundering. She left when she was nineteen and never came back, and never said why, except once, at the end." I stopped. My mouth had gone dry. "She was dying for eleven weeks. Very dignified about it, mostly. Made jokes. Kept the nurses charmed. And then the last night, she gripped my wrist hard enough to leave marks, and she said—" 

I could hear her voice. Gods help me, I could hear it exactly, that papery rasp going suddenly clear, the way dying voices do, like a radio finding its station.

"She said: *Find the keeper on Vellum Street. Tell her the ember remembers. Tell her the book has his face.* And then she said my name — not August, she called me by my christening name, which nobody uses — and she said, *He'll come for the gate. Be kind to him, but don't let him carry it alone.*"

Silence. The lamps hissed. Somewhere overhead, faintly, the tram sang its wire-song up the hill.

Lena hadn't moved. But the book had.

I saw it happen and I still don't have better words than *saw it happen*: the ledger turned its own page. No wind, no hand. The page lifted, hesitated — the way a dog lifts its head at a familiar footstep — and settled flat, presenting itself to us like a card dealt face-up.

A century-old engraving filled the page. A face, inked in fine crosshatched lines, faded to sepia. A young man, dark-haired, sharp-jawed, with an expression I recognized because I shave it every morning: wary, half-smiling, braced for bad news.

My face. Inked a hundred years before I was born.

Below it, in a hand that trembled slightly, as if the writer had been cold or afraid or both: *the ember returns.*

"There," Lena said quietly. "That's why I asked you to dinner, August. That's why I asked you down here. Because I've spent six days trying to decide whether you were a Warden plant, a prophet, a coincidence, or a trap, and I'm very tired, and I'd like to stop deciding alone."

* * *

I sat down on an upturned crate because my legs had filed a formal complaint. For a long moment neither of us spoke. This is the part I keep returning to, afterward — the part I'd underline if my life were a book someone else was reading. Not the face in the ledger. The silence after. Two people sitting in a cold cellar with a century-old lie between them, both waiting to see if the other would run.

"I sought you out," I said. "Deliberately. I found the shop address in her papers — she'd written it forty years ago, on the back of a recipe for plum cordial, of all things. I came to Ashfall for you specifically. Every conversation we've had, every walk, the dinner tonight — I engineered all of it. Or I tried to." I rubbed my face. "You laughed at 'nice weather' and the whole script caught fire, but the intent stands. I lied by omission from the first minute. I'm sorry. I'd do it again, which is the part that makes the apology worthless, and I know that."

"Good," Lena said.

I blinked. "Good?"

"Honesty includes the ugly parts. If you'd apologized like a man expecting absolution, I'd have thrown you out." She pulled a stool over and sat across from me, elbows on knees, close enough that I could see the lamplight doing warm things in her eyes even while her voice stayed cool as well-water. "My turn, since we're confessing. There is a gate under this shop. Under this cellar, under the foundations, older than the city, older than the Accords. I inherited the keeping of it from my mother, who inherited it from hers, back past anyone who wrote it down. The Sundering — the thing that ended the age of magic, the thing the Ember Accords were built on top of like a tombstone — happened *here*. And the book says the man with your face is the one who decides whether the gate stays shut forever or opens again."

"And which is it?" I asked. "Shut or open? Which one am I supposed to be?"

"That's rather the problem," she said. "*Seal or shatter.* The ledger isn't specific. Prophecies rarely are. It's the universe's oldest trick — give a man a destiny with two doors and no key."

Above us, muffled by floorboards, a whistle blew. Once, twice — the flat, doubled note I'd learned in nine days to hate. Warden signal. Then boots, several pairs, on Vellum Street, unhurried, paid by the hour.

Lena was on her feet instantly, snuffing two of the three lamps, and the change in her was remarkable to watch: the woman vanished and something older stood in her place, coiled and quiet, a creature of thresholds. She pressed one finger to her lips and tilted her head toward the ceiling, listening.

The boots stopped outside. Directly outside. I could hear the tick of a cooling lantern, the wet breathing of the drizzle, a man clearing his throat.

Then a voice, filtered through the shop door above: "Evening, missus. Late hours for a charm shop."

"It's a curiosity shop, Warden, and it's closed. Have been since eight." Lena's own voice, pitched upward through the floor, easy, faintly bored, flawless. I felt a stab of admiration so strong it was nearly love — and then corrected it to nearly-fear, because I remembered whose face was in the book below her feet.

"Mind if we have a look round? We're checking premises on this street. There's been a report."

"A report." A pause with a raised eyebrow in it. "Of what, exactly?"

"Unlicensed gatemaking."

The word dropped through the floorboards like a stone through still water. Beside me, Lena had gone absolutely still — not calm stillness; the stillness of a hare that has heard the hawk. Her hand found mine in the dark. I don't think she meant it to. Her fingers were ice.

The pause upstairs stretched. Somewhere in it, I understood three things at once, the way you understand things when the blood leaves your head: that the report was about her, or about me, or about the hum of old power that had probably leaked out of this cellar the night the ledger opened; that the Wardens did not yet have warrant enough to force a door, or they'd have forced it; and that whatever happened next would decide whether I was a man who had dragged a stranger into danger for his grandmother's ghost, or something else.

"Tell you what," the Warden said at last, and I heard the shrug in it, the sound of a man choosing the easier shift. "We'll note it. Keep your shutters down, missus. Curfew bell's at ten."

Boots, receding. The doubled whistle again, farther off. Rain taking up the space they left.

Lena exhaled for what must have been the first time in a minute. In the single remaining lamp's light, she looked down at our joined hands, and then at me, and her expression was doing something complicated — anger and relief and arithmetic all at once.

"They'll be back," she said. "With warrant paper next time. That report didn't come from nowhere, and it wasn't coincidence that they walked this street the same week your face showed up in my ledger." She let go of my hand and straightened, and when she spoke again her voice had gone dry and wry and utterly determined, and I realized I was watching her decide something. "So here's where we are, August-whatever-your-christening-name-is. You lied to me. I hid a forbidden gate from you. The law of this entire city is currently pointed at this cellar like a loaded crossbow. And according to a book older than everyone alive, the two of us are in this together whether we like it or not."

"That about sums it up," I said. "Where do we start?"

She looked at the floor beneath our feet — at whatever slept down there in the dark under the foundations, patient as geology — and something crossed her face that I would spend a long time learning to read. It wasn't fear, exactly. It was the look of a woman about to show someone the room she'd kept locked her whole life.

"Down," she said. "I think it's time you met the gate."
