## 6. The Warden of the Door

Sera Voss had been a Warden for eleven years, and in that time she had learned exactly three things about the job.

One: the paperwork was worse than the danger.

Two: the danger was usually boring until it wasn't.

Three: the city lied to you constantly, and the only honest thing in Ashfall was the smell of the tanneries at dawn.

She stood across the street from Pemberton Charms & Curios at half past nine on a Thursday night, rain needling the shoulders of her grey coat, and thought about how much she hated Thursdays. Nothing good ever happened on a Thursday. Arrests happened on Fridays when people were drunk enough to be stupid. Informants met you on Tuesdays because Tuesdays were forgettable. But Thursdays were the night the Wardens' rotation put her on Ember Watch in the Lantern District, which meant walking past Lena Pemberton's little shop with its crooked awning and its hand-painted sign, and pretending — for the eleven-thousandth time — that she didn't know.

The lamp in the window was lit. That was normal. Lena kept the lamp burning late; Sera had told herself once, years ago, that it was because the woman liked to do her accounts after closing, and she had told herself that lie so many times it had worn smooth as a river stone.

But there were two shadows behind the glass tonight.

Sera's hand went to the ward-sigil at her belt before her brain caught up with her body. Two shadows. One was small and sharp-shouldered and moved the way Lena moved — quick, contained, like someone perpetually about to apologize for taking up space. The other was tall. Broad through the chest. He turned his head and the lamplight caught the line of his jaw, and something about the angle of it made the back of Sera's neck prickle in a way she couldn't name and didn't have time to examine.

*New man,* she thought. *Fine. People are allowed to have new men.*

People were also allowed to buy charms, drink tea, and laugh at things their dates said. None of that was against the Accords. Sera recited this to herself the way other people said prayers. *None of that is against the Accords.*

What was against the Accords was the faint, sweet-smoke smell drifting under the shop door and curling around her boots like a cat looking to be let in. Ozone and burnt sugar. Every Warden learned it in their first month at the Academy: the scent of active charmwork. Most civilians walked through it every day without noticing — a luck-knot here, a fever-charm there, half the apothecaries in Ashfall ran on quiet little enchantments nobody asked too many questions about. The Wardens noticed anyway. Noticing was the whole job.

And Pemberton's shop smelled like charmwork the way a bakery smelled like bread.

Sera had known that for five years. She had written nothing down. She had filed no reports. She had, on two occasions, actively misfiled reports written by other Wardens, and she thought about those occasions now with the specific discomfort of a woman standing on a frozen pond and hearing the ice talk back.

Because tonight the smell was stronger. Tonight it had an undertone she'd only caught twice before in her career — both times in the vaults beneath Belltower Row, both times coming off confiscated artifacts that had to be carried with tongs and buried in salt. Old magic. Covenant magic. The kind the Accords hadn't just outlawed but *named*, the way you name a disease.

And there was a stranger in the shop who made her neck prickle.

"Voss." Her partner's voice crackled through the wrist-band. Corren, three streets over, bored out of his skull. "Anything on your end? I've got a drunk singing hymns to a fishmonger and that's the highlight of my shift."

"Quiet," Sera said.

"You want me to swing by? We could get late supper after."

"I said quiet." She winced at her own tone. "Sorry. It's fine. Finish your rounds."

She watched the shop another full minute. The tall shadow laughed — she saw his shoulders shake — and Lena swatted at him with what looked like a dish towel, and the gesture was so ordinary, so *domestic*, that Sera felt something twist in her chest that had no business twisting. Jealousy, maybe. Or grief wearing jealousy's coat. Eleven years ago she and Lena Pemberton had been girls who shared stolen plums on the schoolyard wall, and now Sera stood in the rain cataloguing her friend's crimes.

*Friend.* She tested the word and found it still fit, barely, the way an old uniform fits after you've grown out of it.

Then the door opened.

Sera stepped back into the doorway of the shuttered cobbler's next door so fast she cracked her elbow on the shutter. The tall man emerged first, collar up against the rain, and paused on the step to say something over his shoulder. Lena appeared in the doorway behind him, holding a paper-wrapped parcel, and even from across the street Sera could see the color in her face — the flushed, bright-eyed look of a woman who has forgotten to be careful.

The man took the parcel. Their hands overlapped on it a beat too long. And then he turned toward the street, toward Sera, toward the dark, and the lamplight spilled fully across his face.

Sera stopped breathing.

She knew that face. Not from the wanted boards, not from the Academy's gallery of historical seditionists. From a book. A woodcut reproduction, yellowed, in a text so restricted that reading it without clearance was itself a flogging offense — and she had read it anyway, at nineteen, alone in the archive stacks, because she was Sera Voss and rules had always struck her as suggestions wearing armor.

The face from the ledger. The face inked beside the entry the archivists wouldn't translate aloud: *the ember returns.*

He was a hundred paces away and a hundred years old and he was smiling at Lena Pemberton like they had all the time in the world.

Sera's training took over, cold and clean. *Do not approach. Do not signal. Observe, mark, report.* Her feet did not move. Her hand found her wrist-band and hovered over it, thumb resting on the call-stone, one press away from summoning every Warden in the district.

The man glanced down the street — straight at her doorway, or near enough — and for one absurd heartbeat Sera was certain he saw her. Certain his eyes, pale as winter water, found hers through the rain and the dark and said, mildly, *not yet.*

Then he tipped an imaginary hat to Lena, and walked off into the wet streets whistling.

Lena stood in her doorway watching him go, and the look on her face was the look Sera had worn once, years ago, at a funeral. Love and terror in equal measure, braided tight.

Sera pressed the call-stone. Then, before the connection opened, released it.

*Not yet.* She didn't know why. She told herself it was strategy — follow the thread, find the spindle. A lone charm-seller was a misdemeanor. A charm-seller entangled with the face from the forbidden ledger was a conspiracy, and conspiracies required patience, and patience was the one virtue the Academy had actually managed to teach her.

That was the story she told herself. She was very good at telling herself stories. It was practically the family trade.

She waited until Lena closed the shop and the lamp went out, and then she crossed the street through the rain, and stood in front of the darkened door of Pemberton Charms & Curios, and knocked.

* * *

I was halfway through locking the till when the knock came, and I knew before I touched the bolt.

There are knocks and there are knocks. August's knock had been two light raps and a sheepish pause, like he was asking permission to exist. Customers knocked in little impatient triplets. But this knock was slow, spaced, deliberate — four beats, evenly measured, the knock of someone who has all night and wants you to know it.

Wardens knock like that.

I killed the last lamp — pointless, she'd already seen the light, they always see the light — and stood in the dark of my own shop doing the arithmetic I'd been dodging for five years. Five years of careful ledgers and legal inventory and charms subtle enough to pass as craftsmanship. Five years of being exactly dull enough to be invisible. And tonight I'd sold a stranger a luck-knot that sang, and laughed too loud, and stood in my own doorway glowing like a festival lantern, and somewhere out in the rain all that brightness had landed on the one pair of eyes in Ashfall that could recognize it.

I opened the door.

"Sera."

"Lena." Warden Sera Voss stood on my step with the rain running off the brim of her hat, and eleven years of history standing between us like a third person neither of us would look at directly. "Lovely evening."

"It's raining sideways."

"I said lovely." Her voice was dry as chalk. Her eyes went past my shoulder into the shop, quick and thorough, cataloguing. "You're open late."

"I was doing accounts."

"In the dark?"

"The lamp broke." Smooth, Pemberton. Truly, the finest liar in the Lantern District.

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, more the ghost of one, the memory of a smile we'd both been young enough to wear honestly. "May I come in? Routine inspection. New district protocols, post-Sundering compliance review. You know how it is. Forms in triplicate."

"I don't think you have forms."

"I don't," she agreed. "But it sounded official, didn't it?"

I should have refused. Every survival instinct I owned, every rule I'd built my double life on, said *refuse, deflect, get her off the step.* Instead I stepped aside and let a Warden walk into the heart of everything I'd spent five years hiding, and if you'd asked me why, I'd have said it was because refusing looks guilty.

That would have been a lie. The truth is that she looked tired. The truth is that under the grey coat and the hat and the eleven years, she was still the girl who'd split a plum with me on the schoolyard wall and sworn, with the total gravity of twelve-year-olds, that whatever came, we'd be on the same side of it.

The truth is I owed her a debt I'd never figured out how to name, and debts have a way of opening doors.

She moved through the shop slowly, trailing one gloved finger along the shelf edges, not touching anything. Rain dripped off her coat onto my floorboards. She picked up a knotwork ring from the display tray, turned it over, set it down. Picked up a dried lavender bundle, sniffed it, made a face.

"This one's just lavender," she said.

"Some of them are just lavender."

"Mm." She set it down. "Your gentleman caller. Tall fellow. Nice jaw. Whistled when he left, which I'll note is either a man with a clear conscience or a man with none whatsoever."

"He's nobody."

"Nobody." She turned, and the lamplight from the street caught her face, and for a moment the Warden fell away and what was left was just Sera, hollow-eyed, asking a question she didn't want answered. "Lena. What was in the parcel?"

"A luck-knot."

"What was really in the parcel?"

"A luck-knot," I said, "that I shouldn't have sold him. That I've never made anything like before and don't entirely understand, and that hummed when he touched it, and I don't—" My voice cracked and I hated it. "I don't know what he is, Sera. I don't know what any of this is. But I know that when he walked into my stall five days ago, my cellar woke up. And my cellar hasn't made a sound in five years."

The silence stretched long enough for the rain to change its mind twice.

Then Sera did something I will remember until I die, whichever death turns out to be mine. She reached up, unclasped her wrist-band — the Warden's band, the call-stone, the thing that could bring a dozen armed Wardens down on my shop in four minutes — and set it on my counter.

"Why?" I whispered.

"Because in about ninety seconds I'm going to ask you a question, and depending on your answer, one of us is going to be a criminal." She pulled off her hat. Her hair was shorter than it used to be, cropped practical, greying early at the temples. She was thirty-one. The job had aged her like weather ages stone. "So I'd rather ask it unarmed."

"Sera—"

"Do you remember," she said, "the night my family was taken?"

The floor tilted. Not literally — though with my cellar, I've learned never to rule it out — but the room went soft and distant, the way it does when the past puts its hand on the back of your neck. Of course I remembered. Everyone in the Lantern District remembered the Voss family. Taken at dawn, six of them, parents and four children, hauled out of their house by the first generation of Wardens under the brand-new Accords. Charged with hereditary sorcery. Never seen again.

I remembered because I'd been there. Twelve years old, barefoot in the street, having run the six blocks from my father's shop because I'd heard the bells and known — known the way you know things you can't explain — that it was the Voss house.

I remembered Sera's mother pressing something into my hands through the fence slats, fast, while the Wardens were distracted with the eldest boy. Something small and warm. Something that whispered.

I'd kept it hidden for a year. Then the searches started house to house, and I panicked, and I buried it in the cellar of my father's shop, and told myself I'd go back for it, and never did. And when my father died and left me the shop, and I finally went down into that cellar with a lantern and a shovel —

Well. That's a story about a gate, and gates keep their own counsel.

"She gave me something," I said carefully. "The night they took you. I hid it. I think — Sera, I think it's part of what's down there. Part of why the gate chose my cellar. Your mother's magic is woven into the wards, or the door, or — I don't know how it works. But I've known for five years that some piece of your family is under my floor, and I never told you, because telling you would either save you or destroy you, and I was a coward, and I chose—"

"Stop." Her voice came out strange. She braced one hand on my counter, and I watched eleven years of armor develop a hairline fracture right down the middle. "The question I was going to ask," she said, "was whether you're planning to burn the city down."

"And now?"

"Now I'm going to ask you something else." She lifted her head, and her eyes were wet and furious and terrified. "Does it hurt? Down there. Whatever it is. Does it hurt?"

"No," I said. "It waits. It's been waiting a long time. And lately, Sera — lately it's started to sound less like waiting and more like counting down."

She closed her eyes. When she opened them, the Warden was back, mostly, the fracture papered over with duty the way you'd patch a wall. She retrieved her wrist-band from the counter, turned it over in her fingers.

"Here's what happens now," she said. "In three days, the district does a scheduled sweep of the Lantern Quarter. Full census, premises inspection, the works. My name's on the roster. If I find nothing, I file nothing. If I find something—" She met my eyes. "Don't make me find something. Whatever he is, whoever he is, whatever's under your floor — you have three days to decide what side of the door you're standing on when I knock again. Because next time, Lena, I won't take the band off."

"And if I'm on the wrong side?"

Her jaw worked. For a second, just a second, she was twelve again, and so was I, and the plum was split between us on the wall.

"Then I'll do my job," she said quietly. "God help us both, I'll do my job."

She settled the hat back on, squared her shoulders, and became, in the space of a breath, entirely Warden — spine straight, face shut, the grief locked away where it had lived for eleven years. At the door she paused, one hand on the frame, rain waiting beyond her like a crowd.

"For what it's worth," she said, not turning around, "your lamp didn't break. I could see it from the corner. It was burning blue."

She stepped out into the rain, and was gone.

I stood in the empty shop a long while, listening to the water tick against the windows. Then I went down the cellar stairs, because I had to, because my feet decided before I did.

The gate was awake. More than awake — the old iron bands across its surface had begun, very faintly, to glow, and the whispering that usually stayed below hearing had risen to something almost like speech. Almost like a name.

And carved into the dust on the cellar floor, in letters no human hand had written, were two words:

*THREE DAYS.*
