## 7. Heirs of the Broken Covenant

The ledger did not close.

I want to be clear about that, because everything else in my life had rules. Charms went in drawers. Drawers went under counters. Counters stayed above the cellar door, and the cellar door stayed locked, and the thing behind it stayed quiet. Five years of careful, boring, invisible living, and now there was a book on my worktable at three in the morning, open to a page I hadn't turned to, glowing faintly like a cat that knows it's not supposed to be on the table and has decided to stay anyway.

I'd tried closing it four times. The fourth time, it closed itself and then reopened with a sound I can only describe as smug.

"Fine," I told it. "Fine. You win. You're a book. This is humiliating for both of us."

The candle guttered. Outside, Ashfall was doing its night shift: the clatter of the tram on Wexler Street, a drunk singing something patriotic and getting half the words wrong, the distant cough of the foundries that never fully slept. A city pretending, the way cities do, that nothing underneath it is listening.

The page showed four names.

Well — one name, inked fresh, still wet-looking after a hundred years: *August*. Beneath it, in the same impossible hand, three more lines, each preceded by a small sigil burned into the vellum rather than written. The sigils matched the ones on the gate below my floor. I recognized them the way you recognize your own handwriting — reluctantly, and with a sinking feeling about what you might have signed.

*The Quill of Two Courts.* *The Saint Who Refused.* *The Blade That Turned.*

And beneath each, in smaller script, an address or a description, as if the ledger were a particularly ominous travel guide. Under *The Quill of Two Courts*: *Canal District. She will tell you she owes you nothing. She will be lying.*

"Oh good," I said. "It flatters people."

My tea had gone cold hours ago. I drank it anyway, because that's the kind of decision-making you get from me at three in the morning, and because my hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

Here is what I knew, laid out honestly, which is more than I usually manage:

One. There was a gate under my shop. My grandmother had bound me to it before I could walk, and I had spent five years telling myself I was a charm-seller who happened to have a basement, rather than a warden who happened to have a stall.

Two. August. August with his crooked smile and his coat that smelled of rain and iron, whose face had been inked into this book a century before he was born. *The ember returns.* He'd kissed my knuckles last night outside the noodle shop like we'd done it a thousand times, and I had let him, and then I had walked home fast and put my hand flat against the cellar door until it stopped humming.

Three. The covenant needed four. The gate needed four. And the book was telling me — patiently, the way you'd explain something to a child — that I already knew where to find the other three.

I set down the cup. I looked at the second line. *The Quill of Two Courts.*

Bramble Quill.

Of course it was Bramble Quill. Half-fae smuggler, canal district legend, the woman who once sold me a luck-charm that actually worked, which in this city is either a miracle or a felony depending on which side of the Accord you're standing on. We'd done business maybe six times over five years. She called me "Pemberton" like it was a joke only she got. She had eyes the color of canal water and a laugh that made wardens nervous, and she owed me exactly nothing, and the ledger thought otherwise.

I should mention, since honesty is apparently the theme tonight: my first instinct was to burn the book.

Not metaphorically. I actually stood up, took it by the spine, and carried it toward the stove, and the whole way across the room it got heavier, page by page, until by the time I reached the stove it weighed as much as an anvil and I was holding it with both arms like a grandmother hauling laundry. It didn't hurt me. It just made its point. Some things don't burn. Some things just wait for you to stop being stupid.

"Fine," I said again, because apparently that was going to be my whole personality now. "Fine."

I put it back on the table. It lightened. Smug.

* * *

I slept two hours and dreamed of the gate.

In the dream it wasn't sealed. In the dream it was a doorway standing alone in a field of ash, and someone was knocking from the other side, politely, endlessly, the way you knock when you know no one's home but you've got nowhere else to be. I woke with my palm pressed flat against the floorboards, which I do not remember putting there, and the wood was warm as skin.

Right. Purpose replacing paralysis. That was the plan. I said it out loud while I dressed, because saying things out loud makes them real, and if I'm going to commit treason against the Ember Accords I'd like to at least narrate it.

Step one: close the stall. Not "take a personal day." Close it. Chalk the little sign — *Back soon, mind the step* — that I'd been writing for five years without ever meaning it. Step two: go find a smuggler.

The canal district at dawn smells like fish, tar, and bad decisions. The water runs green-grey between leaning warehouses, and the barges come in with cargo nobody asks about, and every third window has a charm nailed above it that some honest witch like me sold them, blessed against fire and flood and the tax man. I walked the towpath with my hood up and my heart going like a trapped bird, and I told myself the flutter was fear.

It wasn't entirely fear.

Because here's the thing I hadn't admitted yet, not even to the ledger: part of me — a large, shameful, five-years-starved part of me — was *relieved*. For five years I had kept the gate, alone, secret, carrying it up and down my stairs every day like a stone in my chest, and I had told myself that was strength. It wasn't. It was just loneliness with good posture. And now the book had handed me a list. Names. People. A reason to open the door instead of guarding it.

August's face floated up unbidden — he'd said he'd come by the stall today, and I'd said sure like a person with a functioning sense of self-preservation, and now I was off committing crimes instead. I'd leave him a note. Or I wouldn't. Or I'd see him tonight and say something devastatingly casual like "funny story, I'm a hereditary gatekeeper and you're a hundred-year-old prophecy, also I need to go blackmail a fae, rain check?"

The towpath curved past the salt warehouse, and there she was.

You always know Bramble Quill before you see her, because the air changes. Fae blood does something to a place — makes it sharper, greener, like the world is holding its breath. She was sitting on a bollard with her boots dangling over the water, eating an apple, arguing with a barge captain about a crate that was, according to her, full of legitimate eels.

"Bramble," I said.

She looked up. Her eyes did the thing they do — flickered, quicksilver, cataloguing me in half a second: hood, no stall, wrong hour, wrong street. "Pemberton." She took another bite of apple. "You're off your patch. Charm-witches die fast in the canals. Everyone knows that."

"I need to talk to you."

"You're talking to me."

"Somewhere with fewer ears." I nodded, very slightly, at the barge captain, who was absolutely listening, and at the gull on the roof beam, which in this district might also be listening. You laugh. I've seen Bramble pay a gull.

Something moved behind her eyes — not suspicion exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman who has been waiting for a bad morning and is almost pleased it finally arrived. She hopped off the bollard, flipped the apple core into the water where something large and polite swallowed it, and jerked her head toward the alley behind the salt house.

"Eels are legit, Kess," she called back. "We'll settle up Thursday."

* * *

Her bolt-hole was above a rope-maker's, up a staircase that creaked in a key I think was intentional. Inside: low ceiling, river light through salt-fogged glass, shelves of things that were definitely not eels. A kettle. Three cups, one chipped, all clean — a detail I noted and filed away, because a smuggler who keeps clean cups for guests is a smuggler who wants guests, whatever she says.

She poured tea without asking how I take it. She already knew. That unsettled me more than the ledger had.

"So," she said, folding herself onto the windowsill like she'd grown there. "Five years of buying my charms and never once asking my price, and now you climb into my pocket at dawn. Either you're in trouble, or you're the trouble." She sipped. "Which is it?"

I had a speech prepared. I'd rehearsed it on the towpath. Something measured, something gradual, easing her toward the idea of prophecy and covenant and destiny. I opened my mouth to deliver it.

And what came out instead was: "There's a gate under my shop and a book that won't shut up and your name is in it."

Silence. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere below, the rope-maker's wheel turned with its slow wooden groan, like the building itself clearing its throat.

"My name," Bramble said carefully, "is in a book."

"The ledger. The guardian ledger. It's — old. Older than the Accords. It keeps the gate's accounts." I pulled the folded paper from my coat — I'd copied the page at dawn, because I am not stupid enough to carry the actual book through the canal district — and held it out. "Four names. Four heirs of the old covenant. Yours is the second."

She didn't take the paper. She looked at it the way you look at a snake in your kitchen: respectfully, from a distance, calculating exits.

"What's it call me?"

"*The Quill of Two Courts.*"

The teacup stopped halfway to her mouth. Just for a heartbeat. Then it finished the journey and she drank, slow, and when she lowered it her face was smooth as canal ice — which is to say, smooth, and hiding currents strong enough to drown you.

"That's an old name," she said.

"It's a century-old name. Your name. Written a hundred years ago."

"Half-fae age funny, Pemberton." But her voice had lost its lazy edge; it was flat now, watchful. "Maybe the book met me."

"It didn't meet you. It named you. Along with a saint who refused something and a blade that turned, and—" I hesitated. "And August."

That landed. I watched it land. Her eyes came up sharp. "Your boy from Tuesday. The one who bought the hearth-charm and paid too much and blushed at you like a first-year apprentice." She'd seen that? I'd thought I'd been so professional. "What about him?"

"His face is in the book. Inked beside words written before either of us existed. *The ember returns.*"

For a long moment Bramble said nothing. She set down the cup, and she stood, and she walked to the window, and she looked out at the canal with her arms crossed, and I realized I was watching her decide something — watching the scales move behind her ribs.

Then she laughed. One short, hard note, with no humor in it at all.

"Do you know what my mother told me?" she said, not turning around. "Before they took her. Before the vaults." Her mother — taken in the Purges, eleven years back, one of hundreds of fae-blooded dragged to the Warden vaults for "registration" and never registered anywhere anyone could find them. Everyone in the canal district knew the shape of that story. Few knew its details. "She said: *you'll be asked, one day, little quill. Something old will come asking, and it will dress the ask up as a gift. When that day comes, count what it costs before you count what it gives.*"

"She sounds wise."

"She sounds dead," Bramble said, "which is what wisdom gets you in this city." She turned. The quicksilver had settled into something steadier and sadder. "So. Ask, gatekeeper. Let's hear the ask."

"I don't fully know it yet." Honesty. It felt strange in my mouth, like a shoe on the wrong foot, but it stayed. "The gate needs reforging. The covenant needs its heirs. I think — I think the Sundering broke something that four people were supposed to hold together, and the book thinks you're one of them, and I think the Wardens would burn this whole district if they knew any of it, and I have no right to ask you anything, and I'm asking anyway."

"There it is." She nodded slowly. "Honest. Good. My mother liked honest people. Said they were easier to rob." She crossed back to the table, and I thought — foolishly, hopefully — that she was reaching for the paper.

Instead she reached past it and picked up the teapot and refilled my cup, and hers, and sat down across from me, elbows on the table, chin on her laced fingers, suddenly very still in the way of her fae side — that uncanny animal stillness.

"Here's what happens now," she said. "You drink your tea. Then you go home, and you tell your ember-boy that Bramble Quill says hello and also says he should keep his magic in his pockets, because there are wardens sniffing the canals this week — real sniffing, Pemberton, not the usual theater. Someone high up knows something's stirring. They've doubled patrols on Belltower Row and pulled records from the deep vaults, and when the Order pulls deep-vault records, somebody's mother's locket goes missing in the shuffle." Her jaw tightened, just slightly. "Mine went missing Tuesday."

"Bramble—"

"And *then*," she said over me, "maybe, someday, if the stars align and the tea's strong enough, I'll tell you whether I believe in your book." She smiled, and it was the market smile, the one that sells eels. "But I'll tell you this for free, since you climbed all the way out here: I've known about the gate under your shop since before you did."

The room tilted, gently, like a barge taking wake.

"What?"

"Who do you think charmed your grandmother's cellar seals? Girl, who do you think your grandmother *called*, the winter she died, when the gate started humming and she couldn't climb stairs anymore?" She spread her hands. "Half of two courts, Pemberton. Doors are my whole inheritance. You think I wouldn't feel a gate breathing under Ashfall?" She shook her head, and for a moment the smuggler fell away and something older looked out — tired, fond, grieving. "I've been waiting five years for you to come up those stairs. I'd begun to think you'd sit on that cellar door your whole life."

I couldn't speak. Five years. Five years of thinking I was alone with it, and there had been someone on the other side of the wall the entire time, keeping her own kind of watch, drinking her own cold tea at three in the morning.

"Why didn't you *say* something?"

"Same reason you didn't." She shrugged. "Counting costs."

The rope-wheel groaned below us. Down on the canal, a bell began to ring — not the harbor bell, not a church. The sharp triple-strike of the Wardens' patrol signal, and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, the whistles answering from the direction of Belltower Row.

Bramble was on her feet before the third whistle, moving through her little room with sudden economy, sweeping things into a canvas bag — papers, a tin, a knife with a bone handle.

"Pemberton," she said, not looking up, "how attached are you to walking home the normal way?"

"Not especially. Why?"

"Because that's a vault-run signal, and vault-runs at dawn mean they're moving records, and if they're moving records today, then whatever your book wants from the deep vaults—" she slung the bag over her shoulder and grinned at me, wild and bright and terrified, "—is about to disappear. So. Gatekeeper. Do you want to stand around being paralyzed, or do you want to come steal your destiny back from the people who stole mine?"

I looked at her. I thought about the ledger, patient and smug on my table. I thought about August, somewhere in this city, walking toward a stall that would say *back soon, mind the step*. I thought about my grandmother calling a half-fae girl in the snow, and a locket in a dark drawer, and a door under my floor that had been knocking, politely, forever.

Purpose, I discovered, feels a lot like terror. It just walks faster.

"Lead the way," I said.
