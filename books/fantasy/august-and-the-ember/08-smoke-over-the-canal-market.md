## 8. Smoke Over the Canal Market

I want it on record that I did not plan to become a fugitive before lunch.

The plan—Lena's plan, and I use the word *plan* generously—was to walk into the canal market like two ordinary citizens buying ordinary fish, find a half-fae smuggler named Bramble Quill, and have a polite conversation about destiny. That was it. That was the whole architecture of our morning. Somewhere between "polite conversation" and "destiny," I assumed there would be tea.

Instead I am crouched behind a barrel of pickled eels with my heart doing something medically inadvisable, watching three Wardens chase a small green-skinned woman across the rooftops of the fish stalls, and Lena is gripping my sleeve and saying, very calmly, the way people say things right before the world ends: "That's her. That's Bramble."

"Are you sure?"

"She's carrying a crate of contraband charms while outrunning the law. Yes, August. It's her. It's exactly her."

The canal district of Ashfall City smells the way I imagine old coins smell if you left them in a fish tank for a hundred years. Water runs through everything here—not clean water, but working water, barge-water, water that has opinions. The market sprawls along both banks in a chaos of awnings and gangplanks, and above it all the morning fog hangs low and yellow, the kind of fog that makes the Wardens' lanterns look like eyes.

There are four of them. Wardens, I mean. Grey coats, iron buttons, those long hooked staves they carry that hum at a pitch I can feel in my back teeth. I'd seen them from a distance my whole life—the way you see weather coming—and I had always thought, *glad that's not my problem.*

Note to past me: it's your problem now. It's been your problem since the ledger opened itself in Lena's cellar and showed your face inked beside the words *the ember returns.* You just didn't know it yet.

"They're going to catch her," Lena said. She wasn't asking. She was already calculating, and I could see the arithmetic going badly behind her eyes.

Bramble Quill—I was learning her name even as I learned her silhouette—was fast. Faster than any human should be, all elbows and ankles and a green braid whipping behind her like a banner. But she was carrying the crate, and the crate was heavy, and the rooftops were slick with canal fog, and when she leapt from the chandler's roof to the net-mender's, her foot skidded on wet shingle and the whole market seemed to inhale.

She caught the edge. Barely. Hung there by one hand over a fifteen-foot drop onto stacked crab pots, crate tucked under her other arm like it was a sleeping child.

One of the Wardens reached the roof edge and raised his stave. The hum deepened. I felt it in my sternum this time, a cold pulling sensation, like the air being wrung out like laundry.

"No," Lena breathed. "That's a severance hook. If he tags her with that—"

"What happens?"

"It cuts what she is off from where she keeps it." Lena's knuckles had gone white on my sleeve. "Half-fae who get severed don't die. They just... stop being half of anything. They go hollow."

The Warden swung the hook down toward Bramble's dangling hand.

And here is the thing I need to be honest about, because apparently honesty is going to be the theme of whatever this is that's happening to my life: I didn't decide anything. There was no noble thought, no brave calculation, no moment of heroic resolve scored by strings. What there was, was this: a woman hanging over crab pots, a hook falling toward her hand, and a feeling in my chest like a struck bell that would not finish ringing.

I stood up out from behind the eel barrel.

"August—" Lena hissed.

I put out my hand. Not toward Bramble. Toward the hook. And I said—out loud, like an idiot, like a man hailing a cab—"No. Stop."

The world stopped.

Not metaphorically. Not dramatically-with-slow-motion-and-a-soundtrack. The hook stopped. Mid-air. Three feet above Bramble's fingers, it hung there, humming its cold hum, quivering like a dog told to stay. And then—this is the part I still can't make peace with—it turned around. The same way a compass needle turns. It pointed at me.

Every Warden in the market went still. Every one of them looked at the hook, and then followed its pointing tip, and found me standing in the open with my hand out like I was conducting an orchestra of one very confused piece of government equipment.

"Oh," I said. "That's not good."

* * *

Let me back up. Because I've been telling this story forward, and forward isn't how it happened. Forward is how it feels now, looking back. At the time it happened sideways, in pieces, the way a mirror breaks.

Two hours earlier, Lena and I had come down the canal stairs together, and she had been giving me instructions the entire way, in the low rapid voice of a woman who has spent five years memorizing survival and is now entrusting the syllabus to a man she met eight days ago because a book told her to.

"Rule one," she'd said. "In the canal district, don't look at anyone directly unless they look at you first. Rule two: if someone offers you something free, it costs more than anything sold. Rule three—"

"Never trust a rule that comes in threes?"

She gave me a look. It was becoming her signature look, and I was becoming embarrassingly fond of it. "Rule three: if we see grey coats, we walk away. We do not run. Running is an admission. We walk away like we're bored."

"Wardens bore me anyway," I said. "Terrible conversationalists. All 'halt' this and 'identify yourself' that."

"You're enjoying this too much."

"I'm terrified," I said, which was true, "and when I'm terrified I make jokes, which is also true, and which my grandmother used to say would either save my life or end it." I hadn't meant to mention my grandmother. Her name came out of my mouth the way names do when you're nervous—uninvited, warm, a little guilty. Lena heard it. I watched her hear it. She didn't ask. She'd been very careful not to ask about my grandmother since the night I told her why I'd really come to Ashfall, since the dying words, since the map drawn on the back of a recipe card that led, of all the shops in all the city, to hers.

We crossed the footbridge over the main channel. Below us, barges loaded with ice and eels; above us, laundry lines strung between leaning houses like the rigging of a ship that had decided to become a neighborhood. And everywhere, woven into the noise of the market, a second market—the quiet one, the one you only see if you know to look. A woman selling "luck" in paper twists. A boy with a tray of bottled fog, each bottle a slightly different color of gray. A man mending nets who was, I realized as we passed, mending nothing, his hands moving over knots that glowed faintly when his fingers touched them.

"This whole place is illegal," I murmured.

"This whole place is *necessary,*" Lena corrected. "There's a difference. The Accords made magic illegal. They couldn't make it unnecessary. So it went underground, and it got poorer, and meaner, and smaller—and the people who kept it alive got called smugglers instead of priests." Her jaw tightened. "Bramble Quill runs more charm through this district than anyone. Half-fae. Mother was fae, from the far side of the gate. Father was a lock-warden, back when wardens guarded doors instead of hunting people."

"And she'll help us because...?"

"Because the ledger says she's one of the four. Because her mother came through the gate before it closed and never went home. And because—" Lena hesitated, and I watched her weigh how much truth to spend, "—because everyone in this district owes their life to somebody, and someday soon she'll owe hers to us. That's usually enough."

It should have been enough. It would have been enough, if the universe had any respect for plans.

We found her stall—or where her stall should have been—a shuttered booth near the ice house with a painted sign reading QUILL & DAUGHTER, FINE GOODS, NO QUESTIONS. The shutter was down. Through the gap beneath it, I could see scattered stock, a knocked-over stool, and one shoe. Just one. Small, green leather, buckled.

Lena went very still in the way she does, the way that makes the air around her seem to hold its breath too.

"A raid," she whispered. "They hit her stall. This morning. She ran."

"How do you know she got away?"

"Because there's one shoe and no blood." Lena was already moving. "Because Bramble Quill has escaped eleven raids in six years and the Wardens have started betting on her. Come *on.*"

So we ran—which violated rule three immediately, though in our defense, rule three presumed we weren't chasing anything—and we ran through the market following the sound of shouting, and that is how we arrived at the eel barrels, and the rooftops, and the hook, and the moment where I stood up and told a piece of Warden equipment *no* and it listened.

* * *

Here is what happened next, in order:

The hook hung in the air, pointing at me, humming.

Bramble Quill, still dangling by one hand over the crab pots, looked up at the frozen hook, then down at me, and said in a voice like gravel soaked in honey: "Well. *Finally.*"

The lead Warden—a broad woman with a scar through one eyebrow and the particular stillness of someone deciding whether today is the day she breaks the law or enforces it—said, "Ember-touched. On my count, take him alive. The High Warden wants—"

She didn't finish. Because Lena moved.

I have seen Lena Pemberton angry exactly twice now, and both times it looked less like anger and more like weather arriving. She stepped out from behind the eel barrel, threw back her hood, and said, in a voice pitched to carry across the whole market: "Warden-Captain Hesk! Tell the High Warden that the charm license of Pemberton & Daughters, Ashfall Old Quarter, is backed by forty years of unbroken compliance, and that if her severance hooks touch so much as a hair on that smuggler's head, every licensed charm-seller in this city will close their stalls the same hour, and we will see how the Wardens like explaining to the Council why the tax rolls dropped by a third overnight."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

The scarred Warden—Hesk, apparently—looked at Lena for a long moment. "Pemberton," she said slowly. "You're the one who files the quarterly reports. In triplicate." Something almost like respect flickered under the suspicion. "Nobody's ever threatened the High Warden with *accounting* before."

"I find terror works best when it's bureaucratic."

And that—that absurd, magnificent, completely fabricated bluff—might actually have worked. I believe it might have. Hesk's stave had dipped an inch; two of the other Wardens were visibly recalculating; the fourth had glanced back toward the Belltower, where orders would have to come from.

But the universe, as established, has no respect for plans. Mine, Lena's, or Hesk's.

Because the fourth Warden panicked. He was young—young enough that fear sat on him like a borrowed coat—and he saw the ember-light, because yes, there was ember-light, my hand had begun to glow somewhere in the last thirty seconds like I'd grabbed a stove, and nobody had informed me— and he did the thing frightened men with weapons do.

He swung his stave at Lena.

I didn't think. I want to keep saying that, because it's the only part of this I can defend: there was no decision, no strategy, no understanding of what I was doing. There was just the bell in my chest ringing again, louder, and my body moving between the stave and her, and my hand coming up—

—and the world filled with light.

Not firelight. Not lightning. Something older than either, the color of the space inside the ledger, the color of the word *ember* when it's not a word anymore but a temperature. The stave came apart in the young Warden's hands—not broken, *unmade*, unraveled into a drift of gray ash that fell around him like snow. The force of it lifted him off his feet and set him down ten feet away, sitting in a puddle, entirely unhurt, staring at the stubs of his weapon with the expression of a man whose worldview has just been repossessed.

And every lantern in the canal market—every lamp, every candle, every bottled fog and paper twist of luck and glowing knot in the net-mender's hands—flared at once. Gold. All of it gold. For one heartbeat the whole district burned like the inside of a struck bell, and every soul in it turned to look at me.

Then it went out. All of it. And the dark that rushed back in felt like held breath.

"Well," said Bramble Quill, dropping lightly from the roof edge to the top of the crab pot stack, landing on the highest pot with her crate balanced impossibly on her hip, "either I'm dead and this is a very specific heaven, or the prophecy finally sent somebody worth the wait." She grinned at me. She had too many teeth, in the charming way, and eyes the color of river glass. "Which one of you is August?"

"That depends," I managed, "on whether that question comes with a way out of here."

"Babe." She hopped down, landed beside us, and slung her free arm around my shoulders like we'd gone to school together. "Everything I sell comes with a way out. Question is the price." Her grin sharpened. "And sweetheart, after *that* little fireworks show? The price just went up."

* * *

We went out through the ice house.

Bramble led, because of course she did; she moved through the canal district the way water moves through the district, which is to say via routes that shouldn't exist and don't appear on any map. Under the ice house, through a door hidden behind hanging salted cod, along a plank walkway built in the gap between two buildings where no gap should fit a person, over a fence, down a drainpipe, across the roof of a barge (the barge owner, poling past below, did not look up, which told me everything about how normal this was), and finally into a boathouse that smelled of tar and old rope, where she finally set down the crate she had carried through the entire escape without once putting it down.

Lena was breathing hard. I was breathing harder. Bramble was breathing normally, which I chose to find insulting rather than impressive.

"Eleven raids," Lena said, straightening her hood. "You let them catch you mid-run on purpose. You wanted witnesses."

Bramble flopped onto a coil of rope, produced a flask from somewhere in her many pockets, and took a pull. "Course I did. Wardens watch my stall twice a season. Best time to move real stock is when they're busy chasing decoy stock." She waggled the crate. "This is decoy stock. My real inventory's been on a barge since dawn." She looked up at me, and the humor drained out of her face like water out of a cracked cup, leaving something older and much more careful underneath. "What I didn't plan on was the bait drawing a *third* party." She pointed the flask at me. "Sit down, ember-boy. You're swaying."

I sat. On a barrel, as it happens. My career with barrels continues.

"Now," she said, and her voice changed—dropped its performance, picked up weight. "I'm going to ask you a question, and you're going to answer it honest, because I watched you unravel a Warden stave without breaking a sweat and I watched the light come off you in a color I have only ever seen one other place in my life." She leaned forward. River-glass eyes, fixed on mine. "My mother came through the gate when I was four years old. Before it sealed. She spent the rest of her life trying to tell people what the far side looked like, and nobody believed her, and the thing she described most—the thing she drew, over and over, until the drawings wore holes in the paper—was a light the color of what came off you back there." Her voice went quiet. "So. Honest answer. What are you?"

The boathouse creaked. Somewhere outside, gulls argued over the canal. Lena had gone still beside me, watching me the way she watches everything—like the answer matters more than she'll admit.

And I thought about lying. About saying *I don't know,* which was mostly true, or *just a man from the provinces,* which was technically true, or *my grandmother sent me,* which was the truest thing I had and the hardest thing to say out loud, because saying it made it real, made her death real, made the recipe card and the map and the words she whispered at the end real—

"My grandmother died in the spring," I said. "Last thing she said to me was 'find the keeper of the door in Ashfall, tell her the ember remembers.' I didn't know what it meant. I still don't, mostly. I came here because it was the last instruction she ever gave me and I loved her too much to leave it unfinished." I spread my hands, and they were shaking now, now that it was safe to shake. "As for what I am—I lit every lamp in a market district by accident twenty minutes ago. I stopped a Warden hook by telling it no. I don't know what I am. But I'm starting to be afraid that the ledger in Lena's cellar knows better than I do."

Bramble studied me for a long time. Then she looked at Lena, and something passed between the two of them—some old current, some recognition of two women who had both spent their lives keeping things hidden.

"The ledger," Bramble repeated. "A guardian ledger? Iron-bound, writes itself, smells like a library fire?"

Lena blinked. "You've *seen* one?"

"Saw my mother's copy burn the day the Wardens took her. Wouldn't burn, actually. That's the wrong word. It *left.* Went up in gold light and was gone, and the ash spelled out one word on the floorboards." Bramble's jaw worked. "*Four.*"

The word sat in the boathouse air like a stone dropped in still water.

"Four," Lena said softly. "The covenant needs four heirs. The ledger named you second. There's a third name, and a fourth, and we don't know them yet, and now the Wardens know what August looks like, and the High Warden knows he exists, and by tonight every grey coat in Ashfall is going to be looking for a man who glows."

"Mm." Bramble took another pull from the flask, capped it, and stood up in one fluid motion, cracking her neck. "Well then. Good news and bad news." She kicked the crate open with one heel. Inside, packed in straw, lay dozens of small iron keys, each one faintly, warmly gold. "Bad news: you're both officially the most wanted people in this city, and I'm now accessory to the crime of *existing loudly.*"

"And the good news?" I asked.

Bramble grinned that too-many-teeth grin, and for the first time it reached all the way to her eyes.

"Good news is my mother left me a boat, a route, and a list of every soul in three provinces who ever owed the Quill family a favor. If we're gathering heirs, ember-boy, we're going to need all three." She slung the strap of the crate over her shoulder and headed for the boathouse's water-door, where a slim black skiff bobbed in the gray morning light. "First stop, the vault records under Belltower Row. Because if the Wardens confiscated my mother's locket in that raid this morning—and they did, I counted the manifest through the window while I was fleeing for my life—then I want it back. And you two are going to help me steal it."

Lena made a sound that was half laugh, half prayer. "We met you ten minutes ago."

"Fifteen," said Bramble Quill, stepping into the boat and holding out one green hand to each of us in turn. "And in fifteen minutes you've set the whole canal alight and declared war on the High Warden's accounting department. Face it, loves—we're family now. Family steals lockets together."

Outside, far off across the water, the bells of Belltower Row began to ring. Not the hour. Not the quarter.

The alarm.

Bramble's grin faded by exactly one tooth. "Get in the boat," she said. "Now."
