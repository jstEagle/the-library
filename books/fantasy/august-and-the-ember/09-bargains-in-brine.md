## 9. Bargains in Brine

There are three ways to get a confession out of Bramble Quill, and none of them are legal.

The first is money. The second is a knife held by someone who looks like they'd enjoy using it. The third—the one that got me, that night, in the reeking dark under the canal market with my boots full of brine and my ribs singing a song of complaint—is someone looking at you like you're a person instead of a problem.

The girl with the charm stall did it. Lena Pemberton. She crouched down next to me where I was slumped against a piling, offered me her flask, and didn't say anything clever while I drank. That's how she got me. Not the flask. The silence. Most people can't stand silence; they fill it with questions or pity or their own opinions about my life choices. She just sat there on the wet boards like sitting there was exactly what she intended to do with her evening, and let me breathe.

The boy—August, all elbows and apology—stood behind her wringing his hands like he'd personally invented guilt and was still working out the licensing.

"You're bleeding," Lena said finally.

"I'm leaking," I corrected. "Bleeding implies it'll stop."

She had a kit out before I finished the sentence. Actual salve, actual clean linen, not the canal-market garbage cut with tallow that smells like a candle factory's sins. She worked on the gash along my forearm without asking permission, which I respected, because asking would have given me the chance to say no, and we both knew I needed stitching more than I needed pride.

"Those Wardens were waiting for you specifically," August said. "They knew your route."

"They always know my route." I hissed as the salve found the raw edge. "I have a route. It's a good route. They've had eleven years to learn it and they still only catch me when somebody tips them off." I looked at her then. At him. "Somebody tipped them off tonight. Somebody who knew I'd be moving cargo for the Voss woman's people, and knew which lock, and knew which hour."

Lena's hands went very still on my arm.

"I'm not saying it was you," I said. "I'm saying it was *someone*, and the list of someones who care enough about me to sell me is short, and every name on it owes me money."

"Would you tell us if it hurt?" August asked. Which was an odd question. Odd enough that I stopped chewing on my own suspicion long enough to actually look at him.

He had that face. You've seen faces like it if you've spent time around temples or tide-pools—faces that look like they're listening to something underneath the conversation. Half-fae blood gives me a nose for that sort of thing. His whole self hummed faintly, like a struck bell that hadn't decided to stop.

"Why do you want to find me?" I asked. "And don't say fate. Fate is what people blame when they don't want to admit they made a choice."

August opened his mouth. Closed it. Looked at Lena.

"We think you're one of four people who can seal a gate under her shop," Lena said. "A gate between this world and the other one. There's a ledger. Your name's in it. So is his. We don't fully understand it yet, but the Sundering broke something, and the something wants fixing, and the book says it takes four."

I laughed. It cost me a rib. "You broke into a Warden chase, got me shot at, and dragged me into a flooded stairwell to tell me a *book* says I'm special?"

"The book is a hundred years old," August offered helpfully. "And it opens itself."

"It *what*?"

"And it has my face in it," he added, "inked before I was born."

I stared at them both. Somewhere above us, the market was closing—shutters banging, a fishwife swearing at her son in three dialects, the ordinary music of Ashfall's underside putting itself to bed. My arm throbbed. My satchel, thank the deep water, was still strapped across my chest, and whatever the Wardens had wanted badly enough to set a trap for, it was still mine.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about being half of anything: you spend your whole life being told you're half a person. Too fae for the mortal districts, too mortal for the courts under the hill. The Accords came along and gave everyone a tidy word for what I am: *contraband*. Eleven years I've run cargo through these canals because it's the only trade where what I am counts as a qualification instead of a crime.

And now two strangers in a dripping tunnel wanted to tell me I was part of a prophecy.

Prophecies, in my experience, are just debts with better handwriting.

* * *

We went back to Lena's shop because I refused to talk business anywhere with ears, and the canals have more ears than the Belltower has bells. Her shop turned out to be one of those narrow crooked places wedged between a chandler and a pawnbroker, the kind of storefront you walk past a hundred times and never once see. Inside it smelled of dried lavender and hot copper, and every shelf was a lie. Charms for luck, charms for love, charms against damp—little brass and bone trinkets that any licensed hedge-witch could legally sell, harmless as buttons.

Except I put my hand on the counter and felt the floor *hum*. Felt it through my palms, up my wrists, into the place behind my sternum where the fae half of me keeps its accounts.

"There's something under this building," I said.

Lena didn't even flinch. "Yes."

"How far under?"

"Far enough that the Wardens walked over it for a century without noticing." She lit a lamp and led me to a cellar door I'd have sworn wasn't there a moment ago, which told me everything about how good she was. Better than good. The wards on that door were old work, patient work, the kind of craft you can't buy anymore. "You can feel it because you're meant to. That's rather the point of you."

"Don't flatter me. I make terrible decisions when I'm flattered."

The cellar was cool and dry and lined with shelves of things that made my skin prickle—real charms, real workings, a lifetime of quiet illegal craftsmanship arranged with a librarian's tenderness. And at the center, on a reading stand like a saint's relic, lay a book bound in grey leather that looked like it had been grown rather than sewn.

It was open. Of course it was open. Books that open themselves don't bother closing for guests.

I leaned over it, keeping my hands respectfully to myself—you don't touch strange books, that's rule one of my entire profession—and read my own name in ink the color of dried blood.

*Bramble Quill, daughter of Marrow Quill, keeper of the third key.*

My mother's name. In a century-old book.

I want to tell you I took it well. I want to tell you I asked intelligent follow-up questions about provenance and metaphysics. What I actually did was go very cold and very quiet, the way I go when someone mentions her, and Lena—damn her perceptive heart—noticed immediately.

"You knew her," she said softly.

"Everyone knew Marrow Quill. She ran the same canals I run now, before the Sundering, when the trade was honest smuggling instead of survival." I traced the air above the letters, not touching. "She died the year the Accords passed. Officially, she drowned. Unofficially—" I shrugged, and my ribs objected. "Unofficially, the first Wardens were very enthusiastic about their new law, and my mother was very unenthusiastic about complying. They came to our boat at night. I was nine. I hid in the bilge like she taught me, and I listened."

The lamp flame bent, though there was no draft.

"They took one thing off the boat," I said. "One thing. Not the cargo, not the coin, not the charts. A locket. Iron and river-pearl, with a covenant sigil on the back that I thought was just decoration. My mother wore it every day of her life and told me it was from *my* grandmother, and I believed her, because that's what daughters do." I finally looked up at them. "The Wardens keep confiscated goods in the vaults under Belltower Row. Everything gets catalogued and locked away forever, because heaven forbid a criminal's grandmother's locket circulate freely among the honest public."

"You want us to steal it back," August said.

"I want *you* to understand the terms," I said, "before you get any ideas about me joining your little book club. I don't do destiny. Destiny has never once bought me dinner. But that locket is the last piece of my mother that exists outside my own skull, and it has been sitting in a Warden vault for eleven years while men who never met her decide it's evidence." I straightened up, which hurt, and met both their eyes in turn. "Get it back for me, and I'm yours. Whatever the book wants, whatever the gate needs, keys and rites and prophetic errands, all of it. Fail, and you never saw me. I vanish so thoroughly even the canal rats will forget my smell."

"That's it?" August blinked. "That's your price? We expected—I don't know. Gold. Passage. Something expensive."

"This *is* expensive," I snapped, and heard my own voice crack on the second word, and hated it. "You think a locket is small? You think I'm pricing myself cheap? Fine. Here's the expensive version, since you need it spelled out: that vault is the most secure room in Ashfall City, and getting into it means going through Warden Sera Voss's jurisdiction, and Sera Voss has been hunting me personally since I was fourteen years old. She caught me twice. Both times she looked at me like I was a stain she couldn't scrub out. So yes, my price is that you help me commit the single most dangerous burglary in the history of this city, to recover a piece of jewelry, because it's the only thing in the world I have ever wanted that I couldn't steal myself."

Silence. Even the book seemed to hold its breath.

Then Lena said, "Done."

Just like that. No haggling, no conditions, no *well, actually, the risk calculus*. Done. She said it the way she'd stitched my arm—without asking permission, because asking would have given me the chance to say no.

"You should negotiate harder," I told her. "People will take advantage."

"People already have," she said. "For eleven years, if I'm counting right. I'm not going to be one more of them."

And there it was again. That look. Like I was a person.

I turned away and pretended to study the shelves until my eyes behaved.

* * *

August, bless his hopeless heart, tried to make conversation while Lena drew up plans—which mostly consisted of her pulling books off her own shelves and cross-referencing them with a memory that frightened me a little. He sat on her cellar stairs eating bread she'd thrust at him, and after a while he said, "Tell me about her. Your mother."

"Why?"

"Because you light up when you talk about her, even when you're angry. And because I'm starting to learn that the things in this prophecy aren't just tasks. They're *people*. And I'd rather know the people than the tasks."

I should have told him to mind his own business. Instead I sat down two steps above him, which is the closest to affection my kind admits to, and I talked.

"She sang when she worked the locks. Badly. Off-key in a way that I'm fairly sure was deliberate, because the fae side of the family says song is a door, and she liked her doors shut. She taught me every current in this city by making me swim them. She told me the Sundering was a lie—that nobody tore the worlds apart to save anyone, that fear dressed up as duty did the cutting—and I thought that was just bitterness talking, grief with an opinion." I picked at the linen on my arm. "Now there's a hundred-year-old book in a cellar telling me I'm a *key*, and I keep thinking: she knew. Somehow, some way, Marrow Quill knew what I was before I did, and she never told me, and I don't know if that was protection or cowardice or just the ordinary cruelty of parents who love you wrong."

"My grandmother did the same thing," August said quietly. "Died pointing me at this city like a compass needle. I spent months angry about it. I'm still angry about it, some days. But I'm starting to think maybe loving someone and lying to them can be the same act, if the truth would have gotten them killed."

"Fae proverb," I said. "The kindest cage is the one you never notice."

"Mortal proverb too, apparently."

We sat there in the lamplight, two orphans of two different disasters, and I felt something shift in my chest that I did not appreciate at all. A thread, thin as spider silk, tying me to these two idiots and their impossible book. I'd spent eleven years making sure I had no threads. Threads are leverage. Threads are wounds waiting to happen. Every smuggler worth her salt knows the safest cargo is the kind you don't care about.

But my mother used to say the opposite, late at night, when the brandy was gone and the stars came through the cabin hatch. *Nothing worth carrying is weightless, little bramble.*

Above us, Lena said, "I've got it. The vault intake records are kept in the annex on Belltower Row—separate building, lighter security. If we can find the locket's catalogue number and its storage location before we ever touch the vault itself, we can be in and out in under twenty minutes."

"You've done this before," I said, climbing up to join her.

"No," she said, and smiled, and it was not entirely a reassuring smile. "But I've sold charms to three different vault clerks, and people tell charm-sellers everything."

I looked at her—this quiet woman with a gate under her floor and a war coming for her, planning a burglary over tea like it was inventory day—and I understood, suddenly, why the book had chosen her name first. Not power. Not blood. She was simply the kind of person who, when shown a stranger bleeding in a tunnel, stayed.

"All right," I said, and stuck out my hand, because contracts matter, even the ones written in flesh. "Partners. One job first, then the rest. And Pemberton—if you die in my vault, I'm haunting you personally."

"Fair terms," she said, and shook it.

Her hand was warm. Mine was still shaking, though I'd deny it under torture.

Outside, far off across the rooftops, a Warden bell rang the hour—two strokes past midnight—and beneath its sound, so faint that only half-fae ears and guilty consciences could have caught it, something under the cellar floor sighed in its sleep, like a door dreaming of opening.

Three days to the new moon. Three days to plan the most dangerous burglary in Ashfall's history.

I'd had worse odds. I'd just never had anything I minded losing before.
