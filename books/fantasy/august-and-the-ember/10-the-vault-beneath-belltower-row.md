## 10. The Vault Beneath Belltower Row

The Warden vaults beneath Belltower Row had no door, technically speaking. That was the first thing I learned from Bramble, crouched beside me in the shadow of a rain barrel across the street, and it was also the thing that made me want to go home.

"No door," I repeated.

"No *door*," she agreed, grinning with too many teeth. "Doors are for people who expect visitors. The Wardens use a threshold-ward instead. Fold of space, keyed to blood and oath. You walk at the wall like you belong there and—mostly—you're inside. Mostly."

"And the 'not mostly' part?"

"Sometimes the fold closes early." She said it the way other people said *sometimes it rains*. "You get sheared in half. Very tidy. Very embarrassing for your family."

I looked at August. He looked back at me with the particular calm of a man who had discovered three weeks ago that he was prophesied and had apparently decided to meet every subsequent horror with the serene smile of someone reading a mildly interesting menu. It was infuriating. It was also, I was beginning to admit, steadying.

"So we don't walk," he said. "We're carried."

Bramble's grin widened. "Now he's getting it."

Her plan—if you could call something hatched over stolen eel pies a plan—relied on the fact that the threshold-ward recognized blood and oath, not intent. And that Bramble Quill, half-fae smuggler, had spent four years running contraband through Warden channels by way of a courier's oath sworn to a dead man whose bloodline still, technically, held a maintenance easement on the vault's lower archive. Dead men's paperwork, Bramble liked to say, is the most reliable magic in Ashfall City. Nobody audits the dead.

So we didn't walk into the wall. We were *pulled*—Bramble's oath flaring green as she pressed her palm to the wet stone and spoke a name that made the brick shiver—and the world folded around us like a letter being sealed, and then we were standing in a corridor of grey granite that smelled of cold iron and old paper, lit by lamps that burned without flame.

I had been in Warden custody once, five years ago, for eleven hours. They'd never found my charms; they'd found a girl with tired eyes and no family name worth pursuing, and they'd let her go with a warning. But I remembered this smell. My body remembered it. My hands wanted to shake and I told them they weren't allowed to yet.

"Lower archive," Bramble whispered, already moving. "Confiscated records, pre-Accord ledgers, anything the Wardens seized but couldn't bring themselves to burn. Your mother's locket came through here before it went up to the deep vault—we'll pass the intake desk. Stay behind me, touch nothing that hums, and if anyone sings out an alarm, you both run toward the smell of tallow. There's an ash-chute. It's disgusting. It works."

"And you?" August asked.

"I'm half-fae, love. If we're caught, I'm a hallucination brought on by righteous living."

* * *

The intake desk was unmanned. Of course it was—the Wardens had tripled their patrols since the canal market, since August had lit up like a festival lantern in front of forty witnesses and a very committed pigeon. Half the city's bells had been ringing on and off for two days. Somewhere above us, Sera Voss was presumably tearing her hair out by the handful, and I found, to my surprise, that the thought gave me no pleasure at all. Just a dull ache, like a bruise I kept pressing.

The archive stretched out in long aisles of shelving, and the shelves were wrong. Not disordered—*wrong*, in the way that only a charm-seller notices. Every confiscated object and document had been tagged with a suppression-charm, a little leaden sigil stamped dull grey, designed to smother whatever the item remembered. Thousands of them. Rows and rows of things that had been made to forget themselves.

"That's obscene," I heard myself say.

"What?" Bramble asked, not stopping.

"They're not just storing this stuff. They're *silencing* it." I ran my finger near—not touching—one of the tags. Even that close, I felt the item underneath flinch. A child's primer, maybe sixty years old, its letters trying to glow and being pressed flat. "Every one of these was somebody's inheritance. Somebody's grandmother's recipe, somebody's protection charm, somebody's—" I stopped, because my voice had gone strange.

August's hand settled between my shoulder blades, light as a moth. "Lena."

"I'm fine."

"You're furious."

"I'm *fine*," I said, and pulled a suppression-tag off the nearest shelf because apparently my hands had decided to stage a mutiny against my brain.

The primer exhaled. That's the only word for it. The letters warmed from grey to gold, and somewhere in the paper a voice that wasn't a voice sighed with relief, and I stood there in a Warden vault holding a dead child's schoolbook and shaking with a rage so clean and total it felt almost like clarity.

"Lena," August said again, closer now. "We have a job."

"I know what we have." I wrapped the primer in my scarf and put it inside my coat, against my heart, where contraband goes when it's personal. "Let's find your locket, Bramble."

* * *

We found the intake ledger first, which was how we found everything else. Bramble flipped pages with a smuggler's speed while August kept watch at the aisle's end, and I read over her shoulder: confiscation numbers, dates, disposition codes. Burned. Burned. Transferred. Burned. And then—

**Item 4471. Locket, silver, engraved. Recovered: subject Q.B., canal district. Disposition: DEEP VAULT — HOLD — BY ORDER OF H.W.**

"Hold," Bramble breathed. "Not destroyed. Four years and they never destroyed it." Her usual grin was nowhere. What was left of her face looked young, and fierce, and frightened. "Why would the High Warden hold a smuggler's mother's locket?"

"Because it isn't a smuggler's mother's locket," I said slowly, because the thought was arriving in me whole, the way the worst thoughts do. "It's a covenant piece. They knew what it was. They've known all along."

August turned from his watch. "What makes you say—"

"Because look at the *dates*." I took the ledger gently from Bramble and turned back. And back. Past the locket. Past five years of confiscations. Past ten. To the founding of the Accords, and then past *that*, into records that should not have existed, because the Sundering had happened in a single night and everything after it was supposed to begin from zero.

But it didn't begin from zero. There was a ledger before the ledger. Sealed with black wax. Stamped with a sigil I knew—I'd seen it a hundred times in my guardian-book downstairs, worn nearly smooth by centuries of thumbs—and beneath the sigil, a single line of inventory:

**Item 0001. Instrument, severance. One (1). Condition: used.**

The corridor got very quiet. Even the flameless lamps seemed to lean in.

"Bramble," August said carefully. "What is Item 0001?"

She didn't answer. She was staring past me, at the shelf behind the intake desk, where a single box sat apart from all the others, untagged, unsuppressed, dustless. As though it were maintained. As though it were *visited*.

I opened it.

Inside lay a record book—not a ledger, a *record*, the kind the old covenantors kept, written in a hand I recognized because it matched the hand in my cellar book, my family's hand, generations of Pembertons keeping the gate's accounts. Except these accounts weren't about the gate at all.

They were about the night of the Sundering.

And they were not a disaster report.

* * *

I have tried, in the hours since, to find a gentler way to describe what those pages said, and I can't, so I'll say it plainly: the Sundering was not an accident. It was not the gate failing. It was not realms colliding through no one's fault, the great tragedy that justified the Accords, the law, the wardens, the suppression-tags, the eleven hours I spent in a cell at nineteen years old wondering what was wrong with me.

It was a decision. Made in advance. Scheduled, provisioned, staffed. There were supply requisitions dated weeks before. There was a roster of names—covenantor names, gate-keeper names, names my family's book remembered with love—each marked with a small notation in the margin: *consented under misdirection.* There was a diagram of the gate itself with annotations in a different ink, a colder hand, showing exactly where to cut, and the cut was labeled not *breach* or *failure* but **amputation**, as though the gate were a limb and someone had simply decided the body would be better without it.

And at the bottom of the final page, in that colder hand, a signature and a justification, written out with the terrible neatness of a man doing arithmetic:

*The gate cannot be permitted to remain open, for the gate does not merely permit passage—it permits binding. Realms that bind grow entangled, and entanglement breeds attachment, and attachment between realms will end in love, and love between realms will end in ruin. Better one clean wound than a slow rot. Let them hate each other safely apart.*

Better one clean wound than a slow rot.

I read it twice. Three times. Bramble read it over my shoulder and swore in at least two languages, one of them possibly not human. August said nothing at all, and when I finally looked up at him, his face had gone very still, the way it did when the prophecy-light touched him—like a man listening to something far away.

"They lied," I said. My voice came out flat and strange. "Five years I hid. Five years I sold charms out of a fake stall and flinched at every bell and thanked the Wardens for *protecting* us, and it was—" The word wouldn't come politely, so I let it come impolitely. "It was a *lie*. They cut the worlds apart on purpose and then built a religion of safety on top of the wound and called anyone who grieved it a criminal."

"Not they," August said quietly. "*He.* One man. One signature." He tapped the page, gentle, as if the ink might feel it. "Everything else grew from that. Fear always recruits."

"Well, fear recruited an entire city." I closed the record book, and my hands were steady now, steadier than they'd been all night, because fury, it turns out, is an excellent substitute for courage. It burns hotter and it asks fewer questions. "My grandmother kept the gate her whole life and died thinking she'd failed. Failed! She didn't fail. She was *amputated*. Someone walked into her life's work with a diagram and a requisition form and—"

"Lena." Bramble's voice, low and sharp. "Footsteps."

* * *

We killed the lamps and folded ourselves into the gap between shelving units, and the footsteps came on—two sets, unhurried, the walk of people who owned the building. A lamp-flare swept the aisle. I pressed the record book to my chest and felt it press back, warm, alive, *on our side*, and I thought absurdly of my grandmother saying: *the books remember, Lena-girl. That's why they're dangerous.*

"—triple shifts until the Pemberton woman is found," one voice was saying, bored, professional. "High Warden wants the archive audited. Everything tagged by morning."

"Audit? Nothing down here's moved in years."

"Then it'll be a quick audit, won't it."

The light swept again, closer. Beside me, Bramble had gone absolutely still in the way of prey animals and professionals. On my other side, August had taken my free hand, and I realized distantly that neither of us had decided to do it. His palm was warm. Mine was not. Between us, faint as breath, the ember-light under his skin stirred—the same light I'd seen in the canal market, the light that had outed him to half the city—and I squeezed his hand hard, a silent *don't*, and felt it settle.

The footsteps paused at the intake desk. Paper rustled. The bored voice again: "Huh. Item 4471's flag's been lifted."

A silence with weight in it.

"Flag doesn't lift itself."

"Nothing down here moves, you said."

"I said nothing's *been moved*. Check the deep vault log."

More footsteps, faster now, receding toward the stair. Bramble exhaled against my ear: "Deep vault's the other direction, love, and we have maybe ninety seconds before they work out the locket's already loose. So. Do we run for the ash-chute empty-handed, or do we do something spectacularly stupid?"

I looked at August. August looked at me. And I want it noted, for the historical record, that I was the one who said it:

"The deep vault's on our way out anyway."

* * *

It was not on our way out. Nothing about the next twenty minutes was on anyone's way anywhere. But the deep vault was smaller than I'd expected—a round chamber behind a door of banded iron that Bramble's dead man's oath opened like it was yawning—and there, in a velvet tray built for crown jewels, sat a dented silver locket with an engraving worn soft.

Bramble picked it up with both hands, the way you hold something that might be sleeping. She didn't open it. She just stood there breathing, and August and I, for once, had the good sense to say nothing.

When she finally spoke, her voice was rough. "Four years I thought they'd melted it down. Four years I hated them for destroying the last—" She stopped. Swallowed. "They *kept* it. Why keep it?"

"Because it matters," I said. "To the covenant. Maybe more than we know yet."

"Then they know what she was." Bramble's thumb traced the locket's seam, not opening it. "They knew what my mother was, and they took her daughter's grief and filed it under *hold*." She laughed, short and awful. "Right. New rule. We're not stealing anymore. We're *repossessing*."

Behind us, far above, a bell began to ring—not the patrol-bell's rhythm but something older, deeper, a bell I felt in my teeth. August's head came up. "That's not an alarm."

"No," I said, because I knew it too, everyone raised in Ashfall knew it, though none of us had ever heard it for real. "That's the Sundering bell. Rung once a year, on the anniversary. Except—" I counted, and my stomach dropped. "Except it's not the anniversary."

The bell rang again. And again. And under its tolling, faint but unmistakable, came a sound I'd only ever read about in my grandmother's accounts: the deep, grinding groan of the gate beneath my shop, waking up.

August's hand found mine in the dark. "Lena. What did we just wake up?"

I clutched the record book—the proof, the fury, the truth—to my chest and ran.
