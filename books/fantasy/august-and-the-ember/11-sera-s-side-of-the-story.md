## 11. Sera's Side of the Story

The thing nobody tells you about wearing armor is that it never comes off clean. You unbuckle the straps, you set the pieces down, and still there's the shape of it pressed into your skin, the weight of it ghosting your shoulders, until you can't remember what your body felt like before it learned to carry steel.

I was eight years old when I first put mine on. It didn't look like armor then. It looked like a school satchel and a pair of scuffed boots and my mother's hand around mine, tight enough to hurt, as we walked past the Pemberton charm stall on Tallow Street.

"Keep walking," she said. "Don't look at the witch's daughter."

So of course I looked. Lena Pemberton was sitting on an overturned crate behind her father's stall, eating a plum with both hands like she was afraid someone would take it away from her, which — I would learn later — was exactly why she ate that way. She caught me looking. She didn't look away. Most people, when a Voss catches them staring, find something fascinating about their own shoes. She just raised one eyebrow at me, juice running down her wrist, like she was daring me to make something of it.

I made something of it. I made eleven years of something out of it.

* * *

Here is what the histories will tell you about the Sundering, if the histories are feeling generous: that the old covenant failed, that the gate between realms cracked, that wild magic poured through and burned half of Greyhollow province to glass before the first Wardens sealed what remained. That the Ember Accords were written in ash and grief, and that every man, woman, and child with sorcery in their blood surrendered it or buried it, and that peace has held for a hundred years because we — the Order of the Ashen Veil — keep it.

Here is what the histories will not tell you: what it costs to be the ones who keep it.

My family kept it first. Before the Accords had a name, my grandmother held a Warden's commission signed in the High Chancellor's own hand, and when the purges came — the rounding up, the registries, the severance-brands — she carried them out in Greyhollow because someone had to, and because she believed, truly believed, that mercy lived inside the law if you dug deep enough for it. My mother inherited that belief along with the commission. And I inherited it from her, the way you inherit a debt: without being asked, without being consulted, with interest accruing.

But that's the end of the story. Let me start where it actually starts, which is with a plum, and a girl who wouldn't look away.

* * *

We were friends the way children are friends — completely, without terms or conditions, before either of us knew friendship could have fine print. Lena's father sold charms that were technically legal, wards against damp and bad luck and colicky infants, little knotted things that any magistrate would call folk craft rather than sorcery, provided he never looked too closely. Lena sat behind the stall doing homework and pretending not to watch the street, and I started finding reasons to walk home the long way, past Tallow Street, past the stall, past her.

She taught me to skip stones on the canal. I taught her the Warden's oath, which I'd memorized off my mother's commission papers, all of us solemn and ridiculous, two girls of ten and eleven swearing to protect a city that hadn't asked either of us for anything. She laughed at the part about "unflinching vigilance." She said no one could be vigilant unflinchingly, it wasn't humanly possible, and I said that was the point, that the oath asked you to be more than human, and she got very quiet and said, "Sera, that's not a promise, that's a wound you're volunteering for."

I thought about that for twenty years. I'm thinking about it now.

Because here's the thing about Lena Pemberton, even at eleven, even at fourteen, even at nineteen when everything ended: she saw the shape of a thing before anyone else did. She saw what the oath really was. She saw what I couldn't — that I was already buckling armor onto a body that hadn't finished growing, and calling it honor.

And I loved her for it. Gods help me, I loved her for it, the way you love the person who tells you the truth about yourself before you're ready to hear it.

* * *

The year I turned seventeen, the Accords tightened. There'd been an incident upriver — a hedge-witch in Millbrook whose charms were found to be something more than knots and prayers, whose cellar was found to hold something worse, though what "worse" meant depended entirely on which report you read, and I've read them all since, and none of them agree, and that should have been my first warning. The Order responded the way the Order responds: expanded registries, mandatory inspection of licensed vendors, severance-brands for anyone found practicing beyond charter.

Lena's father's license came up for review in the autumn.

I remember the day because it was the last ordinary day. We sat on the canal wall with our feet dangling over the water, and she was skipping stones — badly, she never did learn properly — and she said, "If they take the stall, I'll manage. Da says we'll move inland, maybe. Start over somewhere they don't know the name Pemberton."

"You'd leave Ashfall?"

"I'd hate it," she said. "But leaving's better than branding." She threw another stone. It sank without skipping. "Your mother signed three brands last month. I heard."

"She follows the law," I said, and heard how it sounded even as I said it, the way a child hears the hollowness in an adult's comfort long before the adult does.

"So did the men who burned Millbrook," Lena said, not cruelly. That was the worst part. Never cruelly. "Sera. What happens if they inspect the cellar under our shop? Because there's a door down there. Da says it's just a root cellar door, says it's been sealed since his grandfather's time, but it hums sometimes, at night. And if it hums, then it's not just a door, and if it's not just a door, then—" She stopped herself. Shook her head. "Never mind. Forget I said it."

I didn't forget. I wrote it in my journal, because I was seventeen and dutiful and already, without knowing it, a Warden: *Pemberton cellar contains possible anomaly. Observe.*

Observe. As if love and surveillance could sit side by side in the same chest and neither would corrode the other.

* * *

They came for the Pembertons in the winter.

Not for the cellar — the inspectors never got that far, thank every god that listens or pretends to. For the charms. A routine audit found a knot-work ward in the stall stock that channeled more than folk craft should, and that was enough. License revoked. Stock seized. Father Pemberton given a choice between the brand and the road, and he took the brand, because he had a daughter with magic in her blood and a branded man's family drew less scrutiny than a fugitive's.

I stood in the crowd and watched it happen. My mother administered it. The iron smelled like the inside of a lock. Lena didn't cry. She stood beside her father with her hands folded and her face arranged into something smooth and empty, and when her eyes swept the crowd and found mine, she didn't look away this time either.

That was the last time she looked at me for six years.

* * *

You want to know what duty is? Duty is what my mother said to me that night, when I came home shaking and asked her, actually asked her, how she could stand it. She was sharpening her commission blade at the kitchen table, because my mother sharpened her blade the way other mothers knitted, and she didn't look up.

"Someone has to keep the wall," she said. "And the wall doesn't get to choose which side of itself it stands on."

"And if the wall is wrong?"

She set down the whetstone then. Looked at me for a long moment, and I saw — I see it now, I was too young to name it then — I saw that she was tired in a place tiredness doesn't reach. "Then the wall falls," she said, "and everyone behind it dies screaming, and it doesn't matter whether it was wrong. Keep the wall, Sera. Love whoever you like. But keep the wall."

Three years later the Sundering's aftershocks took her. Not magic — nothing so dramatic. A fever, in the end, the kind that moves through a garrison like a scythe through wheat, and the healers were stretched thin because half of them had been branded or fled, and my mother died in a cot in Belltower garrison with her commission blade on the table beside her and her last words to me were, "Is the wall holding?"

Not *I love you.* Not *forgive me.* *Is the wall holding.*

I said yes. She died believing it. And I put on her armor, literally, her actual breastplate, resized by a smith who charged me double because grief makes poor bargaining posture, and I reported for my commission, and I have worn it every day since.

Grief wearing armor. Lena would have seen it immediately. Lena always saw the shape of a thing.

* * *

Six years of silence. Six years of passing each other in the market district, her behind a new stall under a new name — clever girl, clever careful girl, selling luck-knots and damp-wards that were exactly, precisely, legally nothing — and me in Warden gray, and neither of us acknowledging the other existed. Once, early on, I stopped at her stall and bought a ward against nightmares, and she wrapped it without meeting my eyes, and said, "Two coppers," in a voice I didn't recognize, flat as canal ice, and I paid four and left the change and she let it sit there until I was gone.

I wore that nightmare-ward for a year. It worked. Of course it worked. It was never just knots and prayers, and we both knew it, and that small shared criminal knowledge was the closest we came to speaking for six years.

* * *

Which brings us to last night. To the narrow house on Tallow Street with its shuttered windows, to the report from the night patrol that lights were moving in a registered-closed property, to the warrant I signed with my own hand while my stomach did something complicated and traitorous.

I went myself. I always go myself when it's Pemberton. Partly because I don't trust a younger Warden not to escalate, and partly — I'll be honest, since honesty is the one luxury this story affords me — partly because some part of me has been waiting six years for a lawful reason to knock on her door.

What I found instead was August.

Tall, dark-haired, wrong in ways I couldn't catalogue — a man standing in a witch's cellar next to a door that shouldn't exist, a door humming like a struck bell, and when I leveled my blade at him he stepped *between* me and it, this stranger, this unknown quantity, this man with no registry entry and no history, putting his body between a Warden's steel and a sealed gate like he had every right.

And Lena came down the stairs after him and stood beside him and looked at me across six years and a drawn blade, and said, "Sera. You owe me. You know you do."

And the terrible thing, the thing I've been chewing on all morning in this garrison office while the reports pile up unfiled, is that she's right. I owe her. In the winter after the branding, when my mother was dying and I was seventeen and drowning, someone left a basket on the garrison steps every week for three months. Bread, fever-tincture, a jar of honey. No note. I knew the handwriting on the tincture labels. I've always known it. I drank those medicines and never said thank you and never returned the basket and told myself the debt was paid by silence.

It wasn't. Debts like that don't clear. They accrue.

So I stayed my hand. I logged the property check as unfounded, lights from a gas leak, and I walked away from a sealed gate and a wanted stranger and the only friend I ever had, and now I'm sitting here knowing three things with absolute certainty:

One: whatever is under that shop, the Order must never learn of it, because the Order's answer to anomalies is fire, and I have seen what fire leaves.

Two: August is not in any registry, was not born in any parish I can find, and moved like a man stepping into a story he'd already read. Which means either he's nothing, or he's the beginning of everything.

Three: my mother's wall is cracking, and I am the crack.

There's a knock at my office door. Vess, night-shift, holding a dispatch flimsy, face pale as tallow.

"Warden Voss. Priority seal from the High Warden's office." He swallows. "They're asking about Tallow Street. Specifically, they're asking about a century-old prophecy reference flagged in the archive — 'the ember returns.' And they're asking why your report on the Pemberton property came back unfounded."

The paper feels cold in my hand. Somewhere across the city, under a charm stall, a door is humming.

I owe Lena Pemberton a debt. The question the dispatch is asking — politely, with the full weight of the Order behind it — is whether I intend to pay it, or collect it.

By dawn, I'll have to know.
