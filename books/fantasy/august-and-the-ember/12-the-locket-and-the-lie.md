## 12. The Locket and the Lie

There are three universal truths in the canal district of Ashfall City, and I am the living authority on all of them.

One: never trust a smuggler who doesn't lie to you at least twice before breakfast. Two: the water remembers everything you drop into it, which is why I've never once told it my real surname. Three: no matter how carefully you plan a heist, something is going to go sideways, and your only real choice is whether you're holding the knife or the map when it does.

Tonight, for once, I was holding neither. I was holding a locket.

It sat in my palm like a small cold heart, silver gone grey with vault-dust, the clasp still bent from where Lena had jimmied it with a hairpin and what I can only describe as an alarming amount of casual competence for a charm-seller who claimed she'd "never done anything illegal before last week." Liars, all of them. I'd decided I liked them anyway.

"That's it?" August asked. He was hovering the way big warm men hover when they think hovering counts as help. "That's the whole reason we broke into a Warden vault?"

"You want to know if it was worth it," I said, turning the locket over. My mother's initials were scratched on the back—*M.Q.* for Maerwen Quill, though everyone who loved her called her Mae, and everyone who feared her called her worse things that aren't fit for a lady's narration. "Ask me again after I open it."

"Is there a trick to it?"

"There's a trick to everything, love. That's truth number four, free of charge."

The safehouse was a barge called the *Drowned Lark*, moored under Belltower Row where the canal smell did most of our security work for us. Lena had wards up that tasted like copper and rain when you licked the air—I didn't lick the air, but I'm half-fae, licking things is how I check them—and Odile was asleep in the back cabin with her rosary wound around her fist even in her dreams, like she expected God Himself to try to pickpocket her.

So it was just the three of us in the lamplight, and the locket, and me.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about grief: it doesn't get smaller. It just gets better organized. For eleven years I'd kept my mother's death folded up small and filed away behind the day-to-day business of not drowning—not literally; I swim beautifully, it's the metaphorical kind that's killed better people than me. And now here was her locket, the one thing the Wardens took off her body when they fished her out of the canal with a severance-brand still smoking on her throat, and my hands were shaking so badly the chain rattled like teeth.

"Bramble." Lena's voice went quiet the way she does it, like she's laying the sound down gently instead of dropping it. "You don't have to do this tonight."

"If I don't do it tonight I'll find a reason not to do it tomorrow, and the day after, and by winter I'll have talked myself into believing the vault was full of turnips." I laughed, and it came out wrong, too sharp. "Open it while my cowardice is still catching its breath."

August put his hand over mine. Warm. Stupidly warm, like a stove with opinions. And underneath the warmth there was that hum I'd been feeling off him since the canal—the same note the old songs hum, the ones my mother sang when she thought I was asleep, the ones about *the ember returning*. I'd assumed it was nerves. I was starting to suspect it was nothing so convenient.

I pressed the clasp.

The hinge gave with a click that sounded, in the silence, exactly like a lock turning somewhere very far away.

Inside the locket there was no portrait. No curl of hair, no pressed flower, none of the sentimental junk people carry against their hearts. There was a sigil.

It was small—a circle the size of a thumbnail, worked in enamel the color of banked coals, and inside the circle four lines crossed at the center like a door seen from above. Or a wheel. Or a cage, depending on your mood, and my mood has been described as "cage-adjacent" by better judges than me.

Lena made a sound like someone had pulled a thread out of her spine.

"What," I said. My voice came out flat. Flat is what I do instead of screaming; ask anyone. "What is that. Tell me what that is, charm-girl, and tell me slowly, because I'm currently deciding whether to throw this thing in the canal."

"It's a covenant mark." Lena had gone pale in the particular way of someone watching a wall develop cracks. She reached out, stopped herself, tucked her hand back against her ribs like she'd remembered touching things without asking gets people branded in this city. "It's—one of four. They're in the ledger. The heirs of the old covenant each carried one. It's how the gate knows them."

The barge creaked beneath us. Somewhere down the canal a drunk was singing a song about a fisherman's wife, badly, with feeling.

"No," I said.

"Bramble—"

"No. No, no. That's—" I held the locket up between two fingers like it was a dead rat, which, emotionally speaking, it was. "This was my *mother's*. She wore this every day of her life. She wore it into the water. Are you telling me she was—" I stopped, because the word that wanted to come out was *marked*, and marked is what cattle are, and I would rather eat glass than say it in front of these two soft-hearted idiots who kept looking at me like I was something worth saving.

"Heir," Lena said softly. Damn her. Damn her gentle voice. "It means you're an heir, Bramble. Third of four."

*Third of four.* The ledger had said three names besides August's. Two we could read. The third had been blacked out with blood-magic, inked over so thoroughly it looked like a wound in the page. We'd spent two days speculating. Sera Voss, maybe. Some dead stranger. Anyone but—

Well. Anyone but the half-fae smuggler they'd broken into a vault for, whose tragic backstory had apparently been pre-written a century before she was born.

I stood up. I don't remember deciding to stand; my body just opted out of the conversation. The cabin felt small. The lamp felt loud. The locket sat in my hand burning cold, and I walked to the porthole and looked out at the black water of the canal, because the water and I have an arrangement: I don't tell it my secrets, and it doesn't drown me.

"Say the rest of it," I said. "Whatever the rest of it is. You've got a face on. Both of you have faces on. Out with it."

Lena and August exchanged a look—that look, the one they do, the one that already has furniture and curtains and a mortgage on it—and then Lena took a breath and said, "The gate can't be reforged without all four heirs. Willing ones. That's why the ledger names you. That's why the third name matters so much."

"And if the fourth heir says no? If she takes her shiny new sigil and rows upriver and opens a stall selling counterfeit luck-charms to tourists?"

"The gate stays broken. The Sundering keeps... leaking. Whatever comes through next time won't be a whisper in a cellar, it'll be—" August started.

"I know what it'll be. I've seen the leaks." I had. Small things, mostly. A bargeman's shadow walking on the wrong side of his body. Fish in the canal with too many eyes, all of them apologetic. Once, a door in Greyhollow that opened onto a room where it was always almost raining. The realm on the other side wasn't evil. It was *hungry*, the way anything is hungry after a hundred years of being locked out of its own house.

"So that's it," I said. "That's the shape of it. I get to choose. Except the choice was written down before my grandmother's grandmother learned to walk, and the man who wrote it knew my mother's face, and my mother died wearing his sigil around her neck like it was a gift." I turned around. The lamp threw my shadow huge against the cabin wall, wings and all, and for once I didn't mind looking monstrous. "Tell me honestly, either of you. Is there a version of tonight where I had a choice? Because from where I'm standing, I've been *cast*. I'm not a person, I'm a part. And parts don't get to quit the machine."

Silence. Good. Let them sit in it. Silence is the only honest currency left in Ashfall.

It was August who broke it, because of course it was. He has this terrible habit of saying true things in a voice like Sunday morning.

"My grandmother sent me here," he said. "Dying words. Find the ember. I didn't know what it meant. I picked Lena's stall out of a market crowd because her charms hummed at me, and I told myself it was coincidence right up until a book showed me my own face in ink older than my family line." He shrugged, big and slow, like the truth weighed as much as he did. "So I know a little bit about being cast. And here's what I've got, Bramble, and it isn't much: whoever wrote that ledger wrote the *role*. Not the performance. The page says an heir of the Quill line stands at the gate. It doesn't say what she says when she gets there. It doesn't say whether she comes for love or spite or curiosity or because somebody owes her money. That part's blank. That part's yours."

"The blood-magic name wasn't blank," I said. "Somebody redacted it. Somebody decided the fourth heir didn't get to read their own part."

"Then we un-redact it," Lena said, and there it was—that spine under all her softness, the thing I'd clocked the first night she haggled me down on a warding charm while lying through her teeth about her supplier. "We go to the scar-field. Where the Sundering happened. The ghosts of the old covenant are supposed to still be there. If anyone knows why the fourth name was hidden, they do."

"The scar-field." I laughed, genuinely this time, which was somehow worse. "The battlefield. The place where the world got cut open. Sure. Lovely. Do they do tours? Is there a gift shop?"

"There's Wardens," August offered helpfully. "And whatever's been riding the rail shadows since we left the city."

"Wonderful. I'll pack my good knife."

But I said it lightly, and lightness is a load-bearing wall in my architecture, and none of them called me on it, which is how I knew they were learning me. Found family, the poets call it. Nobody warns you found family means people noticing when your jokes are scaffolding.

Odile appeared in the doorway sometime during all this—I hadn't heard her wake, which frightened me more than I let show; nuns shouldn't move like that—and she looked at the open locket in my hand with an expression I couldn't read. Then she crossed herself, quick and automatic, and said, "My order taught that the covenant marks were holy. Before the Accords. Before they taught us to be afraid instead." Her knuckles were white around the rosary. "I renounced magic because I believed it took my parents. If it turns out I was born into a prophecy instead..." She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Used destiny wears the same face in every faith.

"I need air," I announced, which was a lie, because what I needed was to be alone with the locket, and liars deserve privacy too.

* * *

I sat on the deck of the *Drowned Lark* with my boots dangling over the black water and my mother's sigil cupped in both hands, and I talked to the canal, because the canal listens and doesn't answer, which is my favorite kind of conversation.

"She knew," I told the water. "She must have known. Half-fae live long enough to read the fine print, and Mae Quill read *everything*. She wore this every day. She let the Wardens take it off her corpse rather than destroy it, or maybe she couldn't destroy it, maybe it's warded, maybe—" I turned it over. The enamel caught the lamplight from below, ember-colored, patient. "Maybe she was protecting me. Maybe she was feeding me to it. Maybe those were the same thing, in her arithmetic. Mothers do that. Make sacrifices that look like gifts from the outside."

The water said nothing, eloquently.

Here's what I actually couldn't decide, down in the hold of myself where I keep the cargo I don't declare at any border: which was worse—to be chosen, or to be used? Everyone wants to be chosen. Chosen means somebody looked at the whole crowded world and pointed at *you*. But used means somebody moved you across a board before you knew there was a game, and every kindness you'd ever been shown becomes suddenly suspect. Had my mother sung me those ember-songs as lullabies, or as *training*? Had she taught me to pick locks because locks are useful, or because the ledger's heir would need to get into places?

And the truly rotten part, the part I'll deny under torture: some small mean spark in my chest was *glad*. Glad to matter. Eleven years of being nobody, a face in a crowd of smugglers and cutthroats, and now a century-old book had said my name—or my line's name—and some starving little corner of me sat down at that table like a dog that's finally been called in from the rain.

Chosen destiny. Used destiny. Same coin, different sides, and I'd spent my whole life flipping it.

Below me, through the hull, I could feel it: a faint pulse, rhythmic, patient. The gate. Lena's cellar gate, miles away, and still I could feel it the way you feel a train through the soles of your feet before you hear it. It had noticed me. Now that the sigil was open, now that the seal on the locket was broken, it had *noticed*.

"Keep your distance," I muttered at it. "I haven't agreed to anything."

The pulse didn't change. Gates are bad listeners. So are prophecies. So, if I'm honest, am I.

The hatch opened behind me and Lena climbed out, wrapped in a coat she'd stolen from my stores without asking, which I respected enormously. She sat down beside me without a word and dangled her own boots over the water, mirroring me, and we watched the canal do its ancient boring beautiful work of going nowhere.

"My mother hid me for five years," she said eventually. "Behind a charm stall. Behind smallness. She thought invisible was safe." A pause. "It wasn't. It was just slow."

"Yeah," I said. "Mine hid me in plain sight. Taught me to be quick and charming and forgettable." I turned the locket over one more time, then—before my cowardice caught its breath, like I'd promised—I slipped the chain over my head and let it settle against my sternum, where it lay cold as a coin from a dead country. "If I find out she used me, charm-girl, I'm going to be insufferable about it for a decade. Fair warning."

"And if she chose you?"

"Then I'll be insufferable about *that*." I stood, and the locket swung, and far away beneath the city something pulsed once, like a heart remembering its job. "Either way, somebody wrote me into a story a hundred years ago, and I intend to find the fourth name and give whoever's hiding it a piece of my mind. Preferably the sharpest piece."

Lena smiled up at me, tired and fierce. "The scar-field, then."

"The scar-field." I looked east, past the rooftops, toward the place where the world had been cut and never quite healed, where ghosts waited with answers and Wardens waited with irons and something older waited with neither. "Pack light, pray hard, and nobody touches my good knife."

Down in the dark of the canal, the water finally spoke—just a ripple, just my own reflection breaking apart and reforming—but for one strange moment the face looking back at me wore the sigil glowing at its throat like a second heartbeat.

I chose to believe that was a promise.

I've been wrong before.
