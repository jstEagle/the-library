## 13. Night Train to Greyhollow

There are three things I learned in my first hour as a fugitive.

One: Bramble Quill swears in at least four languages, and at least two of them are not technically languages but the sounds a person makes when a Warden's net-spell grazes her tail-feathers.

Two: Lena Pemberton can pick a lock with a hairpin and a charm that technically doesn't exist anymore, and she does it while looking mildly annoyed, like the lock has personally wasted her evening.

Three: running for your life does strange things to your heart. It makes it beat faster, yes, but it also makes it beat *toward* things. Toward a person, specifically. Which is a problem, because the person in question is currently pressing her back to a canal-side wall with me, her breath quick and warm against my collarbone, and the last thing either of us needs is a distraction.

"You're staring," she whispered.

"I'm assessing the tactical situation."

"The tactical situation is a wall."

"The wall is very well built," I said. "I admire good masonry."

She made a sound that was half laugh, half groan, and I decided right then and there that I would commit crimes—well, more crimes—for that sound. Somewhere above us, a Warden's whistle cut the fog, long and thin as a blade. Bramble, crouched in the shadow of a mooring post, gestured with the eloquence of someone who had been smuggled, smuggling, and arrested in equal measure: *move, now, idiots.*

We moved.

* * *

The night train to Greyhollow left from a station that wasn't on any map, because the railway company had learned a century ago that if you put something on a map, the Wardens would eventually come to inspect it. It was a squat brick mouth in the side of an embankment, lit by lamps that burned a color I can only describe as *apologetic*, and the ticket seller was a woman whose eyes were different colors and who took our coins without once looking up from her crossword.

"Three to Greyhollow," Bramble said, hunching to hide the shimmer of her fae blood under a coat two sizes too big.

"Compartment or open carriage?" the woman asked.

"What's the difference?"

"Open carriage, you can see who's watching you. Compartment, you can't."

Bramble looked at Lena. Lena looked at me. I looked at the crossword, which had *seven letters, what the brave do anyway*.

"Compartment," we all said at once, and the woman underlined an answer and slid three tickets through the slot like she was dealing cards to a hand she already knew would lose.

The train was old in the way that things get old when they've been *allowed* to—no one had sanded its edges or painted over its history. Our compartment had green velvet seats worn to the weave, a window fogged with the breath of a hundred years of travelers, and a small brass plaque that read *MIND THE THRESHOLD* in letters that had been scratched out and re-engraved so many times they'd gone soft.

Lena sat across from me. Bramble took the corner seat by the door, which I was learning was a Bramble thing—she always sat where she could see exits, and she always counted them twice.

"Rules," Bramble said, as the train shuddered and began to pull out of the brick mouth and into the dark. "One: nobody uses magic on the train. Two: nobody *looks* like they're about to use magic on the train. Three: if I say duck, you duck first and ask questions in Greyhollow."

"Why no magic?" I asked.

Bramble's eyes flicked to the window, to the darkness rushing past, and for a moment her usual sardonic gloss slipped and something older looked out. "Because trains remember," she said. "And because we're not the only things riding tonight."

I wanted to ask what that meant. I have a bad habit of wanting to ask things. But Lena reached across the compartment—not far, just far enough that her fingers brushed the back of my hand where it rested on the seat—and said, "Sleep if you can. Greyhollow's a long way."

I did not sleep. But I did not move my hand, either. And after a while, neither did she.

* * *

Here's what I hadn't told them yet, and what the dark of the train window finally made me confess, somewhere past the city's last gaslight, when the countryside became a rumor of hills and fog:

I'd been on a train like this before. The night my grandmother died, I'd taken the last train out of my town with her final words folded into my pocket like a ticket: *Find the charm seller in Ashfall. Tell her the ember remembers.* I hadn't understood it then. I'm not sure I understand it now. But sitting in that compartment, with Lena's hand a warm weight against mine and the world I'd known receding behind me at fifty miles an hour, I understood something adjacent to it: that I had never been going anywhere. I had only ever been *arriving*.

"You're doing the thing again," Lena said quietly.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stare out the window like it owes you an explanation."

"It might," I said. "It's been giving me a lot of attitude tonight."

She smiled, but it was the careful smile, the one she wore like armor, and I found I was tired of it—not of her, never of her, but of the armor. So I did something reckless, which, if you've been paying attention, is something of a theme with me.

"My grandmother raised me," I said. "My parents were—gone. That's all I ever got. *Gone*, with a capital G and a locked door behind it. She never talked about them. She never talked about much, except at the end, when she grabbed my wrist with hands like paper and told me to find a charm seller in Ashfall." I turned my hand over, so my palm rested against hers. "I spent three months finding you. I told you our first date was a coincidence. It wasn't. I'd watched your stall for two weeks before I worked up the nerve."

Lena went very still. On the train, going very still is a skill; the carriage sways and rattles and everyone rocks with it, and she went still the way a heron goes still, the way a drawn bow goes still.

"You sought me out," she said.

"I sought you out."

"And the charm you bought. The one that lit up like a—"

"That was an accident. That part, I swear on my grandmother's grave, was an accident." I hesitated. "The date wasn't. The wanting to know you—that was real. That was the only real thing I had. I just... built a lie around it to keep it safe."

The train clattered through the dark. Bramble, by the door, had the magnificent stillness of someone pretending very hard to be asleep and mostly succeeding.

"You could have told me," Lena said at last.

"Would you have come?"

"No," she said, and then, softer, "but I would have been angry at you for honest reasons instead of complicated ones, and that's a better foundation for a fugitive partnership."

"Is that what we are? A fugitive partnership?"

"I don't know what we are, August." Her thumb moved, just slightly, across my palm—a motion so small it might have been involuntary, might have been the train's sway. "Five years I built a life out of hiding, and in one week you've burned most of it down and I keep *helping you*. I should be furious. I keep waiting to be furious. But every time I get close, you do something—" she stopped. Started again. "You looked at that child's drawing in my stall for ten minutes. You asked the price of every charm like the prices were *stories*. Who does that?"

"Someone who grew up with a grandmother who told fortunes with tea leaves and charged by the story," I said. "Also, your prices *were* stories. You charged a widow half a copper for a grief-charm and told her it was because it was Tuesday."

"It was a slow Tuesday."

"It was charity with a joke on top."

"Everything good is," Lena said, and then she seemed to hear what she'd said, and the compartment got very warm, or I did, and we both looked at our hands, which had somehow, without either of us announcing it, become laced together.

From the corner, without opening her eyes, Bramble said: "If you two are going to do *that*, do it quieter. Some of us are trying to listen for death."

* * *

Death, as it turned out, was considerate enough to announce itself.

It came at the border station of Marrowfen, where the train stopped for no reason any of the conductors would explain, and the lamps along the platform burned that apologetic color, and the fog came up off the marsh like something being poured. We should have stayed in the compartment. Bramble said so. Lena said so. But the whistle blew—a Warden's whistle, long and thin, answered by another, and then another, from the far end of the platform—and Bramble was up with her hand on the door before I'd finished standing.

"Window," she hissed. "Out the window, into the trees, now, *now*—"

We went out the window. I would like the record to show that I went out the window with something close to grace, which is a lie I intend to keep telling myself, because the alternative is acknowledging that I fell face-first into a marsh fern while Lena landed like a cat and Bramble like a rumor. We crouched in the treeline while Wardens moved down the platform in their grey coats, their lanterns burning white and hungry, and their voices carried across the fog in fragments:

"—the Pemberton woman—"

"—the ember-sign, the High Warden wants it *alive*—"

"—check the train, check every carriage—"

Lena's hand found my sleeve. Her face in the dark was pale and set. "They know," she breathed. "They know it's me."

"They knew before we did," Bramble muttered. "Somebody's been feeding them. Somebody close." She didn't look at either of us when she said it, which was somehow worse than if she had.

And then the third whistle blew, and it was not a Warden's whistle.

I don't know how else to describe it except that it came from *underneath*—from below the rails, below the platform, below, I'd swear, the ground itself—and it was long and low and glad. Every Warden on that platform went silent at once. The lanterns guttered. And in the fog beyond the last lamp, at the far end of the train, something moved. Not walked. Not ran. *Moved*, the way shadow moves when the candle gutters—a long, jointed wrongness, keeping pace with the carriages, and where it passed, the fog went dark and the rails sang a note I felt in my back teeth.

"What," I whispered, "is that."

Bramble's tail-feathers had gone flat against her back, which I would learn was the fae equivalent of every hair on your body standing up. "That," she said, "is why you don't use magic on trains. That's the *Old Fare*. It rides the rails that were laid over the old roads, the roads that were cut when the Sundering—" She stopped. Swallowed. "It collects debts. Passage debts. Things that crossed where they shouldn't have crossed."

"We crossed where we shouldn't have crossed," Lena said. It wasn't a question.

"We're *fugitives*," Bramble said. "Everything about tonight is a debt."

The thing reached the last carriage. I heard the door open—heard it the way you hear a held breath—and then the screaming started, one thin voice, one Warden who had been checking carriages at the wrong end of the train, and it did not last long, and I will not repeat what it said, because I have decided some things don't get to live in my mouth.

"Move," Lena said. "Away from the rails. Now. Quietly."

We moved. Through fern and fog, uphill, away from the singing rails, while behind us the Old Fare went carriage to carriage like a collector with a ledger of its own, and the Wardens—Wardens who had been hunting us ten minutes before—fled past us in the dark without seeing, without *wanting* to see, running from something older than their order and their law and their grey coats.

We ran until the rails' song faded. We ran until the fog gave way to pine. We ran until Bramble, who could apparently run forever, finally called a halt in a hollow of stones where a spring came up cold out of the dark, and we stood gasping in the black, three fugitives and a debt unpaid.

"It let us go," I said. "It went for the Wardens and it let *us* go. Why?"

Lena was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, she was looking at her own hands, at the faint ember-glow that had begun, since the cellar, since the gate, to live under her skin like a lamp behind paper.

"Maybe," she said, "because it knows who we are. Maybe because the old roads remember what the gate was, and what it's going to be again." She looked up at me, and the glow lit her face from below, and she was terrified and certain in the same breath. "Or maybe because it's not collecting tonight. Maybe it's *counting*."

"Counting what?"

"The heirs," she said. "Four names, August. It's counting down."

* * *

We walked through the night and most of the next day, keeping off the roads, following Bramble's unerring sense for the gaps in the world—the smuggler's paths, the ways between. Somewhere in the grey afternoon we caught a mail coach's wake and rode its slipstream of ordinary life into Greyhollow's outskirts, and by the time the town's single spire rose out of the mist, I had learned two more things.

I learned that Lena Pemberton, when she sleeps, curls toward warmth, and that on the second night of walking, in a haybarn, she curled toward me, and that I did not sleep at all that night, and that I regret nothing.

And I learned that when she laughed—really laughed, not the careful stall-keeper's laugh, but the one that escaped her when Bramble did an impression of a Warden trying to look dignified while fleeing an ancient debt—it sounded like a charm lighting up. It sounded like the thing my grandmother's prophecy had been *for*. Not the gate. Not the war. Just that. Just a woman laughing in a haybarn in the middle of being hunted, because the world had not yet managed to make her small.

"Greyhollow," Bramble announced, as the mist thinned. She pointed one clawed finger at the spire, and at the grey stone convent huddled beneath it like a bird under a wing. "Second heir. Odile Voss. Sister Odile, they call her now. Renounced her magic, took the veil, hasn't spoken to her sister the Warden in eleven years." She looked back at us with an expression I was beginning to dread, the expression of someone about to hand you a live coal. "And here's the part you'll love. The convent's gates open once a week. Tomorrow. To *pilgrims*."

"So we go in as pilgrims," I said.

"So we go in," Bramble said, "as pilgrims, into a house of women who have sworn their whole lives against everything we're carrying, to ask one of them to un-renounce the thing that got her parents killed." She smiled, sharp and bright and not at all comforting. "I've smuggled a lot of things through a lot of doors. This'll be the first time I've smuggled a *request*."

Lena stood beside me, looking at the spire, and her hand found mine again—easily now, without announcement, as if it had always known the way. "Whatever happens in there," she said quietly, "we don't split up."

"Agreed," I said.

"Whatever it asks of us. Whatever it *costs*."

"Lena—"

"August." She turned, and the mist moved behind her, and for a second I saw her as the gate must see her: not a charm seller, not a fugitive, but a keeper, a door, an ember. "I've spent five years alone behind a stall. I'm not doing the rest of this alone. Whatever it costs."

The convent bell began to ring, far off, one slow bronze note at a time, and under its sound—so faint I might have imagined it, so faint I *needed* to have imagined it—something beneath the earth of Greyhollow answered. A hum. A warmth. A second ember, sleeping, waiting for its name.

Bramble pulled her coat tight and started down the hill. "Pilgrims," she said over her shoulder. "Try to look penitent."

I looked at Lena. Lena looked at me. And hand in hand, we went down into Greyhollow, where a woman who had buried her own name was about to be asked to dig it up—and where, though none of us knew it yet, the ledger's third name was waiting, red in redaction, at the bottom of a battlefield we would have to cross to reach it.
