## 14. Greyhollow's Sleeping Saint

The train to Greyhollow smelled of wet wool and old incense, which I decided was either a very good omen or a very bad one, depending on how you felt about churches.

I felt ambivalent about churches. I'd spent five years hiding in a shop that sold luck the way a fishmonger sells fish—by the pound, wrapped in paper, no questions asked—and I had learned that most institutions built on faith were just charm stalls with better architecture and worse prices.

"Stop making that face," August said across from me. He had his boots up on the seat opposite, which was rude, and his coat pooled around him like he'd been poured into it, which was unfair. "The nun is going to say yes."

"The nun," I said, "is going to throw us out on our ears."

"You don't know that."

"Bramble, tell him."

Bramble Quill was folded into the corner seat like a cat who resented being transported in a box. She had her hood up despite the empty carriage, her wings—small, moth-grey, expressive as a gossip's eyebrows—twitching beneath the wool. "She's right," Bramble said. "Nuns are worse than wardens. Wardens chase you because they're paid to. Nuns chase you because they think they're saving your soul, and there's no lock in the world that stops a person with a mission." She paused. "Also I stole a candlestick from the last convent I was in, so my opinion may be compromised."

"Why did you steal a candlestick?"

"It was silver, love. Why does anyone do anything?"

August laughed—that easy, unguarded laugh of his that made strangers on platforms smile without knowing why—and I felt the familiar twist under my ribs. The ledger had shown me his face a century before he was born. *The ember returns.* I had loved him since before I met him, which was either romantic or pathological, and I hadn't yet decided which.

The thing was: Odile Voss had renounced magic. Formally, ritually, with witnesses. In the old days—the days the ledger remembered—you couldn't un-renounce any more than you could un-burn a page. You could only write a new one over the ash.

And we were about to walk up to a woman who had spent her whole life building herself into something fireproof, and ask her to burn.

* * *

Greyhollow announced itself the way penitents do: quietly, and with a great deal of stone. The town sat in a fold of hills like an apology, all grey slate roofs and greyer walls, chimneys breathing thin smoke into a sky the color of dishwater. At its center rose the convent of Saint Verity-of-the-Cut-Rope, whose patron saint, according to Bramble—who knew an alarming amount about saints, mostly for burglary purposes—had once held a bridge against an army by refusing, for forty years, to die.

"Cheerful place," August said.

"They're not all like this," I said. "Some of them have a tavern."

We found the convent at the top of a hill reached by three hundred steps, because of course it was. Bramble refused to fly—"wings are for emergencies and showing off, and this is neither"—so we climbed, and climbed, and I used the time to rehearse.

*Hello, Sister Odile. You don't know me, but your sister Sera—the one you haven't spoken to in fifteen years—is hunting us across two provinces. Also, magic is real, the Sundering was engineered, and you are one of four people alive who can reforge the covenant that keeps the world from tearing open again. Also also, it might cost you everything. Tea?*

Yes. That would go well.

"She'll have guards?" August asked quietly as we neared the top.

"Nuns aren't guards."

"I meant the other kind. Voss kind."

I looked at him. He'd grown quieter since the canal market, since the moment his magic had flared out of him in public and painted a target on all our backs. He carried it differently now—not like a secret but like a bruise he kept checking with his thumb.

"If Sera found us already," I said, "we'd know. She's not subtle when she's close. The air gets—" I stopped myself. I'd been about to say *the air gets like her*, which was true and also insane, and I wasn't ready to explain to August that I could feel Warden Voss coming the way sailors feel weather.

"Like what?"

"Like paperwork," said Bramble.

The convent doors were oak, older than the Accords, iron-banded, and carved with Saint Verity holding her famous rope. A small brass bell hung beside them with a sign beneath: *RING ONCE. WE ARE NOT DEAF. RING TWICE. WE ARE NOT AMUSED.*

Bramble rang once.

The door opened on a novice who couldn't have been more than seventeen, round-faced and suspicious in equal measure. Her eyes went over the three of us—a tall man with a stranger's face, a woman in a traveling coat doing a poor job of looking harmless, and a half-fae smuggler radiating guilt like a stove.

"Pilgrims?" she asked, in the tone of someone who hoped very much that we weren't.

"Pilgrims," I said, because it was technically true. Everyone who climbs three hundred steps is pilgrim to something.

"We've come to speak with Sister Odile."

The novice's suspicion deepened into something closer to alarm. "Sister Odile doesn't receive visitors."

"We know," August said gently. "That's why we came."

* * *

They put us in a parlor with hard chairs and a view of the graveyard, which struck me as either hospitality or a threat. Someone brought tea—weak, oversteeped, served in cups that had survived several centuries through sheer stubbornness—and then we waited while somewhere deeper in the convent, a decision was being made about us.

August paced. Bramble examined the candlesticks with naked professional interest until I glared at her. I sat with my hands around the teacup and tried to feel the gate.

It was strange—I'd lived above the thing for five years, and I'd never once thought of it as far away. Even now, a province distant, I could feel it faintly, like pressure behind the eyes, like a word on the tip of the tongue. And threaded through that feeling, since the night August had touched it and it had whispered, was something new. Attention. The gate knew where its heirs were. I suspected it was counting.

The door opened.

I knew her immediately. Not because she resembled Sera—though she did, in the set of the jaw, the dark hair gone iron at the temples—but because she walked into the room the way Sera did, like the floor should be grateful. Except where Sera wore duty like armor, Odile Voss wore absence. Her habit was plain grey. Her hands were bare. No rings, no sigils, nothing. She had stripped herself down to the bone and called it peace.

"Sister Odile," the novice began, "these pilgrims insisted—"

"That will be all, Sister Anselm." Her voice was quiet and absolutely final. The novice fled. Odile closed the door and stood with her back against it, looking at us the way a lighthouse looks at ships: steadily, and with the assumption that at least one of us intended to wreck.

"You're the ones from Ashfall," she said. "Word travels even here. A man burned bright in the canal market and vanished. The Order sent letters." Her eyes found August. "You'd be him, then. The ember."

"I get that a lot lately," August said.

"No," Odile said. "You really don't. No one has been called that in a hundred years, and the last person called it broke the world." She came further into the room but didn't sit. "Say what you came to say. Then leave. I gave you ten minutes out of curiosity, and curiosity is a sin I confess weekly."

So much for easing in. I set down the cup.

"My name is Lena Pemberton. I keep a gate."

Something moved behind her eyes—fast, quickly buried. "There are no gates. There haven't been gates since the Sundering."

"There's one. Beneath my shop. Sealed, mostly. Whispering, occasionally. And it can't stay sealed unless the covenant is reforged, and the covenant can't be reforged without four heirs, and the ledger—" I pulled the ledger from my satchel and set it on the table between us. It sat there looking ancient and smug, which it managed despite being a book. "—names you."

Odile didn't move toward it. I respected that. Only a fool touches a book like that, and this woman was no fool.

"I renounced," she said simply. "Twenty-two years ago. Before witnesses, before the Mother Superior, before God and whatever's left of the old powers. There is no heir named Odile Voss. There is a nun who sweeps floors and buries the dead and has made her peace." Her chin lifted. "Whatever that book says, it's out of date."

"The ledger isn't out of date," I said. "It's patient. There's a difference."

"And what would you know of patience, charm-seller?" The words came out with an edge I hadn't earned yet, which meant they belonged to someone else. To Sera, probably. Sisters sharpen themselves on the same stone. "You hide your magic in trinkets and call it a life. I burned mine out root and branch. Do you know what that rite costs? Do you know what it's like to reach for something that was yours since birth and find only smooth stone where it used to be?" Her voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "I did it because magic killed my parents. Because the Sundering took them, and the Sundering was magic, and I wanted no part of a power that eats children and calls it necessity."

The room went very quiet. Bramble had gone still in the way fae go still—total, predatory attention. August had stopped pacing.

And I heard myself say, before I could weigh it: "The Sundering wasn't an accident."

Her head turned toward me slowly.

"In the Warden vaults under Belltower Row, there are records. Ledgers, correspondence, engineering diagrams for a severance-engine. The Sundering was done deliberately, by the original covenantor himself, because he feared what the realms would become if they stayed joined." I kept my voice steady, though my hands wanted to shake. Righteous fury is a fuel; it burns clean if you feed it carefully. "Magic didn't kill your parents, Sister. Fear did. Fear wearing magic's clothes. And the same fear is wearing the Order's robes now, and if we don't reforge the covenant, it will wear yours too."

For a long moment she said nothing. Outside, wind moved through the graveyard, and I thought about how many Vosses were buried in churchyards across this country, and how many of them died of the same lie.

"You expect me to believe the Order lied," she said at last. "My sister serves that Order. My sister watched our parents' funeral pyre and swore her life to the people who lit it, and you want me to believe she's been kneeling before murderers for twenty years?"

"I think," August said quietly, "that Sera believes it too. Or wants to. We've seen her hesitate."

"Hesitation isn't truth."

"No," he agreed. "But it's the door truth comes through."

He said things like that sometimes—simple, disarming, like he'd read every book ever written and retained only the sentences that mattered. Odile stared at him, and I watched something happen in her face: the recognition of a kindred discipline. Here was a man carrying something enormous and trying to hold it lightly. She knew that posture. She'd invented it.

"Why me?" she asked, and her voice cracked on the second word, just slightly, just enough. "If the ledger names four heirs, why a woman who cut it all away? Why not someone who *wanted* it?"

I opened the ledger to her page. I'd looked at it a dozen times on the train. Ink a century old, in a hand that slanted like it was running out of time:

*Odile Voss, who will refuse, and refuse, and refuse — and whose refusal will be the hinge.*

Below it, smaller, almost an afterthought: *The gate does not need her yes. It needs her no to mean something.*

I turned the book around.

Odile read it twice. When she looked up, her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "That's obscene," she whispered. "That's obscene. My whole life—my vows, my grief, my *peace*—and some dead hand wrote it into a script a century ago and called it prophecy?"

"Yes," I said. "Welcome to my last month."

A sound escaped her that might have been a laugh in a former life. She pressed her fingers to her mouth. For a moment she was not a nun or an heir or a name in a book; she was just a tired woman in a cold room, being asked to un-renounce herself, which is the one prayer no god answers politely.

"I can't," she said finally. "You understand that I can't. Not because I don't believe you—God help me, I believe you, I've believed the wrongness of this place for years and called it doubt—but because if I take it back, if I reach for it again, then everything I told myself about their deaths was a story I told to survive. And I would rather be hollow than wrong."

The honesty of it knocked the wind out of me. I had prepared arguments. I had none that could stand against that.

So I did the only thing left. I told the truth.

"I'm not asking you to come with us today," I said. "I'm asking you to pray on it. Badly. Dishonestly. Pray knowing you might answer yourself instead of God. And when the Order's letter comes—the one asking about the ember, the one that's already been written—we'd like you to remember which side of the rope Saint Verity was standing on."

Odile looked at me for a long time. Then at August, whose face had gone soft and sad in a way that made my chest ache. Then at the ledger, still open, still patient.

"Three hundred steps down," she said, "there's a road east. Take it. Don't stop in the town. And Pemberton—" she picked up the teacup I'd abandoned and drained it in one motion, like a woman taking communion or medicine, "—if my sister catches you before I decide, tell her the rope was always hers to hold too. She'll know what it means. She won't thank you."

At the door she paused, one hand on the iron banding, and didn't turn around.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the gate whispering in a dead language—it spoke to me too. Once. The night before I renounced. It said *wait*. I assumed it was temptation." Her knuckles whitened on the wood. "I'm starting to suspect it was a promise."

Then she was gone, and the door shut, and the three of us sat in the silence of the hard chairs and the graveyard view.

"Well," said Bramble, reaching for a candlestick. "That went terribly."

But outside the window, far down the eastern road, the afternoon light bent strangely—just for a heartbeat, just along one line of the horizon, like a seam straining. And the pressure behind my eyes, the far-off hum of the gate, changed key.

Someone else had found the ledger's third page. And they were burning it.
