## 15. Odile's Refusal

The bell over the chapel door has been broken for eleven years. I know because I broke it—pulled the clapper out with my own hands and buried it in the herb garden behind the infirmary, on the same night I buried my name.

Sister Odile does not have a past. Sister Odile arrived at the Convent of Saint Verity in Greyhollow with a single satchel, chapped hands, and a talent for silence. The Mother Superior asked me once where I came from, and I told her: *from a place that no longer exists.* She took this for piety. It was, in its way. Ashfall City stopped existing for me the day the Wardens carried my parents' names off a list and into a cell, and I have spent every year since making sure it stays deleted.

So when the bell rings—rings, though it has no tongue, though it should be nothing but a hollow bronze throat—I know before I look up from the ledger of candle-tithes exactly who has come to unbury me.

Magic announces itself like an old debt collector. It doesn't knock. It rings bells it shouldn't be able to ring.

I set down my pen. I square the ledger's corners. I give myself three full breaths, which is what the Rule prescribes for anger, and then four more, which is what the Rule would prescribe if the Rule had ever met my sister.

Then I go to answer the door.

* * *

There are three of them standing in the snow of the cloister garth, and they are the most conspicuous trio Greyhollow has seen since the traveling menagerie of '09.

I recognize her immediately, which is a wound all by itself. Sera Voss. My sister. Older, harder, wearing the grey-and-silver of the Warden Order like she was poured into it at birth—and maybe she was; we were both children when the Accords came down, but she grew up inside them and I grew up climbing out. Her jaw is set the way it used to set before she lied to our mother about where she'd been. Some things survive everything.

Beside her: a half-fae woman with canal-water eyes and a smuggler's coat, radiating the specific boredom of someone who would rather be anywhere else and is armed enough to say so. And behind them both—

Him.

He's tall and ordinary-looking, brown-haired, city-pale, holding his hands slightly away from his body the way people do when they've been told their hands are dangerous. There's frost melting on his shoulders. His breath comes out in little ghosts. And he looks at me—not at Sera, not at the convent, not at the famous broken bell—with an expression I can only describe as *apologetic*, as if he's sorry in advance for whatever he's about to do to my life.

I hate him instantly and completely. It's the cleanest feeling I've had in years.

"Sister," Sera says. Her voice does something complicated around the word. "Odile."

"Visitors aren't permitted past the gate," I say. "And sisters don't exist. You're speaking to no one."

"You rang your own bell," the half-fae says, "with your own magic, before we even reached the door. Just so you know. Subtle as a house fire." She examines her nails. "Bramble Quill. Pleasure."

"I didn't ring anything."

"You did," the man says quietly. "It was—" He stops himself, and I watch him decide to tell the truth instead of whatever softer thing he'd prepared. "It was defensive. Your magic felt us coming and rang the bell. Like a dog barking at the gate."

"My *magic*," I repeat, and the word tastes like copper, like the night our parents were taken, like the smell of the severance-wagon's iron wheels. "You've come a long way to insult me."

"We've come a long way to ask for help," Sera says. "Odile. Please. Five minutes. Inside, or out here, I don't care—but five minutes."

I should shut the door. Every rule I have written for myself over eleven years says *shut the door*. The Rule of Saint Verity says hospitality to travelers, but Saint Verity never had a sister in the Warden Order standing in her snow, and rules are beautiful precisely because they don't have to account for Sera.

"Five minutes," I hear myself say, "in the parlor. Touch nothing. Bless nothing."

* * *

The parlor smells of beeswax and dried lavender, and the three of them sit in it like crows on a church pew—wrong shapes for the furniture. I pour tea because my hands need a task that isn't shaking. Sera declines hers. Bramble takes two lumps and drinks it like she suspects it might be confiscated later. The man—August, Sera calls him, August Pemberton, and the surname lands in my stomach like a stone into a well—holds his cup without drinking, warming his palms.

"Say what you came to say," I tell Sera.

She looks at me for a long moment, and I realize she's cataloguing me—the way she used to catalogue our father's workshop before telling the neighbors it was a tannery. Checking what's changed. Checking what survived.

"There's a gate under a charm shop in Ashfall," she says. "Old. Sealed badly since the Sundering. It's failing, and when it fails, everything on the other side comes through, and everything on this side burns. Four heirs of the old covenant can reforge it. The ledger named them." A pause. "One of them is you."

"No," I say.

"You haven't heard the—"

"No." I set my cup down so precisely it doesn't click. "Whatever name your ledger carries, it's wrong. I renounced. Do you understand what that means? Eleven years ago I stood in the Chapter House and spoke the Renunciation in full, before witnesses, and it *took*. Whatever thread of covenant blood runs through the Voss line, I cut it myself. I am the most thoroughly ordinary woman in this province, and I have worked very hard, at considerable cost, to remain so."

"It doesn't work like that," Bramble says, not unkindly, which is worse. "Renunciation is a door closing. Blood is the house."

"Then let the house burn."

"Odile." Sera leans forward. "People will die."

"People die *every day*," I say, and my voice comes out louder than I intended, bouncing off the quiet walls, and somewhere down the corridor a novice pauses mid-step. I lower it. "People died eleven years ago, in a cellar in Ashfall, while the Wardens read the charges off a scroll. Mother didn't even fight them. Father did, and it made it worse. They were taken for a charm that kept our neighbor's lungs clear through winter. A *charm*. That was the crime. And the Order you're wearing, Sera, nailed the notice to our door."

"That was the old High Warden's purge. It wasn't—"

"It was the law," I say. "Your law. The law you chose when you were sixteen and angry, and I chose God instead, and look how well we've both done."

The silence after that has weight. Bramble studies her tea. August watches me with those apologetic eyes, and I turn on him because he is the stranger here and strangers are easier to wound.

"And you. What are you? The fourth heir? The one who found the book?"

"I'm the one the book found," he says. "I'm... honestly, I'm still not entirely sure what I am. But I know what it's like to be handed a role you never auditioned for." He sets the cup down. "I came looking for Lena Pemberton on purpose. I lied to her about it, at first. Or—didn't lie, exactly, just... arranged the truth until it looked like coincidence. She had every right to walk away from me. She didn't. But she should have had the choice, and I took some of it from her, and I've been trying to earn back the difference ever since." He shrugs, helpless. "That's my whole qualification for being here. I'm good at showing up after I've made a mess."

"How inspiring."

"I'm terrified," he says.

The words land strangely, flat and plain, with no performance in them. I was braced for persuasion—for the shining rhetoric that got our parents condemned, that filled the Accords' proclamations, that dressed fear up as order. This is not that. This is a tired man admitting a small true thing in a lavender-smelling room.

"Of the gate?" I ask, despite myself.

"Of all of it. Of the Wardens, of the thing riding the rails behind us, of what the ledger says the price is going to be. Of Lena getting hurt. Mostly of Lena getting hurt." He rubs his thumb along the cup's rim. "But also of you saying yes. Because if you say yes, then this is real, and real means it can cost something. Part of me hoped you'd throw us out. Then it could stay a story we tell ourselves on trains."

"August," Sera says warningly.

"No, she deserves the truth more than the pitch." He looks at me again. "Everyone keeps calling us heirs, chosen, covenant. Nobody's asked whether any of us wanted it. So: did you want any of this? No. Obviously not. You built a whole life specifically to not want it. I'm not going to stand here and tell you your faith is a wall you're hiding behind, because I don't know that, and it's not mine to know. I'm just going to tell you what happened to me, which is that running from a thing you're made of turns out to hurt more than the thing itself. And that I'd rather be afraid next to people than safe alone. That's it. That's the whole speech. I'll see myself out whenever you want."

For a moment nobody speaks. The fire ticks. Somewhere outside, snow keeps falling on Greyhollow with the patience of something that has all the time in the world.

"You think honesty is a key," I say finally, and my voice is not quite steady. "You think if you say the frightened thing plainly, doors open. That's not how wounds work."

"No," he agrees. "But it's how they start to close. I've watched it happen. Slowly. Ugly. But it happens."

"You don't know anything about my wounds."

"No," he says. "Tell me anyway. Or don't. But Sera's been carrying yours and hers both since Ashfall, and I've watched her almost break under the weight, and I keep thinking—if there are two of you, why is only one of you carrying it?"

I look at my sister. Really look, for the first time in eleven years. There's grey in her hair that shouldn't be there yet. There's a scar along her jaw I don't know the history of. And there is something behind her eyes that I recognize with a sick lurch, because I've seen it in the mirror on bad nights: the expression of a person keeping a cellar door shut with their whole body.

"They suffered," I say quietly. "Our parents. At the end. The Warden records said swift, but I wrote to a clerk who owed Father a favor, and he told me otherwise. Eleven years I've known otherwise. And I built this—" I gesture at the parlor, the convent, the whole white quiet of it— "on top of knowing. Prayer over a grave I dug myself, and called it peace."

"Odile," Sera whispers, and it's the voice she had at nine years old, the night we hid under the stairs together while the wagons rolled past, her hand crushing mine, both of us promising each other nothing because there was nothing left to promise.

"I am not an heir," I say. "I am a nun with a broken bell and a dead family and a sister I haven't forgiven. That's what I have. That's what I am."

"Then start there," August says simply. "We're not asking you to be the ledger's version of you. We're asking you to come stand in a cellar with people who are also not what the world decided they were. You can refuse everything else. Just—don't refuse the company."

I should refuse. I want, with a ferocity that frightens me, to refuse—to call the Mother Superior, to have them escorted to the gate, to return to my candles and my ledgers and my beautifully deleted life.

Instead I hear myself ask: "The price. The ledger said reforge the gate. What does it cost?"

Nobody answers fast enough, and their faces answer for them.

"One of us," August says at last. "Memories and love. Given to the gate. Forever."

I laugh. It comes out cracked and awful, the first laugh I've had in months, and it hurts going down. "Of course. Of course it does. Everything worth building costs the builder. That's the oldest lesson there is." I press my palms flat against the table, against the solid honest wood, and feel the faint hum underneath it—the convent's foundations, the town's sleeping wards, the low patient pulse of a power I swore I'd cut out of myself still beating in my blood like a second heart.

"The bell," I say. "When you arrived. It rang."

"It rang," Bramble confirms.

"I pulled its clapper out eleven years ago and buried it in the garden." I stand, and my legs are steadier than they have any right to be. "Come with me. All of you. If I'm going to make the worst decision of my life, I want witnesses."

* * *

The snow has stopped. The garth lies white and untouched, moonlit, the herb beds humped like sleeping animals. I lead them to the far corner, past the rosemary and the feverfew, to a patch of bare earth I have never once planted in eleven years, because some part of me always knew what slept beneath it.

I kneel. I dig with my bare hands, the cold biting in, until my fingers strike bronze.

The clapper comes up crusted with earth—and clean, impossibly, along one edge, gleaming like the day it was cast. And etched into the metal, fine as spider silk, in a script I have never been taught and read without effort:

*a name.*

Not mine. Not anymore. But the letters glow faintly warm against my frozen palm, and the bell above the chapel door swings once in the windless dark, and makes no sound at all, and every hair on my arms stands up.

"Well," says Bramble Quill, into the silence. "That's a yes, then."
