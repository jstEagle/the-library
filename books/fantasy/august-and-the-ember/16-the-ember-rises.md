## 16. The Ember Rises

Here's the thing nobody tells you about carrying a miracle in your chest: it doesn't feel like glory. It feels like heartburn.

I'd been sitting with that particular indigestion for weeks now—since the ledger, since the gate under Lena's shop whispered my name in a language that predates the concept of names, since I'd learned that my grandmother hadn't sent me to Ashfall City on a whim but on a mission older than the Accords themselves. The ember, they called it. The ember returns. Which is a very poetic way of saying: congratulations, August, you're a walking fire hazard with a prophecy attached and no instruction manual.

The instruction manual, as it turned out, was a woman named Odile Voss who had just slammed a convent door in our faces so hard that snow fell off the lintel.

"She'll come around," Bramble said, from her perch on the low wall outside the Greyhollow cloister. She said it the way people say *the weather will improve*—with total conviction and zero evidence. Her wings were folded tight beneath that ridiculous oilskin coat she refused to take off even indoors, and her tail—I still wasn't used to the tail—flicked irritation against the stone.

"Will she?" Lena asked quietly. She stood with her arms wrapped around herself against the cold, watching the shuttered window where Odile's face had been. "Bramble, she looked at us like we were the plague."

"She looked at you like you were the plague," Bramble corrected. "She looked at him—" a claw tipped toward me "—like he was the second coming of everything that killed her parents. Different plagues."

"Thank you," I said. "That's much more comforting."

"You're welcome." She grinned, all teeth and no apology. Half-fae smugglers, I was learning, did not do comfort. They did survival, sarcasm, and the occasional stolen pastry, which she produced from somewhere inside the coat and tossed at my chest. "Eat. You look gray."

"I always look gray. It's my whole aesthetic. Brooding stranger's face."

Lena almost smiled. It was small and tired and it did something unfair to my chest that had nothing to do with embers, or possibly everything. Five days on the night train, two days hiding in a canal-side flophouse while Bramble negotiated passage north, one catastrophic conversation with a nun who hated magic more than sin itself—and somehow the thing I kept coming back to was the way Lena Pemberton looked when she was trying not to hope.

We'd been walking back through Greyhollow's lower town when we heard the screaming.

* * *

Greyhollow in winter is a town built out of exhaustion. Slate roofs, coal smoke, a river that ran black under ice. The kind of place where the Ember Accords didn't need enforcing because everyone had already given up on anything worth burning for. The lower town huddled along the millrace, tenements stacked like bad debts, and it was from the tallest of these that the sound came—a woman's voice cracked down to its boards, screaming a name over and over.

"Tomas! *Tomas!*"

People were gathering in the street below, heads tilted up, doing that thing crowds do—looking without helping, because helping costs something and looking is free. On a third-floor window ledge stood a boy, maybe seven years old, barefoot in the snow, swaying like a candle flame deciding whether to go out.

"He climbed out onto the ledge," someone murmured. "Fever's got him. He's been raving three days."

The mother was leaning out the window beside him, arms outstretched, not daring to grab. One slip. That was all it would take. The drop was maybe forty feet onto packed snow over cobblestones, and packed snow over cobblestones forgives nothing.

"We should keep walking," Bramble said, low. "August. We should keep *walking.*"

She was right. Of course she was right. We were fugitives. There were wanted posters with my face on them in three provinces now—not accurate posters, the artist had given me a jaw like a shovel, but recognizable enough—and every instinct of survival said: be furniture. Be background. Be nobody.

Then the boy slipped.

He didn't fall, exactly. He slid, one bare foot skidding on frost-slick stone, and his small hands scrabbled at the ledge and found nothing, and the mother screamed his name one last time in a voice that I will hear until I die, and time did that thing it does—

—and I was moving.

I don't remember deciding. I want to be honest about that, because later, when everything went wrong, I'd replay this moment a thousand times looking for the decision point, the moment where I could have chosen differently, and there isn't one. There's just the scream, and then my boots hitting the stairs three at a time, and then the ember in my chest going from banked coal to open furnace like it had been waiting its whole long sleep for exactly this.

The stairwell smelled of boiled cabbage and sickness. I took the steps so fast I barely touched them. On the third floor a door hung open and the mother was there, half out the window, sobbing, and beyond her the boy was falling—

No. Not falling. Sliding. Catching. Sliding again. His fingers were too small and the frost was winning.

I reached the window as his grip failed.

Later, Bramble would tell me what it looked like from the street: a man appearing at the window like he'd been poured there, catching a falling child by the wrist one-handed, and then light—light pouring out of him, gold-white, the color of the space between lightning and thunder, streaming down the wall of the tenement like the building had caught the sun and couldn't hold it.

What it felt like from inside was simpler. It felt like *yes.*

The ember came up through me the way a song comes up through a throat that's been silent too long. It went down my arm and into the boy where I held him, and I felt his fever—the fever, the sickness underneath it, the infection chewing at his lungs—all of it laid out before me like a map, like something I could read, and I thought, distantly, *oh, so this is what it's for,* and then I stopped thinking at all.

Heat. Not burning heat. Hearth heat. The heat of bread and bathwater and a mother's hands. It moved through the boy and found every place the sickness had made its home and evicted it, gently, thoroughly, the way you turn out pockets of a coat you love. I felt his lungs clear. I felt his heartbeat steady from a panicked flutter into something slow and strong. I felt him stop being afraid, which was the part that undid me, because fear is heavy and he was seven and he'd been carrying it for three days.

His eyes opened. They were brown and enormous and fixed on my face with the unblinking wonder of the very young.

"You're warm," he said.

"Yeah," I managed. My voice came out wrecked. "Sorry about that."

And then the light went out of control.

* * *

Here's what I understand now, lying awake in safehouses reliving it: the ember doesn't distinguish between healing and burning. It's one fire. It wants to *fix* things—wounds, walls, wards, worlds—and it does not care about scale or subtlety or the fact that a quiet miracle in an Accord town is functionally identical to setting off a flare over a Warden garrison.

The light pouring out of me should have stopped when the fever broke. It didn't. It kept going, wider, brighter, spilling across the snow-packed street below, and everywhere it touched things *answered*. Frost melted off the tenement wall in a spreading bloom. A dead window-box on the second floor erupted into green. Old man Corrigan's rheumatic knee—I never learned his name, but I felt it, I felt *everyone*, the ember reaching through the neighborhood like roots through soil—stopped aching for the first time in twenty years.

It felt wonderful. That's the obscene part. It felt like being loved by something vast, and I laughed, actually laughed, holding a child out a third-floor window while golden light rolled down the street like dawn arriving four hours early.

"August." Lena's voice, from the doorway behind me. Sharp. Afraid. "*August, you have to stop.*"

"I—" I looked down at my hands. The light was coming out of my skin, out of my eyes, out of my breath fogging gold in the cold air. "I don't know how."

That was the truth, and it was the most frightening sentence I've ever said. The ember rose in me like a tide and I stood in it with no banks, no bed, no shape to hold it. Every heartbeat pushed more of it out. Somewhere below, the crowd had gone from muttering to silence to that specific terrified murmur that precedes either worship or stones.

"*Wardens,*" Bramble snarled, appearing at the window beside me in a rush of displaced air—she'd come up the outside wall, wings snapping wide, because apparently that was a thing she could just *do* and had neglected to mention. She pointed east with her chin. Down the frozen length of Millrace Street, blue lantern-light was bobbing. Then north. Then, God help us, west. "Three patrols. Maybe four. They've been ringing this town since the train station incident and you just lit a bonfire visible from the *provincial capital.*"

"The boy," the mother was saying, over and over, clutching my sleeve, tears freezing on her cheeks. "You healed my Tomas. Blessed saint, you—he was dying, they said he was dying—"

"Not a saint," I said. "Really, genuinely, please don't—"

"Holy woman on the corner says the saints are coming back," someone shouted from the crowd below, and someone else shouted back that the saints were heresy and the Accords said so, and the murmur curdled toward something uglier.

"Out," Lena said. She'd shoved past the mother into the tiny room—bed, stove, crucifix, a child's drawings pinned to the wall—and she was already moving with the brisk competence of a woman who had spent five years hiding illegal things from professional finders. "Back stairs. Bramble, can you take the boy?"

"Take the—why would I take the *boy?*"

"Because if the Wardens find a healed child in this room, they don't ask questions, they take him for testing. Because his mother will hang for harboring. And because if you leave him here I will make your life extremely difficult, and I know where you sleep."

Bramble stared at her. Then she swore in at least two languages, one of which I'm fairly sure was river-fae and one of which was definitely Ashfall dockside, and scooped the boy up like he weighed nothing. "One condition. Nobody tells Odile I did a *good deed.* Ruins my reputation."

"Go," Lena said.

They went out the window—Bramble dropping into the dark with the boy bundled against her chest, wings beating once, twice, a shadow crossing the moonlit street below to a chorus of gasps. The mother grabbed my hand, kissed my knuckles, and said something in a whisper I pretended not to hear because if I heard it I'd have to carry it, and I was already carrying too much.

Then Lena took my face in both her hands.

Her palms were cold. Her eyes weren't. They were furious and frightened and ferociously, stubbornly present, and they anchored me better than anything the ember had ever done.

"Listen to me. Breathe it down. You did it before, in the vault—you pulled it small. Small as a coin. Can you do that?"

"I don't—"

"Small as a coin, August. Think of—" she hesitated, and I watched her decide, watched her spend something precious, "—think of the stall. Evening light. Me handing you the luck-charm. Think of that, and put the fire *there.*"

So I did. That's the embarrassing truth of it. While Wardens closed in from three directions and a crowd worked itself into a frenzy below and the fate of two realms balanced on a knife-edge, I saved us by picturing a girl at a charm stall handing me a copper pendant and saying *for the road, since you look like a man who gets lost.*

The ember went down. Not out—never out, I understood that now, it lived in me the way blood lives in me—but down, banked, curled small around that image like a coal around a hearthstone. The light guttered, shrank, sank into my skin. By the time the first blue lanterns swung into the street below, the window showed nothing but a dark room and two silhouettes.

"Back stairs," Lena breathed. "Move."

* * *

We went out through the tenement's guts—servant stairs, a laundry yard, a gap between drying lines that Lena threaded like she'd been born to it, hauling me by the wrist, hissing *head down, walk, don't run, running is a confession.* Behind us the street filled with Wardens in their gray-and-blue, barking questions at a crowd that mostly didn't want to answer. I caught fragments as we slipped away:

"—saw the light, whole street saw it—"

"—half-fae, flew off with a child, heading for the river—"

"—and a man, they're saying a man did the healing, tall, dark hair—"

"They're saying a lot of men are tall with dark hair," Lena muttered. "Keep walking."

We cut through the tannery district, where the stink covered everything including, hopefully, the ember's afterglow, whatever that registered as on Warden instruments. Lena moved through Greyhollow's backways like water finding cracks, and I followed, and somewhere in the fourth alley I realized she hadn't let go of my wrist. Not pulling anymore. Just holding. Like a tether. Like she'd learned something on that night train about what happened to me when she let go.

"Lena," I said, when we finally stopped in the lee of a collapsed granary, breath steaming, hearts hammering. "Thank you. For—for knowing what to picture."

She didn't look at me. Snow was starting again, fat slow flakes drifting down through the dark, settling on her hair.

"Don't thank me yet," she said. "Every Warden in the province is going to be sweeping this valley by morning. Sera Voss will have felt that flare from fifty miles away, if she didn't see it. We have maybe six hours before Greyhollow is a net, and we're the fish."

"So we run."

"So we run." She finally turned, and in the dark her expression did something complicated—pride and terror braided together so tightly I couldn't tell which strand was which. "But August—what you did back there. That boy is alive because of you. Whatever else happens, whatever this power costs, whatever they write about the ember in their ledgers—" her voice caught, just slightly, "—that was good. Do you understand? That was *good*, and I need you to hold onto that, because the world is going to spend the next month telling you it was a crime."

I wanted to answer her. I had a whole speech assembled somewhere—about how the only thing that felt true in my chest besides the fire was her, about how I'd started measuring days by whether I made her almost-smile, about how I was terrified that loving her and losing myself to the ember might turn out to be the same motion. But what came out was:

"You remembered the charm stall."

"What?"

"When you told me to think of something small. You picked—that. Us. The day we met." My heart was doing something that had nothing to do with magic. "Why that?"

Snow gathered on her lashes. For a long moment she said nothing, and the flakes fell between us like the world holding its breath.

"Because it was warm," she said finally. "And because it was real. And because—" She stopped. Shook her head. Took my wrist again. "Come on. Bramble will be at the mill bridge, and we have a long way to go before sunrise, and I know a place we can hide. Nobody knows it. Not even the Wardens."

"Where?"

She glanced back at me over her shoulder, and despite everything—the sirens starting up in the distance, the net drawing closed, the fire banked but not sleeping in my ribs—she smiled. Really smiled, this time. The full version. The one I'd been working five days to earn.

"My cellar," said the keeper of the gate. "Where else?"

Behind us, far off across the frozen town, a bell began to ring—not the hour, not the alarm, but the deep triple toll that meant the province had been raised. Every Warden in Greyhollow province, waking to hunt us. And ahead of us, down through the snow and the dark, the road home to the very door they'd be searching for.

Love and danger, I was beginning to understand, had always been the same fire. The only question—the one I'd be answering for the rest of my life, however long the ledger allowed—was what we chose to burn.
