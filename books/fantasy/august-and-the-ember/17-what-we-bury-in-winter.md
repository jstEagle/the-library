## 17. What We Bury in Winter

The safehouse was a milliner's cottage three days north of Greyhollow, which meant it smelled of hat glue, old felt, and the particular loneliness of buildings that have been waiting for someone to come home. The key had been under a stone shaped like a sleeping cat, which Bramble said was "almost insultingly cute" and then checked twice for traps anyway. Half-fae paranoia is a lifestyle, not a mood.

There were four bedrooms, a kitchen with a stove that lit on the third try, and a window seat wide enough for two if neither of them minded touching. I noticed that last detail immediately and then spent the better part of an afternoon pretending I hadn't.

Here is what nobody tells you about running for your life: eventually, you stop. And when you stop, all the things you were running past catch up with you, and they sit down at your table, and they ask to be felt.

We had stopped. The Wardens were somewhere behind us, and the something older was somewhere behind them, and for the moment—for one snow-hushed, improbable moment—nobody was trying to kill us. The storm had come down off the mountains and sealed us in like a jar, and the four of us stood in a dead hatmaker's kitchen, listening to the wind, and did not know what to do with safety.

Odile solved it the way ex-nuns solve everything: she made tea. Bramble solved it the way smugglers solve everything: she counted the exits. August solved it the way August solves everything, which is to say he found the one object in the room that would cause the most conversation—a wall of hatpins arranged by color, decades of them, each with a tiny glass head—and said, "Do you think she was happy?"

And I solved it the way I solve everything, which is to say I stood in the doorway and watched him and did not say what I was thinking, which was: *I think she loved someone, and I think she kept the colors to remember, and I think that is the whole of the human project.*

I was getting sentimental. It was the snow. Snow does that to me. Also the near-death. Also him.

* * *

We ate stew made of things Bramble had been carrying in her coat, which I have decided not to think about too hard. She called it "canal rabbit" with the confidence of a woman who has never once been asked to produce the rabbit. Odile said grace, out of habit, then caught herself mid-blessing and turned it into something that wasn't quite a prayer and wasn't quite an apology. August held his bowl in both hands like it might leave him.

It was Bramble, of all people, who broke the quiet.

"Right," she said, pointing her spoon at the room at large. "Rules. Nobody dies quietly in a corner. If you're dying, you do it loudly, where we can all be annoyed by it. Nobody keeps watch alone—pairs, alternating. And nobody—" the spoon swung to August, "—nobody does the thing where you apologize for existing. I've seen you doing it. It's insufferable."

"I don't—"

"You do. You knocked over a chair this morning and said sorry to the *floor*."

"The floor didn't deserve it," August said, and Odile snorted tea, and Bramble looked personally victorious, and I felt something in my chest that I hadn't felt in five years of careful, hidden, charm-selling life.

It was the feeling of being *in* something. Not adjacent to it. Not hiding behind it. In it.

* * *

The stories started after dark, the way stories do.

Bramble went first, because Bramble always went first; she'd have volunteered to go first at her own funeral. She told us about her mother, the fae one, who had left when Bramble was six, and the locket we'd bled for in the vaults beneath Belltower Row, and how she'd worn it every day since even though it had tried to set her sleeve on fire twice. "Three times," she corrected herself. "But the third was mostly the sleeve's fault."

She told it funny. She told everything funny. That was the trick of her, I was learning—she wrapped every wound in so many jokes that by the time you found the wound you'd already laughed at it, and laughing at a wound is a kind of first aid.

Then Odile, reluctantly, hands around her cup. She told us about the convent at Saint Verity's, about the silence vows, about the morning she'd watched her sister put on a Warden's coat and understood that grief could become a career. "I thought if I gave up magic entirely, it couldn't take anything else from me," she said. "It's a bad bargain. You pay everything and it takes anyway." She looked at August when she said it, and August looked at his hands, and nobody made it a lesson.

Then it was my turn, and I did what I always do, which is lie by omission with excellent posture.

I told them about my grandmother teaching me the first charm—a warmth charm, for cold hands, the kind of magic so small the Accords barely bothered to forbid it. I told them about the stall, the little brass scales, the customers who came for luck-knots and love-charms that were honestly just good advice with ribbon on it. I told them about the ledger in my cellar, and how it had opened itself one night a year ago and shown me a face inked a hundred years before I was born.

I did not tell them the rest. I was saving the rest. Or hiding it. Those are the same motion performed at different speeds.

"Your turn," Bramble said to August, and he went, and his story was a slow unraveling.

He told us about his grandmother—the one whose dying words had sent him to Ashfall, to a charm stall he'd chosen out of a hundred. He told us about growing up feeling like a room with one wall missing, like weather moved through him that didn't move through anyone else. He told us about the moment in the canal market when his magic had come out of him sideways and saved all our lives and ruined his anonymity in the same heartbeat, and how the terror and the rightness of it had arrived in the same breath and never separated since.

"I keep waiting for it to feel like a mistake," he said. "It doesn't. That's the frightening part. It feels like—" He stopped. He was good at stopping. "Like remembering something instead of learning it."

The fire ticked. Snow pressed at the windows like something listening.

"The ledger says *the ember returns*," I said, before I could stop myself. "Not *arrives*. Returns."

"Maybe it's just old language," Odile said gently.

"Maybe," I said. It was not what I thought. What I thought was: *the gate stirred at your touch like a dog hearing its name.* But the fire was warm and the stew was in us and I had spent five years learning that some truths are load-bearing walls—you don't pull them out to see what happens.

* * *

Later—much later, when Odile had gone up to pray-or-not-pray and Bramble had gone up to count her exits one final time and fall asleep mid-count—August and I ended up at the window seat.

I want to be clear that this was not engineered. I had engineered several other things that evening—the seating at dinner, the topic of the ledger, the precise moment I'd refilled his cup so that our hands did the thing hands do—and I am not proud of the engineering, but I own it. The window seat, though. The window seat was the snow's fault. There was nowhere else to sit that wasn't a bedroom, and it would have been strange to go to a bedroom, and so we sat, shoulder to shoulder, watching the storm erase the road we'd come by.

"You've been quiet," he said.

"I'm always quiet. It's my brand."

"You've been quiet *at* me. It's different. It has a direction."

I laughed despite myself. He was learning my tells, which was either wonderful or catastrophic, and I genuinely could not tell which, and the not-telling was its own strange pleasure.

"August," I said.

"Lena."

"I have to tell you something and you're going to think it's strange."

"I watched a man turn into a flock of crows to escape a Warden checkpoint last week," he said. "My bar for strange is currently underground."

So I told him. Not all of it—I am still me, and five years of hiding doesn't leave a person all at once, the way a coat doesn't come off in a fire, it comes off in burns. But I told him the shape of it.

"A year ago," I said, "when the ledger showed me your face—before you ever walked up to my stall, before the date, before any of it—I knew you. Not your name. Not your story. Just—there's a thing that happens when you guard something alone for long enough. You stop being a person and start being a *post*. You stand where you're told and you don't move and you don't hope, because hoping is a door and doors are what I guard against. And then the ledger opened, and there was this face, a hundred years old, inked by a hand that was dust before my grandmother was born, and the words said *the ember returns*, and I—"

I stopped. He waited. He was good at waiting; it was one of the thousand things I wasn't going to say out loud.

"I sat down on the cellar stairs," I said, "and I cried. Not because I was afraid. Because for the first time in five years, I hoped. And hope felt like a bone breaking and setting straight all at once. And then a week later you walked into my stall, and it was *you*, and you bought a luck-knot and haggled badly, and I thought: *oh no. Oh no, it's him, and he doesn't know, and I'm going to have to be a person again, and I don't remember how.*"

The snow ticked against the glass. Somewhere below the floor, beneath the frost-line, beneath the whole sleeping world, I could feel the gate in my cellar in Ashfall, miles away, humming its low patient note. It hummed louder, I thought, when I thought of him. I had noticed that. I had decided not to have noticed that.

"So when I say I've loved you," I said, "I want to be honest about the timeline. It isn't since the date. It isn't since the canal, or the vault, or the train. It's since the ledger. I loved a face in a book before I ever loved a man, and I don't know what that makes me, and I've been carrying it around like a stone in my boot for a year, and I'm tired, August. I'm so tired of carrying it."

The silence after was very long and very warm, like the inside of bread.

"You haggled me down to a fair price," he said finally, "and then you gave me the charm for free and told me it was a promotion."

"You were the first person in a year to check the knot-work for flaws. Nobody checks. They just want the luck."

"I checked because your hands were shaking when you tied it," he said. "I thought—if her hands shake, the least I can do is look closely at what she made."

I turned to look at him and he was already looking at me, and the space between us had gone small and charged, the way air goes before lightning, and I thought: *this is the moment, this is the exact moment, and if I lean one inch the whole architecture of my careful life comes down, and I want it to, I want it so badly my teeth ache—*

He leaned. I leaned. Our foreheads touched, just that, just bone and warmth and the faint salt-smell of travel, and we stayed there, in the inch before the kiss, in the anteroom of it, and it was somehow more devastating than the kiss would have been. His breath caught. Mine answered it.

"Lena," he whispered, and my name in his mouth sounded like it had been there a hundred years, like the ink had always known it—

The gate-note under my sternum flared, sudden and bright, a struck bell, a held breath—

And the shutter on the downstairs window rattled. Once. Twice. Not the wind. The wind doesn't knock.

We broke apart like teenagers. Bramble was down the stairs before I'd finished standing, knife already in her hand, hair like an insulted cat. Odile appeared at the banister with a candle and no weapon, which was either faith or denial.

Bramble cracked the shutter and looked out into the blue-black snow, and I watched her whole body change—shoulders dropping half an inch, knife hand loosening.

"Fox," she said. "Big one. Watching the house." She paused. "Wearing a collar."

"Collared foxes are just cats with a PR problem," I said, and Bramble snorted, and the moment unclenched, and we all pretended our hearts weren't going like siege drums.

But I stood at the window a long time after they'd gone back up, looking at the fox-shape retreating between the drifts, and the gate-note under my ribs had not settled. It was still ringing, still bright, and underneath the ringing was something I hadn't heard from it before.

It sounded, faint and far and unmistakable, like it was *counting.*

Four heartbeats in this house. Four names in the ledger. And something out in the snow, tonight, taking the measure of us—one, two, three, four—and turning home to report.
