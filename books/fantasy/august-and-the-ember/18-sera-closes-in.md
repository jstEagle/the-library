## 18. Sera Closes In

The rain in Ashfall never falls so much as it *commits*, and tonight it had committed fully, hammering the cobbles of Belltower Row into a gray mirror that reflected nothing I wanted to see. Mostly myself, hunched under an oilskin cloak that had stopped being waterproof somewhere around the second year of my service, standing outside a shuttered dye-works that my informants swore had gone out of business a decade ago.

Informants lie. Buildings lie worse. But the mud didn't lie, and the mud said four sets of feet had gone through that door in the last hour, one of them dragging slightly on the left, one of them barefoot, which in this weather meant either madness or fae blood, and in the canal district those were frequently the same thing.

I'd been tracking Pemberton and her stranger for eleven days. I want that on the record, somewhere, in some ledger that matters: eleven days. Not because I couldn't have found them sooner. Because I kept finding reasons not to look too hard.

Warden training teaches you that hesitation is a door left open for the enemy to walk through. What training doesn't teach you is what to do when *you're* the enemy walking through it.

* * *

Three days earlier, I'd done something no Warden of my rank should ever do. I'd gone into the restricted archive beneath the Hall of Ordinance — the deep shelves, the ones behind the blood-locked door that High Warden Corvane liked to pretend were full of tax records from the Sundering era — and I'd taken a book that didn't belong to me.

Technically it didn't belong to anyone. That was the problem.

The ledger had been misfiled. That's what I told myself, anyway, when my fingers closed on the cracked leather spine: *misfiled*, the way a knife in your back is *misplaced*. It was a warden's field journal, dated one hundred and seven years ago, author's name burned off the flyleaf in the neat, deliberate way the Order burned things it wanted forgotten. I told myself I was doing due diligence. I told myself Corvane would want to know about a document that hadn't been catalogued.

I told myself a great many things that week. It's a skill. We practice.

I read it that night in my quarters with the lamp turned low, the way you read anything you're ashamed of, and by the third page I had stopped breathing at regular intervals.

The journal's author had been at the Sundering. Not at the edge of it — at the *center*. And according to him — I decided it was a him; the handwriting was furious in a masculine way I recognized from every promotion board I'd ever sat before — the Sundering had not been an accident. It had not been the realms tearing at each other, the way the Accords taught it, the way the murals in the Hall showed it, the great wound that sorcery had inflicted on the world and that the Wardens existed to keep from bleeding again.

It had been a *cut*. Made on purpose. By the first covenantors themselves.

Because of love.

I remember laughing when I read that. Alone, in the dark, at one in the morning, laughing like a woman who'd found out the floor she'd stood on her whole life was painted onto a pit. The gate between the realms — the one the Accords called the Wound, the one my Order had sworn a hundred years of oaths around — hadn't been sealed to protect the mortal world from fae predation. It had been sealed to protect the *covenantors* from something worse, in their estimation. From the possibility that the two realms might fall in love with each other. That mortals and fae might marry, and grieve, and blur, and that the borders — and the power the borders gave the people who guarded them — might dissolve.

The first Wardens hadn't been heroes. They'd been *bouncers*. Hired by frightened men to keep the door shut on a wedding.

I sat with that journal until my candle drowned in its own wax, and then I did the second thing no Warden of my rank should ever do.

I didn't report it.

* * *

So now: the dye-works, the rain, the four sets of footprints. Pemberton, the stranger called August, the half-fae smuggler whose file I'd had to pull from the canal district annex under a false case number, and — this was the part that had kept me awake, the part that felt like a splinter working toward my heart — a fourth. A woman. Small, dark-haired, seen from a distance through a spyglass at the night train station in Greyhollow, standing with the fugitives like she belonged there.

I knew her walk. You don't forget the walk of someone you shared a bed with for six years, even when the bed was a cot in a Warden barracks and even when she left it, and you, and everything else, for a cloister in Greyhollow.

Odile.

My sister was traveling with the traitor I'd sworn to bring in.

* * *

Here is what my file on Lena Pemberton says, in the Order's tidy hand: *Charm-seller, licensed, district four. Subject of ongoing surveillance following irregularities at the Belltower vault. Suspected unlicensed sorcery. Approach with caution. Subject is known to Warden S. Voss.*

*Known to.* As if that phrase could hold what it needed to hold. As if two words could carry a summer when we were nine years old and Lena Pemberton taught me to skip stones across the Ash river and I taught her to pick the Warden cadets' locks for fun, back when the Accords were just words old men shouted and neither of us knew which side of them we'd end up on.

The Accords took my family. That's the sentence I've said at every review, every promotion, every funeral for every colleague the gate's creatures ever killed. *The Accords took my family.* It's true. It's also, I have recently begun to suspect, a sentence I built my whole life inside of, the way a snail builds a shell, and I am starting to hear the hollow sound when I knock on it.

Because here is what the journal said that I have not been able to stop hearing, in the small hours, in the voice of a dead man with a burned-off name: *We did not seal the gate to save the world. We sealed it to save ourselves from what we could not bear to lose. And the cost of that cowardice will come due, and it will come due in love, because that is the coin the gate was paid in.*

The Sundering took my parents. I have believed that since I was six years old, since the night the sky over Greyhollow split and the light came through and our house was simply *not there anymore*, and Odile and I were pulled out of the rubble by Warden boots and Warden hands and raised on Warden bread and Warden grief.

And now a dead man's handwriting was suggesting — no, *asserting*, in the flat tone of a man recording what he'd done so he wouldn't have to feel it — that the Sundering was engineered. That the wound that killed my parents was *made*. Deliberately. By the people my Order canonized.

You can't unring a bell like that. You can't unread a page like that. I've tried both. The bell keeps ringing and the page keeps being there, sitting in the false bottom of my field kit like a coal that won't go out.

* * *

I stood outside the dye-works for forty minutes. Long enough that the rain found the seam at my collar and began its slow, cold journey down my spine. Long enough that a cat — canal-born, half-feral, the kind of cat that has opinions — sat on a barrel across the alley and watched me with the contempt only cats and my superiors have ever mastered.

*Go in*, said the part of me that was built by the Order, the part that fits together so neatly with all the other parts, the part that got me my rank and my cloak and my reason to get up in the morning. *Four fugitives, one door, one Warden. You've taken harder rooms than this.*

*Don't*, said the part that had read the journal. *Because if you go in and put irons on Lena Pemberton, you will be doing exactly what the men who killed your parents wanted you to do, and you will do it wearing their badge, and you will call it duty.*

The cat yawned.

I did what any reasonable woman does when torn between two versions of herself. I went around the back.

* * *

The dye-works' rear loading yard was a graveyard of vats gone green with moss, and above the loading door there was a loading hatch, left open a hand's width for air, and through it came light and warmth and the smell of something cooking, and — this is the detail that undid me, I want to be honest about that, this is the detail I will carry — *laughter*.

Not the laughter of people getting away with something. I know that laughter; I've heard it through a hundred doors I was about to kick open. It's bright and fast and scared underneath. This was different. This was slow. This was the laughter of people who had been running for eleven days and had, for one evening, in a ruined dye-works in the rain, decided to stop.

I heard the smuggler — Bramble Quill, half-fae, a file thin as rice paper because the Order has never cared much about the canal district's dead — say something in a voice like gravel soaked in honey, and the room laughed again. I heard Odile's voice, my sister's voice, saying something I couldn't make out, and then saying it again, louder, and the laughter got bigger, and I pressed my back against the wet brick and closed my eyes.

She was laughing. Odile. Who hadn't laughed since the Sundering, since the rubble, since the day she decided God had taken her parents and magic had handed Him the knife, since she wrapped herself in a habit and a vow and walked out of my life with her chin up and her eyes dry. She was laughing in a room full of sorcerers, and she sounded — there's no other word for it — she sounded *safe*.

And I was outside in the rain with a warrant and a journal full of heresy, and I could not for the life of me remember which one of us was the criminal.

* * *

I want to tell you I made a clean decision. Wardens make clean decisions; it's in the recruiting material. I want to tell you I weighed loyalty against truth on some inner scale and came down firmly on one side, and that the chapter of my life turned on a single resolute moment.

It didn't happen like that. What happened was this: I climbed up to the hatch. I looked in.

They were at a long table made of a door laid across two dye vats. Pemberton was at one end, and the stranger — August — was at the other, and between them the table was a mess of maps and the guardian ledger, that impossible book the Order had been hunting since the vault, and Bramble was standing on her chair pointing at the canal district on a map with a knife she was using as a pointer, and Odile was — Odile was *smiling*, tiredly, the way you smile at family, at the table, and August said something I couldn't hear and Pemberton looked at him with an expression I recognized from the inside, an expression I once wore looking at a woman I'd loved before the Order taught me to file that under *prior life*, and the lantern light was gold on all of them, and the rain came down behind me like a curtain between two worlds.

And I thought: *I could end this tonight.* Four of them, tired, fed, unarmored. One of me, armed, warrant in my pocket, backup a whistle away. The Order's whole machinery ready to grind them fine.

And I thought: *And then what?* Then they go to the cells beneath the Hall. Then Corvane asks his quiet questions. Then, if the journal is right — and every mile I'd tracked them, every piece of the puzzle they'd assembled, had only confirmed it — then the gate comes due, the way the dead man said it would, and the price gets paid by someone, and it won't be Corvane, and it won't be the Order, and it won't be me. It'll be the people at that table. Or it'll be the city. Or it'll be both, and my warrant will have made sure of it.

The dead man's journal had one more line, the last one, the one I'd read a dozen times by now, the one that had gotten me out of my bunk at midnight and into the rain and around to this hatch:

*If any Warden ever reads this: we were wrong. We were afraid, and we called our fear law, and we made your grandparents enforce it. Find the heirs. Find the ember. Open the door we were too cowardly to leave open, and forgive us, if forgiveness is a thing the dead can ask for.*

Find the heirs.

I had spent eleven days hunting them. The dead man had just told me I'd been doing the right thing in the wrong direction.

* * *

Below me, the room shifted. Pemberton stood, gathering maps, and said something crisp — I caught the words *"north road"* and *"before dawn"* — and the family at the table began, with the practiced ease of people who had done this too many times lately, to pack.

I should go. Report it. Let the machinery do what machinery does.

I reached into my coat, and my hand closed — I'd like to say it closed on the whistle, but my hand has apparently developed its own opinions this week — on the journal. The cracked leather. The burned-off name. The heresy that fit in my palm like a stone from the rubble of my childhood house.

Below, Odile paused at the door, and looked around the room, and said something soft that I couldn't hear, and Bramble put a hand on her shoulder, and they went out through the front, all four of them, their voices fading into the rain.

I sat on the wet roof of the loading dock for a long time after they were gone, with the cat for company and the journal in my lap, and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding for eleven days.

If I reported their position now, the Order would take them by morning. If I reported nothing, Corvane would notice the gap in my field reports within two days, and he would send someone else, someone without a dead sister's laughter in her ears and a heretical journal in her kit, and that someone would take them anyway, and I would be in a cell beside them for obstruction.

Two days. I had two days, maybe, before my silence became its own kind of warrant.

Unless.

Unless the Warden who found them next was me. Unless I got to them first, not with irons but with a journal and a question — *did you know your Sundering was manufactured? did you know what my Order really is?* — and unless the fourth name on their ledger, the one I'd seen them tracing with a finger on that map, the one the blood-magic had eaten, turned out to be a name I needed to hear spoken aloud.

I got up. The cat watched me go with what I choose to believe was approval.

I had a two-day head start on my own conscience, a stolen book, a sister on the north road, and, for the first time in twenty years, no idea what I was going to do when I caught up with her.

It felt, I'm ashamed to say, like flying.
