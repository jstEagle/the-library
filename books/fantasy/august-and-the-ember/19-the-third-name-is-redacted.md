## 19. The Third Name Is Redacted

I've stolen a lot of things in my life. Lockets, ledgers, a Warden's boots once, off his feet while he was still wearing them—long story, involved eels. But I'd never stolen the truth out of a book before, and I want it on record that I didn't enjoy it.

The ledger sat on Lena's cellar table like it knew we were coming for it. Old thing, bound in leather gone the color of dried blood, with brass corners worn soft by a century of hands. It had opened itself to August's face two weeks ago and hadn't closed properly since. It lay there breathing—that's the only word for it, breathing, pages rising and falling a hair's breadth—and every time I looked at it too long, it looked back.

"So," I said, "we're agreed this is haunted."

"It's not haunted," Lena said. She was grinding something in a mortar, because Lena is constitutionally incapable of standing in a room without doing useful work with her hands. "It's *attentive*."

"Attentive. Right. The book is attentive. And the gate under your floor is *chatty*, and the dead language it whispers is *polite*. You know what we call attentive things where I come from? We call them *haunted*, and then we charge extra."

August laughed from the corner where he was supposed to be practicing warding sigils and was instead drawing what appeared to be a duck. He'd gotten better at magic since the canal market, since the day he lit up like a struck match in front of forty witnesses and three Wardens and made all our lives considerably shorter. He'd also gotten better at laughing at exactly the moments when the rest of us couldn't. I'd decided this was one of his better qualities. Ask me again after the next near-death experience.

Lena set down the mortar. "Bramble. Show them."

I showed them. Reluctantly. With commentary.

Here's what happened: the night before, while the other two slept, I'd sat alone with the ledger—which, yes, was stupid, and yes, I did it anyway, because I'm a smuggler and curiosity is the cargo I can never refuse—and I'd asked it, out loud, for the third name. The covenant needed four heirs. August, ember-born, face inked in prophecy a hundred years before his grandmother ever met his grandfather. Me, half-fae, blood of the other side of the Sundering, which explained a lot about my mother's silences and nothing about my father's gambling debts. Odile Voss, the nun who'd rather die than be magical, currently upstairs pretending she wasn't listening to everything through the floorboards because nuns hear everything, it's a professional skill.

And then there was the third name. Or there should have been.

Where the third name should have been, there was a smear of black. Not ink. Ink sits *on* paper. This was soaked *through* it, page after page, entry after entry, every mention of the third heir blotted out in a darkness that had a texture to it, a wetness, like the paper itself had been cut and the cut had scabbed over. Blood-magic. Old, deliberate, expensive blood-magic. Somebody had paid a price to make sure no one would ever read that name again.

Except when I'd leaned close—because again, stupid, curious, smuggler—I'd seen something underneath. Not letters. A *map*. Faint as breath on glass: a coastline, a river delta, a scar across the land drawn in the same black that hid the name. And beneath the scar, in letters so old they were practically fossils:

*where the world was cut.*

So now the four of us stood around a haunted book in a cellar under a charm shop, looking at a century-old wound in the world, and nobody wanted to say the obvious thing.

Odile said it. Of course she did. She'd come downstairs without her habit, in plain grey wool, which was her way of saying she was here as a person and not a symbol, though she still managed to look like both. "The Sundering field. The battlefield." Her voice did the thing it always did when she talked about the Sundering—went flat and careful, like someone walking on river ice. "That's a week's travel north. Through three checkpoints. Past the Grey Marshes. Into territory the Wardens don't even patrol, because the Wardens are afraid of it."

"The Wardens are afraid of it?" August said.

"Everyone's afraid of it," Odile said. "That's what makes it effective."

I flipped the ledger toward them so they could see the scar-map properly. Up close, the black blot pulsed. Slowly. Like a heart. "Here's my read," I said. "Somebody bled themselves dry hiding this name. That kind of working doesn't just cover paper—it covers *memory*. It'll have eaten every record, every gravestone, every gossiping ghost that ever knew who the third heir was. There's exactly one place left where the truth might still live, and it's the place where the truth got its legs broken in the first place."

"The battlefield," August said quietly.

"The battlefield," I confirmed. "Where the realms were torn apart. Where the old covenant died screaming. Where—" I gestured vaguely at the ceiling, meaning the gate, meaning the whole rotten architecture of the last hundred years, "—all *this* started."

Lena had gone very still. I'd learned to read her stillnesses by then. This one was the bad kind—the kind where her eyes went somewhere else entirely, somewhere below the floorboards, somewhere behind a sealed door she kept even from herself. "There are ghosts there," she said. "If the ledger is pointing us there, it's not pointing us at paper. It's pointing us at whatever's left of the people who made the covenant. What's left of them might not be... friendly. Might not even be *people* anymore."

"Wonderful," I said. "Ghosts, marshes, checkpoints, and an army of Wardens who've been hunting us since the canal market. Anyone else want to add to the itinerary? Plague? Bears? A wedding?"

"Actually," August said, and I watched his ears go pink, "I did write to my grandmother's lawyer—"

"*No,*" the rest of us said together.

He put his hands up. "Fine. No lawyers."

* * *

We voted. That's the part people never believe when I tell this story later, usually in taverns, usually for drinks. They expect drama. They expect someone slamming a fist or drawing a blade or delivering a speech about destiny. What actually happened was four exhausted people sitting around a cellar table eating cold rice while a haunted book breathed at them, taking a vote.

Lena voted yes, but you could see what it cost her. She was the keeper; the gate was hers in the way a debt is yours. Every day we delayed, the cracks in it spread another finger-width, and every night she went down alone to pour her own strength into the seal and came back up thinner. She didn't say any of that. She just said, "Yes," in the voice of someone agreeing to surgery.

Odile voted yes last, and made us wait for it. She paced. She prayed—quietly, under her breath, in a language I suspect was mostly complaints. Then she sat down and folded her hands like a woman signing away a farm. "My sister is out there right now," she said, meaning Sera, meaning the Warden who used to be a little girl who shared a bed with her during storms. "If Sera finds the third heir before we do—if the High Warden does—then everything we've suffered has been for nothing, and everyone who comes after us inherits the same cage. So. Yes. I'll go back to the place where my parents died." Her jaw did something heroic and terrible. "But I want it noted that I think this plan is insane."

"Noted," I said. "Seconded. Carried unanimously."

Which left me. And here's the thing about being the funny one: sometimes the funny one gets to hide behind the jokes right up until the moment the jokes run out, and everyone's looking at you, and the vote needs your voice to be real.

Because here's what I hadn't told them. Here's what I'd only admitted to the ledger, alone, at three in the morning, with my mother's empty locket-shaped absence sitting against my sternum where it had sat since the vault, since we got it back and found the sigil inside and learned that my whole life—the smuggling, the running, the clever exits—had been a script somebody else wrote before I was born.

I was afraid of that field. Not ghost-afraid. Not Warden-afraid. I'm half-fae; fear works differently in us. When mortals stand in a haunted place, they feel cold. When I stand in one, I feel *called*. The other side of my blood leans toward old wounds like a plant toward light, and the Sundering field was the oldest wound in the world. Somewhere in my mother's line, someone had stood on that field when it was made. Some piece of me remembered it, and that piece had been whispering to me ever since I saw the map: *come home, come home, come home.*

And I didn't know what would be left of Bramble Quill if I listened.

"Bramble?" Lena said gently.

"Yeah." I spun my knife—one of my thinking habits, don't judge me—caught it, put it down, picked up the rice instead. Ate a bite. Put the rice down. "Look. I want to say something honest, and I'd like it if nobody made a face about it afterward, because I have a reputation."

"We would never," August said, making a face immediately.

"I'm scared of this one." The words came out smaller than I meant them to. I pushed on. "Not the Wardens. Wardens I can handle; they're just bullies with paperwork. Not the ghosts either. Ghosts are just people who lost their exit. I can talk my way past almost anything that used to be alive." I looked down at my hands. Half-fae hands, quick and clever, my mother's hands, and my mother had never once told me why she flinched at open fields. "It's the place itself. My blood knows that ground. And my blood doesn't ask politely. If we go there, some part of me is going to want to stay. And I need—" My voice cracked, damn it, right in front of everyone. "I need you all to promise that if that happens, you won't let me. Whatever I say. However good my reasons sound. Because I am *very* good at reasons. It's the family trade."

The silence lasted three heartbeats. Then August reached across the table and put his hand flat on mine—not grabbing, just anchoring, the way you'd weight something valuable against wind. "Then we go together," he said, "and we leave together. All four. Count heads at the gate, count heads on the road. Nobody stays. Not you, not anyone."

"That's not how these stories usually go," Odile said softly.

"Then the stories are wrong," August said, and the ember under his skin flickered, just barely, gold along his knuckles, and for once none of us flinched from it.

Lena nodded slowly. "Courage isn't deciding you're not afraid," she said. "My grandmother told me that. Courage is deciding the people beside you matter more than the fear. So we decide. Together. Now, before the road gives us excuses to decide separately."

"Well," I said, blinking hard and blaming the cellar dust, "when you put it like that, I *have* to be brave. It's practically contractual." I stuck out my hand, palm down, over the center of the table. Old smuggler's custom—a bargain sealed skin to skin, witnessed, binding. "Hands in. All of it on the table. We go to the field, we find the name, we come back with four heads and zero haunting incidents. Agreed?"

August's hand went down first, warm as banked coals. Then Lena's, steady, calloused from a thousand charms. Then Odile's, pale and trembling and placed there anyway, which took more spine than anything else she'd done since I'd known her, and she'd done plenty.

Four hands stacked in a lamplit cellar above a sleeping gate, while outside, Ashfall's bells rang the hour and somewhere in the dark, Wardens were sharpening their orders into knives.

"Together," we said. It sounded ridiculous. It sounded like the truest word I'd ever spoken.

* * *

We left two nights later, before dawn, in a coal barge I'd chartered with money I absolutely should not tell you the provenance of. Odile rode in the hold among the sacks, hooded, praying or sulking—hard to tell the difference with her, and honestly I think that was her strategy. Lena worked the tiller like she'd been born on water, which surprised everyone except her. August stood at the bow watching Ashfall shrink behind us, the city where he'd arrived six weeks ago as a stranger with his grandmother's dying words and a train ticket, and I watched him watch it, and I thought: *he looks like a man leaving a house he grew up in.*

Me, I faced north. Toward the marshes. Toward the scar.

The ledger rode in my pack, wrapped in three layers of oilcloth, because I was the only one it didn't try things with. It had sung at Lena until she made it stop. It had shown August dreams. It had once, briefly, displayed Odile's own childhood to her in the margins, which she described as "an invasion" and which I privately suspected was the ledger's idea of an apology. But with me it just lay there, heavy and patient, like a debt collector waiting for payday.

Near midday, with the city gone and the river running wide and grey, it finally spoke to me. Not in words. In warmth. The oilcloth heated against my back, gentle as a hand between the shoulder blades, and I felt it say—clear as my mother's voice, clear as the whisper in my own blood—

*Thank you for choosing.*

"Yeah, well," I muttered, low enough the others wouldn't hear. "Don't get sentimental. I'm still charging you rent."

Ahead, past the last bends of the river, the land rose into hills, and beyond the hills, faint even at this distance, I could see it: a line across the world where nothing grew quite right, where the trees grew crooked and the light fell strange, a scar visible from miles off, the way a lie is visible on a friend's face.

The others gathered at the rail beside me, one by one, and nobody spoke, and nobody turned back.

The black blot in the ledger pulsed once against my spine, like a second heartbeat learning to keep time with mine, and the barge carried us north toward the place where the world had been cut—and, if the old blood had its way, toward the place where it might cut me too.
