## 20. Where the World Was Cut

### Lena

The scar-field announces itself before you see it.

We'd been walking since Greyhollow's last outlying farms gave way to grass that grew wrong—too pale, too even, like a lawn someone had painted on. Bramble stopped complaining about her boots somewhere around noon. Odile stopped praying aloud. August kept glancing at me the way you glance at a door you're pretty sure is locked but check anyway.

Then the birds stopped.

Not flew away. Stopped. Mid-song, mid-wingbeat, as if someone had lifted the needle off the record of the world. And in that silence I felt it through my boots: a flatness, a hollowness, the sensation of standing on a page where something had been cut out with scissors.

"There," August said.

I'd known what we'd find. The ledger had described it in its infuriatingly precise way—the Sundering rendered as an accounting entry, *one (1) world, divided; remainder: scar*. But knowing a thing and seeing it are different animals entirely, and this animal was enormous.

The field stretched for miles, and it was *cut*. Not burned, not cratered—cut, as though a blade had come down through the earth and sky together and left a seam of absence running to the horizon. Along the seam, the grass simply ended. Not browned, not dead. Ended, the way a sentence ends when the writer runs out of ink. Beyond it there was nothing—not darkness, which is a thing, but nothing, which is the lack of one. My eyes kept trying to focus past the edge and my eyes kept filing complaints.

"Charming picnic spot," Bramble said, and her voice came out smaller than she intended. Half-fae nerves, maybe. Or half-fae honesty, which amounts to the same thing at moments like this.

"The battlefield," Odile murmured. She'd crossed herself twice already and was working up to a third. "The histories say a hundred thousand died here when the realms tore apart."

"The histories are wrong," I said. "It wasn't a battle."

They all looked at me.

"It was a surgery," I said. "Somebody cut the worlds apart on purpose. We've suspected it since the vault. This is where we confirm it."

August reached over and took my hand. He'd been doing that more lately—small, wordless, like he was checking I was still there. Given everything the ledger had promised about sacrifice and memory, I understood the impulse. I returned the grip and pretended my pulse was calm. It was not calm. It was doing something more like a drum solo.

"We should wait for dark," Odile said. "Ghosts keep better hours after sundown."

"Ghosts," Bramble repeated. "Lovely. Love that for us."

But Odile was right, and so we made camp at the seam's edge—a fire that burned strangely quiet, no crackle, as if even flame respected the silence here—and waited for the sun to go down behind a horizon that, on the far side of the cut, simply didn't exist. Watching a sunset get swallowed by nothing does something to a person. I don't recommend it.

* * *

### August

I couldn't sleep, which surprised exactly no one.

The others had gone down by degrees—Bramble first, curled against her pack with a knife in her hand even unconscious, then Odile, who prayed herself under like a diver, then Lena, whose breathing finally went slow around midnight while her hand stayed loosely closed around mine, as if sleep hadn't fully informed her body yet.

So it was me alone when the ghosts came up.

They didn't rise out of the ground. That's the image everyone carries, and it's wrong. They came up out of the *cut*—out of that edge of nothing—like figures stepping through a doorway from a room with no light in it. They were faint, silvered, worn thin as old coins. There were dozens of them. Then hundreds. They arranged themselves along the seam in a long silent line, facing our little fire, and every hair on my arms stood up and saluted.

"Lena," I whispered. Nothing. "Lena." I squeezed her hand hard enough to matter.

Her eyes opened instantly—she'd been sleeping the way soldiers and liars sleep, one layer deep—and she saw them, and I watched her go very still in the particular way she goes still when she's terrified and refusing to advertise it.

"Oh," she breathed. "Oh, they're *here*."

"You knew they might be?"

"I hoped." She sat up slowly. "The ledger said the covenantors bound their testimony into the place itself. Witnesses can't be redacted. Only living records can be edited." Her voice shook slightly on the last word. "Blood-magic can black out a name in a book. It can't black out what a hundred thousand people saw with their own dying eyes."

One ghost detached from the line and drifted toward us. Up close, it resolved into a woman in robes that had been fine once, scorched now, hem to collar. Her face had the terrible calm of someone who has already done the worst thing available to do.

"You are the heirs," the ghost said. Her voice sounded like wind through a keyhole. "Four names. We have waited a century to speak them."

The others were awake now—Bramble with her knife up, Odile on her feet with a psalm half-formed, both of them frozen by the sight.

"We seek the third heir," Lena said carefully. "The ledger's third name is redacted. Blood-magic. We came to recover it."

"We know what was done to the record." The ghost's calm cracked, just barely, into something older and angrier. "We were there when it was done. We are the ones it was done *to*."

"Then tell us," August said. My own voice, but steadier than I felt. "Tell us the name."

The ghost looked at me for a long moment. There was recognition in her face—not of me specifically, but of what I was. What the ledger called *the ember returns*. It made me want to step backward. I didn't.

"The covenant was fourfold," she said. "Warden, witch, wanderer, and witness. Four hands to hold the gate. Four bloodlines bound across generations, so that whenever the gate weakened, the hands would be born again to mend it." She raised a hand, silver fingers spread. "The witch's line—" she gestured to Lena. "The wanderer's—" Bramble, who went pale under her freckles. "The witness's—" Odile, who made a small wounded sound. "And the warden's line. The wardens were the gate's first guardians. Before the Accords made them hunters, they were *keepers*. Their blood was the lock's own metal."

"And the warden's heir?" Lena asked. Her voice had gone very quiet. Very careful. I'd learned that quiet. It was the quiet she used when she already knew the answer and was buying time to grieve it.

The ghost turned her head toward the road we'd come by. Toward the south. Toward Ashfall.

Toward the woman who had stood in Lena's doorway with drawn steel and let her live.

"No," Odile said. It came out of her like a reflex, like a flinch. "No. You're wrong. Sera isn't—Sera hunts us. Sera has spent her whole life hunting—"

"Because that is what the leash was made to do," the ghost said gently, horribly. "After the Sundering, the covenantor who cut the worlds apart feared the warden line most. Keepers can become breakers. So he bound their blood into his new order—made their duty a chain, their grief a harness. Every Voss born since has been leashed to the hunt without ever knowing why the hunt felt like home."

The silence that followed was the kind you could stand a spoon up in.

"Sera Voss," Lena said at last. Not a question. A confirmation, wrung out slow, like she was hoping the words would taste different on the way out.

"Sera Voss," the ghost agreed. "The fourth heir. The fourth hand. The gate cannot be reforged without her—and she is marching toward you with orders to burn what you love."

* * *

### Lena

I want to tell you I took it well.

I want to tell you I stood there in the ghost-light and thought, *of course, how elegant, how the pieces fit,* the way heroes do in books. Here's what actually happened: I sat down on the cold ground rather abruptly, because my knees had submitted their resignation, and I laughed—one short, awful bark of a laugh that had no humor anywhere in it.

"Of course it's Sera," I said. "Of course. The woman who held a sword on me and *didn't use it*. The debt. The door. I've been wondering for months why she stayed her hand, and the answer is that her hand was never free to begin with."

"That's not the same as her joining us," Bramble said. She'd lowered her knife at some point, which from Bramble was practically a hug. "You heard the ghost. Leashed. She'll come at us with the whole Warden order at her back."

"She came at us at the shop with a whole Warden order at her back too," I said, "and she looked at me across that threshold and *hesitated*. I told myself it was the old debt. Maybe it was. Maybe debts are just leashes we've named something kinder." I pressed the heels of my hands against my eyes. "She was my friend. Before the Accords. Before they took her family. I knew her when she was a girl who caught fireflies because she liked the way they blinked, not because she was cataloguing magical fauna for violations. And some clerk a century ago decided her entire bloodline would be a component in his machine."

"The covenantor," August said quietly.

The lead ghost turned to him. "You will meet what remains of him," she said. "At the heart. He did not die with the rest of us. He *stayed*." Something moved through her translucent face that I hesitated to call pity, because pity implies distance, and whatever she felt for August was close enough to touch. "Ember-bearer. When you meet him, remember: he was not a monster. He was afraid. Fear built all of this—the cut, the Accords, the leash on the warden's blood. Fear is the only architect history has ever needed."

"Why are you telling us this?" Odile demanded, and her voice broke in the middle. "Why wait a hundred years? Why show us now?"

"Because you asked," the ghost said simply. "No one has asked in a hundred years. The Wardens march past this place and do not look. The faithful build walls around it and call it cursed ground. You four walked to the edge of the cut and *asked*." She began to fade, her edges going soft as breath on glass. "The third name is yours to recover now. We can point. We cannot pull. That was always the covenant's terms—hands must reach willingly."

"Wait," I said, scrambling up. "The redaction—if Sera's name was blacked out by blood-magic, can it be undone? Can *she* be freed of the leash?"

The ghost was nearly gone, a suggestion of silver, a voice like the last note of a bell. "A leash is only rope," she said. "Rope can be cut. Rope can also be *held*, from the other end." And then she was smoke, and the smoke was night air, and the line of ghosts along the seam dimmed back into the cut like candles being carried away down a long corridor.

We stood there in the quiet firelight, four people holding one unbearable fact between us like something hot.

"So," Bramble said finally, into the silence. "Our fourth heir. The one we need to save the world." She sheathed her knife with a click that sounded obscenely loud. "Is currently leading the army trying to kill us."

"Not currently," Odile said. Her face was wet; I don't think she knew it. "Not yet. She doesn't know what she is. None of us knew." She looked at me, and there was something raw and pleading in it—the look of a woman watching her sister get conscripted into a prophecy against her will, having just escaped that exact fate herself. "When she finds out. When someone tells her. What does that do to a person?"

"I know what it did to you," I said gently.

"Yes," Odile said. "Exactly. Now multiply it by every person she's ever arrested. Every charm-seller she's dragged to the cells. Every—" Her voice failed. She sat back down heavily and put her face in her hands.

August crouched beside her. I watched him do the thing he does—no speeches, no comfort-shaped platitudes, just presence, steady as a hearth. "Then we don't tell her like an accusation," he said. "We tell her like an invitation. There's a difference. It's the whole difference."

"You sound very sure," Bramble said.

"I'm not sure of anything," August said. "I'm sure of her hesitation, though. I saw it too. Whatever leash she's wearing, she fought it once already." He glanced at me, and something passed between us that had nothing to do with prophecy—something warm and frightened and certain. "People fight their chains right up until they learn the chains are load-bearing. Then they either break or they choose. We're betting on choose."

I got up and walked to the edge of the cut.

Don't ask me why. Some part of me needed to see it close—to stand at the rim of that absolute nothing and make myself look, because in three days' time, if the ledger was honest, one of us would walk into something like it forever. The void didn't look back. It didn't need to. It just waited, patient as arithmetic, and I felt the ledger's weight in my satchel like a second heartbeat.

Behind me, the camp stirred. August's footsteps came up beside mine, and his hand found my hand again, automatic now, the way breathing is automatic.

"Penny for the doom," he said softly.

"I was thinking about Sera," I lied, mostly.

"Liar," he said fondly, and I loved him a little for calling it, and hated the ledger a lot for what it planned to charge us.

"We ride south at dawn," I said instead. "Back toward Ashfall. Back toward the army." I turned to look at the other two—Odile hollow-eyed by the fire, Bramble already repacking with the grim efficiency of someone who'd expected the worst and budgeted for it. "We're going to intercept a Warden column and recruit its commander by telling her that everything she's built her life on was a cage. And if we're wrong about her—"

"Then we're standing in front of an army," August finished, "with three heirs, one ember, and a lot of good intentions."

"When you say it like that it sounds almost fun."

"I'm given to understand that's my function on this quest."

I laughed despite everything—which, I was beginning to understand, was also his function. Behind us, far off to the south, the night sky over the direction of Ashfall held a faint orange stain, like a bruise coming up. Too distant to be certain. Too orange to ignore.

Fires. Many fires. Marching toward the gate.

"Dawn," I said again, and none of us slept after that.
