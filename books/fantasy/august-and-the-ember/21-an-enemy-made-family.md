## 21. An Enemy Made Family

I had arrested forty-one sorcerers in my career as a Warden of Ashfall. I could tell you the number because I kept a ledger of my own—small, leather-bound, carried against my heart the way other people carry a locket. Forty-one names. Forty-one sets of hands bound in severance-cuffs. Forty-one times I told myself it was justice, or duty, or grief wearing armor so well-fitted I'd forgotten I was ever naked underneath.

The forty-second name in that ledger was going to be Lena Pemberton. It had been penciled there for six days, smudged where my thumb worried at it like a rosary bead.

Instead, standing in the scar-field where the world had been cut open a century ago, I watched a dead woman's ghost point at me and say my name, and I understood that the ledger had been lying to me all along. Not about the count. About whose hands were bound.

"Sera Voss," the ghost said again, patient as frost. "Third heir of the broken covenant."

"Absolutely not," I said.

It was, I am told by Bramble Quill—who has never once let me forget it—the most Warden thing anyone had ever said while standing in a haunted battlefield with their entire worldview collapsing around them like wet plaster.

* * *

Let me back up, because the ghost comes later and the ghost deserves context, and context is something I have spent thirty years rationing out to myself in careful spoonfuls.

I grew up two doors from Lena Pemberton. This is not a secret I've guarded well; it's a secret I've guarded completely, which is different. Her mother taught mine to braid ward-knots into window frames. My father sold her mother apples every market day and pretended not to notice when the apples ripened three days early. We were children together on Belltower Row before the Sundering had a name we were allowed to say, back when magic was just the smell of Lena's cellar and the way her laugh made the streetlamps flicker, which everyone agreed was charming and no one reported.

Then came the Accords. Then came the night the Wardens came for my family—not because we'd done anything, but because my grandmother's name was in an old book somewhere, and old books were evidence, and evidence was enough. My parents were taken east for "assessment." They never came back. I was eleven. The Wardens who processed us were polite. That's the detail that broke me, if you want it: not cruelty, but courtesy. A man with kind eyes gave me a sweet and told me the city would take good care of me, and then he took everything, and he did it all without raising his voice.

So I became a Warden. You can laugh. Everyone does eventually. I joined the machine that ate my family because I believed—truly, stupidly, with the whole burning sincerity of a child—that if I got inside it, I could make it gentler. That I could be the kind eyes that didn't take anything. That the system wasn't rotten, only understaffed by decent people.

Thirty years later I was High Warden's right hand, and my ledger had forty-one names, and I still believed it, the way you believe in a religion you've stopped praying to: out of habit, out of terror of what the empty space would look like.

And through all of it, one door down from my patrol route, Lena Pemberton sold charms to schoolgirls and lied to my face twice a year at the harvest festival, and I knew—I always knew—and I never wrote her name in ink.

People assume that was weakness. Sentiment. The soft heart of Sera Voss, betrayed by childhood affection. I let them assume it. It was easier than the truth, which is that some part of me, some part older than my training, looked at Lena Pemberton and felt *obligation*—a wordless, humming certainty that I owed her something, that she was mine to protect, that the law stopped at her door the way weather stops at a threshold.

I thought it was love. Childhood love gone strange and subterranean, maybe, but love.

It was not love. It was a leash. And last night, in the scar-field, I finally saw the hand that held it.

* * *

They hadn't meant to bring me. That's the part that stings, if I'm honest, and I've decided to be honest these days because lying has become exhausting in a way it never was before. August—the ember-boy, the walking prophecy with the disarming smile—had argued for knocking me unconscious. Bramble had voted for a canal and weights. It was Odile, my own sister, estranged for fifteen years, who said, "She deserves to see it," in a voice that dared the others to disagree.

My sister. Who renounced magic and took vows and scrubbed her own bloodline out of her life with prayer and bleach. Who stood in a haunted field beside a smuggler and a shopkeeper and a boy who glowed, and spoke for me.

We'd come to the scar-field because the ledger's third name was redacted—blacked out with blood-magic, readable only here, where the Sundering happened, where the old covenant's ghosts still paced their endless rounds. I had followed them under pretense of arrest. That was the plan. Corner them at the battlefield, call it in, redeem myself for six days of hesitation with one clean capture.

The plan lasted approximately four minutes, which for my plans lately was practically a golden age.

The ghosts rose out of the ground like heat-shimmer given opinions. They were the covenantors—the four who had sealed the gate a century ago, or what remained of them, worn thin as communion wafers. And they did not flee from us or threaten us. They simply gathered, all four of them, around *me*, and the eldest—a woman with a Warden's posture and a farmer's hands—looked at me with recognition so raw it hurt to witness.

"You're the image of your grandmother," she said.

"I don't know who you are," I said.

"No," she agreed. "You wouldn't. They cut that out of the histories very thoroughly." She raised her hand, and the air above her palm filled with light, and in the light was a document—a covenant, written in four hands, signed in four kinds of blood. "Read the third signature."

I read it. I would like to tell you I refused, that I turned away, that I held the line of my disbelief until the end. But I have always been helpless before documents. It's why I made such a good Warden and such a bad liar.

*Voss*, it said. My family name, inked a hundred years ago, bound into a covenant of protection alongside names I now knew as Pemberton and Quill and Odile's forgotten middle name. Not a coincidence. Not a family that had merely *supported* the covenant. A family *bound* to it—sworn into the gate's service, generation upon generation, our blood tied to the seal itself.

"The Voss line was the covenant's sword arm," the ghost said gently, in the tone you use for a diagnosis. "When the Sundering scattered the covenant, the binding didn't break. It bent. Your family forgot why they felt called to guard the gate. But the calling stayed. Generation after generation, Voss children grew up feeling a pull toward duty they couldn't explain. Some became priests. Some became soldiers." She paused. "Most became Wardens. The leash pulls toward the gate, child. It always has. Your whole order is threaded with our blood, but yours—" her finger touched my sternum without touching me, cold as a key left out overnight— "yours is the thickest strand. You weren't guarding Ashfall from sorcerers, Sera Voss. You were guarding the gate's door without knowing there was a gate. Every arrest you ever made near Pemberton's shop, every time you passed her stall and felt that ache of obligation—you were feeling the covenant tug its leash."

The field was very quiet. Somewhere behind me, August made a small sound like he wanted to say something comforting and had been warned off it.

"My parents," I heard myself say. "The Wardens who took them—"

"Were pulling on the same leash, badly," the ghost said. "The Accords were built by frightened men who found the covenant's records and mistook guardianship for conspiracy. When they purged the old families, they were—without knowing it—punishing the gate's own keepers for keeping it." Her light dimmed. "Your duty was real, child. It was simply never theirs to command."

I want to describe what that does to a person, having the central story of your life revised in front of you. There is no clean way. Everything I was—every choice I'd dressed up as virtue, every cruelty I'd filed under necessity—suddenly had a different author. I had not chosen the Wardens. The Wardens had been *installed* in me, hereditary as eye color. Thirty years of service, and I learned I'd been less a woman than an instrument, a hand on a leash that someone else had fastened before my grandmother's grandmother drew breath.

And worse—oh, so much worse—the forty-one names in my ledger. How many of them had been bound folk too? How many leashes had I mistaken for crimes? I thought of a charm-seller in the canal district I'd cuffed three winters ago, who had wept and said, "You don't understand, I *have* to do this," and I had written *defiance* in my report. She hadn't been defiant. She'd been leashed, like me, and I had dragged her.

I sat down in the ash of a century-old battlefield, in front of a ghost, in front of my estranged sister and three fugitives, and I did not cry, because I had used up all my crying at eleven years old on a train platform, but something in me lay down and refused to get up for a while.

Odile sat next to me. She didn't touch me. She never touches me; touching is still a country she needs a visa for. But she sat close enough that our shoulders almost met, and she said, "I renounced everything because I thought our blood was the problem. Turns out our blood was the *point*." A pause. "I'm still deciding how I feel about it. You can decide however you need to."

"That's the most sisterly thing you've ever said to me."

"I've been practicing."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about defection: it isn't a moment. It's a series of small surrenders, each one humiliating in its own special way.

The first surrender was admitting the ghosts were right, which took until dawn, because I made them show me the covenant three more times and interrogated the eldest ghost about forgery until even Bramble said, "Blimey, you'd interrogate your own rescue."

The second surrender was opening my ledger—all forty-one names—and reading them aloud to the group by firelight, telling them what I'd done to each, because Odile said, "If you're going to stand with us, stand with your whole weight," and she was right, damn her monastic clarity. August listened to every name without flinching. Lena—Lena, whose name had lived smudged in my pages for six days—reached over when I finished and closed the book herself, gently, like shutting a coffin.

"The leash cut both ways," she said. "Every time you didn't report me, the covenant was holding your hand back. You think those were your mercies? They were ours. Shared custody." Her smile was tired and crooked. "We were both guarding the same door, Sera. We just couldn't see each other doing it."

"That's either beautiful or the most infuriating thing I've ever heard."

"Both. Usually both."

The third surrender was practical and the hardest: I sent word to my second-in-command dissolving my authority, knowing exactly what the High Warden would make of it. There was no graceful exit from the Wardens. There was only the version where you walked out and the version where they carried you. I chose walking.

The fourth surrender I haven't finished yet. It happens every morning: waking up without the leash, reaching for the certainty of duty and finding only my own hand there, empty, asking me what *I* want. I'm told it gets easier. I'm told that by people who talk to ghosts, so calibrate accordingly.

But here is what I gained in exchange, and I record it precisely because I was a ledger-keeper and ledgers should balance:

Bramble Quill, who threatened to drown me forty-eight hours ago, now saves me the crusts off her bread because she noticed I eat them last, and says nothing about it, which from Bramble is a sonnet.

August, who should fear me most, asked me—me, the woman who hunted his kind for a living—to teach him how Wardens track, so he'd know how to evade his own pursuers better. He trusted my expertise before he trusted my heart. There's a wisdom in that I'm still learning from.

Odile laughs now. Small laughs, quickly suppressed, but laughs. Fifteen years of silence between us, and it turns out the password back in was neither apology nor forgiveness but a shared enemy: the lie we'd both been told about our own blood.

And Lena. Lena, who I spent thirty years pretending not to know, walks beside me now in full knowledge, and it turns out that being known is not the wound I feared. Being known is just being *accompanied*. All those years I thought I was protecting her by staying away, and really I was just lonely in an organized fashion.

* * *

We came down off the scar-field at dusk, four heirs and one defector, and the ghosts of the old covenant watched us go. At the edge of the field the eldest ghost spoke once more, and I write her words here because I will need them in the days coming, and because someone should keep them:

"The gate will ask a price," she said. "It always asks. Do not let it ask one of you alone. That was our error—we let the covenant decide for the individual, and the individual paid, and the paying curdled into the Sundering. Break that pattern, Voss. You of all of them know what it costs to obey alone."

I carry that sentence the way I used to carry my badge.

Because this morning, riding hard for Ashfall with the wind carrying smoke-smell from the city—more smoke than cookfires make, more than festival fires, the particular greasy black of severance-engines spinning up—I understood that the pattern was already breaking whether we willed it or not. The High Warden had found the truth too. He'd simply reached the opposite conclusion: if the gate's power was real, then the gate's power was *his*.

The others saw the smoke and started planning defense. I saw it and felt something new settle into the space where the leash used to hang—not duty, which was never mine, but choice, which is. They are going to march an army on Lena's cellar, and I know every drill, every signal, every weak joint in the Warden war-machine, because I helped forge half of them.

"Bramble," I said, turning in the saddle, "how do you feel about teaching a former Warden to fight dirty?"

Her grin was all teeth. "Thought you'd never ask."

Behind us, the scar-field sank back into its long sleep. Ahead, Ashfall burned its warning into the sky. And in my breast pocket, where the ledger used to sit, I carried instead a single page torn from a covenant a century old—my name in my grandmother's blood, the leash transmuted at last into a thread, and threads, I am learning, are for weaving.

The army reaches the walls in two days.

We intend to be standing on them.
