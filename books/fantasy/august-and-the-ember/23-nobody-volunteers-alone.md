## 23. Nobody Volunteers Alone

### Lena

I have always been good at lying to myself. It's a professional skill, really—five years of telling customers that a luck-charm was "just a trinket" while knowing full well it was woven from gate-light and stolen hope. So when I sat down at the kitchen table in the safehouse with a cup of tea going cold beside me and began composing the argument for why it had to be me, I did it with the calm confidence of a woman who believed she was being reasonable.

The ledger agreed with me. That was the thing. The ledger *agreed*.

I'd read the passage eleven times by candlelight while the others slept. *The gate requires memory and love freely given. The covenantor's line runs strongest through the keeper.* Keeper. That was me. Five years of feeding the gate stray drops of my magic, five years of learning its moods like a difficult cat. If anyone's memories were already half its property, it was mine. It was practically accounting logic. Depreciation. Sunk cost. I'd even written it down that way, because if I dressed the sacrifice up in bookkeeping language, maybe it would stop feeling like what it was.

Which was: I would walk into the dark and forget August Dravenmoor's laugh, and the way he said my name like it was a place he'd been looking for, and the fact that I had loved him since a century-old book showed me his face before I ever saw it in person.

I was on my fourth draft of a letter to him—I kept starting over because every opening sounded either too final or not final enough—when I heard the floorboard outside the kitchen creak.

Not Odile's step. She walked like a penance. Not Bramble's; Bramble moved through houses like water through a net, all slip and no sound. And Sera Voss announced herself the way she did everything, with her whole spine.

That left August, who had never once learned to walk quietly in his life, and who was now standing very still in the hallway, holding a piece of paper, wearing the expression of a man caught red-handed by his own reflection.

"What's that?" we both said at the same time.

We looked at each other's papers. His was addressed to me. Mine was addressed to him.

"Oh," he said.

"Don't," I said.

"Lena—"

"*Don't.*" My voice cracked on the second syllable and I hated it. I stood up so fast my chair skidded. "You don't get to do this. You're the ember. The ledger says the gate wants an heir of the old blood, and you're the most—"

"The ledger also says the keeper's line runs deepest." He held up my draft—the one with the depreciation joke—and something in his face was breaking and hardening at the same time, which is a trick I still can't do. "You wrote *sunk cost*. You tried to make forgetting me sound like arithmetic."

"It *is* arithmetic—"

"It's you deciding your love is worth less than mine." He crossed the kitchen in three steps and took my hands, and the paper crumpled between us, both our letters crushed together like they belonged to each other, which was the whole problem. "You told me once that the gate whispers to you. Does it whisper kindly?"

I didn't answer. It whispered kindly. It whispered like a friend. That was exactly why I couldn't trust it.

"That's what I thought," August said softly. "It's been grooming you for years, Lena. Of course it sounds reasonable when it asks you for yourself. That's how it got the last keeper too."

And there it was, the argument I hadn't let myself think, sitting in the open air between us. The last keeper had gone into the gate smiling. I'd found her notes. They were serene. Serene people don't leave serenity behind unless something ate the rest of them.

"I hate you," I said, which meant the opposite, and he knew it, and he kissed my forehead anyway, and neither of us threw away our letters. We just put them in the same drawer. Which should have been a warning to me about how this night was going to go.

### August

Here is what nobody tells you about planning your own death-by-metaphor: the logistics are surprisingly domestic.

I'd spent two days on my letter to Lena. Two days! I'd written it at the table, in bed, standing in the safehouse's narrow yard under a sky the color of unpolished pewter. I'd burned three drafts because they sounded like a man saying goodbye, and I refused to say goodbye. I was saying *see you later*. There's a difference. The difference is the entire point of me.

My reasoning was simple, and I stand by it even now, even after everything: the gate asked for memory and love. I had more love than sense—that was medically verifiable—and my memories of Lena were the best things I owned. Better things than anyone should own. If someone has to pay with treasure, you give the man with the fullest vault. Also, and I admit this is less noble: I could not survive watching her forget me. I'd seen her face when she talked about the last keeper's notes. I knew what forgetting looked like from the outside. I wasn't going to be the one left holding the empty shape of a person.

So yes. I volunteered alone. Quietly. In secret. Like an idiot.

Because here's what else nobody tells you: everyone else in that house was doing exactly the same thing.

I discovered it the way you discover anything in a house full of conspirators—by accident, at two in the morning, going downstairs for water. Bramble was in the kitchen, cross-legged on the counter—which she considered a chair, and had made it one through sheer force of personality—turning something over in her fingers. A coin. No. A sigil-stamped token, gate-brass, the kind the old covenant used for oaths.

"Bramble," I said. "What are you doing?"

"Inventory," she said, far too fast, hiding the token behind her back with the smoothness of a woman who had smuggled contraband past actual Wardens and clearly expected me to be easier.

"At two in the morning."

"Time is a construct." She hopped down, and I noticed—because I was now, post-Lena-kitchen, tragically attuned to the signs—a folded paper sticking out of her vest, sealed with wax she'd definitely stolen from Odile's devotional kit. "What are *you* doing?"

"Getting water."

"You're holding a letter with my name on it."

I looked down. I was. Apparently I'd decided to write letters to everyone, and had gotten as far as hers, which began *Dear Bramble, you absolute menace,* and went downhill emotionally from there.

We stared at each other across the kitchen like two burglars surprised in the same house.

"You're going to offer yourself," she said flatly.

"You're going to offer yourself."

"Don't be stupid, ember-boy, the gate needs *me*, I'm half-fae, my memory works different, I'm basically pre-sacrificed—"

"That's the worst sentence I've ever heard—"

"—whereas you're the prophesied one, it literally says *the ember returns*, you showing up is the whole plot—"

"We are not having this argument in the kitchen at two in the morning—"

And then the lamp flared.

Not broke. *Flared*—light pouring upward like it was being poured from below, and Odile Voss was standing in the doorway in her nightclothes with a candle in her hand and the expression of a nun who has caught two sinners and fully intends to make it three.

"I heard arguing," she said. "I assumed Wardens."

"Worse," Bramble said. "Feelings."

Odile's gaze went from me, to Bramble, to the letter in my hand, to the telltale lump of paper in Bramble's vest, and I watched her arrive at the conclusion with the terrible speed of a woman who had already gotten there herself and was only now realizing she wasn't unique.

"You've written your goodbyes," Odile said quietly. "Both of you."

Silence. The kind with weight.

"So have I," she said.

### Odile

I want it recorded, somewhere, in some ledger that matters, that I held out the longest.

Lena and August had been caught in the kitchen days ago—they thought the rest of us didn't know, but a house this small keeps no secrets, and besides, August writes letters the way other men sweat, visibly and copiously. Bramble lasted until the following night. But I had composed my renunciation-of-the-renunciation in perfect silence, sealed it with my own wax, and hidden it inside the false bottom of my prayer book, where I hide everything I'm ashamed of, which lately is a growing collection.

My argument was the cleanest. I want that understood too. I spent nine years believing magic murdered my parents. I built a whole self out of that belief, stone by stone, vow by vow. And then I learned the Sundering was engineered, that my grief was harvested, that my order's founding story was a lie with incense on it. Very well. Then the woman I built from that lie is a forgery, and a forgery should be returned to the fire that minted it. I am the least of the four of us. I am spare parts. It is simple theology: the greatest gift is laid down by the one who values it least.

I was awake before dawn, kneeling in the yard with my prayer book open, rehearsing how I would tell them—not ask, tell; I had done asking for nine years and it had made me small—when Sera Voss sat down on the step beside me.

She does that. She has begun doing that. Six months ago she would have stood, arms crossed, radiating institutional authority. Now she sits, like a person, and it is still disorienting.

"Couldn't sleep," she said, which was a lie, because Sera sleeps like a soldier, in short efficient installments, and her eyes were clear.

"Sister," I said, which is what I call her when I am annoyed, and also what I call her always, because habits are load-bearing.

"You're up early."

"Prayer."

"Mm." She looked at the prayer book in my hands. At the false bottom, whose seam I realized with dawning horror was visible in the dawn light, because I had opened it and forgotten to press it shut. "Interesting scripture."

"This is private."

"So is this." And she drew from her coat—not armor anymore, just a coat, though she wears it like armor—a single sheet of paper, folded once, and set it on the step between us without handing it to me. My name was on the outside. Her handwriting, square and unhurried.

I did not touch it. Touching it would make it real.

"Sera. What did you do."

"What you're all doing." She said it tiredly, the way you report weather. "The gate needs a Voss. That's the whole prophecy, Odile. Our blood was bound into the covenant before either of us was born—it's why Mother joined the Wardens, why I joined, why they took everything from us. We were leashed into this. Well." Something moved through her face, quick and cold, the ghost of the duel on Belltower Row, the High Warden's blade, the scar she doesn't talk about. "I'm choosing the leash. That's the difference. That's the only dignity left in it."

"You are not dying for me."

"I'm not dying for you. I'm dying for the city. For the realms. For—" and here her voice did the thing it almost never does, the crack, the girl underneath the warden, the twelve-year-old who watched the Accords take their parents' shop and names and future, "—for the version of us that deserved better. You don't get to monopolize sacrifice, little sister. I've been practicing longer."

"You practiced *wrong*!"

I hadn't meant to shout. The yard was grey and quiet and my voice went off across it like a thrown stone. Sera didn't flinch. She never flinches. She just looked at me with those eyes that are our mother's eyes, and said:

"Then teach me right. Before Thursday."

Thursday. The rite. The gate. The price named twice, and then named four times, apparently, because my family has never once been able to share anything except catastrophe.

I picked up her letter. I read it. It was three lines long, because Sera wastes nothing, not even words. The third line was an apology to me, dated as if she expected me to read it after, and I felt something in my chest tear along an old seam and then—strangely—heal along a new one.

"No," I said.

"Odile—"

"*No.*" I stood, and the prayer book fell open on the step, and my own sealed letter slid out into the light between us, and Sera looked at it, and then at me, and laughed—one short, broken, astonished note. "Oh, saints. All of us."

"All of us," she agreed.

### Bramble

Right. Everyone's secrets are out, everyone's crying or shouting or doing that Voss sister thing where they argue like it's a contact sport, and me? I'm on the roof. Where I do my best thinking, and also my best avoiding.

Let me catch you up, since apparently I'm the narrator now. Four heirs. One gate. Price of admission: your memories and your love, forever, non-refundable, no exchanges. And all four of us independently decided—we being allegedly a *found family*, allegedly *in this together*—to sneak off and pay it solo, like a bunch of heroes in separate novels who never got the memo they were in the same book.

Idiots. All of us. Me included. *Especially* me.

Here was my logic, and I'll defend it: I'm half-fae. Half of me belongs to the other side of that door already. When the Sundering cut the realms, it cut *me* in half—ask my mother's locket, ask the ache I've had behind my ribs since I was a kid that no doctor and no priest could name. The gate wouldn't be taking my life. It'd be giving me back my missing piece. For everyone else it's a sacrifice. For me it's practically a homecoming. I had it all worked out. I had the letter sealed and everything. Wax and all. Stolen wax, but still.

Then I came down off the roof at midmorning and walked into the kitchen, and there they all were. Lena. August. Both Voss sisters. Sitting around the table like a tribunal, and on the table, arranged in a neat row, four letters. Mine in the middle. Someone had picked my pocket. I genuinely did not feel a thing. I'm slipping. Or I'm home. One of those.

"Sit down, Bramble," Lena said, in her shopkeeper voice, the one that means the customer is wrong but will be treated with respect until they stop being wrong.

"I'm fine standing."

"Sit. Down."

I sat. August pushed a cup of tea at me. Odile had the grace to look embarrassed about the whole pickpocketing situation, which I noted for later leverage.

"So," I said. "Everyone wants to die alone. Great meeting. Love the agenda. Motion to adjourn—"

"Nobody," Lena said, "is volunteering alone."

And then she turned the ledger around to face us, and I'll give her this: the woman had been up all night doing what she does, which is read the fine print everyone else misses. Because there it was, in the margin of the price-passage, in ink so faint it might have been breath: *the covenant is fourfold; the price, likewise, may be divided.*

Divided. Split. Quartered.

"Not one of us," August said slowly, catching it the same moment I did. "All of us. A piece each."

"A quarter of the cost apiece," Sera said. "A quarter of the memories. A quarter of the—" she stopped, because the word *love* does something to a room when you say it out loud with your sister watching.

"Forget a quarter of your life instead of all of it," Lena said. "Keep three-quarters. Keep enough to know each other after."

"It might work," Odile whispered, and then, louder, with the fierce scandalized joy of a nun discovering a loophole in scripture: "It might actually *work*—"

"Or it might quarter all of us badly," Sera said, because she's contractually obligated to be the pessimist. "Four half-remembering ghosts wandering around Ashfall—"

"Better than one whole ghost and three mourners," I said.

And the table went quiet, because that was the truest thing anyone had said all week, and I don't say true things often, so when I do, people listen.

August reached across the table and put his hand flat on the ledger, over the faint marginal ink. "Together, then. Nobody volunteers alone. Agreed?"

There was a pause—you could hear the whole house thinking—and then Lena put her hand on top of his. Then Odile. Then Sera, last, slowest, like setting down a weapon she'd carried for twenty years.

"Agreed," they said.

I looked at their stacked hands. I looked at my letter on the table, and the three lines of Sera's, and the crumpled drafts of Lena's and August's in the drawer they thought nobody knew about. Found family. Saints help me, it's real, and it's the most terrifying thing I've ever smuggled.

"Yeah, all right," I said, and put my hand on the pile. "But if I forget how to pick locks, I'm haunting every one of you."

Which is when the bell above the safehouse door rang—not the front bell, the *ward*-bell, the one Lena strung with gate-light against exactly one kind of visitor—and Sera was on her feet with her hand on her blade before the sound finished, and through the window, down the lane, we saw them.

Wardens. Dozens. And at their head, on a black horse, carrying something on a cart behind him that hummed like a struck bell and made my teeth ache and the gate-light in the ward-bell gutter and *die*—

The High Warden. And a machine I'd only heard whispered about in canal-side bars, the thing they built to cut magic out of people like a tumor.

The severance-engine. Coming up the road. Toward us.
