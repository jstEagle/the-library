## 24. The Wardens March

I had been a nun for eleven years, and in that time I had learned exactly three things about power: it smells like ink and old stone, it never announces itself honestly, and it always, always arrives wearing the face of someone you trusted.

The High Warden's face was one I'd known since childhood. Corvus Ashgrave had been a fixture at our parents' funeral—tall, grey-eyed, his mourning coat pressed so sharply it could have cut bread. He'd put a hand on Sera's head and mine and said, *The Accords will keep you safe,* in a voice like a promise and a threat sharing a bed.

Eleven years later, he stood in the parlor of the Convent of Saint Verity with snow melting off his shoulders, and I understood that the promise had always been the threat.

"Sister Odile." He did not use my name kindly. He used it like a key he was trying in a lock. "You've been busy."

"I've been praying," I said. "It looks similar from the outside."

Behind him, two wardens in field-grey stood at parade rest, and behind *them*, visible through the frosted window, stood something that made my stomach drop through the floor and into the cellar of the world: a wagon. Not a supply wagon. A severance-engine, its brass ribs wrapped in oilcloth, its silhouette unmistakable even shrouded. I had seen one only in engravings—in the histories we were given, which is to say the histories someone wanted us to have—and even in engraving it had looked like what it was: a machine for cutting people out of themselves.

"May I sit?" Ashgrave asked, already sitting.

"You may not smoke," I said, because it was the only boundary left to me that he might actually honor.

He smiled the way winter smiles at a late harvest. "Your sister has been busy too."

My hands went cold in my lap. I kept them folded anyway. Eleven years of practice: fold the hands, still the face, let the prayer do the work the panic wants to do. "Sera serves the Accord faithfully."

"Sera," said Ashgrave, "has committed treason."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about renouncing magic: you don't actually renounce anything. Magic isn't a coat you hang by the door. It's marrow. What you renounce is the *permission*, and permission can be revoked, restored, or—as I learned over three days of interrogation disguised as hospitality—weaponized.

They didn't torture me. That would have been crude, and Ashgrave was never crude. They simply told me things. In order. With citations.

They told me my sister had defected. They showed me the warrant, her name in black ink, her face in a daguerreotype taken without her knowledge—she looked tired in it, and angry, and more alive than she'd looked in any family portrait I remembered.

They told me about Lena Pemberton and the charm stall, about the gate beneath the shop, about the ledger. They told me about August—the ember, the prophecy, the boy whose grandmother had died whispering his name into history like a seed into soil.

And then they told me the part they thought would break me. They told me the Sundering was engineered. They showed me vault records, recovered from Belltower Row after Pemberton's little burglary, and they laid them out on Mother Abbess's good table like a tarot spread, and they said:

*The old covenantor did this out of fear. Fear of love between realms. And the Voss family was bound into the covenant from the beginning—your bloodline, Sister. Your duty was a leash.*

They expected me to weep. Or rage. Or collapse into their arms so they could aim me like an arrow at my sister's heart.

Instead I laughed. It came out wrong—a cracked, ugly sound, nothing like the measured serenity I'd cultivated for a decade—but it came out honest, and honesty was so unfamiliar in my mouth that it startled us all.

"You knew," I said. "You've known for years. You sat across from me at review boards. You signed off on my renunciation papers. You watched me bury myself alive in this place, and you knew my whole life was built on a lie your office maintained."

Ashgrave's composure flickered—one degree, no more. "The truth is dangerous."

"The truth is *inconvenient*," I said. "There's a difference, and the difference is that dangerous things you fight, and inconvenient things you bury. You buried my parents' deaths in it. You buried Sera's childhood in it. You buried—" My voice caught on the edge of something I hadn't planned to say. "You buried *me* in it."

"Sister Odile." He leaned forward, and here it came, the real offer, the one the warrant and the records were merely packaging for. "The gate must be sealed permanently. The severance-engine can burn the threshold closed forever—no covenant, no heirs, no price. No sacrifice. No one has to give up their memories. No one has to die for a door. Isn't that what you want? Peace? An end to all of it?"

And God forgive me, for one full breath, I wanted it. I wanted it the way a drowning woman wants air—with her whole body, past reason, past everything. An end to the war before it reached another city. An end to the choosing. An end to the terrible arithmetic where love was currency and everyone I cared about kept volunteering to pay.

One breath. Then I looked at the shrouded engine through the window, and I asked the question that saved me, though I didn't know it yet:

"And what does the engine take with it? When it burns the threshold?"

Ashgrave paused a half-second too long. "Residue."

"Residue," I repeated. "Meaning the memories it severs don't return to the people they belong to. Meaning it doesn't close a door—it amputates. Everyone touched by the gate, everyone who ever loved across the veil, everyone carrying even a drop of the old blood—they lose whatever the flame touches. Permanently. Irretrievably." I stood. My legs held, which surprised me. "You're not offering peace, High Warden. You're offering a lobotomy for the world, administered by the state."

"I'm offering *certainty*."

"You're offering yourself as the hand on the lever," I said, "forever. Because someone has to decide who counts as residue. And I have read enough history—real history, now—to know how men like you make those decisions. First the fae-blooded. Then the charmers. Then anyone who's ever hummed a lullaby in the old tongue." I walked to the window, to the shrouded machine, and put my palm against the cold glass between us. "My mother sang in the old tongue. Would she have counted as residue?"

He didn't answer. That was answer enough.

* * *

They locked me in my cell that night—not as punishment, officially; as *precaution pending review*. The door had a viewing slot, and through it I watched the convent courtyard fill with grey coats and lantern light, the army assembling for a march it had been planning, I realized, long before Ashgrave ever knocked on my door. I was not a recruit they'd come to fetch. I was a loose end they'd come to tie off, and if I converted conveniently to their cause, so much the tidier.

I sat on the narrow bed in the room where I had slept for eleven years, and I took inventory of my life the way you inventory a house before a fire.

I found my faith. To my surprise, it survived the search. Not the institutional kind—the kind that had gotten me through the nights after my parents died, when Sera was being forged into a weapon and I was being filed away into silence. The raw kind. The kind that says: *there is something in the dark larger than your fear, and it is not your enemy.* I had mistaken the Church for that thing for a decade. The Church, it turned out, was just a building where frightened people huddled together and called the huddling holiness. But the thing itself—the thing in the dark—I still believed in it. I believed it had been waiting under a charm stall in Ashfall City all along, patient as roots.

I found my anger. It was enormous. It had been accumulating interest for eleven years, compounding quietly beneath the vespers and the scrubbing and the small obedient days, and now it came due all at once, and I nearly drowned in it. I let myself drown for exactly as long as it took to understand the shape of it. Then I stood up inside it and made it useful.

Because here is what the anger showed me, when I finally stopped flailing: Ashgrave had made a mistake. He had shown me the records. He had *cited his sources*. A man certain of victory does not show his cards to a hostage—he shows them to a convert, and he had misjudged which one I was. Which meant somewhere in this convent, in his field desk, in the saddlebags of his officers, there existed copies of the proof. Proof that the Sundering was murder. Proof that the Voss bloodline was bound into the covenant. Proof that every execution, every confiscation, every family torn apart by the Accords for five generations had been justified by a lie that the men at the top had known was a lie.

You cannot march an army on a lie once the lie is legible. Armies are made of mothers' sons, and mothers' sons have consciences, and consciences are slow but they are not stupid.

I needed that proof. And I needed it in the hands of people who could read it aloud where it mattered.

Which meant I needed to get out of a locked cell in a convent full of soldiers, cross a province under martial law, and reach four fugitives who had every reason to believe I hated them.

I lay back on the bed and almost laughed again. Almost prayed. Did both, honestly; at some point in the last week the two had stopped being different activities.

*Sera,* I thought, at the ceiling. *You absolute mule. You couldn't have written to me like a normal person? You had to get yourself declared a traitor first?*

But even in the thought, I knew why she hadn't. Writing would have endangered me. Silence was her way of loving—badly, at distance, with her back turned so I wouldn't see her face do anything soft. We were our parents' daughters to the bone: Mother's stubbornness, Father's terrible habit of sacrificing himself in advance of anyone asking him to.

Well. Tonight the inheritance ran the other direction.

* * *

The novices changed the watch at midnight. This I knew because I had been the novice who changed the watch, for six years, and because Sister Maren—who was ninety-one and blind as a mole and sharp as a tack rolled in glass—had taught me that the true schedule of any institution is never the posted one. The posted schedule is for superiors. The real schedule lives in the bodies of the people doing the work.

Maren came down the corridor at half past midnight with her cane tapping her private rhythm—two taps, pause, one—which meant *trouble, wait, act*. She had been bringing me bread I didn't need and messages I very much did, ever since the wardens arrived, because Maren had been at Saint Verity longer than the Accords had existed, and she remembered when the convent had been something other than a place where women were stored.

She stopped at my slot. Her clouded eyes found me with unnerving accuracy.

"They march at dawn," she said. "The whole column. South road."

"I know."

"The machine goes in the middle. Guarded heaviest there." She paused. "You're going to do something stupid."

"I'm going to do something necessary. There's overlap."

"Mm." From the folds of her habit she produced, impossibly, a ring of keys, and held it up to the slot like a relic. "Third one sticks. Lift, then turn. And Odile—" Her voice dropped, and for a moment the dry wit fell away and I saw the girl she had been, a hundred years ago, in a world with open doors between the realms. "I remember the gate. Before the Sundering. I was small, but I remember the singing. Whatever they've told you it was—whatever you've feared it was—it was *song*, child. It was the two worlds singing to each other through a wall they built together. Don't let them convince you the wall was mercy."

I gripped her fingers through the slot. Hers were paper and iron both.

"Come with me," I said.

"At my age?" She snorted. "I'm the decoy, dear. Someone has to be found missing from the infirmary at dawn so they spend an hour searching the herb gardens instead of the south road." She was already moving, cane tapping. "Lift, then turn. Go left at the laundry. And Odile—"

"Yes?"

"Tell your sister the Voss women were always the wall. Tell her it's time we decided what we're holding up."

Then the taps faded down the corridor, unhurried, a blind old woman walking toward a morning she had no intention of witnessing, and I lifted the third key until the lock sighed, and I turned it.

* * *

Getting off the grounds was the easy part; grief makes excellent camouflage, and a sister in a travel cloak walking fast with her head down reads as *summoned to a deathbed*, not *deserter*. The hard part was the south road itself, and the column.

I didn't follow the column. I paralleled it—through the pine breaks east of the road, where the snow lay deep enough to swallow footsteps and the trees swallowed lamplight. For six hours I moved the way I'd learned to move in the convent's orchard winters: deliberate, rhythmic, prayer-paced. My body remembered things my mind had tried to surrender. That, I was learning, was the whole theme of my life.

From the ridgeline above Hollow Ford I stopped and looked down at them, because I needed to see it with my own eyes—needed the truth of it in my body, where decisions are actually made.

Two hundred wardens on the march. Supply carts. Cavalry outriders kicking up rooster-tails of powder-snow. And in the center, on a wagon drawn by eight grey horses, the severance-engine with its oilcloth stripped away at last, brass ribs naked to the dawn, the furnace at its heart already lit—a dull red glow like an eye opening after a long sleep. Even at this distance I felt it. A wrongness in the teeth. A silence with weight, pressing outward, the way a held note presses against a closed door.

It was eating the sound of the valley. Birds had gone quiet for a quarter-mile around it. The horses pulling it had their ears pinned flat.

*Ticking clock made flesh*, I thought, and the phrase arrived in my head in August's voice somehow, though I'd only met him once, briefly, in a chapel doorway with blood on both of us. Everything about that man arrived in your head uninvited and then refused to leave. I was beginning to suspect that was the point of him.

Dawn broke grey-gold over the column, and the horns sounded, and two hundred voices answered, and the army of the Accord marched south toward Ashfall City toward a charm shop and a cellar and a door, carrying in its belly a machine designed to end not a war but the possibility of reconciliation, forever, everywhere, amen.

And I stood on the ridge with frost in my lashes and my sister's name in my mouth and a blind woman's keys still warm in my pocket, and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the parlor.

The column would reach Ashfall in three days, maybe four if the weather turned. Four heirs. One gate. One rite that required a price no one should pay alone—and an army arriving to make sure that whatever price got paid, it got paid *their* way.

Three days to reach the city ahead of them. Three days to find four people who believed I was their enemy and convince them I was something worse: their ally, with all my father's talent for self-destruction finally pointed in a useful direction.

I pulled my hood up, found the road's shoulder where the pines grew thinnest, and started running.

Somewhere behind me, the engine's furnace breathed, and the valley went quiet, and I prayed the only prayer left worth saying:

*Let me be in time. Let me be in time. Let me—*

The horn sounded again, closer than it should have been, and I realized with a lurch that the column had outriders on the ridge line too—

—and one of them had just turned his horse toward me.
