## 25. Letters Not Meant to Be Read

There is a particular kind of cowardice that looks, from the outside, like organization.

I discovered this at two in the morning, sitting at the kitchen table in the safehouse with four sheets of paper, a pen that had run dry twice, and the smug certainty of a man who has found a loophole in a tragedy. The ledger said one heir must give their memories and their love to the gate forever. It did not say which heir. It did not say the choice had to be democratic. And it most certainly did not say I couldn't simply volunteer before anyone else could beat me to it.

I'd been rehearsing arguments all week — in the bath, on watch, in the margins of my dreams. *I'm the newest here,* I'd say. *You three have history. Found family takes years to build and I've only got months of equity.* Or: *My magic is the ember-magic; logically the gate wants mine.* Or, if those failed: *I read the terms more carefully than any of you and I'm invoking a clause.*

None of them would work, of course. I knew that even as I sharpened them. Because Lena had already stood in front of me once and said, with the terrifying calm of someone who has done math you haven't, "Nobody volunteers alone." She'd said it to all of us, standing in the doorway of the safehouse with snow melting in her hair, and we'd all nodded like chastened children, and then we had all gone off to secretly plan our individual betrayals anyway, because love makes liars of us all and none of us are as clever as we think.

So: letters. If I couldn't argue them out of grief, I could at least leave them something better than grief. Instructions. Jokes. A map of where I hid the good tea.

The first letter was the easy one, which is how I knew it was a trap.

*Bramble —*

*By the time you read this, I'll be a very exclusive kind of ghost. Don't take it personally; I'm told the gate has excellent taste and I've always been flattered too easily.*

*I owe you an apology for the canal market. You were mid-escape, beautifully, professionally mid-escape, and I turned a Warden's net into dandelion fluff and ruined everything. I know you said you forgave me. I also know you keep a ledger of debts, written in what I sincerely hope is ink, and that you crossed mine out with a flourish you thought I didn't see. So consider this the interest payment: the tea cache is behind the loose brick in the cellar, third row, left side. The good stuff. The stuff you called "insultingly drinkable" and then finished.*

*Take care of Odile. Take care of Sera, though she'd hex you for saying so. Take care of Lena — no. Scratch that. Nobody has to take care of Lena. Just make sure somebody laughs with her, loudly and often, preferably at things that aren't funny, because she does this thing where she snorts and then pretends she didn't—*

The pen stopped.

Here is the problem with goodbye letters nobody warns you about: they are practice funerals. Every sentence you write is a small rehearsal of your own absence, and you find out very quickly which details of your life are load-bearing. Apparently mine was Lena's laugh. Not the prophecy. Not the gate. Not the ember burning a hole through my ribs like a coal someone swallowed whole. A snort.

I wrote *sorry about the mess* under it, crossed it out, and moved on.

* * *

Sera's letter took three drafts, and I burned two of them in the stove, which felt appropriately dramatic for a former Warden who once arrested me on suspicion of "unlicensed warmth."

Draft one had been too formal. *Dear Warden Voss.* She'd have thrown it back through whatever veil separates the living from the recently sacrificed, just on principle.

Draft two had been too honest. It had contained the sentence *I know what it cost you to stand against your order, and I know you didn't do it for me,* and I'd sat there staring at it until the candle guttered, because underneath that sentence was another one I wasn't ready to write: *and if you hadn't defected, I'd have died a stranger to you, and somehow that mattered more than dying.*

Draft three went like this:

*Sera —*

*You're going to be angry. Good. Anger is your best armor and you'll need it, because after me there will be a hole shaped exactly like a man who made terrible jokes during tactical briefings, and holes are worse than graves.*

*I want you to know the duel on Belltower Row bought us everything. I watched from the cellar door. You fought like someone who had finally stopped asking permission. Whatever the order took from you — your family, your name, twenty years of your life wearing their colors — you took it back up those stairs, one strike at a time. That's not redemption. Redemption implies you owed a debt. You didn't. They did.*

*One more thing. When the gate takes my memories, some part of me will still remember you, somewhere below the forgetting, the way a river remembers rain. Be insufferable about it. Visit the shop. Insult Bramble's cooking. Teach Odile to spar properly, she telegraphs her left. Live loudly enough that whatever's left of me hears you.*

*Thank you for choosing us. I know it broke you to do it. I know broken things can be rebuilt into stranger, better shapes.*

*— August*

*P.S. Your sister says you snore. She says it with tremendous affection, but she says it.*

That one I kept. My hands were shaking by the end of it, but I kept it, and folded it, and wrote SERA on the outside in block letters so she couldn't pretend it was a grocery list.

Odile's letter came easier than I expected, which frightened me, because nothing about Odile should ever come easy.

*Odile —*

*You told me once, on the night train, that faith is just love that learned to kneel. I've thought about that every day since. I think you were wrong, actually — I think faith is love that learned to stand back up. You knelt when the world told you magic killed your parents. Standing back up looked like renouncing everything, and then it looked like walking into a cellar with a woman you'd sworn to hate, and then it looked like holding Bramble's hand while the healers worked, praying to a god you weren't sure was listening. That's not kneeling. That's the bravest standing I've ever seen.*

*Don't let them make the Wardens into hunters again. You and Sera are building something that matters — guardians, not gaolers. Guard the gate. Guard each other. Guard the memory of a man who was mostly jokes and occasionally fire, and who loved every single one of you with the specific, helpless intensity of someone who grew up with no family at all and then, at the worst possible moment, acquired one.*

*That's the joke, isn't it. I waited thirty years to belong somewhere and then had to leave to pay for it. If you find that funny, laugh. If you find it sad, laugh harder. It's what I'd have done.*

*— August*

Four letters. Four people. I laid them out on the table in a row and looked at them, and the safehouse was so quiet I could hear the snow settling on the roof, and somewhere beneath the floorboards — beneath the cellar, beneath the cellar's cellar — the gate hummed its low, patient note, like a tuning fork waiting for the orchestra.

And then there was the fifth sheet of paper. The one I'd been avoiding since midnight. The one with LENA already written at the top in handwriting steadier than I felt, because apparently my hand understood before I did that anything less than perfect would be unworthy of her.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about loving someone while planning to forget them: it turns every ordinary moment contraband.

Tuesday, Lena fell asleep in the armchair with the ledger open on her lap, and I stood there for forty minutes doing absolutely nothing, guarding her sleep like it was a country I'd been granted citizenship in. Wednesday she caught me staring and said, "What?" and I said, "Nothing," and she said, "Liar," and I said, "Memorizing," and she went pink to the ears and didn't ask me to stop. Thursday she taught me a charm for keeping bread warm and her fingers overlapped mine on the sigil and I thought, *this, this exact pressure, five points of contact, I want to keep this,* knowing full well the gate doesn't do requests.

So the letter to her couldn't be instructions or jokes or maps to tea. It had to be everything, and everything doesn't fit on paper, and I am not a novelist. I'm a man who sells charms and accidentally fulfills century-old prophecies and is, at present, crying onto a piece of stationery in a safehouse kitchen.

*Lena —*

*If you're reading this, then I won, which means I also lost, which is the whole shape of this thing, isn't it?*

*I need you to hear some things that I won't be able to say anymore, and I need you to hear them from paper instead of from the silence where I used to be, because silence lies and paper at least tells you the truth once and then lets go.*

*I knew your face before I met you. Not from the ledger — later, yes, but the first time, in the market, when you sold me that luck-charm and it hummed — I swear to you, on whatever's left of my honor, that some part of me recognized you. Like a song heard through a wall. I followed the sound. I'd follow it again through a hundred Sunderings.*

*You are the best thing the prophecy ever did, and it never meant to. It meant to use us both. We refused. Remember that. Whatever the gate takes, it cannot take the fact that we refused.*

*Don't you dare carry this like a wound. Carry it like a coin in your pocket — something you touch sometimes, that reminds you the world once weighed something precious in its palm and gave it to you, even briefly, even—*

The scratch of the pen faltered. Outside, the wind worried the shutters. Down in the deep places, the gate hummed.

And that was when the floorboard creaked.

I knew her step the way I knew my own heartbeat — slightly ahead of it, always, a half-second of warning that had become the truest clock I owned. There was nowhere to put the letter. Under my thigh, absurd. Into the stove, unforgivable. So I did the only thing my panicking hands could manage: I slid it under the other three, face down, and picked up Bramble's letter and pretended to reread it with the absorbed intensity of a man studying scripture.

Lena stood in the doorway with two cups of tea and the expression of someone who had been awake for longer than she admitted and had heard more than she let on.

"Couldn't sleep either?" she said.

"Writing," I said. "Letters. To — the grocer. About an account dispute."

"Mm." She set the cups down. Steam rose between us like a small, doomed ghost. Then she reached across the table, past Bramble's letter, past Sera's, past Odile's, and drew out the fifth sheet with two fingers, unhurried, the way you lift a card you already know.

"Lena—"

"*Carry it like a coin in your pocket,*" she read aloud, softly, and her voice did something I had never heard it do — it cracked in the middle, a clean break, like ice on a pond in early spring. She kept reading. I let her, because taking it from her would have been the cruelest thing I've ever done, and I was already planning the second-cruellest.

She read to the end of what existed. Which was not the end. The last line on the page was *even briefly, even—* and then nothing, and the nothing sat there between us, enormous.

"You were going to finish it," she said. Not a question.

"I was going to finish it," I agreed. "Tomorrow. Tonight. Some night when the words cooperated."

"And then?"

"You know what 'and then.'"

"I want to hear you say it."

The gate hummed beneath us. The tea cooled. Somewhere upstairs, Bramble muttered in her sleep, dreaming, probably, of lockets and ledgers and debts unpaid.

"I was going to finish it," I said, "and seal it, and go down to the gate alone, and pay the price myself, and spare all of you. Especially you." I made myself look at her. "That was the plan. It was a bad plan. I've had better."

For a long moment Lena said nothing at all. Then she set the letter down, smoothed it flat with one hand — gently, the way she handled the oldest charms in the shop, the ones that might still wake — and looked at me with eyes that were wet and furious and more loving than anything I had ever seen directed at a living person.

"August," she said, "you absolute idiot. You beautiful, self-sacrificing, *unbearable* idiot. Did you really think—" Her breath caught. She pressed the heel of her hand against her sternum, right over the place where, these days, we both sometimes felt the gate's hum answer our heartbeats. "Did you think I wouldn't find it? I found your fake name in a Warden registry in six hours. I found a smuggler in a city of forty thousand by following rumors about *cheese*. You wrote me a goodbye letter and left it on the kitchen table."

"In my defense—"

"You don't get a defense." She was crying now, quietly, furiously, wiping her eyes with the back of her wrist like the tears were an administrative error. "You don't get a defense and you don't get to go alone and you don't get to decide what I carry. *Even briefly, even—* Even *what*, August? Finish the sentence. Right now. Out loud. Since you clearly can't be trusted with paper."

And I opened my mouth to give her the ending — the real one, the one I'd been carrying around for days like a coal in cupped hands — and that was when the ledger screamed.

Not hummed. Not whispered. *Screamed* — a sound like a page tearing lengthwise through the whole house, through the floorboards, up out of the cellar, and every lamp in the safehouse guttered blue, and above us Bramble's muttering cut off into a shout, and Odile's feet hit the floor overhead with a thud like a dropped anvil.

Lena grabbed my wrist. The letter lay forgotten between us, five words short of a confession.

"That's not the gate settling," she breathed.

No. It wasn't. I knew that hum the way I knew hers now, patient and low. This was different — this was the sound of something on the far side of the door waking up, and pressing its weight against the wood, and finding, for the first time in a hundred years, that the wood was starting to give.

From the top of the stairs, white-faced, clutching the ledger against her chest, Bramble said:

"It's writing again. It's writing *now*. And August — it's writing about *tonight*."
