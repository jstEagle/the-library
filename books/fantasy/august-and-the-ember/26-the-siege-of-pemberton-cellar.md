## 26. The Siege of Pemberton Cellar

### LENA

The bells started at dawn, and they were wrong.

Ashfall's bells had a grammar. I'd lived under them five years—market bell, curfew bell, the two-note warning when a Warden patrol turned down your street. This was none of those. This was every bell in the city ringing at once, no pattern, no mercy, just noise piled on noise until it stopped being sound and became weather.

I stood in my cellar with my hand flat against the gate and felt it answer.

Not fear. The gate didn't do fear; I'd learned that much in twenty-six years of keeping it. What it did was *lean*—a slow pressure against my palm like a dog hearing its name, like something vast turning over in its sleep to look at the door.

"They're coming," I said, because saying things out loud made them real, and real things could be fought. "You know that, don't you."

The gate hummed. Somewhere above me, August was moving furniture.

That sounds absurd. It wasn't. We were barricading a charm stall with display shelves and a counter I'd sanded smooth myself, wedging them against a door that an army was going to come through anyway, and August was doing it with the particular cheerfulness of a man who has decided that if he stops moving he will think about what's coming, and so he will not stop moving. He'd been like that since the letters. Since I found his half-finished goodbye folded into my ledger where he thought I'd never look.

*Dear Lena. By the time you read this—*

He hadn't gotten past *by the time you read this.* Neither of us had mentioned it. Some things you carry by not looking at them directly, like the sun.

Bramble came down the stairs three at a time, which for Bramble meant she was scared and hiding it behind speed. She had her mother's locket on a cord around her neck and two knives per boot and a third knife somewhere I've never found and have decided not to ask about.

"Canal district's gone," she reported. "Wardens pulled out of everything east of Belltower Row. They're all coming here." She paused on the last step. "The High Warden brought the engine. Saw it on the barges. Big as a chapel, humming like your gate's evil cousin."

"The severance-engine," Odile said from the doorway. She'd spent the night praying, or arguing with God, or whatever Odile did instead of sleeping, and it showed in the gray under her eyes. "It doesn't kill. It unmakes. Whatever it touches stops having ever been able to be magic." Her voice caught on the theology of it. "It would burn the gate out of the world and leave a hole shaped like a door."

"Then we don't let it touch the gate," August said, coming down with a shelf under each arm like they weighed nothing, which, given what he was, they might have. He set them down and looked at me, and for one second the cheerfulness fell off him like a coat, and underneath it was just a man who loved me and was terrified, and I loved him so much it was physically inconvenient.

"So we hold the street," I said. "Stair by stair if we have to. Until Sera does whatever Sera's doing."

Nobody asked what Sera was doing. Nobody knew. She'd gone out before dawn with her old Warden's blade and a face like a closed book, and Bramble had watched her go and said, quietly, "She's not planning to come back the same," and none of us had argued.

The first explosion took out the market square clock tower at half past seven.

I felt it through the floor. The gate felt it too—it flared, gold light bleeding through the seams of the stone, and the whispering that lived in it rose from a murmur to something almost like singing.

August put his hand over mine on the stone. "Together," he said. "Whatever happens up there—together."

"Together," I said, and went up to defend a charm stall with everything I had.

* * *

### AUGUST

Here is what nobody tells you about magic: it doesn't feel like power. It feels like honesty.

Every ward I threw that morning was just me refusing to lie. The street outside Pemberton Cellar is narrow, cobbled, lined with shops that sold hats and secondhand books and, in one memorable case, taxidermied cats, and when the first rank of Wardens came up it in formation, shields locked, severance-lanterns swinging, I stood on the roof of the charm stall and told the truth at them: *you cannot pass.*

And the street agreed with me. That's the part that still gives me vertigo. Cobbles heaved themselves into a wall. Rain gutters unwound and became ropes of silver water. A hat shop's entire inventory rose into the air and formed itself into a drifting, ridiculous cloud of felt and feathers that confused the front line absolutely.

Lena fought differently. Lena had spent five years making small charms for small coin, and every one of those charms had been a promise kept, and promises accumulate. She reached into her sleeve and the whole five years came out at once—a hundred little protections she'd woven for customers who never knew what they carried, all of them answering her call like dogs whistled home. A widow's luck-charm turned aside a crossbow bolt meant for Odile. A child's nightmare-ward wrapped a Warden's lantern-light and smothered it.

"You built an army," I shouted to her across the rooftops, "out of *customer service*—"

"I build *quality products*—" she shouted back, and even mid-battle, mid-siege, mid-everything, she grinned at me, and I thought: this. Whatever the gate takes, whatever it costs, I have had this. No price balances against this.

Then the engine fired for the first time.

I want to describe it properly, because it deserves description. It didn't make a sound so much as it made an absence—a hole in the morning where sound used to be—and where its beam touched, the world forgot how to be more than it was. The taxidermy cat shop simply became a shop that had always been empty. The rain gutter-ropes fell and were only water. And a stretch of cobbled wall I'd raised crumbled—not broke, *crumbled*, the way a thing does when it remembers it was never alive.

Odile screamed. Not in fear. In grief. It was her faith's darkest instrument, wielded by the church she'd given her life to, unmaking the world's soft places, and she stood in the smoke with her arms out and her voice cracking and said, "Stop. *Stop.* You're cutting the world."

They didn't stop. Armies rarely do, once someone gives them permission not to.

We fell back to the shop door. Then to the stairs. Street by street, like the plan said, except plans don't account for the way Bramble laughed while she fought, wild and bright, or the way Lena's hand found mine in the dark between wards, or the way the gate below us sang louder with every step we gave up—as if it weren't afraid at all, as if it were *hungry* for us to come down to it.

I held that thought away from myself carefully, the way you hold a hot pan. Later. Think about later later.

We held the landing outside the cellar door for eleven minutes. I counted. Counting keeps you honest.

At minute twelve, the ceiling came down.

* * *

### BRAMBLE

Right. So. Ceiling. Falling.

Let me tell you how it actually happened, because history's going to get it wrong—history always gets smugglers wrong, we're either villains or comic relief and usually both.

The High Warden's vanguard breached the landing with a ram-ward that cracked the lintel like an egg. Standard breach doctrine: shield wall first, lantern-bearers second, the engine's focusing crew behind them with that horrible little brass eye on a pole that tells the beam where to look. I've fought Warden formations out of canal boats and warehouse windows and once, memorably, a wedding. I know their choreography.

So when the lintel cracked, I wasn't looking at the shield wall. I was looking at the brass eye, doing arithmetic. Beam angle, Odile's position—she'd planted herself dead center of the landing, arms spread, *refusing* to yield ground to her own church, which was the most Odile thing I'd ever seen and also the most dangerous—and the math came out bad. The math came out *very* bad. The eye swiveled toward her and the lantern-bearers knelt to focus and there was maybe a breath and a half before the world forgot her.

A breath and a half is enough, if you've spent your whole life being fast for money.

I crossed the landing in one jump off the banister—I'd like it noted that the banister jump was *textbook*—hit Odile at the waist, and took us both over the top of the stairs just as the beam swept through where we'd been standing.

Mostly.

See, here's the thing about breath-and-a-half jumps. Your body makes it. Your trailing arm, apparently, has opinions.

I didn't feel it at first. That's the honest truth, and I'm nothing if not honest when it's inconvenient. We landed in a heap at the bottom of the stairs, in the cellar, in the gate's gold light, and Odile was saying my name and I was saying something clever—I want to believe it was clever—and then I looked at my left side below the elbow and understood, in a distant academic way, that the beam had clipped me, and that where my forearm used to be there was now a smooth absence that didn't bleed because the wound hadn't just cut me, it had *edited* me. The magic in that arm—the fae blood, the quickness, the whole inheritance of my mother's people—wasn't wounded. It was *gone*. Unwritten. The skin ended clean as a page torn out.

Huh, I thought. So that's what it does.

Then it hurt. Oh, it hurt. Not the arm—the *missing* of the arm, a pain with a shape but no location, grief wearing a nerve's clothing. I heard someone screaming and was annoyed at them for a full three seconds before I worked out it was me.

Odile's hands were on me. Her hands, which had renounced magic, which had spent fifteen years building walls around whatever gift she had, and those walls came down all at once because I was dying and her faith didn't survive contact with that fact. Light poured out of her palms—warm, gold-white, nothing like the engine's cold erasure—and where it touched the edit, the tearing *hurt worse*, because you can't heal a page back in without reading what was lost.

"Stay," she was saying. "Bramble. Stay with me. Stay—"

"Wasn't," I managed, "going anywhere. Got a locket. Got a debt. Got—" the laugh came out wet and cracked, "—got a *family* now, nun, you don't get rid of me that cheap."

Her light sank into the wound and the bleeding stopped and the pain went from screaming to shouting to a dull argument in the background, and then August was there, and his hands joined hers, and his magic—his enormous, terrifying, honest magic—wrapped around the missing piece of me like a bandage made of *yes*.

"It can't regrow what the engine erased," he said, grim, sweat pouring. "But I can hold the edge. Keep it from spreading. Bramble—hey—look at me. You're going to keep the arm you have. That's a promise."

"Promises accumulate," Lena said, kneeling beside us, pressing her charm-scarred hands over ours. "I heard that somewhere. From a very good saleswoman."

Above us the landing collapsed inward, and the shield wall came through the smoke, and there was no more time for any of it.

* * *

### ODILE

I held Bramble Quill's life in my hands and understood, finally, what my sister had been trying to tell me for fifteen years.

Duty is not a wall. I had built it as a wall—vows like mortar, prayer like brick, and behind them the child I'd been, whose parents died in the Sundering's aftermath, whose sister chose the sword, whose own small gift frightened her so badly she buried it under a habit and a rule and called the burial holiness. I believed the wall was love. I believed the wall was *safety*.

But duty is a door. It only means anything if it opens, and you walk through it, toward the thing that frightens you, because someone is on the other side and they need you.

Bramble needed me. This sharp-tongued, knife-riddled, infuriating smuggler, who had taken a severance-beam meant for a woman she'd known three weeks, needed me, and my vows said *renounce* and my God—who I am now certain was never on the wall's side of it at all—said *open the door*.

So I opened it.

The light hurt coming out, the way a scream hurts, the way birth hurts. Fifteen years of refusal burning off like fog. And in the middle of it, holding the ragged edge of her wound shut while August anchored it and Lena fed us both strength through the gate's rising song, I felt my sister arrive.

Sera came down the cellar stairs like winter coming down a hill.

She was bleeding. Her Warden's blade was notched. Behind her, muffled by stone and distance, the fighting at the top of the house changed key—from assault to alarm—and I knew, the way you know family, that she had done something at the street door that would cost her everything, and had done it anyway.

"Sera—" I started.

"Don't." She knelt beside Bramble, checked the tourniquet Lena had rigged with the brisk competence of someone who'd bandaged recruits for a decade, and her hands shook only a little. "Don't thank me yet. The High Warden's coming down himself. He wants the gate, and he wants the four of you, and I have bought us minutes, not hours." She looked up at me, and my sister's eyes—so like our mother's—were wet and furious and free. "Odile. Your magic. It's beautiful. I always knew it was."

I couldn't speak. I put my forehead against hers, blood and ash between us, and forgave her for the years, and asked her silently to forgive me, and felt her say yes.

Lena stood. She had the ledger open on her knee, and the gate behind her was blazing now, singing in that dead language that had started sounding less dead every hour, and her face when she turned to us was terrible and calm.

"The rite needs the threshold," she said. "All the way down. August and I go in. The rest of you hold the door." Her eyes found mine. "Can you hold the door?"

I looked at my hands, still glowing. At Bramble, pale and grinning up at me with one arm and all her insolence intact. At Sera, rising, drawing her notched blade, turning to face the stairs.

"We're Vosses," I said, standing beside my sister. "We hold doors."

From far above, through the broken house, through the smoke of Ashfall, came footsteps. Slow. Deliberate. Accompanied by the hum of the engine, descending.

Sera rolled her shoulders, spat blood on the cellar floor, and smiled the smile I'd seen her wear exactly twice in our lives—once before our parents' funeral, and once now.

"Good," she said. "I was hoping it'd be personal."
