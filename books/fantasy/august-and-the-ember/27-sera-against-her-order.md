## 27. Sera Against Her Order

The severance-engine sang as it came up Belltower Row, and I hated that it was beautiful.

That was the thing nobody told you about the Order's worst instruments: they were always beautiful. The engine was a cathedral on wheels, all brass ribs and silver veins, its heart a suspended shard of null-glass that drank the light around it and gave back nothing. Where it passed, the streetlamps guttered. The pigeons fell silent. A flower-seller's marigolds wilted in their buckets like held breath finally let go.

High Warden Corvane rode at the head of his column on a white courser that had no business being that calm amid three hundred armed men and women. He wore the full ceremonial plate, the one with the sunburst pauldrons, polished to a shine so perfect it looked like vanity made solid. Behind him marched the Fourth, Seventh, and Ninth Companies—the pride of Ashfall's Wardens, my brothers and sisters in gray, carrying pikes and lanterns and the particular righteousness of people who have never once been asked to doubt.

I stood on the roof of the bell tower with my sword drawn and my old life burning down below me, and I thought: *I used to be one of them. I used to be the best of them.*

Corvane had given me a choice that morning, in his office that smelled of ink and lye soap. He'd laid out the reports—my duel with the fugitives at the safehouse, my "unexplained" failure to fire the binding bolt, the testimony of two junior wardens who'd seen me let Pemberton's girl walk. His voice had been gentle. That was the worst part. Corvane was gentle the way a surgeon is gentle, right up until the cutting.

"You're tired, Voss," he'd said. "Grief does that. Grief makes us mistake sentiment for duty." He'd slid a vial across the desk. Null-tincture. It would have burned the magic out of my blood and left me clean, obedient, hollow. "Drink this, stand with me at the gate tonight, and we'll say the rest was exhaustion."

And I had looked at the vial, and I had thought about my mother's hands, and Odile's face when she'd slammed that door, and Lena Pemberton at eleven years old, teaching me to skip stones on the canal and swearing she could make them *skip twice* if I promised not to tell—and I had said, "No, sir."

He hadn't even been angry. He'd just nodded, sad as a funeral, and said, "Then I'm sorry, Sera. I truly am."

Now here we were.

Below me, the column reached the mouth of Pemberton Lane. The engine's null-glass heart flared, and every charm-light in the lane died at once. Somewhere in the dark of the cellar beneath the charm shop, I knew, four heirs were standing around a door older than the city, and the last defense between them and that singing machine was—

Me.

God help us all.

* * *

Let me tell you what I knew about Corvane before tonight, because it matters.

I knew him for nineteen years. He recruited me at fifteen, off the steps of the Foundling Chapel where the Order had taken me after the Accords took my parents. He taught me to hold a blade, then to hold a line, then to hold my tongue. He promoted me seven times. He pinned the Warden's cross on my chest himself and said, "Your parents would be proud," which was either kindness or cruelty depending on the day, because my parents were taken *by* the Order, and he knew it, and I knew he knew it, and neither of us ever said it out loud for nineteen years.

I also knew—I had learned it three days ago, in the archive beneath Belltower Row, in a ledger older than the Accords themselves—that Corvane's own grandfather had signed the original covenant. That the Sundering wasn't an accident or a tragedy but an *engineering*, deliberate and cold, done by men who feared what love between realms might build. And that the Voss name was written in that ledger's margin, bound into the covenant by blood, generation after generation, leashed to a duty we never chose.

My lifelong duty was a dog collar. And Corvane had been holding the leash.

Or so I'd believed, standing in that archive with the truth spread out before me like a corpse on a table. But there was one more thing in that ledger, one more line, and it changed the shape of everything:

*The High Warden's line was struck from the covenant in the second year. Struck by their own request.*

Corvane's family had *asked* to be cut free. Asked to be unbound from the gate, from the oath, from the cost. Everyone else's blood paid the toll across a century. His bought itself an exemption.

So when he marched on that gate tonight with a machine designed to sever magic from the world forever—not seal it, not guard it, *sever* it—I understood at last what I was looking at. Not a protector gone astray. A man finishing his family's work. A man who had decided that if the covenant demanded a price, the answer was to burn the whole covenant down and call the ashes law.

He wasn't defending Ashfall from the other realm.

He was defending himself from the bill.

* * *

The bell above me weighed four tons and hadn't rung in sixty years, not since the Sundering silenced it—they said the crack in it sang wrong, sang *other*, and the Order forbade it. It hung above me now in the dark like a sleeping giant, and I put my palm flat against its cold bronze side and whispered the only prayer I remembered from the chapel, the one they taught foundlings before they taught them anything else:

*"Keep what is kept. Guard what guards."*

Old words. Covenant words, though the chapel had forgotten that. My blood knew them anyway; I felt it hum in my teeth, the same hum I'd felt at the scar-field, the same hum that had been getting louder in me for days, like something waking up under my skin after a century of sedation.

"Sorry," I told the bell. "I'm about to be very loud."

Then I drew my sword, stepped to the tower's edge, and dropped the rope ladder down the far side onto the rooftops—because I'd spent the afternoon rigging this, and if I was going to die tonight, I was going to die *inconveniencing* Corvane, which was the only eulogy I'd ever wanted.

The plan was simple, the way plans are simple right up until they meet reality. The engine couldn't reach the cellar door while the lane was blocked, and the lane was blocked by the barricade Bramble Quill's smuggler friends had thrown together from canal barges and pure spite. But Corvane didn't need the lane. He needed only to bring the engine within thirty feet of the shop's foundation, and the null-glass would do the rest—eat through stone, eat through wards, eat through the gate's fragile half-sealed skin and leave behind a silence that would never heal.

My job was to make sure the engine never got those thirty feet. Alone. On a rooftop. With one sword and whatever my newly woken blood could manage without instruction manual or warranty.

I'd sent word to the others: *Hold the door. Don't come up. Whatever you hear, don't come up.* August would want to come up. August was the type. That was the whole problem with August, and the whole reason the prophecy had picked him, probably—he was constitutionally incapable of letting someone else bleed alone.

Which meant I had maybe ten minutes before he defied me, and I intended to spend them well.

* * *

I heard the column before I saw it turn into the square below—a sound like a river deciding to be an army. Then torchlight washed the rooftops gold, and Corvane's voice carried up, calm and carrying, the voice of a man reading a proclamation rather than starting a battle:

"People of Ashfall! Tonight the Order ends a century of fear! Tonight we finish what our grandfathers began!"

A cheer went up. Three hundred voices. Some of them I knew by name. Tam Wilder, who cried at his daughter's naming ceremony. Maren Ostwick, who'd once shared her rations with me through a fever-week in the marsh garrisons. They weren't monsters. That was the trap of it. They were good people, loyal people, marching behind a lie dressed as a legacy, and every single one of them would sleep soundly tonight believing they'd saved the city.

"High Warden!" I called down, and my voice cracked across the square like the whip-crack it had been trained to be. "You're standing on a grave!"

The column stopped. Torches tilted upward. And Corvane, magnificent bastard, simply turned his horse and looked up at me with an expression of mild, paternal disappointment, as if I were a foundling caught stealing bread.

"Warden Voss." He said it loudly, for the crowd. "You are relieved of command. You are ill. Come down, and we will get you well."

"I'm not ill," I said. "I'm *awake*. There's a difference, and the difference is standing in a ledger under your feet with your grandfather's signature in it. Ask him what the Sundering cost, Corvane! Ask him who paid! Your family wrote themselves out of the bill and now you've come to collect from everyone else!"

Murmuring moved through the ranks like wind through wheat. Good. Doubt was a weapon too, and I'd sharpened mine all afternoon.

But Corvane just smiled—that surgeon's smile—and raised one gauntleted hand, and the ranks parted, and the severance-engine rolled forward into the square.

"You mistake grief for truth, Sera," he called up. "It's a common error. I'll correct it."

The null-glass heart flared.

Every hair on my body stood on end. The hum in my blood turned to a scream. And I understood, viscerally, in the marrow the covenant had claimed a century ago, exactly what that machine did—it didn't kill magic, it *unmade the agreement*, tore the treaty written into the world's fabric, and everything bound by it, everyone bound by it, would come apart along the seams.

Including me. Including the four downstairs. Including, probably, the city, the canal, and several foundational assumptions of reality.

"Well," I said to no one. "Inconvenience it is."

I jumped.

* * *

Falling off a bell tower onto a severance-engine teaches you things about yourself.

Mostly it teaches you that you should have planned the landing better.

I hit the engine's brass spine hard enough to see the chapel, my mother, and several constellations that don't exist, rolled, and came up on my feet on the narrow catwalk along its back—one boot on each side of the great glass heart, which pulsed beneath me like a caged star trying to decide whether to hate me. Wardens shouted below. Crossbow bolts started arriving with opinions.

"Voss, get DOWN—" someone yelled. Tam Wilder's voice. He was aiming high. Bless him and damn him both.

"Can't!" I yelled back. "Occupied!"

The engine's crew scrambled up the lattice toward me—engineers in padded leather, six of them, brave and terrified and doing their jobs, which was the most tragic sentence in any language. I met the first one with the flat of my blade, because he was twenty and shaking, and knocked him back down into the arms of his friends rather than through the glass. The second I tripped. The third actually swung at me, and I caught his haft on my crossguard and leaned in close and said, quiet, just for him: "Ask your grandfather what he signed."

He hesitated. That hesitation bought me four seconds, and four seconds was enough for the first wave of my blood to arrive.

Because here's what waking covenant-blood feels like: it doesn't feel like power. It feels like *permission*. All my life I'd been strong—stronger than the drills explained, faster, harder to kill—and I'd assumed it was training. It wasn't. It was the leash, pulled taut, holding back something enormous. And now the leash was off, and the thing underneath was old and patient and *mine*, and it came up through my boots into the brass and the bronze and the null-glass and it said, in a voice like a struck bell:

**NO.**

The engine shuddered. The glass heart flickered. Below me, Corvane's face went through surprise, calculation, and decision in the space of a breath, and the decision was: *kill her.*

"Seventh Company!" he roared. "Take the tower! Ninth, clear the machine! And someone put a bolt in Voss before she—"

I didn't hear the rest, because the ninth company's sergeant threw a grappling hook that took me across the shoulder, and the world became impact and rotation and the taste of copper, and I went off the catwalk, caught the brass rib one-handed, and hung there over the singing heart while bolts thudded into the frame around me like rain on a coffin lid.

This, I remember thinking, hanging there with my sword arm screaming and my blood humming its terrible bell-note, is a genuinely terrible place to have an epiphany. But epiphanies are rude like that.

Because I looked down—past the glass, past the machine, past the army—at the door of the charm shop, where light was leaking up through the cracks in the boards. Warm light. Gold-and-ember light. The gate, half-sealed, breathing. Four people down there who'd taken me in when I was their enemy, fed me, argued with me, trusted me further than I deserved. Bramble, who'd called me "the enemy with nice boots" and then shared her dinner anyway. Odile, my sister, who'd looked at me two nights ago across a candle and said, "I'm glad it was you who came back," and meant it, and I had *survived long enough to hear it*.

Nineteen years I'd served the Order because I thought duty was the only family that wouldn't be taken from me.

Turns out family finds you anyway. It finds you in cellars and canal boats and candlelight, and it asks nothing except that you stand between it and the dark for as long as you can.

"Okay," I said to the heart of the machine, to the army, to the bell above me, to my mother wherever she was. "Okay. Let's be loud."

I let go of the rib, dropped to the catwalk, and stopped holding back.

* * *

What happened next, the histories—if anyone writes them—will get wrong, because witnesses describe miracles and survivors describe chaos, and I was neither, I was the person *doing* it, and honestly my memory of it is mostly sound.

Sound first: the bell. Sixty years silent, and it rang. I never touched it. My blood reached up through the tower stone and *rang it*, one great cracked bronze note that rolled over the city like a tide, and every warden in that square flinched, because they'd all grown up on the story that the bell's crack sang *other*, and here it was, singing, and it sounded like judgment and it sounded like morning and it sounded, faintly, underneath, like my mother's voice saying *keep what is kept.*

Sound second: the engine, screaming. Because my covenant-blood poured into it and found the treaty-thread woven through its guts—the stolen thread, the thread Corvane's family had cut from their own line a century ago and wound into this machine instead—and I *claimed* it. Blood calls to blood. The engine's null-heart stuttered, guttered, dimmed from killing-star to dying coal.

Sound third: me, laughing. Which surprised me most of all. Somewhere between the bell note and the claiming, terror tipped over into something wilder, and I laughed like a woman who has finally, *finally* stopped asking permission.

"SERA VOSS!" Corvane's voice, raw now, the calm finally cracking. He was off his horse, climbing the lattice himself, greatsword drawn, sunburst pauldrons blazing in the torchlight. Of course he came himself. He'd never sent anyone to do what he could take personally. "Step aside. Last chance. You were the finest of them—you can still BE the finest of them—"

"No," I said, and met him on the catwalk, blade up. "But thank you for nineteen years of trying to make me you."

He was better than me. I have to be honest about that, because redemption bought on a lie isn't redemption. He was stronger, fresher, armored where I wasn't, and he'd trained me, which meant he knew every flaw in my form because he'd installed half of them. The first exchange opened my forearm. The second cost me the sword.

It went ringing off into the dark below, and his point came up at my throat, and the whole square went quiet enough to hear the null-glass guttering under my feet.

"Yield," he said. Almost tender. "Sera. Yield, and live."

And here's the thing. I thought about it. Honestly. One heartbeat, two. Yielding meant a cell, maybe, or the tincture, or worse—but alive. Alive to see Odile again. Alive to see how it ended.

Then I looked past his shoulder, at the shop door, where the ember-light was growing. Brighter. The rite had begun. And I heard—faint, impossible, through stone and steel and three hundred bodies—August's voice, lifted in something that wasn't quite a song, and the bell above us answered it, and my blood sang with both, and I understood:

The price was being paid down there, right now, willingly, by people I loved. And my job—my one job, the job I'd chosen instead of the one I'd been assigned—was to make sure nobody interrupted them.

If I yielded, Corvane would drag the engine those thirty feet, and it wouldn't matter what anyone sacrificed downstairs, because there'd be no gate left to seal.

There was no yielding. There was only the math of love, and love's math is always the same: *them first.*

"No," I said again. And I smiled at him, bloody, disarmed, exhausted beyond measure, and I said the truest thing I've ever said to another human being:

"You cut your family out of the covenant because you couldn't bear the cost. Well, High Warden. Here's mine."

I grabbed his sword-blade with my bare left hand—covenant-blood doesn't much care about edges—and held it there, and let my right hand fill with the oldest thing my blood knew, the bell-note, the keep-what-is-kept, and I drove it into the null-glass heart of the severance-engine like a key into a lock.

The engine screamed. The glass went white, then dark, then *cracked*—a hundred hairline fractures blooming like frost—and Corvane hauled his blade free and staggered back, staring at the death of his family's masterpiece, and I stared at my hand, which was burned from fingertip to elbow in a pattern I recognized.

A sigil. The covenant sigil. Blooming on my skin like a brand, bright as the ember-light leaking from the shop door below.

"Impossible," Corvane breathed. "Your line was bound. Bound lines can't—"

"Bound lines *can*," I said, "when they stop fighting it."

He came at me anyway. Of course he did. Men like Corvane don't surrender to evidence; they surrender to arithmetic, and the arithmetic hadn't finished yet. We went down the lattice together, grappling, falling, and hit the cobblestones of Belltower Row in a tangle of steel and torchlight, and the square erupted—wardens shouting, some advancing, some, gods bless them, hesitating, because they'd all just watched their High Warden's invincible machine die at the hands of a traitor who glowed.

We fought in the wreckage of the engine's shadow. Fist and hilt and knee and elbow, no artistry left, just two people who'd loved the same institution tearing at each other in the ruins of it. He was still stronger. He got me against the dead engine's flank, forearm across my throat, and leaned in close, and said, low, only for me:

"It didn't have to be this way, Sera."

"Yes it did," I wheezed. "One of us had to pay the bill."

And then the bell rang again.

Not me, this time. Not my blood. From below—from the cellar, from the gate, from the heart of the Sundering itself—a note rose up through the foundations of Ashfall City, through the cobblestones, through the soles of every boot in that square, and every warden felt it, and every warden *stopped*, because it was the most beautiful sound any of them had ever heard, and it sounded like a door closing gently, and it sounded like a promise kept.

The reforging. Downstairs. Now.

Corvane's arm loosened. Just slightly. Just a fraction of a fraction—and in that fraction, I saw his eyes go distant, hearing what I heard, feeling his own struck-out blood-line tremble in response to a covenant it had abandoned, and I watched a man realize, in real time, that he had spent his whole life running from a debt that had just been paid by braver people.

I put my knee into him and rolled him off me, and he let me. Mostly.

Around us, the square was dissolving. Tam Wilder had lowered his crossbow and was looking at his hands. Maren Ostwick had planted her pike in the cobbles and sat down on the engine's dead chassis like a woman whose legs had resigned. Someone was crying. Someone else had started pulling off their Warden's cross.

And Corvane lay on his back in the shadow of his broken cathedral-on-wheels, armor scorched, breathing hard, looking up at the bell tower and the cracked bell that had sung twice in one night after sixty years of silence.

"What have you done," he whispered. Not a question. A eulogy—for himself, maybe, or for the version of history where he was the hero.

"The right thing," I said. "Late. Badly. At terrible cost." I spat blood onto the cobbles. "You should try it sometime."

Then the world tilted, and my knees informed me of the accumulated invoice—burned hand, wrenched shoulder, grapple-gash, the deep bruising of a duel with a man built like a fortress—and I sat down heavily beside my former High Warden, because standing had become a rumor.

Above us, the ember-light from the shop door swelled, peaked—

—and went out.

All of it, at once. Total dark. The kind of dark that has weight.

The square held its breath. I held mine. Beside me, Corvane whispered, "What was that? What did they—"

And then, from the cellar beneath the charm shop, soft as a closing book, came a sound I will hear for the rest of my life:

A latch. Clicking home.

The gate, sealed.

And underneath the latch-click, so faint I might have dreamed it, two voices—one warm, one steady—saying something I couldn't make out. Saying names, maybe. Saying each other's names.

I closed my eyes and let myself, for exactly three seconds, lean sideways until my shoulder touched the scorched pauldron of the man I'd fought for nineteen years and against for one night, and I said:

"It's done. You lost. We all won. Get used to it."

Somewhere down in that dark cellar, two people I loved had just paid a price I couldn't imagine, and the first thing I was going to do when I could stand was go down those stairs and find out what was left of them.

The second thing was going to be arresting Corvane with his own handcuffs, which I'd taken off his belt during the fall, and which he hadn't noticed yet.

Priorities.
