## 28. Into the Threshold

**Lena**

The cellar has never felt small before.

Five years I've kept this room—swept it, warded it, lied to it about what I wanted. It held my stall's overflow stock, a broken chair, and beneath the false floor, the oldest secret in Ashfall City. It was always cramped and honest and mine. Tonight it feels like the antechamber to something with teeth.

August stands at the edge of the opened floor, looking down into the light that isn't light. The gate is awake now. I can feel it through my boots, through my ribs, a hum like a struck bell that never finishes ringing. It knows we're coming. I think it's been waiting longer than either of us has been alive.

"You don't have to say anything brave," I tell him. "I'd rather you said something true."

He huffs a laugh. Even now—even with Bramble's bandages upstairs and Sera bleeding somewhere on Belltower Row and an army hammering at the city like a fist on a door—he still finds the joke in things. That's the part of him I refuse to forget first. If the gate takes anything from me, let it take my fear of spiders, my memory of every awkward dinner party I've ever endured. Not this. Not him laughing when he should be terrified.

"True," he says. "Okay. True is: I'm scared out of my mind, and I've never been more certain of anything." He looks at me sideways. "Both of those are about you, by the way."

"Flatterer."

"Realist." He holds out his hand, palm up, the way he did on our first date outside the charm stall, when I thought he was just a warm-eyed stranger buying a luck-charm he didn't need. "Ready?"

No. God, no. My whole body is a closed door.

"Yes," I say, and take his hand.

Above us, muffled by stone and spellwork, the sounds of the siege continue—the deep concussion of the severance-engine testing its range against the wards, Odile's voice raised in something between prayer and profanity, Bramble insisting from her sickbed that if anyone dies down there she will haunt them personally and creatively. Our family. Loud, impossible, temporary.

We leave them at the top of the stairs. That's the bargain none of us said aloud: they hold the door; we hold each other.

The steps down aren't stairs, exactly. They're more like the idea of stairs, remembered by stone that loved someone once. Each one gives slightly underfoot, warm as a sleeping animal. The light rises to meet us—not firelight, not moonlight, but ember-light, the color of a coal that refuses to die. It moves across August's face and makes him look the way he looked in the ledger's century-old ink: inevitable.

"The last time I came down here alone," I say, because talking keeps the terror at arm's length, "the gate whispered at me for three days afterward. Just one word, over and over. I never told anyone what it said."

"What did it say?"

"*Soon.*"

His fingers tighten around mine. "Cheerful houseguest."

"It's been alone a long time," I say. "So have I, if I'm honest. Maybe that's why I never reported it. Why I hid it instead of handing it to the Wardens like a good little citizen." I laugh, and it comes out cracked. "I told myself it was survival. It wasn't. It was company."

"Lena Pemberton," August says solemnly, "confessing she committed the crime of loneliness."

"Life sentence, probably."

"Life sentence," he agrees, "with me. If we pull this off."

*If.* The word drops between us like a coin down a well. We both hear how long it takes to land.

* * *

**August**

Here is what nobody tells you about walking into a god's mouth: it's warm.

I expected cold. I expected the void, the abyss, all the vocabulary of endings. Instead the passage breathes around us like a lung, and the walls are veined with that patient ember-glow, and somewhere far below—or maybe very near, distance doesn't work right here—something vast turns over in its sleep and murmurs.

The whispering starts at the fourth step.

It's not the dead language this time. Or—it is, but I understand it now, which is worse. The syllables slide past my ear and arrive already translated, meaning pressed directly into my chest like a seal into wax:

*Two who were one. One who chose twice.*

"That's new," Lena whispers.

"You hear it too?"

"I hear everything too," she says. "Since the rite stabilized the gate. It's been... narrating. Quietly. Like a very old woman commenting on her neighbors." She pauses on the step below me so our eyes are level. In the ember-light her hair is copper and her fear is completely visible, and she carries it anyway, upright, chin set. I love her so much it feels structural. Load-bearing. Like if they removed it, I'd collapse.

"What's it saying about us?" I ask.

She hesitates. "That we're familiar."

"Familiar how?"

"August." Her voice gentles into the tone you use for someone standing on ice. "You know what we're walking down there to do. Some of what it recognizes—we're about to feed to it on purpose."

Right. The price. Memories and love, freely given, forever. One heir was supposed to pay it whole. We decided—defied—the arithmetic. Split it down the middle. Half my remembering, half hers. Half my loving, half hers. A wound cut neatly in two so neither of us dies of it, and both of us live with it.

Some nights I think it's the bravest thing anyone's ever done. Other nights I think it's the cruelest: we'll walk back up those stairs holding hands with two strangers wearing our faces, and the strangers will feel the ache and never know its name.

But then I look at her, and I think: better an ache shared than a grave borne alone. Better half of her than all of nothing.

"Hey," I say. "Tell me something you want to keep. So it's said out loud. So it's witnessed."

She doesn't even pause. "The night of our first date. You bought a charm for 'protection from bad first impressions,' which is the most ridiculous thing anyone's ever purchased from me, and when I laughed, you looked at me like—" Her breath catches. "Like you'd finally arrived somewhere."

"That's exactly what happened," I say. "Your turn's coming. Warn me now so I can pick."

"Pick?"

"What I want to keep. Witnessed."

She waits. The gate waits. Even the siege above seems to hold its breath.

"You," I say. "Not a moment. Not a single scene. Just—you. However much of you survives the cutting. I want the stranger who walks out of here to look at you and feel like home. That's the wish. Take it or leave it, gate. I'm told you grant things."

The whispering swells—and for one heartbeat it sounds almost tender. Almost like grief.

Then the last step takes us.

* * *

**Lena**

The chamber at the bottom is not a chamber.

It is a wound in the shape of a room, and we stand inside it, and everywhere around us the Sundering is still happening—slowly, endlessly, the way a scream stretches in a dream. Light tears along seams in the air. Through them I glimpse the other realm: towers of glass and growing things, a sky the color of dawn deciding, shapes moving that might be people. On this side, Ashfall's reflection shivers like a painting left in rain. Between the two, the cut. A hundred years old and still bleeding.

And in the center, where the floor should be, the heart of the gate: a sphere of ember-light no bigger than a lantern, hanging in nothing, beating.

Once per beat, the whole chamber brightens. Once per beat, the whispering resolves into a voice—not the old woman commenting on neighbors anymore, but something older and wearier, worn smooth as river stone:

*You came.*

"We came," August answers. His thumb strokes over my knuckles, once, deliberate. Anchoring himself or me; I can't tell anymore. Maybe there's no difference left.

*One was promised,* the gate says. *The covenant asks one. Whole. Willing. You bring two halves.*

"We bring two people," I say, and my voice comes out steadier than I have any right to expect. "Who decline your math."

A silence. The ember-heart pulses. Somewhere above us, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, the severance-engine strikes the wards again, and dust sifts down through the torn light like gray snow.

*The Sundering was made by one who feared,* the gate says. *Feared what crossed the cut. Feared most what loved across it. She built the law of one-for-one because she could not imagine two choosing together. Every covenant since has inherited her imagination. Her smallness.*

"Then break the inheritance," August says quietly. "Let us be the new math."

I feel it then—the gate considering us. It moves through the chamber like weather, like pressure before a storm, pressing against the inside of my skull, reading me the way I once read charms for customers: turning me over, finding the true name of the thing. And what it finds, apparently, interests it, because the whispering changes key.

*Show me,* it says. *What you would keep. What you would give. Show me, and I will weigh it.*

"How?" August asks.

The answer arrives without words. The ember-heart drifts toward us, slow as a lantern in floodwater, and stops between our joined hands. Waiting.

I look at August. He looks at me. And I understand—we don't get to rehearse this. There's no speech, no ceremony. We simply open our hands, together, and offer up the whole unedited truth of what we are to each other: the charm stall and the bad joke, the ledger with his face inked a century ago, the canal market and the smoke, the safehouse and the almost-kiss, the letters he wrote and the one he didn't finish, every ordinary hour that turned out to be treasure.

The light takes it all in.

And then, gently—so gently, the way you'd take something fragile from a child's hands—it begins to count.

* * *

**August**

Counting hurts.

Not the way a blow hurts. The way an inventory hurts—each item lifted, examined, weighed, and set aside into one pile or the other. *Keep. Give. Keep. Give.* I feel my life sorted by something older than mercy, and I grab for the pieces as they pass, and some of them slip through my fingers like water, and some of them—

Some of them it sets down on our side, deliberately, and I realize the gate is not just taking. It's negotiating.

*This one,* it murmurs, lifting something I hadn't known I carried—the smell of Lena's cellar, beeswax and old paper and the particular dust of hidden things. *This is load-bearing. It stays.*

"This one"—a flash of Bramble swearing magnificently from a rooftop while Wardens shouted below—"is needed above. It stays."

*This one*—Sera's face the moment she learned her bloodline was a leash—*grief teaches. It stays.*

Piece by piece, the gate builds a case for us. As if it wants, desperately, to find a way to keep us whole. As if a hundred years of solitude taught it the exact value of what we're offering, and it is ashamed to accept.

"We came to pay," I say aloud, and my voice cracks down the middle. "Don't talk yourself out of us."

*Foolish, generous creature,* the gate says, and there is something in its voice I didn't expect, something raw. *Do you know why the covenantor feared? Not power. Power she understood. She feared this—that love does not obey borders. That it crosses. That it multiplies instead of dividing. She sealed the realms to stop the multiplication, and in doing so she built the loneliest machine in creation, and put me inside it.*

The ember-heart flares. Through the tearing light, the other realm leans close—I can see faces now, watching from the far side of the cut. Watching us. Hoping, the way you hope for strangers.

"So take the payment," Lena says. She steps forward, dragging me with her, our hands still locked. "Half from each. Memories and love, split clean. And in exchange—seal the cut. Mend it. Not locked—*mended*. Let the realms touch again someday, carefully, when the scar's had time to heal. That's the deal. That's the only deal on the table."

The gate goes very still.

*You ask me to heal,* it says, *instead of merely to close.*

"I'm a shopkeeper," Lena says. "I fix things. It's the whole business model."

And the gate laughs.

I swear to whatever gods survived the Sundering: the gate laughs—a sound like the first fire ever lit, like a bell rung after a hundred silent years—and the whole wounded chamber shudders with it, and somewhere far above I'd bet money that Odile felt it and started praying and Bramble felt it and started celebrating.

*Then come closer, new math,* the gate says. *Bring your hands. Bring your names. We will see what remains when the counting is done—and perhaps, if you are as stubborn as you seem, what remains will be enough.*

We step forward together.

The light rises to meet us like a tide, and the last thing I hear before it closes over us is Lena's voice, calm and fierce and entirely her own:

"Hold on to me. Whatever else goes—hold on."

*I've got you,* I say.

And the gate begins to take.

* * *

**Lena**

Fire, but painless. Pages, but burning upward instead of down—returning to the book instead of leaving it. I feel the first memories lift out of me like birds startled off a wire, and I reach for August's name the way you reach for a railing in the dark, and I find it, I hold it, I say it into the light like a password, like a prayer, like a promise—

"August. Your name is August. Say mine. Say it, say it, say—"

The gate opens its pages.

And far above us, four sigils ignite—one by one by one by one—across the hearts of everyone we love.
