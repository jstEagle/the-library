## 29. The Heart of the Sundering

The dark inside the gate was not empty. That was the first thing I understood, and the last thing I wanted to.

Empty dark is just a room with the lights off. This was a dark that was *looking* at us—patient, vast, and faintly amused, like a cat watching two mice argue about which direction to run. Lena's hand tightened around mine. Her palm was slick, her pulse hammering against my fingers in a rhythm I'd have known anywhere by now, even blindfolded, even dead.

"August," she whispered. "Don't let go."

"Noted," I said. "Adding it to the list of things I refuse to do today, right under 'die politely' and 'let you do anything brave without me.'"

She made a sound that was half laugh, half sob. Good. Fear and laughter live in the same room of the body; I've checked.

We had descended—I don't know how far. Stairs that hadn't been there when we started walking them, stone worn smooth by feet that predated Ashfall, predated the Accords, maybe predated the idea of cities at all. The gate beneath Pemberton Cellar had opened for us like a throat, and now here we were, swallowed, somewhere between realms, where the air tasted of ozone and old rain and something sweeter underneath—ember-smoke, woodsmoke, the smell of a fire someone once kept burning all night for someone else.

Then the light came.

It didn't arrive so much as *remember itself*—a slow gold bloom out ahead of us, and standing in it, a figure. I want to say a person, but that would be flattering to both of us. It was shaped like a person the way a river is shaped like a road: same general outline, entirely different intentions. Tall, robed in grey that moved like water remembering it used to be a cloud. Its face kept almost resolving—a woman, then an old man, then someone young and furious—and never settling, as if it had been so many people over the centuries that it had forgotten which one it started as.

"You came," it said. The voice was every voice at once, layered like a chord. "After everything. You actually came."

"We're funny that way," I said. My mouth does this when I'm terrified. It's a documented condition. "Lovely place you've got. Very atmospheric. The stairs could use a railing."

Lena elbowed me. The remnant tilted its head, and something in the gesture was so humanly curious that my chest hurt.

"You're him," it said. "The ember. The one the ledger has been waiting a century to write about." Its gaze—it didn't have eyes, exactly, but the attention landed on me like a hand—shifted to Lena. "And you. Little keeper. Five years feeding crumbs of charm to a city that outlawed your nature, guarding a door you were never told how to open. You must hate me."

"I don't know you," Lena said carefully.

"No," it agreed. "You know what I *did*. Everyone knows what I did. Nobody knows why. That was rather the point."

* * *

It gestured—or the space around it suggested a gesture—and the dark around us filled with images. Not visions, exactly. Memories, hanging in the black like lanterns: a world before the Sundering.

I saw it. Gods help me, I saw it.

Two realms, folded against each other like hands. No wall, no gate, no border—just a seam where mortal country faded into fae country the way dusk fades into night, gradual, unguarded, unremarkable. Mortal farmers trading apples for fae light. Fae children learning mortal songs off-key. Weddings across the seam. Markets where you paid in coin or in secrets, whichever you had more of.

And in one of those markets—a memory closer than the others, sharper—a young woman in a grey robe laughing at something a fae man said, his antlers hung with ribbons, her hand resting on his arm like it had always lived there.

"That's—" Lena breathed.

"Me," the remnant said simply. "Or the part of me that was ever anyone. My name was Ilseva Marrow. I was a covenantor—one of nine who tended the seam. And the man with the ribbons was Auren. He was of the other realm. We were going to be married in the spring."

The memory-lanterns dimmed. In their place, new ones lit, and these burned red.

"The Sundering wasn't a war," Ilseva said. "Your histories will tell you it was a war—the mortal realm attacked, or the fae realm attacked, depending on which side of the wall your school stood on. It was neither. There was no battle. There was no enemy army. There was a ritual, performed by nine keepers of the covenant, on a night in autumn, and it tore the worlds apart along a seam that had held for ten thousand years."

"Why?" Lena asked. Her voice was very quiet. "Why would the keepers break the thing they kept?"

Ilseva was silent for a long moment. When it spoke again, the layered chord of its voice had thinned down to something almost single, almost small.

"Because of what happened at the wedding."

* * *

The memory that bloomed next, I would give anything to unsee. But you can't unsee things inside a gate; the gate keeps what you see, files it away in whatever drawer it uses for the shape of your soul. So I'll describe it, because describing it is the only way I know to make it smaller.

There had been another couple. Before Ilseva and Auren—years before. A mortal man and a fae woman who loved each other badly, or too much, or in some way that curdled. I never learned the details; the memory blurred there, deliberately, the way a wound blurs when you press on it. What I saw was the end of it: a village on the seam, burned from both sides. Mortal dead by fae craft. Fae dead by mortal iron. And in the middle of it, holding each other, the couple themselves—dead by each other's hands, or by their own, the memory wouldn't say, only that they died together and everyone around them died apart.

"The elders called it proof," Ilseva said. "Love between the realms was a contagion. It always ended in ash. Every treaty, every law that followed, was built on that one burned village—that one ruined love. And the nine of us believed them. We were keepers. We trusted the old stories the way priests trust scripture." A pause. "Auren and I were to be married in the spring. The Sundering was performed in autumn. Do you understand? We did it *first*. We cut the worlds apart while our love was still legal, still possible, still ours—and then we sealed the wound behind us so that no one could ever again be what we had been. Because we had seen what we became when it went wrong, and we were afraid. Not wise. Not righteous. *Afraid.*"

The dark around us seemed to lean in.

"They told us the ritual required a willing sacrifice," Ilseva went on. "Memory and love, given freely, to bind the gate forever. Nine of us gave pieces. I gave—" it laughed, and the laugh was terrible, dry as pages turning, "—I gave the largest piece. I gave him. I gave every memory of Auren, every hour, every argument, every plan for spring. And the gate took them and grew strong, and the realms fell silent on either side of the wall, and I lived eleven hundred years as a custodian of a door I built out of my own amputation."

"But you're still here," I said. "Inside it. Why?"

"Because the gate needs a heart," Ilseva said, "and I volunteered mine. That is the joke no one survives hearing: the Sundering was engineered by people who loved too well and trusted fear more. Your villain, little ember, is an old wound that learned to call itself wisdom."

* * *

Lena had gone very still beside me. I know her stillnesses by now—the thinking-stillness, the angry-stillness, the one where she's holding herself together with both hands. This was the third kind.

"You said the gate requires a price," she said. "Memory and love. Willing sacrifice. That's what the ledger showed us. One heir gives everything."

"Yes."

"And that's what you did. Eleven hundred years ago."

"Yes."

"So the gate isn't asking for anything new." Her voice cracked open like ice on a pond. "It's asking us to repeat you. To become you. Two people who loved each other, torn apart by duty, one of them hollowed out and left to tend the silence forever. That's not a solution, that's a *pattern*. You built a machine that manufactures your own tragedy and calls it peace."

For the first time, the remnant flinched. Actually flinched—the grey robes rippling, the almost-face shivering through a dozen expressions before settling on something raw.

"I know," Ilseva said softly. "I have had eleven hundred years to know it. Do you imagine I haven't watched, from in here, every time the gate stirred? Every generation, someone finds the ledger, someone gathers the heirs, someone stands where you stand—and every time, I hope they will refuse. And every time, either they fail before the choice, or—" the chord-voice frayed, "—or they choose correctly. They pay the price. They become me. And the wall stands another century, and I get another companion for a while, until the gate digests them too, and I am alone again with the shape of a man whose name I gave away."

My throat was tight. "What was his name?"

"What?"

"Auren. You said you gave away every memory of him. But you remembered his name just now. You said it plain as weather."

Silence. The long kind.

"The gate takes memories," Ilseva said finally. "It cannot take the fact that a memory existed. I remember that I loved someone. I remember that he had a name. I have spent eleven hundred years circling the hole where he used to be, and the hole has edges, and the edges spell *Auren*, and that is all I have. Do you understand what I'm telling you? That is the price. Not forgetting. Remembering that you've forgotten. Forever."

Lena's grip on my hand had gone white-knuckled. I looked at her—at this impossible woman who'd hidden magic behind a charm stall for five years, who'd shown a stranger her cellar full of forbidden light, who'd walked into a god's throat holding my hand—and felt something settle in me, cold and clear as well-water.

Because I already knew what she was going to do. And I already knew what I was going to do about it. And we were going to have a truly spectacular fight about it, probably within the hour, possibly within the minute.

But first—

"You brought us here to tell us this," I said to Ilseva. "Eleven hundred years alone, and the moment the right four heirs show up, you spend your one conversation confessing. Why? What do you want from us?"

The almost-face turned toward me fully, and for a heartbeat it resolved completely—not a woman, not a man, but someone tired beyond the reach of language, wearing a face the way you wear a coat that stopped fitting decades ago.

"I want you to do it differently," it said. "I want you to look at the price I paid and find the flaw in it, because after eleven centuries I cannot see it anymore. I want you to be cleverer than a grieving girl in a grey robe. I want—" and here the great layered voice dropped to nearly nothing, "—I want someone, *anyone*, to walk out of this place still whole, so that I can stop believing the wall was worth it."

* * *

The dark shifted. Somewhere above us—far above, up the endless stair, through the throat of the cellar, through the floor of a city that was even now filling with soldiers and severance-engines and a High Warden's ambition—I felt the gate tremble. Not with our presence. With theirs. Iron and intent, marching toward Pemberton Cellar.

Lena felt it too. I saw it hit her face like weather.

"How long?" she asked.

"Hours," Ilseva said. "Perhaps less. The engine they carry does not knock. It unmakes. When it reaches this threshold, the gate will not seal—it will *shatter*, and everything I am, everything the covenant was, everything keeping the two realms from grinding against each other like broken plates, comes apart with it."

"Then we do the rite now," Lena said. "Tonight. Before they arrive."

"There is a price," Ilseva reminded her gently. Cruelly. Both.

"I read the ledger," Lena said. "I know the price."

And I watched her square her shoulders in the gold-and-red light of a dead woman's regret, watched her start to step forward, watched her begin to say the words—I could see them forming, *I offer myself, I pay it freely, take me and spare the others*—and every alarm I owned went off at once.

So I did the only reasonable thing available to a man madly in love with a heroic idiot in a collapsing underworld.

I stepped in front of her.

"Lena," I said, "we need to talk about the bill."
