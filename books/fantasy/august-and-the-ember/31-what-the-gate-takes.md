## 31. What the Gate Takes

### LENA

There is a moment—right before you do the bravest thing of your life—when your body betrays you completely.

Mine chose that moment to notice that August's hand was warm. Not magically warm, not ember-warm, just *human* warm, the kind of warm that comes from a pulse and a stubborn heart and a man who once paid for a luck-charm he didn't need because he liked my laugh. My palm memorized his. My traitor heart did a little skip it had no business doing on the threshold of eternal sacrifice.

"Your hands are shaking," August said.

"Yours too."

"Yeah, but I'm allowed. I'm the dramatic one."

"You are *not* the dramatic one," I said, because if we stopped bantering even for a second, the dark around us would get in, and the dark down here was old enough to have opinions.

We stood in the heart of the Sundering—the gate's inner chamber, which was not a chamber so much as a held breath. The walls were neither stone nor shadow but something between, veined with light like amber with a wasp caught in it. Above us, faint through leagues of earth and cellar floor and shop boards, I could almost hear Bramble pacing, Odile praying, Sera standing guard with her sword drawn against an army. Our people. Holding the line so we could hold this one.

The gate waited. It had been waiting a hundred years. It could wait another minute while I committed the shape of his knuckles to memory.

"Lena." August's voice had gone quiet in the way that always undid me. "If this goes wrong—"

"It won't."

"If it does," he insisted, "I want you to know that out of every terrible decision I've ever made—and there have been *so* many, I once ate a canal oyster on a dare—choosing you twice was the only good one."

I laughed. It came out wet and cracked, but it was a laugh, and the gate seemed to lean toward it, hungry for the sound.

"That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me," I told him, "and it contains an oyster story."

"I'm saving the oyster story for our anniversary."

"We may not remember anniversaries."

"Then I'll invent new ones." He lifted our joined hands, mine still bound to his with the covenant-cord, red thread shot through with gold. "Every day we wake up and don't know why we're holding hands? That's an anniversary. We'll collect hundreds."

The gate exhaled.

I felt it the way you feel weather change—not a sound but a pressure, a vast attention turning toward us like a lighthouse beam. The amber light in the walls began to move, flowing inward, converging, and the whispering dead language that had haunted my cellar for five years rose into something almost like speech.

*Two who would be one payment,* it said, in a voice made of every voice it had ever swallowed. *The gate accepts.*

"Wait," I said, and my voice rang strangely loud. "Before you start—I want terms."

August looked at me. "*Terms?*"

"You heard me." I squared my shoulders at a wall of ancient light. Five years of haggling with charm-buyers, three years of haggling with Warden inspectors, one lifetime of being small and overlooked and *useful anyway*—it all went into my spine. "You take memories. You take love. Fine. But you take them *evenly*. Half from each of us. And you leave the rest alone. No taking the years before him, no taking my mother's face, no taking—" my voice caught— "no taking the fact that we *chose*. You can have what we remember. You don't get what we are."

Silence. Long enough that I started composing the eulogy for my own dignity.

Then the light rippled, and I swear on my mother's grave the gate sounded *amused*.

*Negotiated,* it said. *Small keeper. Fierce keeper. As the first covenantor never was.*

"Good," I said. "Then let's—"

It took the first memory before I finished the sentence.

### AUGUST

I have read descriptions of drowning. I have read descriptions of burning. Neither prepared me.

The first thing the gate took was small—a mercy, maybe, or an appetizer. A Tuesday. Some unremarkable Tuesday when Lena and I shared an umbrella and she complained about the rain in a way that made me love her more. I felt it go the way you feel a page torn from a book you've read a hundred times: the story continued, but there was a ragged edge where something used to be, and my mind kept reaching for the missing paragraph and finding only smooth nothing.

"No," I heard myself say. "No, wait, I wasn't ready—"

*Readiness is not required,* the gate said. *Only willingness. You gave it. I am collecting.*

And then it stopped being gentle.

Centuries poured in.

That was the part nobody warned us about—the trade wasn't just *out*, it was *in*. For every memory the gate stripped from us, it pressed its own hoard into the vacancy: the Sundering from the inside, the screaming of a world torn along its seam, ten thousand years of watching, of holding, of *waiting*. I saw the first covenantor build the gate with hands that shook. I saw her seal it with a name she refused to speak again. I saw wars on both sides, migrations of creatures beautiful and terrible, children born in the borderlands who belonged to neither realm and both. I saw everything, all at once, forever—

—and through all of it, one anchor.

"*August.*"

Her voice. Lena's voice, cutting through the flood like a blade through smoke. I couldn't see her anymore; the gate-memory had filled the chamber with centuries, and I was drowning in other people's yesterdays. But her voice found me the way a lighthouse finds a ship.

"*Say my name.*"

"Lena," I gasped. "Lena Pemberton. Ashfall City. Charm stall, corner of Belltower Row, she overcharges tourists and undercharges orphans and she—she—" Another page tore free. I felt it go: our second meeting, the one where she'd pretended not to recognize me and failed miserably. Gone. Smooth nothing. "She has green eyes," I said desperately, clinging. "Green eyes. She bites her lip when she's lying. She lied about liking my coat."

*I can give you a thousand coats,* the gate murmured, almost pitying. *Names are heavier than coats, ember-child. Names are how mortals cheat eternity.*

"Then I'll cheat," I said.

And I did. I cheated. I took the name *August*—my own, fine, keep it, I barely needed it—and I wrapped it around *Lena* like wire around two poles, and I held on. Every time the gate pried loose another memory—our third date, the night train, the safehouse, her laughing with flour on her cheek—I fed the fire with her name. *Lena. Lena. Lena.* A rosary. A war drum. A rope thrown across a chasm.

Somewhere in the flood, I understood what the original covenantor had feared. Not magic. Not the other realm. *This.* Love between realms burns hotter than either realm can hold, and fear builds gates, and gates demand prices, and the price is always the same: forget each other, and be safe.

Well. I had been safe for twenty-six years before I met her. It was the worst I'd ever felt.

"*Lena,*" I shouted into the centuries. "Whatever you're losing—hold the name! Hold—"

* * *

### LENA

Holding the name was like holding a door shut against a hurricane.

I lost things in pieces. That's the cruelty of it—it didn't take whole memories, it took the *load-bearing parts*. I kept the shape of the night train but lost the reason we were on it. I kept the feeling of August's shoulder against mine in the safehouse but lost the story he'd been telling, the one that made Bramble snort tea through her nose. I kept *warmth* and lost its address.

And the gate-memory kept pouring in to fill the gaps. I was starting to know things no living person knew. I knew the weight of the first covenantor's grief. I knew the color of the sky before the Sundering—there had been a second moon, pale as a fingernail, and when the realms tore apart it fell, and half the old songs about falling stars are really mourning songs for it. I knew the names of dead kings and the mating habits of creatures that no longer existed anywhere but in me now.

Knowledge was flooding in. *Meaning* was draining out.

"Lena!" August's voice, far away, fraying. "Say it back!"

"August," I said. My lips knew the shape even as my mind began to lose the map. "August. Your name is August. You're—you're here. You're with me. We're—we're paying a price. Why are we paying a price?"

*Because you love each other,* said something deep in me that was not the gate. Something the gate could not reach, because I had negotiated for it, because I had drawn the line myself: *what we are stays.*

Oh, I thought, through the roaring. Oh, that's what I bought.

The gate took our first kiss. I felt it leave—an entire evening folded up and pulled away like a splinter—but the *want* stayed. The pull. My body turned toward his voice in the dark like a compass needle that had forgotten north but remembered the magnet.

The gate took the ledger—the moment I opened it and saw his century-old inked face. But it left the vertigo, the certainty that the past had been waiting for him, and left me knowing, without knowing how, that *he was the one the prophecy meant.*

The gate took the siege. Took Bramble bleeding, took Odile's prayer, took Sera's duel above us—all of it, the whole terrible night—but it left the knowledge that people we loved were holding a door open upstairs, and that we owed them a world.

"August," I said again, and now I was crying, and the tears felt important, like they were carrying something out of me that needed to leave before it was taken instead. "August, I think I'm forgetting why. I'm forgetting *why* and I don't care, do you understand? I don't need the why. I'd choose you blank. I'd choose you empty. I'd walk out of here remembering nothing but your name and I would still—"

The gate took the sentence.

But it didn't take what came after it, because what came after it wasn't a memory. It was a decision, made fresh, in real time, by whatever was left of me: *I would still.*

* * *

### AUGUST

Here is what I learned inside the flood, in the last minutes before there was no more *me* to learn anything:

Memory is not where love lives.

The gate had taken nearly everything by then. Our history lay around us like ash—pages burned, paragraphs gone smooth. I couldn't have told you how we met. I couldn't have told you her mother's name, or mine, or what city we'd fled, or why my chest hurt when I thought the word *warden*. The centuries had filled me so full of other people's pasts that my own had washed away like chalk in rain.

But when the gate reached for the deepest vault—for the love itself, the thing it had actually been promised—it hit the wall I'd built.

*Lena. Lena. Lena.*

Ten thousand repetitions, laid down like brick while the flood raged. The gate could burn every page of the book, but I had written her name on the *cover*, and the cover was load-bearing, and somewhere in the wreckage of my mind a stubborn, oyster-daring fool stood in front of that cover with his arms out and said: *no further.*

*You are cheating,* the gate said. There was wonder in it. Ten thousand years of watching, and I had managed to surprise it.

"Yes," I said.

*The covenantor did not cheat. The covenantor paid in full and asked to forget faster.*

"The covenantor was a coward," I said, and meant it with my whole hollowed-out heart. "Love isn't supposed to be *safe*. It's supposed to be *kept*. There's a difference."

The gate went still. All that light, all that hunger, paused—and in the pause I heard, very faintly, from very far above, a sound like a bell and a battle and a woman named Sera buying us time with her sword.

Then, deeper than the pause, I heard Lena.

Not her voice. Her *name*, coming back down the wire I'd strung—she'd been doing the same thing, wrapping herself in *August, August, August*, and the two wires had touched, and current ran between them, and for one impossible moment the flood parted and I *saw* her.

She was on her knees. So was I. Our bound hands were still bound—the cord glowed like a coal—and her face was streaked with tears and absolutely, ferociously *defiant*, and she was the most beautiful thing in ten thousand years of gate-memory, and I did not remember a single moment of how I knew that.

"Hi," I said.

"Hi," she said. "Do I know you?"

"No," I said, grinning like an idiot, crying like one too. "But I think I'd like to."

* * *

### LENA

The last thing the gate took was the biggest thing, and it took it gently, the way you take a splinter from a child.

I felt our whole story lift out of me at once—not torn, *lifted*, every page at once, the charm stall and the prophecy and the canal market and the scar-field and the letters and the siege, the entire architecture of us, removed whole. And in the space it left, the gate poured the final gift: completion. The rite closing. The realms knitting shut above us like a wound healing in fast-forward. Somewhere far away, four sigils flared on four heirs, and a severance-engine died mid-scream, and the Sundering ended a hundred years late.

We had done it. I knew we had done it the way you know you've slept: by what was missing.

What was missing was everything.

What remained was a name on a cover. *Lena*, written in someone's handwriting, in a book whose pages were ash. And a hand in mine. Warm. Human warm. Pulse-and-stubborn-heart warm.

The light dimmed to embers. The centuries receded, settling into their shelves. And the gate—the gate that had watched for ten thousand years, that had been built by a coward and completed by two fools who refused to pay alone—did something it had never done before.

It let go softly.

*Kept,* it whispered, in a voice like a banked fire. *Not erased. Kept. In the deep pages. What is given freely in defiance cannot be destroyed—only stored. Sleep, small fierce keeper. Sleep, ember-child. Perhaps someday I will find a way to give it back.*

And then there was a stair, going up, and darkness behind us, and a hand in mine that fit like an answer to a question I couldn't remember asking.

We climbed.

At the top, light. Faces—strangers, all of them, a blue-haired smuggler with bandages, a nun with soot on her habit, a warden with blood on her sword—and every single one of them was crying, and I didn't know their names, and my chest ached looking at them like a letter I couldn't read.

The man beside me—August, my mind supplied, *his name is August, hold onto it, hold onto it*—looked at me with an expression I recognized from nowhere and everywhere.

"Do I know you?" he asked quietly.

"No," I said. My hand tightened on his, entirely of its own accord. "But ask me again tomorrow."

Above us, through the broken ceiling of my—of *someone's*—shop, dawn was coming up over Ashfall City, and the second moon, the fallen one, the one I now remembered dying a hundred years ago, was rising whole over the rooftops.

The gate had kept more than our memories.

It had kept a promise.
