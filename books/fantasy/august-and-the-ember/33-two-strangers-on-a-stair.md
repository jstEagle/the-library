## 33. Two Strangers on a Stair

The stairs were the last thing I remembered clearly, and even that memory is suspect now, because I have since learned that memory is a liar with excellent posture.

There are thirteen steps between my cellar and my shop. I know this the way you know your own heartbeat—by counting it in the dark, over years, when sleep won't come and the floorboards creak their old complaints. Thirteen steps. On the way down, five days ago or five centuries ago, I had counted them out of habit, August's hand in mine, his palm sweating against my palm, both of us pretending we weren't terrified.

On the way up, I did not count them at all.

I want to tell you I emerged from the threshold like a heroine in one of Bramble's smuggled ballads—triumphant, singed, holding aloft some token of victory. What actually happened was considerably less dignified. The door at the top of the stairs opened onto my shop, which smelled of dust and dried lavender and the particular staleness of a room left empty too long, and I stood in the doorway blinking like an owl dragged into daylight, holding a stranger's hand.

Because that's what he was. A stranger.

He was tall. He had dark hair going grey at the temples, though he couldn't have been older than thirty-five. He had a scar through his left eyebrow that I found myself staring at with an emotion I can only describe as homesickness, except I'd never been to whatever place that scar was from. He was looking at me the same way.

"Hello," he said.

"Hello," I said.

And then, because I am Lena Pemberton, charm-seller, gate-keeper, woman who once talked her way past three Wardens with nothing but a forged permit and a convincing limp: "I'm sorry, do I know you?"

His face did something complicated. It's hard to describe a face doing something complicated, but imagine a man watching his house burn down while also being handed the deed to a better one, and you'll have the general shape of it.

"I don't think so," he said slowly. "But I feel like I should."

"Yes," I said. "That."

* * *

Let me back up. Or rather, let me try to back up, which is a different thing entirely, because backing up requires somewhere to stand, and most of where I used to stand is gone.

The rite worked. That much I know the way you know a storm passed—you weren't conscious for the worst of it, but the trees are down and the gutters are full and there's a strange clean smell to everything. The gate is sealed. Odile told me this, later, in her careful nun's voice, her hands folded like she was praying for patience with me specifically. The severance-engine is dead. The High Warden's army broke apart like a bad argument. The Accords are falling; Bramble says there are already people painting over the prohibition notices in the canal district, and half of them are doing it badly on purpose, as art.

The price was paid. Four heirs, one covenant, and a bill that came due in memories and love—and August stepped forward to pay it alone, and I bound our hands together and said *no*, we do this together or not at all, and the gate, being ancient and bureaucratic in the way of all ancient things, apparently accepted a split payment.

What it took from me, I cannot itemize. That's rather the point of taking something from someone—they don't leave you a receipt. But I've been doing inventory. It's a grim little game, sitting in my shop with tea going cold, poking at the holes in myself like a tongue probing a missing tooth.

I remember my mother's face. Good. I remember how to make every charm in my catalogue, and several that aren't in the catalogue. I remember Sera Voss's childhood nickname for me—I don't remember why she had a nickname for me, or when we stopped using them, but the word *Lenny* sits in my mouth like a stone from someone else's river. I remember loving someone. I know this because the shape of the love is still there, the way a furniture's shadow stays on the carpet after the furniture is sold—the outline of devotion, worn smooth by years of use, fitted to no one I currently know.

Except.

Except he keeps walking through that outline. And it fits him exactly.

* * *

The others gave us space, which was kind of them, and also suspiciously coordinated, the way kindness among conspirators always is. Bramble pressed something into my hand before she left—a small tin of salve, "for the headache you're about to have"—and squeezed my arm and said, "Take your time. The shop's not going anywhere." Then she looked at August, and then at me, and her throat worked around something she decided not to say, and she went.

Odile said a blessing over us both. I didn't understand a word of it, which felt appropriate.

Sera lingered longest. She stood in the doorway of my shop—my shop, which she had presumably spent years trying to find reasons to raid—with her Warden's coat hanging off her like a skin she'd outgrown, and she looked at me with an expression I couldn't read, and she said, "You did it, Lenny." And then she flinched, like the name had come out without permission, and left before either of us could make it strange.

I didn't ask her who Lenny was. Some doors you don't open until you've found the key, and some keys you don't go looking for until you've slept.

So it was just the two of us, in a shop full of charms neither of us fully understood, on a stair that led up from a sealed door that hummed behind us like a cat that finally got fed.

We sat on the steps. It seemed important not to go too far from each other, though neither of us said so. He sat two steps below me, which put his head roughly level with my shoulder, and after a while—not immediately, because we were strangers, and strangers don't do this—he leaned, very slightly, so that his shoulder rested against my knee.

Neither of us moved away.

"This is going to sound mad," he said.

"Everything sounds mad today. You'll have to be more specific."

"I keep almost saying things to you." He turned his head, and the scarred eyebrow caught the light from the shop above. "Like—there are sentences in me that start with your name. Whole paragraphs. And then I get to the part where I'd need to actually remember something, and it's—" he made a gesture, opening his hand like a book whose pages had been torn out. "Smoke. There's just smoke."

"I know," I said. "I have smoke too."

"Do you?" He sounded relieved, which was absurd, and touching, and I found myself smiling at him before I'd authorized the smile. "What kind?"

"The kind where I know I loved somebody." I looked at my hands. They had burn scars across the knuckles I didn't remember earning. "The love's still here. It's just—unaddressed. Like a letter with the direction burned off."

He was quiet for a moment. When he spoke again, his voice had gone rough. "Was it me? Do you think? The somebody?"

"I don't know," I said honestly. "Does it matter?"

"Yes," he said, and then, softer, "no. I don't know. Both. It matters enormously and it doesn't matter at all, because whoever they were, they taught me—" he stopped. Started again. "There's a way I feel when you talk. Like hearing a song from the next room over. I can't place it. But I know all the words anyway."

* * *

We went upstairs eventually, because stairs are no place to have a life-altering conversation, and made tea, because I am Ashfall-born and Ashfall-born solve everything with tea first and feelings second, if at all.

He knew where the cups were.

That's the thing I keep circling back to, the thing I can't stop poking at. He reached past me—past me, a stranger, in my own kitchen—and took down the chipped blue cup from the third hook, the one I always reach for, the one with the hairline crack shaped like a river delta. He set it beside the teapot without thinking. Then he froze, cup in hand, staring at it like it had spoken.

"I knew that," he said. "I knew where that was."

"You did," I agreed.

"Is it mine?"

"It's mine." I hesitated. "Or it was. It might be ours now. I genuinely don't know how ownership works anymore."

He laughed. It was a good laugh—startled out of him, a bit crooked, the laugh of a man surprised by his own mouth. And the sound of it went through me like a bell struck in an empty church, resonating in places that had been swept and dusted and left deliberately bare. My chest ached. Not metaphorically. There was an actual physical ache, right behind the sternum, like a bruise on the inside.

"That hurt you," he said. Not a question. He'd seen me flinch.

"A little."

"Me too." He set the cup down with exaggerated care, as if it might detonate. "Every time something almost comes back, it's like—grabbing a hot pan. You learn not to reach. But I keep reaching."

"Why?"

He considered this with the seriousness of a scholar. "Because you're worth burning for," he said, and then went scarlet, and added hastily, "That sounded less deranged in my head."

"It didn't," I said. "But I liked it anyway."

* * *

Here is what we pieced together, over that first pot of tea and the second and the pot after that, trading fragments like two survivors of the same shipwreck comparing flotsam:

His name is August. He remembers his name, and his grandmother, and her dying words—which he recited to me in a low voice, haltingly, like scripture in a language he only half speaks. Something about an ember returning. Something about finding the keeper. He does not remember how long he searched for me, or what he felt when he found me, or anything at all about the weeks between his grandmother's death and the moment he walked into my stall and bought a luck-charm he probably didn't need.

My name is Lena Pemberton. I remember the shop, the cellar, the ledger, the gate. I remember guarding something for five years with my heart in my teeth. I do not remember why I started, or what I was afraid of at the beginning, or the face of the person the ledger promised me. I opened the ledger this morning—it sits on my kitchen table now, because I no longer trust it in the cellar—and the pages are blank. All of them. Whatever it recorded, it gave to the gate, or the gate took, and now it's just a book-shaped absence with a leather cover.

Bramble, Odile, Sera. I know their names and their faces and the fact that I trust them, bone-deep, the way you trust your own hands. I don't know why. When I asked Odile, she got a look on her face like a woman deciding how much truth a convalescent can bear, and said, "You'll remember when you're ready," which is what people say when they mean *you won't remember, but I love you too much to say so.*

The four of them remember everything. This is the cruelest arithmetic of the whole arrangement, and I've had days to sit with it. The gate took its payment from two of us, but the other two paid in a different coin: they carry the whole story alone. Every argument, every near-kiss, every joke that landed and every one that didn't, every time August and I were brave or foolish or tender—they hold all of it, and we hold none of it, and they have to look at us every day and pretend that doesn't hollow them out.

Sera handles it by staying away. Odile handles it by praying. Bramble handles it by showing up at the shop every morning with pastries and talking loudly enough to fill any silence where grief might pool.

August handles it by looking at me.

That's what I can't get past. That's the thing that keeps me awake at night, in a bed that smells faintly of someone else's soap, listening to him breathe in the room across the hall—we've taken to sleeping under the same roof, because being apart feels like drowning and neither of us could explain why, so we didn't try. He looks at me the way you look at a fire in winter. From a respectful distance. With helpless longing. Like warmth is a thing that has happened to him before and might happen again if he stands very still and doesn't scare it off.

A stranger who feels like home. That's the phrase I keep coming back to, and I hate it, because it's true, and true things about your own devastation should at least have the decency to be original.

* * *

It was evening, the fourth day, when it happened on the stairs again.

We'd fallen into routines with alarming speed—tea in the mornings, inventory in the afternoons (he's better with numbers than I am, and neither of us knows why), Bramble's pastries, Odile's visits, the slow unspooling of two lives braiding themselves back together along grooves that already existed, worn deep by some previous water. We bickered about the teapot. We finished each other's—well. He starts sentences and I end them, and we both stop afterward, staring, like people who've heard a ghost clear its throat.

That evening we were carrying a crate of stock down to the cellar—jointly, because he refuses to let me lift heavy things, and I refuse to ask why he refuses, because the answer is written all over his face in a language I can't read but can feel.

Halfway down, he stopped.

"What?" I said.

"I don't know." He was frowning at the steps. "I've been counting these. Thirteen. I don't know why I count them."

"Thirteen," I confirmed. "I count them too."

"When did you start?"

"I don't remember."

He laughed—that startled crooked laugh—but it died quickly, and he stood there in the dimness with the crate in his arms, and the humming of the sealed door below us filled the silence like held breath.

"Lena," he said.

"Yes."

"Can I tell you something mad?"

"You've told me eleven mad things today. Twelve would be practically traditional."

"I dream about you." His voice had gone quiet. "Not—look, not like that. Or yes, like that, sometimes, I'm being honest, sue me. But mostly I dream about a dark place, and you're laughing in it. Holding my hand. And I wake up and my hand hurts from gripping something that isn't there." He looked at me then, and his eyes were wet, and he didn't wipe them, and I loved him—I knew that suddenly and completely, the way you know a door is locked, not by examining it but by walking into it. "Whoever you were to me," he said, "I'd like to be that again. Even if I have to start from nothing. Even if I have to earn it like a stranger would."

The crate was between us. I set it down on the step. It seemed important to have my hands free.

"You don't have to earn it," I said. "Whatever it was, it wasn't earned. I'm fairly sure of that. I'm fairly sure it was a disaster from start to finish, honestly—two idiots and a prophecy and a door that eats memories. Nobody earns that. You survive it, and then you—" I stopped. My throat had closed.

"Then you?" he prompted gently.

"Then you choose it," I said. "Again. On purpose. With the receipts."

He came up one step. Just one. It put us nearly eye to eye, close enough that I could see the grey in his temple and the exhaustion under his eyes and the terrible hopeful stubbornness that I suspect has always been his defining feature, whatever else the gate stripped away.

"Is that an offer?" he asked.

"It's a first date," I said, and my voice cracked on the word *first*, and he heard it, and his breath caught, and for one suspended moment we stood there on the thirteenth step with five years of smoke between us and something ember-bright underneath, patient, waiting, refusing to die.

"Tomorrow," he said. "Seven o'clock. I'll bring flowers. I don't know what kind you like yet, but I intend to find out."

"Lilies," I said instantly, and then frowned. "Why did I say lilies?"

"I don't know," he said softly. "But I believe you."

And downstairs, behind a door that no longer needed guarding, something hummed—contented, or amused, or perhaps just keeping time, like a heart that had learned to beat for two.
