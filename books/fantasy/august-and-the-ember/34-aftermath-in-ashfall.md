## 34. Aftermath in Ashfall

The morning the Accords fell, I was hungover on Odile's good communion wine and lying face-down on a pew, which is the most honest prayer I've ever offered anybody.

I heard it before I understood it: bells. Not alarm bells—Ashfall had enough of those to last a century—but the deep, slow bells of Belltower Row, rung in the old pattern that nobody under sixty had ever heard rung for anything but coronations and funerals. Then shouting. Then cheering, which in Ashfall is a sound like a building collapsing, except joyful.

Odile shook my shoulder. "Get up," she said. "They're tearing down the posting boards."

"Good," I said into the wood. "Those boards were ugly."

"They're tearing them down because the warrants are being burned. All of them. The High Warden's order fell last night—the whole Order, dissolved and reformed. Sera's doing." A pause, and when she spoke again her voice had gone strange and soft. "Ours, too. The sisters voted this morning. We're not renouncing anymore. We're *guarding*."

I lifted my head off the pew and looked at her, this nun who'd thrown a chair at August six weeks ago for the crime of existing near her with magic in his blood, and I saw she was crying without any shame about it at all.

"Well," I said, because somebody in every room has to be the one who doesn't cry, and it's been me since I was nine years old. "Somebody should tell Lena before she reads it off a wall like everybody else."

---

Here's what nobody tells you about the end of a war: it's mostly paperwork.

The Accords didn't fall in a single dramatic stroke. They fell the way a rotten tooth comes out—a push, a crack, a surprising amount of blood, and then everyone standing around with their tongues in the gap going *huh*. Writs of severance revoked. Registry lists unsealed. The charm-stalls along Copperlane that had operated behind false bottoms and lying smiles for five years simply... opened their shutters all the way, one morning, and sold love-charms in daylight like it was nothing. Like it had always been nothing.

It was not nothing. I watched an old hedge-witch stand in front of her own stall for ten full minutes, hands shaking too hard to make change, because a customer asked for a luck-knot and didn't whisper. Didn't look over his shoulder. Just asked, out loud, in the street, like a person asking for bread.

She gave him the knot free. He tried to pay three times. She wouldn't take it. I bought a protection sigil off her afterward just so she'd have coin in hand and something to do with her fingers, and if she charged me double, well. Worth it.

Me, I celebrated properly: I went back to the canal district, walked into the Brinehouse—which is not a house and barely brine, it's a smuggling den, keep up—and announced to the room that I was retired from running contraband because contraband no longer existed. Half the room cheered. The other half looked genuinely bereft, the way people do when you tell them the thing they loved hating is gone.

"You can't retire," said Doss, who fences stolen wardens' badges and has the moral compass of a weathervane. "Smuggling's your whole personality."

"My whole personality," I told him, "is *surviving*, and as of Tuesday, surviving got easier." I flipped him a copper for the sentiment and walked out into the sun like a woman with a future, which, for the first time in my adult life, I technically was.

Then I went to Pemberton Cellar, because there was a shop half-burned and two people half-alive, and futures are heavy things to carry alone.

* * *

You have to understand what the siege did to that street.

Pemberton Lane used to be nothing—a crooked little lane behind the chandler's, smelling of tallow and rain, with Lena's charm stall tucked at the end like a secret kept so long even the secret forgot it. After the army came through, it was a scar. Three buildings gutted. The cobbles torn up where the severance-engine's vanguard had dragged their machines. Scorch marks on the doorframe of the shop in the shape of a hand, which I try not to look at, and which Lena looks at constantly, and which neither of us mentions.

And beneath it all, under the cellar, behind the door that only opens for four names now instead of none—the gate. Sealed. Whole. Humming so quietly you'd miss it unless you knew to listen, and I know to listen, because I spent twenty years listening for Wardens and the habit hasn't died yet. It hums like a hive in winter. Contented. Fed.

Fed on *them*. That's the part I can't say out loud, so I say everything else instead.

Lena and August came back up those stairs eleven days after the reforging, holding hands, walking carefully like people carrying water they couldn't set down. Alive. Whole. And missing—I want to say *pieces*, but pieces sounds like damage, and what they're missing isn't damage exactly, it's more like... pages. Whole chapters torn out clean, no ragged edges, no memory of the tearing.

August remembered how to get home and not why he'd come. Lena remembered every charm she'd ever made and not the winter she'd spent learning to make them, or the person who'd sat on her cellar steps while she failed at it, laughing. They remembered each other's *names*. They just didn't remember each other.

I've seen a lot of terrible things. I grew up between two worlds that both wanted me gone, so believe me when I say I've developed a professional eye for grief. What those two had wasn't grief. Grief knows what it lost. This was worse. This was two people standing in an empty room, knowing the furniture had been there, feeling the shape of it in the floorboards, and having no idea what color it had been.

So. We rebuilt the shop around them. It was the only thing any of us could think to do.

* * *

Sera arrived first, three days after the Accords formally died, still wearing the grey of a Warden though the Order had reformed around her overnight into something new—Guardians, they're calling themselves, which is either better or just a prettier word, ask me again in ten years. She stood in the ruined doorway of the shop with her arms crossed and said, "This roof is structurally compromised," which is Sera for *hello, I missed you, I nearly died for you, please let me help.*

"It's structurally *honest*," I said. "There's a difference."

"I'm sending carpenters."

"We don't have money for carpenters."

"The Guardians owe this street a debt." Her jaw did the thing it does when she's swallowing something large and bitter. "Consider it paid in lumber."

That's how it started. Sera sent crews; Odile sent sisters, who turned out to be terrifyingly efficient at restoration once you got past the vows-of-silence thing, which lasted about a day and a half before Sister Maren caught me charming mortar to set faster and demanded I teach her. The ledger—Lena's impossible, self-writing, century-old guardian ledger—sat open on the counter the whole time, and I swear on my mother's locket it *supervised*. Every time we did something right, a small line appeared in its margins. Every time Sister Pell dropped a beam on Doss's foot, the ink wrote, in careful script: *again.*

Nobody talked about whether the ledger was supposed to be doing that. Nobody wanted to jinx it. When a book has watched you almost die and then starts making jokes, you let it make jokes.

We pulled the scorched doorframe out on the fourth day. Lena stood in the street watching us lever it loose, and when it came free she reached out and put her palm flat against the burn-mark—that hand-shaped shadow—and held it there for a long moment, eyes closed, like she was listening for something on the other side of the wood.

"Do you remember?" I asked her. Quietly. Stupidly. I'd sworn I wouldn't ask.

"No," she said. Opened her eyes. Smiled at me with the specific steadiness of someone who has decided, daily and deliberately, to keep going. "But my hands do. Isn't that strange? I touched this door every morning for five years and my hands still know the grain of it better than my head does."

"Yeah," I said, thinking of a locket I'd worn against my heart for twenty years through smuggler's holds and Warden cells, whose weight I knew better than my mother's face. "Not strange at all."

* * *

August, meanwhile, fixed things.

That's the strange grace note in all of it—healing magic, ember-blood, the boy from a prophecy who broke a century of law by existing, and what he *wanted*, more than anything, was to repair things. Chairs. Shutters. The little brass bell above the shop door, melted half-shut in the fire, which he worked at for two evenings straight with his sleeves rolled and his tongue between his teeth until it rang again, clear and bright, and the whole street stopped to listen.

He didn't remember ringing it the first time. He didn't remember that Lena had bought it at a market the week before they met, or that she'd told him later—it was in one of the letters, the ones he wrote goodbye and never finished, the ones I've read and will deny reading—that the bell's voice was the first thing about the shop he'd ever loved.

But when it rang, he stood under it with his head tipped back and his eyes wet, and he said, "I don't know why that sound makes me happy. It just does. Is that mad?"

"No," said Lena, from across the room, not looking up from her charms. "It's not mad."

And the two of them stood there in the dust and the hammering, strangers under a bell, both crying a little, neither looking at the other, and I had to go outside and have a smoke I didn't even want because somebody in this operation has to maintain emotional containment and apparently it's me forever.

* * *

Here's a thing I learned rebuilding a shop with found family: grief is a group project whether you enroll or not.

Odile came every day she could get away from Greyhollow, and she never once pushed Lena or August to talk about the gate. Instead she cooked. Enormous, monastic, aggressively nourishing meals in the half-finished kitchen, feeding masons and sisters and smugglers and one extremely confused ex-Warden commander with the serene authority of a woman who has decided that bodies must be fed and will brook no argument on the matter.

Sera came on her rest days, out of uniform, which took some getting used to—she owns *one* civilian coat and it's forty years out of fashion and she wears it like armor anyway. She did the structural work nobody else could do, because it turns out Warden training includes engineering, and by the second week she'd rebuilt the cellar stairs wider than before. "For carrying things," she said. We all understood she meant *in case they ever need to carry each other down again.* We built it anyway. You build the stairs.

Doss showed up once, looked at the whole scene—nuns sanding banisters, a Guardian hauling timber, a half-fae smuggler teaching a prophecy-boy to glaze windows—and said, "Bramble Quill, you have become *respectable*, and I need to sit down," and then he stayed three weeks and turned out to be the best plasterer in Ashfall, the absolute traitor.

And in the middle of it, always, Lena and August. Working alongside us. Quiet. Careful. Watching each other the way you watch weather—not staring, just aware, always aware, tracking without meaning to. He'd reach for a tool and she'd already be handing it to him. She'd hum while she worked and he'd pick up the tune two bars in, a song neither of them could name. Once I saw him catch her when she slipped on a wet plank, fast as reflex, his arm around her waist before either of them had decided anything—and they stood there a beat too long, and then let go too carefully, and went back to work, and the whole crew pretended very loudly to be busy.

"That's going to kill them," Odile murmured beside me, kneading bread.

"Or save them," I said.

"Both?"

"Both," I agreed. "It's usually both."

* * *

The shop reopened on a grey-gold morning six weeks after the siege, and I'm telling you honestly: I have been to coronations with less ceremony.

Lena had rebuilt her charm stall proper—no more false bottom, no more hidden drawer, no more lying sign. The new sign over the door read PEMBERTON'S, and underneath, smaller, in August's handwriting because he'd insisted: *charms, remedies, & odd jobs.* The bell rang when the first customer came in—an actual customer, a nervous young man with a hedge-talent he'd hidden his whole life, come to ask if the rumors were true, if it was really legal now, if he could really stop hiding.

Lena took his hands across the counter and said, "You never had to hide here. But yes. It's legal. Welcome."

I was restocking shelf-ends and pretending to be busy and absolutely not crying, and then August appeared at my elbow holding two cups of tea and handed me one without a word, and I thought: *he doesn't remember that I take it with honey, and yet here's honey in it.* His hands remembered too. Everybody's hands remembered. The whole world was walking around full of hands that remembered what heads had lost, and if that's not a kind of hope, I don't know what is.

That evening, after close, the five of us sat on the repaired back steps—me, Sera, Odile, and the two quiet heroes—with wine and the last of Odile's bread, watching the light go amber over Ashfall. The city glittered below, and somewhere in it, a hundred little lights were flickering on that weren't lamps. Hedge-witches lighting their stalls. Charms glowing openly in windows. Five years of snuffed candles, relit all at once.

"To the Ember Accords," Sera said, raising her cup. "May they rot."

"To the Ember," Odile corrected gently, "which was never theirs to burn."

"To the shop," I said, "because I did most of the work."

And August laughed—really laughed, head back, easy—and Lena looked at him when he did, the way you look at a sound you know from somewhere, a song from another room, and her chest did the thing. I saw it. The ache. The name-shaped hollow working itself raw.

Then she looked away, and topped up his cup, and said, "More?" like it was nothing, like her heart wasn't bruising itself against a memory it couldn't find.

* * *

Later—much later, when the others had gone and I was locking up, because apparently I live there now, nobody discussed it, my cot just *appeared* in the storeroom one day—I heard voices from the cellar stairs.

I shouldn't have listened. I listened.

August's voice, low: "Can I ask you something strange?"

"You've asked me eleven strange things today. I'm keeping count."

"When you say my name. Does it—" a pause, the sound of someone deciding to be brave. "Does it hurt? A little? Like a bruise you can't find?"

A longer silence. Long enough that I stopped breathing on the stairs.

"Yes," Lena said finally. "Every time. Does mine?"

"All the time." Another pause. "Do you think that means something? That we hurt in the same place?"

And Lena—steady, deliberate, choosing, the way she chooses everything now—said:

"I think it means we should find out. Properly. I think—" and here her voice did something I'd never heard it do in all the years I'd known her, something young and terrified and hopeful, "—I think maybe you should take me to dinner. A first one. Since apparently we're starting over."

A laugh from August, wet and wondering. "A first date. Again."

"A first date. Again."

I crept back up the stairs, grinning like an idiot in the dark, and lay down on my smuggler's cot in a rebuilt shop above a humming gate, and thought: *they're going to choose each other all over again, from scratch, with nothing but the ache to guide them.*

And then I thought: *the ledger's due for a new entry.*

Because I'd seen it that afternoon, when I was dusting the counter. A fresh line of ink, still drying, in that careful old script, right beneath the record of the reforging:

*A bond the gate could not erase. Watch what grows from the split root.*

Watch what grows. Well. I'm a smuggler by trade and a survivor by nature, and I know a planted thing when I see one.

Whatever blooms next—we'll be here for it. All of us. Hands remembering.
