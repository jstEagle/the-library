## 35. Things the Body Remembers

Here is what nobody tells you about losing your memory to an ancient interdimensional gate: you don't miss the memories. You can't miss what you can't find. What you miss is the *shape* of them—the way a house misses furniture that's been carried out, leaving pale rectangles on the walls where pictures used to hang.

I have a lot of pale rectangles in me.

For instance: I know I loved Lena Pemberton. I know it the way you know you've been somewhere before when you smell rain on hot stone—not as information but as weather. The gate took most of five years from me, and it took the specific texture of her, the sound of her laugh in the dark of a cellar, the exact weight of her hand in mine on a stair that led down into forever. But it left the ache where all of that used to be, and honestly? The ache is doing a lot of work.

We've been back in Ashfall for four months now. The Accords are ash—literally, in some places; Bramble keeps a framed scrap of the repealed law over the shop door like a hunting trophy—and the city is learning to live with magic the way you learn to live with a limp: carefully, resentfully at first, then one day without noticing.

And every day, Lena and I keep doing things we shouldn't know how to do.

* * *

It starts small. It always starts small.

Tuesday morning, restocking the charm stall—or the charm stall's respectable new incarnation, Pemberton & Co., Purveyors of Fine Enchantments, Licensed, which still makes me snort every time I paint the word LICENSED on anything—we're unpacking a crate of candle-wax from the canal district, and I say, "Careful with the—"

"—blue tinsel, it sticks to everything," she finishes, and we both stop.

The crate sits between us like a confession.

"I was going to say that," I say.

"You did say that."

"No, I mean—" I set down the jar I'm holding. "How did you know I was going to say tinsel?"

Lena looks at me across the crate, and there's a moment where something moves behind her eyes, some quick bright fish surfacing and diving again before I can name it. "Because you said it last week," she says finally. "When the shipment came in. You complained for ten minutes about the tinsel."

"Did I?"

"You did. You called it 'the glitter plague.'" She smiles, and it's a real smile, but it arrives a half-second late, like a letter that's been forwarded through several addresses. "You were very dramatic about it."

"I'm allowed to be dramatic. I died into a door once."

"You didn't die."

"I was gone long enough to count." I toss her a jar; she catches it without looking, which she does every time, which should be impossible, which neither of us mentions anymore because mentioning it makes the silence afterward too loud.

Here's the thing I've started keeping track of, in a little notebook I hide under my mattress like a teenager hiding contraband love letters:

She catches things I throw without looking. Nine times out of ten.

I finish her sentences. Or she finishes mine. Roughly twice a day now, up from once every three days in the first month.

We make tea the same wrong way—both of us warming the pot first, both of us adding honey before the steep instead of after, which everyone else in the shop finds mildly horrifying.

When one of us hums, the other picks up the tune within three notes. Always the same tune. Neither of us knows what it is. Odile says it sounds like a lullaby from the eastern provinces. Sera went very quiet the one time she heard us do it and left the room.

And sometimes—this is the one I don't write down, because writing it down makes it real—sometimes I'll reach for something on a high shelf at the same instant she does, and my arm goes around her instead, and we end up standing there in the half-embrace of two people who both reached for the same jar of pickled moonroot, and neither of us pulls away fast enough, and the whole shop goes quiet around us like it's holding its breath.

Bramble has a betting pool. I'm not supposed to know about the betting pool. Odile is winning.

* * *

Thursday night, closing time. Sera's taken the evening patrol, Odile's at the rebuilt chapel arguing doctrine with anyone who'll hold still, and Bramble announced she had "smuggling-adjacent errands," which everyone pretends is a normal thing to say. So it's just me and Lena and the shop settling into its evening creaks, and I'm sweeping, because apparently I'm the kind of person who sweeps now, and she's behind the counter doing inventory, and we're not talking, and it's the best conversation I've had all day.

That's another entry for the notebook: silence with her fits better than silence with anyone else. Silence with other people has edges. Silence with Lena is a room I've been in before.

"August," she says, without looking up from her ledger. "You're staring."

"I'm observing."

"You're staring *observantly*."

"I contain multitudes." I lean the broom against the shelf. "Question."

"That sounds ominous."

"When you look at me—" I come around the counter, and she watches me come, and there's that flicker again, that fish, that pale rectangle. "What do you see?"

"A man holding a broom badly."

"Lena."

She sets down her pen. For a while she doesn't answer, and outside the window the gaslamps come on along Belltower Row, one by one, the way they do now that the wardens are guardians instead of hunters, the way they do because Sera fought a duel on a rooftop so that they could.

"I see someone who feels like a sentence I already know the ending to," she says quietly. "But I've lost the beginning."

My chest does the thing. The ache-thing. The pale-rectangle-thing.

"Same," I say, which is inadequate, but it's what I've got.

"We could write it down," she says. "What we remember. Compare notes. Like—" she waves a hand, "—witnesses reconstructing an accident."

"Do we want to know what the accident was?"

She's quiet again. Then: "I think I'd rather have the truth than the hole where the truth was. Even if the truth hurts." A pause. A wry tilt of her mouth that I know—I *know*—I've seen before, in some life I can't reach. "Besides. You'd probably be dramatic about it. I'd hate to miss that."

"I died into a door once."

"So you keep saying."

* * *

So we try it. Saturday night, after close, sitting on the floor of the cellar with our backs against the sealed gate—which hums now, low and content, like a cat that has been fed and forgiven—and a bottle of canal-district plum wine between us, and two sheets of paper.

The rule is simple: write down everything you remember about *us*, even the fragments, even the nonsense, especially the nonsense. No comparing until both are done.

I write:

*I remember a book with a face in it. My face. Inked a hundred years ago. I remember being angry about that, or scared, or both.*

*I remember a train going north, and her asleep against my shoulder, and thinking: whatever this costs, pay it.*

*I remember a child with a fever. My hands glowing. Every warden in the province coming for us like we'd lit a beacon. I remember not being sorry.*

*I remember letters. I wrote letters. I don't know what they said. I remember the pen scratching and my heart going like a fist on a locked door.*

*I remember her saying no. To something. To the biggest something. She said no like it was the easiest word in the world.*

Across the cellar, Lena writes for a long time. Longer than me. When we finally trade papers, hers says:

*I remember showing him the gate and being terrified he'd touch it and more terrified he wouldn't.*

*I remember laughing in a safehouse in winter. Real laughter. The kind that hurts after.*

*I remember loving him before I ever kissed him. Before he ever knew. I watched him sleep on a night train and thought: oh no. Oh no, it's you. It was always going to be you.*

*I remember choosing him. Twice. Maybe three times. The number matters and I don't know why.*

*I remember his name meaning something. Not the word—August, the month, the ordinary word—but underneath it. Ember. His name means ember, somewhere below the surface, and I don't know how I know that.*

I read that last line four times. The wine is suddenly very far away.

"My grandmother," I say slowly, "called me that. Before she died. Her dying words, actually—she grabbed my wrist and said, *find the ember-keeper, the gate remembers.* And I thought she was delirious." I look up at Lena. "I found you because of a dead woman's riddle. Did I ever tell you that?"

"You told me," she says. "In chapter— " she stops. Blinks. "I don't know why I almost said that."

"In chapter what?"

"Nothing. Nothing, it's—" She laughs, but it comes out strange, frayed at the edges. "It's like there's a word on the tip of my tongue except it's a whole *story* on the tip of my tongue. About us. Someone telling us. Like we're characters in something."

Above us, faintly, through the floorboards, the shop bell rings—even though the sign says CLOSED, even though Sera has the only other key, even though—

No. Wait. The bell isn't ringing. The bell is *humming*. The same note as the gate behind us. The same note as the tune we keep catching each other humming.

Lena and I look at each other, and then at the gate, and the gate's hum shifts—deepens—settles into something that is unmistakably, impossibly, a rhythm. A pulse. Almost like handwriting.

Almost like ink moving under paper.

"The ledger," we say, at exactly the same time, in exactly the same voice, and then we're both laughing and neither of us knows why, and the gate hums beneath us like it's laughing too, patient as stone, warm as a banked fire, waiting—for what, we don't remember, but waiting the way you wait for someone whose footsteps you'd know anywhere.

Upstairs, on the counter, in the dark, the old guardian ledger falls open all by itself.

Neither of us has touched it in months.

Neither of us needs to, yet, to know—it's writing something new.
