## 37. Choosing Again

### August

There is a particular kind of terror in asking a woman out for the second time when she doesn't remember the first.

I'd know. I've been standing outside Pemberton Charms for twenty minutes rehearsing, and the rehearsal has gone through eleven drafts, all of them bad. Draft one was *Lena, would you like to get dinner sometime*, which sounded like I was asking to borrow a ladder. Draft four involved a joke about charms that I abandoned when I realized I couldn't remember how the joke went. Draft nine was just her name, said out loud to an empty street, which made a passing cat look at me with what I can only describe as pity.

The trouble is this: I know everything about her, and I know nothing about her, and both of those are true at once, and neither of them helps me at all.

I know she takes her tea with too much honey because Bramble complained about it for a week after we moved into the shop's upstairs rooms. I know she hums when she wards the door at night — three notes, low, like a lullaby someone taught her before she could talk. I know that when she thinks no one is watching, she touches the left side of her chest, right over the sternum, quick and unconscious, the way you'd check a wound that had already healed. I know I do the same thing. We've caught each other doing it twice now, and both times we looked away like children caught stealing.

I know that the gate took five years of my memories and gave them to itself, and it took more of hers, and what's left between us is a shape where something used to be. I know her name makes my ribs ache. I know that when she laughs, some animal part of me relaxes so completely it's almost embarrassing, like a dog that had been braced for a blow its whole life finally being told the storm passed.

What I don't know is whether any of that means anything to *her*. Whether the ache is mutual or whether I'm a stranger who keeps staring. Whether she lies awake cataloguing my gestures the way I catalogue hers, or whether she sleeps fine, dreamless, free of me.

Bramble says ask her. Odile says ask her. Sera said, in her flat warden way, "You survived a siege together. Asking her to dinner will not kill you," which from Sera is practically a love sonnet.

So: draft twelve. No jokes. No speeches. Just the question, plain as bread.

I knock on the shop door even though it's open, because knocking gives me one extra second to not flee.

She looks up from behind the counter, where she's sorting dried lavender into paper twists, and the lamplight does something unfair to her face, and every draft I prepared evaporates.

"August." She smiles, and my chest does the thing. The ache-thing. "You're hovering."

"I'm considering," I say. "There's a difference. Hovering is passive. This is active, strategic consideration."

"Uh-huh." She ties off a twist of lavender. "Consider out loud, then. You're making the lavender nervous."

I take a breath. Here goes everything, which is to say, here goes very little, which is to say, here goes the only thing that has ever mattered.

"Lena Pemberton. Would you like to go on a date with me? A first date. With me. Tonight. Or another night. Whichever. I'm flexible on the night. I'm less flexible on the 'with me' part, obviously, since I'm the one asking, but —"

"Yes."

"— and I thought maybe the canal market, because Bramble says the fried dough there is — wait." I stop. "Yes?"

"Yes." She sets down the lavender. Her ears have gone pink, which I note with the satisfaction of a man collecting evidence. "Yes, August. A first date. Tonight. The canal market. Fried dough."

"You didn't let me finish being terrible at it."

"I did, actually. That was the whole performance." But she's smiling, really smiling, the kind that crinkles the corner of her eyes, and she comes around the counter and stands close enough that I can smell the honey-and-smoke smell of her that I couldn't name if you paid me and would recognize blindfolded in a crowd of thousands. "For the record," she says, quieter, "I was going to ask you first. Tomorrow. I had a whole plan. There was going to be a charm involved."

"A charm?"

"A luck charm. Except I was going to enchant it so it glowed when you held it, and then act surprised, and say something devastatingly smooth about fate."

"That's cheating."

"That's *craft*," she corrects. "Fate owes us. I figured I'd collect."

And there it is again — the word *fate*, dropped casual as a coin, and the ache flares hot under my sternum, and I watch her hand twitch toward her own chest and stop herself. We stand there in the lavender-smelling shop, two people who used to be the most important thing in the world to each other, negotiating the terms of a first date like it's the first thing that ever happened between us.

Maybe it is. Maybe that's the point.

"So," I say. "Seven o'clock?"

"Seven o'clock." She hesitates, then adds, "August? Don't be late. I hear the guy's terrible about that."

"I don't know him," I say, "but I'll warn him."

### Lena

Here is what nobody tells you about starting over with someone: it's exactly as terrifying as starting over with anyone, except your body keeps insisting it isn't starting over at all.

My hands knew his before I did. That's the part that undoes me. Last week he handed me a hammer in the cellar while we repaired the shelf the siege cracked, and our fingers overlapped on the handle, and my pulse didn't spike the way it does with strangers — it *settled*, dropped into some deep familiar rhythm, like a lock recognizing its key. My skin has a file on August Vane. My heart keeps referencing documents that were burned.

So tonight, getting ready for a first date, I catch myself doing absurd things. Checking the mirror three times. Wearing the blue dress, which Bramble swears I bought "for no reason whatsoever" six months ago, though Bramble also swears she saw nothing suspicious about a woman buying a dress in a color that matches a certain man's eyes, so Bramble's testimony is worthless. I braid my hair the way my hands insist on braiding it — over the left shoulder — and only afterward wonder why the left shoulder, why that specific pattern, who taught my fingers that.

The gate, probably. Or whatever the gate left behind in us. The ledger's last page said we made something new, something it couldn't erase, and I believe it the way I believe fire is hot: theoretically, and then constantly, in practice, whenever he walks into a room.

He's waiting for me at the foot of the stairs when I come down, and he's wearing the grey coat, and he's holding flowers — actual flowers, slightly crushed, clearly clutched too hard during a long internal argument — and when he sees me he goes still in a way that makes my whole traitor body go warm.

"You look —" He stops. Restarts. "Hi."

"Hi yourself." I take the flowers. They're ember-lilies, which grow wild along the canal and are technically weeds and cost nothing, which means he picked them himself, which means everything. "These are dying."

"They were dying *with dignity* until about ten minutes ago."

"I'm putting them in water immediately and never mentioning this again."

"Thank you." He offers his arm, formal as a courtier, and there's something in the gesture — old-fashioned, deliberate, chosen — that makes my throat tight. I take it. His sleeve is warm under my palm. Of course it is. Everything about him is warm. That was always —

That was always *something*. Something I don't remember. Something my chest remembers for me.

The canal district at dusk is all gold water and lantern-light and the smell of fried dough, which Bramble has described to us with religious fervor approximately forty times. The market's rebuilt since the siege — new stalls, brighter awnings, magic worked openly now, little glimmers and cantrips woven into the shopfronts like embroidery. A child runs past us trailing harmless sparks, laughing, and neither of us flinches, and I realize we're both smiling at it, and I realize we turned toward each other to smile at it at exactly the same moment.

"First date protocol," August says solemnly, as we join the crowd. "I propose we observe all the traditional rituals. Awkward small talk. Pretending not to notice each other looking. One moment where we almost touch hands and both panic."

"We're holding arms already."

"Then we're ahead of schedule. Excellent. I've never been good at the slow parts."

"What are you good at?" I ask, genuinely curious, genuinely not knowing, and the strangeness of that — asking a man I'd die for what his talents are — almost makes me laugh.

"Let's see." He considers. "I make a very good stew. I'm told I give decent advice, though the source is biased. I once talked a Warden patrol out of arresting Bramble by describing, in detail, the legal history of smuggling law, until they arrested themselves out of sheer boredom." He glances down at me. "And apparently I once stood in front of a gate at the end of the world and refused to do the brave stupid thing alone. Though I'll be honest, I don't remember it. I just have everyone else's account, and they all describe me like I was better than I feel."

"How do you feel?"

"Like a man reading letters addressed to someone braver." He shrugs, easy, but his eyes aren't. "You?"

"I feel like the letter-writer," I say, before I can stop myself. Then, because it's a first date and first dates are for honesty or nothing: "Everyone tells me stories about us. About what we did. And I believe them, the way you believe a story. But sometimes I look at you and I think — whoever loved you, she must have been so tired of being careful. And I think I'd like to meet her someday. And I think —" My voice does something embarrassing. I push through it. "I think maybe I am her. Just the version of her that gets to start clean."

August stops walking.

We're in the middle of the market, lanterns swinging above us, the canal glittering, fried dough smoke curling around us like incense, and he turns to face me fully and takes both my hands, and his palms are warm, and my pulse does the settling thing, the key-in-the-lock thing.

"Lena," he says. "I don't remember loving you. I want you to know that I've checked — thoroughly, at three in the morning, more than once — and it's not there. Whatever we were, the memory of it is gone." His thumb moves over my knuckles, slow. "But here's what I do know. Every single day since the stair, I've liked you more. Not remembered — *liked*. Chosen. You make a joke and I want to hear the next one. You're stubborn about the shop's opening hours and it delights me. Yesterday you argued with a delivery mule and lost, and I have thought about it eleven times since." 

"The mule started it."

"The mule absolutely started it. My point is —" He breathes out. "Everyone keeps telling us we were fated. Prophecy, ledger, covenant, all of it. Fine. Maybe we were. But fate already had its turn, and fate got us sealed inside a door and wiped blank. So this —" he squeezes my hands — "this part isn't fate's. This is mine. I'm choosing you on purpose, with no prophecy backing me up, based entirely on the mule incident and the way you hum when you ward the door. And I'll keep choosing you tomorrow, and the day after, until either I run out of days or you tell me to stop. That's the whole speech. It's not as good as the one I apparently gave at the end of the world, but I wasn't there for that one, so."

The ache in my chest isn't an ache anymore. It's a hearth. It's a lit room.

"August Vane," I say, "that was a terrible first-date speech."

"It was draft thirteen."

"It was perfect." I rise on my toes and kiss his cheek, and he goes pink to the ears, and somewhere behind us Bramble — who has absolutely been following us at a discreet distance despite promising not to — makes a sound like a teakettle achieving enlightenment. "Now buy me fried dough. First dates have rules."

### August

We eat fried dough on the canal steps with our shoes off, because Lena kicks hers off without hesitation and dares me with a raised eyebrow to be the kind of person who keeps shoes on, and I am not that person, apparently, and possibly never was.

The dough is everything Bramble promised. The lanterns come on along the water one by one, and a ferryman sings something old and off-key, and Lena leans her head on my shoulder like it's the most natural architecture in the world, and maybe it is. Maybe shoulders are just doors that haven't been introduced yet.

"Can I tell you something strange?" she says, around the last bite.

"You've told me seven strange things already. I'm invested."

"When the gate took our memories — when we split the price — I remember one thing. Just one. It shouldn't have survived; nothing else did." She's quiet a moment. The water laps the steps. "It's not a scene. It's not words. It's just — dark, and warm, and your hand in mine, and the feeling of *not letting go*. That's all. A grip. That's what I kept." She tilts her head back to look at me, upside-down, hair spilling everywhere, utterly unguarded. "What did you keep?"

I should lie. It's a first date; lying is traditional. But I find I physically can't.

"The same," I say. "Exactly the same. I thought it was just a feeling I'd invented. A guess about what we must have been."

"No." She sits up, turning to face me, and her eyes are bright and fierce in the lantern light. "No, August. It's not a guess. It's evidence. The gate took everything it could reach, and it couldn't reach that. Which means it's not memory. Memory burns. That's —" she presses her hand flat against her sternum, and I press mine against mine, and we stare at each other across the small space between us like two people discovering they've been speaking the same language all along — "that's *load-bearing*. That's the thing the ledger wrote about. The bond it couldn't erase. It's not in our heads. It never was."

The ferryman's song drifts past. A spark-child laughs somewhere upstream. And I understand, suddenly, completely, why my ribs ache at her name: because the important things don't live in the mind, where fires can reach them. They live lower. Deeper. In the grip.

"So what do we do with it?" I ask.

Lena smiles — the real one, the one that starts in the eyes — and reaches out and takes my hand, deliberately, threading our fingers together, a grip with intent behind it.

"We practice," she says. "Every day. Until it's not just what we kept. It's what we build."

Above us, on the far bank, the old belltower catches the last of the light, and for half a heartbeat I'd swear the bell moves — though there's no wind, and no rope, and no reason. Neither of us notices. Or rather: both of us notice, and both of us decide, silently, that tonight is not the night for omens.

Tonight is the night for fried dough and a grip that wouldn't burn.

Tomorrow, the ledger on my desk will open itself to a fresh page, ink blooming like a bruise, spelling out words in a dead language that Odile will spend a sleepless week translating — and what she finds will send all of us down into the dark again, one more time, together.

But that's tomorrow.

Tonight, Lena Pemberton asks me if I'd like to see the lanterns launched from the bridge at moonrise, and I say yes, yes, yes — and mean it in every language, remembered and forgotten, that I have ever known.
