## 38. The Ember Kept

*Odile*

I used to think forgiveness was a door you walked through once, and then you were through it, done, on the other side where the light was better and the tea was warmer and nobody flinched when you said your own name.

Nobody warned me it was a staircase. Down and down and down, the same steps every morning, and every morning you had to decide to take them again.

The Warden barracks on Belltower Row had been a fortress for a hundred years. Arrow slits instead of windows. Doors thick enough to stop a battering ram and thin enough, I'd always thought, to keep out mercy. Now there were curtains in the arrow slits—Bramble's idea, dyed canal-green because she claimed grey made people "sad and stupid"—and the great doors stood open from dawn until dusk, because Sera had ordered it so, and because half the city still didn't believe her and came to check.

They came for charms now. For blessings over new babies and safe passage over old bridges. They came because their grandmother's knee ached and the physickers were useless, because their son talked to things that weren't there and they wanted someone to tell them whether that was sickness or gift. They came because for five generations we taught them that magic was a knife at the throat of the world, and unteaching takes longer than teaching, and people are patient with their fear in ways they are never patient with their hope.

"Saint Odile," an old man called me last week, doffing his cap in the courtyard like I was a relic in a niche.

"I'm not a saint," I told him. "I'm a nun who lost the argument."

He laughed. Most of them laugh. It turns out that's most of the work—not the warding, not the sigils, not the long slow rewiring of a century of law. The work is letting people laugh at the thing they were taught to kneel before, until the kneeling wears out at the knees and they can stand up straight again.

My sister thinks I'm good at this. My sister is wrong, but she thinks it so loudly and so often that I've stopped correcting her, which is either growth or surrender, and I've decided not to look too closely at which.

Here is what I know about myself, written plainly, the way I write everything now: I renounced magic because I believed it had killed our parents. I built a life out of that belief the way you build a house out of grief—every beam load-bearing, every room too small. And when August Vane stood in my chapel at Greyhollow with ash on his sleeves and terror in his honest eyes and said, *I'm afraid all the time, Sister. Does your faith make you less afraid?*, he cracked one beam, and then another, and the whole house came down on me in the space of a single conversation, and I have been living in the rubble ever since, sorting it, keeping what's true.

What's true: magic did not kill our parents. Fear did. Fear wearing the uniform of duty, fear signing its name as law, fear that looked, from certain angles, exactly like love. The Sundering was engineered by a woman who could not bear the thought of loving across a boundary, and she cut the world rather than risk her heart, and a hundred years later her wound was still bleeding, and I had been pressing my palms against it calling it holiness.

Forgiveness, I have learned, is mostly bookkeeping. You go back through the ledger of yourself and you find the entries you wrote in blood and you correct them in ink, knowing the bloodstains never fully lift, knowing the page will always be warped where the old ink soaked through. You forgive your parents for dying. You forgive your faith for being a shelter that became a wall. You forgive your sister for choosing the uniform when you chose the habit, because you both chose armor, only hers had a sword in it and yours had silence.

And some mornings—the hard ones—you forgive the girl you were, the one in the chapel at Greyhollow with her hands folded and her heart locked, who turned away two strangers at the gate because helping them would have meant admitting she'd been wrong for twenty years.

That girl wasn't wicked. She was just early. She hadn't lived far enough into the story to know how it turned out.

I light a candle for her anyway. Old habits. Some of them are worth keeping.

* * *

*Sera*

There's a particular sound a Warden's boots make on Belltower Row at dawn. I heard it every morning for fifteen years—two thousand heels striking stone in rhythm, the heartbeat of the law—and I confess there are mornings now, standing in the courtyard listening to the same boots walk the same stones, that I have to close my eyes and remind myself which side of history I'm standing on.

Because it sounds the same. That's the thing nobody tells you about reforming an institution: the building doesn't change. The boots don't change. What changes is what the boots are walking toward, and you have to hold onto that difference with both hands or you'll wake up one day convinced you've accomplished nothing but rearranging furniture in a prison.

We changed the oath first. Took me four drafts and one spectacular shouting match with Odile, who wanted poetry where I wanted precision, and who won, because she usually wins arguments that involve words and I usually win arguments that involve knives, and this one was words.

The old oath: *To keep the veil. To hunt the hidden. To suffer no ember.*

The new one hangs in the courtyard now, carved in stone because paper can be burned and we have all seen enough burning:

*To keep the gate. To guard the gifted. To carry the ember, and be warmed by it, and warm others.*

Odile cried when she read the final draft. She'll deny it. She cried, and then she said, "It needs a comma after 'warmed by it,'" and I told her no one reads commas off a war memorial, and she said "it's not a war memorial, it's a promise," and that, more or less, is my sister in a single exchange.

The High Warden's chair is gone. I had it broken up for firewood the week after the siege, and Bramble—half-feral, bandaged to the ribs, propped up in a doorway on her mother's cane—insisted on lighting the first fire herself and roasting chestnuts over the remains of the office that had hunted her since she was nine years old. She saved me a bag of them. Left it on my desk without a note. I ate every one and I'm not ashamed to say I cried into the shells, quietly, where no one could see, which is a skill Wardens learn young.

Not everyone stayed. A third of the order resigned the day the Accords fell—some in fury, some in relief, a few in genuine grief, because I will not pretend we were monsters; we were people who had organized our fear into a career, and when the fear was taken away some of us didn't know who we were without it. I understood those ones best. I'd been them.

But the ones who stayed. Gods, the ones who stayed. Young Warden Kess, who once arrested a charm-seller's daughter for a luck-knot, now runs the schoolhouse outreach program and has learned to braid warding-cords with her own clumsy hands. Old Halloway, who served under three High Wardens and buried his doubts so deep even he couldn't find them, stood up at the first open assembly and said, "I spent thirty years making people afraid of us. I'd like to spend what's left making them unafraid." He got a standing ovation and then immediately complained about the noise. He's my favorite. Don't tell him.

And every week, the ledger. Not the guardian ledger—that belongs to Lena Pemberton, and I have learned not to touch other people's sacred objects, mostly—but ours. The Warden rolls. For a century they recorded arrests, seizures, executions. Now they record something else, and it took Odile to design the format, because she has a nun's genius for confession:

*Name. Gift. What they need. What they fear. What we owe them.*

That last column is mine. I insisted on it. Every entry ends with what we owe them, because for a hundred years this institution took and took and called it protection, and if we're going to be anything better than a leash cut loose, we start by writing down the debt.

Today's entry, in my own hand:

*Name: Maren Quill-Copperkettle. Age: eleven. Gift: hears the river speak. Needs: a tutor who won't call it madness. Fears: that her gift makes her a target. What we owe her: an apology on behalf of everyone who ever made a child hide what she is.*

Her mother came to collect her registration papers yesterday and shook my hand and did not smile, and I did not ask her to, and we both understood that the handshake was worth more than a smile, because it cost her something to give it, and I intend to be worth the cost.

* * *

*Odile*

Sera thinks I don't notice when she stands in the doorway of the old High Warden's office—which is a supply closet now, full of mops and honest dust—and just looks at it. She thinks her face does nothing. Her face does plenty. Fifteen years of wearing a Warden's mask and she never learned that her mouth goes soft when she's grieving, like a bow unstrung.

I let her have her moments. She lets me have mine. This is, I have decided, what sisters are for: not the fixing, but the witnessing. You stand in the doorway of each other's griefs and you say nothing, and somehow that is the whole job.

Though tonight I broke the rule.

She was doing it again—standing in the mop closet doorway at the end of a long day, torchlight catching the grey at her temples that she pretends doesn't exist—and I came up beside her and said, "You know, when we were children, you told me you'd grow up to be a knight."

"I told you I'd grow up to be a dragon," she said. "You were the knight. You had the stick."

"You had the blanket cape. The evidence favors dragon."

"The evidence," she said, "favors neither of us, because here we are, two middle-aged women staring at cleaning supplies, and the world got saved by a charm-seller and a boy who writes letters." She paused. "Did you know he wrote me one? August. Before the descent. I found it after."

"What did it say?"

"That he was sorry he'd dragged his fear into my chapel. That he hoped someday I'd get to choose my faith instead of hiding inside it." Her voice did the thing it does when she's quoting something she's memorized against her will. "He was twenty-three years old and he was apologizing to me. For being afraid. In my chapel. Where I'd thrown him out."

"And?"

"And I burned it," she said. "The night I read it. Then I dug it out of the grate and pieced it back together, and it's in my desk drawer, and I take it out sometimes and I don't know why."

"Because he saw you," I said. "Before any of us did. Strangers see us first, sometimes. Family's too busy remembering who we were."

She was quiet a while. The mop closet smelled of lye and lavender, because Bramble had declared war on the smell of institutional despair and was winning.

"Sera," I said, because I'd been carrying it all day and my arms were tired, "the anniversary is tomorrow."

"I know."

"A year since the gate. Since—" I stopped, the way I always stop, at the shape of what happened below. None of us went down. We stood in the cellar of a charm shop and held hands around a table while the floor hummed like a struck bell, and then it stopped humming, and two people climbed the stairs looking at each other like the answer to a question they'd forgotten asking. "Do you think they're happy? Lena and August. Do you think whatever's left of them is enough?"

Sera looked at me then, really looked, the way she used to when we were girls sharing a bed during the storm season and trading fears in the dark like contraband.

"I think," she said slowly, "that I watched a woman refuse to let the man she loved pay the whole price alone, and I watched him let her share it, and I think whatever the gate took, it couldn't take that. The choosing. I think the choosing survived." She shrugged, awkward, a woman still learning to carry tenderness in a body trained for swords. "Ask them tomorrow. Bring wine. Bramble will bring the terrible canal brandy, and Odile, they'll look at each other across that table and forget what anyone else said, and you'll see it."

"See what?"

"What the body remembers." She almost smiled. "Lena told me that once, in a cell, when I was holding her prisoner and she was holding my whole worldview hostage. *Things the body remembers.* I didn't understand it then."

"And now?"

"Now I watch you fold your hands in prayer and then catch yourself mid-gesture turning it into the old warding-sign, and you do it every time, and you don't notice, and I think maybe grace isn't forgetting who you were." She pushed off the doorframe. "Maybe it's the two selves shaking hands."

She walked off down the corridor, boots ringing on stone, and I stood alone in the torchlight and did the thing—I folded my hands to pray and felt my fingers drift into the old sign, thumb to ring finger, the covenant sigil, the locket-shape, the ember kept—and this time I didn't stop it. I held both at once. Nun's hands and warden's hands, the same hands, finally.

Then I went to find the wine, because tomorrow we would need it, and because somewhere across the city a charm shop was closing for the night, and two people who had paid everything for each other were about to be surprised by what the year had been saving up to give them back.
