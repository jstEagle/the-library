## 39. Anniversary of a Door

The bell over the shop door has a crack in it now, and I refuse to fix it.

It happened during the siege—Bramble swears it was a stray severance-bolt, Odile insists it was Bramble herself knocking a shelf over in a panic—but the result is that the bell doesn't ring so much as it *clanks*, like a spoon dropped down a well, and every customer who enters Ashfall's most beloved charm shop does so to the sound of small domestic disaster.

I've decided I love it. There's something honest about a broken bell. It says: we were here when it mattered, and we're still here, and we never got around to being perfect again.

"Morning, Lena." Mrs. Halloway from the tannery pushes through, clank, and sets a paper bag of cinnamon buns on my counter like she's paying rent. "You look terrible."

"You say the sweetest things."

"I mean it kindly. You've got shadows under your eyes deep enough to store winter root vegetables in. Bad dreams?"

I wrap her usual—a luck-knot for her son's apprenticeship, harmless, mostly string and good intentions these days—and smile the smile I've had a year to practice. "Just the ordinary kind. Teeth falling out. Showing up to Warden trials in my nightdress."

She pats my hand on her way out, unconvinced, and the bell clanks her goodbye, and I stand alone in the warm morning light of Pemberton's Charms & Curiosities—*Pemberton & Co.*, technically, since the sign repainting, since Bramble threatened to paint *Quill's Quality Contraband* on the other side if I didn't share billing—and I let the smile fall off my face like a coat sliding off a hook.

Because they weren't ordinary dreams.

They were August, laughing in the dark.

* * *

Here is what a year looks like when you build it out of the wreckage of a war:

In spring, the Accords came down. Not all at once—that would have been too clean, and Ashfall has never once done anything the clean way—but brick by brick, law by law, until one gray Tuesday the Wardens' posting-house took down the old prohibition notices and put up a new one that just said MAGIC IS REGISTERED AT THE BELLTOWER OFFICE, THANK YOU, in the cheerful bureaucratic font of a city trying very hard to pretend it hadn't spent a century being afraid of itself.

Sera Voss signed the new charter. Odile stood beside her in full Warden grays, which caused three separate fainting spells among the old guard and one among the sisters of Greyhollow, who apparently thought their nun had been kidnapped by an evil twin.

Bramble got her mother's locket back permanently, then got a smuggling license, which she calls "the funniest document in the city," because now she pays taxes on moving goods through canals she used to move them under.

And the shop—

The shop thrives. That's the word people use, and I've stopped flinching at it. We sell charms openly now: real ones, small ones, the kind that keep milk sweet and headaches quiet and lovers honest-ish. August works the front counter three days a week because he's better with customers than I'll ever be—he remembers names, asks after children, laughs at jokes he's heard four times—and I work the back room where the real craft happens, and between us we've built something I don't have a word for yet.

A life, maybe. The word keeps hovering there like a moth around a lamp, and I keep not looking directly at it.

Because here is the other thing a year looks like:

August and I live above the shop, in separate rooms, in a careful arrangement everyone else finds hilarious and neither of us finds funny at all. We finish each other's sentences. We reach for the same jar at the same time and our hands collide and we both apologize, every time, as though touching were a crime we'd forgotten the statute for. He hums when he thinks no one's listening, and I know the tune—I *know* it, somewhere below knowing—and I cannot for the life of me tell him where I learned it.

We are two strangers sharing a house shaped exactly like a history neither of us owns.

The gate took its price. We paid it together, split down the middle like a loaf, and what's left is this: two people who ache at each other's names and can't say why, running a shop above a sealed door, waiting for something none of us will admit we're waiting for.

* * *

The dreams started three months ago.

Not nightmares. That's what unsettles me. If they were nightmares I could drink chamomile tea and blame the cheese and get on with things. But they're not. They're *fragments*, little slivers of something, arriving without context like postcards from a country I've never visited:

August's hands, bound in mine, glowing ember-gold.

A voice older than language saying *the price named twice*.

And always, always, the same one: darkness, and the feeling of descending, and August turning back to look at me in the black of the threshold and *laughing*—not scared laughter, not brave laughter, but the giddy, helpless kind, the kind you do when you're terrified and happy in the same breath and your body picks happy because it has to pick something.

I wake up from that one with tears on my face and no idea whether they're grief or joy, and honestly, after a year, I've stopped thinking those are different substances.

This morning I woke up with his name in my mouth. Just *August*, said aloud to an empty room, in a tone I didn't recognize—soft, worn smooth, like a stone carried in a pocket for years.

 downstairs, the kettle was already on. He'd made two cups. He always makes two cups. Neither of us has ever asked why.

"Dream again?" he said, not looking up from the ledger—the accounting ledger, the boring one, thank the saints.

"How could you possibly—"

"You said my name. Through the floor. You have a very carrying voice for someone who claims to be discreet."

I took my cup and hid behind it. "What did I say?"

"Just my name." He turned a page. His ears were pink. "You said it like—" He stopped. Started over. "Like you were checking I was still there."

I didn't answer. The gate, beneath us, hummed.

That's another thing. The gate hums now. Contentedly, Odile says, like a cat. It used to whisper in dead languages and pull at the seams of the world; now it just sits under the cellar doing whatever sealed, satisfied ancient doors do, and occasionally—this is the part I haven't told anyone—it hums *louder* when August and I are in the same room.

I've decided that's coincidence. I'm a professional; I can decide these things.

* * *

At noon, Bramble arrived via the front door, which for Bramble constitutes a major character development, clank, carrying a box of canal-plum preserves and an expression I've learned means trouble wearing its Sunday clothes.

"So," she said, setting the box down next to Mrs. Halloway's buns, creating on my counter a small shrine to baked goods. "Tomorrow's the anniversary."

"Don't," I said.

"The anniversary of the Reforging. Of the siege ending. Of the day you two walked into a hole in the world holding hands and came out as amnesiacs with excellent retail instincts."

"Bramble."

"There's going to be a ceremony. Sera's organizing it. There's a plaque. There's a *plaque*, Lena, they're putting a plaque on Belltower Row, bronze, with all our names on it, including mine, which I intend to steal eventually—"

"We're not going."

The words came out faster than I meant them to, and sharper, and Bramble went quiet in the way she only goes quiet when she's decided to be kind instead of funny, which is rarer and more alarming.

"Lena—"

"He doesn't remember." I kept my eyes on the preserves. "He doesn't remember any of it, and they're going to stand him up in front of a crowd and read out everything he gave up, everything we *both* gave up, and he'll have to smile at strangers describing the inside of his own heart. And I'll have to stand next to him and do the same, and afterward we'll come home and make two cups of tea and not talk about it, because that's what we do, we don't talk about it, we run a shop, we're fine, we're *fine*—"

My voice did something embarrassing at the end there. Bramble came around the counter and hugged me, smelling of brine and plums and canal water, which is the smell of safety as far as I'm concerned.

"You're not fine," she said into my hair.

"No," I admitted. "But I'm open Tuesdays through Saturdays."

She laughed wetly against my shoulder. Then she pulled back, held me by the arms, and looked at me with those fae-green eyes that see entirely too much.

"For what it's worth," she said, "he's going. To the ceremony. He told Sera yes last week."

The floor tilted slightly. "He what?"

"He said somebody should represent the people who actually paid. His words." She shrugged, awkward. "And he asked—if you came too. He said it would feel wrong, standing up there without you. He said—" She hesitated, and I watched her decide whether to hand me the knife or the bandage. "He said standing next to you feels like remembering something. Even now. Especially now."

The gate hummed. Loudly. Traitorously.

Bramble glanced down at the floorboards, then up at me, eyebrows raised. "It does that every time, you know. Every single time you two are in a room together. Odile thinks it's trying to tell us something."

"It's a door, Bramble. Doors don't talk."

"Doors don't hum, either. Not like that." She picked up her empty box, headed for the door, then paused with her hand on the frame. "One year ago tomorrow, you two chose each other. First time, before the ledger ever showed his face—you chose him at a charm stall over a copper charm he couldn't afford. Don't think I forgot that. Somebody in this city has to keep the records straight, since your fancy guardian ledger's been blank since the Reforging."

I frowned. "Blank?"

"Hasn't written a word in a year. Odile checks. Says it's like the book finished its job." Bramble grinned, crooked and fae and fond. "Or like it's saving up for something. Books like that don't go quiet, Lena. They hold their breath."

Clank. Gone.

* * *

I closed early that evening, which I never do, and went down into the cellar.

The gate stands where it has always stood, in the deep heart beneath the shop: a door of black iron and older wood, seamed with gold where the Reforging sealed it, beautiful now instead of terrible, the way a scar is beautiful if you're the sort of person who collects scars. Which, evidently, I am.

I put my palm flat against it, the way I used to before any of this, when it was my secret and my burden and my whole lonely vocation.

"Hello," I said. "You've been quiet."

The gold seams warmed under my hand. The hum rose—not louder, exactly, but *closer*, like a voice leaning in.

And for the first time in a year, I dreamed awake. Just a flash, just a heartbeat: darkness, descending, August's face lit ember-gold as he looked back at me and laughed, and underneath the laugh, clear as a struck bell, his voice saying words I suddenly, desperately needed to hear again—

*Lena. Whatever it takes. Twice.*

Then gone. The cellar was just a cellar. The gate was just a door. My heart was hammering like the cracked bell upstairs.

"Twice," I whispered. "Whatever what takes?"

The gate hummed. Patient. Almost amused. Like a book holding its breath.

I climbed the stairs slowly, one hand on the wall, and found August in the kitchen lighting the lamps, and he looked up at me with that expression I've catalogued a hundred times this year—stranger, friend, home, question mark—and said, "You went pale. What's down there?"

"An anniversary," I said, before I could stop myself. "Tomorrow. Apparently we're supposed to be there."

His face did several things at once. "Sera told you."

"Bramble told me. You said yes?"

He set down the lamp-lighter. For a moment he looked at his own hands like they belonged to someone braver. "I keep having this feeling," he said quietly, "like tomorrow matters. Not plaque-and-speeches matters. Real matters. Like there's something I'm supposed to remember and I left it somewhere and tomorrow's the only place it could be." He laughed, embarrassed, and the sound snagged on something. "That's mad, isn't it? Tell me that's mad."

The kettle began, softly, to sing.

"It's not mad," I said. "I've been dreaming about you laughing in the dark."

He went very still. Outside, far away across the rooftops, the evening bells of Belltower Row began to ring—and underneath them, faint but unmistakable, rising up through the floorboards like a tide, the gate began to hum a tune.

I knew the tune. Below knowing, I had always known it.

It was the one he hums when he thinks no one is listening.

August's eyes met mine across the kitchen, wide and bright and frightened and hopeful, and neither of us moved, and the gate sang on, patient as a held breath, like a door deciding—after one long year of silence—that tomorrow it would finally speak.
