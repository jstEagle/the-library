## 40. August and the Ember

**AUGUST**

One year is a strange unit of measurement for a person.

I know this because I've spent twelve months measuring myself against it—against the night we walked up out of the cellar hand in hand, two strangers wearing each other's names like borrowed coats. Against the morning I woke in Lena's spare room and couldn't remember why my chest ached when she poured the tea wrong. She made it with honey. I drank it anyway. My body told me that was right even when my head had nothing to say on the matter.

The body keeps better books than the mind. That's the first thing I learned this year.

The second thing I learned: Bramble Quill cannot be trusted near a festival planning committee. The canal district's Ember Night celebration now features a barge shaped like a dragon that is, by her own cheerful admission, "seventy percent flammable," and she has stationed herself at its prow wearing a coat that used to be someone's curtains. Odile stands beside her in her grey traveling habit, no longer a nun but not quite anything else yet either, holding a lantern like a woman who has decided to be careful about joy instead of refusing it entirely. Sera Voss—Warden Sera Voss, though the title means something different now, something closer to *guardian* than *hunter*—stands at the canal's edge with her arms crossed, pretending she isn't smiling.

And Lena.

Lena Pemberton is standing next to me on the shop's back step, watching the lanterns go down the water, close enough that our shoulders touch, far enough that touching could still be an accident. We have spent a year in that exact distance. It's become its own country, with its own weather. I know the shape of her silence the way I used to know—I'm fairly sure I used to know—the sound of the gate humming under the floorboards.

"Your tea's gone cold," she says.

"You made it with honey."

Her head turns. Just slightly. "Did I?"

"I don't know why I said that." I look down at the cup. Honey-brown, cooling, ordinary. "It felt true."

She doesn't answer right away. Overhead, the first of the ember-lanterns lifts off the barge, and the whole canal turns briefly to drifting firelight, and in that light her face does something complicated—hope and grief holding hands, which is, I've come to understand, simply what her face does.

"It usually is," she says quietly. "True, I mean. Even when we don't know why."

* * *

**LENA**

Here is what nobody tells you about losing your memories to an ancient gate: the ledger keeps receipts.

I found out in the spring, when the guardian book in my cellar began writing entries I hadn't earned back yet. Little things. *He hums when he thinks no one can hear.* *She counts stairs because counting calms her.* *They argued about the word "lovely" for eleven minutes and both secretly enjoyed it.* The gate had taken our payment—centuries of memory, the whole architecture of us—but it had apparently kept a copy, filed away in ink and patience, and once a year, on the anniversary of the reforging, it opens the file.

Tonight is the anniversary.

I haven't told August. Or rather—I've told him everything except the one thing. He knows the ledger stirs on this night; he helped me carry it upstairs last week when it started glowing through the floorboards hard enough to wake the neighbors' cat. He knows the others are coming. What he doesn't know—and what I've been carrying around all day like a stone in my apron pocket—is that the gate sent me a dream three nights ago, and in the dream I remembered everything, and I woke up crying into a pillow that smelled like him, and I have been walking around since then as a woman who knows exactly how much she lost and exactly how much is about to come back.

You'd think remembering would be simple. You'd think it would be like opening a drawer. But memory, I've learned, is more like a charm stall—you never get exactly what you came for, and half of what you find changes the price of everything else.

The knock comes at eight. Bramble, Odile, Sera, filing into the shop above the gate they bled to seal, carrying wine and bread and one suspiciously large wrapped parcel that Bramble refuses to explain. The four sigils of the covenant are quiet marks on our skin now—a faint warmth at the wrist, the collarbone, the palm. They only burn when the gate is troubled, and tonight the gate is not troubled. Tonight, if anything, it sounds almost like purring.

"To the year," Bramble says, raising her glass. "To the year we didn't die."

"To the year," Sera echoes, and there's a weight under it, because Sera remembers what it cost to stand on Belltower Row against her own order, and some prices don't stop being paid just because the invoice closed.

Odile touches her glass to mine. Her forgiveness of herself is still a work in progress—I see it in the way she flinches from kindness sometimes, like a hand reaching toward a stove that burned her—but she is here, and she stays, and staying is its own kind of rite.

We eat. We laugh. Bramble unveils the parcel, which turns out to be a portrait of the four of us rendered entirely in embroidery, wildly unflattering, deeply beloved. And underneath all of it, under the laughter and the wine and the warm lamplight, the ledger sits on the table between us, closed, patient, waiting for midnight the way a door waits for a key.

At five minutes to twelve, August catches my eye across the room.

He knows. Of course he knows. He's known all evening—he's been watching me watch the clock with the particular gentleness of a man who has decided that whatever happens next, he'll meet it standing next to me. That's the thing about August that survived even the forgetting: he meets things standing next to me.

"Whenever you're ready," he says softly, so only I can hear.

"We're ready," I say. "All of us."

I open the ledger.

* * *

**AUGUST**

The pages turn themselves. I should be used to that by now. I am not used to that.

Ink blooms across the paper like frost forming in reverse, and the light that comes off it isn't candlelight—it's ember-light, deep orange shot through with gold, the same color as the charm I bought from her stall two years ago that hummed with prophecy before I knew what prophecy was. The whole shop fills with it. Bramble swears in two languages. Odile's lantern gutters and then steadies, burning brighter than it has any right to.

And then the memories come home.

I want to describe it properly, because it deserves proper description, but language was built for smaller events. It is not like watching a play of your life. It is like being handed back organs you didn't know were missing. One moment I am a man who loves a woman for reasons his body knows and his mind cannot cite. The next moment—

—the ledger open in her cellar, my own face inked beside *the ember returns*, a century old, waiting for me like a chair pulled out at a table—

—her voice cracking across a safehouse in winter: *I've loved you since the ledger showed me your face,* and the terrifying, enormous silence after—

—her hands binding mine together in the dark beneath the world while the gate demanded its price and I stepped forward alone and she said *no*, said *not like this*, said *we pay it together or not at all*—

—the rite. The burning. Memories going like pages in a fire, and both of us fighting it, screaming our own names into the dark, screaming each other's names, *Lena, Lena, hold on, hold on, August, remember August*—

—choosing her. Choosing her twice. Once at the beginning without knowing the cost, and once at the end knowing it completely, and choosing her anyway, both times, freely, with my whole self.

I come back to the shop with tears on my face and her name in my mouth.

"Lena."

She's already looking at me. She's crying too, and laughing, which is the most Lena expression there is, and I realize with a jolt that I remember that—that I remember *everything*—that the woman across the table is the woman who bound our hands in the dark and refused to let duty eat us alive, and the ache in my chest that has been a locked door for a year swings open all at once.

"You came back for me," I say. It's not the most eloquent sentence ever produced. It might be the truest.

"You stepped forward alone," she says. "You absolute idiot. You beautiful, honorable idiot."

Bramble is sobbing openly into the embroidered portrait. Sera has turned away to face the window, shoulders shaking, in the manner of a woman who will deny to her grave that she cried. Odile has both hands pressed over her mouth, whispering something that might be prayer and might be thanks.

But I only have eyes for Lena, and she for me, and between us the ledger lies open to its final page, where new ink is still drying:

*What was split cannot be wholly kept. What was given freely cannot be wholly taken. The bond endures. The ember is kept.*

"They gave it back," she whispers. "The gate gave it back. Why—"

"Because we paid together," I say, and the understanding arrives whole, the way the best ones do. "It wanted one whole person. We gave it two halves of two people instead. It couldn't keep us apart because we were never separate to begin with. So it—" I laugh, wet and disbelieving. "So it kept a copy. Filed us away. Waited for the anniversary."

"A year of interest," Lena says. "On love. Paid in full."

* * *

**LENA**

Later—after Bramble has been poured into a cab with her portrait, after Sera has gone back to the wardens' hall with red eyes and a straight spine, after Odile has blessed the doorframe on her way out in a gesture that is half faith and half habit and entirely hers—the shop is quiet, and August and I are sitting on the cellar stairs with the door to the gate open below us.

The gate hums. Contented. Warm. It sounds, tonight, like a hearth.

"Do you remember," August says, "the first date? The real first one? Before any of it?"

"The charm stall. You bought a luck-charm and haggled badly on purpose because you thought it would make me laugh."

"It did make you laugh."

"It did make me laugh." I lean my head on his shoulder. His shoulder fits exactly where it always fit, which is a sentence that means nothing to anyone else and everything to me. "And then you asked me to dinner, and I said yes, and I spent the whole evening terrified because your face was in a century-old book and you didn't know it."

"And now?"

"Now I know everything. Every terrible, wonderful thing. The Sundering. The covenantor who tore the worlds apart because he was afraid of love between realms. The price. The letters you wrote me that you thought I'd never read." I feel him go still. "I read them. In the flood. All of them, every goodbye you tried to write, and then I went downstairs and stopped you, do you remember stopping—"

"I remember," he says hoarsely. "I remember all of it. God, Lena. A year. We sat a year in the same room and didn't know."

"We knew." I lift my head and look at him, really look, ember-light and lamplight and the gate's low golden hum rising around us. "That's the thing I finally understand. We never stopped knowing. We just couldn't say it. Your body kept the books. Mine did too. Every time you made my tea wrong and I loved you for it—every time I counted stairs and you waited without asking why—we were choosing each other the whole time. Blindfolded. Still choosing."

He turns to face me fully on the narrow stair, and his eyes are bright, and he is smiling the smile I fell for twice, once with my memory and once without it.

"So," he says. "Third time's the tradition now, apparently."

"Apparently."

"Lena Pemberton." He takes my hand—the left one, the one with the faint warm mark at the wrist where the covenant sigil lives. "I know everything about you now. I know you count stairs. I know you argue with the ledger. I know you once threatened a High Warden with a soup ladle and meant it. I know you split the price of forever with me because you refuse to let duty consume anyone, least of all us. And none of it—not the fate, not the prophecy, not the gate itself—is why I'm asking this."

"August."

"Will you go on a date with me? A real one. Tomorrow. No prophecies, no Wardens, no ancient covenants. Just two people who chose each other twice, choosing a third time. Freely. Because we want to. Because wanting is enough."

Below us, the gate hums a note lower, warmer, like approval, like blessing, like an old door finally resting in its frame.

"Yes," I say. And then, because I am who I am, and he is who he is, and we are what we made together out of sacrifice and stubbornness and a year of unconscious devotion: "Yes. Obviously yes. Ask me again tomorrow, though. And the day after. I intend to collect."

He laughs—the laugh I heard in dreams all year without knowing whose it was—and pulls me close, and the ember-light rises around us both, gold and orange and utterly unextinguished.

Above us, the shop settles. Below us, the gate sleeps, sealed and satisfied. Around us, the city of Ashfall goes on—magic legal, lanterns drifting, a dragon-shaped barge smoldering gently somewhere in the canal district while Bramble Quill insists this was always part of the plan.

And between us, small and steady and ours, the ember keeps burning.

Not fate. Not duty.

Kept. Chosen. Ours.

The ledger, upstairs on the table, turns one last page on its own, and the ink that dries there is simple, and human, and enough:

*They chose again. Let the record show: freely, gladly, and forever.*
