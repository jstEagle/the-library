## 1. The Charm Seller's Stall

The rain in Vellmark came down the way it always did in autumn: patiently, as if it had all the time in the world and intended to use every minute of it. Hazel Kavanagh pulled her awning out a hand's width further over her stall, watched a bead of water gather at the canvas edge, and decided that was as much weather-craft as she was willing to practice for one morning.

Not that anyone would have noticed. That was rather the point.

"Charms!" she called to the gray street, in a voice pitched exactly between cheerful and bored. "Charms for luck, charms for love, charms to keep the mice from your flour and your mother-in-law from your pantry! Harmless as Sunday bread, guaranteed!"

Guaranteed harmless. It was, she reflected, the only honest advertising in the whole of Copperlane Market. Every charm on her table — the knotted red threads, the river-stones wrapped in copper wire, the little tin stars on leather cords — was precisely what it appeared to be: string, stone, wire, cord. She sold them to farmers who wanted their cows to stop wandering, to young men with more hope than sense, to grandmothers who liked the shine of them. And because they were nothing, nothing ever happened, and no one ever looked twice at the small woman with ink-stained fingers who made them.

Twelve years she had kept this stall. Twelve years since the Sundering, since the edicts went up on every gatepost in Vellmark — *the practice of gift is forbidden; the gifted shall be registered; the registered shall be dealt with according to the crown's mercy* — and Hazel had folded her maps into a box, buried the box under the floorboards, and taught herself to be nobody.

She was very good at being nobody now. Some mornings she woke up and had to lie still for a moment, sorting through which name was hers.

"Charm for you, miss?" she said to a woman hurrying past with a basket of leeks. "Keeps the rain off."

"It doesn't," the woman said, not slowing.

"No," Hazel agreed pleasantly. "But it's pretty."

That got half a smile, though no coin. Fine. The leek woman was a regular non-customer; they had this exchange most Tuesdays, and Hazel treasured it in a way she'd have been embarrassed to admit. This was the whole of her life now: small jokes, small sales, small weather. A person could live inside a life that small, like a snail in its shell. The trouble with shells was that they fit so snugly you sometimes forgot there had ever been anything outside them.

She was re-threading a knot when the bell tower began to ring.

Not the hour. Not the alarm, thank goodness. It was the *proclamation* peal — three bells in sequence, the pattern every child in Vellmark learned before they learned their letters, because knowing what the bells meant could save you a fine or worse. Hazel's hands went still on the red thread.

A proclamation. On a Tuesday. In the rain.

The crowd in Copperlane did what crowds do: it slowed, thickened, turned its face toward the tower steps where a herald in royal blue was unrolling a scroll with the ceremony of a man unveiling a portrait of himself. Hazel stood on her stool — carefully, she was forty-one and her balance was no longer a thing she took for granted — and listened.

"*By order of His Majesty, King Aldous the Second, a census of the realm's persons shall be taken,*" the herald read, "*that all may be counted, and none forgotten.*"

A murmur ran through the market. Censuses were taxes wearing nicer clothes, everyone knew that. But the herald wasn't finished.

"*Particular attention shall be given to those possessed of unusual talents. Registration is a kindness of the crown. No harm shall come to any honest subject. Come forward, be counted, be protected.*"

*Protected from what?* someone shouted, and got no answer but the herald's oiled smile.

Hazel climbed down off her stool slowly. Her heart was doing something quiet and terrible, like a clock winding down in an empty house. *No harm shall come to any honest subject.* She turned the sentence over the way she'd once turned over strange coastlines, looking for the trick of it, the place where the map lied. There was always a place where the map lied. You just had to know where to look.

She didn't find it. That frightened her more than if she had.

* * *

The rest of the morning was brisk, oddly — fear made people superstitious, and superstition bought charms. Hazel sold eleven knots against bad luck and two tin stars, and told herself the tightness in her chest was just the weather coming in.

Near midday, when the rain had settled into a fine persistent drizzle, a figure approached her stall who moved like a man trying to take up less space than he occupied.

He was young — nineteen, twenty — with the pallor of someone who worked by lamplight and the ink stains to prove it, deeper even than Hazel's own. He wore the slate-gray coat of a crown scribe, buttoned wrong at the throat, and he stood in front of her table of harmless trinkets as though it were a wall he meant to climb.

"You're the charm seller," he said.

"I'm the charm seller," Hazel confirmed. "What gave me away? Was it the charms?"

Something flickered across his face — surprise, then a reluctant almost-smile. "I need—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I need something that works."

Ah, thought Hazel. One of *those*. They came around every few months: the desperate ones, the ones who'd heard rumors, who pressed coins into her palm and whispered about sick mothers and dying brothers, and Hazel would sell them a knot and a kind word and watch them walk away with nothing, hating herself in a slow, practiced way, like a chore done weekly.

"I sell luck," she said gently. "I sell pretty things. I don't sell miracles."

"I'm not asking for a miracle." He glanced over his shoulder. Down the lane, two soldiers in royal blue were shaking out their cloaks under the eaves of the chandler's shop. The scribe lowered his voice until Hazel had to lean in to catch it. "I need something that points true. North. Or — or whatever it needs to point to. Do you understand?"

Hazel understood several things at once, and disliked all of them. She understood that a crown scribe asking for a working compass was either a trap or a catastrophe, and that from where she sat the odds were roughly even. She understood that the soldiers down the lane had stopped shaking out their cloaks and started watching her stall. And she understood — with the weary clarity of twelve years' practice — that the correct answer here was a red thread and a smile.

Her mouth opened to give it.

And then she looked at him properly. At the wrong-buttoned coat and the sleepless eyes and the way his hands trembled — not with nerves, she realized, but with the specific exhaustion of someone who has seen something and cannot put it back down. She knew that look. She wore it in the mirror some mornings, under the paint of ordinary cheer.

"What's your name?" she asked.

"Wren." A beat. "Just Wren."

"Just Wren," Hazel repeated. "Well, Just-Wren. Wait there."

She crouched beneath her stall, ostensibly to sort stock, actually to press her ear against the floorboard above the buried box, an old habit, a comfort, a promise. Then she straightened, picked through her tray, and set down a plain brass compass case, dull with age, that she had not touched in twelve years.

Inside it — and this was the part that made her palms sweat — was not a compass.

It was a charm she'd made long ago, in another life, from a sliver of lodestone and a thread pulled from her own hair and a drop of ink from her first true map. It didn't point north. It pointed toward *need* — toward whatever the holder most needed to find. She'd made it for herself, back when she'd been foolish enough to think a cartographer's gift might save the world instead of getting it burned. She'd never sold it. She'd sworn never to sell it.

She held it out to Wren.

"Now," she said, keeping her voice light and market-bright, because the soldiers were still watching, "this is my finest piece. Genuine Vellmark craftsmanship. Points you home when you've had too much ale." She pressed it into his cold hand and closed his fingers over it. "One silver."

"That's— I don't have—" He fumbled anyway, producing a silver piece and two coppers, which was more than she'd asked, and their hands touched over the coin, and Hazel felt it happen.

Felt the charm wake.

There was no dramatics to it — no glow, no hum. But she felt the lodestone turn over inside its case like a sleeper rolling toward a window, felt the thread-of-hair sing faintly against the brass, felt the whole small mechanism of it *orient*, the way a flower turns to sun. Twelve years dormant, and it woke at the touch of this trembling boy as if it had been waiting for him specifically, which was a thought Hazel firmly declined to finish having.

Wren felt it too. She saw it hit him — the tiny lurch, the way his breath caught. He opened his hand a crack and stared down at the brass case, and the needle inside, which should not have existed, swung once, twice, and settled, steady as a heartbeat, pointing directly at Hazel.

They both looked at it. Then at each other.

"That's," Wren whispered, "not north."

"No," Hazel said. Her mouth had gone dry. "It isn't."

Down the lane, one of the soldiers pushed off from the chandler's wall and began, unhurried, to stroll closer.

Wren's fist closed over the compass. "You're one of them," he breathed. "You're *gifted*."

"And you're a crown scribe buying illegal magic in an open market," Hazel said, "so I'd say we're both having a very bad day." She swept the rest of her wares into their box with quick, economical motions, years of drill behind every gesture. "Go home, Just-Wren. Forget my face. It's a talent of mine, being forgettable."

"It won't work." His voice cracked. "That's why I came. I tried to forget what I saw in the ledgers, and I can't, and the census lists go out tomorrow, and—" He swallowed. "The registered aren't being counted. They're being *shipped*. To the Marches. Nobody comes back from the Marches."

The bottom dropped out of Hazel's stomach, slow and cold, like stepping off a dock in winter.

Tam, she thought. Tam with his gentle rain-sense and his daughter Sorrel and his door that had no ash sigil on it yet. Tam, who had laughed at her harmless charms for twelve years and never once asked why a woman with her eyes sold nothing that worked.

The soldier was ten paces away now, rain dripping off his helmet brim.

Hazel looked at Wren — at the boy who had walked into her small gray life carrying the end of it in a brass case — and heard herself say, in a voice she barely recognized as her own:

"How fast can you walk in the rain?"
