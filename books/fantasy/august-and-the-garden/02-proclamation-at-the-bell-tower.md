## 2. Proclamation at the Bell Tower

The proclamation was read from the bell tower steps on a morning so gray that even the pigeons looked apologetic about it.

Hazel heard it before she saw it — the crier's voice carrying down Copperlane the way bad news always did, finding every ear that had hoped to sleep in. She was setting out her charms at the stall: little copper acorns for luck, knotted threads against headaches, a jar of river stones painted with tiny smiling faces that she sold to mothers of teething children at a price she pretended was fair.

None of them did anything. That was the point. A charm that worked got you a knock on the door and a long ride in a cart with no windows. A charm that didn't got you a copper coin and a kind word, and Hazel had learned to live very comfortably on kind words.

"—by order of His Majesty King Aldous the Second," the crier boomed, "a census of the gifted shall be taken throughout the realm. Registration only. No penalty. No seizure. The crown asks only that its gifted subjects come forward, be counted, and be protected."

*Protected.* Hazel's hands went still over the copper acorns.

The crowd around her shifted like wheat deciding whether it wanted to be wind. Old Marta from the bakery crossed herself with floury fingers. The cooper's boy, who was all elbows and questions, stood on his toes to see. And near the front, already nodding along as though registration were simply the sensible thing, stood Tam.

He had his daughter Sorrel on his shoulders, her red hair catching what little light the day offered, and he was wearing the expression he wore for everything — mild, patient, faintly amused, as if the world were a slightly clumsy dog that kept knocking things over but meant no harm. Tam could coax rain out of a clear sky and calm a hailstorm by humming to it, and he had done both quietly, carefully, for twelve years, the way you'd do anything illegal and beloved.

Hazel pushed through the crowd. "Tam," she said low. "You're not actually considering this."

"Considering what?" He turned, and Sorrel peered down at her upside-down, which was how Hazel preferred to see most people anyway.

"The census. Tam, they're saying *registration*, but I remember the last time the crown wanted names." Twelve years ago. The Sundering. The night the border between realms tore open like a badly sewn seam, and magic became contraband overnight, and everyone with a gift learned a new word: *hidden*.

"That was the old king," Tam said reasonably. "This one reads proclamations himself. Apparently he cries at them." He said it gently, without mockery, because Tam never mocked anyone, including kings. "Hazel. They promise nothing more than counting."

"Promises are just lies wearing their good coat."

Sorrel giggled. "That's a good one. Say it again."

"Don't encourage her," Tam said, but he was smiling, and Hazel felt the familiar ache of loving someone too trusting to survive in a story like theirs. "Look — if I don't register, and they find out later, that looks worse, doesn't it? Guilty conscience and all. Whereas if I stand in line like an honest man—"

"Honest men don't hum at thunderclouds."

Tam's smile flickered — just at the corner, just for a breath. Then he reached up and steadied Sorrel's ankle where she was wobbling. "I'll think on it," he said, which was what he said when he had already decided, and they both knew it, and neither said so.

* * *

They lined up the next morning outside the registrar's office in Belltower Square, and Hazel watched them go in like watching neighbors walk into deep water one at a time, each certain the bottom would hold.

Marta went first, chin up, dragging her grandson, who had once made bread rise by singing to it and thought nobody knew. Then the cooper's boy, alone, lying about his age and fooling no one. Then a fishwife whose nets never came up empty, then a midwife, then old Fenwick who talked to moths, then a dozen more, shuffling forward in the gray drizzle with the sheepish patience of people doing something they suspected was foolish but couldn't quite name why.

Hazel stood at her stall across the square and did not move.

She told herself it was strategy. One of us stays free, she told herself. If they take everyone, someone has to be left to remember where things are hidden. She was very good at telling herself things. She'd had twelve years of practice; it was practically a gift of its own.

Wren came by around midday — the young royal scribe with ink-stained cuffs and a face like a question that hadn't found its answer yet. Wren bought a compass charm off her last week, and it had *worked*, which had frightened them both into a silence that still hadn't fully lifted. Now Wren lingered at the stall, ostensibly examining a knotwork bracelet, actually watching the line.

"They say it's voluntary," Wren murmured.

"They say a lot of things in ink."

"You didn't register."

"I sell lucky acorns, scribe. What would I register *as*?"

Wren's eyes came up, quick and sharp, and for a moment Hazel thought: *this child knows*. But Wren only bought the bracelet, paid too much for it as usual, and walked back toward the registrar's office with their shoulders drawn up around their ears like a person walking into weather.

By evening the line had gone, and something else had replaced it.

Soldiers moved down the streets of Copperlane in pairs, quiet as bookkeepers, carrying little pots of gray ash mixed with tallow. At each registered household they pressed a sigil to the door — a circle with a line through it, neat as a wax seal, gone in a moment. Marked. Counted. *Protected.*

Hazel watched them mark Tam's door from the shadow of her own doorway three houses down, and her stomach did something slow and cold, because she knew ash sigils. Not from books. From twelve years ago, from the last purge, when they hadn't been marks of protection at all but tallies — a way of keeping track of stock.

She should have warned him. She'd had a whole day. She could have gone to him in the dark, could have said *Tam, listen to me, I'll draw you a way out, I can still do that much, I've kept the one thing I shouldn't have kept precisely so I could do that much* — and instead she had stood behind her stall selling nothing-charms and telling herself stories about strategy.

The soldiers marked her own door last. Of course they did. She hadn't registered either, and apparently the crown's memory for doors was better than its memory for promises. The sigil went on plain and gray, and the soldier gave her a bored glance, and moved on.

A circle with a line through it. She stared at it a long time. It took her embarrassingly long to realize why it looked familiar: it was the surveyor's mark for *unmapped territory*. Here be nothing. Here be no one worth counting.

Or here, said the older part of her brain, the part that had survived the Sundering, *here is everything we haven't collected yet.*

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat up with a single candle and her old maps — the real ones, the living ones, inked on paper that remembered being trees — and she traced escape routes out of Vellmark with one finger until her eyes burned. There. The culvert under the ash gates. The drover's road that wasn't on any crown map because she'd made sure of it herself, years ago, erasing it from every copy she ever drew for the court. She had spent twelve years building exits out of other people's maps, and never once used one herself, because leaving meant admitting the hiding wasn't working.

Somewhere past midnight, or near enough to midnight that it hardly mattered, the dogs started.

Not barking — Vellmark's dogs were too well-fed and cynical to bark. This was a low, collective whining, rising from yards all down Copperlane at once, the sound animals make when the air goes wrong. Hazel's candle guttered though there was no draft. Outside, the rain stopped mid-fall, every drop hanging a half-second too long, like the sky had hiccupped.

Then boots. Many boots, moving not in the bookkeeper-quiet of the afternoon but fast, hard, deliberate, and stopping — she knew it before she heard it, knew it the way you know a stone will drop before it leaves your hand — at Tam's door.

She was at her window before she'd decided to be. Down in the street, lanterns swung, and six soldiers in the king's gray broke Tam's door in two blows, and there was shouting, and there was Tam's voice — *not angry, that was the terrible thing, his voice was still gentle* — saying, "There's been a mistake, my daughter is asleep, please, she's only seven—" and a soldier saying flatly, "By order of the crown, Tam of Copperlane, you're wanted for questioning regarding your census entry," and Tam saying, "But it was only a census," with genuine, heartbreaking confusion, as if the words themselves might be the mistake, as if someone had simply written the wrong thing down and it could be corrected if everyone stayed polite.

Sorrel's scream cut through everything.

Hazel's hand was on the latch. Her whole body was on the latch. Every exit she'd ever drawn blazed in her mind at once — the culvert, the drover's road, the little gate behind the tannery that only she could see — and she could be down those stairs in ten heartbeats, and she could—

Do what? Against six soldiers with a candlestick and a talent she'd spent twelve years pretending was a rumor?

She stood in the dark of her own window, in the house with the unmapped-marked door, and she watched them bring Tam out. He wasn't fighting. He looked back once — not at his door, not at his screaming daughter held in a neighbor's arms, but up, at the row of windows, at hers, and his face in the lantern light was not accusing. That was the worst of it. He looked almost apologetic, as if he were sorry to be causing all this trouble, sorry to be dragged through his own street in his nightshirt, sorry that his friend had to see.

Then the cart door shut, and the wheels ground away south, toward the Sundered Marches, and the rain remembered itself and came down all at once like it had been holding its breath too.

Hazel sat back down among her maps. Her hands were shaking too badly to hold a pen, which was ironic, since drawing was the one thing she could still do, the one thing that mattered now, and she had missed her chance to use it by exactly one day and one coward's worth of caution.

*I could have warned him,* she thought, and the thought did not pass through her; it settled in, took off its coat, and made itself comfortable.

Down the street, muffled by walls and rain, Sorrel was still crying, and no one — not the neighbors, not the soldiers' distant laughter, not the crown with its promises in good coats — was going to tell that child where her father had gone.

Hazel looked at the culvert route glowing faintly on the old paper under her fingertips, and understood, finally, with the clarity that only arrives after it's useless, that hiding had never been survival. It had just been disappearing slowly, in installments, and tonight the crown had come to collect the rest of Tam.

She blew out the candle. In the dark she said aloud, to no one, in a voice she barely recognized: "Hold on, Tam. I'm coming. I should have come sooner, but I'm coming."

And far to the south, in a registrar's office lit past midnight, a young royal scribe sat staring at a ledger full of names — Tam of Copperlane among them, entered in a neat column headed *REQUISITION* — and began, very quietly, to shake.
