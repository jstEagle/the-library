## 3. What the Compass Points To

Wren Ashcombe had copied eleven thousand names in four years of service to the Crown, and had never once wondered where the names went after they left his pen.

That was, he would later reflect, rather the point of him.

He was a small, neat person in a large, untidy office — the third-floor scriptorium of the Chancery of Records, where the light came gray through leaded windows and the ink smelled of iron and old decisions. Wren's desk was the one nearest the door, because Wren was the one who fetched things. He fetched ledgers. He fetched sealing wax. He fetched the census rolls from the Registrar's locked cabinet, carried them to Master Corvane's desk, and fetched them back again without reading them.

Scribes who read were scribes who asked questions. Scribes who asked questions were scribes who got reassigned to the salt-tax annexes.

Wren liked his desk by the door. He liked his inkwell with the chipped rim. He liked knowing exactly what each day would hold: copy, seal, fetch, carry, go home to his narrow room above the chandler's, sleep, repeat. There was a comfort in it, like being a well-kept ledger oneself — every entry in its place.

The trouble began on a Tuesday, with a dropped key.

Master Corvane's cabinet key slipped from the registrar's ring as he handed over the morning rolls, and rang against the floorboards with a bright, terrible little note. Both men froze. Then Corvane laughed — a dry sound, like paper folding — and said, "Well. Pick it up, boy."

And Wren, kneeling there with the key cold in his palm, did not put it back on the ring.

He told himself he simply fumbled it. He told himself he returned it exactly as received. And mostly this was true. But that night, when the scriptorium emptied and the candles burned down to their stubs, Wren found himself still at his desk with the census rolls for Vellmark spread before him, and the key in his pocket, and his heart going like a moth against glass.

*Just a look,* he told himself. *Just to know what I'm copying.*

He let himself into the Registrar's office. The lock turned as if it had been waiting for him, which was the first thing that should have frightened him.

The census ledgers were arranged by district, then by street, then by name. Wren knew the format; he'd helped design the columns. *Name. Gift suspected. Date registered. Disposition.* That last column had always been filled in with a tidy abbreviation he'd assumed meant *filed* or *archived*. FLD. Filed. Fine. Everyone filed away somewhere, safe in the Crown's paper embrace.

Except that tonight, working backward from the most recent entries, Wren noticed the dates. Registered in Bellmonth. Disposition entered within days. Registered in Harrowfair — disposition within days. Always days. No archive held anything that fast. Archives took seasons. Archives took committees and dust and arguments about shelving.

FLD didn't mean filed.

He found the key in an older ledger, a marginal gloss in a clerk's cramped hand, half a century old: *fld. = forwarded. Marches.*

Forwarded to the Sundered Marches.

Wren sat down on the Registrar's stool because his legs had opinions of their own. He pulled the Vellmark roll toward him and began, very carefully, to match entries against the transport manifests stacked in the second drawer — manifests he had also copied, also never read. Carts leaving the city under writ of the Royal Purveyor. Departure dates matching disposition dates. Destinations listed only as *the works*, which was not a place. Places had names. This was a direction and a silence.

Eleven thousand names. He thought of them all flowing through his pen like water through a sluice-gate, and him standing at the mechanism, turning the wheel, proud of how smoothly everything ran.

There was a column he'd never noticed before, added recently, in Corvane's own hand: *Yield.*

Beside certain names, a number. Beside Tam Osgar, weather-worker, registered three days past: a number larger than most.

Tam Osgar. Wren knew the name because it had crossed his desk twice — once in the census intake, once in something else, a requisition order he'd copied last week without thinking. *One (1) subject, weather-gifted, priority conveyance, night cart.* He'd copied it between an order for sealing wax and a complaint about mice.

Priority conveyance. Night cart.

Wren put his head down on the ledger and breathed the smell of iron and old decisions until his stomach agreed to behave.

*They're not archiving anyone,* he thought. *They're shipping them. To the border. Where nothing comes back from.*

And beneath that thought, quieter, worse, in the voice of a man who has spent four years learning not to wonder: *You knew. Some part of you always knew. That's why you never read.*

* * *

He should have gone home. He should have gone home and locked his door and told himself that clerks do not make history, clerks make copies, and copies are not responsible for what the original does.

Instead he stayed up all night with the manifests, and by dawn he had assembled something no one had ever asked him to assemble: the truth, in his own small neat hand, on three sheets of paper.

The registered were conveyed to the Sundered Marches within days of registration. They did not return. Requisition orders described them as *material*. One entry, in the king's own cipher — which Wren could read, because cipher-work was considered beneath a mere copyist and therefore taught to him thoroughly — referred to the border works as *the feeding*, and to the gifted as *provision*.

Provision. As in provisions. As in bread and salt.

As in: the border was hungry, and the Crown was feeding it people. Not their lives — the manifests recorded payment to families, which was somehow more obscene than murder would have been. Their *memories*. Wren couldn't have said how he knew; the cipher only said *extraction*, *yield*, *store*. But he had grown up on nursemaids' tales of the Sundering, of the day the sky over the Marches tore and the two realms began to bleed at the edges, and every tale agreed on one thing: the border was not a wall. It was a wound. And wounds did not close on stone and soldiers alone.

Someone had found something that could seal it. Something living. Something that remembered.

At some point the bells rang for morning watch and Wren discovered his candle had drowned in its own wax hours ago. His eyes felt full of sand. On his desk lay the three sheets, and beside them, the compass charm.

He'd bought it a week ago from the charm-seller in Tanner's Row — the woman with the tired eyes whose stall everyone said was harmless. He'd bought it as a lark, a joke gift for himself: a real charm, imagine that, in a city where magic was a crime and charms were brass and wishful thinking. Except it wasn't brass and wishful thinking. It pointed. Not north — it had done that once, briefly, out of politeness — but somewhere else. Somewhere specific. It had pointed at her, across the market crowd, with a certainty that had made them both go pale, and she had pressed it into his hand and said, too quietly, *Don't come back here.*

He hadn't. Until now he hadn't understood why the thought of going back felt less like danger than like duty.

Because she had known. He saw it now, replaying the moment: she hadn't been alarmed that the charm worked. She'd been alarmed that *his* worked — that whatever she'd wrapped inside that bit of brass had woken for *him*, had chosen him, or chosen to be seen by him. She'd known what he was only beginning to suspect: that the world was full of quiet gifts, and the Crown had started collecting them, and a compass that pointed true was not a trinket. It was a confession.

Wren looked at the three sheets. Then he folded them small and sewed them into the lining of his coat, because a scribe's first instinct is preservation and a scribe's second instinct, newly learned, is that paper must survive even if the scribe doesn't.

Then he picked up the compass.

It swung once, twice, and settled — not north, not toward the Marches, but west and slightly south, toward Tanner's Row, steady as a heartbeat.

"All right," Wren said aloud, to the empty scriptorium, to the eleven thousand names, to himself. "All right."

* * *

Tanner's Row at midmorning was all smoke and haggling, and Hazel Kavanagh's stall sat in its usual place like a boat tied to a dock — present, patient, pretending to be nothing. She was wrapping a luck-knot for a fishwife when Wren arrived, and he watched her see him, and he watched her decide, visibly, not to know him.

"Charms," she said, in a bright market voice. "Boneset, bright-eye, safe-travel. Nothing in any of them, guaranteed."

"I need to talk to you."

"Guaranteed," she repeated, "by royal warrant of my own good sense. Three coppers the knot."

"Hazel." Her name came out of him before he'd decided to use it; he'd learned it off the census intake forms, *Kavanagh, Hazel, cartographer (former), gift unconfirmed*, and hearing it in his own mouth made both of them flinch. He lowered his voice to almost nothing. "I copy the ledgers. The census ledgers. I know what FLD means. I know where Tam Osgar went."

The fishwife drifted off with her luck-knot, sensing weather. Hazel's hands went still over her wrappings. Up close, in daylight, Wren could see what the market distance had hidden: she was afraid the way long-married people are tired — constantly, thoroughly, in a way that had become furniture.

"You shouldn't be here," she said.

"No," Wren agreed. "I really shouldn't." He set the compass on the stall between them. It swung and pointed — not north. At her. At the river gate. At the Marches beyond, all at once, its needle trembling like a struck string. "It's been pointing at things all week. I think it's been trying to tell me what I'm too much of a coward to read. So I read it anyway. And now I can't unsee it, and I've discovered that unseeing was apparently the entire job description."

Hazel glanced left, right, at the crowd. "Stall. Smile. Buy something."

He bought a boneset charm he did not believe in, and she talked while she wrapped it, her lips barely moving, her eyes on her hands.

"Tell me," she said.

So he told her. Quietly, in the language of inventory, because it was the only way he could get through it: transports, manifests, dispositions, yield. The word *provision*. The families paid. The night carts. Tam Osgar, priority conveyance, and the number beside his name that meant his memories were worth more than most — a gentle man who mended roofs and coaxed rain off hay-fields, worth more dead-in-every-way-that-mattered than alive.

When he finished, Hazel was silent for a long time. A customer approached; she turned them away with a smile so wrong it sent them off quickly. Then she said, "The border. You're saying the king is feeding the border. With *them*. With what they remember."

"With what they are," Wren said. "Memories are just how it's measured. Yield." He laughed, a horrible small sound. "I used to think my job was harmless. Ink and paper. Paper never hurt anyone. But paper is where it starts, isn't it? Someone writes a column called *yield*, and someone copies it, and someone carries it, and none of us ever reads it, and that's how a kingdom eats its own people — one unread line at a time."

Hazel looked at him for a long moment. Something in her face shifted — not softening exactly; more like a door unbarred an inch.

"Why are you telling me?" she asked. "You don't know me. I sold you a compass. For all you know I'm the worst person in Vellmark to trust."

"The compass points at you," Wren said. "It hasn't pointed anywhere else since. I checked. I've been checking obsessively. I'm aware of how that sounds." He swallowed. "And because twelve years ago you stopped being a cartographer and started selling lies in a market stall, and I think you did it for the same reason I never read the ledgers. Because seeing is expensive. And I've just paid everything I have, and I find I'd rather not be the only one who's seen it."

The wind came down Tanner's Row, smelling of the river and, faintly, of ash — though no one had burned anything. Hazel's gaze went past him, west, the way the compass pointed, toward the gates and the grey country beyond.

"They took Tam last night," she said. "His daughter's called Sorrel. She's seven." Her jaw tightened. "If what you're saying is true, then every day we stand here is a day the carts keep rolling."

"It is true," Wren said. "I copied it myself. That's the one thing I'm sure of — I have excellent handwriting."

Something cracked in Hazel's face that might, in better light, have been a laugh. She reached under the stall and began, with great efficiency, putting her charms away — the harmless ones, the brass and wishful thinking, twelve years of hiding packed into a basket in under a minute.

"Don't make me regret this," she said. "And Wren—" she pronounced his name properly now, having clearly dug it out of the same census page, "—if we're doing this, understand what we are. We're not heroes. We're a clerk and a charm-seller. We run, we hide, we stay small."

"Yes," Wren said. "Absolutely. Agreed."

The compass in his pocket swung gently, insistently, toward the river gate — and beyond it, though neither of them said so, toward the child named Sorrel, and the wild country past the border where the truth was kept.

"We leave before the ash gates open at dawn," Hazel said. "Bring your three sheets. Bring ink. If we're going to be small, we may as well be *useful*."

Behind them, unnoticed, a soldier in crown black paused at the mouth of Tanner's Row, marked the shuttered stall with a smear of grey ash on the wood, and walked on.
