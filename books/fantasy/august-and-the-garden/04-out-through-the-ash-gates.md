## 4. Out Through the Ash Gates

Hazel had always thought she would know the moment her life ended. She'd imagined it would come with trumpets, or at least a knock on the door and a formal accusation. Instead it came as a smell: woodsmoke where there should have been bread.

She stood in the doorway of Tam's house on Cobbler's Row with Wren pressed behind her like a nervous shadow, and the smell was coming from inside — not from the hearth, which was cold, but from the little iron stove where Tam's daughter kept her secrets warm.

"Sorrel," Hazel said softly, into the dark. "It's Hazel. Your da sent me."

A long silence. Then, from beneath the floorboards — because of course the child hid under the floorboards; Tam had built that hatch the same year he'd built the weather-vane that lied about rain — came a small, suspicious voice.

"Da doesn't send people. Da sends *notes*. With drawings."

"He drew you a fox."

Another silence, longer, in which Hazel could hear Wren breathing like a man about to be sick over the side of a boat. Then the hatch creaked up six inches, and two amber eyes looked out at them, catching the streetlight the way an animal's do.

"Prove you're really Hazel," Sorrel said. "The real Hazel smells like ink and onions."

"That's — I don't—"

"She's right," Wren whispered miserably. "You do."

Hazel leaned down to the gap in the floor. "Sorrel Kavanagh-adjacent child of my oldest friend, your father was taken by the king's men tonight, and if we don't leave this city before the ash gates open at dawn, they will come back for you too. Now please, for the love of all small mercies, get out from under my feet."

The eyes blinked. The hatch opened the rest of the way. And a girl climbed out — eight years old, maybe nine, wrapped in a coat far too big for her, with ears that were very slightly, unmistakably, pointed at the tips, and a rust-red tail curled around one ankle like a scarf she hadn't decided whether to wear.

Hazel had known, of course. Twelve years of shared suppers and Tam never once saying it aloud, and Hazel never once asking. You didn't ask. That was the whole architecture of hiding: you built your silences carefully, load-bearing, and you prayed no one leaned on them.

"You're the fox-child," Wren breathed. "You're actually — the census ledgers call your kind *crossborn*, there's a whole subcategory, I transcribed forty pages of—"

"I'm right here," said Sorrel. "And you're the scribe who smells like government."

"We need to go," Hazel said. "Now. Before first bell."

* * *

They went the back ways, because Hazel knew the back ways the way other people knew their own hands. Vellmark at night was a city holding its breath: shutters drawn, dogs quiet, and on every third door, the fresh smear of an ash sigil that still glistened faintly in the lamplight. Marked houses. Houses where someone had registered, or someone had been suspected, or someone had simply been unlucky enough to have a neighbor with ambitions.

"They're everywhere," Wren murmured, hugging the satchel of stolen ledgers to their chest. "The sigils. I helped design the stencil. Gods forgive me, I *inked the stencil*."

"You can feel guilty while walking," Hazel said. "Walk faster."

Sorrel trotted between them, hood up, tail stuffed down one trouser leg in a manner that fooled absolutely no one and made her walk like a soldier with a cramp. Every few streets she stopped, nose lifting, and reported in a whisper: "Two soldiers by the well. One drunk. One bored." Once: "Cat. False alarm." And once, in a voice that went thin: "That's Mrs. Ellery's door. She does charms. Nice ones. They work a bit."

The ash mark on Mrs. Ellery's door was still wet.

Hazel did not let herself think about it. Thinking about it was a luxury shaped exactly like a knife.

They cut through the tannery yard, over the wall behind the chandler's, down the long stair that smelled of salt and rotting kelp, and came out at last above the Ash Gates themselves — the twin arches in the old sea-wall through which every road out of Vellmark passed, named for the color of the stone and, lately, for what the crown left of the people who walked through them unregistered.

The gates were closed. Of course they were closed. But the road beyond was empty in the pre-dawn gray, and for one foolish moment Hazel allowed herself to believe the worst part was over.

Then Sorrel's ears went flat.

"Riders," she said. "Behind us. Two. Fast."

And ahead, from the gatehouse, torchlight bloomed, and a voice carried across the water like a blade laid flat on a table:

"There. The woman, the scribe, and the crossborn. Captain Vane's compliments — you're wanted alive. Mostly."

* * *

Ilsa Vane did not ride like a man or a monster. She rode like a woman doing arithmetic, which was somehow worse. Hazel watched her rein in at the top of the seawall stair with the torches fanned around her and thought, absurdly, *she has good posture.* Then she thought, less absurdly, *we are going to die on a beach.*

"The scribe comes quietly," Vane called, "and the child is crown property by treaty law, and you—" her eyes found Hazel's across fifty yards of shingle, "—you I've been looking for since the census began. The charm seller. The one whose charms work."

"They don't," Hazel called back. "Terrible quality. Full refund policy."

Vane almost smiled. It was the most frightening thing Hazel had seen all night, and she had spent the night watching doors get marked.

"Run if you like," Vane said. "The gates are barred, the road is mine, and the tide is coming in. There is nowhere on this coast that isn't either mine or the sea's."

Wren grabbed Hazel's sleeve. "There's nowhere," they whispered. "Hazel, there's *nowhere*."

And that — that exact moment, with the torches closing and the tide hissing black over the shingle and a child's cold hand finding hers — was when Hazel stopped being afraid.

Not because the fear went away. Because it finally had somewhere to *go.*

Twelve years she had hidden her gift behind a stall of harmless nothing-charms, and she had told herself it was wisdom, and some nights she had told herself it was cowardice, and both had been true, and neither had ever once been *useful*. But here, now, with nowhere left to hide — hiding was finished, and what remained underneath was the thing itself, the true thing, the thing she'd been born with and buried alive:

Hazel Kavanagh could see the doors in the world.

Not doors, exactly. Not always. Places where the world had been sewn together badly after the Sundering, where the stitches showed if you knew how to look — and she had always known how to look, since she was small enough to believe everyone saw the shimmer in the air over the old sea-wall, the place where the stones remembered being something else.

She turned toward the seawall below the gatehouse. Old masonry, older than Vellmark, older than the crown that squatted on it. And there — faint as breath on glass, visible only because she was finally, desperately, *looking* — the seam.

"Sorrel," she said. "Can you run on sand?"

"I can run on *water* if I'm scared enough."

"Wonderful. Everyone hold onto me and do not let go, and whatever happens, do not stop believing I know what I'm doing, even though I am currently inventing it."

"What?" said Wren.

Hazel took them both by the hand, fixed her eyes on the shimmering seam in the wall where a drainage arch had been bricked over three hundred years ago, and did the one thing she had sworn never to do in public.

She reached for the memory of the map.

Not a paper map — the real one, the living one, the one she'd drawn at fourteen in a notebook she'd burned the same summer, the coastline of everything, the secret cartography of a world with loose threads in it. She held it in her mind the way you hold a match to a wick, and she felt the cost begin immediately, small and sharp: the name of her childhood street going soft at the edges, the tune Tam used to whistle losing its middle bars—

*Later,* she told herself. *Grieve later. Run now.*

The bricked-over arch *shivered.*

"Captain!" one of the torchmen shouted. "The wall — captain, the wall's—"

Where there had been dead stone there was now a doorway, ragged-edged, glowing faintly at the seams like a coal remembering fire, and through it — not the road, not the beach, but a green dark full of the smell of rain on leaves that had never grown on any coast Hazel knew.

"Go," Hazel said, and shoved, and they went — Sorrel first, quick as spilled mercury, then Wren with a sound like a man stepping off a cliff in a dream, and then Hazel herself, one foot in Vellmark and one foot in forever, hearing behind her Vane's voice crack out — *"Stop them! STOP THEM—"* and boots on shingle, and a torch spinning end over end through the air—

The torch hit the threshold and burst apart into moths.

Actual moths. Dozens of them, wheeling up into the green dark, and Hazel stumbled through after her companions as the doorway snapped shut behind her like a mouth, and the last thing she heard from the world she'd been born in was Ilsa Vane shouting orders to a wall that no longer had a door in it.

* * *

They lay in wet ferns for a long time, all three of them, breathing.

Above them stood trees — enormous, patient, silver-barked, hung with moss that glowed very faintly, the way things glow when they've never been taught not to. Somewhere nearby water moved. The air tasted like the first minute of morning anywhere, ever.

"We're through," Wren said at last, in the voice of someone reading their own obituary. "We're actually through. We crossed the border. Illegally. Through a *wall.*"

"Yes," said Hazel.

"I abandoned the crown. I stole state documents. I committed treason via *stonework.*"

"Yes."

"And you—" Wren sat up, ferns cascading off them, and stared at Hazel with an expression caught between awe and accusation. "You opened a door in a *wall.* With your *face.* What else can you do? What else have you been *hiding?*"

Hazel opened her mouth to answer and found, to her surprise, that she didn't know. Twelve years of hiding had been so thorough that even she had lost the inventory. She knew only this: the notebook-map in her mind felt awake now, restless, hungry, its lines shifting like something breathing. And she could feel — distantly, the way you feel weather coming — that the country around them was not fixed. That here, geography might listen, if someone spoke to it clearly enough.

Something small and warm pressed against her side. Sorrel had burrowed under Hazel's arm without asking, the way children and cats do when they've decided a matter is settled, and her tail had escaped her trouser leg at last and lay across Hazel's knees like a claim of ownership.

"Da used to say you were more than a charm seller," Sorrel said, into the dark. "He said you were a *map* of something." A pause. "Is he okay? Where they took him?"

Hazel looked at the glowing moss, at the alien stars just beginning to show through the canopy, at the scribe shaking beside her and the child trusting against her ribs, and she found she could not lie, so she told the truth instead.

"I don't know," she said. "But I know where they took him. And I know how to find it. And Sorrel — I'm going to draw us a path all the way there."

Somewhere in the deep of the unmapped country, something heard her say it, and answered — a low, distant sound, like a door unlocking, or a page turning.

The path, when Hazel looked, was already beginning to appear.
