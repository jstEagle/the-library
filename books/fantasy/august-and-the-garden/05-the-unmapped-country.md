## 5. The Unmapped Country

The first thing Hazel learned about the far realm was that it did not appreciate being hurried.

They had come through the gate in a scramble of elbows and terror — Sorrel clutched to Wren's chest like a stolen ledger, Maren's staff jabbing everyone in the kidneys — and had tumbled out onto a hillside that was, by every law Hazel had ever trusted, impossible. The grass was green at the top of the hill and silver at the bottom. The sky went on for what looked like three days in every direction. And the river they'd crossed on the near side of the border was here too, running uphill beside them, politely ignoring everything water was supposed to do.

"It does that," Maren said, catching Hazel's expression. "Water remembers being rain. Sometimes it wants to go back up."

"That's not how rivers work."

"Neither is your side of the border, dear. You've just agreed to pretend."

Sorrel, meanwhile, had stopped being frightened approximately four seconds after arrival and was now crouched over a patch of moss with her ears — which appeared and disappeared depending on her mood, fox-gifted as she was — standing straight up with delight. "The ground hums," she reported. "Listen. It hums like Tam's kettle."

Hazel did listen. The ground did hum. She decided, privately, that she would think about that later, when she had spare terror available.

They walked. That was the whole plan: walk, keep low, find Hollowfen, don't die. But walking proved complicated, because the country would not hold still.

It wasn't that things moved, exactly. It was that distance refused to behave. A stand of white-barked trees that looked half a mile off took them most of an afternoon to reach, and when they arrived, the trees were enormous and old and clearly had never been anywhere else — but the hill behind them had quietly become a different hill. When Hazel turned around to check their backtrail, the notch in the ridge where they'd descended was gone. Not hidden. Gone. In its place stood a smooth green shoulder of land with no memory of them at all.

"We're lost," Wren said. Wren said it the way scribes say things — flatly, precisely, with the air of someone reading aloud from an official document. "We have been lost since midday. I want it noted that I said so first."

"You're not lost," Maren said placidly. "You're unrecorded. Different problem entirely."

"What's the difference?"

"Lost means there's a right place and you can't find it. Unrecorded means the place hasn't decided to exist yet." Maren tapped her staff against the turf. "This country runs on memory, child. Distance here is just a story places tell about each other. If nothing remembers the road between here and there, there is no road. You walk and walk and arrive nowhere."

Hazel felt something turn over in her chest. Twelve years she had kept her gift folded up small, drawing maps of ordinary cities with ordinary streets, pretending the lines were just lines. But she had always known — the way you know a held breath — that maps were never really about paper. A map was a promise. *This way lies home.* She had drawn promises all her life without understanding that somewhere, in some countries, the promises came true.

"Maren," she said slowly. "If nothing remembers the road... could something be made to remember it?"

The old hedge-witch looked at her for a long moment. Her eyes were the color of pond water, and about as easy to see into.

"Show me," she said.

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of one of the white-barked trees, whose bark peeled away in long curls exactly like parchment, which Hazel tried very hard not to read anything into. Sorrel fell asleep against Wren's side mid-sentence, still arguing that the humming ground counted as music and therefore deserved dancing. Wren, who had spent the entire day radiating the dignity of a man betrayed by geography itself, sat perfectly still so as not to wake her, and pretended this was a strategic choice rather than a soft-hearted one.

Hazel took out her kit. Charcoal, ink, the good pen Tam had given her six years ago — the thought of Tam arrived and she set it down again, gently, like setting down something breakable. There would be time to grieve later. Right now there was a hillside that didn't know where it was.

She drew.

Not a map of the land — there was nothing yet to map. She drew the *road*. A line from the tree to the ridge beyond, curving where a road ought sensibly to curve, avoiding the marshy ground she'd noted that afternoon. She drew it the way she drew all her roads: carefully, lovingly, with the little confidence-marks cartographers use, the ticks and dashes that say *I have walked here, trust me.*

The charcoal moved strangely under her hand. Warm. Almost eager.

And then the world leaned in to look.

There was no better word for it. The dark hillside seemed to tilt toward her sketchbook like a curious animal. The line on the page shimmered — actually shimmered, ink-black turning briefly to silver — and out on the slope, where her road should have been if the world were reasonable, the grass bent apart into a path. Bare earth, packed firm, winding up toward the ridge exactly as she'd drawn it. As if it had always been there. As if the country had been waiting all day for someone to tell it what it was.

"Oh," Hazel said.

"Yes," said Maren.

"Oh," Hazel said again, with considerably more feeling.

Wren leaned over, looked at the path, looked at the sketchbook, looked at Hazel, and said, in the voice of a royal scribe confronting a discrepancy in the accounts, "That is going to cause problems with the census forms."

"Wren."

"I'm only saying. There's no column for 'invented a road.'"

"There's no column for any of this."

"Which is my point."

But Wren was smiling. Hazel could hear it even in the dark, and it did something warm and dangerous to her chest, because twelve years of hiding meant twelve years of people looking at her gift with fear or hunger or both, and nobody — nobody — had ever looked at it like it was funny.

* * *

The cost arrived the next morning, wearing the disguise of an ordinary question.

They followed her road up over the ridge — her road, hers, the thought made her giddy — and down into a valley full of pale flowers that rang faintly when the wind moved them, like a thousand tiny struck bells. It was beautiful. It was the most beautiful thing Hazel had ever walked through, and she was composing the map legend in her head (*valley, ringing; flowers, pale; proceed with wonder*) when Sorrel tugged her sleeve.

"Hazel? What was the song?"

"What song?"

"The one you were humming yesterday. At the gate. Before we ran." Sorrel's head tipped, fox-curious. "You stopped in the middle. I wanted to hear the end."

Hazel opened her mouth to answer and found — nothing. Not a blank, exactly. Worse than a blank. A shape where the song should have been, the way a footprint shows where a foot was. She knew she had known a song. She knew it had mattered. She could feel the edges of it, the space it had occupied, the way you feel a tooth that isn't there anymore.

"I don't remember it," she said. Her voice came out steady, which she was grateful for, because inside she was suddenly very cold. "Sorrel. I don't remember the song."

Maren had stopped walking. The old woman's face was unreadable, but her silence had a terrible specificity to it.

"When did you draw the road?" Maren asked.

"Last night. Why—"

"What else did you draw?"

Hazel thought back. The road. The confidence-marks. And before the road, idly, while waiting for the others to sleep — a little doodle in the margin. A face. Just a face, quick and fond, the kind of face you draw when you're thinking of someone. She hadn't even looked at whose.

She flipped open the sketchbook with hands that had begun, very slightly, to shake.

The margin was empty. Not smudged, not erased. Empty, with the same footprint-shape where the drawing had been. And hovering behind the emptiness, patient as weather, came the question she could not answer: *whose face?*

She knew she loved whoever it had been. The love was still there, intact, warm, pointing at a door that now opened onto nothing.

"The country doesn't take," Maren said quietly. "I want you to understand that. It trades. Memory is the only coin it knows. You paid for the road." She put a hand, light as a leaf, on Hazel's arm. "Small things first. A song. A face. The land takes what you won't miss until it's gone. But it will not stay small, Hazel Kavanagh. Roads are cheap. Rivers are dearer. If you ever try to draw a mountain—" she didn't finish.

Wren had gone very pale. "Then she stops drawing," Wren said. It came out fast and fierce. "That's the end of it. We walk badly. We get lost. Nobody invents any more roads."

"And how far do you suppose we walk badly," Maren asked, "before Vane's hunters find us? They'll have crossed by now. Hunters carry harrow-iron; the land can't hide from it. Your choice isn't between drawing and not drawing, scribe. It's between paying a little at a time, on your own terms, or paying everything at once, on theirs."

Nobody said anything. Somewhere in the ringing valley, the wind moved through the flowers, and a thousand tiny bells played a song that Hazel almost recognized, and couldn't.

* * *

They walked on. Hazel rationed herself the way she'd once rationed food in a bad winter: one small drawing a day, no more, and never anything she loved. She drew a bridge over a gorge and lost the name of a street in Vellmark. She drew a sheltering copse before a night of rain and lost the taste of a particular bread she'd liked. Each time, she told the others. She insisted on telling them — made it a ritual, sitting by whatever fire the country allowed, announcing the price like a merchant counting change. *A bridge costs a street name. Fair. A copse costs the smell of ink. Annoying, but fair.*

"It's so you can't lie to yourself," she explained to Wren, who had asked why bother.

"It's so *we* can't lie to *you*," Wren corrected. "You'd pay silently. You've been paying silently your whole life. I've watched you do it for two weeks." A pause, and then, more gently than Wren ever said anything: "We're keeping the books now. All of us. That's what a fellowship is, I think. I read about it once."

Sorrel took to sitting next to Hazel during the evening accounting, solemn as a magistrate, and after each confession she would offer something in exchange — a fact about foxes, a riddle, a fragment of a lullaby from the far realm, sung in a language with too many vowels. "So you're not less," she explained. "You're traded-even."

Hazel did not cry at this. Mostly.

On the sixth day, the country changed its mind about them. Hazel felt it first — a loosening, a welcome, the ground humming under her boots in a rhythm that matched, she realized with a start, her own heartbeat. The white trees gave way to drowned forest, black trunks rising from green water, lanterns of pale fungus glowing between the branches. And far off across the flooded miles, lights: a village grown into the drowning wood like a pearl in an oyster.

"Hollowfen," Maren said, and her voice, for the first time since the border, wavered. "It remembers me. After sixty years, it remembers me."

Hazel got out her sketchbook, meaning to draw the last stretch of path — one small price, a fair price — and found her hand hesitating over the page. Because she understood something now, something the country had been teaching her all week in its patient, expensive way: the land remembered what was given freely, and it remembered *how* it was given. Every road she'd bought with a stolen song was already beginning to fade at the edges. But the little shelter-copse, the one she'd drawn while laughing at one of Wren's dry jokes — that one had rooted. That one was staying.

The covenant, Maren had called it, back in the gray city. Hazel was beginning to suspect she finally knew what the word meant.

She closed the sketchbook. "No drawing," she said. "We knock."

They were halfway across the first causeway when the lanterns ahead guttered all at once, every one, along a mile of drowned forest — and out of the dark between the trees came a sound Hazel had hoped never to hear in this realm: the thin, wrong whistle of harrow-iron, singing.

Vane had crossed.
