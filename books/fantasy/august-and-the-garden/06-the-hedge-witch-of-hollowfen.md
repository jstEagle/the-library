## 6. The Hedge-Witch of Hollowfen

Wren had been a scribe for nine years, and in all that time they had never once written the word *drowned* without also writing *dead*. It was one of those small certainties that made ledgers bearable: drowned meant dead, taxes meant taxes, and the king's seal meant the king's word. Three days in the unmapped country had already broken two of those certainties, and by the time they reached Hollowfen, Wren was actively shopping for a third to lose.

The village announced itself as a smell first — green and mineral, like rain on old stone — and then as light, filtering up through water in long amber columns. Because Hollowfen did not sit beside the flooded forest. It grew *inside* it.

"Is it under the lake?" Sorrel asked, pressing her fox-nosed face against nothing in particular. She had taken to riding on Hazel's shoulders when her legs tired, which was often, and which she pretended not to enjoy.

"It's *in* it," Hazel said. Wren noticed she said things like that now — flat, certain, the way she'd once said "the road bends north here." Since the ford, since the year of memories she'd traded away, something in Hazel spoke with the voice of the country itself. It frightened Wren more than Captain Vane had.

"The trees hold the water up," Hazel went on. "Or the water holds the trees down. The map can't decide, so neither does the world." She smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "It's very rude, cartographically speaking."

They descended a stair of roots — actual stairs, worn smooth in the centers, someone had *lived* here — into a green-gold twilight where houses clung to tree trunks like barnacles. Water ran through channels between them, clear as glass, full of fish that watched passersby with what Wren could only describe as judgment. Lanterns hung from branches, lit with no visible flame. A woman hanging washing paused to look at them, then at Sorrel, then at Hazel's hands — and something in her face closed like a shop shutter.

"They know we're from over the border," Wren murmured.

"They know you're from Vellmark," Hazel corrected quietly. "That's worse."

An old man mending a net pointed them, wordlessly, toward the largest tree at the village heart — a willow so vast its trunk had rooms, its roots had streets, and its lowest boughs had become, apparently, a front porch. A sign hung there, hand-painted and aggressively cheerful:

**MAREN ODDFOOT — HEDGE-WITCH. CURES, CHARMS, CURSES (REASONABLE RATES). NO REFUNDS ON CURSES.**

Below it, smaller, in different ink: **especially no refunds on curses.**

"She sounds charming," Wren said.

"She sounds like someone who's been waiting a long time," Hazel said.

The door opened before they knocked.

Maren Oddfoot was small, round, and approximately as old as the tree she lived in, with white hair braided into a crown and eyes the color of pond water — green going gray going green again, as if they couldn't settle. She looked at Hazel for a long moment. Then at Sorrel. Then at Wren, and her expression did something complicated, like a woman finding a familiar stain on an unfamiliar shirt.

"Well," she said. "The cartographer's daughter. And a scribe with ink still under his nails from the palace ledger-room. And—" she crouched, and her voice went soft in a way that made Wren's chest ache, "—hello, little fox. You have your mother's ears."

Sorrel went very still. "You knew my mother?"

"I knew everyone, dear, back when everyone still signed things." Maren straightened with a grunt. "Come in before the fish start gossiping. They're terrible gossips. Everything goes straight to the eels, and the eels have no discretion whatsoever."

* * *

Inside, the willow's heart was warm and cluttered and smelled of rosemary and lamp oil. Shelves bent under jars; a kettle boiled itself, which Wren decided not to think about too hard. Maren poured four cups of something dark and bitter and sat down across from them with the air of a judge who already knows the verdict but enjoys the theater of trial.

"So," she said. "You've come about the covenant."

Hazel set down her cup. "How—"

"You're carrying a map that draws itself, child, and you flinch every time it does. You've paid it memories — I can see the gaps, they look like missing teeth. The girl is fox-blooded and homesick for a realm she barely remembers. And the scribe—" Maren turned the pond-water eyes on Wren, and Wren fought the urge to hide their ink-stained hands under the table. "The scribe has been rehearsing a question all day. Ask it."

Wren's mouth was dry. "The ledgers," they said. "In the palace archive. The census records. I found shipping manifests underneath them. The registered gifted — they weren't being sent to settlements in the Marches. There were no settlements. There was a *quota*." Their voice cracked on the last word, and they hated it, and kept going anyway. "I thought the king was hoarding magic. Power. That he was building some weapon against the far realm. But Hazel says the border is failing, and if the border fails both realms bleed into each other, and—" They stopped. Made themselves say it plainly, the way they'd learned to write plainly. "What is he actually doing with them?"

Maren drank her tea. Set it down. And told them.

"He's feeding the border," she said. "Memories. Freely given memory is the only mortar that holds the realms apart — that was the covenant. Both peoples signed it, both peoples paid a little, a tithe of remembering, each generation. Small things. A lullaby here, a birthday there. The border stayed soft and strong, like skin." Her eyes went distant. "I signed it. Last of thirty-one. I was nineteen and furious about giving up my grandmother's humming, and my mother told me that fury was the point — that a price you resent is a price you'll guard."

"And then?" Hazel asked, though Wren could see she already knew. Hazel had known since the ford. Maybe since the ash sigils on the doors.

"And then Aldric's grandfather came to the throne," Maren said, "and discovered that the covenant worked in *both directions*. That memory given freely could shore the border — or memory *taken* could feed something else entirely. He broke the rite open. He stopped paying. He started harvesting." Her voice didn't rise. That was the worst part; it stayed level as a ledger line. "Every year since, the crown has taken the gifted and drained what makes them gifted — their remembering, their knowing, their *seeing* — and poured it into the Marches to patch what his grandfather's theft had torn. And because taken memory doesn't hold — because only what's *given freely* binds — the patch rots faster every year, and so he needs more, and takes more, and calls it a census."

The kettle screamed. Nobody moved to it.

"But that's—" Wren started, and couldn't finish, because the sentences kept reordering themselves in their head like a misfiled page. Nine years of palace history lessons rearranged themselves around this new spine. *The barbarians beyond the border. The treachery of the far folk. The Sundering, caused by their malice.* All of it written in royal ink, in Wren's own hand, in training exercises, in fair copies, in proclamations. "We were taught the far realm broke the covenant. That the Sundering was *their* doing. I copied the anniversary proclamation *four times*."

"You copied a lie beautifully, I'm sure," Maren said, not unkindly. "The far realm has been waiting eighty years for us to come apologize. Some of them are still waiting politely. The patient ones died off first, unfortunately."

Sorrel had gone pale, her small hands gripping her cup. "Tam," she whispered. "They took Tam. He registered because the herald said it would keep him safe." Her chin trembled, and then set, and Wren watched a child decide to be angry instead of scared, and marveled at it. "He's in the Marches right now. Being *drained*."

"Not yet," Hazel said quietly. Everyone looked at her. She was staring into her tea, and when she spoke again her voice had that other cadence, the country's cadence. "Taken memory rots. He's been there weeks. There's still a person left to save. Barely. But there."

"How do you know that?" Wren asked.

"Because the map told me." Hazel looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious. "Because I'm apparently the border's favorite pen now, and it *won't stop talking*."

Maren nodded slowly, unsurprised. "Of course it talks to you. It talked to your mother. Oddfoot blood runs thin these days, but it runs true." She heaved herself up, went to a shelf, and pulled down a jar that glowed faintly, like a caught sunset. When she turned back, all the cheerfulness had drained out of her face, and what remained was older and harder. "Here's what happens next, and none of you will like it. The covenant can be reforged. Not patched — *reforged*, at the Covenant Stone beneath the rift in the Sundered Marches. Freely given memory, from both realms, anchored by a living mapmaker's hand. Your hand, Hazel Kavanagh."

"And the cost?" Hazel asked, in the tone of someone asking the price of bread.

"Everything you're afraid to lose," Maren said simply. "The stone takes what anchors you. For a mapmaker, that's not a face or a song. That's the people who became your map. Twelve years, I'd guess, from the shape of your gaps. Every friend you found after you started hiding. Every family you built in the dark."

The silence stretched. Wren watched Hazel's face do something terrible and quiet — watched her *decide* something, right there at the table, behind her eyes, and file it away where no one could see it. Wren had spent nine years reading other people's drafts. They recognized a sealed letter when they saw one.

"That's not happening," Wren said.

"It's not your choice," Hazel said gently.

"Neither was Tam's. Neither was any of theirs." Wren stood, and their chair scraped, and they heard their own voice go sharp with nine years of filed-away guilt. "I carried the manifests. I wrote the quotas in a fair hand. If the price is memory, then I'll pay some of it, and you don't get to refuse for me, because refusing *for* people is exactly what the crown does."

Maren looked at Wren for a long moment. Then at Hazel. Then she began, horribly, to laugh — a dry, rustling laugh like leaves.

"Oh, you're all going to be insufferable at that stone," she said. "Good. Insufferable people make the best signatories." She tucked the glowing jar into her pocket and reached for her staff. "Pack nothing. The rift won't wait for tidy travelers. We leave at slack water."

"Slack water's at dawn," Hazel said.

"At *this* lake," Maren said, "slack water comes when it likes. Which gives us perhaps six hours — and I'd wager my best curse we'll be interrupted before then." She paused at the door, pond-water eyes narrowing toward the surface of the lake far above, where the evening light had begun to shiver. "The fish gossip, remember? And lately they've been saying the water tastes of iron and boot leather."

Sorrel's ears flattened. "Soldiers?"

"Worse," Maren said. "A hunter who's learned to follow maps that draw themselves."
