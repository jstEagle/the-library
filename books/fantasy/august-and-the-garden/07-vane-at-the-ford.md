## 7. Vane at the Ford

The river found them before they found it.

Hazel heard it first as a change in the birdsong — a thinning, a hush — and then the trees opened like a curtain and there it was: a broad gray-green ribbon of water running fast between banks of pale stone. The Liss. On her old maps, drawn in another life, she had marked it with a careful blue line and the notation *fordable at low water, fords three*. But this was not her map's country anymore. This was the unmapped wilds, where rivers ran on memory and moods, and Hazel had learned in four days of walking that nothing here stayed where you drew it.

"Three fords," Wren said, squinting at the water with a scribe's distrust. "You said three."

"I said my *map* said three." Hazel shaded her eyes. The morning was bright and useless. "My map also said the forest would end two days ago, and instead we've been walking through the inside of someone's dream about forests."

"That was one time," Sorrel said from her perch on Maren's pack, where she rode like a figurehead. "And the dream-person was very sorry about it afterward."

"You apologized to a dream?"

"I'm polite," said Sorrel, with dignity. Her ears — fox ears, sharp-eared and dark-tipped, hidden under a hood when strangers were near — swiveled toward the water. Then they went still.

Hazel felt the stillness spread through the child like ink through cloth. She knew that stillness. She had spent twelve years cultivating it in herself.

"Sorrel?"

"Horses." The girl slid down off the pack without a sound. "Behind us. A lot of them. And—" her nose wrinkled, catching something on the wind that Hazel couldn't smell at all— "*iron.*"

Maren set down her staff. The old hedge-witch looked older suddenly, the way a tree looks older when lightning has been near. "How far?"

"Half an hour. Maybe less if they've got a scent-hound." Sorrel's tail had come out from under her cloak; she didn't seem to notice, or didn't care. "It's Vane. I'd know her anywhere. She smells like cold candle smoke."

Hazel's stomach dropped through the pale stones into the river below. Captain Ilsa Vane. Four days of running, four days of almost-escape, and the hunter-captain had walked them down like a patient cat walking down a mouse that thought itself clever.

"The ford," Hazel said. "If we cross before they arrive—"

"They'll see us mid-river," Wren said. "We're carrying everything we own. We are not fast."

"We could split up," Maren offered, but even she didn't sound convinced, and no one moved to do it. They had been together five days now, and already the idea of splitting had the flavor of a word in a language they were forgetting.

Hazel looked at the river. And then, because she couldn't help it — because it was the thing she did, the thing she *was*, the gift she had spent twelve years pretending was only a knack for pretty drawings — she looked at it the other way. The way that showed her not water and stone but the *shape* beneath them, the way the country remembered itself.

The river remembered being wider here. Much wider. It remembered a bridge once, long drowned, its pilings still standing under the current like the ribs of some patient whale. It remembered a village on the far bank whose name nobody living knew, and it remembered the ford — the true ford, the deep-water crossing — half a mile downstream, where the water slowed and went shallow over gravel the color of coins.

But most of all, right here, it remembered being *angry*.

Rivers in this country held grudges. That was the trouble with geography that ran on memory: memory held grudges, and love, and grief, and this stretch of the Liss remembered a flood that had taken eleven houses and a bell tower, and it had never quite forgiven the people who rebuilt downstream.

"Sorrel," Hazel said slowly, "how much fox have you got left in you today?"

The child grinned, and it was such a wild, sharp, unafraid grin that Hazel's heart broke a little and mended itself differently. "All of it. Why?"

"Because I need you to be a rumor for about ten minutes."

* * *

They went downstream along the bank, fast and low, while behind them the hoofbeats resolved out of the trees. Hazel counted six riders and a lean gray shape on a lead — a scent-hound, or what passed for one out here, a creature stitched together out of a dog's body and a hunting bird's patience. Vane rode at their head, and even at this distance Hazel could feel the woman's attention land on their backs like a hand.

Sorrel stopped at a bend where willows crowded the water. "Here?"

"Here." Hazel crouched and took the child's small hands in hers. They were warm, always warm, warmer than a human child's, and they trembled slightly with the effort of holding so much of herself leashed. "Listen carefully. You know how you can make people see what isn't there? Make the trees look like a road, make a shadow look like a person?"

"It's not making them see things," Sorrel said, offended. "It's asking the world to remember wrong for a little while. There's a difference."

"There is. I apologize." Twelve years of hiding had made Hazel fluent in apology; motherhood, brief and stolen, had made her fluent in this too — in taking a child's face in her hands and meaning every word. "I need you to ask the world to remember a road. Back there, through the trees, going east. A wide one, well-traveled, with cart ruts and a shrine at the fork. Can you hold it? Hold it big enough that six riders believe it?"

Sorrel's grin came back, fiercer. "That's easy."

"It isn't," Maren said quietly. "Child, listen to me. Holding a lie that large against six minds that want to find a road — that's not a trick, that's a war. You let go the moment it costs you more than you can pay. Promise me."

"I promise," said Sorrel, in the tone children use for promises they intend to interpret generously.

Wren caught Hazel's sleeve. "And what do we do while she's winning a war?"

"We run for the real ford." Hazel stood. Downstream, half a mile of pale stones away, the river slowed and remembered being shallow. "And then I do something I've been trying not to do since we crossed the border."

She didn't explain. Wren looked at her, and Hazel watched the young scribe understand anyway — watched it land, the way knowledge lands on people in this country, heavy as weather. Wren had seen what drawing cost her. Everyone had. The song Hazel had lost on the second day, humming for a tune that wasn't there anymore. The face she'd lost at Hollowfen, reaching for her mother's smile and finding a blank where it should have been, like a page torn cleanly out of an atlas.

Small ones first, Maren had said. The country takes small ones first. Like a moneylender establishing trust.

"Hazel," Wren started.

"Run," Hazel said. "Everyone run. Now."

* * *

They ran.

The hoofbeats behind them turned, confused, chasing a road that had never existed, following cart ruts made of moonlight and a shrine made of suggestion. For a quarter mile it worked beautifully. Hazel dared, absurdly, to hope.

Then the hope tore like wet paper.

It happened as a sound first — a thin, high cry from behind them, cut off — and then as a feeling, the way a candle going out changes a room. The false road collapsed. And with it, something else collapsed: Sorrel, stumbling between Maren and Wren, her legs folding, her hood falling back, her fox ears flat and trembling and — Hazel saw it as the child turned her face up — *fading*. Not gone. Fading, like a watermark left in the sun.

"No," Hazel breathed, and caught her. The child weighed nothing. Her skin, always warm, was cool now. "Sorrel. Sorrel, look at me."

"I held it," the girl whispered. There was blood at her nose, a single thread of it. "I held it as long as — Papa says weather-workers burn out sometimes, doing too much. He says it grows back." Her eyes, huge and amber, searched Hazel's face. "Does it grow back?"

Hazel did not know. Tam was a weather-worker of the gentle kind — he coaxed rain onto gardens, teased fog off harbors. What Sorrel had just done was hold six trained minds in a lie while sprinting, and she had done it because Hazel asked, because Hazel had put the whole weight of their survival on a nine-year-old's shoulders because it was convenient.

Because it was cheaper than paying herself.

"Not yet," Hazel said, which was not a lie and not an answer. "Not yet. Maren—"

"I have her." Maren took the child with the practiced ease of a woman who had carried sick villagers for sixty years. "Go. The ford. Do what you were going to do, and do it *now*, because Vane doesn't need a road to follow a river."

The hoofbeats had found the bank again. Ahead, maybe two hundred yards, the water spread wide and slow and shallow over its coin-colored gravel. Behind, closing, the riders — and Vane's voice carrying over the water, clear and cold and horribly calm:

"*Kavanagh!* I only want the scribe!"

"Liar," said Wren, not slowing.

"She's not lying," Hazel panted. "That's the worst part. She wants you alive. She wants all of us alive. Dead gifted folk don't have memories worth harvesting." The words tasted like ash sigils and Tam's door. "Keep running."

They reached the shallows. The water was knee-deep, silver-bright, deceptively gentle. On the far bank the ground rose into hazel scrub — her namesake, she thought wildly, her father's joke, *little Hazel among the hazels* — and beyond it, safety, or the shape of safety.

Vane's horse came out of the trees.

The captain rode alone now, having left her soldiers floundering after phantom roads, and she rode like something poured rather than ridden, all cold forward motion. Twenty yards of open bank between them. Hazel did the arithmetic and hated the answer: the child couldn't run, the old woman couldn't swim a fast river, and Wren —

Wren had stopped. Turned around. Stood in the middle of the ford with a scribe's ink-stained hands raised, as if surrendering, as if buying seconds.

"Wren, *no*—"

"Go!" Wren's voice cracked across the water. "I can talk! I'm very good at talking, I filed reports for the crown for six years, I can talk for *hours*—"

Vane didn't slow down.

So Hazel did the thing she had been trying not to do.

She knelt on the gravel, in the middle of the ford, in the path of a charging horse, and she put both palms flat against the river.

*Show me,* she asked the water. *Show me what you were.*

The river showed her. The flood, the eleven houses, the bell tower toppling slow as a dancer, the grief of it stored in the current like sediment. And beneath that, deeper, the older memory: the wide angry water of a hundred years ago, a hundred and fifty, the Liss in its pride, deep enough to drown a cavalry charge, deep enough to swallow the pale stones whole.

*I'll take it,* Hazel told the river. *Your anger. Give it back to you.*

And the river, being a river of this country, asked its price.

Hazel paid without looking at what she handed over. She felt it leave — a year, a whole year, gone like a coin dropped down a well. Which year? She would find out later, she knew, in some small terrible moment: a birthday that hadn't happened, a winter that had never fallen, a conversation with Tam that had never — 

No. Not Tam. Please, whatever else, not—

The river rose.

It rose the way grief rises, all at once and everywhere, the shallow ford swelling into the Liss's old fury, the water standing up out of its bed like a wall of gray-green glass. Vane's horse screamed and balked, wheeling, and the flood took the space where the captain had been and closed over it, and the river ran deep and fast and furious from bank to bank, and the pale stones were gone, and the ford was gone, and the pursuit was gone under twelve feet of grudge.

Then the water settled. Sulked, almost. Went back to being a river.

Hazel knelt in six inches of water on the far bank, soaked, shaking, her hands still pressed to the gravel. Maren crouched beside her with Sorrel wrapped in the hedge-witch's cloak, and Wren stood dripping and staring at the deep water where a captain had almost died, and no one said anything for a long moment.

"What did it take?" Wren finally asked, very quietly.

Hazel reached inward, the way you probe a missing tooth. The year was simply — absent. Smooth-sided. She could see the edges of it, the year before and the year after, and between them a clean gap like a page razored from an atlas.

"I don't know yet," she said. "That's the worst part. I won't know until I reach for something and it isn't there."

Sorrel stirred against Maren's shoulder. Her ears were still faint, still translucent at the tips, but her eyes had focus again. "Did it work?" she whispered. "Is she gone?"

Hazel looked at the river. Deep, dark, satisfied with itself.

And then, on the far bank, a hand broke the surface of the water — one gauntleted hand, gripping the pale stones — and Captain Ilsa Vane dragged herself out of the Liss coughing and alone and *alive*, and did not reach for her sword.

She sat on the bank, dripping, staring across the impossible water at them. At Hazel. At the place where the ford had been.

"You redrawed it," Vane called across the river, and her voice was not cold anymore. It was hollow. It was the voice of someone who had just seen, with her own eyes, exactly the kind of power the king had been collecting — and exactly what it *cost*. "Mapmaker. How much did it take you?"

Hazel said nothing.

Vane laughed, or something near it — a short, broken sound. "A year? Two? I've read the ledgers, Kavanagh. I've written the ledgers. Do you know what they take from the ones we bring in?" She stood, swaying, water streaming off her. "Everything. All at once. And they call it sealing the border." Her hand curled shut. "I'm coming to Hollowfen. Don't run. I'm done hunting you."

And she turned and walked away along the far bank, toward the drowned forest, leaving five travelers staring at each other across a river that had gotten exactly what it wanted.

Maren spoke first, softly, into the silence. "Well," she said. "Either that was a trap, or the world just got stranger."

Hazel looked down at her own hands, still trembling, still resting on the gravel, and tried to remember what year it was.
