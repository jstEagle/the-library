## 8. The Covenant Stone

Maren had walked to the Covenant Stone four times in her long life, and three of those walks had been funerals.

The path down from Hollowfen wound through the drowned forest's roots, where the water ran silver and slow and the trees grew their rings outward instead of upward — a trick of the old realm that still made Wren mutter about the impossibility of it every single time, which Maren found endearing in the way one finds a child's questions endearing: with patience, and a small private smile. Sorrel trotted ahead, fox-eared and quick, chasing the pale moths that lived in the roots and never seemed to be caught. And Hazel walked last, quiet in the way Maren had learned, over the past days, to distrust.

Hazel was never quiet. She narrated. She named things. She had named a heron *Gerald* within an hour of crossing the border and had defended the name against all reasonable objection. She talked to the map she was always drawing, murmuring to it like a cook murmuring to a pot. A quiet Hazel was a Hazel doing arithmetic in her head, and Maren, who had done this arithmetic herself once, ninety years ago, knew exactly which numbers she was adding up.

The rift came into view as the trees thinned — a long scar in the sky above the valley, stitched with light that pulsed like a slow heartbeat. Underneath it, in a bowl of dark grass, stood the Stone.

It was not an impressive stone, as stones went. Maren had always thought that was the point. It was knee-high, round-shouldered, mossy, the sort of thing a traveler would step over without a second glance. It had been old when the realms were young, older than kings, older than the word *king*. The covenant lived in it the way a song lives in a flute: not in the material, but in what it was shaped to hold.

"Sit," Maren said, when they reached it. "All of you. This is a sitting-down sort of truth."

They sat. Sorrel curled against Hazel's side without thinking about it, the way she always did, and Maren watched Hazel's arm go around the girl automatically, and thought: *there it is. There's the price.*

"You've all heard pieces," Maren began. "Let me give you the whole of it, ugly corners and all. The covenant between the realms was not a wall. That's the first thing. Your king — and mine, though we didn't call him that — built no walls. The border was a *promise*. Two peoples agreeing to remember each other kindly. That's all a border ever should be. The Stone held the promise, and the promise held the border, and as long as both realms fed it freely — a memory given, not taken — the passage between worlds stayed open and gentle and safe."

"And then the Sundering," Wren said.

"Then a man on your side of the border decided he'd like to live a very long time." Maren's voice stayed level, which took work. "He learned the Stone's grammar. He learned that memory is the only currency it accepts, and that it cannot tell a gift from a theft — only a realm can tell it that, by *how* the memory is given. So he stopped giving his own and started taking everyone else's. His reign has been fed on it for three hundred years. Every gifted person your crown has ever 'registered' has walked to a Stone like this one and been *emptied* into it. Not killed. Worse than killed. They're still in there, in a way. Little pieces of them holding up the sky."

Sorrel had gone very still against Hazel's side. "Like the lights," she said. "In the rift. The pulsing."

"The pulsing," Maren agreed, "is them. Holding. And failing. A stolen memory doesn't hold the way a given one does. It frays. That's why the king needs more and more of them. He's bailing a leaky boat with other people's hands."

Wren had gone the color of old paper. "The ledgers. The census. He's not sealing the border. He's *feeding* it."

"He's feeding himself, and the border eats the scraps. But yes." Maren turned to Hazel then, and made herself say the hard part plainly, because she had earned the right to say it plainly by being the one who'd have to live next to the consequences. "Here is what reforging requires. Freely given memory, from both realms, given at this Stone, by choice and in company. Not one great sacrifice — the old covenant was a thousand small kindnesses, and it should be remade the same way. Each of us can give something. A memory. More than one, if we're willing. The Stone will take what's offered and no more, if it's offered freely."

"And the anchor?" Hazel asked. Her voice was perfectly calm.

Maren had been dreading that calm for two days.

"The anchor is the mapmaker," she said. "The one who can draw. The covenant needs a shape to hold it, and yours is the only gift left on either side that can give it one. Your memories won't just feed it, Hazel. They'll *structure* it. Every path you've ever drawn, every place you've ever loved enough to map — the Stone will use them as the bones of the new border. Which means the Stone will want the memories that made the mapmaker. All of them. The deep ones. The ones with people in them."

The silence that followed had weight. Even the rift's slow pulse seemed to lean in.

"Twelve years," Wren said quietly. "Vellmark. Tam. Sorrel."

"Tam," Hazel said, "is in a king's cellar somewhere, or worse. Sorrel is sitting right here. The strangers the covenant saves will have families too." She said it the way one reads a shopping list. "It's arithmetic."

"It's *not*—" Wren started, and stopped, because Hazel looked at them, and whatever was in her face made the scribe swallow the rest.

Maren recognized that face. She had worn it herself, once, kneeling at this same stone with a memory of her mother's kitchen held out in her mind like a plate of offerings, having decided in advance exactly how much of herself the world was allowed to have. It was a calm like frozen water. Solid. Clear. And underneath it, a long cold drop toward a darkness the person doing the arithmetic had already stopped counting.

*She means to give all of it,* Maren thought. *The whole twelve years. She's decided, and she's decided alone, and she'll smile at us right up until the moment it's done, and then she'll wake up a stranger to the only people who love her, and she'll call that a fair price because she'll have no memory of anyone to tell her otherwise.*

Ninety years ago, Maren's covenant had failed — not the great one, but her own small one, the promise she'd made to herself never to let the Stone take more than she chose. She'd given her mother's kitchen and kept her grief, and the grief had grown in the empty space like a weed in a cracked flagstone. She knew what a person looked like who'd paid too much. She was looking at one who hadn't paid yet and was already arranging herself for it.

"Well," Hazel said, standing, brushing off her skirt. "It's a fine spot for it. Good light. I'll want to draw the approach paths before the rite — the Stone takes shape more easily if the ground is already listening." And she unrolled her map case and began, calmly, horribly calmly, to sketch the valley, naming the landmarks as she went, and Maren saw that she was naming them *for the last time*. Memorizing her own goodbyes. *Gerald's river. The ford where Sorrel burned bright. The hill where Wren slept and talked in their sleep about ledgers.*

* * *

They made camp in the bowl of the valley, because the rite wanted dawn and dawn was hours off, and because — though nobody said this — nobody quite wanted to arrive at the Stone too early, the way one doesn't want to arrive too early at a sickbed.

Maren took first watch, and watched Hazel.

The mapmaker sat apart, as she had all evening, drawing by rift-light. But she wasn't drawing the valley anymore. She was drawing faces. Maren watched her do it with a cartographer's terrible precision: Tam, from memory, the exact crooked set of his smile. Sorrel, mid-laugh. Wren, scowling at a ledger. And then — Maren leaned forward despite herself — a dozen more. A market stall. A gray city. Neighbors whose names Maren didn't know. Twelve years of small ordinary love, laid out on paper like a patient being prepared for surgery.

Hazel was making a *record*. Because she knew that after dawn, she wouldn't have the originals, and some part of her — the part doing the arithmetic — had decided that a record was the next best thing. That the Hazel who woke at the Stone would find these drawings and understand, the way one understands a stranger's family photograph.

Maren's old heart broke a little, and then, being an old heart and therefore practical, hardened.

She looked at the others. Sorrel slept curled in Wren's cloak, and Wren sat awake beside her, staring into the fire with the expression of someone drafting a letter they'd never send. And Maren thought about the thing she had told Hazel at Hollowfen and had not said plainly since: *the covenant was a thousand small kindnesses.* She had told Hazel the shape of the answer without telling her the answer.

The Stone did not require one person's everything. It required everyone's *something*, freely given, in company. That was the whole grammar of it. That had always been the whole grammar of it. The king's crime — the original crime, the crime underneath all the other crimes — was not theft. It was *solitude*. It was one man deciding the price was his alone to set and others' alone to pay.

And here was Hazel, three hundred years later, making the same shape of choice with a gentler face.

Maren rose, quietly, and crossed the grass to where Hazel sat. The mapmaker looked up, rift-light on her face, and smiled the frozen-water smile.

"Can't sleep either?" Hazel said.

"No," Maren said. "I was just thinking about arithmetic." She sat down heavily beside her. "And about how you've always been wrong about it, and I let you be wrong, and I'm not going to let you be wrong at dawn."

Hazel's pen stopped.

"Because I've been where you're sitting," Maren said, "and I know the trick of it. You think you're being brave. You're not. Bravery would be asking for help with the bill." She nodded at the sleeping forms by the fire, at the fox-child and the runaway scribe. "You've spent twelve years learning that family is something you build. Don't spend your last night as yourself deciding you have to pay for them alone. That's not the covenant's grammar, child. That's the king's."

Hazel looked at her drawings. All those faces. Her hand trembled, once, over Tam's crooked smile.

"Maren," she said, very softly, "if I don't come back from this — the *me* of me —"

"You will," Maren said, "because I intend to make a scene of it."

Far above them, the rift pulsed — and pulsed again, quicker now, the stolen lights within it flickering like a held breath about to be released. And down in the dark grass, the old Stone seemed almost to lean toward the camp, listening, waiting for dawn, and for what dawn would bring — which was not, though none of them yet knew it, what any of them had planned.
