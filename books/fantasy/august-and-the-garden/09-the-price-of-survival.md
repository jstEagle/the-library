## 9. The Price of Survival

The Covenant Stone did not look like much, which Hazel supposed was the way of important things. It sat in a bowl of grass beneath the Sundering rift — that long tear in the sky where the stars on one side were the wrong stars — and it was no taller than Tam's kitchen table had been, gray and lichen-spotted, with a seam down its middle like a loaf split for sharing.

Maren had walked them down through the rift's shadow in silence, her staff clicking on stones that clicked back. Sorrel rode on Wren's shoulders and kept reaching up to touch the wrong stars, as if she could pluck them like fruit.

"It's smaller than I imagined," Wren said.

"Most true things are," said Maren. "It's not the stone that does the work. It never was. The stone is only where two hands can meet."

Hazel set her satchel down at the stone's base and did not open it right away. Inside were her maps — the ones she'd drawn since crossing over, each one paid for out of her own head. A face from the market in Vellmark. The tune Tam used to hum while he coaxed rain out of a clear sky. Her mother's laugh, or most of it. She knew what was left in the satchel, and she knew what was left in herself, and she had spent the whole walk deciding how to spend it.

Twelve years. That was the arithmetic she'd settled on. Twelve years since the Sundering, twelve years since she'd come to Vellmark with nothing but a gift and a fear of it, twelve years of Tam teaching her to cook, of Sorrel being born, of found family stitched together out of stray people who had also lost their first ones. If the covenant needed an anchor, it would take the anchor's whole weight. She would not hand it a corner of herself and hope it held.

"Hazel." Maren's voice was gentle in the way old voices are gentle when they already know your answer. "You've gone quiet the way people go quiet before they do something foolish and noble."

"I'm deciding what to have for supper," Hazel said.

"You're lying," said Sorrel, from Wren's shoulders, "very badly."

"I'm lying moderately badly," Hazel corrected. "There's a difference."

She opened the satchel anyway. She took out the last map — the one she'd drawn of this very meadow, though the meadow hadn't needed drawing; she'd done it so her hands would remember the motion when the rest of her didn't — and she laid it flat on the stone's gray crown. Then she put both palms against the seam.

The stone woke under her hands like a hearth in winter. Warmth climbed her arms. And something in the ground beneath her, vast and patient and wounded, turned its attention toward her the way a great animal turns its head.

*What will you give,* asked the border, in a voice like wind through a keyhole.

"Everything," Hazel said aloud, because it seemed rude to think at something that old. "Twelve years. All of it. Take the whole shelf."

And the border began to reach into her —

— and stopped.

Because there were hands on the stone that were not hers.

* * *

Wren had gone pale as uninked paper, but their palm was pressed flat beside hers, fingers trembling, jaw set in the particular way they got when they'd made up their mind about a ledger entry.

"No," Hazel said. "Wren, no, you don't have anything you need to —"

"I have plenty," Wren said. "I have an entire childhood of being the scribe's son nobody noticed. I have the memory of my father's face when I passed my examinations, which he pretended not to be proud of, which is the best version of it. I have the taste of the honey cakes in the chancery kitchen." Their voice cracked and they pushed on. "Take those. Not hers. She's given enough just getting here."

"That's not how —"

"The covenant takes freely given memory," Maren recited, "from either realm, in any measure, so long as none is taken wholly." The old witch's eyes were wet and furious about it. "I wrote the phrasing myself, child, four hundred years ago, and I'll thank you not to argue grammar with me at my own stone."

Then a small hand slapped down next to Wren's, and Sorrel scrambled up onto the stone itself to kneel among the grown-up hands like a fox cub claiming a warm hearthstone.

"Sorrel, no," Hazel said, and her voice came out broken down the middle. "Sweetheart, you've already burned half your gift at the ford, you can't —"

"I'm giving *memories*, not magic," Sorrel said, with the towering patience of a nine-year-old explaining weather to adults. "I'm giving the bad ones. The night the soldiers came for Papa. The smell of the ash sigil. I don't want those anyway." Her chin wobbled. "But I'm keeping Papa's face. You can't have Papa's face. Nobody gets Papa's face until he comes home."

The border, Hazel thought wildly, was going to have to negotiate with a child, and she was not entirely sure the child wouldn't win.

Maren laid her hand down fourth, gnarled and steady. "Four hundred years I carried the covenant alone," she said. "Alone was always the wrong word for it. Let me finally be right."

And then boots on the grass.

Hazel's heart went cold before her mind caught up — Captain Ilsa Vane, hunter-captain of the crown, armor dulled with travel, sword at her hip, standing at the edge of the meadow with three soldiers behind her. Sorrel hissed, an actual fox-hiss, small and fierce. Wren stepped in front of the stone with nothing but a scribe's ink-stained hands raised.

Vane looked at all of it — the stone, the map, the child, the kneeling witch — and then she did the last thing anyone expected.

She knelt.

Not to fight. To kneel, the way one kneels at a bedside, the way one kneels in a chapel whose god has disappointed them. She pulled off one gauntlet and set it in the grass.

"I followed the shipments," she said. Her voice was flat as a road after frost. "When you slipped me at the ford, I went back. I went into the Marches. I saw the pens." She swallowed. "I saw what the king does with the registered. They sit in rows and they smile at nothing, and every week there are fewer of them smiling, and the border eats what's left of them. I have spent eleven years believing I was keeping people safe." She looked up, and her eyes were terrible and clear. "I was the wolf, and I didn't know it because someone kept feeding me sheep and calling it duty."

"Captain," Maren said quietly, "the covenant takes freely given memory. Do you understand what you're offering?"

"I have memories I'd like to be rid of," Vane said. "And some I'd rather keep, and I find I don't get to choose which is which, so the stone can sort them. That's more trust than I've given anyone in eleven years." She set her bare hand on the stone, last of five. "Do it."

Hazel looked at the five hands on the gray crown of the stone — a scribe, a fox-child, a hedge-witch older than kingdoms, a huntress, and her own — and felt the thing she'd been carrying since Tam was dragged from his house finally crack open. Not guilt. Something warmer and worse.

"You absolute idiots," she whispered. "Every one of you. This was supposed to be mine."

"Family doesn't work that way," said Sorrel.

"Out of the mouths of babes," Maren murmured, "and straight into the mouth of the border. Now, Hazel. Anchor us. Draw the shape of the giving."

So Hazel drew.

That was the part no one had told her, the part her gift had been waiting twelve years to say: a covenant was a map. It was lines between points, a path from one realm to another, and paths could be drawn into being by someone willing to pay for the ink. She closed her eyes and drew with everything left in her — the seam of the stone, the tear of the sky, five hands as five landmarks, and between them roads, and along the roads the memories flowing like water finding level.

She felt Wren's father's hidden pride pass through her, warm as bread. She felt Sorrel's bad nights lift away like fog off a morning river, and felt the child's grip on her other memory — Papa's face — hold fast, immovable, a mountain the water simply went around. She felt four hundred years ease out of Maren's spine. She felt Vane give up something that made the captain flinch and then, strangely, sit straighter, lighter, as though a pack she'd forgotten she wore had been cut away.

And the border drank it all, and wanted more, and reached for Hazel's twelve years —

— and found them ringed. Held. Five hands' worth of held. It pressed, and the press became a question, and the question became, at last, acceptance: *enough, given together, is enough.*

The seam in the stone glowed like dawn through a shutter. Above them, the Sundering rift shivered, and the wrong stars swung — slowly, majestically, like a compass needle finding north — until they were the right stars, or nearly, or stars that had agreed to share the sky.

Then it was quiet. Grass. Wind. Five people around a gray stone, all of them patting their own heads as if checking for missing hats.

"What did it take?" Wren asked at last. "From you? From any of us?"

Hazel searched herself. She still had Tam's humming. She still had Sorrel learning to walk on a rug shaped like a river. She still had twelve years, every crooked beam of them. But there were absences, small and clean, like gaps in a hedge where deer had passed: she couldn't quite recall the name of Wren's hometown. Maren was frowning at her own hands, having apparently misplaced a century of grudges. Vane touched her cheek and found a scar she couldn't account for and looked, bizarrely, relieved.

"I lost the chancery honey cakes," Wren said mournfully. "I hope they were worth it."

"They were," said Sorrel solemnly. "I had one once. They were worth it."

Hazel laughed — a real laugh, cracked and wet and surprised out of her — and it echoed off the healed sky, and somewhere far to the south, she would swear later, a door that had been shut for twelve years stood suddenly ajar.

Which was when the grass at the meadow's edge rustled, and out of it trotted a fox with amber eyes and a gait Hazel knew better than her own heartbeat, and sitting upright on its back, disheveled and blinking in the starlight like a man waking from a long nap, was Tam.

"Papa!" Sorrel was moving before anyone could catch her.

Tam slid off the fox, caught his daughter, and looked over her head at Hazel with an expression she'd seen a thousand times across a thousand shared suppers — the look that said *you did something reckless and I am too tired to scold you properly.*

"The border let me go," he said. "It said the debt was paid." He frowned. "It also said something about supper. Did you promise the fabric of reality supper, Hazel Kavanagh?"

Hazel looked at her strange, whole, diminished-and-enlarged family, at the healed sky, at the fox rolling happily in the grass, and realized she had absolutely no idea what happened next.

"Well," she said. "First, supper."
