## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The poison was in the stew, and Elodie knew it before the spoon touched her lips.

She knew it the way she knew rain coming—something in the air, something in the weight of it. She stood at the king's left hand with a silver spoon and a face like a locked door, and she let the smell of it settle against her tongue without letting it past her teeth. Bitter underneath the marrow and thyme. Not enough to kill. Enough to make a man wish it had.

"Your Majesty." Her voice came out flat and even, the way Sef had drilled into her fifteen years ago, when she was nine years old and still learning which smells meant death. "I'd wait on the stew."

King Aldric looked up from his maps. He was not a large man, but he had a large man's way of making rooms feel small around him. "Again, Coldharbour?"

"The onions," she said. "Something in the onions."

Across the table, Lord Perrin Vasse—a minor lord of a minor estate, the kind of man whose name Elodie would have to look up twice—was already lifting his own spoon toward his mouth. He was hungry. He'd ridden hard from somewhere and it showed in his shoulders.

Elodie moved.

It wasn't graceful. It wasn't dramatic. She simply crossed the three steps between them and put her hand over his bowl, and Lord Vasse stared up at her with the affronted confusion of a man who has never been touched by a servant in his life.

"My lord," she said, "you'll want to thank me later."

"You dare—"

"I dare because there's wormwood ash in this batch, and you've got a wife and two sons, and I've got a job." She turned to the king. "Sire. Every bowl came from the same pot. Whoever did this didn't care who died, so long as someone did."

The hall went quiet in that particular way great halls do—all the small sounds stopping at once, the rustle of silk, the scrape of a chair, a page's breathing. Aldric set down his quill. He looked at the stew. He looked at Elodie. And then he did something she had never seen him do in fifteen years of tasting his food: he smiled.

"Sef," he called. "Your girl has a nose."

Sef appeared from the service corridor with the speed of a man who had been listening through the door for exactly this. He was sixty now, thick through the middle, with hands that shook only when no one important was watching. He took the bowl from Elodie's hand, sniffed it, made a face like a man biting a lemon, and set it down with exaggerated care.

"Wormwood ash," he confirmed. "Masked with honey. Clever. Cheap. The work of someone who learned cooking in a garrison, not a kitchen."

"Find them," Aldric said, and two guards peeled away from the wall, and just like that a cook somewhere in the palace was about to have a very bad afternoon.

Lord Vasse was still holding his spoon, looking at it as though it might bite him. "You saved my life," he said to Elodie, and there was wonder in it, and something else too—the faint disgust of a nobleman realizing he owes a debt to a servant.

"I did my job, my lord."

"No." He stood. He actually bowed, shallow but real. "You did more than your job. Name anything within my modest means."

*Anything.* Elodie had learned long ago what *anything* cost at court. Favors were hooks. Gratitude was bait. She thought of her little shop on Tanner's Row, of the charms wrapped in oilcloth beneath her floorboards, of the law that said what she did after dark could drown her by morning.

"A new cloak," she said. "Mine's worn through at the shoulder."

Vasse blinked. Then he laughed—a short, surprised bark—and some of the court laughed with him, relieved, and the moment passed, and Elodie Coldharbour went back to her spoon and her locked-door face and tasted the king's wine, the bread, the pears in syrup, all of it clean, all of it safe, while somewhere behind her eyes a small voice asked the question it always asked:

*Is that all you want? A cloak?*

It was not all she wanted. But wanting things, in Vellamarch, in the year of the usurper's reign, was a luxury for people whose names weren't written anywhere dangerous.

* * *

The shop on Tanner's Row closed at dusk like every other shop, shutters drawn, sign turned, lamp lit low. Elodie counted the day's honest coin—tasting paid well, if you didn't count what it cost—and then went down into the cellar where the dishonest coin lived.

She worked by candlelight, the way her mother had taught her, back when her mother was still alive and still whole and still calling the magic *knitting*, as if that made it smaller. A charm was mostly patience. Dried rowan. A thread pulled from cloth the wearer loved. Three knots tied with the breath held between them, and a word spoken so softly it barely qualified as speech.

The luck-charms were her best sellers. Not because they worked—though they worked, a little, the way a kind word works—but because desperate people needed to hold something in their hands while they were being brave, and a knotted cord fit nicely in a fist.

The knock came at the back door at half past nine, three taps and a pause, the rhythm she'd taught maybe forty people in fifteen years and trusted maybe six.

The laundress was named Marta. Elodie knew her by sight from the palace laundry—a broad, gray-faced woman with knuckles like walnuts, who washed the sheets the king bled on when his gums went bad in winter. Tonight Marta looked like a woman who had been crying in a way that used everything up, so that what was left was just dry and hollow.

"He's conscripting them," Marta said, before Elodie had the door fully open. "My boy. Tam. Sixteen. They're posting the levy lists tomorrow and his name will be on it, I know it will, the crier's boy told me they've already written it. They're sending them south, to the border, to—" She stopped. Her hands were twisting her apron into a rope.

"Marta. Come in. Sit."

"I can't sit. I need—" She thrust out her hand, palm up, the way beggars do, except she wasn't begging. She was buying. "I need a charm. I heard—I heard from Greta, Greta who does the queen's linens before there was a queen, she says you sell them, she says they're good ones, real ones. I want one for my boy. Something to keep him alive. I don't care what it costs."

*Magic is punishable by drowning.* The edict hung on every church door in the city, painted letters tall as a man. Elodie had read it every day of her adult life. She had also, every day of her adult life, sold a little of the thing it forbade, to laundresses and stable boys and soldiers' wives, because the alternative was watching people carry their fear empty-handed.

"How much have you got?" she asked.

Marta emptied a purse onto the worktable. Coins, mostly copper, a few silver. Maybe four months of washing other people's filth.

"That's too much," Elodie said.

"It's my son's life."

"It's a knot and a thread, Marta. Sit down before you fall down."

She made the charm properly, though. That was the thing about Elodie that nobody at court ever saw—that she did not cheat the poor. She chose the thread herself, a coarse brown strand she'd pulled weeks ago from a shirt Marta's boy had mended in the laundry yard, because she'd noticed things; noticing was the whole of her trade, both halves of it. She tied the three knots with her breath held, and she spoke the word under it, the old word, the soft one, and felt the small warm turn in her chest that meant it had taken.

Then she pushed most of the coins back across the table.

"I can't—"

"You can. You'll buy him boots with the rest. Good ones. Soldiers die of bad feet faster than bad swords, I've seen it." She folded Marta's fingers over the money. "And Marta. Listen. A charm is not a promise. It tips things. It doesn't decide them. You tell him to keep his head down and his feet dry and come home, and the charm will do the rest. Understood?"

Marta was crying again, but differently now, and she gripped Elodie's hand with those walnut knuckles and said, "Bless you. Whatever your name is. Bless you."

*Whatever your name is.* Elodie almost laughed. If only the woman knew how much hung on that particular question—if only either of them knew—

"Out the back," she said gently. "And Marta? Don't come here again. Send someone else. Please."

When the door was shut and bolted, Elodie sat alone in the cellar with the candle burning down and the last of the warmth fading out of her chest, the way it always did after working a charm. Alone was the word for it. Alone was the shape of the room.

She was useful everywhere. The king trusted her tongue. The laundresses trusted her knots. Half the city had passed through her back door or knew someone who had, and not one of them knew her, not really—not her mother's name, not the lullaby she still hummed sometimes when the nights got long, not the fact that she kept her grandmother's ring on a chain under her shirt and had never once dared ask why.

Useful everywhere. Welcome nowhere. That was the bargain she'd struck with her life, and for fifteen years she'd kept her side of it.

She blew out the candle and climbed the stairs to bed, and that was when she saw the letter.

It had come under the front door sometime in the evening—no wax seal, no name, just her own handwriting somehow, or something close to it, on cheap paper. One line only:

*Bramwen Hale, archivist, asks after you. Tomorrow. Come before the raiders do.*

Elodie stood in the dark shop with the paper trembling slightly in her hand, and outside, far off toward the river, a bell began to toll the hour—and then stopped mid-stroke, as if someone had caught the rope.

She did not sleep much after that.
