## 2. A Date Written in Ink

The charm-book arrived wrapped in oilcloth and tied with a fisherman's knot, which told Elodie two things before she ever touched it: the seller came from the Docks, and the seller was frightened.

Frightened people tied knots they could undo one-handed in the dark.

She sat at her worktable with the shutters drawn against the morning, the book between her palms like a bird with a broken wing. *A Commonplace of Household Virtues*, said the tooled leather cover, innocent as a hymnal. But the spine had been rebound at least twice, and beneath the smell of tallow and old glue there was another smell, faint as a memory: river-stone and crushed vervain. The smell of working ink.

Elodie had spent fifteen years learning to read with senses that had nothing to do with letters. Poison had a taste; so did magic. Both announced themselves the same way—a small wrongness on the tongue, a whisper behind the teeth—and both would kill you just as cheerfully if you ignored them.

She untied the knot. She did not open the cover. Not yet.

First she held the book to the light through a sliver of shutter-gap and turned it slowly, watching how the dust moved across the boards. Ordinary dust drifted. Charmed dust clung, or fled, or fell in patterns no wind could argue with. This dust drifted. Good sign. Amateurs charmed their covers; professionals charmed their pages, where it mattered.

Then she wetted her thumb on a rag and pressed it to the corner of the back board, where the leather was thinnest, and waited three heartbeats for the taste.

Salt. Iron. And underneath, delicate as lacework, the ghost of a binding-spell done properly—old grammar, patient hands. Not a luck-knot or a fever-charm. Something older than the city's law against it. Older, possibly, than the city.

"Well," Elodie said quietly, to the empty room. "You're worth more than you look."

Which was a problem. Because things worth more than they looked were exactly the kind of thing that got people drowned.

She was still weighing it when the knock came—three quick raps, then a pause, then two more. Bramwen's rhythm. The scholar never waited politely; he knocked like a man arguing with a door.

She let him in through the alley entrance, because Bramwen Hale did not use front doors. He filled the doorway the way a stack of books fills a shelf—crookedly, entirely, shedding lint. His coat had been mended with thread of four different colors, and his eyes behind the smudged spectacles were doing the thing they did when he'd slept badly over an idea.

"You have it," he said.

"I have a hymnal."

"Elodie."

"Come in before someone sees you being obvious in my alley." She pulled him inside and shot the bolt. "It's genuine. Pre-Interdiction binding, professional warding on the leaves, and whoever owned it knew what they were doing right up until they didn't." She tapped the cover. "There's a scorch under the new leather. Someone tried to burn it and failed. That's not a bookseller's story. That's a survivor's story."

Bramwen had stopped listening somewhere around *genuine*. He was staring past her shoulder at the worktable with an expression she'd only seen on him once before—the day he'd shown her a fragment of pre-covenant verse and his hands had shaken so badly he'd dropped it in the inkwell.

"Bramwen."

"Hm? Yes. Genuine. Good. Excellent." He blinked. "I need you to come to the archive."

"It's morning. I have orders. The Vintners' Guild wants charms for their harvest festival, and half of them are going to be love-charms, which means half of them are going to be disasters, and I'll need to spend all afternoon talking people out of the worst ones."

"The archive," he repeated, in the voice of a man who had already decided the afternoon belonged to him. "Please. There's something I've been translating. It's—it concerns your family name."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere below, the laundress's boy was singing a song about a herring.

"My family name," Elodie said, "is Coldharbour, and there is nothing to know about it. My mother sold thread. Her mother sold thread. We are a long line of women who sold thread and died poor, and the most interesting thing any of us ever did was get fleas."

"That is what everyone with an interesting family name says."

"It's what everyone with a boring family name knows."

But her hands, she noticed, had gone cold around the charm-book. And Bramwen was still looking at her with those sleepless eyes, and fifteen years of being useful everywhere and welcome nowhere sat up inside her chest and paid attention.

*Thread-sellers,* whispered something old in her blood. *Is that what we were.*

"Tomorrow," she said. "After the guild. I'll come at dusk, when the streets go quiet."

"Tonight," Bramwen said. "Dusk. Yes. Fine. Tonight." He was already fumbling for the door. "Bring the book. Don't open it again. And Elodie—" he paused in the doorway, and for a moment the pedant fell away and she saw the fear underneath, plain as a bone through skin, "—if anyone asks, you've never heard of me."

"I haven't," she said. "Who are you?"

He almost smiled. Then he was gone down the alley, coat flapping, a crooked stack of books walking itself home.

* * *

The Archive of Saint Verenne occupied a former granary near the Temple Ward, which meant it was built like a fortress and smelled like mice. Bramwen met her at the grain-door with a lantern hooded in green glass, and led her down corridors where the shelves rose higher than the windows, higher than sense, whole forests of dead paper stacked into a cathedral nobody prayed in.

"You shouldn't be here after dark," he murmured. "The archivists keep hours. But the night clerks owe me money, and the wards on this wing are mine, so we won't be disturbed."

"How much do they owe you?"

"Enough." He unlocked a door at the corridor's end with a key from a ring of perhaps forty. "This is the restricted collection. Technically it doesn't exist. Technically neither do we."

Inside was a small round room, windowless, lit by a single lamp, dominated by a table and a lectern. On the lectern lay a book.

Elodie had handled hundreds of forbidden things. She had sold charms out of a false-bottomed flour bin for fifteen years; she had tasted poison for a king who would drown her if he learned what her thumb could do. Very little made her breath catch anymore.

Her breath caught.

The Hollow Codex was bound in grey leather gone silver at the edges, and it seemed to drink the lamplight rather than reflect it. There was no title on the cover. There didn't need to be. Every child in Vellamarch knew the name the way children know the names of wolves: *the Codex*, the century-old book of prophecy, the reason the covenant existed, the reason the fae realm was sealed, the reason magic was punishable by drowning. Half the laws of the kingdom traced their authority back to that book, and possessing a page of it carried a sentence no magistrate needed to read aloud.

"You're showing me a death warrant," she said.

"I'm showing you a translation." Bramwen's hands hovered over the codex without touching it, the way a man hovers over a sleeping animal. "I've been working on the marginalia for six years. Everyone studies the prophecies themselves—the big verses, the ones the priests quote. Nobody reads the margins. But the margins are where the original scribe thought out loud. And last month I found a sequence I couldn't parse. Names. Lists of names, generations of them, with dates. I thought it was a genealogy. Warden families, maybe. Record-keeping."

"And?"

"And it isn't a record." He finally looked at her, and his spectacles caught the lamplight and went blank white, twin moons. "It's a ledger of succession. The Coldharbour line, Elodie. Your line. Sworn wardens of the covenant. Keepers of the keystones. The scribe wrote out every heir for a hundred years, and beside each name, the date they were called."

"Called to what?"

"To the covenant. To the work. To—" He exhaled. "Just read it."

He turned the codex to its final written page.

The lamp guttered. Or the room did. Elodie would never afterward be able to say which.

The column of names ran down the margin in a hand that started formal and grew hurried, generation by generation, as though the scribe were running out of time or faith or both. *Maren Coldharbour. Sera Coldharbour. Ysolt.* Each with a date. Each date passed, decades ago, a lifetime ago.

And at the bottom of the column, in ink that looked fresher than it had any right to look:

**Elodie Coldharbour** — *the ninth of August.*

Tonight.

The room tilted. Not metaphorically—her inner ear simply resigned, and she put a hand flat on the table to keep the world from sliding off it. The lamp flame stood perfectly still. The mice in the walls had gone silent. Even her own heartbeat sounded distant, as if it were happening to someone standing in the next room.

"That's tonight," she said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. Fifteen years of tasting poison for kings taught you to keep your voice level while everything else burned. "That's today's date."

"Yes."

"Written a hundred years ago."

"A hundred years ago, give or take the calendar reforms." Bramwen spoke gently now, the way you speak to someone standing on a ledge. "Elodie, the codex doesn't predict. It records. Whatever the scribe saw, whatever was *shown* to the wardens when the covenant was struck—your name was in it. You were always in it. Everything you've ever—"

"Don't." The word came out sharper than she intended. "Don't say *everything I've ever done*. I built my life. Do you understand? Nobody gave it to me. I learned the poisons. I learned the charms. I found every customer, every safe house, every lie, myself, with my own wits, because nobody was coming to save me and nobody ever had." Her hand was still flat on the table; she noticed distantly that it had gone white at the knuckles. "If some dead monk wrote my name in a book before I was born, then I didn't build anything. I walked a cage and called it a road."

"Or," Bramwen said carefully, "the cage has a door, and the book is the map to it."

"And what's outside the door?"

"That's the part I haven't finished translating."

Of course it was. Of course the scholar had brought her to the edge of the cliff and lost the last page on the way down. Elodie laughed—one short, unsteady note—and dragged both hands through her hair.

Outside, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of boots. Many boots. In cadence.

Bramwen went rigid. "The night clerks don't march."

The lamp went out.

Not guttered—went out, snuffed by something that moved through the restricted stacks like weather, and in the sudden dark Elodie felt it: iron. Old iron and consecrated hemp and the particular sour tang of men who had been given permission to hate. Witch-finders carried their trade in their gear the way bakers carried flour. You could taste a raid three rooms away if you'd spent your life tasting things.

"Lamp," she breathed. "Now."

"No time." Bramwen's hand closed around her wrist in the dark. "Back stairs. Move when I move, breathe when I breathe."

"They can't know I'm here. They can't—Bramwen, if they find me with the codex—"

"They won't." A pause, and then, quieter, honest: "They might. Run anyway."

The door opened onto darkness and the smell of a thousand years of paper, and somewhere above them, floor by floor, the boots began to descend.

* * *

They nearly made the grain-door.

Nearly. The word would haunt her. They were twenty feet from it, pressed into the shadow of a shelving row taller than a hangman's scaffold, when the lanterns swung into the far aisle and the light found them—not them, not yet, but the space they would have to cross, and crossing it became dying.

Four witch-finders in the aisle ahead. Grey coats, iron chains at their belts, and the leader carrying a censer that smoked with something Elodie's tongue recognized from across the room: hexfoil ash. Truth-smoke. One breath of it and her trained lies would curdle in her mouth like milk.

Behind them, more boots. Ahead, light. Between, twenty feet of open floor and a locked door.

"Chapel side," Bramwen breathed against her ear. "There's a window in the scriptorium—"

"There's a window in the scriptorium three floors up."

"I'm aware."

"You're sixty years old."

"Fifty-eight," he said with dignity, "and motivated."

They doubled back through the stacks, Bramwen navigating by touch with the terrifying fluency of a man who had spent his life in places he wasn't supposed to be. The scriptorium door groaned; he winced; they slipped through into a high dark room where moonlight fell in pale bars through leaded glass, and below, in the courtyard, torches were blooming one by one like a crop of orange flowers.

They'd surrounded the building. Of course they had. Witch-finders did not raid archives; they besieged them.

"The window," Elodie said. "Tell me you have more than a window."

"I have a window and a fall."

"That's not a plan, that's an obituary."

"It's the plan I have." He was already dragging a writing desk toward the wall, splinters screaming, and every scream of wood was a shout to the men in the halls. "Roof of the cloister, then the wall, then the Tanner's Lane roofs—they slope toward the river—"

The scriptorium door burst inward.

Not opened—burst, hinges shrieking, and the first witch-finder came through with his censer swinging and his chain already loose in his fist, and behind him the corridor was full of grey coats and lantern-light and the sour reek of hexfoil smoke rolling in like fog off a grave.

"*By warrant of King Aldric,*" the leader began, in the bored voice of a man reciting words he'd said a hundred times, "*this place is seized for the crime of—*"

Bramwen stepped in front of her.

It was such a small motion. Such an ordinary motion—an old scholar squaring his shoulders, spreading his arms, putting his soft body between the iron and the thing he'd spent six years protecting. Six years of marginalia. Six years of a name in a margin. Elodie understood, in one vertiginous flash, that she had been the project all along—that the charm-book, the visits, the careful friendship built one authenticated forgery at a time, had been a scholar's long patience aimed at this exact moment.

"*Run,*" Bramwen said.

The chain came up. The censer swung. Hexfoil smoke curled toward her mouth, hunting for her truths, and Elodie Coldharbour—who had survived fifteen years by never running when watched, by always having a receipt, a story, a harmless explanation—did the one thing her training forbade.

She ran.

Through the smoke, past the chain's arc, shoulder into the second finder hard enough to feel his ribs give, out the shattered door and into the corridor where lanterns swung and boots thundered and the whole fortress-archive woke around her like a beast stung in its sleep. Behind her she heard Bramwen's voice, high and pedantic even now, citing statutes at men who had never read one—*you have no authority over the restricted collection, the restricted collection does not exist, sir, you cannot seize what does not exist*—

Then the corridor turned, and the sound cut off, and Elodie ran alone through the dark with a dead woman's name in a dead monk's book and her own name at the bottom of the list, dated tonight.

The ninth of August.

The stairwell ended at a door she didn't recognize, and beyond the door was not the courtyard but the river road—she would wonder forever how, and never get an answer—and beyond the river road was the river itself, black and broad and patient, the water where they drowned witches, glittering under the moon like a blade laid open.

Boots behind her. Torches ahead. The water waiting.

Elodie ran for the river, because it was the only direction left, and because some part of her—the part that had known her name before she did—was not afraid of it at all.
