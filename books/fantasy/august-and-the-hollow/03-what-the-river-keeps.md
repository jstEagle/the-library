## 3. What the River Keeps

The river had a name that nobody used anymore.

Officially it was the Verre, the same silver thread that fed the lake and turned the millwheels and carried the city's filth out to sea. But everyone in Vellamarch knew what the stretch below the Tanners' Bridge was really called, even if they only said it in whispers, even if they pretended not to when they crossed it on their way to market: the Drowning Stair. Nine stone steps descending into black water, worn smooth by a century of knees and chains and last prayers. The law of Aldric's grandfather required no trial for magic. Only water, and witnesses, and enough rope.

Elodie had stood at the top of those steps twice in her life. Both times she had been part of the crowd. Both times she had told herself the same lie: *they must have done something. Real magic. Not like my little charms.*

She was not telling herself that lie now. Now she was running toward the river with three witch-finders behind her and Bramwen Hale's voice still ringing in her ears—*your name, Elodie, your name is in the Codex*—and her lungs full of fire and the taste of old paper and fear.

Behind her, boots on cobbles. Ahead, the water.

*There's nowhere else,* she thought, which was not true, but panic is a poor cartographer. Panic draws every map with one road on it, and the road always ends where you're most afraid to go.

She burst out of the alley onto the embankment and the river opened before her, wide and dark and patient, and the Drowning Stair gleamed wet under the moon like something freshly licked clean.

* * *

She had been fifteen years learning to read danger the way other people read weather. A taster's tongue could not tell her what was coming, but her body had its own instruments—the prickle at the nape, the sudden silence of birds, the way a room's air changed when someone in it meant you harm. She had survived Vellamarch by treating those signals as scripture.

Every signal she owned was screaming now.

She skidded to a stop at the head of the stairs, chest heaving, and looked back. Three figures emerged from the alley mouth, unhurried. That was the worst part. Witch-finders never hurried. Hurrying implied doubt about the outcome, and witch-finders were the king's certainty made flesh: grey coats, iron pins at their collars, faces like closed ledgers. The one in front carried a net of fine chain over one shoulder, coiled neat as a fisherman's cast. The second held a lantern, and the light of it did not waver even though his hand should have been shaking from the run.

The third was a woman, tall, with a scar through one eyebrow, and she was smiling.

"Elodie Coldharbour," the woman said. The name sounded wrong in her mouth—too loud, too official, like a bell rung in a sickroom. "You've given us a fair chase."

"I don't know what you think I've done." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. Fifteen years of practice. You learn to speak clearly while your mind runs the exits. There were no exits. The embankment wall rose sheer to her left; the stair dropped into the river to her right; the alley behind them was the only way out and they were standing in it.

"We think nothing," the woman said. "We know. We have a warrant with your face drawn on it, and a book that says your family sold this realm's soul a hundred years ago, and tonight—" she smiled wider, "—tonight we found you kneeling next to that book like a daughter at a grave."

"It's not mine. I'd never seen it before tonight."

"No," the woman agreed pleasantly. "And I've never stolen anything, and my colleague has never lied to his wife. Shall we do confessions, or shall we do the work?"

The one with the net began, very casually, to uncoil it.

Elodie's back found the embankment wall. Cold stone through her shirt. Below her, the river moved without sound, without shine, drinking the moonlight and giving none back. She thought, absurdly, of the laundress from two nights ago, the luck-charm, the woman's cracked hands pressing coins into hers. *For my boy's apprenticeship. Just a little luck, miss, just enough.* Elodie had taken the coins because refusing would have raised questions, and she had made the charm well, and somewhere across the city a boy might be slightly luckier than he deserved, and none of it—not the shop, not the tasting, not the small careful goodnesses she had rationed out for fifteen years—none of it had bought her one inch of distance from this stair.

*They must have done something real,* she had told herself twice at the top of these steps. *Not like me.*

The net-man took a step forward. The lantern swung, and shadows leapt along the wall beside her like things trying to get free of the stone.

"There's a boat clause," she said quickly. "In the old edicts. If the accused reaches open water under their own power, the finding is—"

"The finding is whatever the Crown says it is," the woman said. "King Aldric revised the edicts in his first year. You'd know that if you'd ever bothered to learn the laws governing your own crime."

*But I'm not a criminal,* said some stubborn, stupid part of her, the part that had spent fifteen years being careful, being useful, being small. *I'm just a woman who sells luck.*

The net came up.

Later—if there was a later—she would try to remember the exact order of what happened next, and she would fail, because the order was wrong. Things happened out of sequence, or all at once, or in the space between one heartbeat and the next, and her mind refused to file them properly.

The net-man threw. She saw the chain spread against the sky, silver and beautiful, a flower of links opening to take her. She saw the woman's smile. She heard the river.

That was the thing she could not explain afterward. In the half-second before the net touched her, she *heard* the river—a sound beneath sound, deep and slow, like a word being spoken by something with an enormous mouth. And her body answered it.

Not her mind. Her mind was screaming *run, duck, fight, anything*, and her legs were doing none of it. But her blood—her blood did something. It turned toward the water the way a sunflower turns toward noon. She felt it happen, actually felt it, a warm dragging pull behind her ribs, as if someone had reached inside her and rotated her heart a few degrees to face the river.

The water rose.

Not a wave. Waves break, scatter, spend themselves. This was different. The whole surface of the river lifted—lifted, all of it at once, a mile of black water rising like a curtain—and hung there between Elodie and the thrown net. The chain hit it and stopped dead, links flattening against the surface as though it were glass, then sliding slowly down, down, swallowed without a ripple.

The river stood upright in the night. It did not fall.

The witch-finders had gone very still. Even the lantern seemed afraid to flicker.

"That's not possible," the net-man said. His voice had lost all its ceremony. He sounded young suddenly. He sounded like a boy who had been told all his life exactly how the world worked and was watching the world disagree.

The woman's smile was gone. She had her iron pin out—witch-finders wore them for exactly this, cold iron against hot blood—but her hand holding it was shaking, and the pin looked very small, a needle raised against a mountain.

"Coldharbour," she breathed. Not a curse. Almost a prayer. Almost a warning.

Elodie understood, distantly, that she should be running. Instead she stood with her back against the wall and watched the river hold itself upright for her, and terror curdled in her chest and turned into something else, something that frightened her more than the witch-finders did.

Awe.

Because the water wasn't attacking. It wasn't wild. It was *attentive*. It rose the way a dog rises when its person whistles—eager, exact, waiting for the next instruction. And the whistle hadn't come from her mind. Her mind had been busy being terrified. The whistle had come from somewhere older, somewhere below thought, in the place where her grandmother's lullabies lived, in the marrow-deep rooms of her body that she had never once visited on purpose.

Her body knew things her mind had never been taught.

*What else do I know?* she thought, and the river shivered, delighted, like a student raising its hand.

"Don't," the woman snapped—at Elodie, at the water, at the night itself. "Whatever you are, girl, whatever's left in your rotten line, the Crown will drown it out of you. We have iron. We have—"

"You have a choice," Elodie said, and was surprised to hear her own voice come out level. "Walk away, or find out what else I know."

It was a bluff. She didn't know anything. She was a food-taster standing in front of a miracle she hadn't asked for and couldn't repeat, and her knees were shaking so hard she could hear them knocking against each other. But the river backed her play—it surged a foot higher, groaning, the whole standing wall of it trembling with something that sounded horribly like eagerness—and the net-man broke first. He dropped the chain and ran. Then the lantern-bearer, the light swinging wildly, guttering, dying away down the alley.

The woman stayed longest. She looked at Elodie with an expression that was not fear and not hate but something worse: recognition. The look of someone seeing a debt come due after a hundred years.

"This isn't mercy," she said. "It's arithmetic. The Crown counts everything, Coldharbour. It counted your family once, all the way down to the last name. It will count again."

Then she was gone, and the night was just a night again, except for the river, which was still standing up.

* * *

"Put it down," said a voice behind the barrels. "Slowly. Like setting down a full cup you've decided not to drink."

Elodie's whole body flinched. The river lurched upward another foot in sympathy, and she felt the motion tug at her ribs, nauseating, intimate.

"Bramwen?" Her voice cracked on the scholar's name.

He unfolded himself from behind a stack of salt barrels, dusty, hatless, one sleeve torn at the shoulder where he'd gone through a window rather than the archive door. In his arms, wrapped in his coat like a swaddled infant, was the Hollow Codex.

"Yes. Hello. Very glad to see you, extremely unhappy to see that." He nodded at the standing water without looking directly at it, the way you avoid looking directly at royalty or fire. "Put it *down*, please. Before it does something neither of us can explain to the authorities."

"I don't know how."

"I know. That's rather the point. It's listening to what you *are*, not what you want. Which means the longer you stand here feeling things, the taller it gets." He picked his way toward her along the embankment, quick and precise, a man crossing a floor he suspected of being rigged. "Breathe out. Think of something boring. Ledgers. Bread prices. The tedium of committee meetings."

She laughed—one sharp, hysterical note—and the river sighed and began, slowly, joint by joint like a vast animal lying down, to lower itself. The surface settled. The moonlight went back to being drunk instead of returned. When the last of it folded flat, Elodie's knees finally gave, and she sat down hard on the embankment stones, and the cold of them came up through her clothes and grounded her like a peg in a tent.

Bramwen crouched beside her. Up close he smelled of ink and smoke and fear-sweat, and his hands, when he pushed back his hair, were not steady at all.

"They'll bring more," he said quietly. "Those three were a scouting pair plus a finder-captain. By dawn there will be forty, and by tomorrow, a decree read from every pulpit, and by week's end, your face on every gate in the March. We need to be underground and moving before any of that."

"Underground." She stared at him. "Bramwen. The water *stood up*. I didn't tell it to. I didn't know I could—I still don't know what I—"

"I know." Something in his face softened, and for a moment he looked less like a fugitive scholar and more like the man who had spent twenty years defending forbidden books from people who burned them, patient as erosion. "I know exactly what that feels like. The vertigo. The sense that the floor of your life was never a floor at all, just a lid." He glanced at the river. "It answered you because it remembers your family. Water keeps things, Elodie. Rivers especially. They keep names longer than stone does. Somewhere in that current is a memory of a Coldharbour walking these banks with authority, and when your blood called, the memory got up to serve."

"My blood called?"

"Your blood has been calling your whole life. Tonight is simply the first time anyone answered." He stood, wincing, and offered her his hand. "Which brings us to the unpleasant part. We cannot stay above ground, and we cannot go back for anything. Your shop, your things, your life as it was—that life ended tonight at the top of those stairs. I'm sorry. Grieve it fast and walk."

She took his hand and let him pull her up. Her legs held. Barely.

"The sewers," she said.

"The sewers."

"You've been in the sewers."

"I've been in the sewers *extensively*. Forbidden texts were hidden in Vellamarch's drains during the Purge, and I spent four years cataloguing them before the university revoked my post for knowing things it wished I didn't. I know which tunnels flood, which ones the rat-catchers abandoned, and which one comes up behind a chandler's yard in Lowmarsh where a friend owes me a debt and asks no questions." He was already moving, hugging the embankment wall, and she fell in beside him because the alternative was standing alone at the Drowning Stair waiting for forty witch-finders. "There's a grate two hundred paces south. It's heavy, so you'll want to be angry rather than tired by the time we reach it. I recommend thinking about your landlady. Or the captain's smile."

"She knew my name. Before the warrant. She said *Coldharbour* like she'd been waiting years to say it."

"They've all been waiting years to say it." Bramwen's voice dropped, and the Codex shifted in his coat, and for a heartbeat the moon caught the edge of a page—ink, dense and old, and somewhere in that density, her own name written down before she was born. "The question that should be keeping you awake isn't why they waited, Elodie. It's what your family did that made a hundred years of kings terrified to let the name be spoken."

Ahead, the grate loomed out of the dark, rusted bars thick as her wrist. Bramwen set down the Codex, spat on his palms, and gripped the iron.

"And the answer," he said, straining, metal shrieking, "is underneath us. Literally. So—down we go."

The grate came free. Black air breathed up out of the ground, smelling of rot and old rain and, faintly, impossibly, of something green and growing, like a garden shut in a cellar for a century.

Elodie looked back once at the river. It lay flat and silent, keeping what it kept.

Then she climbed down into the dark after Bramwen, and the city closed over her head like water.
