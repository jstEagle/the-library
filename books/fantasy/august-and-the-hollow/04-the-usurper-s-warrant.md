## 4. The Usurper's Warrant

The bells woke her before the criers did.

Elodie lay in the narrow bed above Bramwen's borrowed garret—no, not borrowed, she corrected herself, *hidden*—and counted the strokes. Nine. The morning bell rang at seven. Nine meant the king's justice, and the king's justice meant a crowd, and a crowd in Vellamarch meant someone was about to die or be named for dying.

She dressed in the dark. Her hands knew the work without her: boots laced double so they wouldn't slap the stairs, hair bound back under a plain wool cap, the little knife she'd carried for fifteen years sliding into its sheath at her wrist like a word into a sentence. Below her, Bramwen was already awake. She could hear him moving among his papers with the careful, guilty tenderness of a man handling things he expected to lose.

"You heard it too," he said when she came down.

"Nine bells."

"Aldric's called a proclamation." He didn't look up from the codex page he was copying by candlelight, though the sun had risen an hour ago and the light through the shutters made his ink look black as tar. "Witch-finders posted it on every gate before dawn. I sent a boy to read it aloud from the Cornmarket steps."

"And?"

Bramwen set down his pen. That was answer enough, but he gave her the words anyway, in the flat voice scholars use when precision is all that's left of courage: "*Elodie Coldharbour, last of the traitor line, sought for high treason against the crown. Reward of two hundred crowns for her capture alive. One hundred for her head.*"

"They want me alive," Elodie said. "That's almost flattering."

"It's worse than flattering. It's practical." He finally looked at her, and his eyes behind their smudged spectacles were old and tired and certain. "A dead Coldharbour is a story. A living one is a key."

Outside, the criers began.

Their voices carried down the crooked streets the way water finds drains, and Elodie stood at the shutter-crack and listened to her own name spoken in the official cadence—the one that turned a person into a warrant, a warrant into a hunt. *Elodie Coldharbour. Traitor. Witch. Enemy of the realm.* They had given her crimes she hadn't committed yet, which struck her as efficient, if nothing else. Aldric's clerks had always been thorough. She'd tasted their soup.

"The shop," she said suddenly.

"What?"

"My shop. My rooms above it. Maren—" Maren Ostwick, her landlady, sixty-one years old, hands like knotted rope, who had never once asked what Elodie kept in the locked chest under the counter because asking questions about your tenants' business was bad manners and Maren Ostwick had better manners than the whole royal court. "They'll go there first. It's the first place anyone would look, and everyone in Vellamarch knows where the charm-seller works, even the ones who don't know they know."

She was already pulling on her coat. Bramwen caught her arm at the door.

"If you go, you're seen. If you're seen, you're taken, and everything we learned last night dies with you."

"Then I'll be quick."

"That's not—Elodie." He said her name like an argument. "You are the last heir of a covenant older than this kingdom. You do not get to be quick."

She looked at his hand on her sleeve, then at him. "Maren took me in when I was nineteen and had nothing but a good palate and a bad reputation. She's burned my bathwater every winter without being asked. If witch-finders kick her door in because of me, I will carry that to whatever grave they dig for me, and I'll deserve it. Move your hand, scholar."

He moved his hand.

* * *

They went separately, Bramwen trailing forty paces back with his collar up, Elodie cutting through the tanner's yard where no one respectable followed and therefore no one respectable looked. The streets were wrong. She'd known they would be, but knowing didn't soften it. Fifteen years she had walked these lanes invisible—not safe, never safe, but *unremarked*, a face the city filed under *harmless*. Now every glance snagged on her. Now the baker's boy stared too long. Now two women by the well stopped talking when she passed, and the silence had a shape, and the shape was her name.

Two hundred crowns. She could buy the whole street for two hundred crowns. Half of Vellamarch would sell her for fifty and apologize for the discount.

The smoke told her before she rounded the corner.

It rose off her own roof in a fat grey column, wrong-colored for a chimney fire, and beneath the smoke-smell was another smell she knew intimately, professionally, the way a physician knows fever: pitch. Someone had fed the fire pitch to make it hungry fast.

"Maren." She said it out loud, stupidly, to no one, and broke into a run.

The shop was already gone. Not burning—*gone*, the flames having done their work with the efficiency of anything well-fed, leaving a black socket of a building, beams like broken ribs, the counter she'd weighed charms on collapsed into glowing charcoal. A bucket line was working the edges to keep it from spreading to the neighboring houses, and at the center of the wet ash, sitting on an overturned tub with a blanket around her shoulders and soot to the elbows, sat Maren Ostwick, watching her property burn with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic.

Elodie stopped ten feet away, because ten feet was as close as she could come without the crowd noticing, and the crowd was already noticing anyway.

"Maren," she said, low.

The old woman turned. For a moment her face did nothing at all, and then it did everything, grief and fury and something fierce and bright underneath, and she said, loudly, in the voice she used for disputing bills: "And there's the girl! Come to see what your carelessness cost me, have you? Leaving a lamp lit, I told her, I *told* her, some people shouldn't be allowed candles—"

"I'm sorry," Elodie breathed. "Maren, I'm so sorry, I'll pay you back, I swear it, every—"

"Pay me back?" Maren stood, blanket falling, and gripped Elodie's wrist hard enough to bruise, hauling her close so the words went straight into her ear, under the crowd's noise. "Listen to me, girl, because I've only got one speech in me and I'm spending it now. I saw the witch-finders coming up the lane an hour ago. Six of them, with the iron collars rattling on their belts like dinner bells. And I thought: they're here for her books, her chest, her papers—and then they'll be here for *her*, wherever she's sleeping tonight, and they'll ask me what I knew, and I'll say I knew nothing, and they'll believe me exactly as long as it takes them to check my cellar."

Her grip tightened.

"So I burned it. All of it. Your chest, your ledgers, your little bottles—everything that had your name anywhere near it. Let them dig. There's nothing left but an old woman's bad luck with lamps." She pulled back and looked at Elodie full in the face, and her eyes were wet and absolutely unrepentant. "Thirty years I've rented rooms, and do you know how many tenants ever once asked after my knees? One. So don't you dare stand there apologizing to me for a building."

"Maren—"

"Go." She released the wrist and raised her voice back to the crowd, theatrical, aggrieved. "And don't come round here again till you've learned to mind a lamp! Go on!"

Elodie went. She made it three streets before she had to stop in a doorway and press her wrist, the bruised one, against her mouth, and breathe, because grief had arrived all at once like a tide—grief for the shop, for the chest with her mother's charm-book in it, for the small orderly life she had built plank by plank over fifteen years and which had, she understood now, only ever been a disguise she'd mistaken for a home. Maren had burned it. Maren had burned it *for her*, freely, gladly, and Elodie could not even stand at her funeral for it.

Behind her, faintly, the crowd murmured. Ahead of her, the city stretched out, every street now a corridor with doors closing.

There was nowhere in Vellamarch she was welcome. But somewhere in it, apparently, she was loved, and the difference between those two things, she was beginning to understand, was going to matter more than the difference between life and death.

* * *

Sef Orlo counted spoons.

It was the first thing he did every morning, before the fires were lit, before the sous were shouted at, before the day's menus arrived with the king's moods folded inside them like notes in a cipher. Four hundred and twelve spoons, silver, each one accounted for, because in the palace kitchens a man survived by accounting for everything. Sef had been taster-master for nineteen years. He was a small, neat, grey man with a small, neat, grey soul, and he had one talent, which was noticing, and he had spent nineteen years making sure the palace never noticed him noticing.

This morning he noticed three things.

First: the king's breakfast order included a dish Elodie used to test personally, the almond custard, and the under-taster assigned to it—a boy called Pell, seventeen, ambitious, stupid—handled the tasting spoon wrong. Left-handed grip, thumb over the bowl. Everyone held it wrong except her. Sef had watched her hold it a thousand times and had never once, until this moment, understood why watching her had felt like watching the trade done properly.

Second: the kitchen was quiet in the wrong way. Servants quiet when a master walks in; that was normal. This was servants quiet when they're afraid of what they might say out loud. The proclamation had gone up at dawn. Two hundred crowns. The scullery girls were washing dishes like the plates owed them money.

Third: a witch-finder had come to the kitchens at dawn and asked to see the taster-master's records. Which tasters had served the king's table in the last five years. Their names, their lodgings, their habits.

Sef had given him the book. Of course he'd given him the book. A man did not refuse the iron collars; a man was not stupid. But he had copied out the page first, last night, in his own hand, before the finder ever came, because Sef Orlo noticed things, and what he had noticed, months ago, in the slow accumulation of small wrongnesses that preceded every purge like weather preceding a storm, was that the storm was coming for the girl.

Not because she was guilty. Because she was *useful*. A usurper needed witches the way a poor cook needed salt: constantly, and in quantities that ruined everything else.

At midmorning, alone in his office amid the ranked gleam of four hundred and twelve spoons, Sef unlocked the drawer where he kept the things that could hang him, and took out the copy he'd made, and looked at it for a long time.

He was not a brave man. He wanted that understood, by history if history bothered to record him, by God if God was taking notes: Sef Orlo was not brave. He had a daughter. Liss, eleven years old, a gap in her smile and her mother's temper, living with his sister in Ravenmoor since his wife died, because the palace paid a taster-master enough to keep a child safe in the country and not enough to keep her happy in the city. Everything Sef had ever done—all the noticing, all the silence, all the small neat greyness of his life—had been for her.

But he had also trained Elodie Coldharbour. Eight years ago, a frightened girl with a remarkable tongue, and he had taught her the discipline of it, the patience, the way you let a flavor tell you its whole story before you condemned it. She had been the best student he ever had, and he had liked her the way you like a well-made tool, which he had believed, until this week, was the whole depth of the feeling.

Then yesterday he had watched her walk out of the palace gates for the last time, and this morning he had watched Pell hold the spoon wrong, and he had understood that what he felt was not the liking of a tool. It was the shame of a craftsman whose finest work was about to be smashed by men who couldn't tell a spoon from a shovel.

And underneath the shame, quieter, older, something he had not permitted himself in nineteen years of palace survival: the girl had once stayed past midnight teaching his kitchen boy his letters, unpaid, unasked, because the boy was slow and ashamed of it. Nobody had told her to. Nobody had rewarded her. She had simply done it, and never mentioned it, and Sef had only found out a year later, and had said nothing then either.

He was tired of saying nothing. That was all it was. Tiredness. Certainly not courage.

Sef copied the witch-finder's route list onto the back of a market chit—the finders would sweep the riverside wards at dusk, the tanners' quarter tomorrow, the shrine district after—and wrote beneath it, in the cramped hand he used for inventory:

*They think you'll run for the gates. Don't. Every gate has a finder posted with your face drawn on slate. The sewers under the fishmarket connect to the old chapel crypts. Trust the scholar's maps, not mine—I'm watched.*

He wrapped the chit around a dried herring, the way fishwives wrapped purchases, and gave it to a delivery boy with instructions to leave it at the Ashlar Lane pie-shop, no name, payment already made.

Then he went back to counting spoons, because the count had to be right, because the count was always right, because a small neat grey man could do one useful thing per day and today's thing was done.

He told himself that was all it was. He was very good at noticing things, including lies, just not his own.

* * *

The pie-shop message reached Elodie at dusk, in the cellar beneath Bramwen's friend's printworks, where they had gone to ground among reams of paper and the vinegar-sharp smell of ink. She read the chit twice by candlelight while Bramwen hovered.

"Sef," she said. "My old master."

"He's informing on the crown?" Bramwen took the chit, examined it like a suspect manuscript. "Why?"

"Because I held a spoon correctly, apparently." She almost smiled, and it hurt her face to do it, so she stopped. "He's watched. We can't contact him. He knows that—he wrote it himself." She traced the last line with her finger. *Trust the scholar's maps.* "He means your archive charts. The ones the finders didn't burn."

"The sewer routes." Bramwen went pale in the candlelight. "Under the fishmarket. Elodie, that's where they drowned them, back in the purges—the old chapel crypts open right onto the river channel. That's why nobody uses them. That's why the finders won't expect—"

"—anyone mad enough to hide in the place the city drowns its dead witches." She folded the chit and put it inside her coat, over her heart, which was ridiculous, and she did it anyway. "Dusk tomorrow, he says, they sweep the riverside. Tonight they're still drawing my face on slates."

She looked at the candle flame and saw, superimposed on it, her shop burning, Maren's soot-black elbows, the bucket line working wet ash. Everything erased. Everything she'd built, gone in an hour of pitch and good intentions, and still—one herring, one market chit, one burnt building—still, somehow, the ledger of her life showed entries on the credit side she'd never known she'd earned. An old woman's arson. A grey man's treason. Loyalty arriving from directions she'd never thought to look, precisely because she'd never thought anyone was looking at her at all.

"We move tonight," she said. "Through the fishmarket, down into the crypts, out along the river channel beyond the walls. Before the sweeps start."

Bramwen was already reaching for his maps. "And after the walls? Where does a traitor go, when the whole kingdom's bought?"

Elodie touched the chit through the cloth, thinking of a small grey man counting spoons in a palace that wanted her dead, and gave the only honest answer there was.

"Somewhere they haven't priced yet."
