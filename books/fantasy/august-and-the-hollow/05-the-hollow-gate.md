## 5. The Hollow Gate

They walked through the night and most of the next day, and by the time the trees began to thin, Elodie had stopped asking how much farther.

Bramwen answered anyway, every hour, as though the question were a metronome she needed. "Half a league." "Past the stream." "When you see the split oak." The scholar had a way of narrating the world that made even terror feel like a syllabus. Elodie had decided, somewhere around dawn, that this was either a gift or a defense mechanism, and that she didn't have the energy to determine which.

Her feet were blistered through. Her boots—her good boots, the ones she'd worn to the palace for nine years without a single scuff—were ruined, caked in sewer muck and river clay and now the black loam of the deep wood. She had been a food-taster. Her hands had smelled of nutmeg and silver polish. Now they smelled of smoke and fear-sweat and the copper tang of the charm she'd kept clenched in her fist for two days straight, a little luck-knot braided from her own hair that she'd made at fifteen and never had the heart to sell.

"You're limping," Bramwen said.

"I'm walking with character."

"You're limping with character. Sit."

"I'd rather not."

"Elodie." Bramwen stopped on the path and turned, and in the green half-light under the canopy her face looked older than her forty-odd years, lined like a map of somewhere difficult. "In an hour, possibly less, you are going to stand in front of a door that has been shut for a hundred years, and something on the other side of it is going to try to take you apart to see what you're made of. Rest your feet now. I am begging you, as the last person in Vellamarch who still thinks you're worth the trouble."

Elodie sat.

The forest here was old in a way the city had taught her not to believe in. The trees didn't grow in the tidy rows of orchards or the managed stands around the king's hunting lodge; they grew in argument, in tangle, in great leaning conspiracies of trunk and root. Moss climbed everything. The air had a taste she couldn't name—green and cold and faintly sweet, like water that had been sitting in a copper cup.

"Tell me again what we're walking toward," she said. "Not the history. The actual thing."

Bramwen settled onto a fallen log across from her and pulled an apple from her pack, tossing it over. "A standing stone. Older than the kingdom, older than the covenant, probably older than the language we're speaking. When the covenant was forged, the Coldharbours built gates—thresholds where the two realms touched. Most were sealed when the covenant was struck. Sealed, not destroyed. The magic never went out of them. It's been waiting." She bit into her own apple. "Waiting for a Coldharbour."

"And if I'm not what the book says I am?"

"Then we've had a very long walk and I'll owe you an apology and a hot meal."

"That's it? That's your contingency plan?"

Bramwen chewed, swallowed, and looked at her with the mild, infuriating patience of a woman who had spent her life being shouted at by people who hadn't read the primary sources. "Elodie. I watched the river rise around you like a cupped hand. I watched a witch-finder's iron brand go cold in his grip when you screamed. I don't *believe* you're the heir of the Coldharbours. Belief is for priests. I have *documentation*."

Elodie almost laughed. Almost. What came out instead was smaller and less manageable. "My mother used to sing me a song about a door. I thought it was just a song."

Bramwen went very still. "What song."

"Doesn't matter. It was nonsense. Something about a stone that remembers, and knocking with your own name." She shrugged, aggressive and casual. "She sang it when she braided my hair. I was six."

Bramwen set down her apple. "Elodie. That is almost certainly a *warding verse*. That is a Coldharbour gate-song. There are three lines of it in the Codex fragment I recovered in—" She stopped herself, visibly recalibrating, and Elodie watched her do it, watched the scholar's instinct to lecture get wrestled down by the scholar's instinct for mercy. "We'll come back to it. Eat your apple."

They walked on.

* * *

The stone stood alone in a clearing where the forest simply gave up, as if the trees had agreed among themselves to leave this one place bare.

It was taller than two men, a single finger of grey granite thrust up out of the earth, and it was wrong in a way Elodie's eyes kept sliding off of. The light fell strangely around it. The birds didn't cross the clearing—she watched for a full minute and not one wingbeat passed over it. And the air near it had a pressure, a fullness, like the moment before a storm breaks, except the sky was clear and had been clear all day.

Up close, she could see that the stone was carved. The carving was so worn it was nearly invisible—spirals, she thought, and something that might have been hands, and a line of script in no alphabet she knew. Except.

Except the middle of it. One line of the script sat a little deeper than the rest, a little sharper, as though it had been traced and retraced by fingers over years. Over generations.

"Elodie," Bramwen said quietly, "what does that line say?"

"I don't know. It's not letters."

"Look again."

She looked. And the way she looked at things was her trade, her one inheritance from fifteen years of court kitchens—she looked at the stew and saw the oil sheen that shouldn't be there, looked at the wine and saw the sediment that didn't match the vintage. She looked at the carved line the way she looked at everything, with her whole attention, holding it still in her mind the way she'd hold a suspect flavor on her tongue.

And it opened. Not the stone—the *line*. The letters resolved the way a face resolves out of a crowd, and she heard them in her mother's voice, in the cadence of the braiding song, and the sound of them was: *knock with your own name.*

"Oh," she said. Her voice came out very small in the big empty clearing. "Oh, I hate this."

"I know," Bramwen said. "I'm so sorry. Do it anyway."

Elodie stepped forward. The pressure in the air thickened until it felt like wading. Ten feet from the stone, she stopped, because someone was standing in front of it.

He hadn't been there a moment ago. She was sure of it—she'd been looking at the stone, at the whole clearing, and the space in front of it had been empty grass and dappled light, and now there was a man standing there with his arms crossed, watching her with an expression of profound, weary irritation.

He was tall and lean, dressed in travel-worn grey that might have been fine once, and he was beautiful in the way that a knife is beautiful, all clean lines and cold edges. His ears came to subtle points. His eyes were the color of deep water over slate, and they were fixed on her with an intensity that made her skin prickle.

"You," he said, and his voice was low and rough, like a file drawn over iron. "You're the Coldharbour girl."

"I'm a food taster," Elodie said, because it was reflex, because it had kept her alive for fifteen years. "And you're standing on my— on the— what *is* this, exactly?"

"A door," he said. "And I'm not standing on it. I'm standing in front of it, which is the only job I have left." He uncrossed his arms. "Go home, taster."

"I don't have a home. It burned down. You'd know that if you read the warrants."

Something flickered across his face—not quite sympathy, more like recognition, the look of one exile acknowledging another. It was gone in an instant, smoothed away, but Elodie had spent fifteen years reading micro-expressions across a banquet table, catching the twitch that preceded poison or betrayal or a king's sudden, dangerous generosity. She'd seen it. She filed it away.

"Whatever you think is through that door," he said, "you're wrong. Whatever she told you—" he flicked a glance at Bramwen, "—is incomplete. And whatever you think you are, you're not ready. Turn around. Walk back to your burning city and die like a mortal, warm and ignorant. It's kinder."

"Kinder for whom?"

His jaw tightened. "For everyone."

"No," Elodie said. "See, that's the thing about people who tell me something is *kinder*. I taste for a living. I know what bitter things get wrapped in honey. Who are you?"

"Ivenn," Bramwen said quietly, from behind her. "Ivenn of the Autumn Court. Banished, a century ago, for—"

"For defending your kind during the wars, yes, I've heard the ballad," Ivenn said, and there was something ugly in his voice now, self-directed. "The ballad is wrong. Go home, Coldharbour. The door doesn't open for the doubtful, and you are *drowning* in doubt. I can smell it from here."

"Can you," Elodie said. "And what does doubt smell like?"

"Like fear. Like a woman who wants to be told what she is because she can't bear not knowing anymore."

The words landed like a slap, precisely because they were true. Elodie felt them sink in, find the soft places, the fifteen-year-old who'd stood in her mother's empty kitchen after the fever took her, holding a braid that was coming undone because no one had taught her how to redo it. The girl who'd spent her whole adult life being useful everywhere and welcome nowhere, tasting poison for a usurper, selling luck to laundresses, keeping her name small and her head down and her heart in a locked box.

"I said go *home*," Ivenn said, and his voice changed.

It deepened, or widened, or did something that a voice shouldn't do, and the clearing changed with it. The light went amber and gold, late-summer light, kitchen light, and the smell hit her before anything else did—bread rising, and rosemary, and the particular smokiness of their old stove—and Elodie's whole body turned toward it like a compass finding north.

Her mother was standing by the stone.

Not the mother of the blurred photograph-memory Elodie kept in her chest, not the faded, half-remembered shape of her. *Her*. Brown hair coming loose from its braid, flour on her forearm, the exact worn blue of her dress with the mended hem, the little scar on her chin from a dropped kettle. She was humming. The braiding song.

"Elodie, love," her mother said, and her voice was *right*, it was pitch-perfect, it was the voice that had sung her to sleep for six years and then stopped forever. "You look tired, sweetheart. Come here. I've been waiting so long."

Elodie's feet moved.

She was three steps closer before she caught herself, and even then she didn't stop—she slowed, her body fighting her, her eyes drinking in details her mind was screaming were impossible. The flour on the forearm. The way her mother stood with her weight on her left hip. The exact way she tucked her chin when she smiled.

"Elodie." Bramwen's voice, far away, thin as paper. "Elodie, it's not her. It can't be her. She's been dead nineteen years—"

"Let her speak for herself," Ivenn said, and there was something in his voice that Elodie, even drowning, even drowning in the smell of bread and rosemary, registered as *pity*. Pity, and something that looked like it hurt him to do this.

Her mother opened her arms.

And Elodie stopped.

Because—fifteen years. Fifteen years of tasting, of standing at a king's table with her life in her mouth, and the one thing they'd drilled into her, the first lesson, the lesson before all the others, was this: *the trap is always the thing you want most.*

The stew that smells perfect is the stew that's been doctored. The lord who praises you unprompted wants something. The wine that goes down easy is the wine to watch. Her taster-master Sef, gruff and gray and kind in his flinty way, had hammered it into her a hundred times: *Coldharbour, the poison is never in the bad dish. It's in the one you reach for first.*

And this—this was the dish she'd reach for first. This was the dish she'd crawl across broken glass for.

Her mother's smile faltered. Just a fraction. Just the way a mask falters when the thing wearing it isn't quite sure how the face works.

"You're dead," Elodie said. Her voice was steady. Her voice was the only part of her that was. "You died when I was six. Fever. Mrs. Halloway from next door buried you because I was too small to dig. I sat on the stairs and I didn't cry because I thought if I didn't cry, maybe it wasn't happening." She took a breath that shuddered going in and came out level. "And you never called me *sweetheart*. You called me *little wren*. Because I was small and noisy and I nested in the kitchen. So."

The clearing held its breath.

The illusion flickered—the flour, the blue dress, the amber light, all of it guttering like a candle in wind—and for one instant Elodie saw through it, saw the tall grey figure of Ivenn with his hand raised and his eyes closed and his face twisted with something that looked horribly like grief.

Then it was gone. The light snapped back to green and grey. The smell of bread was gone so completely it was like it had never been, and the absence of it was worse than any smell, and Elodie's knees very nearly went, and she locked them, and she stood.

Ivenn opened his eyes.

He looked at her—really looked, for the first time, the weary irritation gone, replaced by something more complicated, something that might have been the beginning of respect, or the end of it.

"The taster's tongue," he said. "You tasted the lie."

"I tasted the *honey*," Elodie said. Her hands were shaking now that it was over, shaking so hard she shoved them in her pockets so he wouldn't see. "The lie underneath was fine. Very good work, actually. The flour was a nice touch. But you got the name wrong, and the name is the whole dish."

"I didn't get it wrong." His voice was flat. "I didn't have it. No one living has it. That's the point, Coldharbour—that's the *whole point* of the test. The door doesn't open for the woman who wants her mother back. It opens for the woman who knows the difference between her mother and a *costume* of her." He stepped aside, finally, and the stone stood bare behind him, the worn line of script catching the light. "The Hollow took everything from me a century ago because I couldn't tell the difference. I've been standing at this door ever since, making sure no one else makes my mistake."

"That's a terrible job."

"It's the only one I'm qualified for."

Behind her, Bramwen made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Elodie didn't turn around. She looked at Ivenn, at the knife-clean lines of him and the grief underneath like a watermark, and she thought about the ballad Bramwen had quoted—the exile who defended mortals and paid for it—and she thought about the way he'd said *go home* like a man begging.

"Move aside properly," she said. "Or don't. But I'm knocking."

Ivenn held her gaze for a long moment. Then he stepped back, one pace, two, and gestured at the stone with something that was almost courtesy and almost surrender.

Elodie walked to it. The pressure in the air was enormous now, a held breath a hundred years long. She put her palm flat against the granite—cold, cold as river water—and she found the worn line with her fingertips, and she closed her eyes, and she did what her mother's song had always said, what the stone had been waiting a century to hear.

She knocked with her own name.

She said it aloud—*Elodie Coldharbour*, her whole name, the name she'd kept small for fifteen years, spoken at full size for the first time since she was a child—and the stone *sang*. A low, deep note, felt in the teeth and the breastbone more than heard, and the air in front of the granite split like a seam in cloth, peeling open onto—

Not the clearing. A road. A road of white stone under a sky the color of late afternoon that never quite ended, with trees along it whose leaves were every color at once, and far off, impossibly far, a spire catching light like a needle catching thread.

Bramwen made a sound behind her that was half prayer, half citation.

Ivenn looked at the open gate, and his face did something complicated and old. "Well," he said. "A century, and it opens for a taster with blisters." He shouldered his pack. "Someone has to make sure you don't walk into the first illusion on the other side and lie down in it. I suppose that's still my job."

"Your job," Elodie said, "is terrible."

"I'm aware." He glanced at her, and for a moment—just a moment—the watermark of grief in his face shifted into something drier, almost wry. "The flour *was* a nice touch. I stole it from your own memory. That's the part that should worry you."

It did. It worried her all the way through the gate, one hand on the warm stone, Bramwen's breath quick behind her, the mortal world falling away at her back like a coat she wasn't sure she'd ever get to wear again.

And ahead, on the white road, the light was already beginning to change—dimming at the edges, going grey and thin, and far off, the trees along the spire road were shedding their impossible colors like a molting bird, drifting down as dust.

Bramwen saw it too. Elodie heard her stop breathing.

"That," the scholar said finally, "is not in any of the accounts."

The leaves fell. And fell. And the Hollow, on the first day of its gate opening in a hundred years, was dying.
