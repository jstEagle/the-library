## 6. Keystone One: The Drowned Bell

The lake did not want them.

Elodie felt it in the way the water pressed against her chest as she waded in, cold climbing her ribs like fingers counting them off—one heart, two lungs, all of it belonging to Lake Verre now. Bramwen had said the chapel lay forty feet down, drowned when the old kings flooded the valley to erase what was built there. Erasing, Elodie had learned, was the one thing Aldric's line did better than ruling.

"You don't have to breathe yet," Ivenn said beside her, his voice carrying strangely through the shallows, too clear for a mortal throat. "The Coldharbour blood buys you air. It bought your grandmother an hour beneath this water, if the stories are true."

"And you?"

"I'm fae." He smiled without warmth. "Drowning is a mortal invention."

Bramwen stood on the shore with the codex strapped to his back under oilcloth, refusing to come further than knee-deep. "I'll keep watch," he'd said, which meant *I can't swim*, which meant none of them said anything about it at all.

Elodie went under.

* * *

The world below the surface was green dusk and silence, and then—gradually, as her ears surrendered to the pressure—it was not silent at all. The lake hummed. A low, vast note, like a bell struck once a century ago and still deciding whether to finish ringing. She could feel it in her teeth, in the soft bones behind her eyes, in the scar on her palm where a king's stew had burned her fifteen years ago.

She had expected ruins. What she found was a chapel entire, sunk whole and kneeling, its spire bent but unbroken, its windows glowing faintly with light that had no source. Fish moved through the nave like congregants late for a service no one had ended. The bell tower leaned over her, and hanging in it, furred with silt and trailing weeds like hair, hung the bell.

It was enormous. Bigger than any bell she'd seen in Vellamarch's cathedrals, big enough that its shadow had its own weather. And set into its bronze throat, catching the impossible light, was a wedge of pale stone no larger than a loaf of bread—the first keystone, fused into the metal as if the bell had been cast around it, or grown around it, or swallowed it and never let go.

Her grandmother's voice rose in her memory, unbidden, the way voices do underwater.

*Sleep, little eel, the lake is deep,*
*but deeper still the keeper keeps—*

The lullaby. The one her mother sang badly and her grandmother sang like a spell, because it was one. Elodie had thought for years it was nonsense verse, the kind old women make up to quiet children. Then Bramwen had shown her the codex page, the marginalia in her grandmother's hand, and every silly syllable had rearranged itself into instruction.

*Ring the bell, ring it well,*
*and speak the name the water tells.*

But the codex had been specific, in Bramwen's careful scholar's script: *the name is not written because names of wardens are not written.* Her grandmother's true name—the one given at her binding, the one that made her Coldharbour and nothing else—lived only in the lullaby, folded into it like a coin in bread dough.

Elodie had sung the lullaby ten thousand times. To herself, in her shop after closing. To the luck-charms as she sealed them, though charms didn't need songs. She knew every verse by heart.

She just hadn't known which words were the name.

* * *

She surfaced gasping, and Ivenn's hand was there before she finished gasping, hauling her toward the rocks where Bramwen waited, gray-faced.

"Well?" Bramwen demanded.

"It's there. The keystone's in the bell." She pushed wet hair out of her eyes. "I can reach it. But it won't come loose by force—I tried pulling at the metal through the water and the whole tower shuddered. Like the bell objected."

"Of course it objected," Ivenn said. He sat on a rock with the boneless grace of someone who found gravity optional. "You were manhandling a covenant-anchor. That bell isn't decoration, girl. It's a lock. Your family built locks into everything they loved—that's what wardens do. They make the important things hard to take."

"Then how do I take it?"

"You don't take it," Bramwen said quietly. "You ask. With the name."

"I've been through the lullaby a hundred times since the archive." She hugged her knees, dripping. "There's no name in it. There's a fish, a moon, a spoon, a girl who sleeps in a hollow tree—"

"The hollow tree," Ivenn said.

Both of them looked at him.

He shrugged, uncomfortable, as if being right cost him something. "The Hollow. In the old tongue, the word for the fae realm and the word for 'hollow' are the same word. *Hollow* means both the empty place inside a thing and the other world inside the world. If your grandmother hid her name in a children's song, she'd hide it in the one word that opens doors."

Bramwen was already unstrapping the codex, hands moving fast despite the damp. "The verses. Sing them. All of them. Every version you know—children change songs, they drop lines, add lines. The original will have a verse that doesn't scan. A verse that's wrong. Wrongness is where people hide things."

So Elodie sang, sitting on the cold stones of Lake Verre's shore with her two strange companions, the lullaby her grandmother had sung over a cradle in a house that no longer existed. The fish verse. The moon verse. The spoon verse, which had always been her favorite because the spoon was made of starlight and stirred a pot of morning.

And then the last verse, the one that had always felt slightly off, slightly too long, the one her mother always skipped:

*Hollow tree, hollow me,*
*hollow home where none can see,*
*Maren-sleeps-the-water-through,*
*Maren-wakes-when-bells-ring-true.*

Silence. Even the lake seemed to hold its hum.

"Maren," Bramwen whispered. "Your grandmother's name was Maren."

"How do you—" Elodie stopped. "Everyone called her Gran. Mother called her Mother. Nobody ever—"

"Because that wasn't her name." Bramwen's eyes were bright and terrible with understanding. "That was the name she was given. Maren is the name she *was*. The warden-name. The binding-name." He closed the codex gently, like closing a coffin. "She put herself in the song so that someone who loved her would carry her true name out of the drowning. Elodie—they drowned the valley to kill the Coldharbour line. She made sure the line survived in a nursery rhyme."

Elodie looked at the water. Green, deep, patient. Somewhere under it, her grandmother's bell waited with her grandmother's name folded into its bronze, and the woman who had been Maren had walked into that water a hundred years ago—or fifty, the dates blurred—and had never walked out, and had spent her last safe evening teaching a baby a song about spoons.

"Inheritance," Ivenn said softly, watching her face, "is a strange gift. It fits no one. It weighs what it weighs."

"Shut up," Elodie said, not unkindly, and stood up, and walked back into the lake.

* * *

Down was easier the second time. Her body remembered the trick of it now—the way to let the Coldharbour blood open in her chest like a second set of lungs, the way the water's cold stopped being an attack and became an attention. The lake was paying attention to her. She could feel it the way she felt poison on her tongue: not as flavor exactly, but as information. The water knew her blood. It had been waiting a long time to be introduced.

She swam down the length of the leaning spire, past windows where pale shapes drifted—fish, she told herself firmly, only fish—and into the bell tower through the rotted-open arch where the roof had failed. The bell hung above her, vast and dark, and the keystone glowed in its throat like a held breath.

She found a handhold in the tower frame and pulled herself up until she floated level with the bell's mouth. This close, the hum was almost solid. It moved through her sternum and rearranged something there. She thought of Sef, who had pressed a key into her hand three nights ago and said *I owe you a debt I can't repay safely,* and she thought of her landlady Marta standing in the street watching her shop burn and saying *better ash than chains, girl.* People kept giving her things lately. Heavy things.

"All right, Gran," she said into the water, and her voice came out as bubbles and as something else, something that traveled, something the lake carried down into the dark like a message delivered by hand. "Let's wake you properly."

She filled her lungs with the borrowed air her blood allowed her, and she sang.

*Maren-sleeps-the-water-through,*
*Maren-wakes-when-bells-ring-true.*

The name left her mouth and the lake took it. Not the sound—the *name*. She felt it go, felt the syllables peel away from the melody and sink into the bronze, and the bell began to move.

Not swinging. Remembering. The silt slid off it in sheets, a hundred years of the lake's patience falling away, and the weeds released their grip like hands opening, and the bell turned slowly on its beam until its mouth faced downward, faced her, and rang.

No sound. Underwater, a bell makes no sound she had ever learned. Instead the note went through her, through the water, through the stone of the drowned valley and the hills above it and the roots of the mountains beyond those, a single stroke of a single hour that had been waiting a century to be struck. The keystone loosened in the bronze like a tooth. Elodie reached up and took it, and it came free warm, warm as a living thing, warm as a hand, and the light in the chapel windows flared once and settled into something steadier, something like a lamp left burning in a window for someone expected home.

In her palm, the keystone was pale grey shot through with veins of silver, and it beat. Slowly. Once every few seconds. Like a heart that had been sleeping.

She held it against her chest and wept into the lake, where no one could see, and the salt of it vanished instantly into all that water, and that felt right. Grief belonged here. So did the strange fierce joy underneath it, because her grandmother had done it—had beaten the drowning, had hidden the line inside a lullaby, had trusted a granddaughter she'd never live to meet to sing the right verse at the right hour. Burden and gift, gift and burden, indistinguishable, the same object turning over in her hands.

*I have you now,* she told the keystone. Or it told her. Or they told each other.

Above, muffled by fathoms, something exploded on the shore.

* * *

She broke the surface to smoke and shouting.

Bramwen was dragging their packs away from the fire where a soldier's torch had landed; Ivenn stood waist-deep in the shallows looking bored in a way that made Elodie's stomach drop, because she'd learned that his boredom was what he wore instead of violence. On the shore road, maybe thirty soldiers in Aldric's black-and-gold, dismounting fast, crossbows leveling.

"Witch-finder's scouts spotted us an hour ago," Bramwen shouted over the water. "I thought we'd lost them in the hills—"

"They followed the bell," Ivenn said. His voice carried across the water effortlessly, infuriatingly calm. "A covenant-anchor waking rings in iron and blood alike. Every witch-finder within twenty miles felt their teeth ache. Congratulations, Coldharbour. You've announced yourself."

"Can we outrun them?"

"On foot, with a scholar? No." Ivenn glanced back at her, treading water, and for the first time since she'd met him he looked genuinely uncertain. "But they can't follow us down, and the lake goes farther than they think. There are ways under. Old ways. Your family cut them."

"Then we go under."

"With the soldiers between us and the far shore?"

"We're already past them," Elodie said, and dove.

Behind her, muffled and distant, she heard the officer shouting orders, heard the first crossbow bolts slap harmlessly into the water where they'd been, heard Bramwen's undignified yelp as Ivenn hauled him into the lake whether he could swim or not. She swam down into the green dusk with the keystone beating against her heart, and the lake parted for her—not dramatically, not with spectacle, just the small courtesy of a place recognizing its own.

At the bottom, where the chapel's foundation met the bedrock, she found it: a doorway in the stone, carved with the same lullaby verses running around the arch like a border, sealed by a slab that bore a handprint worn smooth by generations of Coldharbour palms.

She pressed her hand into it. It fit the way the keystone had fit her grip.

The slab ground aside, and cold dry air breathed out of the dark—an old tunnel, a warden's road, running beneath the lakebed toward whatever came next. She turned in the water and saw Ivenn coming down with Bramwen clutched in a grip the scholar would describe later, at length and with grievance, as *undignified in the extreme.*

They reached her. They crowded into the doorway's breath of air, three refugees in a hole in the world, while above them thirty soldiers searched the emptying lake for a woman who had already gone.

"One," Elodie said. Her voice echoed strangely in the tunnel mouth. "One keystone. Two to go."

"And now Aldric knows exactly what you are and exactly where you're going," Ivenn said. "The next one won't be waiting politely in a bell, girl. The Thorn Crown is in the Briarwood, and the Briarwood has opinions."

Elodie looked down at the keystone in her hand, beating its slow patient beat, her grandmother's heartbeat loaned forward across a hundred years of drowning.

"Good," she said, and stepped into the dark.
