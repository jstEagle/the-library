## 7. Terms of Exile

Ivenn had walked the borderlands for a hundred years and never once slept beside a fire built by mortal hands.

He told himself it was strategy. The girl needed rest; the scholar needed warmth; a cold fae could watch through the dark hours without tiring. That was all this was—watchfulness, not company. He sat on the far side of the flames with his knees drawn up and his knife across his thighs, and he told himself the arrangement was practical, and he almost believed it.

The girl—Elodie, though he refused to use the name yet, refused to let it settle into him like a splinter—had fallen asleep sitting up, her back against Bramwen's pack, one hand still curled around the first keystone as if it might be stolen in the night. It couldn't be. He'd warded the hollow three times over. But mortals clutched things even when the things were safe. It was one of their more exhausting habits.

Bramwen Hale was not asleep. The old scholar sat cross-legged opposite, feeding twigs to the fire with the fussy precision of a man who had spent his life arranging footnotes.

"You're staring at her," Bramwen said.

"I'm guarding her."

"You're staring at her *and* guarding her. They're not mutually exclusive." He set another twig down. "In my experience they rarely are."

Ivenn said nothing. Above them the pines hissed in a wind that smelled of snow from the high passes and, faintly, of rot—the Hollow bleeding through where the veil wore thin. Three days since the standing stone. Three days since he had tested her with her dead mother's face and she had chosen truth over comfort, which was either courage or a kind of blindness, and he hadn't decided which, and not deciding was beginning to itch.

"She asked about you," Bramwen said. "While you were scouting. Asked why a fae would bother warding a mortal camp."

"And what did you tell her?"

"The truth. That I don't know." Bramwen smiled thinly. "Scholars are allowed to say that. It's our one privilege."

The fire popped. Elodie stirred but didn't wake. In the light her face looked younger than it had any right to look, given what she'd survived in the last week—a raid, a river rising to answer her blood, a kingdom's warrant with her name on it. Mortals burned fast. That was the whole tragedy of them. They burned so fast, and then they were ash, and the ash didn't even remember being warm.

"Ask me tomorrow," Ivenn said.

"I wasn't going to ask anything."

"You were building up to it. You've been building up to it since the stone gate. You do that thing with your hands when you want to ask something and are pretending to be patient about it." He nodded at the scholar's fingers, which were indeed lacing and unlacing around a twig. "Mortals always think they're subtle. You're a species of open windows."

Bramwen laughed—a dry, surprised sound—and for a moment the camp felt like something other than a truce between strangers. Then the laugh faded, and the question came anyway, because of course it did.

"Why *were* you banished, Ivenn?"

* * *

There were answers he could give. He had a hundred years of practice at answers: *I broke a law. I spoke out of turn. I loved wrongly.* Answers shaped like locked doors.

Instead he heard himself say, "Do you know what the covenant wars actually were? Not the songs. Not the histories your kind wrote afterward, which are mostly lies with dates attached."

"I've read the primary sources," Bramwen said, with the mild stubbornness of a man who took insults to his profession personally. "Treaties, correspondence, the Vellamarch accords themselves."

"The accords." Ivenn turned his knife over so the flame ran along the blade. "Written by kings. Signed by kings. Enforced by kings. Do you notice the pattern?"

"I've noticed," Bramwen said carefully, "that you're avoiding the question."

And there it was—the trap of his own making, sprung by his own mouth. A hundred years of silence, and one tired night by a small fire, and he opened it like a fool.

He looked at Elodie instead of at Bramwen. It was easier to say it to someone sleeping.

"There was a village," he said. "On the mortal side, near the Thornmarch. Callyn's Ford, it was called. During the wars, both courts used the borderlands as a corridor—for troops, for raids, for the moving of things that shouldn't be moved. And the villages in the corridor were—" He paused. "Expendable. That is the diplomatic term. Both sides agreed the border villages were expendable. It was in an annex. Annexes are where kings put the people they intend to sacrifice."

Callyn's Ford had been forty households. He could still name eleven of them. The miller with the burn-scarred forearms. The twin girls who'd dared each other to touch his shadow. The priest who'd known exactly what Ivenn was the moment he walked into the chapel and had offered him bread anyway, because hospitality was a law older than either court.

"A fae raiding party came through in the autumn of the last year of fighting," he went on. "Not soldiers. Foragers—cruel ones, the kind who take children for sport and call it gathering. My queen's orders were explicit: the corridor was to remain deniable. Whatever happened in the mortal villages, we were not there."

"What did you do?" Bramwen asked quietly.

"I was there." The words came out flat as slate. "Loudly. With witnesses. I put myself between the foragers and the village and I made sure every surviving soul saw my face and knew my name, because a named witness is the only protection mortals have ever had against us. Deniability died that night. So did two of the foragers, because they argued the point." He shrugged. "The village lived. Mostly."

"And your queen?"

"Banished me. Stripped my name from the rolls, which for us is worse than death—it means no door opens for you, no bargain holds, no kin may speak your true name without penalty. She called it mercy." His jaw tightened. "She was right, technically. The alternative was the rusting. But mercy from a queen is just cruelty with better tailoring."

The fire had burned low. He fed it a branch, harder than he meant to, and sparks went up into the dark like escaping thoughts.

"So," he said. "That's the shape of it. I defended mortals during the covenant wars, and my own court cast me out for it, and I have spent a century telling anyone who asks that I don't care." He finally looked at Bramwen directly. "You'll observe that I've just admitted I care enormously. I'd thank you to forget it by morning."

Bramwen was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had lost its pedantic edge entirely.

"They never taught us that version. In the archives, the fae who broke ranks are villains. Traitors to their own kind, which the histories treat as self-evidently monstrous."

"Because your historians were writing for kings, and kings need traitors more than they need truth." Ivenn bared his teeth—not quite a smile. "A traitor proves loyalty exists. A hero who crosses lines proves the lines are arbitrary. Kings can survive the first. Never the second."

* * *

Elodie woke somewhere in the middle of it—Ivenn knew because her breathing changed, that particular held quality of a person pretending to sleep—and she lay still through the rest, which told him she was either courteous or afraid, and he found he minded less than he should have which.

When he finished, she sat up and pushed her hair out of her face and said, "Callyn's Ford."

His whole body went still. "What?"

"My grandmother sang about it. A lullaby." Her voice was rough with sleep. "I thought it was nonsense verse—*the ford runs red, the ford runs free, hold the bridge for those who flee*—children's rhyme stuff. There was a second verse about a fae who stood in the water." She frowned, digging for it. "*And the tall one in the shallows stood / with winter in his open hand.* Something like that. I haven't thought of it in years."

"That song is a hundred years old," Ivenn said.

"Yes."

"Mortals don't keep songs a hundred years unless they matter."

"No," Elodie said. "They don't."

He turned away and busied himself with the fire, because there was nothing on his face he wanted witnessed. A hundred years he had assumed the village had forgotten him within a generation—assumed it gladly, because being forgotten was easier than being remembered wrong. And instead some miller's daughter had taught her daughter a rhyme with him in it, and that daughter had taught hers, until the thing had traveled a century to land in the ear of a food-taster who turned out to be the last heir of the Coldharbour line, and was now sitting at his fire repeating his own deed back to him in a child's singsong.

"You stood in the shallows," she said softly. "With winter in your open hand."

"It's a nonsense verse," he said.

"It's a memory somebody thought was worth keeping."

"Elodie." He made himself look at her. "Whatever you're building in your head—some story where I'm your ally because I once liked mortals—take it apart again. I don't help people. I survive near them. There's a difference, and the difference has kept me alive for a century."

"All right," she said, in the tone of someone agreeing with a child who insists the moon is cheese.

He hated her a little for it. He suspected, distantly, that this was going to become a problem.

* * *

Later—deep in the dark, when even Bramwen had finally succumbed—she spoke again from across the embers, and this time there was no sleepiness in her voice at all.

"Do you believe in the covenant?"

He should have pretended to be asleep. He was out of practice at pretending; a hundred years alone will do that. "Define belief."

"Do you think it ever served anyone but kings?"

Ah. There it was—the real question, the one underneath the question, the one she'd been carrying since the codex, since the river, since the warrant. He considered giving her the diplomatic answer. He was very good at diplomatic answers. He had a century of them, polished smooth as river stones.

Instead he said, "Sit up properly. If we're doing this, we're doing it awake."

She sat up properly. Bramwen, damn him, surfaced from sleep like a fish rising to feed, because scholars can smell an argument the way sharks smell blood.

"The covenant," Ivenn began, "was sold to both realms as a wall against chaos. No fae predation on mortals; no mortal magic bleeding into the Hollow. Peace, order, the end of the wild years. That was the pitch." He drew a line in the dirt with one finger. "Here's what it actually was. A tariff. Every crossing regulated, every gift taxed, every bargain brokered through offices both crowns controlled. Before the covenant, a fae and a farmer could trade a song for a jar of honey without asking anyone's leave. Afterward, that same trade required a license, and the license cost more than the honey, and the office that issued it answered to a king."

"The wild years weren't peaceful," Bramwen objected. "The sources are clear—raids, changeling thefts, whole valleys emptied—"

"Some of that is true. Some of it is the same lie wearing armor." Ivenn met the scholar's eyes. "Your kings wanted monopoly. Ours wanted containment—they feared mortal magic the way you fear plague. The covenant served both fears at once. And the price was paid by everyone who wasn't a king: the fae exiles like me, stranded or banished with no recourse; the mortal cunning-folk, criminalized overnight for gifts their grandmothers had practiced openly; and the border folk, who went from neighbors to subjects of a closed frontier." He flicked the dirt from his finger. "So. Did it serve anyone but kings? It served the comfortable. Which is nearly the same set, and overlaps the kings completely."

Elodie was quiet, turning something over. When she spoke, her voice was careful. "My family kept it. The Coldharbours. Wardens of the covenant—if the histories are right, we enforced it. Held the keystones. Bound the gate."

"Yes."

"Then my whole line was—" She stopped. Started again. "We were the lock on a cage, and we thought we were the honor guard."

Ivenn had expected anger, or grief, or the brittle defensiveness of someone defending their ancestors. What he hadn't expected was how quickly she got there—how little argument it took. Most mortals needed days to reach the point where their inheritance became a question. She'd done it in a sentence, sitting in the dirt, hugging her knees.

"Or," Bramwen said slowly, and Ivenn recognized the tone—he'd heard it in archives before, the sound of a man watching his life's work tilt, "or the wardens believed the covenant was a bridge, and the kings let them believe it, because a bridge-keeper guards the crossing for everyone while a jailer only guards it for the crown. Same stones. Different story. And whoever controls the story controls the keeper."

"That's very neat," Ivenn said.

"It's very *true*."

"It's very neat *because* it's true. That's the trouble with truth. It fits too easily into whatever argument is nearest." He poked the fire. "Here's what none of your histories will tell you, scholar, because it reflects badly on everyone including me. The covenant didn't fail because kings corrupted it. It failed because both peoples forgot they'd ever been able to talk to each other without it. A hundred years of locked gates, and now the fae can't imagine trusting a mortal, and mortals drown any child who shows a spark. The cage worked so well that everyone inside it forgot there was ever an outside." He looked at Elodie. "Including the wardens. Especially the wardens."

She absorbed that without flinching. "Then what would you do? If it were yours to decide. Reforge it? Let it die?"

The question hung there, bigger than the fire, bigger than the night.

"I don't know," Ivenn said, and the admission cost him more than the story of Callyn's Ford had. "A hundred years ago I'd have said burn it. Now I've seen what the borderlands look like with no covenant at all—thin places, hungry things, villages like yours learning to fear the dark again. And I've seen what it looks like *with* the covenant, which is a prison with very clean corridors." He spread his hands. "Maybe the answer isn't reforging or burning. Maybe it's something neither court has ever allowed anyone to build, because it requires asking ordinary people what they want, and no crown survives that conversation."

Silence. The wind moved through the pines. Somewhere far off, something in the thin places cried out—not a wolf, not a bird, one of the other things, the things that had crept back into the world as the veil decayed.

Elodie pulled her cloak tighter. "The Queen of Rust," she said. "You said she holds territory near the deep crossings. That she might help us find the other keystones."

Ivenn's stomach dropped, slow and cold, the way it always did at that name. "I said she *might*."

"What's her price, usually?"

"Truths," he said. "She trades in truths the way your merchants trade in salt. And her truths are never the ones you want to sell." He looked into the fire rather than at her. "If we go to her—and we may have to—we go knowing that everything we carry, she will weigh. Everything we hide, she will find. That's not a threat. It's just her nature. Rust gets into everything eventually."

Elodie nodded slowly, and he watched her file the warning away next to all the others she'd collected in one impossible week, and he thought: *she doesn't yet understand what she's carrying. She thinks her secrets are luggage. She doesn't know yet that some of them are detonators.*

But that was a lesson for another day. Tonight the fire was warm, and the wards held, and for the first time in a hundred years Ivenn sat at a flame with people who knew the worst thing he'd ever done and had not, apparently, found it disqualifying.

It was a dangerous feeling. He catalogued it the way he catalogued all dangerous things, and resolved to keep it at arm's length.

Across the fire, Elodie had gone quiet again—but not asleep. She was looking east, toward the low hills and the deep crossings beyond them, and her hand had drifted to the keystone in her lap, and her expression was the one he would learn to dread in the months ahead: the look of a woman doing arithmetic with her own life.

"We'll need more than truths," she said at last, half to herself. "We'll need an army."

Ivenn followed her gaze east, toward the Queen of Rust, and felt the future close around them like a hand.

"Yes," he said. "And I know exactly how much she charges for one."
