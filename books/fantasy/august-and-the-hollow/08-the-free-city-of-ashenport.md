## 8. The Free City of Ashenport

They smelled the city before they saw it.

Salt and tar and woodsmoke, and underneath it all something Elodie hadn't smelled in fifteen years of living in Vellamarch: bread that wasn't rationed. Yeast and warm grain drifting on the wind off the water, thick enough to make her stomach cramp with want.

"Smugglers' town," Ivenn said, from the crest of the dune road. He said it the way other men said *cesspit*, but his ears had gone forward, the way they did when something interested him despite himself.

"Free town," Bramwen corrected. He was leaning on his walking stick more heavily than he'd admit, his scholar's softness worn down to gristle by three weeks of hard road. "Aldric's writ doesn't run here. Hasn't since the Salt Riots, before your time."

"Before my time," Elodie said, "is a phrase people keep using around me as if I were a child."

"You're twenty-three."

"And you're sixty-one and still alive because I taste things. Respect the tongue, scholar."

He smiled at that—Bramwen's smiles came rarely and crookedly, like furniture left behind by a previous tenant—and they crested the last dune together, and Ashenport opened below them like a wound that had learned to sing.

The city sat in the crook of a gray harbor, built upward in terraces of black timber and whitewashed stone, its rooftops crowded with gulls and laundry and signal fires. Ships crowded the anchorage beyond counting—fishing smacks, coastal traders, two lean raiders flying no colors at all—and every pier was a brawl of crates and shouting stevedores. On the highest terrace, above the tiled roofs of the merchant quarter, stood a squat stone tower flying not a king's banner but a plain white flag marked with an open hand.

"The Harbor Council," Bramwen said. "Merchants, shipwrights, one reformed pirate, and a woman who used to be Aldric's customs mistress until she embezzled half his fleet. They govern by argument and bribery. It's hideous. It works."

"It's loud," Ivenn said, and pulled his hood lower over his ears. Three weeks on the road had taught him that hoods fooled no one for long—his eyes caught lamplight like a cat's, and he moved wrong, too smooth—but fools were fools everywhere, and a hood bought them the extra seconds a knife required.

Elodie looked down at the harbor and felt something loosen in her chest that she didn't have a name for. In Vellamarch she had been useful everywhere and welcome nowhere: the taster who ate first and spoke last, the charm-seller whose door stayed shut and shuttered. Here, nobody knew her. Nobody knew what she was. For one afternoon, she could be a tired woman on a road, hungry for bread.

"Come on," she said. "Before Ivenn decides to be difficult about boats."

"I am always difficult about boats," Ivenn said. "Fae don't float. It's a matter of dignity."

* * *

They came down into the Salt Quarter as the evening markets opened, and Ashenport swallowed them without ceremony.

That was the thing that undid her, in the first hour. No one looked twice. A witch-finder's notice might as well have been wallpaper here; she saw one pasted to a chandlery door, her own face inked in crude miniature beneath TRAITOR TO THE CROWN, and a fishwife had hung a string of gutted mackerel directly over it, obscuring everything but her own eyes. The fishwife caught Elodie looking and shrugged.

"Pays better than the reward," she said, meaning the mackerel, and went back to her knives.

They found lodging above a tavern called the Drowned Rat, which Bramwen swore was respectable by Ashenport standards, a claim the Rat itself did nothing to support. The landlady—a broad, gray-haired woman named Maren with forearms like anchor chain—took their coin, looked at Ivenn's hood, looked at Bramwen's stick, looked at Elodie's face, and said only:

"Bathhouse is through the yard. Trouble stays outside or it gets thrown outside. Breakfast is whatever the boat brings in."

"That's it?" Elodie asked. "No questions?"

Maren looked at her the way a smith looks at a nail that has asked why it should be hammered. "Questions cost extra," she said, and went back to her ledger.

Elodie lay that night in a bed that smelled of salt and lavender—actual lavender, someone had been allowed to grow lavender—and listened to the harbor bells count the tide, and did not sleep so much as sink, gently, like a stone wrapped in wool.

* * *

It lasted four days.

On the fifth morning, the Drowned Rat's common room was full of a different kind of silence—the kind with edges. Maren stood behind her bar with her arms crossed, and around the trestle tables sat a dozen dockhands and fishermen, and in the middle of them a boy of maybe ten was crying without any sound at all, which was somehow worse than wailing.

"—third shipment turned back at the Verre road," a stevedore was saying. "Soldiers at the toll bridge now. Real soldiers, not toll-takers. They're burning what they confiscate. Burning *grain*, Marta. In front of the farmers who grew it."

"Aldric's making a point," said a woman with a harbormaster's chain of office around her neck. Elodie would learn her name was Odile Ferrant, the reformed pirate, though "reformed" seemed generous. "Starve the Quarter into kneeling. He can't take the port—he hasn't the ships—but he can choke the roads."

"The mills are empty," another voice said. "The relief barges from the north are three weeks late, if they come at all. There are four hundred souls in the Verre camps eating one meal a day. Come winter there won't be camps. There'll be graves."

The boy cried on, silently. His mother held him and stared at nothing.

Elodie stood at the foot of the stairs with her bowl of breakfast porridge going cold in her hands, and the old instinct rose in her the way it always did—not heroism, nothing so grand, just the taster's reflex: *this is wrong, and I know how wrongness tastes.*

She set down the bowl.

"What's the grain situation in the city itself?" she asked.

The room turned. A stranger's voice, a stranger's face. Odile Ferrant looked her over with a pirate's frank arithmetic. "Who's asking?"

"Someone who used to work with food for a living." Careful. Not a lie, exactly. "Warehouse stocks. Spoilage rates. What's fit to eat versus what's fit to sell."

"Why?"

"Because spoilage is a lie," Elodie said. "Half of what granaries call spoiled is only touched. Weeviled, damp, beginning to turn. There's flour in this city right now that some clerk wrote off as loss, sitting in a warehouse getting worse while children eat once a day. And there's more than that." She hesitated. This was the part where the old fear lived, the part that had kept her shop shuttered for fifteen years. But the boy was crying without sound, and the fishwife had hung mackerel over her face without being asked, and Maren charged extra for questions, which meant questions were worth paying for.

"And there's charm-work," she said. "If anyone here still remembers that word without spitting."

The room went very still. Somewhere a chair creaked. Odile's eyes narrowed, and Elodie watched the arithmetic run behind them—reward posters, witch-finders, the price of sheltering magic in a free city that stayed free partly by not asking.

It was Maren who broke it. The landlady came around the bar, took Elodie's cold porridge, and refilled the bowl without comment, which in the grammar of the Drowned Rat was a speech.

"Show me," Odile said.

* * *

The warehouse was on Tern Street, down among the salt-stained timbers, and it belonged to a grain factor named Hobb who met them at the door sweating through a shirt worth more than Elodie's entire former wardrobe.

"There's nothing wrong with my stock," he said. "Nothing that concerns the Council, anyway. Some of the spring wheat took damp in the crossing, yes, naturally, that's ocean freight for you, but it's written off, it's accounted, it's—"

"Open the third bay," Odile said.

Hobb opened the third bay.

The smell hit Elodie before the light did: damp and rot and the sweetish edge of beginning fermentation, and under it, faint but unmistakable to a trained tongue even through the air, the bitter almond ghost of cheap rat-poison scattered carelessly near the sacks. She breathed through her mouth and walked the rows, dragging one finger along the sacking, pressing kernels between thumb and forefinger, cracking a few against her teeth when Hobb wasn't looking.

"Well?" Odile said.

"Three kinds of problem." Elodie held up fingers. "This row is truly gone—rotten through, feed it to fish or burn it. These two rows are weeviled but sound; sift it fine, toast it hot, and it makes bread good enough for anyone who isn't a fool. And this last section—" she knelt, split a sack, rubbed the flour between her palms, tasted the barest dust of it on her tongue, and waited, professionally, while her body catalogued the answer. Her heart did not speed. Her mouth did not numb. "—is merely damp and lonely. It thinks it's ruined. It isn't. It wants to be bread. It's been told it can't be."

Hobb made a noise like a man being introduced to a new religion.

"And the rest?" Odile said quietly. "You said charm-work."

Elodie stood. Fifteen years of hiding rose in her throat like bile, and she swallowed it, because the boy on the trestle bench was still crying somewhere in her memory, silently, and because a fishwife had covered her face with mackerel, and because Maren had refilled her bowl.

"I need a brazier, clean water, a sieve, and everyone who isn't willing to watch magic done badly to leave."

Nobody left.

* * *

What she did was not impressive, to look at. That was the joke of charm-work; the great workings got the ballads, but hunger was fed by small things. She warmed the water and spoke to it—not words, exactly, more a shape of intent pressed into the steam, the way her grandmother had taught her mother had taught—no. Not taught. Never taught. The way her blood simply knew, the way her tongue knew arsenic from almond oil, knowledge arriving from a room in her she'd never been shown.

The charm settled into the damp flour like warmth settling into a cold room. Mold spores died. Excess water loosened and lifted. The flour sighed—it did, actually sigh, a faint exhalation of musty air rising off the sacks—and turned pale and sweet-smelling, and Hobb sat down on a crate as if his legs had been repossessed.

By noon, the Tern Street ovens were running. By evening, the smell of fresh bread moved through the Salt Quarter like weather, and people came out of their doors to stand in it.

Word travels fast in a hungry city. Faster than ships, faster than soldiers. By the second day, factors were finding Elodie at the Drowned Rat with samples under their arms, sheepish as schoolboys: *took damp, mistress, whole lot of it, worthless, unless.* By the fourth day, Odile Ferrant put her on the Council's emergency grain docket with a title nobody had voted on—"the Quarter's taster," which made Bramwen laugh until he wheezed—and by the sixth day, children were following her through the lanes chanting a name she hadn't given them.

"Coldharbour! Coldharbour girl! Charm us a sunny day!"

"They don't know," Bramwen murmured, watching her from the tavern steps, his stick across his knees. "About the Codex. About the covenant. To them you're just a clever woman with warm hands."

"Good," Elodie said. Then, honestly: "I don't know which is worse. Being feared for what I am, or loved for what I'm not."

"Both are cages," Bramwen said. "But the second one has better bread."

* * *

On the ninth night, the Quarter threw what Maren refused to call a festival, on the grounds that festivals invited gods' attention, and called instead a "large supper."

Long tables ran the length of Tanner's Lane under strings of lanterns. There was fish, obviously, mountains of it, and the famous Ashenport bread, and a thin sharp cider that Ivenn drank with an expression of profound betrayal—he'd expected swill and gotten something excellent, and fae apparently considered being pleasantly surprised a personal humiliation. Someone had produced a fiddle. Someone else had produced a drum. The boy from the Drowned Rat, whose name was Pip and whose mother was called Sela, appointed himself Elodie's shadow and spent the evening demanding charms: a floating lantern, a knot that untied itself, a seashell that whispered the sea when you held it to your ear.

"That last one isn't a charm," Elodie said. "That's just a shell."

"Do it anyway."

She did it anyway. The shell whispered the sea, and Pip's eyes went wide as portholes, and Elodie felt something in her chest crack slightly along an old seam.

Sela found her later, at the end of the tables where the lanterns thinned out. The woman sat down beside her without asking, handed her a cup of cider, and looked out at the lane—at the fiddle, the dancing, the bread, the hundred small fires of ordinary joy.

"My man drowned two winters back," Sela said. "Deep-water crew. And after, I thought, well. That's the shape of things. You lose, and you keep losing, smaller and smaller, till it's just you and the boy and the dark." She drank. "Then you come along. Don't know where you learned your tricks, don't care. But the bread came, and the neighbors came with it, and Pip laughs again." She turned and looked at Elodie straight on, unflinching, the way people in Ashenport seemed to do everything. "Whatever you're running from, girl—you've got a table here. You had it before you charmed the first sack. You had it the day you walked in and asked about the grain."

Elodie's throat closed. Fifteen years in Vellamarch, and no one had ever said anything like that to her. Useful, yes. Necessary, tolerated, paid, suspected. Welcome—never once welcome.

"I'm not—"

"Don't argue with a grieving woman," Sela said, "it's bad luck," and went back to the music, and left Elodie sitting alone with a cup of cider and a feeling so large and so frightening that she nearly preferred the witch-finders.

Belonging, she thought. So that's what it tastes like.

Like bread. Like bread, and salt, and cider, and a child laughing at a seashell. Nothing rare in it at all. That was the terrible secret: belonging wasn't a treasure. It was a meal. Ordinary, daily, eaten in company, gone by morning and needed again by night.

Across the lane, Ivenn was teaching dockhands a fae drinking song that was almost certainly obscene, and they were roaring it happily in garbled translation. Bramwen sat wrapped in a borrowed shawl arguing theology with a fisherman's widow and, by the flush on his cheeks, losing. The lanterns swung. The harbor bells rang the tide.

And then the music faltered.

Not stopped—faltered, the way a room falters when a stranger comes to the door. Down the lane, at the mouth of Tanner's Street, stood a man in a mud-stained traveler's coat, gray-faced, limping, one arm bound in a sling made from what had clearly been his own shirt. He was a small man, round-shouldered, built like a question mark, and Elodie knew the walk of him before she knew the face—knew it from fifteen years of palace kitchens, from a thousand mornings of tasting before kings.

"Sef," she said, standing, the cup falling from her hand.

The taster-master of Vellamarch looked up the lane at her, and his eyes were red-rimmed, and his mouth was trembling, and every instinct she owned—the tongue, the blood, the animal brain older than both—went suddenly, perfectly cold.

Because Sef had ridden nine days hard to reach her. And Sef would not look her in the eye.
