## 9. Betrayal in the Salt Market

Sef had lied to kings before. It was, in a sense, his profession.

A taster-master's whole trade was deception performed in reverse—swallowing truth so that others need not taste it. He had spent thirty years learning which poisons wore the perfume of almonds and which wore the perfume of loyalty, and he had told himself, all those years, that he was good at his work because he could hold two truths in his mouth at once without choking.

He told himself that now, standing in the witch-finder's antechamber with a sealed letter burning a hole through his coat like a coal.

The letter was addressed to *the Coldharbour girl, care of the salt market, Ashenport*. It contained a warning: *They know where you are. The codex must be moved tonight. Trust no one who arrives from Vellamarch.*

Every word of it was true except the one that mattered. They did know where she was. The warning would bring her out of hiding, into the open, at the hour the witch-finders chose. And the man who arrived from Vellamarch—the tired old taster with kind eyes and a daughter in the capital—was the trap itself.

"Master Sef." The witch-finder's voice came through the door like something dragged over gravel. "You're certain you understand your part?"

"I've played parts for kings," Sef said. "One more won't kill me."

The door opened. Inquisitor Vael was younger than Sef expected, which somehow made it worse. Young men did cruelty the way apprentices did knife-work—all enthusiasm, no economy. He had a scholar's soft hands and a zealot's clean nails, and he smiled as though smiling were a duty he performed for the state.

"Your daughter is well," Vael said. "She eats well. She sleeps well. She asked after you this morning—*Papa, when does he come home?*—and we told her soon."

"She's seven."

"Six and three-quarters. We checked." Vael set a small tin soldier on the table between them, the kind sold outside the palace gates for a copper. Maren's. She'd lost it months ago; Sef had torn the house apart looking. Here it was, polished, placed upright on the scarred wood like a piece in a game whose rules only one player knew.

"You don't have to do anything else," Vael said gently. "You've already done it. You wrote the letter. You'll deliver it. You'll stand in the salt market at dusk and watch her read it, and then you'll walk away, and in a week you'll be home, and none of this will ever have touched her. That's the whole of it. A letter and a walk."

"And if she runs?"

Vael's smile didn't move but something behind it did, the way water moves under ice. "Then my colleagues are bored men with fast horses, and Ashenport is a long way from anywhere. But she won't run. From everything we've learned about Elodie Coldharbour—and we have learned a great deal—she doesn't leave people behind. It's her defining flaw." He picked up the tin soldier, examined it, set it down again with its face toward Sef. "Yours too, I think. It's why you're here instead of some hired courier. She trusts you. Isn't that right? She trusted you enough to feed you secrets across a year of kitchen conversations. What did she call you? Old Sef. *Old Sef, who taught me which mushrooms lie.*"

Sef said nothing. His mouth had gone dry in the way it did before a tasting—not fear exactly, but the body's professional attention to what was coming.

"The tide turns at dusk," Vael said. "Be at the salt market an hour before. Wear the grey coat. And Master Sef—" the young man's voice dropped into something almost tender, almost human, which was the cruelest register he owned— "*thank you.* Most men in your position would have needed more convincing."

Most men, Sef thought, walking out into the grey Vellamarch drizzle, would have needed a daughter.

* * *

The road to Ashenport took four days by carrier's cart, and Sef spent every one of them rehearsing.

Not the words—he was good at words, words were the easy meat—but the face. He practiced the face against the cart's canvas flap, against the reflection in his soup, against the memory of himself at twenty-five, when his hands still shook before each royal tasting and an older taster named Corvin had told him: *The trick isn't to feel nothing, boy. The trick is to feel everything and let none of it reach your tongue. A taster who swallows his own terror dies honest. A taster who serves it up smooth lives to be ashamed.*

Corvin had died honest, in the end. Almond in the wine, spring of the old king's last year. Sef had tasted it first, spat it, saved the court, and been promoted for it into a life of smooth service and private shame. He understood now that the whole profession was a rehearsal for this: the carrying of poison in a vessel that must show nothing.

He arrived in Ashenport on the fifth morning, smelling of brine and other people's smoke.

The free city hit him like weather. He'd heard the reports—*den of sedition, haven of witches, a boil to be lanced*—but reports didn't prepare him for the simple fact of it: children running unchaperoned through streets where a charm-seller's sign hung openly from a hook, painted with a little blue hand, no fear in it at all. An old woman at a fish stall muttering over her scales and the scales *glowing faintly*, answering her, and nobody crossing the street to denounce her. A wall covered in chalked names—*the disappeared, the drowned*—and beneath them, fresh chalk, someone's defiant arithmetic: *WE COUNT.*

In Vellamarch, magic was a crime you drowned for. Here it was a woman selling luck-knots beside her husband's herring.

*This,* Sef thought, *is what they sent me to burn.* And then, because he was who he was—a small man, a practical man, a man who had survived three reigns by keeping his eyes on his own bowl—*and what choice do you have, old man? What choice has any of us ever had?*

He found lodging above a chandler's shop and asked, casually, the way travelers do, after the Coldharbour girl. The chandler's wife looked at him a long moment—too long—and then said, pleasantly, that she didn't know anyone by that name, and charged him double for the candle he bought, and he understood that the whole city was a closed fist around her, and that his letter would have to find her some other way.

It found her, or she found it, on the fourth evening.

He was eating the city's terrible spiced eel at a stall near the salt market when he felt it—that prickle on the neck, the taster's oldest instrument, the skin's own tongue. Someone watching. He turned with his bowl in his hand and there she was.

Thinner than the kitchens had made her. Sun-browned, wind-chapped, hair tied back with string like any dockside girl. But the eyes were the same—those quick weighing eyes that had once swept a banquet table and found the one dish that would kill a minor lord, while the whole glittering room went on chewing, trusting, oblivious.

She looked at him the way she used to look at suspicious venison.

"Old Sef." Not moving closer. Not moving away. "They said you'd died. When the shop burned, they said the taster-master had been questioned and released and then vanished."

"Released," Sef said. "Vanished. Both true, in their way." He set down the bowl. His hands were steady. Thirty years of practice, and they were steady, and he hated them for it. "I came with a warning, girl, and you're not going to like the messenger any more than the message."

"Try me."

So he gave her the letter. He watched her read it—watched her lips move slightly over *trust no one who arrives from Vellamarch*, watched her look up at him, at his Vellamarch mud still on his boots, at his grey coat, and he watched the exact moment the suspicion crested and broke against the older thing underneath: trust. Trust given freely, in a kitchen, over years, over stolen bread and whispered warnings, over the night he'd looked away while she palmed a charm off a serving tray.

Trust was the poison. He was the vessel. Serve it smooth.

"They have the codex?" she said.

"They have where it *isn't*," Sef said, and heard his own voice do the thing, the warm gravelly thing, the old-Sef-who-taught-me-which-mushrooms-lie thing. "Bramwen moved it before the raid—I don't know where, the scholar's careful—but the finders got enough of it to know the rest. There's a man coming, girl. Not soldiers. Worse. Vael. He travels light and he pays in gold that smells of blood, and he'll be here within the week, asking after a scholar with a limp and a fae with a temper. If the codex is found in Ashenport, the city burns. You know what he's done to towns that sheltered less."

Elodie folded the letter slowly. Around them the salt market roared on—herring and hemp and haggle, a fiddler tuning, gulls screaming theft—and Sef thought: *she could die here in this noise and the noise wouldn't even stumble.*

"Why come yourself?" she asked. "Why risk it? You're known. Your face is known in every palace kitchen from here to the capital."

Because my daughter is six and three-quarters, he did not say. Because they keep her in a clean room with a tin soldier and a woman who braids her hair and asks her questions, and every answer she gives is a coin spent buying my obedience, and she doesn't even know she's rich.

"Because someone had to warn you," he said, "and I'm expendable."

Something crossed her face then—recognition, maybe, or grief, the look of a girl who had also spent a lifetime being useful everywhere and welcome nowhere. She put her hand briefly on his arm. Her grip was stronger than it looked. Working hands now.

"Dusk tomorrow," she said. "There's a boat. Bramwen will want to argue about it—you know how scholars are, they'd rather be right than safe—but we'll move the codex out on the night tide, north to Ravenmoor, where the smugglers owe me. You should come with us, old man. Whatever you ran from, it can't follow you onto a smuggling boat."

"No," Sef said. "I have to go back."

"To *him*?" Her eyes sharpened. "Back to that nest? Sef, they'll hang you if they catch you twice."

"They won't catch me," he said, and it was the truest sentence he'd spoken in five days, because they already had him, they'd caught him whole, hook to gill, and there was no second capture required. "I have people in the capital. People I have to get out. Let an old man keep one secret, girl—it's the only kind of wealth I've ever held."

She studied him. He let her. It was the last honest thing he could give her: his face, unguarded, full of everything, reaching his tongue not at all.

"All right," she said finally. "Dusk tomorrow. The salt market, by the counting-house steps. I'll bring the codex myself—one last look at the pages before it goes north. And Sef—" she paused, and the market noise seemed to lean in around them— "whatever you're not telling me. Whatever it costs you. I've carried things like that. I know the weight. So: thank you."

She squeezed his arm again and melted into the crowd, and Sef stood alone among the herring barrels with her gratitude sitting in his stomach like the almond wine had sat in Corvin's, sweet going down, fatal after.

* * *

That night he did not sleep. He sat in the chandler's loft with the shutters open, listening to the free city breathe, and he took out the tin soldier he had stolen back from Vael's table when the young man wasn't looking—stolen with a taster's hands, quick and light, the one theft of his career—and he turned it over and over in the moonlight.

Maren had cried for three days when it was lost. Not because it was precious—tin soldiers were a copper apiece—but because it had been *his*, bought at the autumn fair, the day Papa had lifted her onto his shoulders and bought honey-cakes and let her stay up past dark to see the lanterns. It was the only object in the world that remembered that day besides Sef himself.

*They will braid her hair,* he thought. *They will ask her questions. And when I have done what they want, they will either return her—or they will not, because men like Vael do not spend assets they can keep, and a taster-master who has betrayed one side can always be made to betray the other, and a child who knows her father's face is worth more to them free than returned.*

He knew this. He had known it since the antechamber. He served the poison anyway, smooth, because the alternative was to imagine the clean room going dark, the braids undone, the questions turning into something else—and a man can swallow a great deal rather than imagine that.

Toward dawn he made his decision, and it was not a noble decision, and he was honest enough with himself to know it. He would not confess. He would not throw himself on Elodie's mercy or Bramwen's scholarship or the fae's indifferent justice. He was a small man and he would act like one: he would deliver the warning he'd been paid to deliver, and then, in the confusion, in the shouting and the iron and the smoke, he would go to the harbor and steal a boat, and row out to the smugglers' anchorage, and buy his daughter back with gold and guilt and whatever else a coward's purse could purchase. And if the free cities burned a little faster for it—if Ashenport's chalked names grew longer by a few lines—then that was the price of one small girl with braided hair, and he would pay it, and hate himself, and live, which was the arrangement he had made with the world thirty years ago and had never once managed to break.

* * *

Dusk came up gold and salt-smelling over the rooftops, and the salt market emptied of daylight the way a cup empties—slowly, then all at once.

Sef stood by the counting-house steps in his grey coat, an hour early, as instructed. The witch-finders were already in position; he'd marked them through the afternoon the way he'd once marked dishes—a lean man mending a net badly, a heavy woman drinking in the Salt Cellar with her back to the wall and her eyes on the doors, two more lounging by the harbor chain pretending to be sailors and fooling no gull in the city. Vael himself was nowhere visible, which meant he was everywhere visible, which meant close.

The lamps came up. The fiddler packed his fiddle. And Elodie came out of the crowd with a wrapped bundle under her arm and a scholar and a fae flanking her like mismatched bookends, and Sef's heart did the thing hearts do in the last moment before a tasting—that lurch, that animal knowledge ahead of the mind.

Bramwen Hale was thinner than his reputation, ink-stained, limping exactly as described, clutching the argument he'd clearly been having for hours: "—all I'm saying is that the northern route passes within forty miles of a garrison, whereas the coastal route merely requires us to survive the *sea*, which is at least an honest enemy—"

Ivenn, tall and grey-eyed and radiating the specific boredom of the immortal inconvenienced, said, "Both routes end with us alive or dead. Choose faster."

And Elodie stopped, ten feet from Sef, and looked at him.

Later—in the years after, in the retellings, in the history Bramwen would eventually write—Sef would never learn how much she saw in that final moment. Whether the taster's daughter of instinct read the poison in his face the way she'd once read it in a stew, or whether she simply walked into the trap because that was who she was, the girl who didn't leave people behind, the girl who thanked the man betraying her because she knew the weight of unsayable things.

What happened was this: she smiled at him, tired and real, and said, "You waited. Good. Come on, then—there's a boat, and Bramwen's about to lose an argument about it—"

And the lean net-mender stood up from his bad mending, and the woman came out of the Salt Cellar with iron already gleaming in her fist, and the two false sailors dropped the pretense of sailorhood along with their coats, and the beautiful ordinary noise of the salt market tore straight down the middle.

"*Witch-finders!*" someone screamed, uselessly, the way people scream the name of a storm already overhead. "*Finders in the market!*"

"Elodie!" Sef's voice cracked out of him, thirty years of smooth service shattering in a single syllable. "Run—it's me, it was always me, the letter, I—"

But she was already moving, bundle clutched to her chest, Ivenn's blade out and singing, Bramwen stumbling backward with his arms full of nothing, and the market erupted around them—stalls overturning, the chandler's wife hauling children behind the fish racks, the old woman with the glowing scales standing her ground and throwing light like a handful of thrown sand.

They took Bramwen first. Of course they did; he was slowest, and scholars run the way books fall. Sef saw the heavy woman's iron catch the scholar across the shoulder and saw him go down under the fish-rack awning, saw the codex slip from Elodie's arms as two finders closed on her and Ivenn cut a shrieking arc that bought her a heartbeat, two—

"Go!" Sef screamed at her, lunging into the path of the net-mender's cudgel and taking it on his own shoulder, hearing bone speak, going down among the herring barrels with the taste of blood and brine in his mouth. "Girl, *go*—"

She went. He saw her go—saw Ivenn haul her toward the harbor shadows, saw her look back once, one look, the weighing eyes, the eyes that had trusted him across a year of kitchen truths, and in that look was not forgiveness and not hatred but something worse: understanding. She had tasted the poison after all. She had swallowed it anyway, because swallowing was what survivors did.

Then the harbor shadows took her, and the market belonged to the finders.

They bound Bramwen Hale in the lamplight and stacked the seized codex—wrapped, intact, damning—into Vael's waiting hands, and Vael emerged at last from wherever he'd been watching, smiling his dutiful smile, and stopped in front of Sef where he lay broken-shouldered among the barrels.

"Well done, Master Sef," the young man said softly. "Your daughter sends her love. She's learned to write her letters, you know. All of them. Even the difficult ones."

He set the tin soldier down on a barrel head, upright, facing Sef, and walked away to count his prizes.

Sef lay in the spilled salt and the fish-smell and the ruin of the only family that had ever chosen him, and he began, very quietly, in the manner of a man who has finally tasted his own dish, to laugh—or to weep; the two flavors were indistinguishable now, and he was, after all, a professional.

Above the rooftops, far to the north, a smuggler's boat slipped out on the night tide carrying a girl who now knew exactly what he was.

And Sef, broken and grinning and drowning in shame, made the only vow left to a small man who had finally grown too large for his own lies: *Ravenmoor gaol, then. Whatever it holds. Whatever it costs.*

*I am coming for both of them.*
