## 10. Interrogation of the Scholar

They took his spectacles first.

Bramwen Hale had expected the manacles, the hood, even the bucket of river water they emptied over him in the second hour—witch-finders were nothing if not ritualistic, and ritual was merely pedantry with a grudge. But the spectacles stung worst. Without them the world became a smear of torchlight and shadow, and he resented it the way a scholar resents a misprinted page: personally.

"Name," said the witch-finder. His voice came from somewhere to Bramwen's left, which meant the man liked to circle. Circling men wanted you dizzy. Dizzy men talked.

"Bramwen Hale, formerly of the Royal Archive at Vellamarch, dismissed under circumstances I have documented at length and would be happy to produce."

A pause. The dripping of water somewhere behind him. "Formerly."

"I was dismissed for correcting the Lord Chancellor's genealogy in an open court session," Bramwen said. "He claimed descent from King Osric the Second. He descends from Osric's *steward*. The steward's line is perfectly respectable, I might add. There was no need to lie about it."

The fist that caught his jaw was professional, unhurried—the blow of a man who had done this ten thousand times and found it dull. Bramwen's head rang against the chair back. He tasted copper and, beneath it, something faintly bitter: the dregs of whatever they'd put in his evening meal. Foxglove, perhaps, or hemlock cut with wine. A sedative, not a poison. They wanted him pliable, not dead.

*Interesting,* thought the scholar in him, absurdly, through the ringing. *Low dose. Titrated. Someone with pharmacological training wrote their procedure manual.*

"Again," said the witch-finder. "Your name."

"Bramwen Hale." He worked his jaw. "And yours? I like to know whom I'm disappointing."

The circling stopped. Footsteps came around front, and through his blurred vision Bramwen made out a broad shape in grey wool, iron pins at the collar—the uniform of the King's Finders, though *king* was doing generous work in that phrase. The man leaned close. He smelled of tallow and wet stone.

"You were taken with the Coldharbour woman," the finder said. "With her books. With *this*." Something heavy landed on Bramwen's thigh: the Hollow Codex, wrapped in oilcloth. Even half-blind he knew its weight, its smell of old vellum and older secrets. His heart did something complicated in his chest—grief and relief tangled together. They had it. Which meant Elodie didn't, which meant she might live.

"Do you know what this is?" the finder asked.

"A book," Bramwen said. "Late binding, earlier text. The vellum is calf, poorly scraped on the final quire, suggesting haste or poverty in the scriptorium. The marginalia spans four hands across roughly ninety years. It is catalogued in my own index as—" he paused, savoring it— "*Item 114, restricted shelf, do not remove without countersignature.* Which someone has clearly ignored, and I intend to raise the matter with the Archivist when I am released."

"When you are released," the finder repeated, flat.

"When," Bramwen agreed. "Not if. If is for poets. When is for archivists. We deal in certainties."

The blow this time caught his ear and set the whole world bell-tolling. He rode it out, breathing carefully, counting the drips of water behind him the way he counted everything—seven between blows now, six before. They were accelerating. Good. Impatience was information.

* * *

They gave him three hours in a cell afterward, which he spent composing a mental catalogue of the room by sound alone: drip interval, echo decay, the scrape of the door's iron hinge (left side, rusted low), the breathing of two guards who changed watch every forty minutes precisely. Forty minutes. Sloppy rotation discipline. He filed that away too, the way he'd once filed away the Chancellor's false genealogy—not because it would save him tonight, but because truth hoarded was truth armed.

When they came for him again, it was not the grey-wool finder but a different quality of silence entirely. The guards' breathing changed. Someone important had entered his cell.

"Light," said a voice Bramwen knew from proclamations nailed to every post in Vellamarch. Smooth. Patient. The voice of a man who had learned charm as a second language and never quite lost the accent of ambition underneath. "Give the scholar light. He'll want to see what he's looking at."

Torchlight bloomed. Through squinting eyes Bramwen beheld King Aldric—no crown, just a plain circlet, a man dressed like a general pretending to be a clerk—and felt the peculiar thrill of the authenticator meeting the forgery.

"Master Hale." Aldric crouched to his eye level, which was either courtesy or theater. "I've read your dismissal papers. Correcting the Chancellor's lineage. You have a reputation for being unable to leave an error uncorrected, even when correction costs you."

"It's not a virtue," Bramwen admitted. "It's a condition."

"Good." Aldric smiled. "Then correct me. Tell me what the Coldharbour woman is, and what she intends, and where she has gone, and I will see you restored to your archive with a pension and an apology read aloud in court. By the Chancellor. In the matter of the steward."

It was, Bramwen reflected, a very good offer. Precisely calibrated. The apology detail proved Aldric had done his research—which meant Aldric feared him, or feared what he knew, or both. Fearful men overpaid. That was the first law of interrogation, and Bramwen had written a monograph on it that nobody had read.

"Before I answer," Bramwen said, "I should like to establish terms of accuracy. You've asked four questions. I can answer at most one truthfully per day without compromising the integrity of the source material. Prophecy doesn't reward haste. Ask any oracle."

"I'm not asking the oracle. I'm asking you."

"The codex speaks *through* its custodians. That's rather the point of custodians." He gestured weakly at the oilcloth bundle. "May I?"

Aldric considered, then nodded to the guard. The Codex was placed in Bramwen's lap, and his hands—shaking, bruised, but *his*—found the familiar cover. He did not open it. He simply held it, the way a musician holds an instrument, and felt his pulse slow.

"There is a passage," Bramwen said, "in the third quire, concerning the warden line. Would you like to hear it?"

"If it serves the crown."

"It serves whoever hears it accurately. Whether it serves the crown depends entirely on the crown." He cleared his throat, and began, in the flat cadence of citation rather than performance:

"*When the last warden stands unbound, the covenant shall ask its price anew: not of kings, who made it, nor of priests, who kept it, but of the blood that bears it. And the throne that claims the warden's hand shall find the warden's hand remembers older oaths.*"

Silence. Water dripped. Somewhere above them, Vellamarch went about its evening, buying bread, telling lies, drowning no one yet.

"That's vague," Aldric said at last.

"It's prophecy. Vagueness is load-bearing." Bramwen adjusted the Codex on his knees. "But notice the structure. *The throne that claims the warden's hand.* Not receives. Not inherits. *Claims.* The text distinguishes claiming from receiving with great care—it does so eleven times across the full corpus, and in nine of those eleven instances, the claimant comes to a bad end. I've tabulated it."

"You've *tabulated* prophecy."

"Somebody had to. The Archive certainly wasn't going to." He looked up, letting the blur resolve into the shape of the usurper's face. "You want to know what she is. She is the last heir of the line that made your crown possible, Your Majesty, and also the only creature alive whose word could make it impossible again. That's what the covenant is. Not magic. *Legitimacy*, bound in vellum. Every coronation since the founding was sworn over the Coldharbour seal. Yours wasn't. Do you wonder why the free cities don't kneel? Why the fae realm withers at your borders? You are a king with a stolen keystone and no covenant to stand it in, and the Codex says—shall I continue?—that the throne which *claims* rather than *receives*—"

The backhand came fast, but Bramwen had been counting, and he turned into it enough to keep his teeth intact. Mostly.

* * *

They left him chained after that, and the questions stopped being questions and started being statements delivered with implements. He catalogued those too, distantly, the way he catalogued everything: the iron rod (standard issue, Finder pattern), the intervals (shortening), the voice of the grey-wool man reciting the same four demands on a loop until the words lost meaning, which was, Bramwen suspected, the actual technique. Wear the truth down by repetition until the prisoner will say anything to change the words.

He held the archive in his head instead. Shelf by shelf. Item by item. *Item 12, the Ashenport grain ledgers. Item 47, the drowned chapel survey. Item 114—* his fingers ached to touch the real thing, but they'd taken the Codex back, and so he touched the memory of it, and the memory was enough. Memory was the one library no witch-finder could burn.

He thought about Elodie, and hoped she was far away, and hoped she was being careful, and understood—with the particular clarity of a man chained in the dark—that hoping was not a strategy. Time was the strategy. Every hour he kept Aldric's attention on this cell was an hour she wasn't hunted. Every error he planted in the usurper's ear would bloom later in the wrong place. So when they returned, he gave them truths bent just enough to be wrong: a direction north-northwest instead of northeast. A rite requiring a lake (true) and a fortnight of preparation (false). A name for the second keystone's resting place that belonged to a chapel demolished sixty years ago.

Let Aldric send soldiers to dig in ruins. Let the map of his fear grow longer and more wrong with every passing day. Pedantry, Bramwen had always believed, was a form of prayer. Tonight it was also a weapon.

Near what he judged to be dawn—he measured it by the guard rotation, forty minutes, forty minutes, forty minutes, the world's most reliable clock—the cell door opened one more time, and the footsteps that entered were wrong. Too soft. Too many.

"Scholar," said the grey-wool finder, and there was something new in his voice. Not triumph. Unease. "His Majesty has a message for you."

"Written or verbal? Verbal messages degrade in transmission. I'd want the original."

"He said to tell you this." A pause, and Bramwen heard the man reading, haltingly, from a paper he plainly didn't understand. "*Tell the archivist: the seal is broken, the bell is raised, and the briar has drunk. Tell him his tabulations are noted.*"

Bramwen went very still.

Three keystones. Three facts. The drowned bell—Elodie had the first. The briar—the second was waking. And *the seal is broken* meant the standing stone, the Hollow gate, open and passable. She was moving faster than any projection he'd built. Faster than the Codex's own margins suggested.

And Aldric knew it. Which meant Aldric had sources inside the Hollow's edge—or inside her company.

"His Majesty adds," the finder said, "a question. He wishes to know: if the warden gathers all three keystones, what happens to a king who never swore the covenant?"

Bramwen smiled in the dark, and it hurt his split lip, and he let it hurt.

"Tell His Majesty," he said, "that the Codex answers that question in full. Chapter and verse. I'll need my spectacles."

The hook, he thought, as boots shifted uneasily around him, was set. Now he only had to teach the usurper to read—and survive the lesson long enough to see what Elodie did with the time he'd bought her, hour by stubborn hour, drip by counted drip.

Because forty minutes was sloppy rotation discipline. And somewhere out there, people who loved badly and fought well were already coming for him.
