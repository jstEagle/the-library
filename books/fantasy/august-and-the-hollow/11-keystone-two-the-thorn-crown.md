## 11. Keystone Two: The Thorn Crown

The Briarwood did not want them.

Elodie had known that from the moment they crossed the treeline, when the first bramble hooked her sleeve and held on like a beggar's hand until Ivenn cut it free with a knife of grey glass. Now, three days in, the wood had stopped pretending to be merely hostile and become something closer to deliberate. Paths closed behind them. Thorns thickened across the game trails they followed, braiding themselves into walls overnight. Twice they woke to find the forest had rearranged itself around their camp, as if the trees were conferring about what to do with them.

"It knows why we're here," Bramwen said on the third morning, pushing his spectacles up his nose. He was thinner than he'd been at Ravenmoor, the gaol still written on him in hollow cheeks and a limp he refused to discuss. "The Briarwood is covenant-ground. Old ground. It remembers what a Coldharbour looks like."

"Then it should be rolling out a carpet," Elodie said.

"It remembers what a Coldharbour *did*."

She didn't ask. She was learning not to ask, learning instead to let the answers arrive the way weather arrived—unbidden, usually unwelcome, and always eventually true. Her grandmother's lullaby had given her the drowned bell beneath Lake Verre. The bell had given her the keystone, cold and heavy in its oilcloth wrapping, humming against her hip like a sleeping animal. And the codex pages Bramwen had memorized before the raid gave her this: *the second stone sleeps in the crown of thorns, and wakes only for blood freely given.*

Blood freely given. The phrase had been sitting in her chest for three days like a swallowed stone of its own.

They made camp early that evening in a clearing where the briars grew in a perfect ring, twenty paces across, dense as woven iron. At the center stood a tree unlike any other in the wood—a blackthorn grown vast and twisted, its branches crowned with thorns the length of knives, and among those thorns, catching the last red light, something that glinted.

"The crown," Bramwen whispered.

It was a circlet of living thorn, grown into the heartwood of the great blackthorn, and set into it, dark as a dried drop of blood, was the second keystone.

* * *

Ivenn went first.

Of course he did. He had been going first since Lake Verre, since the chapel where she'd spoken her grandmother's name and the water had parted like a curtain being drawn by a considerate servant. He went first through the briar tunnels, first across the flooded stream, first into every room of every ruin, and he did it without asking, which Elodie had decided was either chivalry or a way of avoiding conversation, and she suspected both.

Now he stepped toward the ring of thorns and the thorns reached for him.

Not metaphorically. She watched it happen—the outer brambles lifting from the ground like serpents scenting warmth, turning toward him, straining. He stopped an arm's length from the ring and stood very still, and Elodie saw the thorns tremble with something that looked horribly like hunger.

"Ivenn." Bramwen's voice cracked. "Get back."

"They won't take me," Ivenn said. His voice was flat, but Elodie had spent enough weeks beside him now to hear the effort underneath it, the way a taster hears the bitterness under honey. "That's rather the point."

"What do you mean, that's rather the—" Bramwen stopped. Elodie watched him stop, watched the scholar's mind assemble the sentence he didn't want to say. "Fae blood isn't freely given here. You're exiled. The covenant marked you when it cast you out. Your blood belongs to no one—not to this wood, not to the Hollow, not even to yourself. It can't wake anything. It can barely keep *you* alive."

"I know what my blood can do," Ivenn said quietly.

"Then why—"

"Because if there's another way, I wanted to find it before she bled for it." He turned then, and looked at Elodie, and for once he didn't armor his face. What was underneath was worse than any expression she'd seen him wear: it was naked, and young, and ashamed. "You've bled enough for other people's wars. Your grandmother bled. Her mother, and hers, all the way back a hundred years and further. A whole line of Coldharbours, opened vein by vein to feed a bargain none of them wrote." His jaw tightened. "I defended mortals during the covenant wars because I couldn't stand watching it happen to them. And then I let myself believe exile was the price of principle, when really I just ran. So no. Not this one. Not you. Let me try."

"You already know it won't work," Elodie said.

"I need to know it won't work," he corrected. "There's a difference. One is a conclusion. The other is a choice."

He stepped into the ring.

The thorns took him the way winter takes a garden—not violently, but totally. They wrapped his arms, his throat, threaded through his hair, and where they touched, they drank. Elodie saw it plainly: a slow greying moving through the brambles, the green leaching out of them as they pulled at whatever ran in fae veins, and Ivenn standing in the middle of it with his eyes open and his hands loose at his sides, offering himself up to a forest that could not accept the gift.

He lasted less than a minute. Then the thorns recoiled, all at once, flinging him backward out of the ring like a mouth spitting out spoiled food. He landed hard in the dirt and lay there, grey-faced, his sleeves shredded, thin lines of silvered blood welling along his forearms—blood that caught the light wrong, that shimmered faintly, that was already slowing.

Bramwen was at his side before Elodie moved, pressing cloth to the wounds, muttering. Elodie stayed where she was. Because Ivenn was looking at her over Bramwen's shoulder, and there was something in his face she needed a moment to understand.

It took her the length of three heartbeats.

He wasn't ashamed that he'd failed. He was afraid—afraid that having shown her his own willingness, he'd made her sacrifice heavier. As if courage were a debt and he'd just raised the price.

"Ivenn," she said.

"Don't," he said.

"Ivenn, listen to me." She crouched so their eyes were level. "What you just did—that was the bravest useless thing I've ever seen, and I once watched a man challenge a bear to a duel over a cheese wheel. But you can't take this from me. Not because it's mine by right. Because it isn't mine by right. That's the whole point."

"That makes no sense."

"It makes perfect sense." She sat back on her heels. "My whole life, everything I've done has been assigned to me. Tasting, because Sef chose me off a cart of orphans. Charm-selling, because hunger chose me. Even this—" she touched the wrapped keystone at her hip— "this chose me before I was born. My name was in a book a century before I learned to write it. Every duty I've ever carried, someone else handed me." She looked at the blackthorn, at the dark glint of the keystone in its crown. "This is the first thing I get to pick up on purpose. Don't you dare make it smaller by making it a debt."

Ivenn stared at her. Then, slowly, he laughed—a short, rough sound, surprised out of him, the first laugh she'd ever heard him give away for free.

"A cheese wheel," he said.

"It was a very good cheese."

* * *

They waited for full dark, because Bramwen insisted the rite required it—*the stone wakes between one day and the next, in the hinge-hour*, the codex said, and Bramwen recited it twice more while Elodie tried not to think about hinges, about doors, about how many times in her life she'd stood in doorways belonging to other people's houses, welcome nowhere, useful everywhere.

At moonrise she walked to the ring alone.

The thorns turned toward her the way they'd turned toward Ivenn, but differently—slower, almost reverent, the way courtiers turn when the old king enters a room they thought empty. She felt them reading her. Felt them find the name written in her blood, the one she'd carried unknowing for fifteen years while she tasted poison for a usurper and sold luck-charms to laundresses who deserved better luck than charms could give.

"Elodie Coldharbour," she said aloud, to the wood, to the stone, to the dead women whose duty she was about to make her own choice. "Last of a line I never asked for. Warden of a covenant I intend to break, eventually, if I have anything to say about it—which, apparently, I do."

The thorns shivered.

"But tonight," she said, "tonight I'm yours."

She unbound her sleeve. Rolled it past her elbow. And she understood, finally, what *freely given* meant—not the absence of fear, because her hands were shaking badly enough that she had to press them flat against her thighs to still them. It meant walking forward anyway, with no one ordering her, no book commanding her, no dying mother's voice in her ears. Just her. Choosing.

She stepped into the ring.

The first thorn touched her wrist like a question. She answered it by not pulling away. The second found the crook of her arm; the third her palm. They were careful, she realized—they were *careful*—and that nearly undid her more than cruelty would have. All her life, hands on her had been either testing or taking. These held her like something being returned.

When the thorn pierced her forearm, the pain was bright and clean, and with it came knowing.

She saw the Briarwood as it had been: a border garden, planted by the first wardens, tended by generations of Coldharbour blood spilled season after season into its roots. She saw her grandmother here—young, laughing, her sleeves rolled exactly as Elodie's were—and saw the year the laughter stopped, the year the covenant began to sour, the year the blood asked for grew thicker and the reasons given grew thinner. She saw that the wood had been waiting a hundred years not for a Coldharbour, but for a Coldharbour who came *willingly*. Every heir before her had come because the book said to. The thorns had drunk obedience all that time, and obedience, she understood now, was a thin vintage. It kept the stone asleep.

Love woke it.

Not love of duty. Love of the two battered figures at the edge of the clearing—one bleeding silver, one clutching a memorized codex like a shield—who had walked into a dying fae realm and a burning mortal one and a gaol and a lake and a briar maze, all for a woman they'd known a matter of weeks. Love of a landlady who burned her own shop to buy Elodie a head start. Love of a taster-master who died holding a gate. Love of laundresses and smugglers and a free city that had called her *the Coldharbour girl* as though it were a good thing to be.

Her blood ran down the thorn-crown and into the heartwood, and the blackthorn bloomed.

In the dead of night, in the teeth of autumn, the great tree flowered—white blossoms opening along every black branch, thousands of them, filling the clearing with a scent like honey and rain. And in the crown, the second keystone kindled from within, dark red flushing to a deep, living rose, and it loosened from the thorns that had held it and dropped into Elodie's bleeding hand as gently as fruit.

The ring of briars bowed. All around the clearing, the Briarwood sighed—a long exhale through ten thousand leaves—and the paths that had closed behind them for three days opened wide, all at once, in every direction, as if the forest had finally made up its mind.

Elodie stood in the falling petals with two keystones against her heart and her own blood drying on her arm, and found that she was crying, which annoyed her enormously.

* * *

She bandaged her forearm by firelight while Bramwen hovered and Ivenn pretended not to hover, which was somehow worse.

"The third stone," Bramwen said, for the fourth time, as though repetition might change the words. "Aldric wears it. On his hand. The signet he seized from your family's bodies." He rubbed his eyes behind the smudged spectacles. "Elodie, reclaiming it means Vellamarch. The capital. The palace. The witch-finders' own den."

"I heard you the first three times."

"And you said nothing."

"There was nothing to say yet." She tied off the bandage. Looked into the fire, where the petals that had drifted onto the flames burned with brief silver light. "But there's something now."

Ivenn looked up sharply. He'd learned to read her silences faster than Bramwen had; exile teaches attention. "What?"

"Back in the Hollow. The Queen of Rust." Elodie kept her eyes on the fire. "Before we left her court, she told me her price. For the army. For everything." The firelight moved on her face. "And it isn't what any of us thinks it is. It changes what the third keystone is *for*."

Bramwen went very still. "You've known this since Ashenport? Since Ravenmoor? Since—" He stopped, recalculating, and she watched the hurt arrive in him like weather. "You've carried it alone all this way."

"Yes," Elodie said. "Because it's mine to carry. And because once I say it out loud, none of us gets to unhear it."

The fire cracked. Somewhere in the reopened dark, the Briarwood went on breathing, patient as an old friend waiting for bad news.

"So say it," Ivenn said quietly.

Elodie took a breath, and gave them the truth.
