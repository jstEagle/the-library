## 12. The Hollow's Withering

The gate took them sideways.

That was the only way Elodie could describe it afterward—crossing through the standing stone was not a step forward but a step *askew*, as though the world had a seam and they'd slipped along its thread. One moment the Briarwood's damp green dark; the next, a sky the color of old pewter, and light that came from everywhere and nowhere at once, sourceless and thin.

She staggered, caught herself on Bramwen's arm, and looked up.

The Hollow stretched before them in every direction: meadows of silver grass that chimed faintly when the wind moved them, trees with bark like hammered copper, rivers that ran uphill without apology. It should have been beautiful. It was beautiful, in the way a fresco is beautiful—and it was dying the same way, flaking from the plaster.

The silver grass grew in patches, bald earth showing between like scalp through thinning hair. The copper trees stood leafless in whole groves, their branches frozen mid-gesture, as if they'd reached for something and forgotten why. And the light—that generous, sourceless light—it flickered. Not like a candle in wind. Like a lamp running out of oil.

"Gods," Bramwen whispered. He had his codex out—the stolen copy Sef had smuggled to them in Ravenmoor, wrapped in oilcloth against the gaol's damp—and he was turning pages with hands that shook. "The marginalia. The old scholars wrote about this. 'The Hollow dims where the covenant thins.' I thought it was metaphor. I thought—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I thought everything in that book was metaphor."

"It isn't," Ivenn said.

He had gone very still since they crossed. Elodie had learned to read him over the weeks of travel—the set of his shoulders, the angle of his jaw, the way his too-beautiful face arranged itself into expressions mortals found legible only because he'd practiced them for a century. Right now every practiced expression was gone, and what remained underneath was rawer and younger than she'd expected. He looked like a man coming home to find the house burned down and the family still inside.

"This is the Court of Bells," he said quietly. "There were ten thousand of us here. Festivals lasted a season. The bells rang so long you forgot silence existed." He gestured at the nearest hilltop, where a tower stood—or the memory of one, its upper half dissolved into drifting grey motes that never quite fell and never quite rose. "You can see the bell tower."

"There's no tower," Bramwen said.

"There is," Ivenn said. "It's just not finished being here anymore."

Elodie felt it then, for the first time—not with her eyes but with her tongue. Fifteen years of tasting had taught her that the mouth knew things before the mind did; bitterness announced poison before any color or smell confessed it. Now her tongue told her the air itself was bitter. Faint, mineral, wrong—the taste of a well going dry. The Hollow wasn't just dimming. It was *starving*.

"The covenant," she said. "When my family bound the realms together, the Hollow fed on... what? What did it get?"

"Passage," Ivenn said. "Belief. The slow traffic of mortal wonder crossing back and forth." His mouth twisted. "Your kings called it tribute. My queens called it trade. Either way, when Aldric's witch-finders started drowning charm-sellers and your grandmother's line went quiet, the road closed. A hundred years of famine, and we're the last course."

They walked. There was nothing else to do. The path toward Ivenn's kin wound through the failing country, and with every mile the evidence accumulated like debt: villages of white stone with doors standing open, tables set for meals no one would eat, cradles holding shapes of mist that might have been infants once and were now only weather. Ivenn did not look at the cradles. Elodie made herself look, because someone had to, because looking felt like the only mourning available.

Bramwen walked with his nose in the codex, muttering. "The Third Concordat mentions 'the Rust Queen's levy.' The Fifth says she 'holds the forges of the deep court.' She's real, then? Not allegorical?"

"She's real," Ivenn said. "And she will be delighted you brought a book."

"Why?"

"Because she'll want to know exactly how much you mortals know," Ivenn said, "before she decides how much of it to let you keep."

* * *

They found the living clustered around what remained of life.

A valley where the grass still held—thinly, stubbornly—and a river that still ran in the correct direction, though its water ran clear as glass and tasted, when Elodie cupped a handful, of nothing at all. Water without taste. It frightened her more than the mist-shapes had. Around the valley's rim stood dwellings grown rather than built, pale wood spiraling out of the hillside like antlers, and fae moved among them: tall, luminous, and translucent. She could see the hills through their shoulders. When they spoke to one another their voices carried the chime of the silver grass, but thinner, as though each word cost something.

They saw the three travelers coming and the valley went silent.

"They know what I am," Ivenn murmured. "Stay behind me. Let me—"

An older fae stepped onto the path. He wore armor of woven thorn, blackened and rusted at the joints, and his face had the terrible composure of a statue left out in rain for decades—features still fine, edges softening. Behind him gathered perhaps thirty others, and Elodie understood with a lurch that this was not thirty of thousands. This was what remained. This was the army.

"Ivenn." The old fae's voice was flat as slate. "The exile returns. Tell me the mortal realm has finally rotted through, and you've come home to watch."

"Father." Ivenn's voice cracked on the word, just slightly, and Elodie looked away to give him privacy he would never ask for. "The covenant can be reforged. The keystones exist. Two are already woken. We need the third, and we need the Hollow's strength behind us when we take it."

"You need." The old fae's gaze slid past his son to Elodie, and the weight of it pressed on her like a hand. "And this is the Coldharbour whelp. The last warden. Come to bind us again."

"To *save*—"

"We are not drowning, girl. We are *fading*. Do you understand the difference?" He stepped closer, and up close she could see stars through his chest, actual stars, the night sky showing where his heart should have been solid. "Drowning ends. Fading goes on. Every year we are less, and every year we remember being more, and there is no bottom to it. Your family's bargain did that. Your kings' edicts did that. And now you walk into our last valley asking us to spend our final strength fighting *mortal* wars so the bargain can be tied around our throats again, tighter, neater, with better knots."

"No one's asking you to fight," Elodie said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "I'm asking—"

"You're asking," he said, "because asking is what your line does instead of commanding, when commanding fails. I knew a Coldharbour once. She also asked. She also smiled. She also took." He turned away. "Go back through your stone, little warden. Fade with the rest of us or don't. But do it elsewhere."

The valley's people turned away with him, thirty faces like frosted glass, and the path ahead stood empty.

Ivenn hadn't moved. Elodie watched him stand in the space between his father's dismissal and his own pride, and she saw the exact moment the pride lost. His shoulders dropped half an inch. A hundred years of exile, and the door had been open the whole time—open, and rotten through, and leading nowhere worth entering.

"I'm sorry," she said.

"Don't." He laughed, and there was nothing of laughter in it. "Don't be kind to me right now. I haven't the reserves."

Bramwen cleared his throat. "There's another option," he said carefully. "The Concordats mention a second power in the deep Hollow. The Queen of Rust. If the court of bells won't—"

"The Queen of Rust doesn't rule anything," Ivenn said. "She rules what's *left*. Which is increasingly the same thing." He looked at Elodie, and his eyes had the flat shine of a man about to recommend a thing he hated. "If anyone in this dying country will hear you out, it's her. Because she hates the old courts almost as much as she hates your kings. But understand what you're walking into. She deals in truths. Not riddles—truths. You pay her with things you'd rather never say aloud. And she always knows which ones those are."

* * *

The forge-country began where the meadows ended, and it announced itself by smell: hot iron and cold ash, a smell Elodie knew from Vellamarch's smithies, transposed into a key that made her teeth ache. The land sloped down into a vast basin where the sky's grey light failed entirely, and the darkness below was lit instead by forges—hundreds of them, thousands, scattered across the basin floor like fallen constellations, each one tended by a figure whose silhouette bent and straightened, bent and straightened, hammer rising and falling in rhythms that did not quite synchronize, so that the sound was less like industry than like a heartbeat with arrhythmia. Making something. Refusing to stop making it.

At the basin's center stood a fortress that had not been built so much as *accumulated*—layer on layer of slag and salvaged stone and broken bell-metal, towers welded from wreckage, walls studded with the rusted crowns of dead kings. Faerie kings, maybe. Mortal ones. Elodie decided she didn't want to know.

The gates opened before they knocked.

Inside, the noise fell away, and the Queen of Rust sat waiting in a hall whose ceiling was a net of chains, each link hung with a small bell that made no sound. She was smaller than Elodie expected—small as a human woman, seated on a throne of fused horseshoes, and her skin was the grey-brown of weathered iron with veins of true orange rust climbing her throat like ivy. Her eyes were the only bright thing about her: two points of forge-light, patient and appraising.

"So," the Queen said. Her voice was ordinary. That was somehow worse than thunder. "The warden's last daughter, the exile who chose meat over starlight, and a scholar carrying a book that belongs to my library." Her gaze fixed on Bramwen, who clutched the codex to his chest with both arms. "Relax, pedant. I collect debts, not books. Though I note the debt exists."

"We've come to ask for help," Elodie said.

"Yes. Everyone comes to ask for help. It's the one export your realm still produces in quantity." The Queen leaned forward, and the rust-veins at her throat brightened faintly, like coals breathed upon. "The court of bells refused you. I felt their refusal from here; refusal travels well in thin air. So now you've come down to the rust, to the queen the pretty courts whisper about, hoping desperation looks different by forge-light." She smiled, and her teeth were iron filings. "It doesn't. But sit. Desperation is at least honest company."

They sat. Servants—fae with hammers for hands, gentle despite them—brought water that tasted of iron and bread that tasted of smoke, and Elodie ate because her body insisted even while her mind spun. The Queen watched her eat with an expression Elodie couldn't parse until she realized what it was: recognition. The look of one taster watching another sample a dish.

"You taste before you trust," the Queen said. "Good. We'll get along."

"What do you want?" Elodie asked. "For your help. For your—forges, your people, whatever you can give. Name it."

"Truths," the Queen said simply. "One per favor, priced according to weight. That is the law of my hall, and even I obey it. So let us begin cheaply." She settled back. "Tell me why you want the covenant restored."

"That's not a truth, that's a—"

"It's the most expensive question I own," the Queen said, "and tonight I'm selling it at a loss, because I am curious, and curiosity is my only vice. Answer."

Elodie opened her mouth to say the obvious thing—to say *to save both realms, to stop Aldric, to end the war before the free cities burn*—and stopped. Because the Queen's forge-bright eyes were on her, and because fifteen years of tasting had taught her that a lie detected late costs triple, and because some instinct older than training told her this room ate prepared answers the way rust ate iron: slowly, thoroughly, all the way through.

So she said the true thing. The thing she'd been not-saying since the standing stones, since the thorn crown, since the drowned bell rose dripping from Lake Verre at the sound of her grandmother's name.

"I don't want it," Elodie said. "The power. Any of it. I never wanted to be the last of anything. I wanted a shop, and regular customers, and friends who stayed past closing. I want *people*—Bramwen, and Ivenn, and Sef's daughter who I've never met, and the laundress I sold luck-charms to, and strangers in Ashenport who fed me before they knew my name. That's all. The covenant, the keystones, the crown everyone keeps trying to hand me—I'd give every scrap of it away tomorrow if it bought one more year for the people I love. I don't want to be powerful. I want to not be alone. That's the truth, and I'd rather have kept it."

Silence. The chained bells overhead stirred in a wind that didn't exist, and made, at last, the smallest sound—a single thread of chime, high and clear, like ice cracking on a spring pond.

The Queen of Rust looked at her for a long moment. Then she laughed—a short, rusty, genuine laugh, the first sound in the whole dying country that had seemed alive.

"Oh, you *stupid, splendid* creature," the Queen said. "Do you know what every warden of your line said when they sat where you're sitting? Every one, across three centuries? They said, 'I accept the burden.' They said, 'the power is heavy but I will carry it.' They performed sacrifice like a coronation." She rose from her throne of horseshoes, and she was taller standing than sitting, taller than she should have been, rust spreading up her neck and across her cheeks like a blush of iron. "And you sit there and tell me the truth no ruler is permitted to feel: that you'd trade the whole cursed inheritance for a table of friends. Do you know what that makes you?"

"Tactless," Elodie said.

"*Safe*," the Queen said. "The first safe pair of hands this covenant has ever touched. Power that wants itself grows. Power that wants to be rid of itself can finally be *spent*." She descended the dais steps, and the forge-light bent toward her. "Very well, Elodie Coldharbour. Here is my price, and it is not a truth—it is a term, and you will hate it, and you will agree anyway. My armies march with yours against the usurper. My forges arm the free cities. And when the third keystone is in your hand and the moment comes to reforge the covenant—" her forge-bright eyes held Elodie's, and Elodie felt the floor tilt beneath a suspicion she couldn't yet name— "*you will bring it to me first. Before you speak any binding. Before your scholar translates one syllable of the old words. You will come down into the rust and show me what you mean to build, and I will decide whether it gets built.*"

"That's not a price," Bramwen protested, "that's a veto—"

"It's insurance," the Queen said, without looking at him. "The last time a Coldharbour reforged the world, my people got the short end of the bargain and a century of famine to remember it by. Never again. Swear it, warden. On your grandmother's name—the one that raises bells from drowned chapels. Swear, and my host marches at dawn."

Every instinct Elodie owned whispered that this was a trap with very polite hinges. Every instinct also whispered that Ashenport had perhaps six days before Aldric's legions arrived, and that six days did not permit shopping for better offers.

She thought of the mist-shapes in the cradles. She thought of the laundress's hands. She put out her hand, and the Queen of Rust shook it, and the rust-veins were warm as a hearthstone.

"On my grandmother's name," Elodie said. "You'll see what I build before I build it."

"Then we have a war," the Queen said briskly, already turning to shout orders in a language like hammer-strikes, and the great gates swung wide, and somewhere above them the arrhythmic forges began, one by one, to beat in time.

It was Ivenn who caught her elbow as they walked out, his voice low and strange. "Elodie. What she said—about seeing what you build first. About deciding whether it gets built." His face, in the forge-light, had gone carefully blank. "She didn't ask what you meant to build. She said *show me what you mean to build*. As if she already knows there's more than one thing it could be." He paused. "As if she already knows what *you're* planning to do with the third keystone. The thing you haven't told us."

Elodie kept walking. She kept her face steady, her stride even, her breathing calm—fifteen years of practice, swallowing poison with a smile.

But her mouth had gone dry as ash, and the taste of the lie she was about to need was already on her tongue.
