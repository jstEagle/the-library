## 13. Armies on the March

The pigeons came at dusk, always at dusk, because Sef had learned in thirty years of palace service that dusk was when a man could stand on a balcony and look idle without anyone asking why.

He held the bird against his chest, feeling its heart hammer like a small trapped drum, and worked the message tube free with fingers that had stopped shaking only that morning. The shaking had lasted two days after Ravenmoor—after the letter he'd written that had put witch-finders on Bramwen Hale's trail—and Sef had decided, somewhere in the long gray hours between one tremor and the next, that if his hands were going to betray him again, they would have to do it while doing something useful.

*Legions march in three columns. Verre road, Thorn road, coast road. Fifteen thousand. Siege train follows the coast. Ashenport is the first target. They mean to make an example.*

He read it twice, memorized it, and fed it to the brazier's coals. Then he took up the second slip of paper—the coded one, the one that looked like an order for kitchen stores—and tied it into the tube.

*Send double salt. Send double salt to every house you can reach. Burn nothing.*

Salt meant danger. Double salt meant armies. It was a code he had built himself, out of grocery lists, out of the most boring language in Vellamarch, because no witch-finder alive would torture a man over a requisition form. He had spent his whole career being boring. It was, he was beginning to understand, the only armor a small man ever got.

"Master Sef."

He didn't jump. Thirty years had burned the jumping out of him. He turned, pigeon still tucked under his arm like a loaf of bread, and found Captain Vess of the witch-finders standing in the balcony doorway with her hands folded and her smile arranged just so.

"Captain." He bowed the precise degree owed to her rank. "Cold evening."

"It is." She came to the rail beside him and looked out over the city, where the lamps were coming on one by one, each one a little lie of safety. "You keep birds?"

"For the kitchens. Messages about deliveries. Fish, mostly." He let a note of apology creep in. "You know how Lord Ammel is about his eels."

"I do." She studied him with the flat patience of a woman who had watched a hundred men lie about fish. "You knew Elodie Coldharbour, I'm told. Before."

"She was a taster. A good one. Better palate than mine, if I'm honest." That part was true, and true things made the best mortar for false walls. "I can't say I knew her well. Tasters don't socialize. We're too busy not dying."

"Mm." Vess picked a bit of lint from her sleeve. "Her Majesty's regent—that is to say, His Majesty—wants the traitor's associates identified. Everyone she spoke to. Everyone who fed her. Everyone who—" the smile widened by a hair's breadth "—taught her."

Sef felt the cold come up through the floor stones and into his boots. *Taught her.* Someone had talked. Someone in the taster's hall had given his name, or was about to.

"Well," he said mildly, "she learned her letters from a chaplain who died eight years ago, and her trade from me, and her manners from nobody at all. If any of that's treason, Captain, arrest the alphabet."

For a moment—one long, terrible moment—he thought she would laugh, or strike him, or both. Instead she inclined her head. "The alphabet is safe. For now." She turned toward the door, then paused. "We march soon, Master Sef. The whole household knows it. Cooks, grooms, tasters. There will be those who think about running. About warning their friends in the free cities." Her eyes came back to his, mild as milk. "I'd hate to see a useful man drown over a pigeon."

She left. Sef stood at the rail until his knees remembered how to hold him, then carried the bird inside and fed it grain from his own hand.

*Double salt,* he thought. *God forgive me. Double salt.*

* * *

The war council was held in the map room, which Sef attended because taster-masters attended everything food touched, and food touched everything. He stood by the wall with the other servants-invisible: the wine steward, the chamberlain's clerk, a boy whose whole job was refilling the inkwell. Fifteen years of standing in corners had taught Sef how to be furniture with ears.

King Aldric did not sit. He never sat in the map room; he prowled it like something pacing a cage it had built itself.

"Ashenport falls first," he said, tapping the coast with one gloved finger. "It is the largest, the loudest, and the closest to the water they love so much. When it burns, the others will remember what obedience costs versus what rebellion costs." He looked up. "Casualties?"

"Minimal, if the quarter surrenders," said General Corvane, a gray man with a gray voice. "Their walls are old. Their militia is fishermen and smugglers. Two days of bombardment and they'll open the gates."

"And if they don't?"

Corvane's pause was very small. "Then we make the example thorough, Majesty."

Aldric nodded as though this were arithmetic. Sef kept his eyes on the inkwell and poured the boy's thoughts a calming draught of nothing at all. *Two days of bombardment.* He had seen what royal bombardment did to a dockside quarter. He had tasted the ash in the water supply after Vellamarch's own riots, twenty years back, and never forgotten the flavor: char, lime, and copper. Somewhere in Ashenport right now there were children who would taste that same water.

"The Coldharbour woman?" Aldric asked.

"Rumored in Ashenport. Unconfirmed. The fae exile with her—unconfirmed. Hale escaped Ravenmoor four nights ago." Corvane's jaw tightened around the words like a man chewing gravel. "There was... negligence at the gaol. The gaoler's guards were compromised. Poisoned wine."

Something in Sef's chest went very quiet and very bright. *Poisoned wine.* He didn't know how she'd done it—didn't need to. She had used the trade. The despised trade, the trade that got you spat on in markets and thanked with a nod if you were lucky. She had walked into a gaol and turned its own teeth against it.

Aldric smiled, and it was the worst thing Sef had seen all day, worse than the maps and the numbers. "Good," the king said softly. "Let her gather her little flock. Sheep herd easier than wolves run." He swept from the room, and the council dissolved behind him like sugar in hot water.

Sef refilled the inkwell himself, slowly, and read the map upside down from where he stood. Three roads. Fifteen thousand men. Eleven days to Ashenport at forced march, twelve with the siege train.

Eleven days. He had sent the pigeon yesterday.

* * *

The daughter's name was Maren, and she was seven, and she had her mother's stubborn chin and her father's habit of counting things when she was nervous. Sef counted with her sometimes—stairs, coins, the tiles in the kitchen floor—because counting was a kind of holding-on.

Witch-finders held her mother's debt papers. That was the whole architecture of his betrayal, laid bare: not torture, not threats of fire. Debt. Sixty crowns owed to a lender who answered to Captain Vess, and a sick wife, and a girl who needed shoes. Sef had written one letter, given one name, drawn one small circle on one small map, and told himself it was the smallest betrayal ever committed. Just the scholar. Just the book. Not the girl. Never the girl.

And then Ravenmoor had fallen anyway, and Bramwen Hale had been dragged out in chains, and Sef had understood that there was no such thing as a small betrayal. Betrayal was a poison, and he of all people should have known: dose meant nothing. A drop in a king's cup killed the king. A drop in a servant's cup killed the servant. You could not poison someone gently.

So now he stood in the taster's hall at midnight, alone among the rows of covered dishes, and did the thing he had been circling for three days like a moth around a lamp.

He wrote to Elodie Coldharbour.

Not a pigeon this time—pigeons could be taken, codes broken, and Vess had already looked at him once too long. No. This message would travel by the oldest courier in Vellamarch: a fat, slow, honest man named Doss who drove the palace's fish cart to the coast market every week and had never once been searched, because Doss was exactly what he appeared to be, which was dull. Sef had paid him a year's wages to carry a sealed tin of preserved eels to a certain chandler's shop in Ashenport and ask for the owner by name.

Inside the tin, wrapped in waxed linen, a single sheet:

*To the girl with the good palate, from the man who taught her.*

*They come in eleven days. Three roads; the siege train takes the coast. Fifteen thousand, plus finders, plus iron. The king means Ashenport to be a lesson, not a battle. Do not defend the walls—you cannot. Take your people and go inland, go north, go anywhere the roads don't lead.*

*I know what I did. I know what it cost. I am not asking forgiveness. I am asking you to live, and to hate me somewhere far from the bombardment line.*

*One more thing, and then I will stop wasting your time. The king wears a ring he never removes. Black stone, silver setting. He calls it his signet. The scholars call it something else—I heard Corvane say the word keystone, and I saw the king's face when he said it, and I have served kings for thirty years and I know fear when it sits down to supper with them.*

*He is afraid of you. Remember that. Fearful men are predictable, and predictable men lose.*

*S.*

He sealed it, hid it in the eel tin, and gave it to Doss before dawn, along with the money and a lie about a gift for a cousin. Doss took it without curiosity, which was the finest quality a man could possess.

Then Sef went back through the sleeping palace to the nursery wing, where Maren slept, and sat in the corridor outside her door until his legs went numb, keeping watch over a child whose father had sold his soul for sixty crowns and was now trying to buy it back one pigeon, one eel tin, one coward's kindness at a time.

It wasn't enough. He knew it wasn't enough. Eleven days, fifteen thousand men, and all he could send was paper.

But paper had started wars before. And paper, tonight, might end one.

* * *

In Ashenport, the chandler's wife opened the eel tin, read the linen-wrapped page twice, and ran barefoot through the rain to the harbor house where the smugglers kept their council, hammering on the door until it opened.

By morning, the whole quarter knew. By noon, Elodie Coldharbour stood on the seawall reading Sef's letter a third time, wind pulling at her hair, while behind her the militia drilled with fishing spears and reloaded cannon older than the covenant.

"He's afraid of me," she said quietly.

Bramwen, convalescing in a chair dragged up onto the wall, huffed something that was half laugh, half wince. "He's afraid of what you'll refuse to become. There's a difference, and it matters."

Elodie folded the letter along its worn creases and looked east, where the coast road ran straight and empty toward Vellamarch, and where, in eleven days, fire would come walking.

"Eleven days," she said. "Then we give him something new to fear."

Far to the south, in a dark corridor outside a sleeping child's door, a small man sat very still in the dark, and did not move, and did not run, and was—for the first time in thirty years—not small at all.
