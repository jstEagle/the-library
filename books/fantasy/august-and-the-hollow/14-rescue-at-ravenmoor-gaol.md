## 14. Rescue at Ravenmoor Gaol

The smugglers called it a wine run, which told Elodie everything about how they planned to die.

"Ravenmoor sits on a rock above the moor road," said Captain Vess, drawing lines in spilled flour on the galley table of her ship, the *Salt Lark*. She was sixty if she was a day, built like a barrel with opinions, and she had agreed to this madness in exchange for a favor Elodie had done her grain charms three weeks past. "One road in. One road out. Gaol's cut into the cliff itself—old king's work, back when kings built things to last. Two hundred steps down to the cells, and every one of them watched."

"And Bramwen is at the bottom," Ivenn said.

"Bramwen is *alive* at the bottom," Vess corrected. "Which is more than he'll be by week's end. Witch-finders don't keep scholars long. They ask their questions, they get their answers or they don't, and then they drown what's left." She looked up at Elodie, and her eyes were the flat grey of the Ashenport sea. "You understand there's no clever way into Ravenmoor. There's only the front door and a great deal of noise."

"Then we bring noise," Elodie said.

Ivenn made a sound that might have been a laugh in some older, crueler language. "You've been spending too much time around soldiers."

"I've been spending time around you."

* * *

They went over the moor in the last grey hour before dawn, nineteen smugglers and one fae and one former food-taster, wrapped in oilcloth against the drizzle. The gaol rose out of the mist like a tooth—black stone, iron-capped, a single lantern burning above its gate as though the building itself were too proud to sleep.

Elodie crouched in the heather beside Vess and studied it the way she'd once studied a banquet table: not for beauty, but for weakness.

Every dish had a flaw. Every fortress did too. You just had to be patient enough to find where the cook got lazy.

"The gate's hopeless," Vess murmured. "But look there—the supply door, on the seaward side. Carts go in twice a day. Bread, water, lamp-oil." Her mouth twisted. "And wine. Ravenmoor's gaoler is one Aldric's own, and Aldric's men drink like the office is a punishment they intend to survive."

"How many guards?"

"Twelve inside, give or take. Plus the witch-finders' detachment—six, quartered separately, and those six are the ones who frighten me. Iron-badged, charm-scented hounds. They can smell working magic through stone."

Elodie turned that over. "Then we don't use magic until we have to."

"You're asking smugglers to storm a royal gaol without charms."

"I'm asking them to do what they're best at." She looked at Vess. "Lie, cheat, and pour drinks."

* * *

The plan, such as it was, took shape over two days of watching from a shepherd's bothy on the ridge. Twice daily the cart came. Once daily the gaoler himself came out to meet it—a fat, comfortable man named Odo Rennick, whose jowls wobbled when he shouted and whose guards flinched when he did. He inspected the wine barrels personally. This, Vess explained, was famous along the whole coast: Gaoler Rennick trusted no man's palate but his own.

"He tastes every cask before it goes down to his men," she said. "Spits it in the gravel if it's off. Had a vintner flogged once over a sour batch."

Elodie felt something turn over in her chest—an old, cold recognition, like meeting a face from a mirror in an unexpected place.

"I know that man," she said quietly.

Vess glanced at her. "You know him?"

"I've *been* him." She watched Rennick dip his ladle, swish, spit, nod. Fifteen years of tasting other people's food so they could eat without fear. Fifteen years of being the least person at every table, the one whose tongue mattered and whose life didn't. "He thinks his mouth is his power. It's the only thing anyone's ever valued in him, so he guards it like a crown jewel."

"And?"

"And that means he trusts it absolutely." Elodie stood, brushing heather from her knees. "Which means we don't need to beat his guards. We need to beat his tongue. And I promise you, Captain—" she smiled, and it wasn't a kind smile, "—no tongue in Vellamarch has ever beaten mine."

* * *

The cart arrived on the third morning, and it carried poison.

Not much. A whisper of it, folded into a cask of decent red that Vess's people had "liberated" from a wrecked merchantman off the Teeth. Wolfsbane extract, cut with something sweeter to mask the bite—Elodie had spent the ride out grinding and blending it herself with a mortar borrowed from the ship's surgeon, her hands remembering work they hadn't done since she fled Vellamarch. Not enough to kill. Not enough even to sicken badly. Just enough to make a careful tongue notice something wrong, and to make a careless one dismiss it.

Because here was the thing fifteen years at court had taught her, the thing no grimoire ever recorded: poison was never really about chemistry. Poison was about *trust*. A poisoned dish could pass a hundred lips untouched if the taster was watching, because the taster knew what to look for—and a clean dish could kill a king if the taster was complacent. The weapon was never in the cup. It was in the certainty of the man holding it.

Rennick dipped his ladle into the new cask, swished, held it on his tongue with the solemnity of a bishop at mass.

Elodie, watching through Vess's spyglass from the ridge, saw him frown. Saw him taste again. Saw the frown deepen—and then smooth away, because the wine was good, genuinely good, and a man who trusted his own palate above all others would sooner doubt the vintage than himself.

He waved the cart through.

"Right," Elodie breathed. "Now we wait for evening, when the cask is opened and half his guard detail is drinking."

"And then?" Ivenn asked.

"Then the guards get sick. Badly sick—vomiting, cramps, blind panic. They'll think the wine was poisoned on purpose. By whom? Not us—we haven't come yet. They'll think it came in the cart. From outside. From *Ashenport sympathizers*." She let that sit. "Rennick will believe his own garrison has been infiltrated. He'll lock down the cells, pull men off the stair, start hunting traitors among his own people. And while twelve men tear each other apart looking for an enemy that doesn't exist—"

"We walk in the front gate," Ivenn finished, "as the relief column."

Vess stared at her. Then she laughed, a great bark of a sound that scattered birds off the bothy roof. "Gods preserve me. Fifteen years tasting soup, and this is what it makes you."

"It makes you very good at knowing exactly how much a man can swallow," Elodie said, "before he starts asking questions."

* * *

Evening came down purple and cold. In the gaol below, lamplight bloomed behind arrow-slits; supper was served; the cask was tapped.

They gave it an hour. Then, from the ridge, Elodie watched the first window flicker wrong—shadows lurching behind the glass, a lamp dropped, a shout swallowed by stone. Another light joined it. A third. Somewhere below, a man was retching his soul out onto flagstones, and somewhere above him, a fat man in a gaoler's chain was arriving at a conclusion that would dismantle his own house.

"Now," Elodie said.

They came down the moor road openly, nineteen smugglers in stolen militia cloaks, marching in the sloppy-tight formation of soldiers who wanted to be seen. Ivenn walked at the center with his hood up and glamour wound tight—not hidden, exactly, but blurred, the way a face blurs when you try to recall it later. Elodie wore a witch-finder's badge Vess had produced from a dead man's effects, cold iron against her breastbone, and hated every second of its weight.

At the gate, a sentry challenged them, voice already frayed with fear from the shouting within.

"Relief from the coast garrison!" Vess bellowed, in an accent so perfectly official it made Elodie's skin prickle. "Captain's compliments! Word came at dusk—poison in the provisioning line. We're to secure the prisoners before the traitors inside finish the job!"

The sentry's eyes went wide. Behind him, through the gate arch, Elodie could hear chaos—men groaning, boots hammering stone, Rennick's voice cracking across it all like a whip: *"Seal the cell block! Seal it! Nobody touches the prisoner—nobody breathes on him—"*

The sentry hesitated one heartbeat too long, and Ivenn stepped forward, and something in the air between them bent. The sentry's gaze slid sideways, finding nothing worth arresting in the blur of a face, and he hauled the gate open to let his rescuers in.

They moved through the outer yard in a tight knot, past guards doubled over in the lee of the wall, past a bucket brigade of miserable soldiers washing vomit from the flags. Rennick stood at the head of the cell-block stair, purple-faced, haranguing a sergeant.

"—infiltrated, I tell you! Someone in my own stores! Search every bunk! And double the watch on the scholar—if the traitors meant to poison my men to reach him, then by the crown, they'll find him gone before they—"

His eye fell on Elodie's badge, and he straightened, grasping at authority like a drowning man at rope. "Witch-finder! Thank the saints. Your order's detachment—they're not answering—"

"Quarantined themselves," Elodie said crisply. "Iron protocol. They suspect contamination of their badges." She let the lie hang, glittering and terrible. "Show me the prisoner. If your garrison's compromised, the codex and the scholar are crown property now, and I answer for them directly to the King."

Something flickered across Rennick's face—the ancient war between fear of superiors and love of his own importance—and love won, as it always does in small men. "This way. Mind the stairs. And if any of my men so much as sneezes near him—"

"Lead," Elodie said.

Two hundred steps down, spiraling into wet black stone. Torchlight guttered. The air thickened with mildew and old despair, and beneath it—faint, unmistakable to a nose trained on fifteen years of kitchens and courts—ink, paper, and the particular chalk-dust smell of a man who had been writing on his cell wall.

Bramwen Hale was alive, and he was still working.

The cell at the bottom was iron-barred, and behind the bars sat a thin man with a broken-left-hand slouch and ink-stained fingers, looking up at them with the expression of a professor interrupted mid-lecture. His eyes found Elodie's badge first, and something wary flickered there—then found her face above it, and the wariness collapsed into naked, disbelieving hope.

"You're late," he croaked. "I had them down to a schedule."

"Hello to you too," Elodie whispered.

"Keys," Ivenn said flatly, hand out to Rennick.

Rennick fumbled at his ring, and that was when the night broke open—because from the top of the stair came a scream, and then another, and the sound of iron-badged boots taking the steps two at a time. The witch-finders' detachment, six strong, had finally decided that quarantine was for lesser men.

"They're coming," Vess hissed from the landing above. "All six, and they've got the hounds—"

Rennick froze, keys in hand, caught between the devil descending and the deep he'd just unlocked. Elodie made the choice for him. She stepped close, put her mouth near his ear, and used the voice she'd once used to talk kings out of bad moods and kitchen boys out of panic—the voice of absolute, unimpeachable authority.

"Gaoler. Your garrison is poisoned, your witch-finders are compromised, and your prisoner is escaping under a finder's seal. When the inquiry comes—and it will come—you will want to say you cooperated fully." She paused, letting him hear the gallows in the silence. "Open the rest of the way."

The keys turned.

Bramwen came out of the cell like water finding a crack, and Elodie grabbed his arm—thin, trembling, real—and hauled him toward the stair as the first witch-finder rounded the spiral above them, iron badge blazing, hound straining at its chain with its nose full of fae-scent and freedom.

"*There!*" the finder screamed. "*Stop them—stop—*"

What happened next, Elodie would remember in fragments forever. Vess's smugglers turning to meet the charge with boat-hooks and belaying pins. Ivenn stepping forward into the torchlight and letting his hood fall, letting the glamour drop entirely, and the sight of him—true and terrible and *other*—stopping three armed men colder than any spell. The hound whimpering and flattening itself against the wall. A finder raising an iron rod that spat white fire, and Bramwen, half-dead, shoving Elodie aside and snapping something in a scholar's Latin that made the fire bend like grass in wind.

And Elodie herself, doing the only thing she had ever been truly trained to do: reading the room. Reading the men. Seeing which guard was nearest the oil lamps, seeing that his hands shook, seeing that he was the same man who'd been vomiting an hour ago and was now terrified of his own stomach—seeing, in short, exactly how much fear a man could hold before it tipped into something else.

She seized a fallen wine flask from the floor, held it high, and shouted in her best court voice:

"Your gaoler poisoned you! Rennick dosed the cask himself—he's been paid by the traitors in Ashenport to silence every witness, starting with *you!*"

It was a lie built entirely out of truth, which is the only kind that works. The guards looked at Rennick. Rennick looked at the flask. And twenty years of small cruelties, of flogged vintners and flinching sergeants, settled over his jowls like a sentence.

The guards turned on him as one.

In the chaos that followed—Rennick's shrieks, the witch-finders fighting both smugglers and their own garrison, Ivenn carving a path of pure terror toward the stairs—Elodie kept hold of Bramwen's arm and climbed. Two hundred steps, his weight growing heavier against her shoulder, his breath coming in ragged scholar's gasps, and her legs burning with the effort of carrying them both toward the grey rectangle of the open gate.

They burst out into the night air, into rain and heather-smell and the impossible sweetness of a sky with no roof on it, and Vess slammed the gaol door behind them and barred it from outside with a spar off her own ship.

Nineteen smugglers had gone in. Seventeen came out. Elodie learned their names on the march back over the moor, in the dark, because Vess said that was the rule: you carried the names home or you weren't fit to sail.

Bramwen walked between Elodie and Ivenn, wrapped in a smuggler's cloak, coughing, laughing, crying a little, all at once.

"The codex," he managed at last. "Did you—did anyone think to—"

"In your cell," Elodie said. "We saw the walls. You wrote it all out from memory. Every page you could recall."

"Three months of nothing else to do." He wiped his face with a filthy sleeve. "The witch-finders took the original, but memory isn't property, Elodie. Memory can't be confiscated." He stopped walking. Turned to her. In the moonlight his eyes were fierce and wet. "You came. For me. With smugglers and a fae and a plan built on *wine*."

"I told you I was useful everywhere," Elodie said. "Welcome nowhere."

"No." Bramwen gripped her wrist, hard, with the strength of a man who had spent three months in the dark deciding what mattered. "You had it backwards the whole time. Listen to me. The trade they despised—that's why we're alive tonight. Nobody storms a gaol with prophecy. Nobody breaks chains with scholarship. You read that gaol the way you read a banquet table, and you found the flaw, and you *used* it." His grip tightened. "Your tongue saved my life. Own it."

Elodie opened her mouth to deflect—to joke, to minimize, to do all the small disappearing acts she'd perfected over fifteen years—and found she couldn't. Something in her chest had cracked open along an old fault line, and what came out instead was simply:

"I missed you."

"And I," said Bramwen, "have been composing arguments for you the entire incarceration. We'll discuss the miss at length. Over food. Preferably food someone else has tasted."

Ivenn snorted. Ahead, Vess raised a fist, and the column halted at the crest of the moor, and Elodie looked back once at Ravenmoor—lanterns swarming over it now like disturbed ants, horns beginning to sound across the dark hills.

They had perhaps until dawn before Aldric's riders swept the roads.

"Captain," Elodie said. "How fast can the *Salt Lark* sail?"

Vess grinned into the rain. "Depends entirely on how fast you can run."
