## 16. The Queen of Rust's Price

The Queen of Rust received them in a garden that had forgotten how to grow.

Elodie had expected thrones, or at least the suggestion of one—some seat of antler and slag from which the fae monarch could look down on petitioners. Instead there was only a courtyard of grey sand ringed by iron fences gone to orange bloom, and in the center a single dead tree, its branches hung with keys. Thousands of them. They turned on their rings in a wind Elodie couldn't feel, and they did not chime. The silence of them was worse than sound.

The queen stood beneath the tree as though she'd been waiting there since before the covenant was signed. She was not beautiful the way Ivenn was beautiful, all clean lines and cold light. She was beautiful the way a burned house is beautiful—the way ruin insists you keep looking. Her gown was chainmail softened to lace. Her crown was a circlet of rusted hinges. When she moved, the keys above her stirred, and Elodie's skin prickled with the certainty that each one opened something that should have stayed shut.

"You are the Coldharbour girl." Her voice was dry leaves over stone. "I have been listening to your footsteps for three days."

"Your Majesty." Bramwen bowed the way scholars bow—precisely, and slightly too late. Ivenn did not bow at all. His jaw was set in the shape he got when he was about to be stubborn about something, which, Elodie had learned, was most shapes.

"We come to ask for an army," Ivenn said.

"I know what you've come to ask." The queen's eyes found Elodie and stayed there, pinning her like a specimen. "I asked why you came to *her* first."

"Because she carries the keystones," Ivenn said. "Because her blood signed the covenant."

"Her blood *broke* nothing," the queen said. "That is what interests me. Come closer, taster. You don't mind if I call you that? In my realm we are called by our functions. It saves time when names begin to rot."

Elodie crossed the grey sand. Up close, the queen smelled of struck flint and old rain, and beneath it something faintly sweet that made the back of Elodie's throat itch—a taste she recognized the way a taster recognizes anything: instantly, involuntarily, and with a full inventory of consequences. *Foxglove,* she thought. *Or something wearing foxglove's coat.* Poison, or the memory of poison. This woman had been poisoned by something once, or had poisoned something, and had never rinsed the cup.

"You want to know what I'll charge," the queen said.

"I assumed there would be a price," Elodie said. "There always is."

"Good. Then you're less foolish than the last Coldharbour who stood here." Something flickered across the ruined face—not quite a smile. "My grandmother hosted yours. Did you know that? Before the signing. Your grandmother came through this same courtyard and asked the same favor, and my grandmother gave the same answer I am about to give you, and your grandmother took it because she believed she had no choice. That is how the covenant was born. Not in wisdom. In a courtyard, under a tree, by two women who each thought the other held all the cards."

"The covenant kept the peace," Bramwen said carefully. "A century without war between the realms—"

"A century," the queen agreed, "in which my people withered like cut flowers in a vase someone forgot to refill." She lifted one hand, and the keys overhead shivered. "You mortals think the Hollow is a country. It is a lung. And the covenant is a hand closed around its throat. Every year the gate narrows. Every year more of us go to mist. You saw the withering on your way here. You walked through my people's graves without knowing they were graves, because we do not bury—we simply thin."

Ivenn's voice came out rough. "Then help us reforge it properly. With terms that hold both realms. That was always the point of the keystones—"

"No." The word fell like a key striking stone. "You misunderstand me, exile. I did not say the price was steep. I said there is no version of restoration I will accept. My price for an army—one thousand spears, iron-forged, mortal-proofed—is this: **dissolve it.** Shatter the keystones. Speak the dissolution. End the covenant entirely, and let the gate stand open or let it close forever, but let no one—no king, no warden, no bloodline—hold the other end of the leash again."

The silence that followed had weight. Elodie felt it settle on her shoulders like wet wool.

"That would unmake everything," Bramwen whispered. "The wardenship. The balance. A century of law—"

"A century of law written by the victors," the queen said, "and enforced on the defeated. Do you know what the covenant actually says, scholar? Not the pretty version in your archives. The binding clause. The clause your kings deleted from every copy but one?" She looked at Elodie. "Ask her. She has the true text in her blood. She just hasn't read it yet."

* * *

They were given a chamber in the palace of rust—a room where the walls were hammered tin and the candlelight came up amber, making everything look preserved rather than alive. Bramwen paced. Ivenn sat with his elbows on his knees and his face in his hands, and for a long time nobody spoke.

"She's lying," Ivenn said finally. "Or twisting. Fae twist. It's what we do."

"Is she?" Bramwen stopped pacing. "Ivenn. I have spent thirty years studying the covenant, and I will tell you honestly: I never read the original binding clause. No mortal scholar has. It was sealed into the keystone-rite itself. If anyone can read it now, it's Elodie."

"Then she shouldn't," Ivenn said flatly. "Because whatever it says, the answer can't be dissolution. You dissolve the covenant, the gate goes wild. Things cross that shouldn't. Mortal magic runs unchecked, fae hunger runs unchecked—"

"You're describing the last hundred years," Elodie said quietly. "From the other side of the gate."

He looked up at her, wounded. She hadn't meant it as a blade. Or maybe she had. She was tired, and tired people reach for sharp things.

"I need to see it," she said. "The clause. Whatever the queen thinks I'll find—I need to see it myself. Not through her mouth. Not through prophecy. Through mine own eyes."

Bramwen nodded slowly. "The rite requires blood on the first keystone and intent spoken aloud. But reading—that you might do alone. Tonight. Here, where the veil is thin and the old words sit close to the surface." He hesitated. "Elodie. Whatever you find. We decide together. All three of us. That was the promise."

"Yes," Elodie said. "Of course."

It was the easiest lie she had ever told, and she hated herself for how easy it was.

* * *

She waited until the tin-walled palace went quiet, until even the rust seemed to sleep. Then she knelt on the floor of her chamber, drew the first keystone from its wrapping of Ashenport sailcloth, and set it before her.

Up close, in candlelight, it was less a stone than a tooth—pale, curved, veined with something dark. The Drowned Bell, they called it, raised from Lake Verre by her grandmother's name sung backward through water. It hummed against the floorboards, a note below hearing, and Elodie felt it hum in her teeth, in the roots of her, in the place where the river had risen to shield her and her body had known things her mind was never taught.

She pressed her palm to it. Cut her thumb on the edge, the way the rite demanded, and let one drop fall.

The keystone opened.

Not physically. It opened the way a book opens behind your eyes. Words rose in her—not in any language she knew, and yet she understood them the way she understood poison: completely, immediately, and with her whole body. The binding clause. The true text, sealed for a century, surfacing now through Coldharbour blood onto Coldharbour sight.

*And the Hollow shall be bound to the mortal realm, and the mortal realm to the Hollow, and neither shall pass the gate save at the sufferance of the Warden; and the Warden's line shall draw its strength from the Hollow's substance, taking as tribute the years, the light, and the names of the fae, that the border may stand fast; and this taking shall continue unto the ending of the world, and no fae shall refuse it, and no fae shall speak of it, and no fae shall remember it save in the hour of its choosing.*

Elodie read it twice. Three times. Looking for the loophole, the mercy, the misreading. There wasn't one.

The covenant was not a wall between realms. It was a tap. The wardens' power—the power humming in her own veins right now, the power that had raised the bell and parted the briar and answered her blood at the river—was drawn *from* the Hollow itself. From its years, its light, its names. Every charm her family ever worked, every ward, every wonder, was paid for out of fae lives, steadily, silently, for a hundred years. The withering she had walked through on the road here—the fading faces, the mist where people used to be—that wasn't the covenant failing.

That was the covenant *working.*

And no fae shall speak of it. No fae shall remember it save in the hour of its choosing. The queen couldn't have told them outright. Even now, half-telling it, she'd had to dress it in metaphor—lungs, vases, cut flowers. Ivenn had lived his whole exile beside this truth and couldn't see it, because the spell that hid it was woven into his memory too.

Elodie sat back on her heels. The candle had burned down to a stub. Somewhere beyond the tin walls, the keys in the dead tree turned in their soundless wind.

*We decide together,* Bramwen had said. And she had said yes, yes, of course—and already known, even then, in some cellar of herself, that she would be deciding alone. Because what would she tell them?

*Ivenn, your whole exile, your shame, your banishment—it was purchased with stolen years from your own people, and you couldn't feel it because the theft was spelled invisible. Bramwen, your life's scholarship, the covenant you defended as law—it was a feeding trough with a legal seal. And me—I am the heir of thieves. The last of a line of gentle, beloved plunderers. My grandmother sang lullabies with a mouth full of stolen light.*

If she told them tonight, raw and bleeding with it, they would argue. Ivenn would rage and deny. Bramwen would want corroboration, sources, the slow machinery of proof—and Aldric's legions were burning toward the free cities *now*, on a clock measured in days, not debates. The queen's army would march or it wouldn't. The decision would be made in this courtyard tomorrow morning, and it would be made by whoever stood there holding the truth.

Only her. Only the taster, whose whole trade was knowing what was in the cup before anyone drank.

She thought of Sef, who had betrayed his friend out of love for his daughter, and who had died—would die, she corrected herself, *might still live*—carrying the weight of a choice no one else could make for him. She thought of her landlady burning the shop. Of the laundress buying luck she couldn't afford. Everyone she had ever admired had carried something alone for a while. It didn't make it right. It just made it human.

Elodie wrapped the keystone back in its sailcloth. Her thumb had already stopped bleeding; Coldharbour blood healed quick off fae debt, she realized, and the thought made her stomach turn.

Tomorrow, in the courtyard, she would give the queen her answer. She would accept the price—an army for dissolution—and she would not tell Bramwen yet, and she would not tell Ivenn yet, and the secret would sit in her chest like a swallowed coal, and she would carry it to the gates of Vellamarch if she had to.

Not because she trusted herself above them. Because there was no version of this conversation that ended with all three of them walking out of the Hollow in time. Some choices could only be carried by the person standing closest to the scale.

She blew out the candle. In the dark, the keystone hummed against her ribs where she'd tucked it, patient as a bell waiting to be rung—or broken.

Outside, far off across the withering fields, she heard horns. Not fae horns. Mortal ones, deep and brassy and wrong for this sky.

Aldric's scouts had reached the borders of the Hollow.
