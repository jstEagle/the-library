## 17. March on the Capital

The column left Ashenport at dawn, four thousand strong and growing by the mile.

Elodie stood on a cart bed at the head of it and watched the road behind her fill with people who had no business marching anywhere. Dockhands with boat-hooks ground to points. Salt-wives with skinning knives. Two brothers from the tannery who had brought nothing but themselves and a shared pair of boots, trading them off every league. Behind the Ashenport banners came the Thornwood irregulars in their moss-green cloaks, fae and mortal mixed without distinction, which was the part that made old men cross themselves in the villages they passed through.

An alliance unthinkable a season ago. She'd heard Bramwen say it twice already, once to her and once to his notes, and she suspected he was drafting the sentence for whatever book he now claimed he would write instead of prophecy.

"Your face is doing the thing," Ivenn said beside her.

"What thing."

"The thing where you look at all of them and calculate exactly how many will die so you can feel properly guilty about it later."

"I don't feel guilty about them," Elodie said. "I feel responsible *for* them. It's different."

"It isn't." He adjusted the strap of his quiver with one long finger. "But go on pretending. It's very human."

She might have argued, except that the first village came up over the rise—Marrow's Cross, forty houses, a well, a shrine to some river god whose name had been repainted so many times it was now just a blue smear—and the villagers were standing along the road watching the column come, and among them, at the front of the little crowd, was a girl of perhaps fourteen holding out her hands like she was asking for alms.

Elodie knew that stance. She'd worn it herself across a hundred market stalls: the particular posture of someone carrying something they can't put down.

"Hold the column," she told the Ashenport captain, and jumped down before anyone could tell her not to.

---

The girl's name was Tansy and what she carried was fire.

Not metaphorically—that was the trouble. Her palms blistered when she got frightened, which she was now, badly, because her mother had seen the scorch marks on the bedsheet and wept, and everyone in Marrow's Cross knew what happened to marked children since Aldric's edicts. The witch-finders had passed through three weeks ago and taken the cooper's grandson. Nobody said drowned. Everybody thought it.

Elodie crouched in front of her on the packed dirt of the road, ignoring the crowd, ignoring the column, ignoring Ivenn's shadow falling cool across both of them.

"Show me."

"I'll burn you."

"You won't." She held out her own hands, palms up, taster's hands, scarred faintly at the fingertips from fifteen years of silver spoons and worse. "Fire wants somewhere to go. Right now it's going into your skin because you're holding the door shut. So open the door."

"That's mad."

"Yes," Elodie agreed. "Most true things are. Do it."

Tansy closed her eyes. Her breath went ragged—and Elodie watched, with the same detached attention she'd once given the king's stew, the heat gather under the girl's skin like light through a held-up coin. She didn't flinch. When the flame came it came as a small clean tongue above Tansy's open palm, steady as a candle, and the girl's eyes flew open wide and wet and astonished.

"Oh," she whispered. "Oh, it doesn't hurt."

"It never did. You just kept telling it to." Elodie folded the girl's fingers closed over the flame and felt it wink out against her skin like a kiss. "Listen to me. There are teachers ahead on this road. Find the woman with grey braids and a limp—Maren. Tell her Elodie sent you and you're a hearth-talent. She'll teach you banked-fire first, then shaped. And if anyone in a black coat asks, you're a cook's daughter with a burn scar, and I never touched you."

"You're the Coldharbour girl," Tansy blurted. "Everyone says—"

"Everyone says a lot of things." Elodie stood. Her knees ached; she was thirty-one years old and sleeping on the ground. "Go on. Your mother's crying harder than she needs to, which means she loves you. Go fix that."

---

By midday she had stopped six times.

A ferryman whose talent for water ran so deep he could feel rain coming in his teeth. A pair of twins who finished each other's charms without meaning to, doubling everything, dangerous as unloaded crossbows. A boy of eleven who simply made things *lighter*—stones, buckets, once, catastrophically, a cow—and who had been hiding it since he was seven because lighter things float, and he'd floated his baby sister's cradle across the millpond and been beaten for sorcery by a father too frightened to understand what he'd seen.

None of them wanted power. That was what struck her, hour after hour, standing in ditches and doorways and one memorable barnyard while the column waited and the captains cursed. They wanted permission. They wanted someone to say *what you are is not a death sentence*, and then, mostly, they wanted to be left alone.

She had spent fifteen years being useful everywhere and welcome nowhere, selling luck in thimbles and tasting poison for a king who would drown her if he knew her name. She had built an entire life on the principle that magic hidden is magic survivable. Now she walked up and down a road teaching farm children to open their fists, and the strange truth of it settled into her like sediment:

Leadership wasn't command. Command was Sef's world—orders, hierarchy, the palace arithmetic of who eats first. This was something else. This was service wearing the costume of authority. You led by kneeling in the dirt first. You led by giving away everything you knew until your knowledge lived in other people's hands.

"You're collecting them," Ivenn observed that evening, as the campfires kindled across a fallow barley field. He nodded toward Tansy, who had found Maren within the hour and was sitting at the older woman's feet with the rapt expression of someone handed a key to a locked house. "Strays."

"I'm pointing them at people better qualified."

"Mm." He fed a stick into the flames. In the firelight his features went less sharp, more the shape of the exile who had guarded a standing stone alone for longer than she'd been alive. "In the Hollow, talent was inherited. Blooded lines, court appointments, the whole suffocating lattice of it. We never imagined—" he gestured vaguely at the field, the strays, the smoke "—that it might simply grow wild. Like weeds. Like anything unowned."

"Is that contempt I hear?"

"It's envy," Ivenn said, and did not elaborate, and Elodie let it lie between them like the ember it was.

Bramwen arrived with three bowls of barley porridge and the expression of a man about to commit pedantry. "You should know the numbers," he said, handing them out. "Two hundred and eleven raw talents identified since Ashenport. Forty-one with combat applications. Nine—nine—who could stand against a witch-finder's iron warding, given training and a week we don't have."

"And the rest?"

"The rest are the point, Elodie." He sat down heavily, knees cracking. "I've been reading the wrong books my whole life. Prophecy tells you what one person does. History—" he tapped his satchel, where a new notebook had replaced the codex-shaped hole in his heart "—history tells you what everybody does. Two hundred people who know they aren't monsters anymore? That's worth more than any keystone."

"Don't let the Queen of Rust hear you say that."

"I intend to shout it at her," Bramwen said mildly, "if she gives me the chance."

---

They crossed the Verre at the old ford on the fourth day, and Elodie made herself look at the water.

Lake Verre lay east, where the sunken chapel held the first keystone—or had, before she spoke her grandmother's name into its drowned dark and felt the bell toll through her bones. But the river that fed the lake ran through here too, wide and brown and patient, and this was the water where they drowned the gifted in Vellamarch. She had nearly been one of them. She remembered the cold closing over her ears, the crowd's faces blurred green above her, the impossible moment when the river rose to meet her like a mother bending down.

Now soldiers and fishermen waded the ford side by side with fae who could walk on the surface if they cared to show off, and nobody looked at the water with fear. A boy splashed his brother. A salt-wife washed laundry in the shallows, beating cloth on stones, singing something tuneless and old.

Elodie stood at the crossing a long time. Ivenn waited without hurrying her, which from Ivenn was practically a love letter.

"It keeps what it keeps," he said finally. "Rivers don't take sides. People do."

"People drowned me in that logic."

"People pulled you out of it too." He tipped his chin downstream, toward the capital, toward Vellamarch and its walls and the man on its throne wearing her family's ring. "Come. You have two hundred more strays to collect, and I'm told the next village has a girl who talks to bees, and Maren is hopeless with bees."

---

The news caught them on the sixth day, riding fast from the south: Aldric's legions had burned Thornford and were moving on the capital road ahead of them, trying to reach Vellamarch first and close the gates. Behind them, refugees. Ahead of them, war.

The council of captains met that night around a map weighted with knives and river-stones, and Elodie discovered that she was expected to speak. Not because anyone had appointed her. Because everyone had simply assumed, the way water assumes downhill, and fighting the current cost more than going with it.

"If he closes the gates before us," said Captain Vess of Ashenport, a broad woman with a smuggler's squint, "we siege our own capital through winter. Four thousand mouths. More coming daily."

"Then we don't give him the road." Elodie leaned over the map. Her finger found the low hills west of the highway—the Shepherd's Downs, sheep country, thin soil, bad ground for infantry squares and good ground for people who knew how to disappear. "We cut across here. Fae irregulars screen the flanks; nothing moves in the briar country that Ivenn's people don't allow. We reach the walls first, or we reach them alongside him, and either way he doesn't get a sealed city and a warm garrison waiting."

"They're raw," Vess said. "Half your mortals have held a spear for a fortnight."

"So train them on the march." Elodie straightened. Somewhere beyond the map-light, Tansy was showing three younger children how to hold a flame small enough to read by—a study circle, glowing soft in the dark like a hearth in a window. "That's what the road's for. Every day we walk, they learn. By the time we see those walls, they won't be four thousand farmers. They'll be four thousand people who chose to be there, and there is no weapon Aldric owns that beats that."

Silence around the map. Then Vess grinned like a knife coming out of a sheath and started assigning companies, and it was done.

Later, Bramwen found her at the edge of the camp, looking north. The sky above the far hills held the faint orange stain of Thornford still burning, sixty miles away.

"Aldric will offer terms again," Bramwen said quietly. "When he sees us coming. Legitimacy, title, the whole poisoned feast. He offered once before, I'm told."

"He did."

"And you refused."

"He was offering me a seat at his table." Elodie turned the signet-less ring finger of her right hand, an old habit. "I'm not hungry for chairs, Bramwen. I never was. I'm hungry for—" she waved at the camp, the study circles, the cookfires, the sound of a fae fiddler teaching an Ashenport drummer a rhythm from before the covenant "—this. Tables with room enough."

Bramwen smiled and went to write it down, because he was Bramwen.

Elodie stayed a moment longer in the dark. Far off to the northeast, past the hills and the burning town and the armies crawling toward each other like weather fronts, Vellamarch waited with its river walls and its drowned law. And somewhere inside it, behind stone, Sef's daughter was alive because her father had traded everything, and Sef himself—

She hadn't let herself think of him in days. She made herself think of him now: small, careful Sef, taster-master, a man who had chosen devotion over honor and paid for it in the only coin he had. Whatever end of this he reached, she owed him a debt no throne could settle.

A horn sounded from the pickets—not alarm, but the two-note call that meant travelers. Elodie turned, and saw riders coming up the moonlit road under a scrap of white cloth, and at their head a figure she recognized even at distance by the way he sat his horse like a man apologizing to it.

Witch-finder grey, but the coat was torn and travel-stained, and the rider raised both empty hands high.

"We need to talk," called Warden Corvane, the man who had hunted her from Vellamarch to the Hollow's gate, "before Aldric does something we'll all regret. I bring word from inside the walls—and proof that your friend Sef's warning was truer than any of us knew. The legions aren't coming to defend the city, Coldharbour girl. They're coming to burn it, covenant or no covenant, and Aldric means to blame it on you."
