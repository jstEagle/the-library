## 18. The Gates of Vellamarch

The river walls of Vellamarch had never been breached. Every child in the city learned it the way they learned their prayers: the walls rose from the Verre itself, black stone slicked by a century of current, and no army had ever found purchase on them.

Elodie stood on a smuggler's barge at dawn and watched Aldric's legions try anyway.

They came down the far bank in columns, banners snapping, siege ladders like insect legs against the stone. The free-city militia lined the parapets above her—fishermen with boar spears, Ashenport smugglers with cutlasses, fae irregulars moving through the ranks like silver thread through grey cloth. Ivenn stood at her shoulder, bow unstrung across his back because arrows were wasted before first light.

"They'll come at the water gate," he said. "Aldric's engineers will have spent all winter on one answer to that wall."

"Then we'd better be there when they do."

She felt the city's fear through the soles of her boots, up through the barge's deck, a low tremor like a struck bell that hadn't finished ringing. Behind the walls, Vellamarch held forty thousand people who had done nothing but live under a king who feared magic more than famine. Ahead of her, ten thousand soldiers who believed they were putting down a rebellion.

Her trained tongue tasted the air off the river—iron, tar, wet stone—and beneath it something else. A bitterness she knew. Witch-finder reagents. They had brought the drowning cages.

"Ivenn." Her voice came out flat. "They've got iron on the water."

He looked at the barges massing near the eastern pilings and his face went still the way it only went still before violence. "Then we go fast, and we go loud."

* * *

Sef had been awake for three days.

He moved through the palace kitchens in the dark before the army's departure, tasting the morning porridge out of habit, finding nothing, tasting nothing ever again being the point of him now. His daughter was in the witch-finders' keeping—a house in the Sallow Quarter with guards at the door and a promise that she would be returned whole if the taster-master served. He had served. He had given them Elodie Coldharbour's habits, her routes, her face drawn from memory in charcoal. And then he had watched the legions march east to burn the free cities, and understood that the promise was ash, that men like Aldric's witch-finders did not return what they borrowed.

So last night he had written a letter in taster's cipher—the old code of pinch and grain, measurements disguised as kitchen inventory—and bribed a fish-cart driver to carry it through the lines to Ashenport. *Postern gate, river side, third stair below the water door. The lock is mine. Come at dusk on the third day.* And beneath it, the thing he could not cipher: *I am sorry. I was small. Let me be large once.*

Now the third day had come, and the battle with it, and Sef walked the river wall with the garrison commander's wine in his hand, pretending to test it, while below him the postern gate waited behind three feet of stone and one very tired lock.

The assault began at the water gate as Ivenn had said it would. Sef heard the great chain groan, heard the ram strike the sea-doors like a heart learning to beat too fast, and he set down the wine cup and walked, unhurried, a small grey man with a taster's keys on his belt, down the spiral stair toward the third landing.

Two guards at the postern. Young ones. He offered them a drink from the commander's own flask—"celebrating the wall holding, lads"—and watched the poppy syrup do its work inside a hundred count. He had spent fifteen years detecting poisons. It turned out the trade worked both ways.

The lock took his hands longer than he liked. His fingers shook. Not from age—from the knowledge of what came after. When the bolt finally slid, the gate swung inward onto the river stairs, and the dusk air came in cold and smelling of freedom, and Sef sat down on the step to wait for the people he had betrayed to come collect their debt.

* * *

Elodie came through the postern at full dark with thirty smugglers and Ivenn's fae at her back, and stopped dead when she saw him.

"Sef."

He looked smaller than she remembered. Greyer. He tried to stand and couldn't quite, and settled for lifting his chin the way he used to when presenting a dish he wasn't proud of.

"The lock is yours," he said. "And the wall above is thin here. Forty men could hold this stair until the gate's forced wide enough for your lot." He swallowed. "Elodie. What I did—"

"I know." She crouched to his level. "Bramwen told me about the letter. The cipher. We got your warnings about the legions too." She saw him flinch. "You gave them to me twice, Sef. Once with a knife at your throat, once without. That second time is the one I'm keeping."

His eyes went bright and he blinked it away like a professional. "There's a witch-finder captain named Corvane," he said quickly. "Iron gauntlets. He'll be first through any breach. Don't let him touch you—the iron burns your kind of blood, Bramwen's notes say so, don't ask how I read his notes—"

"Sef."

"And my daughter. Mira. If—"

"If we win, she eats at my table," Elodie said. "That's a promise, not a bargain. No price on it."

Something in the little man's face broke open then, quietly, the way ice breaks on a spring river. He nodded and got to his feet, and took up a fallen spear from beside the door, and said, "I know these stairs better than anyone alive. I'll hold the turn at the third landing."

"You don't have to—"

"I've been small my whole life," Sef said. "Let me be large once."

* * *

The postern became a wound in the wall by midnight, and the battle poured into it.

Elodie fought on the third landing with a smuggler's cutlass she held wrong and a charm of steadiness burning on her wrist, and around her the stair ran red in the torchlight. The militia fed men up from the river two at a time; Aldric's soldiers came down four at a time and died on the narrow turns where numbers meant nothing. And at the center of it, impossibly, stubbornly, stood Sef—spear braced against the wall, shouting the garrison's own drill-calls back at them in the garrison's own voice, turning each wave with nothing but geometry and fury.

She saw him take the first wound high in the shoulder and keep fighting. She saw him take the second and fall to one knee and rise again, screaming at the smugglers to come up faster, come *up*, the gate is open, the gate is open—

Then Corvane came through.

The witch-finder captain filled the stairwell like a slab of iron given legs, gauntlets gleaming, and the men around him parted, and Elodie felt her blood recoil from him the way water recoils from a hot blade. He looked past every soldier on the stair, straight at her, and smiled.

"Coldharbour," he said. "The king wants you breathing."

"He can want," she said, and threw everything she had into the landing—not power, she had almost none left, but craft, the luck-charm inverted, a curse of misstep woven fast and dirty. Corvane stumbled on the blood-slick stone. Half a heartbeat. Enough for the smugglers to surge.

Not enough. Iron closed around her wrist and the world went white.

She had been burned before—kitchen fires, a candle, ordinary pain—but this was different, this was her *blood* being scalded in its own channels, and she heard herself scream, heard Ivenn roar somewhere behind her, and then Bramwen was there.

The scholar had no weapon. He had never carried one. He had a book-bag and twenty years of stubbornness, and he put both between her and the witch-finder—threw himself bodily into the space where the iron was falling, arms spread over her like a page laid flat to protect the writing beneath it.

The gauntlet caught him across the temple and shoulder. She heard the crack of it. He dropped across her legs, and his blood, shockingly red, shockingly ordinary, ran warm down her arm and mingled with her own where the iron had opened it.

Corvane raised his fist again.

It never fell. Ivenn's knife took him through the gap beneath the gauntlet, once, twice, a third time with a savagery Elodie had never seen in the exile's careful hands, and the witch-finder captain folded down the stairwell like a banner with its pole cut.

The counterattack came up the stairs behind him—militia, fae, smugglers, the tide reversing—and Elodie knelt in it with Bramwen's head in her lap and pressed her palms against his wounds and felt, under her fingers, the terrible arithmetic of a body deciding.

"Bramwen. Bramwen, look at me. You pedantic, impossible man, look at me—"

His eyes found hers, unfocused, and he smiled the small smile he saved for footnotes. "Told you," he whispered. "Prophecy's... unreliable. People aren't. I chose... this part myself..."

"Don't you dare die and leave me an unfinished manuscript."

"Wouldn't... dream of it." His eyes drifted. "Elodie. The keystone. Aldric wears it. Finish the... the citation..."

She screamed for the healers.

* * *

Sef died at the fourth landing, though it took the story years to get the number right.

He held the turn alone for eleven minutes after the smugglers were pushed back down—eleven minutes measured afterward by a fae archer who counted time the way mortals counted coins. Eleven minutes in which a small grey man with a taster's spear kept a stairwell against soldiers trained since boyhood, because the gate behind him was open and the people coming through it deserved every second he could buy.

When they finally reached him, the soldiers were gone and Sef was sitting against the wall as if resting between courses, the spear across his knees, his eyes on the doorway where the free-city men were pouring through.

Elodie knelt beside him. There was nothing left to taste, nothing left to stop. She took his hand anyway.

"Mira," he said. "My table. Yours. You promised."

"She eats at my table," Elodie said. "Every word of it. No price."

He nodded, satisfied, a craftsman signing his work, and closed his eyes.

Above them, the great chain of the water gate groaned and parted, and the gates of Vellamarch—unbreached for a hundred years—swung open onto the river and the dawn and the army of the free.

* * *

They carried Bramwen down to the healers' tents as the sun came up, and Elodie walked beside the litter with her burned wrist bound in Ivenn's shirt and would not let go of the scholar's hand.

Through the open gate, the city was waking to the sound of its own liberation—bells, shouting, the witch-finders' barracks burning orange against the pale sky. Somewhere in the Sallow Quarter, a girl named Mira was about to learn her father had kept his promise after all.

Ivenn appeared at her elbow, blood-spattered, quiet. "The palace guard has broken," he said. "Aldric's fallen back to the keep. He's sent for you, Elodie. By herald. Under flag of truce." His mouth twisted. "He offers terms."

Elodie looked down at Bramwen's grey face, at the bandages already blooming red, and thought of Sef on the stairs, and Corvane's iron, and a signet ring on a usurper's hand with a Coldharbour keystone set in it like a stolen jewel.

"Tell the herald," she said, "that I'm coming. And tell him I won't be negotiating."
