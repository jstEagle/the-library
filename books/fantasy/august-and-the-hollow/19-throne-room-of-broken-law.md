## 19. Throne Room of Broken Law

The throne room smelled of lilies and old blood.

Elodie noticed the blood first—her trade, her curse, her gift. Fifteen years of tasting had taught her that every room had a flavor, and Vellamarch's great hall tasted of iron beneath the sweetness, of scrubbed stone that had never quite given up what soaked into it. The lilies were new. Aldric's touch. He'd scattered them along the dais steps like a man trying to perfume a corpse.

She walked the length of it alone.

Behind her, the doors had boomed shut. Ivenn was somewhere in the city with the fae irregulars, holding the river gate against the witch-finders' retreat. Bramwen lay in a surgeon's tent with an iron wound that should have killed her and hadn't, because Bramwen Hale did not intend to die before finishing at least three more books. Sef was dead. The postern gate he'd opened still bore his blood on the winch, and Elodie had made herself look at it, because looking was a kind of debt.

So she walked alone, past ranks of exhausted soldiers who didn't know yet whose side history would say they'd been on, past courtiers pressed against the walls like moths avoiding flame, up to the foot of the dais where Aldric sat on a throne built by better kings and waited for her to kneel.

She didn't kneel.

"You're bleeding," he said.

She looked down. There was a cut along her forearm she didn't remember taking, shallow, already clotting. "It's not mine," she lied, because it was easier than the truth, which was that everything today was hers—the blood, the cost, the victory still smoking in the streets below.

Aldric rose. Up close he was smaller than his coinage made him look, a compact man gone soft at the edges, with the careful hands of someone who had learned early that power was mostly paperwork. He wore the signet on his right hand.

Her family's keystone. Her grandmother's work, or her grandmother's grandmother's—a shard of pale stone set in blackened silver, humming so faintly that only a Coldharbour could feel it, like a word spoken just below hearing.

"You took my city," Aldric said.

"Your soldiers left it. I just stopped chasing them."

"My witch-finders are regrouping at Thornmark."

"I know. Ivenn is counting their campfires as we speak."

Something moved behind his eyes—not anger. Calculation. She recognized it because she'd seen it a thousand times across a tasting table: a man deciding how much poison the moment could bear.

"You could have killed me on the road," he said. "Your fae could have done it in their sleep. Instead you let me retreat to my own walls and sent—" he gestured at her, mud-streaked, swordless, smelling of river smoke and someone else's burnt bread, "—*this*. A food-taster. To negotiate."

"To talk."

"There's a difference?"

"In my experience, Majesty, negotiation is just talking with knives already drawn."

He laughed. It startled them both. Then he descended one step, and another, until they stood level, and held out his hand—not the signet hand, the other—and Elodie understood with a lurch that he was offering to shake it.

"Sit with me," he said. "Not as traitor and king. As the last two people in this room who understand what's actually at stake."

There were chairs. Of course there were chairs; Aldric was a man of chairs, of tables, of documents. Servants brought wine and Elodie watched him drink first from her cup—an old courtesy, inverted, almost funny—and then they were alone except for the guards at the far doors, and the lilies, and the hum of the stone on his finger.

"I've read the codex," Aldric said. "The real one, not the fragments your scholar hoards. Do you know what it says about the covenant? Not the poetry. The clauses."

"It says my family bound two realms together so neither could devour the other."

"It says your family bound *everyone else* so that *your family* could arbitrate." He leaned forward. "Every charm cast in Vellamarch for a century needed a Coldharbour's tacit permission. Every door between realms opened or closed at your line's discretion. You weren't wardens, girl. You were toll-keepers. And when your line failed to produce an heir, the whole machine seized—and my predecessor drowned charmers in the river rather than admit the crown had lost its leash." He turned the signet slowly with his thumb. "I'm offering you the chance to fix what your ancestors broke."

"And what does fixing it cost?"

"Nothing you haven't already paid." His voice went gentle, and that was the most dangerous thing about him, Elodie thought—he had a taster's palate too, for fear, for want, for the exact sweetness a person craved. "Legitimacy. That's all. The realm needs a warden; the Hollow needs a door; the law needs a name it isn't ashamed of. Give me the covenant restored under crown authority, with you as its hereditary keeper and me as its enforcer, and every edict against you dissolves. Your shop reopens. Your landlady's ashes get a pension. Your dead friend gets a hero's burial instead of a traitor's pyre." He slid something across the table.

The warrant. Her own face, inked a year ago, stamped TRAITOR. He struck it through with a pen, once, cleanly, and the sound of the nib was the loudest thing in the room.

"Title," he said. "Land. The Coldharbour name restored to the rolls. Warden of the Marches, Keeper of the Gate. And this—" he drew the signet from his finger and set it on the wood between them, "—returned to its rightful heir. All of it yours. All I ask is that the covenant bind us both. King and Warden. Law and Blood. Together."

The keystone sat there, humming.

Elodie looked at it the way she used to look at an unclaimed dish—reading it, reading herself, asking the oldest question of her trade: *what is this trying to make me feel?*

And the honest answer was: everything. Warmth. Home. Her mother's voice saying the lullaby with the true names in it. A shop with her name on the lease that no one could burn. Bramwen alive and arguing. Sef's daughter safe. Every wound of the last year closed with a signature and a stone.

That was the trap, and she saw it clearly, and seeing it clearly did not make it stop hurting.

Because underneath the warmth was the clause. *Bind us both.* Bind the Hollow again. Bind Ivenn's people back behind a door they'd died to keep shut. Bind every raw talent she'd trained on the road north—Maret with her fire-hands, the twins who could hear rain coming—back into a system where their magic was legal only at a warden's pleasure and a king's enforcement. Bind the Queen of Rust's dying realm to the very crown that had starved it.

Aldric wasn't offering her a seat at the table. He was offering her the table itself—with everyone else still eating scraps off the floor.

"You've read the codex," she said quietly. "Did you read the part where the covenant was written without asking either realm?"

"The covenant kept the peace for a hundred years."

"The covenant kept *your* peace. Ask the Hollow if it was peaceful. Ask the fae fading to mist in their own fields because mortals needed a leash." She turned the warrant over with one finger. "You want me to be the leash."

"I want order."

"No." She met his eyes. "You want *legitimacy*, and you've finally figured out you can't take it—you have to be given it. By me. By the blood the old law actually recognizes. That's why I'm still alive. That's why Bramwen was taken instead of killed. That's why you let me walk into this room." She smiled, and it was her market smile, the one that sold luck-charms to people who needed to believe. "You need my name more than my head."

Aldric's jaw tightened. For a moment the careful man slipped and she saw the usurper underneath, the one who had burned a life's worth of ledgers to build a crown out of other people's fear.

"The alternative," he said, "is that I declare this meeting a trap. That you came to assassinate me. My guards are twenty feet away."

"They are. And my fae hold your river gate, and your legions are scattered from Ashenport to Ravenmoor, and your witch-finders are counting campfires and wondering whose side the story will put them on." She stood. "You taught this realm that power is a thing you seize. I'm here to teach it something older."

She picked up the keystone.

It was warm. Of course it was warm—it had been waiting for her hand the way a lock waits for its key, and for one terrible heartbeat she felt the whole shape of it, the covenant entire, the machinery of binding waking under her skin, whispering that she could have it all, the title, the door, the name, everything her family had bled for—

She closed her fist around it and felt nothing but stone.

"Thank you for the wine," she said.

"That's it?" Aldric was on his feet now. "You walk in here, you take the one thing I cannot replace, and you give me nothing?"

"I'm giving you exactly what you gave everyone else." She backed toward the doors, the keystone heavy in her pocket, the warrant left lying on the table, crossed through and worthless twice over. "Terms."

The guards moved. She didn't flinch, and that unsettled them more than any spell would have—they'd heard the stories, the river rising, the thorns bending, the gaoler's own men turning on him at the taste of poisoned wine. Stories travel faster than soldiers.

At the doors she paused.

"One more thing, Majesty. Your predecessor drowned people in that river for casting charms. In a week, maybe two, there won't be a law left to drown anyone for. When that day comes, you can be the king who signed the repeal—or the footnote in someone else's book explaining why kings stopped mattering." She glanced back at him. "Bramwen Hale is writing that book. She's very thorough. Choose well."

Then the doors opened onto the noise of a city changing hands—not with fire this time, but with something quieter and harder to hold: argument, argument everywhere, in the markets and the barracks and the temples, the sound of a realm remembering it could govern itself.

Ivenn was waiting in the courtyard, leaning against a broken fountain as though he'd been there for hours, which he had.

"Well?" he said.

Elodie pulled the keystone from her pocket and held it up beside the other two, and the three shards sang to each other, faint and hungry, wanting to be whole.

"Three," she said. "All of them. Time to end this."

Ivenn looked at the stones, then at her, and something in his ancient face flickered—hope, she realized, hope so long unspent he'd forgotten the interest it accrued.

"The standing stones," he said. "Tonight, before he changes his mind and sends the guard."

"He won't send the guard." She started walking, fast, toward the river, toward the old gate, toward the place where her family had first said *bind* and she would finally say *unmake*. "He'll send lawyers."

Above the rooftops, the sun was going down on the last day of the covenant—and rising, somewhere beyond the world, on the first morning of whatever came next.

* * *

They reached the standing stone as the light failed.

Bramwen was supposed to be resting. Instead she was propped against the monolith with a slate on her knees, transcribing the dissolution rite from memory, arguing with herself in two colors of chalk. Her bandages were spotted red. She looked, Elodie thought, happier than she had ever looked in a dungeon.

"If you shatter them wrong," Bramwen said without looking up, "the backlash takes the river valley. Possibly the city. Definitely you."

"Noted."

"So don't do it wrong." She finally raised her head, and her eyes were fierce and wet. "Elodie. Once it's done, it's done. No covenant, no wardens, no Coldharbour power. You'll be—"

"A person," Elodie said. "Just a person."

"Yes." Bramwen's voice cracked on it. "The best thing anyone's ever been."

Ivenn stood apart, facing the stone, and when Elodie joined him he spoke without turning. "My people will feel it the moment you speak. Every chain in the Hollow going slack at once." A pause. "Some of them will weep. Some of them will never forgive you for the century it took."

"I know."

"And you?" He turned then, and his eyes were the color of deep water. "What will you feel, when it's gone?"

Elodie looked at the three keystones in her hands—her inheritance, her burden, her family's whole long weight—and found the answer waiting, simple as a taste on the tongue.

"Lighter," she said.

She raised the first stone above the altar-scar at the monolith's base, and began to speak.
