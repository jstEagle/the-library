## 21. Aftermath of the Free

The first thing Vellamarch repealed was the word *illegal* itself.

Bramwen Hale stood in the Council Chamber on a grey morning in late spring and watched the clerks strike the magic laws from the great ledger with strokes of red ink, line by line, like a surgeon closing a wound. It took four hours. Four hours for a hundred years of drowning-orders to become so much scratched-out parchment, and Bramwen wept through most of it, quietly, into a handkerchief that had once belonged to Sef.

Nobody noticed. That was the strange mercy of it. In the old days a scholar crying in the Council Chamber would have been noted, reported, perhaps questioned. Now the clerk beside him simply passed him a second handkerchief and went on striking lines.

"They'll want you to write it down," the clerk said afterward, gathering the spoiled pages. "What happened. All of it."

"I know," Bramwen said.

"They'll want it to sound clean."

"It wasn't clean."

"No," the clerk agreed, tucking the dead laws under his arm like a bundle of kindling. "But it's over. Write it however you like."

* * *

Bramwen had spent thirty years studying prophecy, and he had learned this: prophecy is history told backward by people who are afraid. The Codex had never been a book of what would be. It was a book of what someone, long ago, had desperately wanted—or desperately feared—written in language vague enough to survive being wrong.

He burned no books. He was not that sort of man anymore. But he did go home to his rooms above the archive—the archive that was now, absurdly, open to the public three days a week—and he took out the Hollow Codex, the genuine one, recovered from Aldric's private vaults, and he set it on his desk and looked at it for a long time.

Elodie's name was still there. Her date, too, though the ink had gone strange since the Unbinding, softening at the edges like breath on glass. He touched it once. The page felt warm, then ordinary, then merely paper.

Then he opened a fresh notebook, dipped his pen, and began:

*In the year the covenant broke, the city of Vellamarch smelled of bread.*

That was true. He had checked. Everyone always forgot that after wars came bread, because ovens that had sat cold during siege were lit again all at once, and the whole city exhaled the smell of it. No prophet had ever thought to record that. No prophet had ever recorded what people ate while their world ended. And yet Bramwen had come to believe it mattered more than anything written in the Codex—that history lived not in crowns and keystones but in the smell of bread drifting over a city learning to govern itself.

He wrote until his candle guttered. He wrote about the battle at the river walls, as best he could reconstruct it from testimony, though every time he reached the part where Sef opened the postern gate his pen slowed and his eyes blurred and he had to stop and look out the window at the ordinary dark.

He wrote about the gaoler's poisoned wine. About Ivenn offering his blood to the thorns before Elodie could give hers. About the Queen of Rust's price, which Elodie had carried alone for weeks and told no one—not even Bramwen, not even after. She'd confessed it finally on the road back from the capital, in the flat voice of someone setting down something heavier than they'd admitted lifting. He wrote that too, or tried to. Some things resisted prose. He left a space in the manuscript for them, marked with a small asterisk, meaning *return here when your hands are steadier.*

By midsummer the notebook had become two notebooks, and the two had become a stack, and the stack had a working title scrawled across its spine in Bramwen's cramped hand:

*A History of the Breaking, Being a True Account of the End of the Covenant and What Came After, Set Down by an Eyewitness Who Was Mostly Useless Throughout.*

Elodie laughed for a full minute when she read the title. Then she read the first chapter and stopped laughing, and when she looked up her eyes were wet and she said only, "You made me brave in it."

"You were brave in it."

"You made me brave *on the page*. There's a difference. On the page I don't shake."

"Then let them think you didn't," Bramwen said. "The shaking can be mine. I shook enough for both of us."

* * *

The lease was signed on the first of August.

Bramwen attended as a witness, mostly because Elodie asked him to and partly because he distrusted legal documents on principle and wanted to see one do something good for once. The shop stood on Tallow Lane, three doors down from where her old charm-stall had been burned, a narrow building with a crooked chimney and a window just wide enough to display wares. The landlord was a widow named Marta Quill who had lost two sons to the drownings—one drowned, in fact, for a healing charm meant to save their father—and who had offered Elodie the rent at half rate and refused, absolutely, to hear any argument about it.

"You'll take the discount," Marta said, jabbing the pen at her, "or I'll tell everyone in this quarter that the Coldharbour girl is too proud to accept kindness from a washerwoman's mother, and then you'll have no customers at all out of shame."

"That's extortion."

"That's business," said Marta Quill, and initialled the margin.

So it was done. Elodie Coldharbour, last heir of a line of wardens, signatory to nothing now but a lease, held the pen over the paper and paused.

"What?" Bramwen asked.

"Just—" She turned the document toward him. "Look at it. My name. On a piece of paper that says I'm allowed."

"Allowed?"

"Allowed to exist. Professionally." Her mouth twisted, half smile, half something older and sorer. "Fifteen years I sold charms out of a basket with my sleeve pulled down over my wrist so nobody would see the work-glow. Fifteen years listening for boots on stairs. And now there's a paper." She signed. The signature was quick, unornamented, the handwriting of a woman who had learned to sign fast in case she needed to run. "There. Legal. Gods help us all."

The shop had no name over the door yet. Elodie said she'd get to it. For the first week the sign was a slate that read, in her own blunt lettering: *CHARMS. FAIR PRICES. NO KING'S BUSINESS.*

Customers came anyway.

They came shyly at first—a laundress wanting a luck-knot refreshed, a fishwife with an ache in her hands, a boy of maybe ten who stood in the doorway for twenty minutes working up the courage to ask if magic hurt, because his grandmother had told him it did, right up until the day they took her to the river. Elodie knelt to be at his eye level and said, "It tingles. Like when your foot falls asleep. And it works better if you're laughing, so we'll start with something funny." She taught him a charm that made soap bubbles hold their shape for an hour. He left grinning with a fistful of impossible bubbles and Bramwen, watching from the corner where he'd installed himself with a notebook, wrote down every word.

Because that was the other thing the new book needed. Not just the war. The morning after. The boy with the bubbles. The first wedding where the bride wore a luck-charm openly, braided into her hair, and half the guests cried. The night markets where hedge-witches set up stalls between the cheesemongers, hawking weather-charms and toothache salves, arguing loudly about technique because now they could argue instead of hiding.

A world learning self-rule, one small permission at a time.

* * *

Ivenn stayed.

Everyone had assumed he wouldn't. Bramwen had assumed it himself, and been surprised at how much the assumption hurt—surprised to discover that somewhere in the firelight arguments and the muddy miles and the terrible jokes about mortal cooking, the fae exile had become, without anyone voting on it, part of the household.

But when the realms separated freely at the Unbinding, and the Hollow opened onto the mortal world through gates that neither realm guarded, Ivenn had walked to the nearest gate with his pack on his shoulder and stood before it for the better part of an evening.

Then he'd turned around and come back to the fire and said, "Someone has to teach these idiots which mushrooms are lying to them," and that was the whole speech.

Later, much later, sitting on the roof of the Tallow Lane shop watching mortals light their lamps below, he'd given Bramwen a truer version. Bramwen hadn't asked for it. It arrived the way confessions arrive from the fae—sideways, unbidden, disguised as commentary.

"A century ago I defended mortals and my own people called it treason," Ivenn said. "And I told myself for a hundred years that I regretted nothing, which was a lie, because exile is nothing *but* regret wearing armor. And then the covenant broke, and the gate opened, and I could have gone home. Truly home. Forgiven. Welcomed." He plucked at a loose thread on his sleeve. "Do you know what I found, standing in front of that gate? I found that home had never been the place. Home was the defending. The standing between. And there's no standing between in a realm where everyone is like you." He glanced at Bramwen with those eyes like weather coming. "Your people are still learning to be free. Free people need someone who remembers what chains look like. I'm keeping the job."

"You could have said all that at the time," Bramwen said, "instead of the mushroom remark."

"The mushroom remark was also true. Your people will eat anything. It's a miracle your species survived."

He took a room above the shop. He hung a small carved sign outside it that read *IVENN — CONSULTATIONS*, which alarmed several neighbors until it became clear that consultations consisted mostly of telling people their charms were shoddily constructed and then fixing them while complaining. Within a month, children followed him through the streets in a ragged adoring pack, begging for illusions, and he pretended to be annoyed and produced foxes made of autumn light, and Bramwen filled another notebook.

* * *

On a soft evening in early autumn, Bramwen climbed the stairs to the shop's little kitchen, where Elodie was cooking—Ivanish-spiced stew, by the smell, a recipe Ivenn had surrendered under protest—and found her reading his latest chapter by lamplight.

She didn't look up. "The section on the dungeon."

"Yes."

"You got the words right. What Bramwen—what I said to Aldric." She still stumbled sometimes, talking about herself in the third person of the manuscript. "You were there. You heard it."

"Every word. I wrote it down that same night, before sleep could take any of it."

"Why?"

"Because it was true, and true things spoken to power are rare enough that losing one feels like theft." Bramwen sat down heavily at the table. His knees ached; they had ached since Ravenmoor, since the iron, since the scar across his ribs that ached worse in cold weather and reminded him daily that he had once, improbably, been worth shielding. "Prophecy lies, Elodie. It lies grandly, in beautiful verse, and kings build thrones on the lies. But a scholar with a notebook and a stubborn streak—"

"—can't lie," Elodie finished. "You're physically incapable. I've watched you try."

"I'm *bad* at it," he corrected with dignity. "Which is nearly as useful."

She pushed the bowl of stew toward him and tapped the manuscript. "Then keep going. All of it. Even the ugly parts. Especially those. Someone in fifty years is going to stand exactly where I stood, terrified, holding something they don't want and can't put down, and they won't find me in any Codex." She smiled, tired and real. "They'll find you."

Outside, dusk was settling over Tallow Lane. The lamps were being lit—by lamplighters now, though more than one of them used a flame-charm openly, and no one ran, and no one drowned, and the river kept nothing but reflections.

Bramwen opened a fresh notebook to the first blank page. At the top he wrote the date. Beneath it, the first sentence of the second book, the one that would outlast them all:

*After the breaking, the city smelled of bread, and a woman on Tallow Lane lit a lamp with a snap of her fingers, legally, for the first time in a hundred years—and no one came to arrest her, and that silence, that beautiful absence of boots on stairs, is where this history truly begins.*

He looked up to find Elodie watching him, and beyond her, through the window, the crooked chimney against the deepening blue, and somewhere below the laughter of the bubble-boy selling miracles for coppers.

"Chapter two," Bramwen said, and dipped his pen.
