## 22. August and the Hollow

The peaches came in on a Tuesday, bruised and honey-smelling, and Elodie bit into the first one before she even thought about it.

That was the thing about a year of freedom. The old habits didn't die; they just changed jobs. Her tongue still catalogued everything—bitterness under sweetness, the faint metallic ghost of a knife that hadn't been cleaned properly, the green edge of a fruit picked too soon. Fifteen years of tasting for kings had built a instrument out of her mouth, and instruments don't retire. They just find new music.

"Poisoned?" asked Marta from across the table, deadpan, holding up her own peach.

"Overripe," Elodie said. "You'll forgive me if I don't weep."

Marta laughed, and the sound went around the long table the way laughter does in August when the windows are all open—easily, without permission, spilling into the street. There were eleven people at Elodie's table tonight, which was two fewer than last week because the ferry workers had gone back downriver, but more than she'd ever once imagined feeding in her life. The trestle boards had been Bramwen's idea, dragged out of the old archive salvage and planed smooth by Ivenn, who claimed to hate carpentry and had sanded every inch twice.

The shop below smelled of rosemary bread and lamp oil and, underneath it all, the particular clean mineral scent that drifted up from the standing stones whenever the wind came out of the Hollow. Elodie had stopped noticing it months ago. Tonight she noticed it again, and smiled at it like an old acquaintance.

"You're doing the face," said Ivenn.

She looked up. He was leaning in the doorway between shop and stair, arms crossed, silver-haired even by fae standards, wearing a mortal linen shirt he'd complained about for a full season and now refused to take off.

"What face?"

"The one where you taste something no one else can." He came down the steps. "Wind's turning."

"I know."

"How do you know?"

Elodie held up the peach pit, turned it in her fingers. "Because the peaches are early this year, and the river smells like iron, and there's a thread of cold coming through the gate that tastes like—" she searched for it, the way she'd once searched for arsenic in a lord's wine, "—like the first page of a book you've read before."

Ivenn considered this with the gravity he gave everything, which was either very little or very much depending on whether he wanted you to know. "In the Hollow we'd have called that an omen."

"In Vellamarch we call it Tuesday."

* * *

After the plates were cleared and Marta had fallen asleep sitting up and been walked home by her daughter, after the last neighbor's lantern bobbed away down Copper Lane, Elodie stood in the open doorway of the charm-shop and let the night come in.

The lease was framed above the counter now, behind glass, because Bramwen insisted documents were sacred and Ivenn insisted anything worth keeping should be defended like a keep. *Coldharbour Charms & Tasting — E. Coldharbour, Proprietor.* The city clerk had written the title himself, tongue between his teeth, and when he'd handed it over he'd said, quietly, "My mother bought luck-charms off your grandmother. Before." Before was a word the whole city used now, the way you'd use *before a storm*. Before the drowning law. Before Aldric. Before the covenant broke like a bad knot and both realms came loose into their own hands.

The repeal had taken eleven months of arguing in the new Assembly, and the first legal charm sold in Vellamarch in a century had been a wart-removal salve, which everyone agreed was fittingly unglamorous. Elodie had cried anyway, alone, in the back room, over a jar of salve.

Now the shelves were half-empty because business was good and honesty was better: love charms labeled plainly as *encouragement, results not guaranteed*, fever-tinctures, a whole row of tasting-services for the free-city merchants who wanted their grain checked without hiring a court's worth of paranoia. She charged what people could pay. Some paid in eggs. One fisherman paid in stories, weekly, and she'd have kept him as a customer for the stories alone.

The wind came through the door and it was wrong for August.

Not cold exactly—Vellamarch summers were never cold—but *autumn*, the specific dry leaf-and-smoke breath of a season that hadn't arrived yet, threading through a summer night like a note from another song. It came from the north, from the hills, from the standing stones.

Elodie closed her eyes and tasted it fully, the way Sef had taught her to taste wine: hold it, let it speak.

Sef. A year, and she could think his name without the grief closing over it like water. Most days.

The wind carried something else too. Faint. Familiar. Not a threat—her tongue would have known, her tongue knew threats the way other tongues knew their mothers—but an *invitation*, maybe, or a question. The Hollow had been sending these winds since the dissolution. Bramwen theorized it was weather finding its natural paths again, two realms breathing through an open window. Ivenn said the Hollow missed them. Elodie privately thought both were right, and that neither realm needed guarding anymore, and that the gate standing unwatched in the hills was the single best thing her family's blood had ever done—not the wardenship, not the power, but the *opening*.

She locked nothing. There was nothing worth locking that mattered, and the door stuck anyway.

* * *

Bramwen arrived the next morning with ink on three fingers and news in her eyes, which for Bramwen was the same as having news in her mouth, just faster.

"You felt it," she said, before hello.

"The autumn wind. Yes."

"Not just yesterday." Bramwen set her satchel on the counter among the salves and began unpacking papers with the controlled urgency of a woman who had spent a career being told her life's work was illegal and intended never to be rushed again. "Every settlement along the Verre has reported it. Ashenport says their salt-pans froze overnight—lightly, harmlessly, but in *August*, Elodie. And the Hollow side is reporting the same in reverse. Summer winds in their autumn. The seasons are—" she spread a hand-drawn map covered in annotations, "—trading."

"Trading," Elodie repeated.

"Borrowing. Sharing. Pick your word." Bramwen tapped the map. "The dissolution didn't sever the realms, it *opened* them. We knew that. But nobody modeled what an open border does to weather, to magic, to—" she caught herself, the old scholar's discipline reasserting, "—to anything. I've been writing the history, and the history keeps trying to become current events."

"That sounds like a you problem."

"It is absolutely a you problem." Bramwen looked up, and beneath the pedantry Elodie saw the other thing, the thing that had survived a dungeon and an iron wound and a year of stubborn recovery: affection, worn openly now, like a scar she'd decided to stop hiding. "The Assembly wants to send someone to the stones. Someone the Hollow knows. Someone whose name means something on both sides of the gate."

"No."

"You haven't heard—"

"I don't need to hear it. Whatever title they want to give me, whatever *warden* or *ambassador* or *heir of the Coldharbour line*—no." Elodie wiped the counter with more force than the counter required. "I spent fifteen years being useful to a crown and welcome nowhere. I'm not trading one office for another. The whole point—the entire point, Bramwen, of everything we did—was that no one inherits this. Not me, not anyone. Belonging isn't a position. You can't appoint it."

"I wasn't going to offer you a title." Bramwen's mouth did the small dry thing that was her version of laughing. "I was going to ask if you'd host the delegation. Here. At your table. Because the Hollow's envoys won't meet officials, they'll only meet *you*, and because Marta's peach bread is apparently famous in a realm most mortals can't spell."

Elodie stopped wiping the counter.

"They asked for me by name?"

"They asked for 'the woman who feeds people.'" Bramwen gathered her papers. "Which, for the record, is how the Queen of Rust referred to you in the official record of the dissolution. I checked. Pedantically. It's in the archives under *Treaties, Unusual*."

* * *

The delegation came at the end of the month, and it was four fae and a hedgehog, and the hedgehog was, as far as anyone could determine, the actual envoy.

They sat at Elodie's table in the early evening while Marta's peach bread cooled on the sill and Ivenn pretended not to hover and Bramwen took notes until one of the fae gently turned her notebook toward herself and drew a small flower on it, which Bramwen later described as "the most humbling peer review of my career."

The eldest envoy was a woman made mostly of birch-light and patience. She spoke slowly, the way rivers speak.

"We bring no terms," she said. "There are no terms anymore. That is why we came to a table instead of a throne."

"Then what do you bring?" Elodie asked.

"Questions. And thanks." The birch-woman's gaze moved around the table—to Ivenn, who had chosen them; to Bramwen, who was writing their history; to the neighbors drifting in with dishes because no one in Copper Lane had ever once managed to walk past this table without contributing something. "When the covenant bound us, we forgot how to visit. A hundred years of wall, and then a hundred years of dying behind it. Now there is a gate with no guard, and our young ones walk through it to see your markets, and your children run through it to catch our fireflies, and no one drowns, and no one fades." She folded her hands. "We wished to thank the one who opened it. And to ask her: what shall we call this? Every age needs a name, or it gets named by fools."

Elodie thought about it. She thought about it through the bread and the introductions and the moment when the hedgehog fell asleep in Marta's lap and Marta, who had buried two husbands and survived the burning of her own street, held it like a grandchild.

What should they call it?

Not the Age of the Coldharbour—heavens, no. Not the Unbinding, though Bramwen would use that in the book regardless. Not freedom, because freedom wasn't a name, it was a practice, done daily, like kneading dough or checking a stew.

"Call it August," she said finally.

The birch-woman tilted her head.

"It's the month when everything ripens at once," Elodie said. "When you eat what your neighbors grew, and the nights are warm enough to leave the doors open, and nothing is stored yet, nothing is hoarded—everything is shared before it spoils. It doesn't last. That's rather the point. You don't get to keep August. You get to *be in* it, every year, if you tend the orchard." She shrugged, suddenly shy, the food-taster's habit of self-erasure surfacing one last time before she smoothed it flat. "It's not an age. It's a season. It comes back if we keep showing up for it."

The birch-woman was quiet a long moment. Then she laughed—a sound like wind through high leaves—and the other envoys laughed too, and somewhere in the laugh Elodie felt something settle in her chest that had been unsettled for thirty years: not the weight of a legacy lifted, but the warmth of a table joined.

"So be it," the envoy said. "August. In both tongues. Let the fools try to rename a season."

* * *

They left at moonrise, walking north toward the stones, unhurried, unhindered, unguarded. Elodie walked them to the edge of the lamplight and stood watching until the dark took them, and the autumn-in-August wind came down Copper Lane and stirred her hair like a hand on a child's head.

Ivenn appeared beside her. He didn't say anything for a while. He'd gotten better at silence in the mortal realm; he wore it now like the linen shirt.

"You could go back," she said. "Any time. The gate's open. Your kin would take you."

"My kin," Ivenn said, "spent a hundred years fading behind a wall and called it dignity. I spent those same years learning that walls are just fear with better masonry." He looked down the lane, at the lit windows, at Marta's house where the hedgehog was apparently staying the night, at Bramwen's upstairs room glowing with late lamplight and worse prose. "Besides. Someone has to sand the tables. You'd do it badly. You sand like a taster—all surface, no patience."

"I sand beautifully."

"You sand like a woman in a hurry to feed people, which is admirable and terrible for furniture."

Elodie laughed, and the laugh went up the lane and woke a dog, and the dog barked twice and settled, and none of it mattered and all of it mattered, and that was August.

They stood together in the doorway a while longer. Below the smell of bread and lamp oil, the wind off the Hollow carried its thread of cold and green and otherwhere, and Elodie tasted it one last time before sleep: not poison, not omen, not duty.

Just the future, arriving early, ripe, and shared.

She left the door open behind her.
