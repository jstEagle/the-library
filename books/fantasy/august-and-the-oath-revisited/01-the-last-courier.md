## 1. The Last Courier

The frost came down off the Thornfells before dawn, and Lena Blackwood was already above it.

She moved along the spine of the Grey Stair with the ease of long habit, boots finding the worn hollows in the stone that three generations of couriers had carved into the mountain's shoulder. Below her, the valley still slept under a skin of river-fog, the lamps of Hollowmere guttering out one by one as the night watch went home. Above her, nothing but rock and sky and the thin, whistling wind that never quite stopped singing through the passes. The old-timers said the mountains were talking when the wind rose like that. Lena had stopped listening to what they said years ago.

The cargo rode light against her back: a leather satchel sealed with wax and wire, twelve pounds of letters, ledgers, and — if the seal on the inner packet was anything to judge by — coin-notes worth more than the whole village of Hollowmere would earn in a season. Merchant debt, mostly. Debts owed by merchants to other merchants, and beneath those, debts owed by everyone to the banks of the lowland cities, and beneath *those*, debts nobody wrote down at all: the guild's own, mounting like snowpack on a roof too steep to hold it.

Twelve pounds of paper. It was what remained of the Thornfell Guild's glory, and it was hers to carry because there was no one left to carry it instead.

She counted the switchbacks without thinking — forty-one from the Stair's crown to the frozen ford, thirty-eight if you took the goat track behind the cairn, which she did, because the goat track didn't show against the skyline and the Stair did. Somewhere on the far side of the ridge, a Court patrol would be riding the high road out of Ashfall, grey cloaks dusted white, checking travelers' papers with the bored thoroughness of men paid by the day. They'd find nothing on the goat track. Nobody ever found anything on the courier roads. That was the whole point of them, and the whole pride of the guild, and the whole of what was left.

*Forty-one steps down, then the ledge, then the chimney.* Her grandmother's voice lived in the counting, though her grandmother had been eleven years dead. *Trust the stone, not your eyes. The stone doesn't lie about ice.*

Lena trusted the stone. She trusted the ice. She trusted the forty-one switchbacks and the ledge and the chimney and the hidden door in the waystation wall at Millbrook that opened only for someone who knew to press the third stone from the lintel while humming the first four notes of a harvest song no one sang anymore. She trusted all of it completely, and she hated how completely she trusted it, because trust like that meant the mountains had grown into her bones, and bones don't come out.

Three hundred years, the Thornfell Guild had run these passes. Three hundred years of couriers carrying what kings and courts and merchant princes couldn't carry openly — letters during the ban, grain-records during the famine, children during the worst year anyone still spoke of only in whispers. The guild had been the arteries of the mountains when every official road was a throat someone could choke. There had been two hundred sworn couriers once. There had been a hall in Hollowmere with the names of every courier who died on the lines cut into its beams, so many names the beams had been replaced twice.

Now there were nine. Nine sworn couriers, and the hall's beams were rotten, and last winter they'd burned the west wing for firewood rather than let the cold take the youngest of them, a boy named Tam who'd lasted four months on the lines before a fall took his leg and his nerve together. He'd gone home to his mother's farm in the spring. No one blamed him. Everyone understood. That was somehow worse.

Nine couriers. And this morning, eight, because Wren had taken fever in Duncarrie and old Maren had sent word that Wren wouldn't walk a line again before midsummer, if ever.

Which left the Duncarrie route — the longest, coldest, most exposed line the guild still ran — to Lena. Alone. In the last week of autumn, with the first true snows already sealing the high cols.

*Of course,* she thought, dropping off the ledge onto the shelf below, knees absorbing the impact the way she'd been taught before she'd been taught her letters. *Of course it's mine.*

It was always hers now. That was the arithmetic of a dying guild: fewer shoulders, same weight, until the weight found the strongest shoulder and stayed there. Corvan called it an honor. Corvan called everything an honor — the shrinking routes, the unpaid winters, the slow erosion of the guild's territory into the hands of lowland factors who used mules and hired blades and didn't care what knowledge died with them. Corvan had been guildmaster for six years, and in six years he had not once carried a pack over a pass himself. His hands were still soft. Lena noticed things like that. She'd been noticing them more lately, and noticing made something cold settle behind her ribs that she chose, most days, not to examine.

The chimney came next: a vertical crack in the cliff face, twenty feet of it, wide enough for a body and narrow enough that a body felt the mountain pressing close on both sides. She climbed it with the satchel strapped flat to her spine, fingers reading the rock like text. Halfway up, the wind gusted through the crack and tried to peel her off the wall, and she pressed herself flat and waited it out, cheek against stone so cold it burned.

And there, in the waiting, with the whole grey world reduced to rock and wind, the thought arrived that had been circling her for weeks, patient as a wolf:

*Nobody will remember any of this.*

Not the routes. Not the hand-signs. Not the forty-one switchbacks or the third stone from the lintel or the harvest song. When the last courier died or quit or froze, all of it would go with her — three centuries of mapped knowledge, bought with blood across generations, dissolving like breath off warm stone. The lowlanders would build their cart-roads through the valleys and their toll-gates on the cols, and the mountains would go quiet, and no one would know that they had ever been *spoken through*. That people had once moved inside them like meaning moves through language.

Her grandmother had told her, once, sitting by the stove with Lena's small hands wrapped around a mug of barley tea: *A courier isn't a smuggler, girl. A smuggler moves goods. A courier moves trust. Anyone can carry a box. Only one of us can carry a promise across a mountain in winter and have it arrive unbroken.* Lena had been seven. She had believed it the way children believe — totally, without cost.

She was twenty-six now, and belief had gotten expensive.

She topped out of the chimney, rolled onto the summit shelf, and lay there a moment looking up at a sky going from iron to pale gold along the eastern peaks. Her breath smoked. Her heart hammered pleasantly, the way it always did after hard climbing — the body's simple animal joy in being alive and equal to the morning. That was the part she couldn't kill, no matter how she tried. However bitter the thought of the guild's dying got, the moment her boots touched stone, some bright engine in her chest lit up and *sang*.

Pride, her grandmother would have called it. Pride curdling into grief, and grief curdling back into pride, over and over, like milk left out too many times.

She got up. She ran the ridge.

* * *

Duncarrie sat in a bowl of hills two days' hard travel west, and Lena covered the distance in a day and a half, because the Duncarrie route had a middle section — the Hollow Way, a seam of collapsed mine-tunnels threading under the shoulder of Crag Fell — that shaved a full day off any honest road. The tunnels were guild property in every way that mattered: mapped in no ledger but the ones in courier heads, maintained by no one, known to no one outside the brotherhood of the passes. She walked them by touch and memory, past timber props older than the kingdom, past a shrine some miner had carved into the rock two centuries back — a woman holding a lantern, her face worn smooth by time and by the hands of everyone who'd ever passed and needed luck.

Lena pressed her thumb to the lantern-woman's smooth face, as she always did. Habit. Superstition. Whatever kept the feet sure.

She came out of the Hollow Way at dusk on the second day, into air that smelled wrong.

Woodsmoke, yes — Duncarrie cooked with peat, not wood. Horse, yes — there was always horse-musk near the town. But under both, sharp and recent: ash. Not hearth-ash. *Burned-building* ash, the acrid char-smell of thatch and timber gone past saving, and under it something else, fainter, that raised the hair on her arms before her mind caught up to it.

Cloves. Someone nearby wore cloves, heavy and expensive, the kind of scent that masked other scents — the kind of scent, she'd heard once from a courier who'd run the southern lines, that the Court's agents used, because cloves killed the smell of blood.

Lena went still among the boulders above the town and made herself look properly.

Duncarrie's eastern quarter was black. Two buildings gone to char, maybe three, smoke still lifting off them in the windless evening in straight grey columns. People moved in the streets below — few, hunched, moving wrong, the way people move after violence, quick and flinching. And drawn up in the market square, unhurried, unmistakable even at this distance: horses. Tall, grey, well-bred, standing with the discipline of animals trained to stand through screaming.

Riders waited beside them. Six of them. Their cloaks were the color of old ash.

Lena sank down behind the rock and made herself breathe slowly, the way she'd been taught — *in for four, hold for four, out for four; panic spends the legs before the road does* — and made herself think.

The Ashen Court did not ride into mountain towns. That was the first fact, and it rearranged everything. For three hundred years the Court had governed the lowlands and left the Thornfells alone, contemptuous of the poor grey baronies and their smuggling veins, interested in the mountains exactly as much as the mountains produced nothing worth taking. Couriers joked about it. *We're beneath their notice, and thank every god for it.* The Court collected taxes it never bothered to collect, claimed authority it never bothered to exercise. Its riders appeared on the high roads sometimes, checking papers, and that was all. That was supposed to be all.

Six riders in a burned town square was not *all*.

And they were facing west. All six of them, and all six horses, oriented toward the western passes — toward the deep country beyond Duncarrie where the maps in every courier's head went vague and the routes thinned to game trails and legend. As if they were waiting for something coming out of the east. As if they were watching a road.

*They're not here for the town,* Lena realized, and the understanding settled over her with the weight of snow sliding off a roof. *They're here for a traveler. Someone on the east road. Someone expected.*

She should turn around. Every instinct honed by fifteen years on the lines said *turn around, drop the cargo in a cache, go home, report to Corvan, let the guild decide*. The guild had rules for this. The guild had protocols older than her grandmother for exactly this situation: contact with Court forces, withdraw, observe, report. She recited them to herself like a prayer and found, underneath the prayer, the other thing — the thing that had been growing in her all day, all month, all these long dwindling years.

The guild had nine members. The guild's protocols assumed a guild that would exist long enough to act on a report.

And below her, limping along the ditch at the edge of the east road — moving away from the town, away from the riders, moving with the desperate economy of a man spending strength he didn't have — was a figure. A single traveler, dark-cloaked, bent, leaving a trail in the fresh snowfall that even Lena could read from two hundred yards: drag, drag, step. Drag, drag, step.

He was heading for the tree line. Toward her. Toward the high country where the riders' horses couldn't follow and their patience wouldn't matter.

One of the riders turned his head, tracking the figure, and said something to the others. None of them moved. That was the detail that turned Lena's blood to ice water: none of them moved. They weren't chasing him. They were *herding* him — letting him run himself into the high ground, into the dark, into the cold, where a wounded man would lie down somewhere to rest and simply never get up. Clean. Quiet. No witnesses, no mess in a town square. The mountains doing the Court's work for free, the way the mountains did everything for free.

Lena looked at the satchel on her back — twelve pounds of debt and dying legacy — and then at the treeline, and then at the drag-drag-step trail in the snow, and heard her grandmother's voice one more time, clear across eleven years of silence:

*A courier moves trust, girl. Anyone can carry a box.*

"Damn it," she said aloud, to the mountain, to the frost, to whatever was left listening. "Damn it all."

She came off the rocks and cut downslope through the pines, silent, fast, angling to intercept the trail before the dark finished its work — and behind her, in the square of Duncarrie, the six ash-grey riders finally swung up into their saddles, unhurried as dusk, and began to ride.
